Penalized Estimation in Finite Mixtures of Multivariate Regression Models via the EM‐PGM Algorithm
Authors: Heeyeon Kang, Sunyoung Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn many areas of medical and life sciences research, multiple diagnostic criteria and phenotypic outcomes are assessed simultaneously to characterize diseases and biological traits. These multivariate outcomes are often associated with high‐dimensional covariates and subject to underlying population heterogeneity, presenting challenges for analysis and interpretation. To address these issues, we study a finite mixture of multivariate regression models (mvFMR), which jointly models multiple outcomes while capturing latent subgroup structure. By accounting for both outcome multiplicity and data heterogeneity, mvFMR improves predictive performance and enhances interpretability for multivariate outcomes in complex datasets. We adopt a penalized maximum likelihood approach to estimate mvFMR, which enables scalability and variable selection in high‐dimensional settings. We further develop the EM‐PGM algorithm, an efficient estimation procedure that combines the expectation‐maximization (EM) framework with the proximal gradient method (PGM) to handle high‐dimensionality and the non‐differentiability of the penalty function. Simulation studies demonstrate that mvFMR with EM‐PGM outperforms alternative methods in terms of estimation accuracy, sparsity recovery, and computational efficiency. Applications to analyses of diabetes diagnosis data and Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia data illustrate the practical utility of our penalized mvFMR.Keywords: expectation‐maximization algorithm, finite mixture models, multivariate regression, penalized maximum likelihood, proximal gradient method

Introduction

Many studies in the medical and life sciences involve multiple diagnostic criteria for determining the presence of a disease or multiple phenotypes that are essential for characterizing traits of an organism. These diagnostic criteria are often linked to numerous potential factors associated with the disease, thereby necessitating the identification of relevant risk factors. Similarly, phenotypes may be associated with high‐dimensional biological profiles such as genotypes, proteotypes, and metabotypes. Multivariate linear regression investigates the complex relationships among multivariate responses and high‐dimensional features by taking advantage of correlations among response variables [1, 2, 3].

In medical and biological research, the data collected from diverse individuals are often heterogeneous and complex due to the inherent variability of individual characteristics. To capture and interpret this diversity, finite mixture models are a useful tool, as they allow the presence of latent subpopulations within the data by representing the overall distribution as a mixture of component distributions [4, 5, 6]. A finite mixture of regression models with multiple responses (mvFMR) can identify heterogeneous subgroups with distinct characteristics and discover features associated with multiple outcomes by accounting for correlations among outcome variables.

Penalized maximum likelihood approaches have been considered for simultaneous variable selection and parameter estimation in mvFMR since including all predictors in the model may lead to increased complexity and reduced interpretability. Wang, Zhang, and Mai studied mixture linear regression models using group penalization techniques under the assumption that sparsity patterns are identical across all mixture components [7]. Their method can be extended to multivariate response settings, where the error covariance is assumed to be common to all components and known in advance. Hyun et al. devised a sparse mixture of multivariate regression models using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) to analyze microbial oceanographic data with time‐varying predictors [8, 9]. The mixing proportions as well as the Gaussian means are modeled as functions of predictors in a mixtures‐of‐experts framework such that the mixture proportions vary among individual observations. In addition, the sizes of the deviations of the cluster means at all times are limited away from the corresponding overall centers. In line with the literature, we facilitate high‐dimensional variable selection while maintaining model interpretability by incorporating shrinkage into the mvFMR framework. Our penalized mvFMR framework estimates covariance structures as well as regression coefficients for different components and mixing proportions shared across samples. Our penalized mvFMR approach may provide a more intuitive and interpretable formulation, generalizing penalized approaches for finite mixtures of univariate regression models to multivariate settings [10, 11]. We consider three widely used penalty functions: LASSO, the smoothly clipped absolute deviation (SCAD), and the minimax concave penalty (MCP) [9, 12, 13]. Our work may also be viewed as an extension of Wang, Zhang, and Mai's method [7], allowing it to be applied in settings with unknown noise structures.

Our approach has broad applicability in biological, medical, and public health research. We often encounter large‐scale biological datasets that contain high‐dimensional molecular biological information, such as gene expression levels, together with diverse clinical and biological outcomes. In particular, when incorporating all diagnostic criteria for a disease or condition, we may identify patient subgroups with shared characteristics and detect biological features linked to the disease. Subgroup‐level analysis may reveal heterogeneous patterns and potential causal mechanisms across such subgroups, thereby contributing to the development of precision medicine and targeted treatment strategies.

Proximal gradient methods (PGM) provide efficient and stable optimization procedures for ℓ1 and structured sparsity regularization problems by separating the optimization of a differentiable function from that of a nondifferentiable function [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. The differentiable part of the objective function is optimized using gradient descent, while the nondifferentiable part is handled efficiently using the proximal operator. We propose an EM algorithm combined with the PGM for penalized estimation of mvFMR, named the EM‐PGM algorithm. This approach allows for effective optimization while maintaining the flexibility of the EM framework. In our implementation, the PGM is incorporated into the M‐step of the EM algorithm, similarly to the EM algorithm that Cappozzo, Casa, and Fop proposed to maximize the likelihood of matrix Gaussian mixture models penalized with group LASSO and graphical LASSO [19]. The EM algorithm proposed by Wang, Zhang, and Mai employs a groupwise‐majorization‐descent (GMD) algorithm in the M‐step; however, it assumes the error structures known in advance [7]. Hyun et al. implemented a specific alternating direction method of multiplier (ADMM) solver within an EM framework to fit their model [8]. This approach may be adapted to maximize the penalized likelihood for the FMR; however, it requires solving additional Sylvester's equations. Our EM‐PGM algorithm avoids this requirement and saves computational cost in high‐dimensional scenarios.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the mvFMR framework, and Section 3 presents the penalized maximum likelihood approach for mvFMR. Section 4 details the proposed EM‐PGM algorithm. In Section 5, simulation studies demonstrate the superior performance of the penalized estimation approach for mvFMR implemented via EM‐PGM, compared to several alternatives. Section 6 provides two real data applications. The first analyzes diabetes diagnosis data, where fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels—the primary diagnostic criteria for diabetes—are examined in relation to potential risk factors within the mvFMR framework. The second application examines the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) data, which investigates drug sensitivity, assessing the influence of gene expression levels on compound responses in CCLE cell lines.

Selection of Tuning Parameters

In our model, the regularization parameters to be selected are the penalization parameter λ and the number of components K; the true number of components is denoted by K. We use a modified Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to simultaneously select K and λ by minimizing

over a grid of candidate values of K and λ, where −ℓ(·) is the negative log‐likelihood and de=K+(K−1)+∑r,s1{B^K,rs≠0} is the effective number of parameters [22]. The modified BIC accounts for the reduction in effective degrees of freedom induced by λ, by penalizing the effective degrees of freedom. However, the standard BIC, which penalizes the total number of parameters, cannot account for this reduction. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), which is optimized for predictive accuracy, can also be adapted in a manner similar to BIC; however, it may still lead to overfitting [23, 24, 25]. The modified BIC has also been used in other penalized model‐based clustering approaches [19, 22, 26].

In both simulation studies and real data examples, we search a grid of K, K={2,3,4,5,6} (including K=1 in Section 5.3 to verify recovery of the true number of components K). For each K, we fit with standardized predictors over a logarithmically spaced grid of λ with about 30 to 40 values. The final K^ is chosen based on the λ‐optimized fits. These are practical settings that can be used in applications, although the grid size may be increased if needed.

Simulation Studies

We considered a Gaussian finite mixture of regression models ∑k=1Kπk𝒩Bk⊤x,∑k with πk>0,∑k=1Kπk=1. The coefficient matrices Bk were sparse in the sense that lots of elements of Bk are exactly 0. The predictors x were generated from a multivariate normal distribution with mean vector 0 and variance‐covariance ∑ with size n=500,1000. We considered two designs: (i) independent coordinates, ∑=Ip and (ii) equicorrelated coordinates with common pairwise correlation 0.5, that is, ∑ii=1 and ∑ij=0.5 for i≠j. In Section 5.1 and 5.2, penalized estimators for mvFMR (mvFMR‐LASSO, mvFMR‐SCAD, mvFMR‐MCP) were compared with maximum likelihood estimators for univariate Gaussian FMR, oracle estimators for mvFMR assuming that the zero coefficients were known a priori, and maximum likelihood estimators for mvFMR. Both the EM‐PGM algorithm and the EM‐ADMM algorithm were considered to implement the penalization methods for mvFMR. Univariate Gaussian FMRs with each of the m responses are independently fitted using the R package flexmix [27, 28].

Both oracle estimators and unpenalized estimators for mvFMR were obtained through the EM algorithm, where the coefficient matrices of oracle estimators were computed element‐wise in the M‐step. In Section 5.3, we evaluated the performance of unpenalized estimators for mvFMR, mvFMR‐LASSO, mvFMR‐SCAD, and mvFMR‐MCP applying either the EM‐PGM or the EM‐ADMM algorithm.

In Section 5.1 and 5.2, we evaluated the performance of the estimators by investigating four metrics: true positive rate (TPR; the ratio of the number of truly identified active predictors over the total number of active predictors), false positive rate (FPR; the ratio of the number of falsely identified inactive predictors over the total number of inactive predictors), false discovery rate (FDR; the ratio of the number of falsely identified inactive predictors over the total number of identified predictors), and mean squared errors between the true parameters and the estimators (MSE). In Section 5.3, we evaluated the performance of the estimators with the predictive log‐likelihood loss, which is twice the negative log‐likelihood. All estimators were rounded down to the sixth decimal place. A summary of the purpose of each scenario is presented in Table S1.

We generated 500 independent simulated trials for each combination of model, method, and algorithm. Details of the step size choices used in the simulations are provided in the Appendix. Also, we fixed a=3.7 for the SCAD and MCP in both, while an absolute tolerance ϵabs=10−6 with a relative tolerance ϵrel=10−4 and the relaxation parameter α=1.5 in the EM‐ADMM algorithm. We initialized the EM algorithm with πk(0)=1/K,Bk(0)=0, and ∑k(0)=Im, for k=1,2,…,K. We checked the range of λ from 10−4 to 1, totaling 30 values, and selected the λ for which the modified BIC (21) was minimized by rounding down to the sixth decimal place.

The TPRs for all models are always 1, indicating that all methods are successful in identifying active predictors, but the FPRs and FDRs vary between methods. The univariate Gaussian FMR (Flexmix) and the unpenalized mvFMR (mvFMR) show poor performances, which are attributable to the fact that Flexmix supposes that each response is independent, and mvFMR does not perform variable selection. However, penalization methods, in particular, mvFMR‐SCAD and mvFMR‐MCP with PGM, have values close to 0, which implies that they are successful in identifying inactive predictors and excluding inactive predictors from the identified set across Models 1‐3. The oracle estimators, knowing which elements of the coefficients are 0 a priori, have the smallest MSEs. Both Flexmix and mvFMR show poor performance in terms of MSEs, which may be due to the inability to exactly estimate the sparse true coefficient matrices Bk. Both mvFMR‐SCAD and mvFMR‐MCP have similar MSEs, which are much smaller than those of Flexmix and mvFMR. Overall, both mvFMR‐SCAD and mvFMR‐MCP performed as well as the oracle estimators; therefore, we conclude that the use of SCAD and MCP penalty functions, solved via PGM, is desirable in the fitting of mvFMR. Real Data Analysis

Diabetes Diagnosis Data

According to the American Diabetes Association (ADA), the diagnosis of diabetes is determined by both glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting blood glucose level [29]. HbA1c is a blood test that measures the average level of blood sugar during the last two to three months. It provides a long‐term view of blood sugar control. Fasting blood glucose level gives a snapshot of the current blood sugar level at that specific time. HbA1c is positively correlated with fasting blood glucose level, as they both provide information on the presence and control of elevated blood sugar levels. According to ADA, an HbA1c level of 6.5% or higher, or a glucose level of 126 mg/dL or higher, is used as criteria to diagnose diabetes [29]. We aim to cluster patients into groups with similar characteristics while finding group‐specific relationships among HbA1c, glucose levels, and potential risk factors. We used the dataset obtained from interviews conducted in a study to determine the prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and other cardiovascular risk factors among African Americans in central Virginia by Dr. John Schorling, Department of Medicine, University of Virginia School of Medicine [30]. The dataset consists of HbA1c, stabilized glucose levels, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), age, gender, height, weight, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, waist circumference, and hip circumference of 375 samples. The stabilized blood glucose levels provided by the dataset may be used as a proxy for fasting blood glucose levels. The predictors, except gender, were centered and scaled.

We applied mvFMR‐MCP using the EM‐PGM algorithm (PGM) for K∈{2,3,4,5,6} and checked the range of λ from 10−4 to 1, totaling 30 values. For comparison, we also fitted mvFMR‐MCP using the EM‐ADMM algorithm (ADMM) over the same ranges of K and λ. In both cases, K and λ were selected by the modified BIC (21), resulting in K^=2. The corresponding BIC values and runtimes for both algorithms are presented in Figure S1 and Table S4, respectively. We additionally applied a multivariate Gaussian mixture model using the R package mclust, which selected K^=3 with mixing probabilities 0.4940,0.1772, and 0.3288 [31].

We first examined the clustering results of the PGM fitting using hard labels based on argmaxkwik for intuitive separation. Interestingly, the criteria for dividing the data into two groups are a glucose level around 126 mg/dL and an HbA1c level around 6.5%, which are consistent with the criteria for the diagnosis of diabetes by ADA [29]. Thus, our analysis based on mvFMR‐MCP considers people in Group 1 as healthy patients and Group 2 as diabetic patients, which is a well‐defined partition of the patients. Next, Table 11 shows the mixing proportion estimates, the regression coefficient estimates, and the correlation estimates between glucose and HbA1c levels. The two subpopulations have a ratio of 0.7767 and 0.2233, and the correlations for glucose and HbA1c levels are 0.1733 in Group 1 and 0.5076 in Group 2. The estimates obtained from the ADMM, summarized in Table S5, are broadly consistent with these results, yielding similar mixing proportions, regression coefficients, and correlations. As reported in Table S4, it takes 76 and 19 min to run PGM and ADMM, respectively, for K=2,…,6, and both runtimes are moderate. This is expected as this is a low‐dimensional case with 10 predictors. In Group 1, glucose and HbA1c levels are affected by some risk factors, such as HDL, age, weight, and waist circumference. On the other hand, total cholesterol, in Group 2, is found to be associated with both glucose and the HbA1c level. This shows that diabetes is quite closely related to hypercholesterolemia, the presence of high levels of cholesterol in the blood [32]. Glucose level, in Group 2, is found to be affected by all other risk factors as well, which is attributable to the fact that it has more variability than the HbA1c level. The glucose level measured at that specific time is highly affected by daily diet, physical activity, stress, and illness [33]. Glucose levels decrease on average by 0.0425 mg/dL in Group 1 and 0.5046 mg/dL in Group 2 for every 1 mg/dL increase in HDL, which is known as good cholesterol. Not only HDL, but other risk factors also have a greater impact on glucose level in Group 2 compared to Group 1, which may indicate that Group 2 has a more fluctuating metabolic system compared to Group 1. Our framework enables individualized prediction for a new patient. In contrast, the multivariate Gaussian mixture model captures only the joint distribution of glucose and HbA1c levels and does not assess how risk factors influence these responses.

The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia Data

The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) is a large‐scale cancer cell line characterization project led by the Broad Institute. We analyzed a public dataset available from the DepMap portal and the CCLE website to identify genes associated with the sensitivity of chemical compounds [34, 35]. CCLE provides 8‐point dose‐response curves for 24 chemical compounds in more than 1000 cell lines, as well as the expression data of 18 926 genes for each cell line. We used the area under the dose‐response curve, which is known as the activity area, to measure the sensitivity of a drug [7, 36]. We considered three widely used chemical compounds for cancer treatment: Erlotinib, AZD6244, and PD‐0325901. The responses are the activity areas of the three compounds, and the predictors are the gene‐expression profiles of the cell lines. We used n=489 cell lines tested for all three compounds. We keep p=300 genes that are highly correlated with the responses by calculating the sum of their absolute correlations with the three responses.

We applied mvFMR‐MCP using the EM‐PGM algorithm (PGM) and the EM‐ADMM algorithm (ADMM). For both algorithms, K and λ were selected by the modified BIC (21) over the grid of K and λ: K∈{2,3,4,5,6} and λ={10−2,…,1}, totaling 30 values. The corresponding BIC values for the mvFMR‐MCP using PGM and ADMM fits are presented in Figure S1, and the optimal number of components is K^=2 for both. Table 12 presents the estimates obtained from the PGM, including the mixing proportions, the correlations among the three drugs, and the regression coefficients for the variables selected. The mixing proportions of the two subpopulations in the data are 0.5018 and 0.4982. There are no gene expressions that affect all three drugs in both groups. In Group 1, TMEM55A and C3orf64 are selected as gene expression markers, while RXRG, ACTR1A, PRRG4, FUT3, EVPL, C16orf52, and SYTL1 are selected in Group 2. Increased TMEM55A has been reported to accelerate the removal of activated EGFR, leaving fewer targets for erlotinib to block; the corresponding estimate of −0.0090 in Group 1 is consistent with this mechanism [37, 38]. The results of the ADMM fitting, summarized in Table S7, are similar to those of the PGM fitting. However, obtaining these estimates with PGM takes much less time than obtaining them with ADMM. In Table S6, the runtimes of both the PGM and ADMM for K=2,…,6 are reported. PGM is roughly 12 times faster than ADMM overall, which represents a substantial computational advantage. Although the ADMM fit has additional genes selected, both fits have many genes selected in common, including TMEM55A, RXRG, and ACTR1A. For the genes selected by both fits, the estimated coefficients have the same sign.

We also applied a multivariate Gaussian mixture model using the R package mclust to diagnose baseline heterogeneity in the sensitivity of the three drugs without considering gene expressions [31]. While our proposed regression‐based method partitions the data into two components, the multivariate Gaussian mixture model fit suggests four clusters with probabilities 0.2358,0.2277,0.3242, and 0.2123. The additional two clusters identified by the multivariate Gaussian mixture model may be attributable to the distributions of the gene expressions rather than the distinct patterns in the activity area. Our penalized mvFMR can be used to predict the three drug activity areas given the gene expressions, whereas the multivariate Gaussian mixture model only captures the joint distributions of the activity areas.

Discussions

In this paper, we considered a sparse mixture of regression models with multiple responses. In the analysis of high‐dimensional data, we focused on the non‐time‐varying and intuitively interpretable models. We proposed the EM‐PGM algorithm, which is an EM algorithm that applies the PGM in its M‐step. The proposed algorithm is more efficient than the EM‐ADMM algorithm in that it has a lower computational cost and fewer hyperparameters to be determined. Furthermore, through simulation studies in Section 5, we demonstrated that the EM‐PGM algorithm performs successfully even when there is no information on the number of components within the data. In Section 6, the analyses of the diabetes diagnosis data and the CCLE data with mvFMR‐MCP identified key factors, their impacts, and the diversity within the data by discovering groups with similar characteristics.

It would be a significant direction for future work to consider non‐Gaussian distributions, such as multivariate t‐distributions and skewed distributions, to enhance the flexibility of the mvFMR. Such an extension would better capture the clusters and relationships in diverse data that adapt to real‐world complexities. Furthermore, our approach can be applied to analyze various datasets, including environmental data (e.g., air quality index, temperature change, noise level) and economic data (e.g., income levels, consumption patterns, unemployment rate). We would be able to enhance the generality and practical contributions of our approach by exploring potential applications in public policy formulation, economic trend analysis, urban planning, and resource management.

Choice of Step Size

We chose step sizes from a decreasing grid between 1 and 0.01, selecting for each setting the largest value such that, for 10 iterations, all parameters remained finite and the objective did not diverge.

For the EM‐PGM algorithm, we used a fixed step size η in each setting. Specifically, we set η=1 for independent designs and η=0.25 for correlated designs in all models except Models 5‐6, where a smaller η was required. In Model 5, we used (0.5, 0.05), and in Model 6, we used (0.5, 0.01) for the independent and correlated designs, respectively.

Similarly, for the EM‐ADMM algorithm, we chose the step size ρ=1 for both independent and correlated designs in all models except Model 6, where we used (0.75, 0.5) for the independent and correlated designs, respectively.
