Nasopharyngeal live leech infestation: an unusual cause of posterior bloody nasal discharge: two case reports
Authors: Mahboobe Asadi, Homeira Mansurzade, Fatemeh Jahanshahi, Kei Armin Ghavam, Amir Hossein Ehsani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Leech infestation is a rare but important cause of epistaxis and bloody posterior nasal discharge, particularly in individuals exposed to non-potable or infested water sources. Prompt diagnosis and removal are essential to prevent complications.Case presentation:We present two cases of nasopharyngeal leech infestation from endemic regions of eastern Iran. The first case involved a 42-year-old man from Khaaf, Razavi Khorasan Province, with intermittent epistaxis and bloody nasal discharge following river swimming. The second case involved a 35-year-old man with intellectual disability from Torbat-e Jam, Razavi Khorasan Province, who developed throat discomfort and bloody discharge after drinking non-potable water. In both cases, nasopharyngoscopy revealed a live leech attached to the mucosa and extending into the oropharynx. The leeches were successfully removed using Luc’s forceps under endoscopic visualization without anesthesia. Both patients underwent complete head, neck, and airway examinations, including fibrolaryngoscopy, confirming no additional infestations. They were prescribed oral ciprofloxacin and remained asymptomatic at follow-up.Discussion:Leech infestation, though uncommon, should be considered in patients presenting with unexplained epistaxis, particularly those with a history of water exposure in endemic regions. Aquatic leeches secrete anticoagulants that cause persistent bleeding. Diagnosis requires careful nasopharyngoscopic evaluation. Endoscopic-guided removal is safe and effective in an outpatient setting. Prophylactic antibiotics, especially those covering Aeromonas spp., are recommended. Socioeconomic conditions, including the use of unsafe water sources, increase the risk of infestation.Conclusion:Leech infestation should be included in the differential diagnosis of unexplained posterior epistaxis, particularly in endemic or low-sanitation areas. Early recognition and careful removal can prevent complications.

Introduction

Leeches are hematophagous parasites found predominantly in freshwater environments, especially in regions with poor sanitation. Human infestation typically occurs after swimming in or drinking from contaminated natural water sources[1]. Leeches attach to mucosal surfaces using two suckers: an anterior sucker for feeding and a posterior sucker for anchoring[2]. Their saliva contains anesthetic, vasodilator, and anticoagulant substances, such as hirudin, which facilitate painless attachment and prolonged bleeding[3].

Although medicinal leeches have been used for centuries and continue to play a role in modern reconstructive surgery, accidental infestation of the upper aerodigestive tract remains a rare but clinically significant condition[4]. Previous reports in otolaryngology have documented leech presence in the nasal cavity, nasopharynx, oropharynx, larynx, and trachea. Symptoms vary depending on the location but commonly include epistaxis, voice changes, foreign body sensation, or, in rare cases, airway obstruction[5].

In this report, we describe two cases of nasopharyngeal leech infestation, highlighting the clinical features, management approach, and key points for diagnosis and prevention. This study was reported in compliance with the SCARE 2025 criteria[6].

Case presentation

Case 1: A 42-year-old man from Khaaf, Razavi Khorasan Province, presented with a 5-day history of intermittent, spontaneous epistaxis and posterior bloody discharge. He reported recent river swimming but denied trauma or respiratory symptoms. Oropharyngeal examination revealed a mobile, dark object on the posterior pharyngeal wall, extending from the nasopharynx (Fig. 1A). Endoscopic examination revealed a dark, motile organism attached to the mucosa of the posterior pharyngeal wall in the nasopharyngeal region. Under direct vision and without anesthesia, the leech was gently grasped using Luc’s forceps, detached from its attachment site, and extracted transorally (Fig. 1B). The 7 × 2 cm leech was alive upon extraction. Post-removal fibrolaryngoscopy confirmed no additional parasites. The patient received oral ciprofloxacin and recovered uneventfully. Case 2: A 35-year-old man with intellectual disability and no significant past medical history from Torbat-e Jam, Razavi Khorasan Province was referred with a 1-week history of a foreign body sensation in the throat and bloody posterior nasal discharge. He had a history of drinking non-potable water prior to symptom onset. The patient also reported unsuccessful attempts to remove the object manually. Physical examination showed no fever, dysphagia, or respiratory distress. On oropharyngeal examination, a dark, motile organism was observed on the right posterior pharyngeal wall, extending from the nasopharynx (Fig. 2A). The leech was successfully removed using Luc’s forceps in an outpatient setting. The removed organism was identified as a cylindrical, black leech, which was still alive at the time of extraction (Fig. 2B). Fibrolaryngoscopy and a full physical examination ruled out additional infestations. As with the first case, the patient was prescribed oral ciprofloxacin. At follow-up 1 week later, the examination was unremarkable, and the patient reported complete resolution of symptoms. Discussion

Leech infestation of the upper aerodigestive tract represents a rare but noteworthy cause of epistaxis and pharyngeal bleeding. The rarity of this condition often results in misdiagnosis, particularly in rural and endemic regions. A clear understanding of the parasite’s biology, transmission pathways, and management strategies is critical for clinicians[7].

Leeches that infest humans are divided into land-based and aquatic groups. Land-based leeches have strong jaws for adhering to human skin, whereas aquatic leeches require soft tissue to facilitate attachment because of their weak jaws[7]. Aquatic leeches have been found in the upper aerodigestive tract, trachea, and, more recently, in the conjunctiva, vulva, vagina, urethra, and rectum[8]. Leeches attach to mucosal or cutaneous surfaces using two suckers: the anterior sucker for feeding and the posterior sucker for anchoring. During feeding, they release saliva rich in bioactive compounds such as hirudin (a potent thrombin inhibitor), histamine-like vasodilators, anesthetic peptides, and calin (a platelet aggregation inhibitor). These substances ensure prolonged bleeding and pain-free attachment, allowing the parasite to feed undetected for hours or days. As a result, patients frequently present with persistent or recurrent bleeding [9–11]. The estimated blood intake per feeding is small, but repeated or prolonged infestations can cause severe anemia, particularly in children or individuals with chronic illness[12].

The risk of infestation is closely linked to socioeconomic and environmental conditions. Individuals living in agricultural or mountainous areas, such as those in the Khorasan provinces of Iran, often use untreated river or spring water. These habitats provide an ideal environment for aquatic leeches. Cases have also been reported in Ethiopia, Uganda, Sudan, and Southeast Asia, emphasizing the global presence of this condition in low-resource, high-humidity regions[8,11,12]. The seasonal incidence appears higher during warmer months when water activities increase. Awareness campaigns and the provision of filtered or boiled water are essential public health interventions to reduce the incidence of such cases[12].

Symptoms vary according to the site of attachment. In nasopharyngeal infestations, patients often report posterior epistaxis, bloody saliva, throat discomfort, or a foreign body sensation. If the parasite extends into the oropharynx or larynx, it may cause hoarseness, dyspnea, or airway obstruction[5,7,9]. Since leeches secrete local anesthetics, pain is typically absent, making the diagnosis particularly challenging.

A high index of suspicion is vital, particularly in patients from endemic areas or those reporting recent water exposure. While anterior rhinoscopy can occasionally reveal the organism, nasopharyngoscopy and fibrolaryngoscopy remain the diagnostic gold standards. These modalities allow direct visualization, precise localization, and safe extraction. Imaging studies, such as computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, are rarely required but may assist in excluding differential diagnoses, such as vascular malformations or neoplasms[9,11,13].

Immediate and complete removal of the leech is essential. The preferred approach is endoscopic extraction using blunt-tipped or Luc’s forceps under direct visualization. Caution must be exercised to avoid squeezing or tearing the leech, as incomplete removal may lead to persistent bleeding or infection. Alternative detachment methods include irrigation with saline, lidocaine, hypertonic saline, vinegar, or topical anesthetic agents, which can induce spontaneous detachment[7,8]. Furthermore, airway reassessment through nasoendoscopy or fibrolaryngoscopy is critical to rule out multiple infestations. Since Aeromonas hydrophila is a known commensal organism in the leech digestive tract, antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended. Fluoroquinolones or combined therapy with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole are appropriate options[10,13].

Leeches have long been employed therapeutically – specifically medicinal leeches (Hirudo medicinalis) – in microsurgery and plastic reconstruction, where they assist in venous drainage of congested flaps. Leech therapy reduces tissue capillary pressure and enhances local anticoagulation through three primary mechanisms: active suction, the release of vasoactive substances such as hirudin, and the passive flow of blood from bite wounds following the leech’s removal. The distinction between therapeutic and parasitic leech exposure underscores the dual clinical importance of this organism. Although medicinal leech use is safe under controlled conditions, it can lead to Aeromonas infections, necessitating prophylactic antibiotics[4,14,15].

From a preventive standpoint, public health education and improved water sanitation remain the cornerstones of control. Local health authorities in endemic regions should emphasize safe water practices, including boiling or filtering drinking water. Integrating environmental hygiene programs with community-level awareness could substantially reduce infestation rates[12,13,16,17].

Conclusion

Leech infestation should be considered in patients with unexplained epistaxis or throat discomfort following water exposure. Early endoscopic evaluation, safe removal, and appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis are curative. Preventive strategies, including clean water access and public health education, remain vital to reducing the incidence.
