Association between body weight time in target range and risk of type 2 diabetes in adults with obesity
Authors: Soojin Park, Seohyun Kim, Sang Ho Park, Minkyeong Kim, You-Bin Lee, Sang-Man Jin, Kyu Yeon Hur, Jae Hyeon Kim, Gyuri Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a major risk factor for type 2 diabetes, underscoring the need for effective weight management strategies for its prevention and control. We evaluated the association between body weight time in target range (TTR) and the risk of type 2 diabetes in adults with obesity.MethodsUsing the 2010–2022 Korean National Health Insurance Service database, we included 248,367 adults with obesity with no history of diabetes. Body weight TTR was defined as the proportion of time maintaining a ≥ 3% reduction from baseline body weight. Participants were categorized into body weight TTR groups: 0%, > 0–50%, and > 50–100%. The primary outcome was the incidence of type 2 diabetes. Cox proportional hazards models were used.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 5.6 years, 46,737 participants (18.8%) developed type 2 diabetes. Individuals with body weight TTR > 0–50% and > 50–100% had significantly lower risk of type 2 diabetes compared with individuals with body weight TTR = 0%, with adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) of 0.86 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.84–0.88) and 0.66 (95% CI: 0.64–0.67), respectively. Each 10% increase in body weight TTR was significantly associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes (aHR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.95–0.96). Five-year absolute risks declined across body weight TTR groups: 12.82%, 11.10%, and 8.62%.ConclusionAmong adults with obesity, a higher body weight TTR was significantly associated with a reduced risk of developing type 2 diabetes, highlighting the importance of sustained weight management in diabetes prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02139-7.

Introduction

Obesity is a major public health concern worldwide and is strongly associated with the development of multiple chronic diseases, including type 2 diabetes [1–3]. Among individuals with obesity and complex multimorbidities, adult-onset diabetes is the most prevalent, affecting 75.4% of this population, followed by hypertension, sleep disorders, osteoarthritis, and arrhythmia [1]. Given this strong association, obesity management is a primary target for disease prevention and control [2].

Numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that effective obesity management, particularly through moderate physical activity and dietary improvements, can delay the progression of prediabetes to type 2 diabetes [4, 5]. Current clinical guidelines from the American Diabetes Association recommend weight management as a cornerstone strategy for preventing type 2 diabetes in individuals with overweight or obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 25 kg/m2), prioritizing lifestyle modifications over pharmacotherapy or metabolic surgery [2]. Even modest weight loss (3–10% of initial body weight) for ≥ 3 years has been associated with potential improvements in glycemic control and blood pressure in individuals with obesity who maintain a mean weight loss of approximately 10% [6, 7]. Consistent with these findings, the Look AHEAD study reported that intensive lifestyle intervention resulted in a mean weight loss of 4.7% over 8 years, accompanied by sustained improvements in metabolic parameters [8]. These results highlight the significant benefit of modest, sustained weight loss for long-term health management in individuals with obesity.

Recently, the concept of time in target range (TTR) has expanded beyond glycemic control and has been effectively applied to other health metrics, such as blood pressure, to assess stability within a target range [9]. However, research on its application to body weight remains limited. Building on this concept, a novel body weight management metric, body weight TTR, was introduced to assess the stability of weight loss over time [10]. Unlike traditional weight loss measures that are focused solely on short-term changes, body weight TTR enables the quantification of the proportion of time an individual maintains their weight within a predefined target range, offering a dynamic and comprehensive measure of sustained weight control to evaluate the long-term impact of weight stability on metabolic health [10].

Previous studies have shown that individuals with obesity and diabetes who maintain long-term weight loss experience a 16% and 19% reduced risk of cardiovascular and kidney diseases, respectively [10, 11]. However, despite the promising implications of body weight TTR for chronic disease management, no studies have been conducted to specifically examine the relationship between body weight TTR and the incidence of type 2 diabetes in individuals with obesity.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the association between body weight TTR and the risk of incident type 2 diabetes in adults with obesity, focusing on the maintenance of weight loss over time. We used ≥ 3% sustained weight loss as the primary threshold for defining body weight TTR, as this level of weight reduction has been associated with clinically relevant metabolic benefits in prior research [12, 13] and additionally evaluated alternative TTR thresholds based on higher guideline-recommended weight-loss targets (≥ 4–7%).

Methods

Data source

We used data from the 2010–2022 Korea National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database, which is derived from government-mandated insurance programs that cover the Korean population [14]. The NHIS dataset includes patient demographic and medical treatment data based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes, as well as prescription records. The health checkup database contains data from annual or biennial examinations, including anthropometric measurements, health-related behavior questionnaires (smoking history, alcohol consumption habits, and physical activity), and past and current medical histories and laboratory findings. A detailed explanation of the NHIS database has been published previously [15].

Study population

Figure S1 and Fig. 1 show the study design and participant selection flowchart, respectively. We included individuals who underwent a general health examination between January 2010 and December 2017 (N = 2,054,095), and excluded individuals who met any of the following criteria: [1] aged < 20 years (N = 2,859); [2] less than four examinations or with examination intervals < 1 year (N = 822,379); [3] BMI < 25 kg/m2 (N = 560,112); [4] missing data for a relevant covariate (age, sex, region, income status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, BMI, fasting plasma glucose [FPG], and estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]; N = 51,447); [5] a previous diagnosis of cancer (N = 269,423); or [6] a previous diagnosis of diabetes (ICD-10 E10 or E11–14), receiving prescribed oral anti-diabetic medication, or exhibiting an FPG level > 126 mg/dL before the last examination (N = 99,508). After applying these criteria, 248,367 individuals were included in the final analysis. Participants were followed from their last examination to the end date (December 31, 2022) and were censored at the date of death or last follow-up.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (approval no. SMC 2025-04-030) at Samsung Medical Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea. The requirement for written informed consent was waived because the study involved only a database analysis and did not use any personally identifiable information.

Measurements and definitions

The NHIS database provides baseline data on demographics (age, sex, income status, and region), lifestyle (smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), medical history (diagnoses and prescriptions), anthropometry (body weight, height and waist circumference), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and laboratory results (FPG, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, and creatinine).

Income status was defined as below or above the 20th percentile based on health insurance payments for all registered Koreans. Data on smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity were collected using self-administered questionnaires. We categorized smoking status as current, former, or never smoked, and alcohol consumption as non, moderate (< 30 and 20 g/day for men and women, respectively), or heavy drinker (≥ 30 and 20 g/day for men and women, respectively) [16]. Alcohol intake was calculated by converting the average alcoholic beverage consumption to grams of pure alcohol consumed per day.

Physical activity was classified as either high- or moderate-intensity exercise based on self-reported frequency (inducing extreme or significant shortness of breath; ≥20 min at least three times per week for high-intensity and ≥ 30 min at least five times per week for moderate-intensity). We calculated BMI by dividing weight in kilograms by height in meters squared, and the eGFR using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula [17]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, and hypertension as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, use of antihypertensive medication, or at least one medical claim with ICD-10 codes I10 or I11. Dyslipidemia was defined as at least one annual claim with ICD-10 code E78, use of prescribed lipid-lowering medications (statins, ezetimibe, fibrates, nicotinic acid, omega-3, and evolocumab), or a total cholesterol level ≥ 240 mg/dL. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated based on 14 predefined conditions [18].

Body weight time in target range definition

In accordance with the 2025 American Diabetes Association clinical guidelines on obesity and weight management for the prevention and treatment of type 2 diabetes, we defined body weight TTR as the proportion of time between 2010 and 2017 during which an individual maintained a ≥ 3% reduction in body weight from the baseline weight [2].

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, defined as either: [1] ICD-10 codes E11–14 with a prescription for anti-diabetic medication, or [2] FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL. The diagnosis date was defined as the earliest occurrence of either criterion between the index year and December 2022 or the end of the health examination period.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics are presented as numbers with percentages for categorical variables and as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Baseline characteristics were obtained from the initial health examination. We compared participant characteristics at the first examination using analysis of variance and Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. In the analysis of categorical body weight TTR, participants were classified into three groups: 0%, > 0–50%, and > 50–100%. We used K-means clustering for longitudinal data to estimate body weight trajectories, with the optimal number of clusters (k) determined using silhouette and elbow methods [19]. This analysis was performed to validate the alignment between body weight TTR categories and the longitudinal weight trajectories identified through k-means clustering.

We estimated cumulative event rates for type 2 diabetes using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and compared differences across groups using the log-rank test. Incidence rates (per 1,000 person-years) were calculated as the number of new cases divided by the total follow-up duration. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to estimate the association between body weight TTR and the risk of type 2 diabetes. We estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the 0% body weight TTR group as the reference. Model 1 was unadjusted; the multivariable model included age, sex, income status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, region, physical activity, BMI, FPG, eGFR, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CCI. We further assessed the continuous relationship between body weight TTR and type 2 diabetes risk using restricted cubic splines with three knots in multivariable-adjusted Cox regression models. Absolute risk differences were calculated to quantify effects more fully. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the association between the incidence of type 2 diabetes and each adjustment variable using stratified analyses, and the p-value for interaction was calculated.

For sensitivity analyses, we recalculated the body weight TTR as the proportion of the time participants maintained weight loss within 4–7% of their baseline body weight [2, 10]. Because glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was unavailable in the NHIS dataset, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis to account for participants’ usual glycemic status. Two measures were derived: the mean FPG and variability independent of the mean (VIM); the latter calculated by adjusting the SD for the mean using a power (χ) obtained from nonlinear regression analysis. These two measures were included as covariates in the Cox regression model. In addition, a sensitivity analysis of body weight TTR for incident type 2 diabetes was performed with further adjustment for waist circumference. We also censored cases of type 2 diabetes occurring within 1 year to evaluate the long-term effects of sustained weight loss in type 2 diabetes and to reduce the influence of early-onset cases, ensuring a more accurate estimation of the association.

Furthermore, overlap weighting was applied to reduce potential confounding and selection biases across the three body weight TTR groups, aiming to balance factors that might contribute to unintended weight regulation, such as severe chronic conditions or low income [20]. Overlap weighting was performed using a multinomial logistic regression model, with weights ranging from 0 to 1 based on baseline characteristics influencing group assignment and outcomes [21]. Predicted probabilities were estimated using a multinomial logistic regression model adjusted for covariates, including index year, age, sex, income status, region, alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity, BMI, and the CCI. Covariate balance was defined as a standardized mean difference of < 0.1. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1 for Windows (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and RStudio version 4.0.3.

Results

Characteristics of the participants categorized by body weight time in target range

Baseline characteristics stratified by body weight TTR (≥ 3% weight loss from baseline) are presented in Table 1. Overall, the mean age was 47.0 years; 63.7% were men; and the mean BMI was 27.2 kg/m2. The prevalence of CKD, hypertension, and dyslipidemia was 4.5%, 39.8%, and 30.8%, respectively. Participants with a higher body weight TTR (> 50–100%) were more likely to be older, to be non-smokers and non-drinkers, and to have a higher BMI and FPG, as well as higher rates of CKD, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or multiple comorbidities based on the CCI than those with a lower body weight TTR (≤ 50%). Figure S2 and Figure S3 show the percentage change in body weight and the weight trajectories across the three body weight TTR groups during the assessment period. Participants with a TTR of 50–100% most consistently achieved and maintained the target weight loss (3–7% below baseline). Consistently, trajectory analysis (Figure S3) identified five distinct weight change patterns (Trajectory groups 1–5). When we compared these trajectory-defined groups with the three predefined body-weight TTR groups, we found that most individuals in trajectory group 2 were classified as having body weight TTR > 0%, whereas about 60% of trajectory group 5 remained at body weight TTR = 0%. Overall, trajectory clustering identified five distinct weight-change patterns that aligned with the body weight TTR categories: higher body weight TTR reflected sustained weight-loss trajectories, while body weight TTR = 0% corresponded to weight regain or gain. This alignment was further supported by cross-tabulation analysis, yielding a Cramér’s V of 0.30 (p < 0.001), which indicates a moderate association between the two classification methods.

Association between body weight time in target range and incident type 2 diabetes

The median follow-up duration was 5.56 years. Of the 248,367 individuals with obesity, 46,737 (18.8%) developed type 2 diabetes. The cumulative incidence of type 2 diabetes was significantly lower in individuals who achieved a ≥ 3% weight loss from baseline (body weight TTR > 0–50% and > 50–100%) than in those who never reached the target weight loss range (body weight TTR = 0%) (Fig. 2; p < 0.001, log-rank test).

Table 2 shows the results of the multivariate Cox regression analysis of type 2 diabetes incidence. In the unadjusted model, the HRs for incident type 2 diabetes were 0.89 (95% CI: 0.87–0.91) for participants who maintained ≥ 3% weight loss for up to half of the time (body weight TTR > 0–50%) and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.71–0.74) for those who maintained it more consistently (body weight TTR > 50–100%), compared with the body weight TTR = 0% group. These associations remained significant after adjusting for age, sex, income status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, region, physical activity, BMI, FPG, eGFR, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CCI (adjusted HR [aHR]: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.84–0.88 for 0%< TTR ≤ 50%; aHR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.64–0.67 for 50%< TTR ≤ 100%).

Sensitivity analysis

To strengthen the robustness of our findings, we conducted an additional overlap-weighting analysis, which improved balance across groups (pairwise standardized mean difference < 0.1; Table S1). The overlap-weighted cohort yielded incidence rates and HR estimates similar to those of the main analysis (Table S2). Individuals in the 0%< TTR ≤ 50% group had a lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes (aHR 0.86, 95% CI 0.84–0.88) than those with a body weight TTR = 0%, with an even greater risk reduction observed in the 50%< TTR ≤ 100% group (aHR 0.66, 95% CI 0.64–0.67).

We also conducted a sensitivity analysis using guideline-recommended target weight-loss thresholds of ≥ 4–7% from baseline (Table S3). Across all thresholds, higher body weight TTR was consistently associated with a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes (Figure S4 and Table S4). Moreover, the inverse association became progressively stronger at higher weight-loss thresholds. The aHRs for the highest body weight TTR groups were 0.64, 0.62, 0.58, and 0.54 for TTR targets of 4%, 5%, 6%, and 7%, respectively.

Among individuals who achieved ≥ 3% reduction in baseline body weight, we conducted an additional analysis censoring cases of type 2 diabetes occurring within 1 year. The incidence of type 2 diabetes was highest in the body weight TTR = 0% group (31.7 cases per 1,000 person-years) and progressively decreased with increasing body weight TTR (Figure S5 and Table S4). The aHRs for type 2 diabetes incidence were 0.85 (95% CI: 0.83–0.87) and 0.67 (95% CI: 0.65–0.69) in the 0%< TTR ≤ 50% and 50%< TTR ≤ 100% groups, respectively, compared with those in the body weight TTR = 0% group (Table S4). Even after adjusting for mean FPG and the VIM of FPG, higher body weight TTR (≥ 3–7%) was significantly associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes (Table S5). In a separate model that additionally included waist circumference, the association was similar in both direction and magnitude (Table S6).

Discussion

In this large-scale, population-based, retrospective cohort study, we evaluated the incidence of type 2 diabetes based on long-term weight-loss maintenance, measured as body weight TTR, among individuals with obesity. We found a significant inverse association between body weight TTR and the risk of developing type 2 diabetes among this population, indicating that maintaining even modest weight loss was associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes. The relationship remained robust when body weight TTR was analyzed as a continuous variable (0–100%), with each 10% increase associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes. Sensitivity analysis reinforced these findings, showing that greater body weight reduction from baseline and sustained maintenance were consistently associated with a lower type 2 diabetes risk.

Previous cohort studies have highlighted the critical role of weight management and variability in type 2 diabetes risk [22, 23]. Supporting our results, Oh et al., who reported on community-dwelling populations and examined the relationship between weight fluctuations and type 2 diabetes risk, found that individuals with a baseline BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 experiencing weight variability had a 32% lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes (aHR 0.68, 95% CI = 0.54–0.86) [22]. In contrast, a Finnish cohort study reported that intentional weight loss over a period of 11–15 years was associated with a 1.63-fold increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes [23]. These inconsistent body weight-related findings may reflect the influence of weight regain, which often results from homeostatic feedback mechanisms [24, 25]. Weight regain may therefore increase the risk of type 2 diabetes through the accumulation of visceral adiposity, which is closely linked to insulin resistance [26, 27].

Consistent with this interpretation, a higher body weight TTR, indicating a greater proportion of assessments conducted in a weight-reduced state, was significantly associated with a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes. An RCT reported that participants who maintained their weight loss for 1 year had a 65% lower cumulative incidence of type 2 diabetes than did controls (HR = 0.35; 95% CI = 0.22–0.56), with even greater reductions observed among individuals who achieved ≥ 5% weight loss [4]. Similarly, previous studies have shown that participants who lost ≥ 5–7% of their body weight through lifestyle modifications, including daily physical activity and dietary control, had a reduced risk of developing type 2 diabetes, with incidence reductions up to 80% [28]. In our study, maintaining ≥ 3% weight reduction was significantly associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes, and sensitivity analyses using higher thresholds of 4–7% showed progressively lower aHRs. These findings indicate that, although greater weight-loss maintenance is linked to the lowest risk, a modest 3% weight-loss target may still be a practical and achievable goal in this population and is associated with more favorable metabolic profiles and a lower risk of type 2 diabetes.

Several biological mechanisms may underlie the protective effect of maintaining a modest 3% weight loss on the risk of type 2 diabetes. Even modest weight reduction has been associated with improvements in insulin sensitivity, contributing to better glycemic stability, in part through reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines and favorable modulation of adipokines such as leptin and adiponectin [4, 29, 30]. In individuals with obesity, adipose tissue dysfunction, characterized by adipocyte hypertrophy and chronic low-grade inflammation, contributes to insulin resistance and β-cell impairment [12, 31, 32]. Weight reduction may mitigate these processes by reducing inflammatory signaling, as supported by reductions in leptin and systemic inflammatory markers [30, 33]. Beyond these initial effects, sustained weight loss confers more durable metabolic benefits through adaptive remodeling of adipose tissue, including reduced adipocyte size, improved angiogenesis, and decreased inflammatory immune cell infiltration [12, 32]. These changes collectively enhance insulin sensitivity and metabolic flexibility and appear to be driven more by reductions in fat mass and preservation or gain of lean mass than by changes in total body weight alone [34]. While beneficial effects begin during active weight loss, maintaining this loss is critical for preserving and extending these adaptations over time, potentially through extracellular matrix remodeling and epigenetic regulation of insulin signaling and inflammatory pathways [12, 13, 34].

In our subgroup analysis, higher body weight TTR was consistently associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes across the population, with significant interactions observed for smoking status and baseline BMI. The protective association appeared stronger among non-current smokers, suggesting that sustained weight stability may yield greater metabolic benefits in individuals without ongoing exposure to tobacco-related inflammation and insulin resistance [35]. We also observed that the inverse association was more pronounced in individuals with higher BMI, suggesting that sustained weight stability may be particularly relevant in those with a greater adiposity burden. Moreover, the results were similar after additional adjustment for waist circumference, suggesting that the association appears to be largely independent of central adiposity. Consistent with our findings, meta-analyses also show that smoking, alcohol consumption, and metabolic disorders, including high non-HDL-C, low HDL-C, and hypertension, are significant risk factors for type 2 diabetes, particularly among individuals with obesity [36]. Further research is needed to clarify how body weight TTR interacts with biomarkers and long-term metabolic outcomes, and to determine whether fat-mass reduction, rather than total body weight loss, drives the observed benefits. This underscores the importance of incorporating body composition or visceral adiposity measurements in future studies.

This study offers several strengths. First, body weight TTR was calculated using objectively measured rather than self-reported data, thereby minimizing potential recall bias and enhancing accuracy. Second, we demonstrated that a relatively modest 3% weight-loss threshold, lower than the conventional clinical target, is significantly associated with a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes. Extending prior body weight TTR studies that primarily focused on cardiovascular or kidney outcomes [10, 11], our findings highlight sustained weight loss maintenance as a metabolic risk modifier for incident type 2 diabetes, showing that even a modest 3–7% weight loss is associated with meaningful risk reduction. Beyond its research implications, the body weight TTR metric may have potential applications in clinical practice and digital healthcare. From a clinical standpoint, our findings suggest that achieving and maintaining ≥ 3% weight loss for more than half of the observation period (body weight TTR > 50%) may serve as a preliminary benchmark for long-term weight control. In our cohort, this group had an approximately one-third lower risk of type 2 diabetes than those who never reached the target range, supporting ≥ 3% weight loss maintained for > 50% of the time as a practical, potentially meaningful threshold. Body weight TTR can also be operationalized in routine care and digital health platforms by automatically converting serial body weight records into TTR values [37, 38]. Individuals who fail to achieve ≥ 3% weight loss or maintain < 50% TTR could be flagged for additional counseling or intensified weight-management support, recognizing that these empirically derived thresholds require confirmation in future studies. Finally, our analysis was conducted in a large population-based cohort with a long-term follow-up, and the consistency of findings across multiple sensitivity analyses, including different body weight TTR thresholds of 4–7% and overlap-weighted models, further strengthens the internal validity and robustness of our results.

This study also has some limitations. First, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded, particularly regarding unmeasured behavioral and dietary patterns or anti-obesity medication use. Information on weight-loss intentionality was unavailable; therefore, individuals with cancer or a short health examination period (< 1 year) were excluded, and overlap weighting was applied to reduce bias from socioeconomic factors and comorbidities. Nevertheless, unmeasured confounding may still influence the observed associations. Second, the absence of HbA1c and body composition data limited our ability to examine metabolic mechanisms or stratify diabetes risk by adiposity or glycemic burden. Sensitivity analysis adjusting for mean FPG and its variability yielded consistent results. We also calculated an E-value of 1.66, indicating that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both body weight TTR and incident type 2 diabetes by a risk ratio of at least 1.66 to fully explain the observed association. This provides context for the approximate magnitude of unmeasured confounding that would be required. Third, the generalizability of our findings may be limited because the cohort consisted exclusively of Korean adults classified as obese using the Asian-specific BMI threshold (≥ 25 kg/m²). Therefore, applicability to other ethnic groups or BMI classifications may be limited. Finally, although defining weight change relative to baseline body weight raises concerns about regression to the mean, consistent body weight TTR distributions across k-means trajectory groups support the robustness of our findings. Future studies incorporating diverse populations, region-appropriate BMI classifications, as well as detailed body composition and glycemic biomarkers, are needed to strengthen causal inference and clinical applicability.

Conclusions

In this study, a higher body weight TTR, defined using an empirically derived ≥ 3% weight-loss cut point, was associated with a lower risk of incident type 2 diabetes among individuals with obesity. These findings highlight the potential importance of long-term weight stability beyond short-term weight loss. Given the challenges of calculating body weight TTR in real-world settings with limited repeated measurements, our results underscore the need for continued support to help individuals maintain a stable body weight over time.
