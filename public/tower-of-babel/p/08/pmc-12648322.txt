Unveiling Atomic-Level Synergy of Pt3Fe Alloy Nanoparticles and Fe Single Atoms for Enhanced Electrochemical Stability
Authors: Wen Huang, Jiabao Nie, Zhiying Zheng, Xiaotian Wang, Chunyu Qiu, Xin Li, Zhiyou Zhou, Zhiyao Duan, Yucheng Wang, Shigang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The hybridization of single atom sites (SASs) and nanoparticles (NPs) demonstrates enhanced stability under acidic electrochemical environments when compared to their individual components; however, the underlying synergistic mechanisms remain elusive. Here we synthesize a hybrid catalyst featuring electron transfer from Pt3Fe NPs to high-spin D1-type FeN4 SASs. The online inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry technique confirms a mutual suppression of electrochemical dissolution between Pt3Fe and D1-FeN4. In situ spectroscopy and theoretical calculations elucidate the mechanisms at play: electron enrichment at the D1-FeN4 site strengthens the Fe–N bond, thereby elevating the N-hydrogenation energy barrier. Conversely, electron withdrawal reduces the d-band center of Pt, consequently weakening the oxygen adsorption strength and inhibiting the formation of Pt oxides. Owing to mutual dissolution inhibition, the hybrid catalyst retains 99.6% of its oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) mass activity at 0.85 V (versus RHE) following 30,000 accelerated stress test cycles between 0.6 and 1.0 V in an inert atmosphere, markedly surpassing those of single-component counterparts. This work offers critical insights into the intricate synergistic interactions between SASs and NPs, paving the way for the rational design of SASs–NPs hybrid catalysts for ORR or beyond.

Introduction

Carbon-supported single-atom catalysts (SACs) show great potential in heterogeneous catalysis due to their maximized atom utilization efficiency and unique electronic structures. − Among their applications, electrocatalytic conversion reactions, such as oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), , oxygen evolution reaction (OER), , and CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), , have attracted particular attention, driven largely by the urgency of global energy shortages. However, SACs exhibit poor stability under electrochemical conditions, especially in acidic electrolytes, severely limiting their practical application. − Recent studies have shown that introducing low-loading metal nanoparticles (NPs) into SACs can stabilize the otherwise unstable single-atom sites (SASs) through long-range interactions, such as electronic interactions or site relay. − Interestingly, these NPs also become more stable due to their interaction with the SASs. , Moreover, the incorporation of a small quantity of NPs does not significantly compromise the overall metal utilization efficiency or economic feasibility, nor does it sacrifice catalytic activity. In many cases, the hybrid catalysts even outperform their single-component counterparts. − Owing to these advantages in both cost and performance, SASs–NPs hybrid catalytic systems have garnered substantial research interest.

A typical example arises in the ORR applications for proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs). Carbon-supported Fe SACs featuring FeN4 active sites are regarded as the most promising candidates to replace noble Pt. − While persistent optimization has achieved Pt-comparable catalytic activity and selectivity in Fe SACs, their operational stability remains limited to the hundred-hour levelsubstantially lower than conventional Pt catalysts. − Recent advances reveal that incorporating ultralow-loading Pt NPs (<5 wt %) can effectively stabilize the otherwise unstable Fe SASs. , Conversely, this interaction also appears to enhance the stability of the Pt NPs. , Although this hybrid catalyst system exhibits highly attractive stability, there is still a gap before practical application can be realized. To bridge this gap, a primary task is to elucidate the bidirectional synergistic mechanism behind the enhanced stability. Understanding this mechanism is critical for rationally designing highly stable hybrid catalysts and accelerating their commercialization.

One previously proposed explanation is that Pt NPs mitigate the degradation of FeN4 sites by scavenging reactive oxygen species, such as hydrogen peroxide and free radicals. , However, this mechanism primarily describes a unidirectional protective effect and does not fully account for why the hybrid catalyst exhibits enhanced durability compared to both individual components. This discrepancy suggests the existence of additional synergistic stabilization mechanisms. Recent studies have provided new insights into this phenomenon. Shao et al. reported that FeN4 sites can suppress the electrochemical dissolution of Pt NPs. Conversely, our previous work demonstrated that Pt or Au NPs substantially inhibit the electrochemical dissolution of FeN4 sites. Together, these findings suggest a bidirectional dissolution inhibition phenomenon between Pt-based NPs and FeN4 sites, offering a more comprehensive framework for understanding the hybrid catalyst stability. However, this phenomenon has not yet been directly confirmed within a single catalyst system, and the underlying microscopic mechanisms are yet to be elucidated. A critical open question is how electronic interactions between Pt-based NPs and FeN4 sites modulate the elementary steps of the electrochemical dissolution pathway and ultimately suppress metal dissolution. While theoretical calculations have provided some valuable insights, experimental validationparticularly through in situ spectroscopic studies of dynamic dissolution processesis urgently needed.

In this study, we synthesized a hybrid catalyst primarily composed of FeN4 sites and Pt3Fe NPs. In this catalyst, the mass fractions of Pt and Fe are 1.40 and 0.89 wt %, respectively. Approximately 20.6% of the Fe is present in the form of Pt3Fe, while around 74.5% exists as FeN4 sites. A combination of Fe K-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS), Fe L-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES), and Mössbauer spectroscopy confirmed that the predominant form of FeN4 sites is the D1 state, accounting for 81.5%. Online inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) confirmed a mutual suppression of electrochemical dissolution between Pt3Fe and D1-FeN4. In situ Fe K-edge XANES revealed that the introduction of Pt3Fe accelerates the axial ligand detachment of the D1-FeN4 sites. Additionally, in situ Raman spectroscopy showed that the presence of D1-FeN4 sites inhibits the formation of Pt oxides. Further theoretical calculations proposed the underlying mechanisms: electron enrichment at the D1-FeN4 site lowers the oxidation state of Fe, which strengthens the Fe–N bond via enhanced Fe–N d–π* back-donation, while simultaneously weakening the Fe–O bond by reducing the ionic interaction between Fe and OH. These electronic effects facilitate the detachment of OH ligands but increase the energy barrier for the subsequent N-hydrogenation step, which is the rate-determining step (RDS) in the FeN4 demetalation pathway, ultimately suppressing electrochemical Fe dissolution. Conversely, electron withdrawal from Pt lowers its d-band center, weakening the oxygen adsorption strength, thereby inhibiting Pt oxide formation and suppressing Pt dissolution. Owing to mutual dissolution inhibition, the hybrid catalyst shows a mere 0.4% decay in mass activity at 0.85 VRHE following 30,000 accelerated stress test cycles between 0.6 and 1.0 V in an inert atmosphere, markedly surpassing those of single-component counterparts. Remarkably, the Pt3Fe-FeNC catalyst achieved a peak power density of 200 mW cm–2 and operated for 1000 h with 41% of activity decay in a direct methanol fuel cell (DMFC), highlighting its potential for fuel cell applications.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of Pt3Fe-FeNC

The hybrid Pt3Fe-FeNC catalyst was fabricated via a pyrolysis-driven synthetic protocol. In brief, a Pt precursor was uniformly dispersed with an FeNC substrate in ethylene glycol (EG), followed by thermal reduction at 160 °C for 90 min to facilitate nucleation. Subsequent high-temperature pyrolysis at 900 °C under flowing Ar­(g) for 1 h formed the alloy phases. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) observed the coexistence of NPs and Fe SASs (Figure a). HAADF-STEM further measured a lattice spacing of 2.23 Å (Figure b), consistent with the (111) facet of the Pt3Fe (JCPDS 29-0716). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) confirmed a uniform particle dispersion with an average size of 4.0 ± 1.0 nm (Figure c). X-ray diffraction (XRD) corroborated the Pt3Fe crystalline phase, with distinct peaks matching the JCPDS reference (Figure d, blue curve). In contrast, the pristine FeNC control sample exhibited no discernible diffraction signals corresponding to metallic aggregates (Figure d, gray curve), indicating that the Fe sites are possibly predominantly in an atomically dispersed state. HAADF-STEM also observed the atomically isolated Fe sites in the pristine FeNC (Figure S1). Given that the active site structure of the Fe SASs has been unequivocally determined to be FeN4, the term FeN4 will be employed in subsequent discussions to denote the SASs.

The Pt3Fe-NC control catalyst was synthesized by depositing liquid-phase-synthesized Pt3Fe onto an NC substrate, followed by pyrolysis at 350 °C under a H2/Ar (5:95 in volume) atmosphere for 1 h. The low-temperature selection at 350 °C is aimed at achieving a particle size consistent with the target sample. The H2/Ar mixed gases used subsequently are all in accordance with this volume ratio. The inclusion of H2 facilitated low-temperature surface cleaning, , enhancing catalytic activity (Figure S2). HRTEM revealed uniform particle dispersion with an average diameter of 4.1 ± 1.2 nm (Figure S3), closely matching the Pt3Fe-FeNC. The Pt3Fe crystalline phase was corroborated by XRD (Figure d, orange curve) and HRTEM imaging (Figure S4). Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) quantified Pt and Fe loadings in Pt3Fe-NC as 1.46 and 0.14 wt %, respectively (Table S1), yielding a Pt:Fe atomic ratio of 2.98:1, matching the nominal Pt3Fe stoichiometry. For comparison, the Pt3Fe-FeNC hybrid catalyst contained 1.40 wt % Pt and 0.89 wt % Fe, while the pristine FeNC alone registered 0.93 wt % Fe. Assuming an identical Pt:Fe atomic ratio (2.98:1) within the alloy phase of Pt3Fe-FeNC, ∼15.1% of the total Fe content resides in the Pt3Fe, with the remaining 84.9% distributed as on the NC substrate.

The electronic structure of FeN4 sites was investigated before and after the incorporation of the Pt3Fe. Fe K-edge XANES analysis revealed that the absorption edge of FeNC is positioned between those of Fe3O4 and Fe2O3 (Figure a), indicating that the average oxidation state of Fe is close to +3. Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) spectra exhibited a primary peak at 1.54 Å, corresponding to Fe–N­(O) coordination, supporting that Fe is predominantly present as atomically dispersed FeN4 sites (Figure b). Near-trivalent FeN4 sites typically incorporate an axial OH ligand to balance the excess charge, as supported by an average coordination number of approximately 4.8 obtained from FT-EXAFS fitting analysis (Figure S5 and Table S2). Collectively, these findings reveal that OH-Fe3+N4 represents the dominant Fe species in the FeNC catalyst. Such a site was further identified as D1 state by Mössbauer spectroscopy later. It should be noted that Fe3+ represents only an estimated oxidation state of Fe, not a precise assignment.

Upon the incorporation of Pt3Fe, the Fe K-edge XANES spectra of Pt3Fe-FeNC exhibited a negative shift in the absorption edge relative to FeNC, suggesting that the Pt3Fe alloy reduces the oxidation state of the D1-FeN4 sites. This observation aligns with previous studies, as the electron-rich Pt3Fe alloys can donate electrons to FeN4 sites through the carbon support. However, another possible contribution to the observed decrease in Fe oxidation state is the averaging effect of zerovalent Fe present in Pt3Fe. Further experimental validation was provided to exclude this possibility later. Additionally, FT-EXAFS analysis revealed a negative shift in the Fe–N­(O) coordination peak, revealing a contraction of the Fe–N­(O) bond upon Pt3Fe incorporation. This suggests the strengthening of Fe–N bonds. A distinct peak at 2.60 Å in Pt3Fe-FeNC was assigned to Fe–Pt interactions.

The Fe L3-edge XANES spectra revealed a main peak at 710.5 eV for both FeNC and Pt3Fe-FeNC, which is assigned as near-trivalent D1-FeN4 sites (Figure c). The Fe L2-edge XANES spectra observed that the intensities of the two peaks at 722.3 and 723.7 eV for FeNC are nearly identical, whereas for Pt3Fe-FeNC, the peak at the lower energy exhibits higher intensity (Figure d). This suggests a partial reduction in the Fe oxidation state. Notably, no notable signal corresponding to zerovalent Fe from the Pt3Fe alloy was detected in the Fe L-edge XANES spectra (typically at 706.5–707.5 eV in the L3-edge), potentially due to its low detectable concentration at the alloy surfaces. This effectively eliminates the interference of Pt3Fe on the judgment of the oxidation state of D1-FeN4 sites. These findings provide evidence that the oxidation state of the D1-FeN4 sites is reduced upon the incorporation of the Pt3Fe.

Mössbauer spectroscopy was utilized to differentiate the Fe species in the two catalysts, with the fitting parameters detailed in Table S3. In the pristine FeNC catalyst, the D1 state (IS = 0.29 mm s–1, QS = 1.04 mm s–1) constituted 65.9%, while the D3 state (IS = 1.13 mm s–1, QS = 2.39 mm s–1) accounted for 25.1% (Figure e). The remaining 9% of Fe was present as oxides. The D1 state has been previously assigned as the OH-Fe3+N4 site with a high-spin S = 5/2 state, while the D3 state is described as the Fe2+N4(S = 2). , Following the incorporation of Pt3Fe, the proportions of the D1, D3, and Fe oxides were reduced to 60.7%, 13.8%, and 4.9%, respectively (Figure f). This decrease can be attributed to the extraction of a portion of the Fe to alloy with Pt, which represents approximately 20.6% of the total Fe content (consistent with ICP-OES quantitative results). Interestingly, the QS of the D1 state in Pt3Fe-FeNC exhibited a slight decrease compared to that in FeNC. Direct measurement of the QS from the main peak in the raw spectra revealed a reduction from 0.94 to 0.84 mm s–1, confirming that the observed change is not an artifact of spectral fitting. The decreased QS is likely attributed to perturbations in the d-orbital electronic configuration induced by a decreased oxidation state of the D1 species, although these perturbations do not affect the spin multiplicity. The D1-FeN4 sites constitute over 60% of the total Fe content in both catalysts, which accounts for the dominance of the D1 characteristics in the Fe K-edge and L-edge spectra. This predominance enables a clear determination of changes in the electronic properties of the D1-FeN4 sites.

Notably the number of Pt3Fe nanoparticles should be much smaller than that of D1-FeN4 sites, which suggests that only a minor fraction of single-atom sites reside in close proximity to Pt3Fe nanoparticles, while the majority are located farther away. So, how can the electronic structure of most D1-FeN4 sites still exhibit modulation by distant nanoparticles? This may arise from long-range electronic interactions between the metallic nanoparticles and single-atom sites mediated by the carbon support. Such long-range electronic interactions have been discovered and confirmed early on and were previously applied to regulate the nucleation and growth density of nanoparticles on graphenes. −

The modulation of D1-FeN4 sites on the electronic structure of Pt3Fe was investigated. Pt L3-edge XANES showed a higher white line peak for Pt3Fe-FeNC than for Pt3Fe-NC (Figure a), revealing an increased Pt oxidation state. FT-EXAFS indicated that Pt3Fe-FeNC has a shorter Pt–Fe­(Pt) bond length compared to that of Pt3Fe-NC (Figure b). High-resolution Pt 4f X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) revealed that Pt3Fe-FeNC has a higher proportion of Pt2+ components, corresponding to an increase in the average oxidation state of Pt (Figures c and d). Specifically, the proportion of Pt2+ in Pt3Fe-FeNC increased to 38.3% compared to 33.2% in Pt3Fe-NC, with detailed parameters summarized in Supplementary Table S4. These electronic and structural modulations of Pt3Fe nanoparticles are primarily attributed to the existence of the D1-FeN4 site due to their predominant population in the Pt3Fe-FeNC catalyst.

Electrochemical Dissolution Behaviors

The online ICP-MS technique was employed to monitor the Fe dissolution of the two catalysts in aqueous electrode tests. The iron dissolution rate exhibited a rapid decline over the course of the testing. To address this influencing factor, a potential step protocol, with each potential held for 30 s, was implemented to facilitate a more precise assessment of the dissolution rate at each electrode potential. Following each adjustment of the potential, the electrode was refreshed. The dissolution measurements at each potential were replicated three times to ensure reproducibility and accuracy. The Fe dissolution profiles at different potentials in an acidic electrolyte are shown in Figure a. The total Fe dissolution amount at each potential step was integrated, as shown in Figure b. Fe dissolution initiates at potentials below 0.8 VRHE. Notably, Pt3Fe-FeNC exhibits a marked reduction in dissolution compared to FeNC. For instance, at 0.4 VRHE, the Fe dissolution amounts for FeNC and Pt3Fe-FeNC were measured at 10.6% and 3.2%, respectively, reflecting a 70% decrease. Given that approximately 20.6% of Fe in Pt3Fe-FeNC is distributed within the alloy and assuming this portion is entirely insoluble, the adjusted dissolution amount for Pt3Fe-FeNC is calculated to be 4.1%, still representing a substantial 61.3% reduction (Figure b, dotted line). Moreover, the inhibitory effect of Pt3Fe on the dissolution of D1-FeN4 was further validated in gas diffusion electrode (GDE) configurations (Figure S6). Given that D1-FeN4 exhibits the highest susceptibility to dissolution and represents the predominant species, the dissolved entities detected in the experiments should primarily originate from the D1 state. Consequently, these results provide compelling evidence that the integration of Pt3Fe significantly suppresses the dissolution of the D1-FeN4 sites.

To determine whether the treatment during synthesis alone could substantially reduce Fe dissolution, a control sample (FeNC-2) was prepared following the same protocol as that for Pt3Fe-FeNC but without adding Pt precursors. The FeNC-2 sample exhibited Fe dissolution levels comparable to those of pristine FeNC, both of which were markedly higher than that of Pt3Fe-FeNC (Figure S7).

In situ XANES was employed to monitor the structural evolution of the D1-FeN4 sites during Fe dissolution. As the potential decreased from 0.9 to 0 VRHE, the Fe K-edge XANES spectra of all three catalysts exhibited a gradual shift to lower energies (Figures c–e). Quantitative analysis, based on the energy at half of the normalized peak intensity, revealed shifts of 3.2 eV for FeNC, 2.5 eV for Pt3Fe-FeNC, and 1.1 eV for Pt3Fe-NC (Figure f). These negative energy shifts correspond to a reduction in the Fe oxidation state. The more pronounced reduction observed for FeNC and Pt3Fe-FeNC is consistent with the Fe2+/Fe3+ redox due to the reductive desorption of an axial OH ligand on D1-FeN4 sites, consistent with previous studies. This assignment was also supported by the observation of a redox peak near 0.7 VRHE in the CV measurements (Figure S8). In contrast, the smaller shift observed for Pt3Fe-NC, along with its nearly linear dependence on potential, suggests a minor decrease in the oxidation state driven by the electrode potential, without significant structural alterations. At all potentials, the Fe oxidation state in Pt3Fe-FeNC remains lower than that in FeNC, corroborating ex situ XANES data and further confirming that alloy incorporation reduces the oxidation state of the D1-FeN4 sites. By 0.4 VRHE, Pt3Fe-FeNC completes the ligand dissociation process, whereas FeNC still undergoes progressive dissociation until it reaches nearly 0 VRHE. These findings demonstrate that Pt3Fe alloy incorporation accelerates OH ligand dissociation of D1-FeN4 sites.

Online ICP-MS was further employed to probe the influence of FeN4 sites on the dissolution behavior of Pt in Pt3Fe. To ensure uniformity in particle size and crystallinity between Pt3Fe-NC and Pt3Fe-FeNC in prior electrochemical performance evaluation, Pt3Fe-NC was synthesized under a H2/Ar atmosphere at 350 °C, while Pt3Fe-FeNC was prepared under an Ar atmosphere at 900 °C. Given that temperature and atmosphere can modulate particle–support interactions, potentially altering the Pt dissolution rates, two additional Pt3Fe-NC control samples were synthesized: Pt3Fe-NC (Ar-900) and Pt3Fe-NC (H2/Ar-900). The Pt content across all catalysts was tightly controlled, ranging from 1.40 to 1.50 wt % (Table S1). Under H2/Ar conditions, increasing the temperature led to larger alloy particle sizes, as evidenced by the sharper XRD diffraction peaks (Figure a and Figure S9). At 900 °C, the particle size obtained under H2/Ar was smaller than that under Ar, likely due to H2-mediated suppression of particle agglomeration during pyrolysis, consistent with previous studies. The Pt dissolution rates for the four samples followed the order Pt3Fe-NC (H2/Ar-350) (20.8 ng cm–2) > Pt3Fe-NC (Ar-900) (16.3 ng cm–2) ≈ Pt3Fe-NC (H2/Ar-900) (15.5 ng cm–2) > Pt3Fe-FeNC (Ar-900) (9.4 ng cm–2), as shown in Figure b. This trend underscores that both an elevated temperature and the incorporation of FeN4 sites are critical in mitigating Pt dissolution. Specifically, increasing the temperature reduced the level of Pt dissolution by 5.3 ng cm–2, while the introduction of FeN4 sites contributed to a further reduction of 6.9 ng cm–2. This result suggests that the effect of FeN4 incorporation is slightly more pronounced than that of the temperature. Normalization of Pt dissolution rates to the catalyst mass corroborated this trend (Figure S10).

Electrochemical in situ shell-isolated nanoparticle-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SHINERS) was utilized to probe the oxidation dynamics of Pt during electrochemical dissolution. Three catalysts, Pt3Fe-FeNC (Ar-900), Pt3Fe-NC (H2/Ar-350), and Pt3Fe-NC (Ar-900), were examined. Spectra were acquired over a potential range of 0.8 to 1.5 VRHE. Within the 400 to 1800 cm–1 range, four distinct Raman vibrational peaks were identified, corresponding to Pt–O at 564 cm–1, ClO4 –1 at 935 cm–1, the D band of graphite at 1347 cm–1, and the G band of graphite at 1605 cm–1 (Figures c–e). The potential-dependent evolution of the Pt–O vibrational peak area in the three catalysts is shown in Figure f. Notably, the formation potential of Pt–O bonds for Pt3Fe-NC (H2/Ar-350) and Pt3Fe-NC (Ar-900) was 1.2 VRHE, lower than that of Pt3Fe-FeNC (Ar-900) at 1.3 VRHE. These findings support our primary conclusion that the incorporation of FeN4 elevates the energy barrier for Pt oxide formationa critical factor in mitigating Pt dissolution. At higher potentials, the Pt–O Raman peak areas for the three samples exhibit trends consistent with their dissolution rates, though we caution against overinterpretation given the inherent limitations in quantitatively comparing Raman peak intensities.

Due to the detection limitations of ICP-MS, no dissolution signals were detectable within the 0–1.0 V range under online monitoring mode (Figure S11). To investigate whether the dissolution of Pt is also suppressed at lower potentials, we conducted cumulative electrochemical dissolution experiments. In an Ar atmosphere, we performed an accelerated stress test (AST) between 0.6 and 1.0 VRHE and quantified the amount of Pt dissolved in the electrolyte after 300, 600, and 1000 AST cycles. The results indicated that the dissolution amount of Pt3Fe-FeNC (Ar-900) was notably lower than that of Pt3Fe-NC (Ar-900) and Pt3Fe-NC (H2/Ar-350) (Figure S12). This trend is consistent with that observed in the potential region above 1.0 VRHE. The results show that the synergistic effect works in both the high potential region (>1.0 V) and the low potential region (0.6–1.0 V). Given that Pt dissolution is typically associated with the formation of Pt oxides, , the observed reduction in Pt dissolution also suggests a lower degree of Pt oxidation. The oxidation and dissolution of Pt are primary contributors to the instability of Pt-based catalysts, and the incorporation of D1-FeN4 effectively mitigates this process. This finding provides a compelling explanation for the enhanced stability of Pt observed in our experiments

Theoretical Insights

Theoretical calculations were performed to understand the mutual suppression of dissolution between D1-FeN4 and Pt3Fe. The structural models of the active sites for the three catalysts are depicted in Figure a. The FeNC catalyst adopts a configuration of Fe coordinating with four pyrrolic N atoms and an axial OH ligand, which is commonly identified as the D1 state in previous studies. The calculated partial density of states (pDOS) of the Fe 3d orbitals confirms a high-spin S = 5/2 state for the D1-FeN4 site (Figure S13a), in agreement with experimental Mössbauer spectroscopy observations. For Pt3Fe-FeNC, the structural model was constructed by placing a 27-atom Pt3Fe alloy particle adjacent to the D1-FeN4 moiety, with the nanoparticle’s closest edge positioned 2.7 Å from the Fe atom in FeN4. The Pt3Fe-NC was modeled by removing the Fe SAS from the Pt3Fe-FeNC.

(d) Free energy profiles for D1-FeN4 site dissolution at U = 0 VSHE in Pt3Fe-FeNC versus FeNC. (e) pDOS and Pt d-band center positions for Pt3Fe-FeNC and Pt3Fe-NC. (f) Comparative oxygen adsorption energies on Pt sites. (g) Potential-dependent free energy diagrams for Pt oxidative dissolution pathways at U = 0 VSHE.

Upon introduction of the Pt3Fe alloy, the number of occupied electrons of Fe in the D1-FeN4, calculated by integrating the occupied DOS of Fe, increased from 5.81 to 5.83 e (Table S5), consistent with the experimentally observed reduction in Fe oxidation state. Concurrently, slight modifications in the pDOS of Fe 3d orbitals (Figure S13b) likely correspond to the minor decrease in the QS of the D1 state observed in Mössbauer spectroscopy. Conversely, the introduction of FeN4 sites led to a slight decrease in the average number of occupied electrons of Pt atoms in Pt3Fe, aligning with the experimentally observed increase in the Pt oxidation state. These consistent trends validate the rationality of our constructed models.

The negative projected crystal orbital Hamilton population (−pCOHP) was employed to quantitatively assess the bond strength of Fe–N and Fe–O (Figures b and 6c). Upon the incorporation of the Pt3Fe alloy, the integrated −pCOHP (−ICOHP) for the Fe–N bond increased from 3.00 to 3.07, indicating bond strengthening. In contrast, the Fe–O bond exhibited a slight decrease in −ICOHP from 2.60 to 2.53, suggesting a weakening interaction. The observed strengthening of the Fe–N bond and weakening of the Fe–O bond can be attributed to electronic effects induced by the Pt3Fe alloy. Specifically, the electron-rich Pt3Fe alloy donates electrons to the D1-FeN4 site, lowering the oxidation state of Fe. This reduction enhances Fe–N d−π* back-donation, thereby reinforcing the Fe–N bond. Conversely, the decreased oxidation state of iron weakens the ionic interaction between iron and OH.

The free energy profiles of the electrochemical dissolution pathway of the D1-FeN4 site with and without the Pt3Fe alloy were computed. The initial three hydrogenation steps, encompassing hydrogenation of the axial OH ligand followed by two successive N hydrogenation steps, were identified as pivotal (Figure d). Among these, the second N hydrogenation step was determined to be the RDS for the entire dissolution process. With the introduction of Pt3Fe, the hydrogenation of the OH ligand on the D1-FeN4 site was notably accelerated, which can be attributed to the weakening of the Fe–O bond. This observation is consistent with the trends observed in the in situ XANES spectra. Notably, the energy barrier of the RDS increased from 0.59 to 1.24 eV, which can be ascribed to the strengthening of the Fe–N bond. These insights explain the experimentally observed inhibition of dissolution behavior at the D1-FeN4 site.

The influence of the D1-FeN4 site on the electronic structure of the Pt3Fe alloy was computed. The pDOS revealed that the d-electron center of Pt shifted downward from −1.95 eV to −1.97 eV (Figure e). Correspondingly, the oxygen adsorption energy on Pt increased from 0.93 to 0.98 eV, corresponding to a weaker oxygen adsorption strength (Figure f). Previous studies have also indicated that a downward shift in the d-band center leads to a weaker oxygen adsorption strength on Pt. The calculations of dissolution pathway further showed that, upon the introduction of D1-FeN4, the energy barrier for the formation of PtO4 increased from 4.13 to 5.35 eV (Figure g), which well explains the suppression of the Pt dissolution rate given the determining role of Pt surface oxide formation in the cathodic dissolution of Pt under operational conditions.

Electrocatalytic Performance

The durability of the Pt3Fe-FeNC catalyst was further investigated. An AST was performed using a triangular wave potential program with a range of 0.6 to 1.0 VRHE under an Ar atmosphere. Within this potential window under an inert atmosphere, the metal dissolution emerges as the dominant factor governing catalyst instability (Figure a). Other contributing factors, such as carbon oxidation, contribute in a minor way in this potential window, which is further corroborated by the minor variation in double-layer capacitances observed across all three catalysts following AST (Figure S14).

The polarization curves of the catalyst were recorded at the initial stage, after 20k cycles and 30k cycles. Remarkably, after 30k cycles of AST, the polarization curves of Pt3Fe-FeNC exhibited nearly perfect overlap, with negligible changes in the E 1/2 (Figure b). In contrast, the E 1/2 of Pt3Fe-NC and FeNC shifted negatively by 9 and 29 mV, respectively (Figures c and d). Correspondingly, the mass activities of Pt3Fe-FeNC, Pt3Fe-NC, and FeNC catalysts decreased by 0.4%, 23.7%, and 72.8%, respectively (Figure e). Additionally, the Pt3Fe-FeNC demonstrates superior initial activity compared with both isolated component catalysts. Quantitatively, at 0.85 VRHE, the mass activity of Pt3Fe-FeNC reached 7.64 A g–1, exceeding that of Pt3Fe-NC (4.73 A g–1) and FeNC (2.19 A g–1).

Electrochemical methanol probe experiments suggest that surface Pt sites in Pt3Fe-FeNC contribute to the ORR activity (Figure S15a). A post-treatment experiment by H2O2 shows that D1 sites also contribute to the ORR activity in Pt3Fe-FeNC (Figure S15b). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Pt3Fe-FeNC operates through a dual-catalytic site synergy, involving both Pt3Fe and D1-FeN4 sites. After 30 000 cycles of AST, the Pt3Fe-FeNC catalyst maintained strong responsiveness to methanol and H2O2 (Figure S16), demonstrating that the synergistic catalytic feature between Pt3Fe and D1-FeN4 remained intact despite the rigorous AST.

The Pt3Fe-FeNC catalyst was then applied to DMFC. The Pt loading at the cathode is 0.056 mg cm–2. At a current density of 0.1 A cm–2, the cell voltage achieved 0.51 V, accompanied by a peak power density (P max) of 200 mW cm–2 (Figure a). These performance metrics exceeded those of the FeNC catalyst, which delivered 0.49 V at 0.1 A cm–2 and a P max of 169 mW cm–2. Following 1000 h of operation at 0.1 A cm–2, the cell voltage of the Pt3Fe-FeNC cathode declined to 0.30 V, corresponding to a decay rate of 41% (Figure b). In contrast, under identical voltage retention conditions, the FeNC cathode operated for only 100 h. Compared to pristine Pt3Fe, the Pt3Fe-FeNC catalyst demonstrates a lower radical generation rate (Figure S17), suggesting that the reduced radical concentration may be another factor contributing to the enhanced stability of the hybrid catalyst. While the hybrid catalyst successfully suppresses metal dissolution, a 41% performance loss was still observed, which may be attributed to corrosion of the carbon matrix, radical attacks, degradation of ionomers, or other. ,

Conclusions

In summary, we synthesized a hybrid Pt3Fe-FeNC catalyst, where electronic transfer occurs from the Pt3Fe alloy to the D1-FeN4 sites. Online ICP-MS confirmed the mutual inhibition of dissolution between the D1-FeN4 sites and Pt3Fe. The microscopic mechanism underlying this phenomenon was elucidated through in situ XANES, in situ Raman spectroscopy, and theoretical calculations. The results show that Pt3Fe acts as an electron donor, reducing the oxidation state of the D1-FeN4 site. The reduced oxidation state strengthens the Fe–N bond by enhancing Fe–N d–π* back-donation while simultaneously weakening the Fe–O bond by reducing the ionic interaction between Fe and OH. These electronic effects facilitate the detachment of OH ligands but make the subsequent N-hydrogenation more energy-consuming, which is the RDS in the dissolution pathway, ultimately suppressing the electrochemical Fe dissolution. Conversely, electron withdrawal from Pt lowers its d-band center, weakening the oxygen adsorption strength and thereby inhibiting the formation of Pt oxides and suppressing Pt dissolution. Owing to mutual dissolution inhibition, the hybrid catalyst shows a mere 0.4% decay in mass activity at 0.85 VRHE following 30,000 accelerated stress test cycles between 0.6 and 1.0 VRHE in an inert atmosphere, markedly surpassing those of single-component counterparts. Notably, the Pt3Fe-FeNC catalyst generated a peak power density of 200 mW cm–2 and operated for 1000 h with 41% of voltage loss in a DMFC, highlighting its potential for fuel cell applications. Significantly, this bidirectional dissolution–inhibition mechanism appears to be broadly applicable to other types of SASs–NPs hybrid systems as well as to various catalytic reactions beyond ORR, including the OER, the CO2RR, and others. This work provides atomic-level insights into the synergistic stabilization effect between SASs and NPs, which will help to design stable catalysts for ORR and beyond.
