Rethinking Antibacterial Innovation in Dental Composites: Bridging Nanoparticle Design and Translational Potential
Authors: Schawanya Kaewpitoon Rattanapitoon, Pantip Chitpitaklert, Wirangrong Srithongklang, Nathkapach Kaewpitoon Rattanapitoon
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

We commend Naguib and colleagues1 for their timely and methodologically sound study evaluating polymer-coated magnesium oxide (MgO) nanoparticles as antimicrobial additives in resin-based dental composites. Their work contributes to the pressing need for biofilm-resistant restorative materials, especially in light of the global burden of secondary caries and the rise of antibiotic resistance in the oral cavity.

Their approach – anchoring MgO nanoparticles within a polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) coating – addresses a key limitation in direct nanoparticle use: agglomeration and reduced dispersion within the resin matrix. The reported improvements in antibacterial activity, especially against Streptococcus mutans, and retention of compressive strength over 30 days, represent notable progress in the field of antimicrobial restorative materials.

However, several dimensions warrant further exploration before clinical translation.

Durability and functional stability in oral environment

While the study reports sustained antibacterial activity over 30 days, this may not capture the full degradation spectrum of the PMMA coating or nanoparticle migration under dynamic intraoral forces. Similar systems have demonstrated early surface release followed by material fatigue and biofilm recolonisation.2 Studies simulating salivary enzymatic activity, pH fluctuation, and mechanical wear over extended periods would strengthen evidence of long-term efficacy.

Microbial resistance and ecological selectivity

Although MgO nanoparticles are less prone to inducing resistance than antibiotics, their chronic exposure may still exert selective pressure, particularly on commensal biofilm communities. Prior research on metal oxide-based nanocomposites has reported compositional shifts favouring acidogenic and aciduric strains in multispecies models.3 Incorporating microbial diversity analysis and next-generation sequencing could pre-emptively identify dysbiosis risks.

Host-biomaterial interaction and pulpal cytotoxicity

Despite the improved biocompatibility profile reported, the assessment was limited to in vitro cell viability assays. Histological data from in vivo models, particularly assessing pulpal inflammation and dentin–pulp interface interaction, would contextualise safety, especially given MgO’s known reactivity and potential for reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation.4

Polymer interface engineering and nanoparticle leaching

While PMMA coating improves dispersion, it may also act as a diffusion barrier, potentially reducing the ion release responsible for antimicrobial action. Balancing particle encapsulation with functional ion bioavailability remains a key design challenge. Coating architectures that allow controlled degradation (eg, pH-sensitive hydrogels) may offer a more responsive release system.5

Translational scalability and regulatory hurdles

Finally, though the material demonstrated promising in vitro performance, questions remain regarding reproducibility, cost-effective synthesis at industrial scale, and regulatory pathways for dental nanomaterials. Lessons from silver or zinc oxide nanocomposite commercialisation suggest the need for early engagement with safety, toxicology, and materials certification frameworks.6

In conclusion, the study by Naguib et al1 provides an important stepping stone towards the development of next-generation antibacterial dental materials. A comprehensive translational pipeline – from nanoscale interface design to clinical safety and ecological resilience – will be essential to realising the full potential of MgO-polymer hybrid systems in caries prevention and restorative care.
