Radiomic analysis of medical imaging for classification in oncology and recommendations for clinically initiated research: a literature review
Authors: Kaijing Mao, Lun Matthew Wong, Kuo Feng Hung, Rongli Zhang, Walter Y H Lam, Zhiyi Shan, Tiffany Y So, Qi Yong Hemis Ai, Kyongtae Ty Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveRadiomics is a thriving field that aims to enhance clinical decision-making by enabling the noninvasive, quantitative characterization of lesions on medical images. Despite thousands of studies being published in this field, the adaptation of radiomics in routine clinical practice remains challenging due to the complexity of analytical steps and issues in reproducibility. The goal of this review was to facilitate translation by bridging the gap between radiomics research and clinical applications in oncology.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted of major databases for literature on the application of radiomics to clinical classification in oncology published from January 1, 2005, to November 30, 2025. We reviewed eligible articles, summarized and discussed their content, and provided recommendations for each step of the radiomic analysis workflow.Key Content and FindingsThe literature review generated 13 key recommendations for improving the quality, reliability, and reproducibility of radiomic models for disease characterization in clinical oncology. These recommendations covered important aspects, including data quality assurance, robust feature selection techniques, open data, and model sharing.ConclusionsBy considering these recommendations, researchers and clinicians may improve the clinical applicability of radiomics, aiding its gradual incorporation into routine practice in oncology. The integration of radiomics into clinical settings holds the potential to enhance patient care and contribute to the advancement of personalized medicine.Keywords: Radiomic analysis, medical image, tumor classification, clinical application

Introduction

“Radiomics” is a term in medical imaging comprising the elements “radio” (referring to medical images) and “omics” (referring to fields such as genomics and proteomics that enhance our understanding of medical conditions) (1-4). Radiomics involves extracting quantitative features such as size, shape, and texture from medical images (1), and these features are used to build models that support specific clinical tasks, such as the classification of diseases and outcome predictions.

Statistical and machine learning-based techniques are essential components of radiomic model building and thus typically require interdisciplinary expertise from clinical practitioners, imaging technologists, computer scientists, engineers, and statisticians. Due to improvements in computing power (5,6), the interest in radiomics has increased substantially over the past two decades. A PubMed search conducted on January 1, 2025, with the query “radiomic” OR “radiomics” identified more than 15,163 publications produced since 2012, with over 85% of these published from 2020 onward (Figure 1). However, this notable intensification in research in this field over the past decade has not progressed beyond the preclinical stage, and there are few articles on the translation of radiomics into routine clinical practice in the literature (7). One of the major issues that hampers the further development of radiomic analysis is the unstable performance and low generalizability of the proposed radiomic models (8).

Several guidelines and checklists, including the Checklist for the Evaluation of Radiomics Research (CLEAR) and those outlined in “Radiomics with artificial intelligence: a practical guide for beginners”, “Criteria for the translation of radiomics into clinically useful tests”, “Joint EANM/SNMMI guideline on radiomics in nuclear medicine”, and “Radiomics in oncology: a practical guide” (8-12), have been proposed for performing radiomic analysis in medical images. The purpose of these guidelines and checklists is to standardize the procedures of radiomic analysis and to improve the performance and generalizability of the final models. However, as they emphasize methodological rigor and reproducibility, translational applicability in clinical practices is often neglected. Therefore, we conducted a literature reviewed in order to generate updated and comprehensive recommendations for the key elements at each step of supervised radiomic analysis and thus enhance the quality, reliability, and reproducibility of radiomic models for disease characterization in oncology. Overall, it is hoped that the findings of this review can support the transition in radiomics from research to routine clinical practice. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-aw-2150/rc).

Methods

A comprehensive literature search of the Web of Science, PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and Scopus databases was conducted. The criteria for literature inclusion were as follows: a content focused on radiomic analysis of medical imaging for cancer classification; English language; a publication date from January 1, 2005, to November 30, 2025; and peer-reviewed articles, systematic reviews, clinical studies, and conference proceedings that addressed radiomic analysis of medical images obtained from conventional radiology modalities. Articles that were non-English language or studies on other radiological imaging modalities were excluded. All eligible articles were independently selected by two observers (K.M. and L.M.W.), and any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third observer (Q.Y.H.A.). Results

The eligible articles were reviewed, and any potential concerns or issues were identified for each of the nine steps in the radiomic analysis pipeline. These concerns included unclear research goals, variability and uncertainty in data acquisition, questionable approaches to the segmentation of the region of interest (ROI) and image standardization, lack of clinically guided (blinded) feature extraction and selection, “cherry picking” for model development, and biased validation for model performance.

These concerns and potential solutions and recommendations are summarized and examined in the “Discussion” section for each step in the workflow of radiomic analysis.

Discussion

The following sections provide recommendations for each step of the radiomic pipeline.

Step 1: define objectives

At the beginning of a radiomic modeling cycle, researchers should clearly define the study objectives, in particular, what specific clinical question(s) are being addressed and how these are pertinent to routine practice (13). These considerations are crucial to designing an appropriate and applicable model for the clinical problem at hand. The core principle is to identify what outcome(s) or end point(s) from the model are desired [i.e., the dependent variable(s)], for example, whether the clinical question calls for a binary outcome (e.g., benign vs. malignant lesion) or a multiclass outcome (e.g., lymphoma, squamous cell carcinoma, or adenoma). Apart from differences in usage scenario, the increased number of modeled outcomes also results in greater model complexity. Specifically, binary classification is simpler and generally requires less training data to achieve good and robust performance as compared to multiclass classification, especially when the lesion/tissue has a visible distinction on the target modality. However, multiclass classification offers a higher level of explainability and flexibility that binary models do not share. Several clinical conditions remain suboptimal for the application of radiomics, for example, rare diseases that often lack sufficiently large imaging datasets to enable robust model training; acute and medically unstable presentations, in which the urgency of intervention precludes reliance on model-based decision-making; and disorders with limited therapeutic consequences, including osteoarthritis and degenerative disc disease (14). Those diseases without standardized diagnostic or therapeutic frameworks, such as chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, and irritable bowel syndrome, also represent barriers to the effective implementation of radiomic analysis (14).

Once the research question is defined, the scope of the modeling should remain unchanged while the model is being developed. Therefore, the first and most important step of a radiomic project is to clearly define the clinical question and decide which model category best represents the problem and can directly inform decision-making.

Step 2: dataset acquisition

Data and data labeling form the foundation of a proper radiomic analysis. Their quality and relevance are critical to determining the expected performance and clinical applicability of the trained model. The quality of data should be evaluated from both technical and clinical perspectives. The technical considerations include the size and labeling of the data. The clinical considerations are closely linked to the specific medical conditions of interest. As radiomic analyses often focus on modeling imaging features, biases in clinical information, such as those related to age and sex, might manifest as unexpected instability in the outcome model.

Technical considerations

In clinical practice, data volume critically affects the generalizability of the final model and the risk of overfitting. With consistent quality, a larger data volume can aid in achieving a more reliably trained radiomic model. However, many radiomic studies are conducted without formal sample size justification, often resulting in datasets that are too small to adequately mitigate overfitting (15,16). Thus, sample size justification is strongly encouraged during model development to address this issue.

A minimal requirement for data volume is inherently linked to model complexity. A widespread rule of thumb is to have at least 10 cases for each modeled dependent and independent variable (17), yet in practice, additional cases are often needed to reach an acceptable reliability for clinical use. Recently, concerns regarding this practice have emerged (16,18), and comprehensive frameworks for sample size calculation in radiomic-based binary outcome prediction models have been proposed (18). These structured approaches provide more robust estimates than do traditional methods (i.e., rule of thumb). Practical applications demonstrate that such methods effectively balance predictive accuracy and provide good generalizability, while online tools offer researchers accessible platforms for performing the necessary calculations. By adopting a rigorous and structured calculation framework when the target outcome aligns with its assumptions, researchers can minimize overfitting, ensure accurate risk estimation, and substantially enhance the reliability and validity of their predictive models.

With respect to raw imaging data, a high-quality dataset should accurately reflect the routine imaging condition. A set of perfectly acquired images does not necessarily represent high data quality, as it will deprive the model of the opportunity to adopt for various artifacts, such that it is only valid for perfectly acquired images. However, it remains desirable to acquire data without obvious imaging errors that will be revoked on site, such as obvious motion artifacts or mechanical errors.

With respect to data labeling, data quality is assessed via labeling accuracy (number of correctly labeled data points), labeling consistency (consistency of the data-labeling standard), and class ratio. For the former two criteria, they should be optimized as much as possible, while for class ratio, its reflection of real-world scenarios should also be considered. Despite this, certain machine-learning frameworks exhibit varying degrees of tolerance for labeling errors of up to 30% error (19). However, it should be noted that deep neural networks (deep learning) involve significantly different interactions between data volume/quality, model complexity, and performance as compared to conventional machine-learning techniques, which is known as the double descent phenomenon (20).

Clinical considerations

We recommend thorough consideration of the five awareness dimensions of the METRIC framework, namely measurement process, timeliness, representativeness, informativeness, and consistency (21). The purpose of the METRIC framework is to examine the appropriateness of a dataset’s quality for a given use case in medical practice. A description of these five awareness dimensions and how they may be applied in radiomics are provided below.

Measurement process

The measurement process involves the evaluation of possible technical and human influences that can affect data acquisition. This includes instrumental errors that impact accuracy and precision, as well as human-induced errors (noisy labels, carelessness, and outliers), missing values, and source credibility that hampers the overall reliability of the data (21). In the context of radiomics, relevant considerations are image acquisition protocols (e.g., from single or multiple sources, modalities, and acquisition parameters), segmentation, and class labels. We therefore recommend investigating and comparing the performance between models built with single modalities/sequences and those built with multiple ones (22,23). Given the significant role of human-induced errors, intra- and inter-observer variability remains a major concern in radiomics. We therefore recommend adherence to standardized guidelines, targeted training programs, and consensus-based reading approaches to mitigate noise and ensure consistency (24).

Timeliness

Timeliness consists of the temporal changes and how well they are reflected in the dataset (21). It comprises two aspects: age (the relationship between creation date and usage date) and currency (how up-to-date the data are). Since medical knowledge constantly evolves and since diagnostic criteria may change after a dataset’s creation, examining timeliness is crucial to determining whether the dataset is still appropriate for its intended use. Whether an update is necessary should be determined by whether labeling was conducted with criteria that could influence the reliability or relevance of the results.

Representativeness

Representativeness is how accurately the dataset reflects the target population (21) and consists of three concepts: (I) variety, which includes the range of demographics and data sources; (II) depth, which evaluates the overall data volume and subpopulation coverage; and (III) target class balance, which evaluates whether the classes of target variables are properly proportioned for radiomic analysis. These aspects must be adequately addressed to create a radiomic model suitable for clinical application. In radiomic analysis, target classes often become imbalanced due to factors such as rare diseases. To ensure proper pattern learning, it may be beneficial to intentionally oversample rare classes at the feature level instead of maintaining real-world distributions. Bootstrapping and image-level augmentation (e.g., rotations, intensity, and changes) can also be applied in both classical hand-crafted radiomics and deep learning-based workflows (25). These strategies help improve model robustness and generalizability, but should complement, but not replace, independent external validation. For certain rare diseases, radiomics may still not be suitable.

Informativeness

Informativeness reflects how effectively the data convey their intended information (21) and includes four key aspects: understandability—whether data are clear and unambiguous; redundancy—whether information is unnecessarily duplicated; informative missingness—whether missing values contain meaningful patterns; and feature importance—whether features contribute meaningful value to the application.

Consistency

Consistency focuses on how data are presented and structured within the dataset (21) and comprises three aspects: rule-based consistency—whether data follow a consistent format with internal and external rules; logical consistency—whether data are logically sound without contradictions; and distribution consistency—whether different subpopulations share similar statistical properties.

Step 3: ROI segmentation

A critical step in any radiomic workflow is the accurate definition of ROIs in either two-dimensional (2D) or three-dimensional (3D) medical images. Some studies have limited segmentation to a single slice or a small region within a 3D volume (26), but this practice can compromise the representativeness of extracted features, particularly in histologically or radiologically heterogeneous lesions. Evidence strongly suggests that ROI inaccuracies, such as under- or over-segmentation, can significantly affect classification outcomes in radiomic models, leading to unreliable results and hindering clinical translation (27-29).

Recent studies have consistently indicated that 3D segmentations generally yield more robust and reproducible radiomic features than do 3D segmentation (30-32). For instance, a study on lipomatous soft-tissue tumors found that 3D segmentations had a higher proportion of stable features (58.9%) compared to 2D ones (37.6%), along with better inter- and intra-observer consistency (30). Although the choice between 2D and 3D may still depend on the imaging modality, anatomical site, and clinical objective (33), the general consensus is that 3D segmentation is preferable by virtue of its ability to capture the full volumetric heterogeneity of a lesion.

Although image segmentation can be performed manually, semiautomatically with manual corrections, or fully automatically, both manual and semiautomated methods introduce significant observer bias. Deep learning-based automated image segmentation is rapidly advancing and has demonstrated prediction ability that matches that of clinicians (34,35). This automated approach offers two key advantages: it reduces clinicians’ workload and eliminates variation between observers in radiomic features. However, a major limitation remains: trained algorithms often fail when applied to different datasets and have poor generalizability (36,37). This challenge calls for further research in order to develop more robust and adaptable automated segmentation algorithms. Additionally, many segmentation studies suffer from unreliable performance assessment and statistical bias due to incorrect metric usage (38-41). To ensure reliable segmentation, manual double-checking of the segmentations and necessary adjustments by experts are strongly recommended (42,43).

Step 4: image preprocessing

Image preprocessing occurs prior to feature extraction. The primary purpose of this step is to standardize images across different imaging protocols, scanning devices, patient positioning, scanner orientation, and institutions to improve the generalizability and representativeness of the data and to address variations in spatial resolution and voxel dimensions. Computed tomography (CT) is typically considered more generalizable than is magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (30). This is because CT images are reconstructed based on a physical scale from the attenuation of gamma radiation in each of the image pixels and voxels, whereas MR images are reconstructed by weighting the spin relaxation time among neighboring pixels and voxels. Therefore, MR images are more likely to be influenced by imaging modalities or protocol metrics and are more challenging to align across scanner types than are CT images. Regardless of scanning modality, it is always recommended that image normalization be performed. Common preprocessing steps include image resampling and filtering, which are applied to different situations, as summarized in Table 2 (44-48). The choice of processing parameters significantly impacts radiomic feature values, their repeatability, and model performance (44,45,49). Because optimal parameters differ for each task, researchers should determine the best practice by conducting empirical ablation analysis and thoroughly document all processing details (50).

Perhaps counterintuitively, the inclusion of multiple imaging modalities or sequences for the same task does not guarantee better performance or generalizability. Rather, this may result in various concerns as it complicates the technical processes; for instance, it requires registration to align different imaging modalities or sequences, which can contaminate the reliability of the downstream steps (25-27). Differences in the reconstruction of the digital images obtained from various imaging modalities contribute differently to the generalizability of the final models (28,29). Furthermore, in both CT and MRI, images obtained after the injection of intravenous agents (i.e., contrast agents) can result in lower generalizability, as differences in volumes and rate of the injected agents and postinjection scan time intervals among individuals can increase the risk of image variability. To achieve a high generalizability, we suggest that radiomic research based on MRI or postinjection images should both raise the baseline of data volume and the number of centers involved. In this regard, deep learning-based reconstruction techniques have shown promise for improving feature stability and predictive performance (31-34), but they have not yet been broadly validated. We recommend always formalizing and documenting the image data acquisition and reconstruction protocol to enhance the repeatability and reliability of trained models.

Step 5: feature extraction

After image segmentation and preprocessing, radiomic features are extracted from medical images based on the labeled ROIs. This process typically involves applying an imaging filter, followed by the calculation of the same set of handcrafted features from the ROI. Given the wide range of imaging filters and handcrafted features available, their combinations can cascade into a vast number of features on the scale of hundreds to thousands. Additionally, each imaging filter and handcrafted feature may have configurable parameters that can be arbitrarily specified, further expanding the number of potential features. This highly customizable nature of the radiomic feature extraction process has led to severe reproducibility issues (51), and an international group has launched the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) (52) to standardize the approach for calculating features from radiomic matrices. Here, we introduce briefly the key points for feature extraction and the tools available that can be used to ensure repeatability.

Deciding the extraction parameters requires thorough consideration of the typical imaging characteristics of the target lesion, its clinical properties, and the physical significance of the radiomic features. Among these parameters, two of the most critical settings are the gray-level discretization bins and the radius for spatial heterogeneity quantification. First, most radiomic features have optimal value ranges, and the choice of bin size (e.g., 16 vs. 256 bins) during gray-level discretization (following signal intensity normalization) directly influences the calculated feature values. Too few bins may oversimplify intensity variations, masking subtle heterogeneity represented only by small intensity differences, whereas too many bins may introduce noise, particularly in modalities with low intensity resolution. Additionally, certain radiomic features that quantify intervoxel intensity differences require specification of a radius that encompasses multiples of voxel sizes. For instance, for the extraction of gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features, the radius parameter (e.g., 1 vs. 5 mm) determines the spatial scale at which pixel relationships are analyzed. A larger radius might capture macroscopic heterogeneity better, while smaller radii emphasize local microtextures. The optimal radius parameter depends on the imaging modality, anatomical region, and clinical task. It should be noted that the extraction parameters that are performed after the image filters are applied are distinct from the preprocessing parameters.

Currently, there exist several open-source packages that comply with the IBSI, such as PyRadiomics, MaZda, IBEX, and LifEx (53-56). These packages, especially PyRadiomics, have been widely adopted and have contributed to promoting the reproducibility of extraction. We therefore recommend using one of these packages for feature extraction in order to ensure the repeatability of the feature extraction step. However, it should be stressed that the issue regarding the reproducibility has not been fully resolved and that feature extraction is only one of the many steps in radiomic analysis.

Step 6: feature harmonization

Feature harmonization is similar to image normalization, except it is performed over extracted radiomic features. Both steps are critical to radiomic workflows (57), particularly in multicenter studies where imaging data are acquired with heterogeneous protocols, scanners, and reconstruction settings (58). The goal of harmonization is to reduce nonbiological variability in radiomic features while preserving meaningful biological signals. Applied upon feature extraction, the harmonization step aligns feature distributions across batches or acquisition settings (59,60). Feature harmonization and image normalization complement one another: while image normalization addresses intensity inconsistencies at the image level, feature harmonization targets statistical discrepancies both across and within derived features that may compromise downstream analyses such as feature selection and model training.

Several feature harmonization methods are commonly used. ComBat, which is the most widely used method, adjusts post-extracted radiomic features to minimize technical inconsistencies while preserving relevant biological information (61). It corrects variations in imaging features arising from acquisition parameters by applying an empirical Bayes framework to estimate location and scale parameters (62,63). Previous studies have demonstrated that ComBat can effectively harmonize radiomic features and reduce the number of features with significantly different distributions across batches (64-66). Despite its efficiency and ease of use, ComBat has notable limitations (67,68). First, it assumes that errors in standardized input data follow a normal distribution, which may not hold true when feature distributions are multimodal. Second, the method requires that all batch effects and clinical covariates are known, limiting its ability to correct or preserve variation due to unmeasured factors. Third, current implementations can only harmonize one batch effect at a time, even though datasets are often heterogeneous across multiple sources of variation. Moreover, ComBat requires a minimum sample size of approximately 20–30 patients per batch, and specific transformations must be defined for each tissue type, tumor type, scanner, phantom material, segmentation approach, and feature. Finally, the method is not applicable in prospective investigations when little or no previously acquired data are available.

Other simpler methods, such as Z-score and min-max normalization, can standardize or rescale features within batches (58,69,70). However, they may not fully remove batch-specific biases. Deep learning-based harmonization methods can more effectively address complex nonlinear batch effects, and studies have increasingly integrated deep-learning at this step (71,72).

Step 7: feature selection

When a combination of transforms, imaging filters, and textural feature computations is applied, hundreds to thousands of unique imaging features can be extracted from each ROI. These features collectively define a high-dimensional “radiomics space”, where each feature represents a dimension, and each ROI is encoded into a single point within this high-dimensional space as a numerical coordinate. Machine-learning models are then trained to identify a high-dimensional plane that separates points belonging to different classes. The number of trainable parameters in these models, which constitutes the model complexity, is typically proportional to the dimensionality of the problem. Although increasing model complexity enhances its descriptive power and reduces bias, there is also a tradeoff of increased model variance, which may heighten the risk of overfitting.

Studies have proposed a variety of feature selection strategies, with some relying on further supervised learning techniques, which remain a key contributor to feature selection in stability. Generally, researchers should exhaust the analytical or statistical means as a preselection criterion prior to employing these supervised techniques. For example, basic statistical tests, such as the t-test or analysis of variance, can also be used to preselect features with the potential to discriminate between target end points.

Other more advanced analytical criteria are also implemented. For instance, feature reproducibility is a generally agreed upon to be a desirable quality that applies to the majority of lesions (51). Specifically, the consistency of feature values should be maintained across both intra- and interscan settings. However, there is no definitive consensus regarding which features possess such reproducibility. Although some research indicates shape features are more reproducible than are first-order and texture features (73-75), other studies have produced conflicting results (76,77). Moreover, as redundancy is inherent to some radiomic features, it has been shown that using only one feature category, as opposed to combining multiple categories, is an effective strategy for reducing model complexity without compromising predictive performance (23). Furthermore, certain imaging filters might not be suitable for all lesion types. Studies have reported that features based on wavelet imaging filters, which decompose the input image into directional components, are easily influenced by patient positioning and scanner orientations, resulting in lower reproducibility as compared to other non-wavelet-based features (78).

Explainability is another critical consideration in radiomics, as clinicians tend to favor models that can be meaningfully interpreted. First‑order features (e.g., mean intensity, variance, and skewness) and shape features (e.g., volume, sphericity, and compactness) generally provide higher explainability because they directly relate to the imaging characteristics and anatomical structures that are visible to the human eye. These features are also easy to validate clinically, as they align with intuitive measures of tumor burden and morphology. In contrast, higher‑order texture features (e.g., GLCM or wavelet‑derived features) might indicate subtle heterogeneity but be imperceptible to human vision. Consequently, feature selection strategies should prioritize features with clear biological or anatomical relevance while still balancing predictive performance. As opposed to a sole reliance on algorithmic selection, pathologically informed preselection of radiomic features is recommended to reduce the number of extracted features, mitigate overfitting, and enhance overall explainability.

Additionally, the reproducibility of features is also related to the variation in ROI segmentation. Previous studies have applied intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) of 0.75 to 0.90 for intra- or inter-observer agreement as an objective measure to select features resistant to segmentation variation for further analysis (79). Nevertheless, it should be stressed that the ICC indicates consistency in rank only and cannot guarantee the reproducibility in actual value or how it may affect translation.

After statistical and analytical techniques are exhausted, there can still be a large number of features remaining, and a range of supervised techniques for feature selection in classification tasks have been developed to achieve further deconvolution. These methods fall into three distinct categories: filter, wrapper, and embedded. Table 3 summarizes the common feature selection approaches (80,81). They generally differ in training times, assumptions, and selection criteria (82,83), but no single method consistently outperforms others in predictive performance. However, one study indicated that simpler methods generally provide better stability without sacrificing performance (82). Among these approaches, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR), and elastic net (Enet) are particularly popular choices for radiomic studies, outperforming most other feature selection methods under the reported conditions (82).

Step 8: model development

After feature selection, model development can begin. Ideally, robust models should also incorporate nonimaging patient features (85,86). Clinical, molecular, and genomic data should be integrated, as these significantly impact what can be interpreted from the analysis (87-89). Machine-learning and deep-learning approaches both work as classifiers, although machine-learning models offer better interpretability and are easier to understand than are deep-learning approaches. Deep learning algorithms generally require large amounts of data for effective training. In radiomics, however, data scarcity and imbalance are common challenges due to the limited size of patient cohorts and the high cost of expert-annotated datasets (90). To address these limitations, a number of investigations have evaluated transfer learning as a strategy for improving model performance under constrained data conditions (91). Transfer learning leverages knowledge gained from source tasks and applies it to target tasks, thereby reducing the need for extensive training datasets (91). Several studies have demonstrated the efficacy of transfer learning in radiomics by showing that pretrained models can achieve robust performance even when data are scarce (92-94). A notable example is RadImageNet, an open radiologic deep learning research dataset specifically designed for effective transfer learning (95). Models pretrained on RadImageNet have been shown to provide superior interpretability and performance as compared with those pretrained on ImageNet, particularly for smaller radiologic datasets (95). This highlights the potential of transfer learning to enhance both accuracy and clinical relevance in radiomic applications.

Recently, researchers have applied hyperparameter tuning to the optimization of the preset parameters of machine-learning classifiers to improve performance and prevent overfitting in radiomic analysis (96,97). Common methods include grid search, random search, and Bayesian optimization, which are usually combined with cross-validation to ensure robustness (98,99). However, some studies suggest that simpler classifiers may be more robust, accurate, and efficient, while others support ensemble techniques and the fusion of multiple classifiers to create more robust models (100-104). Since no single classifier performs best across all tasks and data types, it may be beneficial to build models with different approaches and identify the most effective and robust ones.

Step 9: validation and testing of radiomic models

Validation is a critical step in the radiomic pipeline, and its purpose is to verify the model’s performance on unseen data and to ensure its generalizability across diverse clinical settings. Without rigorous validation, models risk overfitting to training data and failing in real-world applications.

Training and validation framework

A fundamental aspect of building robust radiomic models is the establishment of a sound training and validation framework that can prevent data leakage. Data leakage occurs when information from the test or validation set inadvertently contaminates the training process, leading to an overly optimistic and unrealistic assessment of model performance. Leakage may appear to be relatively benign, but it can severely compromise the credibility of results and must be carefully avoided. Importantly, leakage can arise as early as the initial stages of the pipeline. To mitigate this risk, the dataset should be split at the outset, prior to any transformation or preprocessing of the images or associated data. Closely related to data leakage is data-snooping bias, which arises when the test set directly or indirectly influences the training process. In practice, unusually high model accuracy should raise suspicion and prompt a thorough evaluation of the pipeline for potential data or target leakage. Target leakage can be identified by examining features that exhibit strong correlations with the outcome variable, often through conventional statistical analyses.

In the context of 3D medical images, a common pitfall is the splitting of the dataset at the slice level rather than at the patient level. Under this setting, slices from the same patient could be distributed into both training and test cohorts, and the model may learn patient-specific characteristics rather than generalizable patterns, leading to artificially inflated performance metrics, thus undermining true model reliability. Therefore, data should always be split at the patient level to ensure that all data points from a single patient reside exclusively within either the training, validation, or test set.

A common initial approach involves splitting the dataset into three distinct subsets: training, validation, and testing (often in an approximate 7:1:2 ratio) (10,105). The training set is used to teach the model underlying patterns and associations within the data. The validation set, kept separate from the training data, is crucial for monitoring training progress to determine a stopping point and to identify signs of overfitting. Data from this set are not reused for testing to prevent data leakage. Finally, the test set, kept entirely separate and unseen by the model during both training and hyperparameter tuning, is used for a final, unbiased evaluation of the model’s generalizability.

However, a simple, single train-test split, even at the patient level, remains susceptible to selection bias, as the model’s performance might be highly dependent on a specific split. To further reduce bias and variance, k-fold cross-validation is strongly recommended as a minimum standard for model evaluation, particularly with moderately sized datasets. This technique partitions the dataset into k equally sized subsets (folds). The model is then iteratively trained k times, and in each iteration, one-fold serves as the validation set, and the remaining k−1 folds are used for training. This process ensures that every data point is used for validation exactly once and for training k−1 times, providing a more comprehensive and stable estimate of model performance (106,107). In an even more robust validation framework, k-fold cross-validation can be repeated with different splits.

Several robust training frameworks, such as AutoRadiomics, have been developed to support reproducible pipelines by integrating best practices for preprocessing, feature selection, and rigorous model evaluation in a standardized manner (108,109). These frameworks often incorporate systematic cross-validation strategies to ensure robust model development.

External validation and data sharing

The ultimate test of a radiomic model’s generalizability is its performance on external datasets. When available, including external datasets from different institutions, scanners, or acquisition periods is vital measure. These datasets can serve either to augment the training data, thereby increasing the model’s exposure to a diversity of data characteristics, or, ideally, to provide independent, truly unseen validation cohorts for the final model assessment (110). Public datasets (110-112), including dedicated benchmarks such as radMLBench (113), offer valuable opportunities for independent validation and benchmarking against established standards. However, researchers must carefully assess the clinical representativeness and data quality of such public resources to ensure their suitability for the given research question.

In scenarios where external datasets are unavailable due to practical or privacy constraints, researchers should consider sharing their final model or anonymized tabular feature data. This practice enables other researchers to conduct independent external validation, fostering transparency, reproducibility, and continuous model refinement within the radiomics community, even when the sharing of raw imaging data is restricted.

Performance metrics

Robust assessment of model performance in radiomics is essential for ensuring trustworthy clinical adoption. We strongly advocate for adherence to the guidance outlined in “Evaluation metrics in medical imaging AI: fundamentals, pitfalls, misapplications, and recommendations” (114). Key recommendations include employing multiple, complementary metrics tailored to the specific clinical task and dataset characteristics, transparently reporting evaluation methodology, and, most importantly, assessing the clinical utility and real-world impact of model outcomes.

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) is the most commonly used performance metric in radiomics for classification. This metric measures how well a model can distinguish between different classes, with higher values indicating better performance. Both AUC-ROC and accuracy (the ratio of correct predictions to all predictions) are widely used because they are easy to understand and facilitate model comparison. However, these metrics can be misleading when datasets are imbalanced, specifically when less than a third of patients have the positive condition being studied (115). To construct solid models for clinical practice, we recommend using both prevalence data and prevalence-dependent metrics alongside traditional measures for the evaluation of radiomics (116). We recommend reporting sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value, along with AUC-ROC and accuracy, to better understand the performance of a radiomic analysis. Once a model demonstrates reasonable predictive accuracy, it can advance to the testing stage.

Effective evaluation of survival prediction models requires careful prespecification and justification of performance metrics, such as the Harrell or Uno concordance index, depending on censoring levels (114). For dynamic predictions, time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) variants (cumulative/dynamic, incident/dynamic, and incident/static) must be clearly defined and clinically contextualized to avoid misinterpretation (114). Calibration should rely on visual plots and Brier scores, with caution need to avoid overinterpreting Hosmer-Lemeshow P values, which are sensitive to sample size and binning (114). Model comparison calls for appropriate statistical methods, as traditional tests such as the DeLong test are not suitable for time-dependent metrics.

Crucially, clinical utility must be assessed through decision analysis approaches such as net benefit and decision curve analysis to ensure that predictive value translates into meaningful impact in practice (114). Although these methods are being increasingly adopted to complement traditional validation metrics, their application in radiomics remains at an early stage. Decision curve analysis is often limited to binary prediction models and depends on arbitrarily chosen “optimal” thresholds that may not correspond to clinically meaningful decision points, while recalibration is almost always required for new cohorts. As Kerr et al. (117) emphasize, inappropriate use or misinterpretation of decision curves can yield misleading conclusions about a model’s utility, underscoring the importance of methodological rigor. In line with this, a systematic review has indicated that although most studies interpret findings correctly, errors in the implementation of decision curve analysis are not uncommon (118). Taken together, these critiques highlight that while decision analytic frameworks hold promise in bridging predictive performance and clinical practice, their current use in radiomics should be regarded as exploratory, with caution warranted until consensus workflows and clinically validated thresholds are established.
