DAA treatment for HCV reduce risk of hepatocellular carcinoma: a 10-years follow-up study based on Chinese patients with hepatitis C
Authors: Xiaobo Zhu, Linna Jia, Ming Yue, Amei Zhang, Xueshan Xia, Rongbin Yu, Hongbo Chen, Peng Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe long-term benefits of direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy after achieving sustained virological response (SVR) remain uncertain in the Chinese population. This study evaluates the incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), hepatic decompensation, and all-cause mortality among Chinese hepatitis C patients treated with DAAs. We included patients diagnosed since 2011 and followed them until November 1, 2022. The primary outcomes were HCC, hepatic decompensation, and mortality. Multivariable proportional hazards model was used to assess the impact of SVR and cirrhosis status. The cohort consisted of 1272 patients with SVR (92.1%) and 109 without SVR (7.9%), with a median follow-up of 61 months. The incidence of HCC was significantly lower in the SVR group (5.1 per 1000 person-years) compared to the no-SVR group (15.0 per 1000 person-years). Achieving SVR was associated with a significantly reduced risk of HCC (adjusted hazard ratio: 0.32; 95% CI 0.16–0.67). Cirrhosis was linked to an increased risk of developing HCC and hepatic decompensation. These findings highlight the importance of early DAA treatment, particularly for patients with cirrhosis.Keywords: DAAs, SVR, HCC, Hepatic decompensation, All-cause mortalitySubject terms: Cancer, Biomarkers, Diseases, Risk factors

Introduction

Chronic infection with hepatitis C virus (HCV) has long been the leading cause of liver-related morbidity and mortality around the world1,2. Chronic hepatitis leads to fibrosis of the liver, which may further progress to cirrhosis, where individuals are at risk for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and liver-related death2,3. In addition, HCV infection is associated with a small number of non-liver-related diseases. Therefore, chronic HCV infection also reduces overall survival4. In patients receiving antiviral therapy with interferon (IFN), achieving a sustained virological response (SVR) was associated with a reduced risk of liver-related events (LREs), including HCC, end-stage liver disease (ESLD), and liver-related mortality5–7.

The availability of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) significantly increased the rate of SVR in different patient classes, including patients with decompensated cirrhosis (where IFN is contraindicated), thereby altering the course of HCV infection in patients with advanced liver disease8–10. DAAs are expected to reduce the high incidence and mortality of end-stage liver disease. Data on the effects of DAAs on HCC and decompensated cirrhosis have begun to emerge, and there is evidence of markedly decrease in these outcomes9,11–13. However, some studies have shown that DAA-based treatment regimens do not reduce the risk of HCC, on the contrary, may even increase the risk14–16. However, there is a lack of long-term follow-up of DAA treatment in China. Finally, it is unclear whether the beneficial effects of SVR in a high-risk population with chronic HCV infection and cirrhosis also lead to a reduction in all-cause mortality.

The aim of this study was to assess the impact of DAAs treatment and cirrhosis status on the risk of HCC, hepatic decompensation and all-cause mortality in a Chinese population.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

This is a retrospective observational cohort study including 1381 chronic HCV patients receiving DAAs in the outpatient clinics at the Jurong People’s Hospital Affiliated to Jiangsu University from January 2011 until November 2022. In the initial phase of DAA treatment (2011–2013), pegylated interferon α-2b (Peg-IFNα-2b) was used as combination therapy. These patients were 18 years of age and older., Patients with history of HCC (n = 17), history of decompensated cirrhosis (n = 62), HBV or HIV co-infection (n = 14), and other malignant tumors (n = 12) were excluded. Information on HCC occurrence both before and after treatment was retrieved from hospital inpatient and outpatient diagnoses.SVR was defined as nondetectable serum HCV RNA at ≥ 10 weeks post-treatment, and most patients were evaluated for SVR at ≥ 12 weeks post-treatment17.

Follow-up and study endpoints

The index date of the study was defined as the date of receiving DAA regimen. For patients receiving multiple treatments, we will use the first treatment as the baseline. The study outcome were new cases of HCC, hepatic decompensation and all-cause mortality after the index date. Patients were followed until the study outcome or 01/11/2022, whichever came first. Information on HCC occurrence before and after treatment was obtained from hospital inpatient and outpatient diagnoses. HCC was diagnosed according to the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines. Hepatic decompensation was defined as evidence of prior or current diagnosis of ascites, hepatic encephalopathy (grade 3 or 4), spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, variceal hemorrhage, or baseline concomitant medications with a specifc indication for ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis or variceal hemorrhage, or explicit mention of decompensated cirrhosis. All-cause mortality was defined as liver-related death (death due to cirrhosis, hepatic failure, or liver transplantation), and non-liver-related death (any other death). Diagnosis was based on pathology reports, discharge summaries or consultation notes.Information on deaths comes mainly from death certificates for inpatient hospital diagnoses and outpatient diagnoses. Death certificates listing a patient as dead are completed by the doctor caring for the patient. Patients not developing HCC, hepatic decompensation, and who did not die were censored at the last visit or at the date of death, including those who had prematurely discontinued DAA therapy.

Other definitions

Covariates for adverse events including age, sex, previous treatment, smoke, alcohol use, hypertension, diabetes, cirrhosis and SVR. Smoking and alcohol use were collected from medical records. Systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg was diagnosed as hypertension. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a person with typical diabetes symptoms with arbitrary blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L or fasting blood glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L. Cirrhosis was defined as the presence of cirrhosis symptoms such as ascites, jaundice and other manifestations, abnormal serological examination, and B-ultrasound, CT and other examinations showing signs of cirrhosis.

Statistical analysis

Follow-up time was calculated as the time between the start of treatment and the last follow-up, or the date of primary outcome whichever occurred first. Incidence rates and 95%CIs were estimated with an exact method based on the Poisson distribution. Incidence calculation included all-cause mortality, HCC and hepatic decompensation that developed from solid organ transplant (SOT) till the end of follow-up. Continuous variables were presented as median (Interquartile ranges 25–75%, IQR), and categorical variables were presented as frequency (%). Categorical data were compared using Fisher’s exact test or Chi‐square (χ2) test as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared with Student’s T or Mann–Whitney U tests according to their distributions. Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted to illustrate and compare the cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality, HCC, and hepatic decompensation by SVR and cirrhosis status, and log-rank tests were used to assess the differences between these curves. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were constructed to identify the impact of SVR and cirrhosis. Models included age, sex, previous history of treatment, hypertension, diabetes and alcohol use.

Before conducting subgroup analysis, we first divided the patients into SVR group and no-SVR group based on their baseline treatment status, and then conducted subgroup analysis.To investigate potential heterogeneity, patients were classified by cirrhosis, age, hypertension, diabetes, previous treatment, and alcohol use. Cox analyses were repeated in each subgroup and multiplication terms were included to test for the presence of interactions.

All statistical analyses were 2-tailed and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. R version 4.1.2 were used for the analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics

Analysis was performed on 1381 adult HCV patients who completed antiviral therapy with a DAA regiment, had available SVR data; 1272 (92.1%) were SVR and 109 (7.9%) were no-SVR. Baseline characteristics of patients are shown in Table 1. The median follow-up time in this cohort was 61.0 months (quartile range: 39.0–81.0), the mean age of patients was 63.0 years, and 27.3% were male. Compared with the SVR group, the proportion of patients with a history of hypertension is higher in the no-SVR group (44.0% vs. 26.0% respectively p < 0.001). Previous treatment history was also more common in the SVR group than in the no-SVR group (20.5% vs. 4.6% respectively p < 0.001). The mean age of the SVR group was lower than that of the no-SVR group (62.0 years vs 66.0 years, p < 0.05) and the mean follow-up time was lower than that of the no-SVR group (61.0 months vs 104.0 months, p < 0.001).

Incidence by SVR and cirrhosis status

Overall, 12 HCC patients developed during 801 PY follow-up in patients who did no-SVR, with an incidence of 15.0/1000 PY (95% CI 7.7–26.2). The incidence of HCC was significantly lower in the SVR group, with 30 HCC patients occurring at 5.1/1000 PY (95% CI 3.4–7.2) during the follow-up period of 5940 PY. Among those without cirrhosis, 26 HCC patients developed during follow-up 5460 PY, with an incidence of 4.8/1000 PY (95% CI 3.1–7.0). The incidence of HCC was significantly higher in the cirrhosis group, with 16 HCC cases occurring at a follow-up of 1281 PY, with an incidence of 12.5/1000 PY (95% CI 7.1–20.3). The same trend can be seen in all-cause mortality (Table 2). The HCC incidence rate among people with cirrhosis was significantly higher in both the SVR group and the no-SVR group (9.7/1000 PY vs. 34.2/1000 PY) compared to people without cirrhosis (4.0/1000 PY vs. 10.7/1000 PY). Similar trends in incidence rates for all-cause mortality was observed, with significantly higher rates among people with SVR compared to no-SVR.

The effect of SVR and cirrhosis on the risk of morbidity

In a multivariate model, patients with SVR had a significantly reduced risk of HCC compared with those no-SVR (adjusted hazard ratio [HR]:0.32; 95% CI 0.16–0.67). While the relationship of SVR to reduce the risk of morbidity in all-cause mortality and hepatic decompensation were not significant. In the unadjusted analysis, with baseline cirrhosis was associated with an increase in all-cause mortality (HR:3.09, 95% CI 1.09–8.73; p < 0·05) and HCC (HR:2.58, 95% CI 1.38–4.81; p = 0.003). After adjusting for covariates, with baseline cirrhosis was strongly associated with an increase in HCC (HR:2.21, 95% CI 1.15–4.25; p = 0.017) (Table 3).

Subgroup analyses

An exploratory subgroup analysis based on patient characteristics is shown in Figs. 2 and S3. The reduced risk of disease progression was significantly higher in the cirrhosis group with SVR than in the non-cirrhosis group (HR (95%CI) 0.23(0.07–0.70); 0.38(0.15–0.98), respectively). For patients age < 60 and with a history of diabetes, SVR was more likely to reduce the risk of HCC (HR (95%CI) 0.12(0.03–0.57); 0.10(0.02–0.59), respectively) (Fig. 2). The risk of hepatic decompensation and all-cause mortality were significantly lower in previously treated SVR patients than in never-treated patients (HR (95%CI) 0.02(0.002–0.31); 0.06(0.004–0.53), respectively) (Fig. S3A,B). However, the association of SVR with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality and the development of hepatic decompensation was not significant in other subgroups.

Discussion

The effects of DAAs-induced SVR and baseline cirrhosis status on the risk of adverse events morbidity was assessed in this cohort. This is the first study in China to assess the effectiveness of DAAs for death and liver-related events in patients with chronic hepatitis C. SVR obtained with DAAs was associated with a 68% reduction in HCC compared with no-SVR. The risk of HCC was increased in the cirrhosis group compared with the non-cirrhosis group. DAA-induced SVR was significantly associated with a reduced risk of HCC in patients with cirrhosis. These findings suggested that DAAs and cirrhosis status will have a major impact on HCC and hepatic decompensation morbidity risk people with hepatitis C.

The present study found that the SVR effect of DAAs on HCC incidence is consistent with some studies, while other studies have reported relatively high reductions. Of the 21,984 patients treated with the DAAs in the Veterans Affairs system, 445 developed a new HCC during an average follow-up period of approximately 2 years, after adjusting for baseline characteristics of 21 potential confounders, patients who achieved SVR had a 71% lower risk of developing HCC disease than those who failed treatment11. Similarly, in a Canada study of populations, SVR acquired from DAAs was associated with an 80% reduction in HCC risk18. After multivariate adjustment, the presence of cirrhosis remained significant compared with the absence of cirrhosis, suggesting that cirrhosis is an independent risk factor for HCC in patients with chronic hepatitis C. Therefore, cirrhosis status is an important indicator for monitoring hepatitis C patients after antiviral therapy, and patients with cirrhosis should be closely monitored. In a multicenter retrospective cohort study of patients with hepatitis C cirrhosis, achieving SVR was independently associated with improved HCC-free survival (aHR: 0.17; 95% CI 0.09–0.33), this correlation was still present after exclusion of HCC that occurred within 6 months of follow-up (aHR: 0.14; 95% CI 0.07–0.26)19. However, our findings on the effect of SVR on HCC incidence in patients with cirrhosis are relatively low (aHR:0.23; 95% CI 0.07–0.70).

Subgroup analysis results indicate that DAA-induced SVR was significantly associated with a reduced risk of HCC in patients with cirrhosis, young age, and diabetes. Treatment of such people with hepatitis C will be necessary to substantially reduce the risk of future HCC. The HR of hepatic decompensation and all-cause death in previously treated SVR patients were 0.02 and 0.05, respectively. It is suggested that treatment with direct antiviral agents as well as access to SVR is expected to be more helpful to hepatitis C patients who have previously failed treatment and may lead to a reduced incidence associated with adverse outcomes, further suggesting the effectiveness of DAAs, in line with previous studies20–22. Thus, rapid scale-up of treatment is needed to realize the full benefits of DAAs in terms of reduced incidence.

Few studies have assessed the effect of SVR on all-cause and liver-related mortality compared to no-SVR23–25. However, this study showed no significant association between DAAs and the risk of hepatic decompensation and all-cause mortality. And based on the subgroup analysis, which showed that the association between SVR and hepatic decompensation and all-cause mortality was also not significant, this study may not have had statistical power for this outcome event. Finally, despite the multivariate analysis, we cannot exclude the risk of residual bias for confounders associated with untested prognostic factors or another complex time-dependent selection bias.

The study was followed for up to 10 years, making it possible to assess the long-term outcomes associated with DAAs. Second, the study was a single-center longitudinal design, allowing for a comprehensive clinical characterization of the entire cohort.

Conclusions

In summary, in this group of HCV-treated patients followed for 61 months, we found that cirrhosis is an independent risk factor for HCC. SVR of DAAs is associated with a significantly reduced risk of HCC. The lower reduction in HCC risk in patients with cirrhosis after treatment suggests an urgent need for early treatment and scaled-up treatment programs to fully realize the benefits of DAAs.
