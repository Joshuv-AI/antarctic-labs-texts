Dried persimmon peel as a sustainable feed ingredient for goats: Effects on nutrient digestion, energy-nitrogen metabolism, and methane mitigation
Authors: Xiongfei Zhang, Qingyan Yin, Jingtao Hui, Ao Wu, Yanru Zhu, Balamuralikrishnan Balasubramanian, Guangyu Ge, Jing Yang, Zhuangzhuang Liu, Lu Deng, Junhu Yao, Xinjian Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate how substituting corn with different proportions of dried persimmon peel (DPP) in a total mixed ration (TMR) affects methane (CH4) production and nutrient digestibility through an in vitro fermentation batch culture system. It was also to investigate its impact on the performance, nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation, and energy-nitrogen metabolism of goats via an in vivo experiment. Initially, an in vitro experiment was conducted in which 0, 10%, 20%, and 30% corn was replaced with DPP in the TMR to determine the optimum proportion for feeding goats. The results showed that replacing 20% of corn with DPP in the TMR reduced CH4 (mL/g digestible dry matter [DDM]) (P = 0.042) without adverse effects on the digestibility of dry matter (P = 0.708). According to the results of the in vitro batch fermentation experiment, replacing 20% corn with DPP in the diet was selected for the in vivo experiment. In the in vivo study, twelve Guanzhong dairy goats (first-parity, 22 months of age, non-lactating, 53.29 ± 1.92 kg) were divided into two homogeneous groups on the basis of initial body weight. There were six replicates for each treatment, with one goat in each replicate. The dietary treatments in the in vivo study were basal diet (CON) and diet containing DPP (MDPP; 20% of the corn in the basal diet was replaced with DPP on air-dried basis). The experiment lasted 35 d, consisting of a 28-d feeding period and a subsequent 7-d digestion and metabolism trial. The substitution of 20% corn with DPP had no effect on the dry matter intake, average daily gain and apparent total tract digestibility of goats (P > 0.05). Goats fed the MDPP diet had significantly lower serum creatinine levels (P = 0.012), and tended to decrease in aspartate aminotransferase (P = 0.068), aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase ratio (P = 0.073), and blood urea nitrogen (P = 0.076), compared with goats fed the CON diet. The inclusion of DPP in diet significantly elevated the catalase activity in serum of goats (P = 0.046). The rumen valerate concentration of goats significantly increased when 20% of the corn was replaced with DPP in the diet (P = 0.034). The rumen acetate concentration (P = 0.079) and acetate-to-propionate ratio (P = 0.094) tended to increase when 20% corn was replaced with DPP in the diet. Linear discriminant analysis effect size analysis showed that the relative abundance of g_Izemoplasmatales, g_Moryella, and g_Lachnoclostridium were significantly greater (P < 0.05) in goats fed the MDPP diet than those in goats fed the CON diet. Compared with goats fed the CON diet, those fed the MDPP diet presented significantly lower relative abundance of g_Eubacterium_ventriosum_group, g_Candidatus_Saccharimonas, g_Tyzzerella, and g_Clostridia_UCG_014 (P < 0.05). In conclusion, DPP can replace 20% of the corn in the diet of goats, and has the potential to reduce CH4 emissions, which has important implications for sustainable livestock production.Keywords: Goat, Dried persimmon peel, Production performance, Methane emission, Energy-nitrogen metabolism

Introduction

Amid an incessantly escalating global demand for livestock products, the livestock industry is perpetually in pursuit of sustainable solutions to address various challenges. One of the critical challenges is finding alternative feed sources that can reduce costs and environmental pollution, while maintaining or improving animal performance. Nevertheless, corn, being an international commodity, has the propensity to augment the expenses affiliated with livestock production and pose a threat to food security. In addition, as a powerful greenhouse gas, methane (CH4) causes energy losses equivalent to 2%-12% of the gross energy intake in ruminants (Ibáñez et al., 2016).

Dietary strategies, exemplified by the utilization of agricultural residues, have the potential to reduce competition for feed crops (such as corn and wheat). Persimmon peel, an agricultural byproduct from the processing of dried persimmons, has high nutritional value due to its abundance of soluble carbohydrates and polysaccharides, thereby rendering it a potential energy source in animal feed (Fitri et al., 2020). Persimmon peel contains bioactive compounds including tannins, flavonoids, carotenoids, etc. (Cui et al., 2023). These components are instrumental in curbing CH4 emissions and improving production efficiency in ruminants. Subsequently, they serve to mitigate the environmental pollution stemming from livestock production (Min et al., 2020). In ruminants, tannins in persimmon peel form insoluble complexes with proteins, preventing excessive protein degradation in the rumen (Kondo et al., 2014). This reduction in ruminal protein degradation allows more undegraded protein to reach the small intestine, thereby improving overall protein utilization efficiency in ruminants. Additionally, previous studies have indicated that persimmon tannins possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial activities conducive to promoting the health of ruminants (Wang et al., 2023a). The use of dried persimmon peel (DPP) in livestock diets is a potential strategic option for ruminant production systems, with the ability to reduce feed costs and mitigate environmental pollution (Salami et al., 2019). To explore the implications of replacing corn with DPP in goats, two experiments were carried out. In vitro batch fermentation and in vivo experiments were performed to evaluate the effects of DPP on performance, nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation, energy-nitrogen metabolism, and CH4 emissions in goats.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Northwest A&F University (Yangling, Shaanxi, China) gave approval for the experimental animals, experimental designs, and animal management employed in this study (DK2021027). The experiment complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

In vitro experiment

Animals and experimental design

The experiment involved four dietary treatments (CON, LDPP, MDPP, and HDPP), in which DPP replaced 0%, 10%, 20%, and 30% of corn in the total mixed ration (TMR) on a dry matter (DM) basis, respectively. The dietary inclusion levels of DPP were 0, 3.86%, 7.72%, and 11.58% DM for CON, LDPP, MDPP, and HDPP groups, respectively. The chemical compositions of DPP and corn are presented in Table 1. The CON diet was formulated to meet the dietary requirement of dairy goats according to NRC (2007) and the ingredients and nutrient levels of different dietary substrates are presented in Table 2. The dietary substrates were dried in a forced-air oven at 65 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, they were ground using a Wiley mill (HJZC-LH-08B, Beijing Haifuda Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) to pass through a 2-mm screen.

Three healthy castrated Guanzhong male goats (25 months of age, 46.25 ± 1.29 kg) implanted with permanent rumen fistulas were employed as sources of rumen fluid. The animals were fed the same diet as CON group in the present study. During the experiment, the goats were fed twice daily at 07:30 and 16:00, with free access to fresh water. Three hours after the morning feeding, equal volumes of rumen fluid were collected from goats, immediately transported to the laboratory and filtered through four layers of cheesecloth into a flask (Li et al., 2024). Artificial saliva was prepared following the formula of Menke et al. (1979). The artificial saliva was mixed with two parts of a medium consisting of (added in order) 400 mL H2O, 0.1 mL solution A (13.2 g CaCl2·2H2O, 10.0 g MnCl2·4H2O, 1.0 g CoCl2·6H2O, 8.0 g FeCl3·6H2O and made up to 100 mL with H2O), 200 mL solution B (5.0 g NH4HCO3, 4.0 g NaHCO3), 200 mL solution C (5.7 g Na2HPO4, 6.2 g KH2PO4, 0.6 g MgSO4·7H2O and made up to 1000 mL with H2O), 1 mL resazurine (0.1%, w/v) and 40 mL reduction solution (95 mL H2O, 4 mL l.0 mol/L NaOH and 625 mg Na2S·9H2O). The filtration and artificial saliva methods were conducted under a CO2 atmosphere in a water bath kept at 39 °C, and the prepared sample was ready for use.

In an anaerobic chamber, 40 mL of artificial saliva and 20 mL of filtered rumen fluid were added to each serum bottle holding 500 mg of ground dietary substrate. The bottle headspaces were filled with CO2 only. Finally, the bottles with the dietary substrate, artificial saliva and filtered rumen fluid were placed in a shaking incubator at 39 °C for 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after sealing with butyl rubber stoppers and aluminum caps. During the incubation period, headspace gas was collected and recorded at 3-h intervals to prevent excessive pressure accumulation. Gas production was determined in each serum bottle by volume displacement using a system consisting of a needle, tubing, and a 20-mL culture tube (Zhou et al., 2011). At the same time, three bottles containing incubation medium without any substrate were incubated as the blanks to correct the gas production resulting from the activity of the rumen fluid. Each time point included five replicates per group for a total of 83 serum bottles (4 treatments × 4 time points × 5 replicates + 3 blanks). Furthermore, the in vitro experiment with the same treatment sequence was performed twice to provide replication.

Sample collection

At 6, 12, 24, and 48 h of incubation, total gas production was recorded, and headspace gas was collected in aluminum foil gas-collection bags. Subsequently, the serum bottles were chilled on ice to stop fermentation. After removing the stopper, an aliquot of the fermentation fluid was extracted and pipetted into centrifuge tubes, which were then stored at −20 °C for subsequent analyses of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) and ammonia nitrogen (NH3–N). Immediately after the fermentation, the pH of the fermentation fluid was measured using a portable pH logger (HI 9024C, HANNA Instruments Inc., Woonsocket, RI, USA). The fermentation fluid was transferred into 50-mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the samples were oven-dried at 65 °C for 48 h. After this oven-drying step, the sediment mass was measured, and dry matter digestibility (DMD) was calculated.

In vivo experiment

Animals, experimental design, and diets

Twelve Guanzhong dairy goats (first-parity, 22 months of age, non-lactating, 53.29 ± 1.92 kg) were divided into two homogeneous groups on the basis of initial body weight (BW). There were six replicates for each treatment, with one goat in each replicate. According to the results of the in vitro batch fermentation experiment, replacing 20% corn with DPP in the diet was selected for the in vivo experiment. The dietary treatments in the in vivo study were same as the experimental diets in the in vitro experiment. The dietary treatments in the in vivo study were basal diet (CON) and diet containing DPP (MDPP; 20% of the corn in the basal diet was replaced with DPP on air-dried basis). The 35-d experiment was divided into two phases. The first phase of the feeding experiment lasted 28 d. Each goat was housed individually in a clean separate pen (approximately 2 m2) with ad libitum access to feed and fresh water and the separate pen was regularly cleaned. The second phase was a 7-d digestion and metabolism period, consisting of a 3-d adaptation period and a 4-d sampling period. Each goat was housed individually in metabolic cages measuring 1.2 m × 0.8 m × 1.5 m. Wire-screen baskets were placed under the floor of the metabolism cages, alongside urine-collecting funnels and plastic buckets, to complete collection of feces and urine. All goats were fed twice daily at 08:00 and 16:00, with free access to feed and water.

Data and samples collection

The goats’ BW was measured using an electronic weighing system (HY-602, Jinxuan Trading Co., Ltd., Jinhua, Zhejiang, China) at the beginning of the trial (d 0), and on d 28 and 35. In order to reduce the measurement error, the BW was measured for two consecutive days before the morning feeding. To determine the individual dry matter intake (DMI) of each goat, the experimental diet and refusal were precisely recorded throughout the experiment. The samples of fresh experimental diets and refusals were collected biweekly and stored at −20 °C for nutrient composition analysis to calculate DMI. On d 28, rumen fluid, feces, and blood samples were collected. Fecal samples were obtained through rectal massage to assess the fecal pH, NH3–N, and VFAs contents. The collected fecal samples were immediately stored at −80 °C for subsequent determination of VFAs and NH3–N to avoid fermentation after manual sampling. The fecal samples were processed following the method described by Wang et al. (2023b). Briefly, approximately 1.0 g of each fecal sample was weighed and mixed with 3.0 mL of distilled water. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was then collected for the analyses of NH3–N and VFAs. Rumen fluid samples were obtained by a gastric tube-type rumen fluid sampler at 2 h after morning feeding using the methods described by Chen et al. (2022). The pH values of the rumen fluid and feces were promptly determined using a portable pH logger (HI 9024C, HANNA Instruments Inc., Woonsocket, RI, USA). Fecal pH was determined with modifications to the method described by Sari et al. (2009). Briefly, approximately 5.0 g of fresh feces was weighed into a specimen cup, and 10 mL of deionized water was added. The liquid fraction was squeezed through a single layer of cheesecloth, and the pH of the resulting filtrate was measured. The collected rumen fluid samples were evenly partitioned into three portions and preserved at −80 °C for subsequent determination of VFAs, NH3–N, and microorganisms. Before the morning feeding, blood samples were collected from the jugular vein of each goat. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min to obtain serum. Finally, the obtained serum was stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis of serum biochemical indices.

Fecal and urine samples were collected following the methodology described by Sari et al. (2009). During the last 4 d of the experiment’s second phase, the amounts of offered feed, refused feed, and total fecal and urine outputs were recorded daily for each goat. Samples of fresh experimental diets and the refusal were obtained. These samples were used for subsequent analysis of the apparent total tract digestibility of nutrients and energy-nitrogen metabolism. To collect feces, wire-screen baskets were placed under the floor of the metabolism cages. For urine collection, a funnel was used to direct the urine into plastic buckets that contained 100 mL of 10% (vol/vol) H2SO4. The addition of this H2SO4 solution aimed to prevent microbial degradation and the volatilization loss of NH3–N. After thoroughly blending all the diet samples, feces, and urine together, 10% of the mixture was taken as representative sample. The nutrient compositions within these samples were analyzed later to calculate the apparent digestibility of nutrients and assess the energy-nitrogen metabolism.

Sample measurements

Samples of substrates, feed, refusals to feed, and feces were first dried in a forced-air oven at 65 °C for 48 h and then ground to pass through a 1 mm screen. The DM (method 930.15), crude protein (CP) (method 976.05), and ash (method 942.05) were analyzed according to AOAC (2019). The organic matter (OM) content was calculated as the difference between DM and ash content. The neutral detergent fiber (NDF) content was determined with heat-stable amylase and sodium sulfite via a fiber analyzer (A200i, Ankom Technology Inc., Macedon, NY, USA) by the method of Van Soest et al. (1991). The acid detergent fiber (ADF) was subsequently determined in accordance with method 973.18 (AOAC, 2000). According to AOAC (2000), calcium was analyzed by the dry ash method (SX2-10–12TP, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) with method 977.29, while total phosphorus was determined using a spectrophotometer (UV-2300, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) by the method 995.11. The gross energy of the feed, feed refusals, feces, and urine samples were determined via an adiabatic oxygen bomb calorimeter (Parr 6100, Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL, USA). Metabolizable energy was calculated according to NRC (2007). The concentrations of CH4 in the gas samples were determined via a gas chromatograph (HP 5890 Series, Agilent Technologies Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector and an HP-PLOT/Q (30 m × 0.53 mm × 40.00 μm) capillary column coated with porous polymer particles made of divinylbenzene and ethyl vinylbenzene. The concentration of CH4 detection was performed using 1 mL of gas directly drawn from the gas bag. The carrier gas used for CH4 assays was N2 at a flow rate of 5 mL/min.

The concentrations of NH3–N and VFAs in samples of fermentation fluid, rumen fluid, and feces were determined following the methods outlined in a previous study (Broderick and Kang, 1980; Li et al., 2014). The light absorbance at 700 nm was recorded using a spectrophotometer (UV-2300, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) to determine the NH3–N concentration. The gas chromatograph (GC7890A, Agilent Technologies Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) was used to determine the VFAs concentrations. Rumen microbial protein (MCP) was indirectly estimated based on urinary purine derivatives using the method described by Chen and Gomes (1992). Allantoin, xanthine, and hypoxanthine contents in the urine were determined according to the method described by Chen and Gomes (1992). Meanwhile, a uric acid kit from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (C012-1-1, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) was used to quantify uric acid content. The total microbial DNA was extracted from 12 rumen fluid samples using the MagBeads Soil DNA Extraction Kit (SMDE-5010, Tiangen Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity and concentration of the extracted DNA were measured with a NanoDrop2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Rochester, NY, USA), and the DNA integrity was evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The 16S rRNA gene V3–V4 hypervariable regions were amplified using forward primer 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and reverse primer 806R (5′- GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The 20-μL PCR reaction included 5 × reaction buffer 4 μL, 5 × GC buffer 4 μL, dNTP (2.5 mmol/L) 1.6 μL, forward primer (10 μmol/L) 0.8 μL, reverse primer (10 μmol/L) 0.8 μL, DNA template 1.6 μL, ddH2O 7.0 μL, and Q5 DNA Polymerase 0.2 μL. Three PCR replicates per sample were combined, and 5 μL of the pooled products were examined by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Following PCR amplification, purification and quantification steps were carried out on the PCR products. The AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (AP-GX-250, Axygen BioScience Inc., Union City, CA, USA) was used to purify the products, and then a Quantus Fluorometer (E6150, Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA) was used to determine their quantity. Following that, library construction for MiSeq sequencing was performed using the NEXTflex Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (5144–08, Bioo Scientific Corp., Austin, TX, USA). Finally, sequencing was conducted on the MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), as described by Liu et al. (2019). Microbiome bioinformatics analysis was performed using QIIME2 2019.4 following the official tutorial (https://docs.qiime2.org/2019.4/tutorials/) with minor modifications (Bolyen et al., 2019). Briefly, raw sequence data were demultiplexed using the demux plugin, followed by primer removal with the cutadapt plugin. The DADA2 plugin was then applied for sequence filtering, denoising, merging, and chimera removal (Callahan et al., 2016). Taxonomy was assigned to amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using the naive Bayes classifier in QIIME2 with the SILVA 16S rRNA database (v138). Alpha diversity indices, including the community richness (Chao 1 index and Observed species), community diversity (Shannon and Simpson indexes), and community coverage (Good’s coverage) were calculated using QIIME2 and analyzed at the ASVs level. The online genes cloud platform (www.genescloud.cn) was used to analyze the 16S rRNA microbiome sequencing data.

An automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used to analyze various serum biochemical parameters, including glucose (GLU), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), creatinine (CRE), triglyceride (TG), cholesterol (CHOL), total bilirubin (TBIL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), globulin (GLO), creatine kinase (CK), and amylase (AMY). Commercial assay kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) were employed to determine the malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1-1) content, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-1-1), and the enzymatic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD; A001-1-1), catalase (CAT; A007-1-1), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; A005-1-2) in serum.

Statistical analysis

For the in vitro data, a one-way ANOVA was conducted. To further compare the means across different treatments, Duncan’s multiple range tests were performed. Linear and quadratic polynomial contrasts were used to examine effects of different substitution ratios of DPP for corn on in vitro CH4 production and nutrient degradation. The in vivo experiment was conducted by a completely randomized design. For in vivo data, an independent-samples t-test was used. Results

In vitro experiments

In vitro gas and CH4 production and feed digestibility

As shown in Table 3, after in vitro incubation for 6 h, substitution of corn with different proportions of DPP had no significant effects on total gas (mL), CH4 (mL), and DMD (P > 0.05). After 12 h incubation, as the proportion of DPP substitution increased, CH4 (mL), CH4 (mL/g DM) or CH4 (mL/g DDM), and CH4 (% gas) exhibited significant quadratic responses (P < 0.05). Compared with CON, LDPP and MDPP significantly decreased CH4 (mL), CH4 (mL/g DM), CH4 (mL/g DDM), and the CH4 (% gas) (P < 0.05). In addition, there was no difference in DMD among the CON, LDPP, and MDPP treatment groups (P > 0.05). After 24 h incubation, the total gas production and DMD of HDPP were significantly lower than those of CON (P = 0.007). As the proportion of DPP substitution increased, total gas (mL) and DMD decreased linearly (P < 0.05). Compared with CON, MDPP group had significantly lower CH4 (mL) and CH4 (% gas) (P < 0.05). After 48 h incubation, total gas (mL), total gas (mL/g DM), total gas (mL/g DDM), and DMD decreased linearly as the proportion of DPP replacing corn increased in the TMR (P < 0.05). Substituting different proportions of corn with DPP significantly reduced total gas (mL/g DDM) and CH4 (mL/g DDM) (P < 0.05).

The alpha diversity analysis revealed that Good's_coverage index (P = 0.055) tended to decrease when corn was replaced with DPP. Good’s_coverage index is a metric used to assess the completeness of microbial community sampling in high-throughput sequencing studies. The Good’s_coverage index exceeded 0.990, indicating sufficient sequencing depth for diversity analysis. Table 14 shows that the dietary treatments had no impact on community richness (Chao 1 and Observed_species indexes) or diversity (Shannon and Simpson indexes) (P > 0.05). To examine the disparities in the rumen microbial community between the two groups, a beta-diversity analysis was conducted (Fig. 1A). According to the PCoA plots derived from the Bray–Curtis distance matrix, the points representing rumen microorganisms from the CON and MDPP groups were not clearly separated on the coordinate axis. Discussion

In vitro experiment

To alleviate waste treatment-related environmental pollution and improve economic value, sustainable use of agricultural residues in ruminant production systems is essential (Singh et al., 2021). Considering the abundance of soluble carbohydrates and polysaccharides, DPP could be used as an energy source in ruminant production. The DPP is a rich source of multiple bioactive compounds, especially persimmon tannins (Wang et al., 2023a). Digestibility is considered an important indicator of feed utilization efficiency. In the present in vitro experiment, the results showed that gradual replacement of corn with DPP (10% to 30%) resulted in a linear decrease in DMD. The decrease in DMD observed when 30% corn was substituted with DPP can be explained by the increased content of structural carbohydrates in the substrate. Additionally, a high content of tannins may inhibit microbial activities in the rumen, thereby decreasing the degradation of dietary nutrients (Singh et al., 2021). In the present study, total gas production decreased linearly as the proportion of corn replaced by DPP increased from 10% to 30%. During dietary nutrient degradation in the rumen, gases including CO2, CH4, and hydrogen are produced, with gas production positively correlated with DMD (Min et al., 2021). Thus, the decrease in total gas production may be associated with the decline in DMD. Previous studies have shown that an appropriate tannin content in diets can reduce rumen CH4 production (Berça et al., 2023; Nørskov et al., 2023). In this study, substituting 10% to 30% corn with DPP in the TMR reduced CH4 (mL/g DDM). Notably, 20% corn substitution by DPP was the most effective for CH4 mitigation (mL/g DDM). The reduction in CH4 production may be attributed to the inhibitory effects of persimmon tannins, as previous studies have shown that tannins possess CH4 mitigation potential, either by directly inhibiting methanogens or indirectly acting via protozoa (Min et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023). Compared with MDPP, HDPP did not lead to a further reduction in CH4 production but rather an increase in it. This phenomenon may be due to the enhanced promotional effect of elevated structural carbohydrates on CH4 production (Dixit et al., 2023).

Acetate production is associated with the degradation of structural carbohydrates by fiber-degrading bacteria in the rumen (Li et al., 2023). Tannin at low concentrations may stimulate the growth of bacteria by interacting with the cell surface proteins of bacteria (Krause et al., 2005). Consequently, a low concentration of tannin could potentially enhance ruminal fermentation (Singh et al., 2021). In the present in vitro experiment, compared with the CON group, both the LDPP and MDPP groups presented higher acetate contents. These results are associated with the impact of different dietary tannin concentrations on rumen microbial activity and the degradation levels of structural carbohydrates. The NH3–N in the rumen is generated primarily by the degradation of ingested protein nitrogen and nonprotein nitrogen. Valerate and branched-chain VFAs (i.e., isovalerate and isobutyrate) in the rumen primarily originate from ruminal oxidative-deamination and decarboxylation of branched-chain amino acids (Patra and Aschenbach, 2018). Several studies have indicated that tannins can form insoluble complexes with proteins, inhibiting the degradation of proteins in the rumen (Kondo et al., 2014; Patra and Yu, 2014). The decreased NH3–N, valerate and branched-VFAs concentrations of fermentation fluid may result from the inhibition of persimmon tannins on amino acid deamination by microbes, when different proportions of corn are substituted with DPP (from 10% to 30%).

Production performance and apparent total tract digestibility of nutrients

According to the results of the in vitro experiment, replacing 20% of corn in the TMR with DPP led to a reduction in CH4 production while having no adverse effect on nutrient digestibility. Therefore, replacing 20% corn with DPP in the diet was selected to investigate its effects on the growth performance and nutrient digestibility in goats. Abdelazeem et al. (2020) reported that the inclusion of 20% ensiled persimmon peel into sheep diets had no adverse effects on palatability or feed intake. Similarly, Lee et al. (2016) found that dietary supplementation with DPP did not impact production performance of pigs. Consistent with previous studies, the substitution of 20% corn with DPP had no effects on the DMI or ADG of the goats in this study. The inclusion of DPP in goat diets had no impact on growth performance of goats can be explained by the absence of differences observed in nutrient intake and digestion among dietary treatments.

The apparent total tract digestibility of nutrients is an important indicator for evaluating feed digestion. In the present in vitro study, the apparent total tract digestibility of nutrients in goats was not affected by DPP inclusion. These findings suggest that the use of DPP as a substitute for corn has no negative effect on nutrient digestibility in goats. To date, limited studies have investigated the effect of dietary supplementation with DPP on total digestive tract apparent digestibility in ruminants. Compared with corn, DPP has higher levels of structural carbohydrates, with NDF and ADF contents of 40.70% vs. 9.60% and 31.90% vs. 4.00%, respectively. Replacing 20% of dietary corn with DPP altered the diet’s nutritional composition, which was reflected in increased NDF content (from 27.60% to 30.00%) and elevated ADF content (from 15.76% to 17.91%). The promoting effect of low levels of persimmon tannins on the rumen microbial degradation of nutrients could potentially offset the negative impact of increased structural carbohydrate content on nutrient digestibility (Krause et al., 2005; Fitri et al., 2020).

Serum biochemical parameters and antioxidant status

The liver serves as the primary site for biological metabolism and is rich in transaminases, thereby playing an important role in physiological processes. Liver functions can be reflected by serum ALT or AST activity (Hu et al., 2014). In the present study, goats receiving the MDPP diet had lower serum AST activity than those receiving the CON diet. Calculations revealed that substituting 20% of the corn in the diet with DPP furnished goats with circa 4.4 g of persimmon tannins daily, corresponding to an approximate dosage of 83 mg/kg BW. Consistent with the results of the present study, Zou et al. (2012) reported that dietary supplementation with persimmon tannins (50 or 100 mg/kg BW) reduced serum AST and ALT activities in rats. The improved liver functions resulting from the DPP inclusion can be attributed to the increase in antioxidant functionality (Sobeh et al., 2017). Thus, the influence of dietary DPP on antioxidative indicators was further investigated in this study.

As end products of protein metabolism, BUN and CRE are excreted primarily by the kidneys. Serum BUN and CRE levels increase when kidneys undergo pathological changes (Li et al., 2017). Alechinsk et al. (2020) reported that tannins enhanced kidney function by alleviating oxidative stress. Additionally, excessive protein intake or synthesis and abnormal catabolism can increase serum BUN and CRE levels (Yamamoto et al., 2019). In the present study, the decreased serum levels of CRE and BUN may be due to improved kidney function and protein metabolism resulting from persimmon tannins in DPP. The persimmon tannins can form complexes with proteins, thereby reducing protein degradation in the rumen. It may lead to a decrease in the NH3–N absorbed by the rumen and entering the bloodstream to participate in the ornithine cycle, thus resulting in lower serum BUN levels (Kondo et al., 2014).

This study hypothesized that goats receiving MDPP diet would exhibit enhanced liver and kidney functions, potentially associated with increased antioxidant activity. The activity of CAT in serum reflects antioxidant capacity. Tian et al. (2022) demonstrated that dietary supplementation with condensed tannins increased CAT activity in the serum of Sichuan black goat kids. Previous study has indicated that DPP contains abundant tannins and polysaccharides, which possess antioxidant activity (Cui et al., 2023). The improvement in antioxidant functions of goats after replacing 20% corn with DPP in the diet may be due to the intake of persimmon tannins.

Rumen and fecal fermentation parameters

Rumen pH, VFAs and NH3–N are important indicators of the ruminal environment and digestive function. Approximately 70% of the energy for adult ruminants is provided by VFAs (Wei et al., 2021). The NH3–N generated primarily by the degradation of ingested protein nitrogen and nonprotein nitrogen in the rumen is the main nitrogen source for MCP synthesis. In the in vivo experiment, no difference NH3–N content was observed among different dietary treatments. The difference between the in vivo and in vitro experimental results may be explained by variations in the absorption of NH3–N into the blood from the rumen of goats under different dietary treatments, with the difference in serum BUN levels supporting this hypothesis.

Similar to the in vitro study, the acetate content and the A:P were increased by DPP inclusion. This result may be due to the changes in the nutritional composition of the diets and rumen microorganisms. Fiber degradation favors acetate production in the rumen, whereas starch degradation favors propionate production. The A:P has been shown to be positively correlated with the forage NDF content (Cao et al., 2021). Münnich et al. (2017) reported that a graded replacement of corn with molassed sugar beet pulp increased the acetate content and changed the microbial composition in an in vitro experiment. Similar to the results of the present study, the starch content decreased and the NDF content increased in the diet with the graded replacement of corn with molasses sugar beet pulp. Additionally, low concentrations of tannins may stimulate the growth of fiber-degrading bacteria in the rumen, thereby increasing fiber degradation and acetate production (Li et al., 2023). The hindgut is a fermentation compartment that functions to enable additional digestion of feed and absorption of nutrients not fully processed in the foregut. The hindgut is defined as the large intestine in the present study, which consists of the cecum, colon, and rectum. Fecal pH, VFAs, and NH3–N serve as important indicators that reflect hindgut fermentation status. However, in the present study, replacing 20% corn with DPP in the diet did not affect the fecal pH, VFAs, or NH3–N. This may be explained by the fact that the variation in nutrients reaching the hindgut was limited among dietary treatments, and thus insufficient to influence fecal fermentation.

Energy-nitrogen metabolism

Tannins in ruminant diets confer an advantage by positively influencing protein digestion (Tan et al., 2021). Tannins can reduce the degradation of dietary protein in the rumen by the formation of the tannin‒protein protection complex. Costa et al. (2021) reported that increased retained nitrogen and reduced urinary nitrogen excretion were observed in lambs fed diet supplemented with Acacia mearnsii condensed tannin extract. Previous study found that replacing 15% or 30% barley with grape pomace (with a tannin content of 5.51% DM) in dairy cow diets did not affect urinary nitrogen excretion or apparent nitrogen balance (Vinyard et al., 2021). In the present study, no differences in nitrogen excretion or retention were observed among the dietary treatments in goats. The inconsistencies in the improvements of nitrogen metabolism may be attributed to differences in tannin dosage and species, the level and composition of dietary nitrogen, and environmental conditions. The ruminal microbiota can synthesize MCP from ammonia and carbon skeletons, which are synchronized with an adequate supply of carbohydrates as the energy source (Rebelo et al., 2023). Although dietary treatments altered ruminal fermentation, no differences among treatments were detected in purine derivative excretion or the predicted MCP supply in the present study. The mechanisms underlying the effects of diet supplemented with DPP on nitrogen metabolism and MCP production in goats still require further exploration. Energy forms the basis of animals’ metabolic and productive activities. Urine, feces, and CH4 are the primary sources of energy loss in ruminants. In the present study, replacing 20% of corn with DPP in the diet did not alter energy loss through feces and urine in goats. This result indicates that substituting corn with DPP did not impact the energy metabolism (digestible energy) of goats, which corresponds to the absence of negative impacts on production performance. Several studies have reported reduced CH4 emissions when tannins are incorporated into ruminant diets (Abdelazeem et al., 2020; Berça et al., 2023). Vasta et al. (2019) proposed that tannins could directly inhibit CH4 production by reducing methanogenesis pathways and decreasing the activities of certain rumen microbes. Similarly, in the present study, substituting 20% of dietary corn with DPP resulted in a decrease in the relative abundance of bacteria associated with CH4 production (e.g., Patescobacteria). In vitro experiment demonstrated that replacing 20% of dietary corn with DPP effectively reduces CH4 production in goats. Unfortunately, the effect of substituting 20% of corn with DPP on CH4 emissions in goats was not evaluated via in vivo experiments. The effect and underlying mechanism of reducing CH4 production by replacing corn with DPP in goats need further exploration by in vivo experiments.

Rumen microorganisms

Microorganisms play a crucial role in transforming substrates into products. Several studies have reported changes in the rumen microbiota of ruminants fed diets containing tannins (Harun et al., 2017; Salami et al., 2018). In the present study, DPP rich in tannins was used to replace corn in the diet of goats. Replacing 20% of dietary corn with DPP induced a notable change in dietary composition, as evidenced by increased NDF content (from 27.60% to 30.00%) and elevated in ADF content (from 15.76% to 17.91%). Thus, alterations in the rumen microbiota that were observed after substituting 20% dietary corn with DPP may result from the change in the nutritional composition of the diet and the effects of persimmon tannins (Salami et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2022). In this study, the MDPP diet increased the relative abundance of several probiotics and VFAs-producing bacteria in the rumen. Moryella, belonging to the family Lachnospiraceae, has been found to synthesize VFAs through the fermentation of dietary polysaccharides and aromatic compounds (Adhikari et al., 2019; Sung et al., 2017). The increased abundance of Moryella in the rumen may be attributed to the high levels of polysaccharides and polyphenols in DPP, and this elevated Moryella abundance is conducive to VFAs production (Cui et al., 2023). Lachnoclostridium, a genus capable of fermenting lactate to VFAs and enhancing intestinal barrier function, exhibited higher relative abundance in the MDPP group (Guo et al., 2021). Izemoplasmatales has the ability to facilitate the further digestion and decomposition of extracellular DNA polymers, with the released nucleosides then serving as nutrient and energy sources for this taxon (Wasmund et al., 2021). In the present study, the increased relative abundance of Izemoplasmatales may be attributed to the antibacterial activity of persimmon tannins (Min et al., 2020). The antibacterial activity of persimmon tannins may further facilitate Izemoplasmatales’ utilization of nutrients from dead bacteria. The increase in the abundance of these bacteria may mainly account for the elevated contents of acetate and valerate and the higher A:P in the rumen.

The decreased ruminal microflora induced by replacing corn with DPP in the goats’ diet was caused by several bacteria with negative impacts. Kuroda et al. (2022) reported that Patescobacteria and the hydrogenotrophic methanogen Methanospirillum are involved in cross-domain symbiosis. Consistent with the results of CH4 production, the relative abundance of Patescibacteria was decreased by DPP inclusion in the present study. Yu et al. (2021) found that Clostridia_UCG-014 was negatively associated with liver injury. The decreased abundance of Clostridia_UCG-014 was in accordance with the improved liver function observed in the present study. Consistent with the results of dietary nutrient composition changes in the present study, Pang et al. (2022) reported that the relative abundance of Candidatus_Saccharimonas was higher in yaks fed a low-fiber diet than in those fed a high-fiber diet. Therefore, the decrease in the abundance of Candidatus_Saccharimonas was correlated with an increase in the content of dietary structural carbohydrates when 20% of corn in the diet was replaced with DPP (Chen et al., 2021). Nguyen et al. (2022) indicated that the relative abundance of the proinflammation-related Eubacterium_Xylanophilum was decreased. Therefore, the inclusion of DPP in the diet inhibited the proliferation of bacteria with negative effects, which may contribute to the improved general health of the animals.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study shows that, from the perspectives of nutrient digestibility and energy-nitrogen metabolism, it was proved that DPP can replace 20% of the corn in the diet of goats and has the potential to reduce CH4 emissions. This has important implications for sustainable livestock production.
