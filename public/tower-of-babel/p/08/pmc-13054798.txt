DNA methylation dynamics: patterns, regulation, and function
Authors: Jia Gwee, Wenwen Tian, Shuiming Qian, Xuehua Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As the crucial interface between the genome and the environment, the epigenome plays a key role in plant survival and thriving. Despite the identical DNA sequence in each nucleus of an individual, its interpretation by the cell is governed by both spatial and environmental contexts. The field of plant epigenetics is advancing rapidly with groundbreaking discoveries that are transforming our knowledge of how plants regulate gene expression, adapt to environmental changes, and uphold genomic stability. Recent technological advancements have also dramatically enhanced our ability to study the epigenome with precision, offering insights into its role at an unprecedented scale. Here, we highlight the latest findings focusing on the intricate balance of DNA methylation, the dynamic and multi-layered regulatory mechanisms, and the role and evolutionary significance of DNA methylation variations across diverse plant species. Understanding these variations in DNA methylation offers crucial insights into how plant epigenetic mechanisms regulate gene expression, genome organization, development, and responses to environmental changes.

Introduction

DNA methylation (referred to as 5-methylcytosine) is an epigenetic mark that plays a pivotal role in genome stability, gene regulation, development, and environmental responses across eukaryotes. Plant DNA methylation differs from that in animals in context, genomic location, and inheritance stability. In plants, DNA methylation occurs in three contexts, CG, CHG, and CHH (H = A, T, C), commonly found in transposable elements (TEs), whereas gene methylation mostly occurs in gene-body regions (gbM) characterized by extensive intragenic CG methylation [1,2]. DNA methylation is initiated by the de novo methyltransferase DOMAINS REARRANGED METHYLTRANSFERASE 2 (DRM2) through the RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway [1,2]. The methylation patterns are maintained through cell division by METHYLTRANSFERASE 1 (MET1) for CG, CHROMOMETHYLASE 3 (CMT3) for CHG, and CMT2 and DRM2 for asymmetric CHH methylation in heterochromatin and euchromatin, respectively. CHG and CHH methylation are maintained through the self-reinforcing loop between CMT2/3 and the histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9) methyltransferases, SUPPRESSOR OF VARIEGATION 3—9 HOMOLOG 4, 5, and 6 (SUVH4/5/6). DNA methylation can be maintained through generations in Cryptococcus neoformans and duckweed species Spirodela polyrhiza, which have lost de novo DNA methylation machinery [3,4], or can be recovered in an RdDM-independent manner in Arabidopsis after the loss of both non-CG methylation and H3K9 methylation marks [5], underscoring the importance of faithful DNA methylation maintenance in plants. Yet, natural populations of Arabidopsis thaliana showcase widespread methylation variations, pointing to the influence of environmental and stochastic factors [6]. Recent years have witnessed a surge in the study of DNA methylation variations in plants, unveiling intricate patterns and revealing important functions across a variety of species. In this review, we focus on the most recent advancements in plant methylation dynamics, delving into the functional implications of these variations through the lens of adaptation and evolution.

DNA methylation dynamics through plant life cycles

DNA methylation homeostasis is crucial for gene regulation throughout a plant’s life. For germline development, methylation reprogramming occurs through the de novo methylation pathway, where reduced CG methylation levels and the tissue-specific expression of CLASSY3 (CLSY3), a SNF2-like remodeling factor, facilitate the production of small-interfering RNAs (siRNA) in pollen and ovules to modulate methylation levels in gametes [7,8] (Figure 1a). In Arabidopsis, DRM2 establishes CHH methylation in egg cells, contrasting with central cells where the DNA demethylase DEMETER (DME) mediates extensive DNA demethylation (Figure 1a). Like Arabidopsis, rice egg cells exhibit enriched CHH and CHG methylation on TEs, while active demethylation reprograms genic methylation [9]. Compared to vegetative nuclei, Arabidopsis sperm cells have higher CG and CHG methylation but lower CHH methylation [10], whereas rice sperm cells show lower non-CG methylation in comparison to microspores [11]. Interestingly, an extensive two-wave methylation reprogramming has recently been reported in Marchantia polymorpha sperm cells: the first wave establishes non-CG methylation, followed by a sperm-specific increase in genic N4-methylcytosine necessary for fertility and embryonic development in liverworts [12]. These findings suggest species-specific methylation patterns for different reproduction strategies.

Like Arabidopsis, rice zygotes exhibit differential methylation between the two parental alleles. The paternal allele undergoes methylation reprogramming to align with maternal methylation patterns, ensuring proper gene expression in early embryos [13]. As the embryo develops, global CHH methylation levels increase in the heterochromatin to enforce TE silencing [14] (Figure 1b). In angiosperms, the endosperm exhibits significant methylation variation between parental alleles, which forms the basis of genomic imprinting in plants. Studies in Arabidopsis and rice have shown that the demethylation of maternal alleles leads to the expression of maternally expressed genes. This pattern is also observed in maize [15]. However, the silencing of maternal alleles for paternally expressed genes is more complex, involving both DNA methylation and histone 3 lysine 27 methylation deposited by the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) in different parental alleles (reviewed in Ref. [16]). This intricate regulation mechanism reflects the complexity of genomic imprinting and highlights the coordination of multiple epigenetic factors in controlling gene expression in the endosperms of angiosperms.

DNA methylation is crucial for plant development, and its complete loss leads to aberrant shoot meristem development and improper root cell differentiation [17]. The interaction between the transcription factor WUSCHEL (WUS) and the siRNA-loading protein ARGONAUTE 4 (AGO4) has recently been reported to promote DNA methylation that silences WUS-targeted meristem regulators, thereby facilitating shoot differentiation [18]. Conversely, high concentrations of superoxide in the shoot apical meristem activate the DNA demethylase REPRESSOR OF SILENCING 1 (ROS1) to demethylate the promoter of ARABIDOPSIS RESPONSE REGULATOR 12 (ARR12), which encodes an upstream transcription factor of WUS, leading to its activation and the maintenance of shoot stem cell identity [19]. Somatic tissues such as rosette and cauline leaves exhibit lower non-CG methylation levels, where DNA demethylases actively remove methylation from developmental genes, such as FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC), to regulate the vegetative-to-flowering transition [20] (Figure 1c). As plants age, somatic organs like leaves show a decrease in CG methylation levels in the pericentromeric regions, attributed to transcriptional repression of MET1 [21] (Figure 1d). This aging-associated methylation decay is also evident in the natural accessions of Arabidopsis [21], supporting the use of DNA methylation as a biomarker for aging. Unlike the annual Arabidopsis thaliana, perennial plants undergo more gradual senescence involving multiple rounds of the life cycle [22], presenting great opportunities for investigating the role of epigenetic regulation in plant aging.

Regulation of DNA methylation dynamics

DNA methyltransferases and demethylases are the primary genetic factors contributing to methylation variation in plants. The complete knockout of all methyltransferases in Arabidopsis leads to a total loss of DNA methylation [17] and the knockout of all four demethylases exhibits extensive DNA hypermethylation on genes and TEs [20]. Epigenetic recombinant inbred lines created by outcrossing met1 to wild-type plants showed not only patterns of methylation loss inherited from the mutant parent, but also de novo CG methylation on previously unmethylated sites in either parent [23]. Furthermore, the propagation of the DNA demethylase mutant, ros1, through multiple generations results in the transgenerational accumulation of methylation at loci different from the parental ros1 hypermethylated regions [24]. These studies underlie the methylation variations observed in natural populations, which originate from mutations in the DNA methylation machinery. Furthermore, transcription factors, such as the TESMIN/TSO-LIKE CXC domain-containing proteins 5 and 6 (TCX5/6), which are subunits of the master transcriptional repressor DREAM (DP, RB-like protein, E2F and MuvB core) complex that regulates cell cycle genes, repress the expression of MET1 and CMT3 to maintain a balanced DNA methylation state and facilitate coordinated cell division [25].

The distinct presence of these DNA methyltransferases in different plant species, particularly the chromomethylases that control non-CG methylation, contributes to their unique methylation landscapes [1,2,26] (Figure 2a). Structural and functional analyses have recently uncovered DNA deformation as a novel mechanism by which a plant DNA methyltransferase recognizes substrates to establish group-specific DNA methylation (Figure 2b). The catalytic loop of DRM2 intercalates into the DNA, causing substrate deformation that is accommodated by the adaptation of DRM2 target recognition domain to the enlarged DNA major groove through shape complementarity. This unique mechanism promotes DRM2 methylation on CHH substrates [27,28], in contrast to its mammalian counterpart, DNA METHYLTRANSFERASE 3A (DNMT3A), which recognizes substrates in a sequence-specific manner. In a manner akin to DRM2, CMT3 causes substantial DNA deformation upon DNA binding, creating a hydrophobic environment for targeting hemimethylated DNA and alleviating the constraint of the +1 flanking sequence of cytosine for CHG recognition [29,30] (Figure 2c). In higher angiosperm species, the key CHG-recognizing residue in CMT2 differs from CMT3, enabling CMT2 to specifically function in the CHH context [26] (Figure 2a—c). These studies underscore DNA shape-dependent recognition as a novel approach in regulating DNA methylation in plants.

DNA methylation is influenced by histone marks, notably the self-reinforcing loop through the interaction between SUVH4/5/6-mediated deposition of H3K9me1/2 and the non-CG methylation by CMT2/3. Recent findings reveal that histone H3 lysine 18 acetylation (H3K18ac), when adjacent to H3K9me2, affects CHG methylation by preventing the activation of CMT3 [29] (Figure 2c). Additionally, the interplay between CG and non-CG methylation affects methylation patterns, with residual CG methylation inherited from parents that lost heterochromatic non-CG methylation contributing to the recovery of CHG and CHH methylation in F1 progeny [31]. Methylation patterns vary among different plant species by their genome contents, where plants like rice that contain GC-rich clusters in their genomes show higher non-CG methylation in these regions [32]. These findings highlight the intricacy of epigenetic marks that outline the epigenomic landscape. Furthermore, the major nucleosome remodeler, DECREASE IN DNA METHYLATION (DDM1), plays an essential role in facilitating DNA methylation in the heterochromatin. Recent structural studies show a crucial role of DDM1 in maintaining heterochromatin identity during DNA replication. DDM1 remodels nucleosomes by first binding to the histone H4 tail and DNA, increasing DNA flexibility in the heterochromatic H2A.W-containing nucleosome. ATP hydrolysis then shifts DDM1 to a closed conformation, allowing it to slide through the nucleosome [33,34]. Additionally, DDM1 mediates H3.1 deposition for H3.1-containing heterochromatic nucleosome assembly, facilitating the removal of linker histone H1 and heterochromatic R-loops [35—37], thereby enabling methyltransferases to access these regions for the maintenance of DNA methylation (Figure 2d). Moreover, the differential expression of CLASSY remodeling factors contributes to tissue-specific methylation patterns through the production of 24-nucleotide siRNAs [8]. Thus, the chromatin environment plays a crucial role in mediating methylation deposition to maintain global methylation homeostasis across the genome.

Errors in DNA methylation maintenance during replication can lead to spontaneous and heritable methylation changes. These epimutations, while occurring spontaneously, are not random; genomic regions with high epimutation rates, known as ‘epimutation hotspots’, depend on the methylation levels and other heterochromatic marks within the region [38—41]. The rapid, spontaneous, and heritable accumulation of CG epimutations has allowed for the establishment of an evolutionary plant epigenetic clock to infer phylogenetic relationships among plants, including recently diverged species [42]. Furthermore, geographic origin also influences the methylation landscape, leading to variation among the Arabidopsis population [43] (the complex relationship between the environment and DNA methylation is reviewed in Ref. [44]). A comparison between Arabidopsis Col-0 and Cvi-0 accessions showed accession-specific TEs and structural variants that are heavily methylated by the RdDM pathway, suggesting their roles in driving methylation variation among the natural population [45]. Populational methylation variation is also observed in the H3K9 demethylase locus, INCREASED BONSAI METHYLATION 1 (IBM1), where methylation on its 7th intron is crucial for mediating its expression and balancing H3K9 methylation. For example, some accessions, such as Cnt-1, exhibit low levels of methylation on this 7th intron along with high ectopic non-CG methylation on gbM genes [46], suggesting that the crosstalk between non-CG methylation and H3K9 methylation could play a significant role in methylation variation.

Evolutionary significance of DNA methylation dynamics

Genome stability and evolution

Methylation variations may act as a buffer to maintain the chromatin landscape homeostasis for proper genome regulation. In the Arabidopsis population, natural accessions with lower heterochromatic methylation levels show a switch from euchromatic gene-body methylation to heterochromatic TE-like methylation, leading to transcriptional silencing [38]. Additionally, hotspots of spontaneous epimutations in Arabidopsis mutation-accumulation lines correlate with chromatin states of gbM genes that maintain a steady-state intermediate level of methylation [40], highlighting the evolutionary importance of balanced methylation. In Arabidopsis, the assembly of Col-0 centromeres showed that they are densely packed with CEN180 repeats while the adjacent pericentromeric regions are heavily methylated to prevent centromeric meiotic DNA double-strand breaks [47]. This protective mechanism underscores the importance of methylation in genomic stability. Intriguingly, a recent epigenomic engineering approach utilizes the protective feature of DNA methylation to silence the ectopic insertion of CEN180 repeats on targeted genes, showcasing a promising way of generating locus-specific epialleles [48].

Whole-genome duplication events are prevalent in plant evolution, and recent research on gymnosperms has found that many gene duplicates show methylation variations associated with differential expression. Copies of genes with CG hypomethylation or CHG/CHH hypermethylation often exhibit reduced transcriptional levels, suggesting a possible role of DNA methylation in limiting gene redundancy following genome duplication [49]. In angiosperms, gene duplicates arising from whole-genome duplication events also exhibit CG methylation differences between duplicate copies, whereas duplicates from single-gene duplication events show non-CG methylation variation, suggesting that DNA methylation fine-tunes gene dosage after genome duplication [50]. Interestingly, CMT2, which evolved from a CMT3-like ancestor around the emergence of angiosperms, has sub-functionalized to maintain CHH methylation alongside CMT3 [26], potentially reinforcing heterochromatic silencing with genome expansion in angiosperms.

Phenotypic diversity and adaptation

Among natural plant populations, methylation variations are widespread, reflecting the cumulative effects of genetic, stochastic, and environmental influences. These methylation changes can be inherited across generations, resulting in epialleles that represent different methylation states of a gene within the population and may confer phenotypic diversity. Recently, three natural accessions with CMT2 mutations in the methyltransferase domain have been identified to affect CHH methylation, suggesting that methylation variation contributes to environmental adaptation [26,51]. For example, Lhasa-0, a natural Arabidopsis accession from the Tibetan Plateau, has a stop-gain mutation in the CMT2 methyltransferase domain, leading to lower genome-wide CHH methylation compared to Col-0. This mutation enables Lhasa-0 to exhibit heat and UV-B tolerance similar to that of the cmt2 mutant [26], and interestingly, a portion of UV-induced CHH hypomethylation occurs at CMT2 targets [52], underscoring the role of CMT2-mediated CHH methylation in stress adaptation and resilience [26]. Interestingly, DNA methylation profiling of Arabidopsis accessions from various altitudes in the west Himalayas reveals significant methylation variations, with high-altitude populations displaying DNA hypomethylation, which may provide a crucial adaptive advantage to environmental challenges [53].

Natural Arabidopsis accessions also show substantial methylation variations in various groups of non-coding RNA genes, typically exhibiting lower CG methylation and higher non-CG methylation levels than protein-coding genes, but lower overall methylation in all three contexts compared to TEs [54]. The geographically linked methylation variations in non-coding regions result in phenotypic variations that enable adaptation to the environment, such as the epiallele of miR157a, which regulates flowering in the Arabidopsis population [54]. A recent epigenome-wide association study of cotton fiber traits identified CG methylation variations among cotton accessions on protein-coding and long non-coding RNA genes [55], accentuating the role of DNA methylation variation in phenotypic diversity.

Beyond natural selection, domestication has also been a powerful force in shaping plant epigenomes. A recent GWAS of maize inbred lines has demonstrated that a single-nucleotide polymorphism in the 3’ untranslated region of the maize CMT3 ortholog, ZMET2, results in methylation variations that affect the number of husk layers [56]. These methylation changes are likely a product of domestication pressures [56]. A recent comparison of cultivated rice accessions revealed that DNA hypomethylation on the acquired cold tolerance 1 (ACT1) promoter gains cold tolerance, aiding the northward range expansion of rice [57]. The finding underscores the role of epigenetic mechanisms in adaptation, showcasing how environmental factors can drive evolutionary changes through heritable epigenetic modifications. Similarly, studies comparing wild and domesticated rice reveal that domestication leads to decreased methylation at specific domesticated genes, potentially altering their expression to favor traits selected by humans [58]. Conversely, research on lettuce shows that domestication increases DNA methylation levels [59], highlighting that these methylation variations are species-specific and often tied to the traits targeted during domestication.

Speciation and divergence

It has long been hypothesized that spontaneous deamination of 5mC, which results in thymine formation, could lead to DNA mismatches or single-nucleotide polymorphisms, potentially serving as a genetic diversity source for speciation [60]. The slow pace of evolution can make it challenging to study how DNA methylation impacts speciation and divergence. However, recent advancements are illuminating this area. A study of maize inbred lines revealed a surprising connection between inbreeding depression and increased DNA methylation [61]. Remarkably, inhibiting this methylation can reverse the effects, directly linking methylation variation to genetic diversity within a population [61]. TEs, which can rapidly invade and be removed from centromeres, add further complexity. These elements introduce sequence diversity, potentially driving speciation [62]. Moreover, the structural polymorphism of the satellite AtCEN178 arrays in the centromere promotes DNA methylation and heterochromatic silencing to suppress crossovers during meiotic recombination and maintains chromosomal stability [63]. Together, these discoveries provide compelling evidence that DNA methylation variations within the centromeres significantly influence chromosome segregation and meiotic recombination. Such variations could enhance intra-species diversity and potentially drive the speciation process.

Conclusions and perspectives

This review has highlighted recent advancements in our understanding of DNA methylation homeostasis, from the dynamic regulatory mechanisms to the functional and evolutionary roles of DNA methylation variations in plants. DNA methylation variations act as natural epigenetic variants that enable plants to adapt to different environments, potentially more swiftly than sequence variants due to the rapid accumulation of epimutations over time. These epigenetic modifications are crucial for plants to quickly respond to and thrive under varying environmental conditions. However, the intricate influences of complex environmental factors on these methylation variations remain largely elusive, necessitating further investigations. Exploring the evolutionary significance of DNA methylation variations across different plant lineages will provide insights into how epigenetic mechanisms have shaped plant diversity and adaptation. Future studies that incorporate environmental factors, epimutation rates, and phylogenetic relationships of closely related plant species have the potential to reveal both conserved and divergent methylation patterns along with their functional implications.

Future research should also focus on the concept of epigenetic memory and transgenerational inheritance of DNA methylation marks. Understanding how stable these modifications are across generations and under different environmental conditions will have profound implications for plant breeding and conservation strategies.

Epigenome engineering presents a promising avenue for generating specific epialleles in fast-propagating species with various epimutation rates. This technique can be utilized to dissect the functional impact of DNA methylation variations on species evolution and divergence. Development of advanced epigenomic tools, such as single-cell methylome analysis, will enable deeper insights into the spatial and temporal dynamics of DNA methylation across different plant tissues and developmental stages. Leveraging knowledge of DNA methylation mechanisms for crop improvement represents a significant opportunity. Epigenome engineering could be utilized to develop crops with enhanced traits, such as increased resistance to abiotic and biotic stresses, improved growth rates, and better nutritional value.

In conclusion, while the process of evolution unfolds slowly, modern cutting-edge research is uncovering how DNA methylation can act as a powerful force in shaping genetic diversity and driving evolutionary change. Future research integrating environmental factors, evolutionary history, and advanced epigenome editing techniques will deepen our understanding of these processes.
