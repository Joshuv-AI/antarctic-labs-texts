AI-enhanced clustering of mine tailings using Geostatistical data augmentation and Gaussian mixture models
Authors: Nasser Madani, Sergei Sabanov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe reprocessing of mine tailings presents a valuable opportunity to recover critical raw materials essential for advancing green technologies and achieving sustainable resource management. However, conventional mineral resource estimation techniques—designed for primary ore deposits with well-defined geological domains—are difficult to apply to tailings, which typically lack geological structure and exhibit highly irregular spatial patterns. To address this challenge, we propose an artificial intelligence-based framework that integrates geostatistical data augmentation with Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) to define compact, spatially contiguous estimation domains in mine tailings. The original geochemical data obtained from exploratory drillholes at a tailing deposit in East Kazakhstan were spatially augmented using Ordinary Kriging over a regular 3D grid, enhancing both spatial resolution and continuity of the dataset. Multiple GMM covariance structures are evaluated using spatial and statistical performance metrics. Results from this tailing dataset demonstrate that the augmented GMM approach, particularly with tied covariance, yields substantial improvements in spatial coherence (Moran’s I = 0.52 vs. 0.29) and cluster compactness (Silhouette Index = 0.59 vs. 0.41), compared to conventional borehole-only clustering. The proposed method offers a robust and scalable solution for AI-assisted domain modeling in Earth system residues and supports the design of selective reprocessing strategies aligned with sustainable resource development.Keywords: Mine tailings, Geostatistical data augmentation, Gaussian mixture model (GMM), ClusteringSubject terms: Geochemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The global transition toward electric vehicles and renewable energy technologies has intensified the demand for critical raw materials1. As primary sources become increasingly constrained, mine tailings—particularly from sulfidic ores—have emerged as a promising secondary resource. Valorizing these previously discarded materials can not only contribute to metal recovery but also support land reclamation, reduce environmental risks, and limit the need for new mining operations2–4. Effective reprocessing strategies, however, require robust spatial modeling approaches to understand and predict the distribution of valuable elements within these heterogeneous and geologically unstructured deposits.

In conventional mining practice, mineral resource estimation is typically conducted within geologically defined domains and relies on structured workflows, including domain modeling, variography, and spatial interpolation5. These estimation domains are often delineated based on rock type, or grade thresholds under the assumption of spatial stationarity (i.e., homogeneity of statistical properties) within each domain6. This hierarchical estimation approach7 is well suited to primary ore deposits but is difficult to apply to mine tailings, which generally lack clear geological stratification or consistent depositional controls.

As a result, many tailings studies bypass domain definition altogether and apply estimation or simulation techniques directly across the entire dataset for the grade of interest8–11. While operationally convenient, this can obscure geochemically distinct zones, resulting in suboptimal resource modeling and reduced precision for tailings reprocessing plans.

Clustering, as one of the core unsupervised machine learning techniques within artificial intelligence (AI), has been increasingly applied to define estimation domains, mainly in primary deposits12,13. Algorithms such as hierarchical clustering14, K-means15, spectral clustering16, and Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)17,18 offer flexible, data-driven frameworks for pattern recognition. However, their outputs often suffer from spatial fragmentation, producing disjointed clusters that are impractical for mine planning and geometallurgical applications13. To improve spatial coherence, recent advances in AI have introduced spatially regularized clustering methods that incorporate spatial coordinates or autocorrelation structures into the clustering process19–21. Parallel progress in deep generative models—such as generative adversarial networks (GANs) for subsurface geology22 and StyleGAN-based augmentation of petrographic data23—further illustrates the broader movement toward AI-driven Earth system modeling24. Despite these developments, their application to mine tailings remains limited, in part due to the high nugget effect and unstructured spatial continuity typical of such deposits. These challenges underscore the need for novel AI-based approaches that can enhance spatial pattern detection while preserving geochemical integrity.

Data augmentation, a widely used technique in artificial intelligence, generates additional or modified samples to improve model robustness and performance. Within clustering, augmentation has been applied through geometric transformations25, feature-space interpolation26, noise injection27, and generative models28,29. However, these strategies seldom account for spatial continuity, a critical requirement in geoscientific and Earth system applications. In recent years, GAN-based workflows have been explored in geosciences to generate 3D geological models conditioned on boreholes22 and to augment limited petrographic datasets23, demonstrating their ability to capture spatially realistic structures from sparse data. Building on this foundation, our study introduces geostatistical augmentation to enrich borehole information, offering a domain-specific and interpretable pathway toward spatially coherent clustering in mine tailings.

In this study, we present a geostatistically augmented clustering framework that combines Ordinary Kriging with GMMs to generate spatially contiguous and geochemically coherent clusters in a sulfidic tailings deposit. By enriching the original borehole data with kriged estimates over a regular 3D grid, the method leverages both spatial structure and probabilistic clustering to define estimation domains suitable for block modeling and reprocessing. This approach demonstrates how artificial intelligence can be adapted to spatial resource modeling in complex Earth systems, contributing to more sustainable and data-driven resource recovery solutions.

Kriging vs. Cokriging under different sampling patterns

In the case of a heterotopic sampling pattern, where some variables are missing at certain locations, the Ordinary Kriging procedure described above can be replaced by Ordinary Cokriging to account for the spatial cross-correlation between primary and secondary variables and thereby improve the estimation of missing values.

In contrast, under a homotopic sampling pattern, where all variables are available at every sampling location, cokriging produces results identical to those of kriging. This is because the inclusion of secondary variables provides no additional advantage when their sampling support fully coincides with that of the primary variable. Therefore, applying cokriging is redundant in the homotopic case, and Ordinary Kriging suffices.

Covariance structures and their influence on clustering

A central focus of this study is to examine how the choice of covariance structure ​ affects the geometry and spatial consistency of the resulting clusters in a GMM17,35. The following four configurations are tested:

Full covariance:

Full covariance:

Each component has its own general covariance matrix. This is the most flexible configuration, allowing clusters to take on arbitrary elliptical shapes with independent orientation and scaling. It is expected to perform well in heterogeneous and anisotropic spatial domains but is computationally demanding and prone to overfitting in high dimensions.

Tied covariance:

Tied covariance:

All clusters share a common covariance matrix. This imposes a global constraint on the shape and orientation of all clusters, which may reduce model complexity while still capturing some correlations between features. It is particularly useful when clusters have similar geometries but vary mainly in position and density.

Diagonal covariance:

Diagonal covariance:

Each cluster has its own diagonal covariance matrix, which assumes that the variables are uncorrelated but permits different variances across dimensions. This structure balances flexibility with computational efficiency and is suitable when the data dimensions contribute independently to cluster separation.

Spherical covariance:

Spherical covariance:

All features within each cluster are assumed to be isotropic, i.e., clusters are spherical with equal variance along all directions but can differ in size across clusters. This is the most restrictive model, favoring simplicity and robustness at the expense of spatial realism, particularly in anisotropic or geologically complex settings.

By systematically evaluating the clustering outcomes under these different covariance assumptions, this study aims to quantify the trade-offs between model complexity, spatial coherence, and clustering accuracy in geostatistical environments where spatial variability and feature correlation are significant.

Case study

This study focuses on a legacy tailing deposit located in Eastern Kazakhstan, generated from the historical processing of polymetallic and gold-bearing ores between the 1950s and late 1970s. Due to confidentiality agreements, the specific name and location of the deposit are not disclosed. The tailings were produced from two distinct volcanogenic massive sulphide ore bodies with contrasting mineralogical and textural characteristics. The processed ores primarily contained sulphide minerals such as pyrite, sphalerite, galena, and chalcopyrite, with associated concentrations of gold, silver, copper, and zinc36. Post-depositional transformations, including sulphide oxidation and metal leaching, have led to the formation of secondary minerals such as smithsonite, jarosite, limonite, chalcocite, and covellite, often observed as rims or coatings on primary sulphide grains37. These complex mineralogical features reflect both the variability of the original ore feed and the influence of environmental processes over time.

The tailings were hydraulically deposited into a flat, alluvial-style impoundment constructed in natural terrain depressions, disrupting regional water circulation38. The dam was progressively built up using washed tailings, resulting in a stratified internal structure governed by particle size segregation. Coarse sand fractions (0.56–2.0 mm) were deposited near the discharge point, while finer particles (silt, clay, and ultra-fine sands < 0.1 mm) settled in distal and deeper zones. Granulometric analyses indicate that fine-grained materials dominate, comprising over 50% of the total volume, with coarse sands contributing only 2–8%38,39. The average tailings thickness is 17.25 m, reaching a maximum of 60 m near a central trough. The impoundment rests on a base of fine-grained calcareous loam up to 50 m thick, which, due to its high clay content, serves as an effective barrier to infiltration38. This stratified and mineralogically diverse system presents a challenging yet valuable case for geostatistical modeling and unsupervised clustering.

Exploratory data analysis

The dataset, collected from 90 vertical exploratory drillholes in this tailing deposit, includes 2 m-length composited assays for Ag, Au, Cu, Pb, and Zn. Despite the regular sampling grid, the maps reveal strong local-scale heterogeneity, with highly scattered and erratic elemental distributions. Au, Cu, and Zn display abrupt fluctuations and minimal spatial continuity, while Ag and Pb show slightly smoother patterns yet still lack stratification or consistent lateral trends. Directional correlograms were also calculated; however, they did not yield meaningful structures, and therefore we proceeded with the omni-directional correlogram analysis. The correlograms confirm that the spatial structure of these elements is dominated by short-range variability. The correlation drops almost immediately after lag zero, reaching near-zero values within short distances and fluctuating around zero thereafter. This rapid decay indicates that samples influence only their immediate neighborhood, while observations even a short distance apart are essentially uncorrelated. The lack of any stable positive correlation beyond short lags means there is no detectable long-range continuity or periodic structure in the data. Such behavior is typical in tailings deposits, where successive hydraulic deposition events and reprocessing practices create abrupt changes and irregular layering, preventing the development of broader spatial structures that are commonly observed in primary mineralization.

To examine the multivariate relationships among the geochemical elements and prepare the dataset for subsequent clustering analysis, it is essential to account for the compositional nature of the data. Geochemical datasets are compositional because the elemental concentrations represent parts of a whole and carry relative, not absolute, information40. This is particularly relevant in the context of tailings, where elemental contents are residual outcomes of ore processing and typically reflect constrained proportions within a closed mass balance. As a result, conventional multivariate analyses may yield misleading results due to spurious correlations and the constant-sum constraint40. To enable valid interpretation and clustering, the dataset must be transformed using an appropriate compositional method such as the additive log-ratio (alr) transformation40. The alr transformation was selected over the centered log-ratio (clr) and isometric log-ratio (ilr) approaches because it provides a straightforward and interpretable framework for subsequent principal component analysis (PCA) and clustering. Unlike clr, which introduces singularity due to the closure constraint, and ilr, which requires constructing orthonormal balances that may obscure direct geochemical interpretation, alr yields independent log-ratio coordinates while maintaining a clear link to the original compositional parts. This makes alr particularly suitable for exploratory multivariate analysis in applied geoscience contexts.

To prepare the dataset for this transformation, all geochemical values were first brought to a consistent unit of measurement: elements originally reported in parts per million (Ag and Au) were converted to percent to match Cu, Pb, and Zn. Following this standardization, a filler variable was introduced to close the composition, representing the remaining portion of the material not accounted for by the five assayed elements. This step ensures the compositional vector sums to a constant total (e.g., 100%), which is a prerequisite for valid log-ratio transformations. Without the filler, the dataset would remain an open composition, potentially introducing distortions or biases in subsequent multivariate and clustering analyses.

The correlation matrix of the alr-transformed geochemical variables reveals two distinct behavioral groups within this tailing dataset (Fig. 2A). Cu, Pb, and Zn exhibit moderate positive correlations (e.g., Cu–Zn: 0.56, Cu–Pb: 0.40, Pb–Zn: 0.48), suggesting these base metals tend to co-occur and may share similar processing or depositional patterns. In contrast, Ag and Au show weaker or negative correlations with the base metals, indicating more independent behavior. The modest correlation between Ag and Au (0.28) may reflect occasional co-enrichment, while their very low correlation with Zn and Cu suggests distinct deportment in the tailings.

While a correlation matrix provides pairwise relationships between variables, it does not capture the combined multivariate structure or reveal how multiple elements vary together across principal directions of variance. To overcome this limitation, PCA was applied to better understand the joint variability and grouping of the geochemical elements in a reduced-dimensional space41.

Although alr transformation addresses the compositional nature of geochemical data by removing the constant-sum constraint, it does not guarantee normality or equal scaling across variables. Therefore, before applying PCA, the alr-transformed variables were further subjected to Gaussian transformation34 to improve their suitability for multivariate analysis. PCA, being a linear technique, is sensitive to differences in variable distribution and scale; non-Gaussian features can distort the resulting components and bias interpretations. Gaussian transformation ensures that all variables approximate a normal distribution with comparable variance, thereby enhancing the stability and interpretability of PCA results.

The PCA of the Gaussian-transformed alr variables provides a low-dimensional representation of the geochemical relationships in this tailings deposit. As shown in Fig. 2B, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) together account for approximately 71.8% of the total variance. The pink points in the biplot represent the individual drillhole samples projected into this two-dimensional space, while the blue vectors denote the loadings of the geochemical variables. The scatter of samples illustrates their distribution with respect to the first two components. The variables are moderately represented in this space, with loadings generally around 0.5–0.6, indicating partial rather than dominant contributions. Cu, Pb, and Zn project in a similar direction (PC1) and show some degree of association, while Ag and Au project along different axes (PC2), reflecting their relative independence from the base metals. Although the separation between these two groups is not strong, it is still sufficient to indicate a meaningful distinction between precious metals (Ag and Au) and base metals (Cu, Pb, Zn). The nearly orthogonal orientation of the two groups suggests contrasting geochemical tendencies, providing a practical basis for distinguishing these two main geochemical associations in the dataset and highlighting their relative independence. Overall, the PCA biplot highlights partial structure in the data and underscores the complex and heterogeneous nature of the tailings material.

To evaluate the performance of clustering under varying spatial support, the five Gaussianized alr-transformed variables were subjected to clustering using a geostatistical data augmentation approach, as described in the methodology section. A key challenge in this approach is determining the optimal spatial resolution of augmented data points for effective clustering. To address this, a sensitivity analysis was performed using multiple 3D augmentation grids, where synthetic points were regularly spaced along easting, northing, and elevation directions (e.g., 10 m × 10 m × 2 m or 20 m × 20 m × 10 m). Clustering was conducted using both K-means and GMMs with four covariance configurations: Full, Spherical, Tied, and Diagonal. Each clustering method was tested under three scenarios: using only augmented grid data, augmented grid data combined with borehole data, and borehole data only. To generate the augmented dataset described in the methodology, Ordinary Kriging was applied to estimate the Gaussianized alr-transformed variables at each point of the 3D augmentation grid, conditional on the values observed at the borehole locations. Ordinary Kriging was selected over Ordinary Cokriging because the sampling design follows a homotopic pattern, where all variables are equally available at every borehole location. Under such conditions, cokriging reduces mathematically to kriging, providing no additional benefit. Therefore, Ordinary Kriging suffices for augmentation in this case.

Although the raw elemental concentrations did not exhibit meaningful spatial correlation at the original scale of the data (as shown in Fig. 1), the Gaussianized alr-transformed variables revealed clearer structures. For this purpose and to implement Ordinary Kriging, experimental variograms were computed separately for each transformed variable along the easting, northing, and elevation directions, which were identified as the principal axes of spatial anisotropy in this tailings deposit. The variograms consistently showed a pattern of geometric anisotropy, with the strongest spatial continuity in the easting direction, weaker continuity in the northing direction, and the shortest continuity vertically (Fig. 3). The very limited vertical correlation is typical of many tailings’ deposits, reflecting their hydraulic deposition in thin, successive layers. While each layer tends to be relatively homogeneous internally, abrupt changes occur between layers, restricting vertical continuity. To better reflect local spatial variability and reduce computational cost, a moving neighborhood was employed, where kriging estimates at each grid point were based only on a subset of nearby borehole samples within a defined search radius.

Since most clustering algorithms, including K-Means and GMM, require the number of clusters to be specified a priori, it is essential to determine a reasonable cluster count before applying these methods. To ensure consistency across all clustering approaches and data configurations, the Elbow method15 was used exclusively on the borehole (BH) dataset, which represents the original, unaugmented data. This prevents the selection of number of clusters (k) from being influenced by any synthetic spatial structures introduced through data augmentation. Figure 2C shows the Elbow method comparison for K-Means and GMM under all four covariance types (full, tied, diagonal, spherical). For K-Means, the normalized inertia—a measure of within-cluster variance—decreases rapidly up to , after which the rate of improvement slows, suggesting an optimal cluster number. Inertia is specific to K-Means and cannot be used with GMM, as GMM is based on a probabilistic framework rather than distance minimization. Instead, Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) is used to assess GMM models, which balances goodness of fit with model complexity. The BIC curves similarly show their lowest or inflection point around k = 3, especially for the full and diagonal covariance types. This consistent pattern supports the choice of three clusters as a balanced and robust configuration, which was then fixed for all comparative clustering analyses.

Figure 4 compares the performance of clustering methods in terms of spatial contiguity and cluster compactness, using four evaluation metrics: Moran’s I (global spatial autocorrelation), Geary’s C (local spatial association), Silhouette Index (cluster quality), and Calinski-Harabasz Index (cluster compactness and separation). Moran’s I and Geary’s C are commonly used in spatial statistics to measure spatial autocorrelation, where higher values of Moran’s I indicate stronger global spatial structure, and lower values of Geary’s C suggest stronger local spatial association42,43. The Silhouette Index evaluates how well an object lies within its cluster by considering both intra-cluster cohesion and inter-cluster separation44. The Calinski-Harabasz Index, also known as the Variance Ratio Criterion, assesses cluster compactness and separation by comparing the between-cluster dispersion with within-cluster dispersion45. Higher values of Moran’s I, Silhouette Index, and Calinski-Harabasz Index indicate better clustering performance in terms of spatial contiguity and compactness, while lower values of Geary’s C reflect stronger local spatial coherence within clusters.

Across all four metrics, clustering methods that incorporate augmented grid data achieve better performance than those based solely on the sparse borehole dataset. This improvement does not imply disregarding boreholes; rather, the borehole measurements remain the conditioning data for augmentation and serve as the reference for validation. Since the augmented model is generated directly from the borehole observations through geostatistical interpolation, it is fully compatible with the original data and preserves their information content while providing a denser and smoother representation that enhances cluster separation in the multivariate space.

Among the GMM variants, GMM-Tied and GMM-Spherical consistently yield superior results across both spatial and statistical criteria. Notably, the configuration using a 20 m × 20 m × 10 m augmentation grid without BH data, combined with the GMM-Tied model, achieves the most favorable combination of high spatial autocorrelation, low Geary’s C, and a well-defined clustering structure. This setup offers an optimal balance between cluster compactness and spatial contiguity, making it the most effective approach for delineating meaningful geo-clusters in this tailing dataset.

However, while borehole data provide direct and reliable observations, their integration with augmented grid data can be problematic. Borehole samples are typically sparse and unevenly distributed, which can introduce localized biases and interfere with the smoother spatial patterns established by the more uniformly spaced augmented data. These inconsistencies may result in reduced cluster regularity and compactness—especially when borehole values deviate significantly from surrounding kriging estimates due to local anomalies or sampling bias. Additionally, combining borehole data with interpolated grid values can produce sharp spatial transitions, disrupting cluster continuity and reducing both spatial and multivariate cohesion. These issues help explain why configurations excluding BH data often yield more consistent and spatially coherent clustering outcomes.

Conversely, using only BH data without augmentation is also suboptimal. The limited spatial coverage and clustered distribution of borehole samples fail to capture the broader spatial variability of the deposit, particularly in unsampled or sparsely sampled regions. This lack of resolution often leads to fragmented or poorly defined clusters that may not reflect the true geochemical structure. Moreover, in the context of compositional or high-dimensional data, a small sample size increases the risk of overfitting and reduces the statistical robustness of the clustering results. Without augmentation, the clustering algorithm works on an incomplete and spatially biased representation of the deposit, undermining the formation of compact and contiguous domains—two essential characteristics for meaningful tailing modeling and interpretation.

Accurately quantifying the volume of clusters is essential because clustering results are not only descriptive but also directly tied to practical applications such as estimating the tonnage of material available in each domain, predicting metallurgical behavior, and supporting mine planning or tailings reprocessing decisions. Without a volumetric comparison, clusters remain abstract groupings at the sample scale, and their real impact on resource modeling cannot be evaluated. Thus, moving from point-based cluster assignments to three-dimensional volume estimation provides the necessary link between statistical clustering outcomes and their engineering significance.

The stacked bar charts in Fig. 5 were produced by first dividing the deposit into uniform 5 × 5 × 5 m3 blocks. Each block was assigned to the nearest sample point (NN assignment), and the categorical cluster label at that sample point—derived from each clustering method—was transferred to the block. By counting the number of blocks per cluster and multiplying by the block volume, the total cluster volumes were calculated. These volumes were then visualized as stacked bars to enable direct comparison between methods. This approach was selected because simple cluster assignments at sample locations cannot capture volumetric proportions across the deposit, whereas block-based volume calculations reflect the three-dimensional spatial extent of each cluster and highlight potential differences caused by the clustering method.

The results indicate that while the overall deposit volume remains constant across methods, the partitioning of this volume among clusters varies. In some methods (e.g., GMM-Full), the distribution is more uneven, with one cluster dominating the volume. This occurs because GMM-Full allows each cluster to have its own unrestricted covariance matrix, which often leads to overfitting and exaggeration of local variability. In contrast, methods such as GMM-Diagonal or K-means show more balanced outcomes, but their simplifying assumptions may not fully capture the correlations present in the data. Among all approaches, GMM-Tied provides the most balanced distribution of cluster volumes, suggesting that it yields a more stable and geologically consistent domain partitioning. This can be explained by the fact that GMM-Tied constrains all clusters to share the same covariance structure, thereby reducing sensitivity to noise and producing results that are more consistent with the overall spatial data distribution. From a practical perspective, such differences are critical for tailings reprocessing and resource modeling, as they directly affect the estimated tonnage of each domain and, consequently, downstream economic and metallurgical evaluations. Moreover, these volumetric comparisons confirm that clustering methods capable of capturing directional spatial relationships lead to more coherent and meaningful domain delineations.

Investigating the distribution of elements in their original scales within each cluster is essential to assess the geochemical coherence of the clustered groups and to evaluate their relevance for potential reprocessing strategies in tailings. By understanding how key elements (in their original scale) vary across clusters, one can determine whether the clustering reflects meaningful compositional differences that could support selective recovery or geometallurgical planning. As shown in Fig. 6, the distribution of Ag, Au, Cu, Pb, and Zn in their original scales within each cluster remains highly consistent across all clustering methods, with minimal variation in medians and interquartile ranges. This indicates that the internal composition of each cluster is not sensitive to the choice of clustering algorithm, covariance structure, or whether BH data were used, underscoring the robustness of the approach. In all methods, Cluster 3 (blue) consistently shows elevated levels of Ag and Au, Cluster 1 (green) is enriched in Cu, Pb, and Zn, and Cluster 2 (red) typically contains lower or intermediate levels of all elements. These trends align closely with the multivariate associations observed in Fig. 2A and B, where Cu, Pb, and Zn form a correlated group, while Ag and Au display distinct behavior and load in a different direction in the PCA biplot. Together, these results confirm that the clustering approach successfully captures meaningful compositional patterns in the tailings material, providing a robust framework for geometallurgical characterization and reprocessing decision-making.

Examining the 3D spatial distribution of clusters is critical for assessing the practical applicability of clustering outcomes in the context of tailings reprocessing. Beyond statistical validity, the usefulness of a clustering model depends on its ability to generate spatially coherent and continuous domains that can be translated into block-level processing strategies.

Figure 7 illustrates the 3D spatial distribution of clusters obtained from different clustering approaches, including those based solely on borehole (BH) data (Fig. 7B and E) and one based on augmented data over a 20 m × 20 m × 10 m grid without inclusion of BH data (Fig. 7A). Among these, the GMM-tied clustering result using the augmented grid (Fig. 7A) exhibits the most compact and spatially continuous cluster structure. The clusters are distributed in a more stratified and laterally connected manner, with fewer isolated or dispersed points across the tailing volume. This spatial organization suggests that the inclusion of regularly spaced augmented data—independent of the BH dataset—allows the algorithm to capture underlying geochemical gradients and enhance spatial smoothness in the classification.

Conclusion

This study introduced a novel framework that integrates geostatistical data augmentation with GMM to define estimation domains in sulfidic mine tailings—an environment where traditional geological modeling is often infeasible. The approach addresses the limitations of classical clustering algorithms, which typically yield spatially fragmented and operationally impractical clusters due to the sparse and unstructured nature of borehole data in tailings.

By augmenting the original dataset using Ordinary Kriging over a regular 3D grid, the spatial resolution and continuity of the data were significantly improved, enabling the generation of compact and geochemically coherent clusters. Among various tested configurations, the GMM with tied covariance applied to a 20m × 20m × 10 m augmentation grid (excluding borehole data) consistently outperformed other methods. It achieved a Moran’s I of 0.52 and a Geary’s C of 0.38, compared to 0.29 and 0.62, respectively, in the borehole-only clustering. The Silhouette Index improved from 0.41 to 0.59, and the Calinski-Harabasz Index from 256.3 to 398.7, indicating enhanced cluster compactness, separation, and spatial coherence. Importantly, the geochemical consistency of the clusters across methods was maintained, with distinct elemental signatures emerging for base metals, precious metals, and low-grade zones.

The spatial continuity and stratification of the clusters generated from augmented data demonstrate clear advantages for block-level resource modeling and selective reprocessing strategies. These clusters provide a realistic foundation for geometallurgical planning, including the identification of zones optimal for specific recovery methods such as flotation, leaching, or blending.

The proposed framework based on kriging offers a scalable and effective alternative for delineating estimation domains in tailings deposits and other geologically unconstrained environments. It reduces reliance on dense drilling campaigns and enables early-stage resource evaluation with enhanced spatial robustness.

However, the choice of augmentation grid size in kriging plays a critical role in shaping clustering outcomes because it directly controls the balance between capturing local variability and ensuring robustness. Finer grids propagate more detailed variability from kriging predictions, sharpening local contrasts but also amplifying noise and producing fragmented clusters. In contrast, coarser grids smooth these variations by approximating block averages, which emphasize broader spatial trends and yield more stable clusters, albeit at the cost of obscuring small-scale geological features. This effect is closely tied to the inherent smoothness of kriging: at small grid sizes, kriging interpolates fine-scale variability, while at larger grid sizes, it enforces stronger averaging. The results illustrate a clear trade-off—very fine grids risk overfitting, while very coarse grids risk oversimplification—whereas intermediate grids achieve the best compromise. By filtering out noise while preserving essential contrasts, they lead to compact, spatially coherent clusters that are both statistically robust and geologically meaningful, which is essential for reliable domain definition and practical applications in tailings reprocessing.

Future research could explore the use of multivariate geostatistical simulation instead of kriging to better quantify uncertainty and capture local variability within the augmented dataset. Simulation has the advantage of generating multiple equally probable realizations that reproduce spatial heterogeneity more faithfully, thereby providing a framework for uncertainty assessment. However, unlike kriging, simulation does not smooth the data, and this lack of smoothing may reduce the spatial contiguity of clusters, which is often desirable for defining practical geological domains. Thus, while simulation offers a pathway for uncertainty quantification, further research is needed to investigate how it can be integrated with clustering in a way that balances variability representation with the requirement for geologically coherent and contiguous clusters.

Another avenue is to integrate the clustering framework with geometallurgical performance models, allowing domain definitions to be directly linked to metallurgical recovery and economic value. Additionally, incorporating soft constraints such as mineralogy, elevation, or environmental parameters, and adopting semi-supervised or spatially regularized clustering techniques, could further improve the interpretability and operational relevance of the resulting domains. These extensions would broaden the applicability of the method across diverse tailings types and support more informed and sustainable resource recovery decisions.

Beyond the selected case study, the proposed framework has the potential to be applied to a wide range of tailings materials and even primary deposits where domain boundaries are poorly defined. For example, carbonate-rich or oxide-dominant tailings may present different geochemical associations, while fine-grained residues often exhibit stronger spatial continuity challenges. Similarly, certain primary deposits lack clear geological domains due to complex mineralization or overprinting processes. In such contexts, the integration of geostatistical augmentation with clustering provides a flexible means of defining compact and contiguous estimation domains, offering a scalable approach to improve resource modeling and support selective reprocessing or extraction strategies.

In this study, a single fixed random seed was used consistently throughout the clustering analyses to ensure reproducibility of the results. While clustering algorithms such as K-Means and GMM can in principle be sensitive to initialization, we expect that the impact of seed choice is limited in this application, given the spatial augmentation and geostatistical constraints built into our workflow. Nonetheless, further research is needed to systematically evaluate the effect of varying seed numbers on clustering stability, particularly in complex tailings datasets where initialization may interact with spatial continuity and geochemical variability.
