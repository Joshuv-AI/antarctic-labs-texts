Relations between ethnic‐racial identity statuses and critical consciousness among Latine adolescents
Authors: Kristia A Wantchekon, Esther Burson, Olga Kornienko, Norma J Perez‐Brena, Maciel M Hernández, M Dalal Safa, Thao Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEthnic‐racial identity (ERI) development reflects the processes through which adolescents learn about their ethnic‐racial background (i.e., exploration) and develop clarity about what it means to them (i.e., resolution). Research on ERI has been critiqued for not examining connections between ERI and broader social beliefs, such as critical consciousness (i.e., individuals' level of reflection about social oppression, motivation to take action against it, and the extent to which they take such action). When examining connections to ERI, research underscores the importance of considering individuals' patterns of exploration and resolution in tandem (i.e., ERI status) rather than analyzing them in isolation. Accordingly, the current study utilized latent profile analysis to examine how ERI status profiles were associated with dimensions of Latine adolescents' critical consciousness. Cross‐sectional data came from Latine adolescents (n = 338, M age = 16.30, SD: 1.11; 44% boys, 53% girls, 3% another gender) attending a U.S. southwestern high school. Findings revealed three ERI status profiles: Foreclosed (n = 132; lower exploration and higher resolution), Diffuse (n = 59; lower exploration and resolution), and Achieved (n = 147; higher exploration and resolution). Profiles did not significantly differ in critical reflection. The Achieved profile reported the highest critical motivation, the Diffuse profile reported the lowest, and the Foreclosed profile fell between them. The Achieved profile reported higher critical action than the other two profiles, which did not significantly differ from one another. Findings suggest that among Latine adolescents, engaging in ERI development is connected to feeling more agency to change unjust systems and engaging in more action against such systems, but higher ERI development does not necessarily reflect deeper engagement in questioning those systems (i.e., critical reflection).Keywords: critical consciousness, ethnic‐racial identity, latent profile analysis, Latine youth

Identity development is in vibrant flux during adolescence, and in racialized societies like the US, ethnic‐racial identity (ERI) development is an integral facet of adolescents' broader identity developmental processes (Williams et al., 2020). ERI development includes the processes through which adolescents learn about their ethnic‐racial background (i.e., exploration) and develop clarity about what it means to them and its role in their lives (i.e., resolution; Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2014). A wealth of research has illustrated how adolescents' engagement in ERI exploration and sense of resolution with respect to their ERI promotes their psychological well‐being and academic adjustment (Rivas‐Drake et al., 2014), underscoring the role of ERI as a central developmental competency. However, research on ERI has also been critiqued for not adequately examining connections between ERI and adolescents' broader social beliefs (Williams et al., 2020), highlighting a need to connect youths' ERI developmental processes with their social attitudes and behaviors such as their critical consciousness.

Critical consciousness includes youths' level of reflection about societal inequity, how motivated they feel to act against it, and how much action they have taken to counter it (Heberle et al., 2020). Given that ethnic‐racial discrimination and racism are key facets of the complex systems of social oppression that permeate the U.S. sociopolitical context, and youth are often motivated to grapple with their ERI as they encounter these systems, it follows that the extent to which youth have engaged with, reflected on, and developed clarity about their ERI would have implications for their critical consciousness (Mathews et al., 2020).

This may be especially true of ethnoracially minoritized youth in the U.S., like Latine youth. Latine youth today must navigate a climate of heightened and increasing anti‐Latine rhetoric (Barajas‐Gonzalez et al., 2018; Montoya Andarade & Serrano, 2025) and ongoing implementation of anti‐immigrant policies that often target and villainize Latine communities (e.g., limiting paths to citizenship, reduced protections for undocumented residents; Ee & Gándara, 2020). Directly and vicariously experiencing anti‐Latine discrimination and rhetoric can prompt Latine youth to explore their ERI and lead to questions regarding how sociopolitical systems view and treat members of their ethnic‐racial group (Gomez Alvarado et al., 2024; Umaña‐Taylor & Rivas‐Drake, 2021).

A small but growing body of research explores the intersection between ERI and critical consciousness, focusing primarily on the complex patterns between ERI content dimensions and critical consciousness. For example, higher centrality (i.e., one's ethnic‐racial background is a more important part of one's broader identity) has been linked to higher critical reflection and motivation but lower critical action (Bañales et al., 2020; Kiang et al., 2021); whereas lower public regard (i.e., a more negative perception of how one's ethnic‐racial group is viewed in society) has been linked to lower critical reflection and motivation but higher critical action (Kiang et al., 2021). Research on ERI process and critical consciousness is more limited, with some focus on the individual links between ERI exploration or resolution and critical consciousness (e.g., Bañales et al., 2020; Mathews et al., 2022). It may be useful to instead examine how patterns of ERI exploration and resolution (i.e., ERI statuses; Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2014) relate to critical consciousness, as examining each dimension's relation to elements of critical consciousness separately may mask important nuance.

Theory and empirical research underscore that the extent to which ERI exploration and resolution relate to youths' positive identity beliefs and social behaviors is closely tied to their overall ERI status (e.g., Yip et al., 2019), and that youth who jointly report higher exploration and resolution benefit from better psychosocial adjustment (e.g., Wantchekon & Umaña‐Taylor, 2021). This work highlights the utility of person‐centered approaches [e.g., latent profile analysis (LPA)], which are often advantageous over variable‐centered approaches as they have higher statistical power to detect effects, are able to detect nonlinear effects, and do not require the testing of higher‐order interaction terms (Meyer & Morin, 2016). Person‐centered approaches are also consistent with calls for more holistic examinations of the complexities of adolescents' lived experiences (e.g., Byrd & Ahn, 2020; García Coll et al., 1996; Suzuki et al., 2021). Using a person‐centered approach to examine exploration and resolution in tandem is superior to simply combining exploration and resolution scales into a composite score, as the creation of profiles allows for more concrete comparison of differences across ERI statuses. A person‐centered approach also does not conflate youth who are high on only one dimension of exploration or resolution the way that a single composite score might. Further, several studies have found evidence of distinct ERI statuses in different samples of Latine youth (e.g., Douglass & Umaña‐Taylor, 2017; Romero et al., 2018; Toomey et al., 2013) and found differences in how these statuses relate to Latine youths' adjustment. Accordingly, to expand our understanding of how ERI and critical consciousness, two important developmental competencies, interrelate among Latine youth, the current study used LPA to examine how ERI statuses were associated with differences in Latine adolescents' critical consciousness.

Identifying ethnic‐racial identity profiles

ERI is a multifaceted construct that includes ERI process and ERI content (Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2014). ERI process reflects the developmental process through which youth come to understand their ERI and what it means to them (i.e., exploration and resolution), and ERI content reflects the beliefs and attitudes youth ascribe to their ERI (e.g., positive or negative affect, public regard, centrality; Williams et al., 2020). The current study focuses on ERI process.

Our conceptualization of ERI process builds on theory on psychosocial development (Erikson, 1968), which casts identity development as the fundamental task of adolescence, a process through which youth explore a variety of identity domains (e.g., religious identity, vocational identity) and come to understand who they are and want to become. Successfully engaging in this process is thought to be integral to adolescent adjustment, as it provides a more assured sense of self and a stronger foundation for navigating interpersonal relationships in young adulthood and beyond (Erikson, 1968). When applied to the identity domain of ERI, ERI exploration captures the extent to which youth have actively examined their ethnic‐racial heritage, and ERI resolution captures the extent to which youth feel clarity about and stability in their ERI and what it means to them (Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2014). Consistent with Erikson's work, research suggests that engaging with and coming to a sense of clarity with respect to one's ERI supports young people's positive development (Williams et al., 2020), including Latine youth (Alvarado & Ricard, 2013).

Research on ERI statuses (e.g., Yip, 2014) draws on Marcia (1994) to operationalize ERI process into four categories: ERI diffusion, moratorium, foreclosure, and achievement. ERI diffusion represents lower exploration and resolution—those in diffusion have neither examined nor developed a clear sense of self with respect to their ERI and its role in their lives. ERI moratorium represents lower resolution but elevated exploration—those in moratorium have more deeply examined their ERI, but they remain unsure about its meaning in their lives. ERI foreclosure represents lower exploration but higher resolution—these individuals feel clear about their ERI and what it means to them but have not done much self‐driven exploration (e.g., their clarity might come from their family's socialization and messaging rather than their own exploration). ERI achievement represents higher exploration and resolution in tandem—such individuals have dedicated time to reflect on and explore their ERI and have a clear and stable sense of what it means to them. In prior person‐centered research on ERI statuses with Latine samples, there has been consistent evidence of the presence of ERI diffusion, foreclosure, and achievement (e.g., Toomey et al., 2013). Although there is some evidence of ERI moratorium among this demographic (e.g., Douglass & Umaña‐Taylor, 2017), most studies have not found evidence for it (e.g., Douglass & Umaña‐Taylor, 2015; Romero et al., 2018; Toomey et al., 2013; Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2004). Thus, though four ERI status categories are theorized, three (i.e., ERI diffusion, foreclosure, and achievement) are most commonly observed in Latine samples.

Notably, theory suggests that ERI achievement is the identity status most supportive of well‐being, as the well‐reasoned identity clarity that it provides supports adolescents' broader psychological adjustment (Umaña‐Taylor, 2016). Prior person‐centered research examining patterns of ERI among Latine youth aligns with this supposition, illustrating how ERI achievement supports several indicators of academic and psychological adjustment (Meca et al., 2023; Wantchekon & Umaña‐Taylor, 2021) and buffers against the negative relation between ethnic‐racial discrimination and engagement in risky behaviors (Toomey et al., 2013).

Critical consciousness

A wealth of research has shown how adolescents' ERI development relates to positive adjustment (e.g., Rivas‐Drake et al., 2014), but research examining how ERI relates to adolescents' social beliefs and behaviors is more limited (Williams et al., 2020). One important set of youths' social beliefs and behaviors to consider in relation to their ERI development is their critical consciousness, as psychological research on the effects of oppression highlights that critical consciousness is a key developmental asset that empowers marginalized youth to fight for social change (Christens et al., 2016; Diemer et al., 2016; Heberle et al., 2020).

Critical consciousness is typically conceptualized as three separate but related constructs. First, critical reflection encompasses adolescents' analysis and rejection of social inequity; second, critical motivation, also referred to as internal political efficacy, captures both the desire to make social change and belief in one's ability to do so; and finally, critical action, also called critical behavior, refers to actions taken to dismantle or reform oppressive systems (Diemer et al., 2015, 2021; Heberle et al., 2020; Watts et al., 2011). Components of critical consciousness have been linked to several aspects of adjustment among Latine youth, including greater community engagement (Pérez‐Gualdrón & Helms, 2017), greater prosocial competencies (Christens & Dolan, 2011), better defined career goals (Diemer et al., 2010; Diemer & Blustein, 2006; Diemer & Hsieh, 2008), better academic outcomes (Luginbuhl et al., 2016; McWhirter & McWhirter, 2016), and enhanced well‐being (Maker Castro et al., 2022). These findings illustrate and underscore the positive role critical consciousness can play in Latine youths' development, but more research is needed to understand the links between ERI development and critical consciousness, as they are both important developmental assets (Mathews et al., 2020). Additionally, as members of an ethnoracially minoritized and marginalized social group, Latine youths' meaning making related to their ERI and their associated ERI status likely connects to their engagement with and development of the different dimensions of critical consciousness (Kiang et al., 2021; Mathews et al., 2020). As such, a better understanding of the links between ERI and critical consciousness may provide important insight into how identity processes connect to marginalized youth resisting oppression and engaging in social change work.

Linking ERI statuses to critical consciousness

In considering how different ERI statuses may relate to critical reflection, motivation, and action among Latine adolescents, it may be helpful to first consider how ERI exploration and resolution might individually contribute to these different dimensions of critical consciousness. Theory puts forth that ERI exploration supports critical reflection and action, whereas ERI resolution supports critical motivation (Mathews et al., 2020). With respect to ERI exploration, it is proposed that the process of ethnoracially minoritized youth grappling with their ERI requires some examination of the racialized systems of oppression that impact their ethnic‐racial group, an important element of critical reflection (Mathews et al., 2020). Theory also suggests that exploration may promote youths' engagement in critical action in support of their ethnic‐racial group (Mathews, 2023). Given that ethnic‐racial discrimination often sparks periods of intense ERI exploration (Rivas‐Drake & Umaña‐Taylor, 2019), and that ethnoracially minoritized youth often experience ethnic‐racial discrimination more frequently during adolescence (Williams et al., 2020), it follows that adolescents' meaning making around these experiences and their own identity would include engagement in critical reflection and action (Mathews et al., 2020), as well as critical motivation, as critical action and motivation are often linked (Diemer & Rapa, 2016; McWhirter & McWhirter, 2016). Empirical work indicates that higher ERI exploration is associated with increases in critical action among Black young adults (Mathews et al., 2022), adding further credence to these proposed relations, but more empirical research is needed to extend these findings to the other dimensions of critical consciousness, examine these processes among Latine adolescents, and consider exploration in conjunction with resolution.

With respect to ERI resolution, it is proposed that ERI resolution encourages critical motivation because youth who have come to a sense of clarity, stability, and commitment to their ERI may feel more motivated to participate in collective action in support of their ethnic‐racial group (Mathews et al., 2020). This proposal implicitly assumes that youth with higher ERI resolution have committed to more positive ERI identity beliefs, as one would likely be most motivated to take action in support of a group that they are proud to be a part of. Although higher ERI resolution does not guarantee that one feels proud or positively about their ethnic‐racial background, Latine adolescents with the highest levels of resolution are much more likely to report more positive, rather than negative, ERI beliefs (Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2004; Wantchekon & Umaña‐Taylor, 2021), so this connection between resolution, a more positive ERI, and critical motivation may hold among Latine youth. Some empirical work supports the proposed connection between ERI resolution and critical motivation, finding that greater increases in ERI resolution were associated with higher civic beliefs, an element of critical motivation, among Black and Latine adolescents (Bañales et al., 2020). But again, more research is needed that expands our understanding of how resolution relates to the other dimensions of critical consciousness and considers resolution in conjunction with exploration.

Taken together, the highlighted theoretical notions and empirical findings illustrate the ways in which ERI exploration and resolution may individually relate to dimensions of critical consciousness. Examining differences in critical consciousness as a function of overall ERI status would provide deeper insights about the roles of exploration and resolution, and their interactions, in critical consciousness development.

The current study

Research on ERI has long examined its association with psychosocial adjustment, but as we consider how to best foster youths' social and civic engagement in an increasingly sociopolitically polarized society, it is also critical to explore how ERI relates to youths' social attitudes and behaviors (Williams et al., 2020). While an emerging body of literature, described above, explores links between critical consciousness and dimensions of ERI content, and has begun to consider how aspects of ERI process connect to critical consciousness, no research to date has examined how different ERI statuses relate to all three components of critical consciousness, despite theoretical and empirical support that higher levels of exploration and resolution may individually be associated with higher levels of different dimensions of critical consciousness (e.g., Mathews et al., 2020). By examining how ERI statuses relate to critical consciousness, we can illuminate how youths' meaning making about their own identities facilitates their introspection about society at large, their motivation to change society for the better, and their commitment to being a part of that change. Accordingly, the current study identified ERI statuses in a sample of Latine adolescents and examined whether youth in these profiles differed in their critical reflection, motivation, and behavior.

Consistent with prior theory, we expected to find 3–4 profiles of ERI statuses, as prior research with Latine samples has found evidence of all four theorized identity statuses (i.e., achieved, diffuse, moratorium, and foreclosed; e.g., Douglass & Umaña‐Taylor, 2017) or just three (i.e., without moratorium; e.g., Toomey et al., 2013). With respect to how each ERI status profile would relate to each dimension of critical consciousness, we had several hypotheses. We hypothesized that ERI achievement (i.e., higher exploration and higher resolution) would likely be positively associated with all three dimensions of critical consciousness, as youth would benefit from the synergy of higher exploration and higher resolution when thinking about and acting on social change initiatives. Conversely, we hypothesized that ERI diffusion (i.e., lower exploration and lower resolution) may be related to lower levels of all three critical consciousness dimensions, as these youths' lack of introspection about their ERI and lack of commitment to their ERI may mean they are similarly disengaged in critical reflection, motivation, and action. If it emerged within our sample, we hypothesized that ERI moratorium (i.e., higher exploration and lower resolution) could offer both benefits and risks for critical consciousness development. Namely, the identity exploration of ERI moratorium could be tied to increased critical reflection, as youth may be grappling with their ERI and related inequities in tandem (Mathews et al., 2020); however, given evidence that moratorium sometimes makes youth more sensitive to identity threats like discrimination (Yip et al., 2019), the lower confidence of ERI moratorium might undercut critical motivation and critical action. Finally, for ERI foreclosure (i.e., lower exploration and higher resolution), we hypothesized that youths' lack of exploration might be associated with lower levels of critical reflection due to their lack of exploration, but the confidence stemming from their higher resolution could encourage more critical motivation and critical action, though not to the extent of those in ERI achievement, who would benefit from the synergy between their higher levels of exploration and resolution.

METHOD

Participants and procedure

Cross‐sectional data came from Latine adolescents who attended a predominately Latine (89%) urban high school in the U.S. Southwest. The school community was roughly 60 miles from the U.S. Mexico border. Data were collected in April 2023. The research team had university and school district institutional review board approval and obtained both parental consent and student assent. Students 18 years or older were able to provide consent. Students completed an online survey using Qualtrics during one class period, with school staff and research assistants present to answer any questions. Teachers distributed parent consent forms and were given $30 gift certificates as a thank you. Students were compensated $10 for returning a signed consent form, irrespective of whether parents provided consent. Students who completed the survey during school hours received snacks. To meet our recruitment target, we continued recruiting over the summer. In total, 49 students were recruited during the summer. Because these additional students completed the survey during their own time and no snacks were provided, they received a $20 incentive via an online payment method (e.g., cashapp). Different tiers of compensation are consistent with best practices in the field on effectively recruiting harder to reach populations (Knight et al., 2009). Students who completed the survey during the spring versus the summer did not significantly differ on any of the variables of interest.

The current analysis focused on students who identified as Latine (n = 340). The full sample included 170 additional adolescents who identified as White (n = 20), Black (n = 7), Asian American (n = 14), Native American (n = 2), and Multiracial (n = 127). However, given the small size of many of the other ethnic‐racial groups, the ethnoracial diversity of the Multiracial group, and evidence that critical consciousness may operate differently for youth of different ethnic‐racial backgrounds (Godfrey et al., 2019; Godfrey & Burson, 2018), we chose to limit our sample to Latine youth.

In the analytic sample, 44% of participants identified as boys, 53% as girls, and 3% as another gender identity. Thirty percent were in 9th grade, 38% in 10th grade, and 32% in 11th grade. Five percent of participants were 1st generation (i.e., they and their parents were born abroad), 35% were 2nd generation (i.e., they were born in the U.S., but both parents were born abroad), 29% were 2.5th generation (i.e., they were born in the U.S and had one U.S. born parent and another born abroad), and 31% were 3rd generation (they and their parents were born in the U.S.). The sample was primarily of Mexican heritage (94%). Sixty‐four percent of participants' fathers had a GED or higher, and 11% of participants' fathers had a Bachelor's degree or higher. Seventy‐five percent of participants' mothers had a GED or higher, and 26% of participants' mothers had a Bachelor's degree or higher. When asked about their family's financial background, 4% of participants said their family did not have enough money to get by, 47% reported that their family had just enough to get by, 36% reported that they only had to worry about money for fun and extras, and 13% reported that their family never worried about money. Though we did not collect data on who participants lived with, we did collect information on who they viewed as their primary caregiver. Among those who responded, 80% noted that their mother was their primary caregiver, 9% reported it was their father, 3% said both mother and father, 1% said grandparents, 4% said siblings, and 3% responded someone else (e.g., no one).

Measures

Ethnic‐racial identity developmental processes

The Ethnic Identity Scale‐Brief (EIS‐B; Douglass & Umaña‐Taylor, 2015) was utilized to assess ERI exploration and resolution. Exploration had a 3‐item subscale (e.g., “I have participated in activities that have taught me about my ethnicity”), and resolution had a 3‐item subscale (e.g., “I am clear about what my ethnicity means to me”). Higher values indicated higher exploration and resolution. All scales were measured on a 4‐point Likert scale with endpoints of not at all (1) to very well (4). Cronbach's α for exploration (.78) and resolution (.86) were adequate.

Critical consciousness

Three dimensions of critical consciousness were examined: critical reflection, critical motivation, and critical action. The 3‐item critical reflection scale was adapted from Rapa and colleagues' short critical consciousness scale (CCS‐S; 2020) and included such items as “Certain racial or ethnic groups have fewer chances to get ahead.” The CCS‐S was developed with a racially diverse sample of high schoolers in the Midwest and validated with a racially diverse, predominately low socioeconomic status sample of high schoolers in the Southeastern U.S., and has been demonstrated to be invariant across race/ethnicity (Black or African American, Hispanic or Latine, White, and two or more races), grade‐level, and gender (Rapa et al., 2020). The 5‐item Measure of Adolescent Critical Consciousness (MACC; McWhirter & McWhirter, 2016) agency subscale (e.g., “I am motivated to try to end racism and discrimination”) was used to capture critical motivation, and the MACC 3‐item critical behavior subscale (e.g., “I am involved in activities or groups that promote equality and justice”) was used to capture critical action. Both subscales were developed and validated with Latine youth (McWhirter & McWhirter, 2016). All three subscales were measured on a 4‐point Likert‐type scale with endpoints of strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (6). The Cronbach's α for critical reflection (.89), critical motivation (.87), and critical action (.82) were all adequate.

Sociodemographic variables

Adolescents self‐reported their gender (0 = Girls or Another Gender Identity, 1 = Boys) and age. These two variables were tested as sociodemographic controls as some research suggests that they may be associated with differences in critical consciousness among youth (Godfrey et al., 2019; Singh et al., 2020). For ethnic‐racial background, participants responded to whether they identified as White, Black, Hispanic/Latino, Asian American, American Indian or Alaska Native, Pacific Islander, Middle Eastern or North African, or Other. Participants could pick as many as they wanted and then, if they chose multiple, they were coded as Multiracial. Adolescents who chose Hispanic/Latino as their only background were included in analyses.

Analytic approach

Profiles of exploration and resolution

First, we examined descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations among study variables. Then, to identify patterns of ERI exploration and resolution among study participants, we conducted LPA in Mplus 8.5 (Muthén & Muthén, 2018). We estimated models using robust maximum likelihood (MLR), as it is robust to nonnormality and estimates model parameters with less bias than other maximum likelihood approaches (Bakk & Vermunt, 2016). Missingness across the study variables was low, ranging from less than 1%–3.5%. Two participants who were missing data on all the profile indicators and outcome variables were excluded from analyses, as the model could not estimate their missing data.

In LPA, researchers begin by estimating a model with one profile and then continue to add profiles until the models no longer converge (Johnson, 2021). Researchers may also stop adding models if theory suggests a certain range of profiles or profile sizes become too small (Johnson, 2021). In the current study, we fit 1–4 profile solutions in alignment with identity status theory and research, which highlights up to four potential configurations of exploration and resolution (Diffuse, Moratorium, Foreclosed, Achieved; Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2014).

Picking the best fitting model required balancing several factors, including multiple indices of model fit. Fit indices included the Bayesian information criteria (BIC, Schwarz, 1978), the sample size adjusted Bayesian information criteria (ABIC; Sclove, 1987), the adjusted Lo–Mendell–Rubin test (LMRT; Lo et al., 2001), and the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT; Nylund et al., 2007). For the BIC and ABIC, lower values represented better model fit (Vermunt & Magidson, 2002). The LMRT and the BLRT tests compared profile solutions with k and k‐1 profiles (e.g., a 3‐profile and 2‐profile solution), and a significant value suggests the model with more profiles is a statistically significantly better fit to the data. We also examined model entropy and the smallest profile's sample size. Entropy is a measure of classification accuracy that reflects how distinct the profiles within a profile solution are (Celeux & Soromenho, 1996); a value closer to one reflects better entropy and values above .8 are ideal (Tein et al., 2013). For profile sample size, research advises that the smallest profile should not fall below 5% of the sample, as this increases the likelihood of Type II error (McLarnon & O'Neill, 2018) and suggests over‐extraction of classes (Johnson, 2021; Nagin, 2005; Nasserinejad et al., 2017). We weighed these models' fit indicators and characteristics with their conceptual interpretability based on prior theoretical and empirical work in the area (Collins & Lanza, 2010).

After choosing the final model, we then examined whether two sociodemographic controls, age and gender, were associated with the probability of profile membership and should thus be accounted for in subsequent analyses. For this, we used R3STEP in Mplus, the recommended approach for examining potential predictors of profile classification (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014; Vermunt, 2010). This approach takes into account profile classification error while conducting multinomial logistic regressions that capture the probability of being in one profile over another based on a given variable. If age or gender were not associated with profile membership, they were removed from further analyses for model parsimony, as there would not be substantive demographic differences among the profiles that could be driving potential differences in critical consciousness.

Profile association with critical consciousness dimensions

To explore whether the profiles of ERI exploration and resolution differed in their level of critical reflection, critical motivation, and critical action, we used the Bolck, Croon and Hagenaars (BCH) method (Bakk & Vermunt, 2016; Bolck et al., 2004), the recommended approach for examining how profile membership is associated with auxiliary outcome variables (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2020; Bakk & Vermunt, 2016). The approach estimates profiles and outcome mean differences within one model, considering the likelihood of an individual's profile assignment in its estimation of the outcome means for each profile (Bakk et al., 2013; Bakk & Vermunt, 2016). For context, in LPA, everyone is assigned their most likely profile membership, but could, for example, have an 80% likelihood of being assigned to Profile 2 and a 20% likelihood of being assigned to Profile 1 based on their characteristics. The BCH approach takes this into account when computing the outcome means for a given profile—this individual would contribute to both profiles’ outcome means, but more so to Profile 1's. After the model estimates each profile's outcome mean, it compares them through a series of Wald tests, including individual tests of whether pairs of profile outcome means can be constrained to be equal (Bakk et al., 2013; Bakk & Vermunt, 2016).

Transparency and openness

We report how we determined the analytic sample, the data we excluded, and all the study measures. The study design and analytic plan were not preregistered. The study data, analytic code, and materials are not publicly available but can be made available from the corresponding author by reasonable request.

To identify profiles of ERI exploration and resolution (i.e., identity statuses) in our sample, we examined and compared the 1, 2, 3, and 4‐profile model solutions, ultimately choosing the 3‐profile solution. The 3‐profile solution reported a better BIC and ABIC, as well as a significant LMRT and BLRT when compared with the 2‐profile solution. The 3‐profile solution also neatly mapped onto three of the four commonly theorized configurations of exploration and resolution (i.e., Diffuse, Foreclosed, Achieved). Though the 4‐profile solution had comparably stronger fit statistics than the 3‐profile solution, in the 4‐profile solution, the smallest profile size dropped below 5% of the full sample, reflecting potential over‐extraction of classes (Nasserinejad et al., 2017). In addition, the 4‐profile solution split the smallest profile into two groups with below‐average levels of exploration and resolution (i.e., two Diffuse groups) rather than producing the fourth commonly theorized configuration of ERI process, Moratorium (i.e., higher exploration, lower resolution; Phinney & Ong, 2007). Prior research examining identity statuses among Latine adolescents has found evidence of a three rather than four profile solution with the same three identity statuses as our 3‐profile solution (Douglass & Umaña‐Taylor, 2015; Toomey et al., 2013; Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2004), which offered further support that the 3‐profile solution was worth retaining. We thus chose the 3‐profile solution as our final model (Table 2 and Figure 1). In the 3‐profile solution, each profile significantly differed from the other two with respect to their estimated mean of exploration and resolution, in alignment with theory. The Achieved profile reported the highest exploration and resolution (m Exploration = 3.13 m Resolution = 3.86), the Diffuse profile reported the lowest exploration and resolution (m Exploration = 1.82 m Resolution = 1.81), and the Foreclosed profile fell between the two (m Exploration = 2.41 m Resolution = 3.00).

Profile association with critical consciousness dimensions

We then examined identity status profile differences in critical consciousness dimensions using the BCH method (Figure 2). For critical reflection, the three profiles' estimated means did not significantly differ (m Diffuse = 3.26, m Achieved = 3.38, m Foreclosed = 3.55). For critical motivation, the three profiles all significantly differed from one another: the Achieved profile reported the highest critical motivation (m = 5.14), the Diffuse profile reported the lowest (m = 4.29), and the Foreclosed profile fell between them (m = 4.76). The critical reflection items differed from the critical motivation and action items in that they explicitly asked participants about gender and class injustice alongside racial injustice. DISCUSSION

ERI and critical consciousness development are essential processes for today's youth to engage in as they make meaning of themselves and the complex world around them (Mathews, 2023). For Latine youth in the U.S. who are navigating an increasingly sociopolitically hostile society, exploring their ERI, developing a sense of clarity about what it means to them, engaging in critical reflection, developing critical motivation, and implementing critical action offers opportunities for strength, resilience, and well‐being in the face of oppression (Pérez‐Gualdrón & Helms, 2017; Wantchekon & Umaña‐Taylor, 2021). In many ways, ERI and critical consciousness are naturally linked at a conceptual level (Mathews, 2023; Mathews et al., 2020), but limited research has examined their overlap, particularly through person‐centered analytic approaches. The current study utilized LPA to identify ERI status profiles (i.e., patterns of ERI exploration and resolution) among a sample of Latine adolescents and tested how these ERI status profiles related to differences in adolescents' critical reflection, critical motivation, and critical action. Our findings offer notable insights to the work calling for more examination of the social and behavioral implications of ERI development (e.g., Williams et al., 2020). Our work also contributes to research seeking to understand how youth's introspection about and commitment to their ERI, an important but singular aspect of their global social identity, relates to their reflection about, feelings of agency to address, and engagement in action against broader social ills (Mathews et al., 2020).

ERI status profiles

Our initial analyses focused on identifying ERI status profiles (i.e., patterns of ERI exploration and resolution) within the sample. Theory and prior research support four ERI status profiles: achievement (high exploration and high resolution), moratorium (high exploration and low resolution), foreclosure (low exploration and high resolution), and diffusion (low exploration and low resolution; Douglass & Umaña‐Taylor, 2017; Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2014). Contrary to these expectations, three of these identity status profiles (ERI diffusion, foreclosure, and achievement) emerged among our sample. Overall, 17% of participants were in the Diffuse profile, 44% were in the Achieved profile, and 38% were in the Foreclosed profile, suggesting the vast majority of the sample reported higher levels of ERI resolution, and a sizable minority reported higher ERI exploration and ERI resolution in tandem.

As previously noted, profiles emerging that are reflective of ERI diffusion, foreclosure, and achievement but not ERI moratorium is inconsistent with theory, but consistent with prior empirical work with Latine youth, which has also found evidence of three ERI status profiles but no ERI moratorium (e.g., Douglass & Umaña‐Taylor, 2015; Romero et al., 2018; Toomey et al., 2013). One reason that an ERI moratorium profile often does not emerge among Latine youth could be the higher levels of family ethnic socialization that Latine youth often report (Umaña‐Taylor et al., 2009). Higher levels of family ethnic socialization are associated with both higher ERI resolution (Constante et al., 2020) and a greater likelihood of ERI foreclosure and achievement, compared with ERI diffusion (Douglass & Umaña‐Taylor, 2015). It may be that these family processes decrease the likelihood of finding an ERI moratorium profile in a sample of Latine youth, as even when Latine youth are in a period of identity search and rumination, they often do so with a more stable sense of their ERI and its role in their lives due to their family's contributions. Meta‐analytic evidence also suggests that family ethnic socialization is more strongly associated with the ERI development of Latine youth than their Black and Asian American counterparts (Huguley et al., 2019), so it follows that these socialization processes would be particularly tied with higher levels of ERI exploration and/or resolution for these youth. Research also suggests that Latine youth seek out and experience higher rates of ethnic‐racial socialization from their peers, schools, and community members (e.g., Kornienko et al., 2024; Sladek et al., 2022; Witherspoon et al., 2023), and this higher engagement with socialization across contexts may even further buoy their ERI development and identity stability compared with their peers from other ethnic‐racial backgrounds. Future research should examine predictors of profile membership among Latine youth in greater depth and should also explore whether similar profiles emerge in additional Latine samples, particularly samples outside of the U.S. Southwest, where we and most of the above cited studies collected data.

Relations between ERI status profiles and critical consciousness dimensions

Conceptual and empirical research suggests the subcomponents of ERI are likely related to critical consciousness constructs (Bañales et al., 2020; Mathews et al., 2020, 2022), yet no empirical research to date has explored how overall ERI status is associated with critical consciousness subcomponents. The present results elucidated how specific ERI status profiles are linked to distinct dimensions of critical consciousness, finding evidence of the particularly promotive aspects of ERI achievement.

Youth in the ERI achievement profile displayed the highest levels of critical motivation and critical action, indicating both the desire and self‐efficacy to make social change and take concrete actions toward this goal. With respect to critical motivation, this finding is aligned with both theory (Mathews et al., 2020) and empirical evidence (Bañales et al., 2020) suggesting that youth with a higher sense of ERI resolution show higher agency to engage in action in support of their ethnic‐racial group. Youth in the ERI Achieved profile might have also shown the highest critical motivation as a result of having explored and familiarized themselves with their ethnic‐racial group's history, as this exploration might lead to higher drive to rectify social injustices, promoting the development of critical motivation. Additionally, the increased levels of critical action for the ERI Achieved profile, compared with the ERI Diffuse and Foreclosed profiles, align with patterns seen with ERI exploration among Black young adults (Mathews et al., 2022), suggesting this process might be salient among youth from different ethnoracially marginalized backgrounds. Further, that the profile with the highest ERI resolution and ERI exploration showed the highest critical action suggests that having time to explore one's heritage and feel clarity and stability in one's ERI supports youth taking critical action to redress social injustice.

The ERI Foreclosed profile displayed a moderate level of critical motivation that fell between that of the other two profiles, suggesting that although these youth have not yet deeply explored their ERI nor their sense of commitment to social justice, they may have begun both processes. Notably, critical action levels among the ERI Foreclosed profile were similar to that of the ERI Diffuse profile, suggesting that youth in the ERI Foreclosed profile have not yet catalyzed their elevated critical motivation into action. Youth in the ERI Diffuse profile had the lowest levels of critical motivation and lower critical action on par with the ERI Foreclosed profile, suggesting that these youth had not yet engaged with the meanings of their own ERIs or a desire to make the world more just for their ethnoracial group and other marginalized groups. These findings further underscore work emphasizing the importance and salience of ERI meaning making for critical consciousness among youth (Mathews et al., 2020).

Notably, contrary to our expectations and past conceptualizations, we did not find ERI status to be linked with variability in youths' reports of critical reflection. We at first considered that this might be due to critical reflection items including items related to gender and class inequities, but even when restricted to the one item on ethnic and racial inequities, no significant differences emerged across the ERI status profiles. Although empirical research to date has not linked ERI exploration and resolution with critical reflection, this finding contrasts past research that found that Latine youth with higher ERI centrality (i.e., the importance of ERI to one's broader identity) and higher ERI private regard (i.e., one's level of positive affect toward their ethnoracial group) reported higher critical reflection (Bañales et al., 2024).

These findings may be connected to centrality and private regard reflecting content dimensions of ERI, whereas the present study focused on the process dimensions of ERI. It is possible that ERI content—but not process—components may be more salient and promotive of critical reflection because holding a central and positive sense of one's ERI might propel youth to question how and why their social group was treated negatively in the past, as such mistreatment is inconsistent with their own perspective of their group. This questioning could lead to deeper reflections on the structural and social inequalities that their group has faced and that they encounter in their daily lives. In sum, although the developmental process of ERI (i.e., its process dimensions) may help build the kind of confidence that supports critical motivation and action, perhaps the affective components of ERI (i.e., ERI content) may be more closely tied to the depth of critical reflection youth have engaged in. Given the potentially distinct implications of ERI content and process dimensions in shaping aspects of critical consciousness, future work should comprehensively measure aspects of ERI content and process and use person‐centered approaches to test how dimensions of ERI process and content jointly promote critical consciousness among youth.

Implications, limitations, and future directions

Taken together, our findings add to existing research by highlighting how identity relates to the ways youth engage with their social environment via critical consciousness. These findings have important implications for intervention work looking to increase critical consciousness. The bulk of successful critical consciousness interventions to date have been shown to increase critical reflection and, to some extent, critical action, but interventions to increase critical motivation are less common (see Heberle et al., 2020 for a review). Given that critical motivation is the component of critical consciousness most consistently linked to youth well‐being (see Maker Castro et al., 2022 for a review), strategies to increase critical motivation are key to positive youth development. Our findings offer a potential pathway to augment the development of critical motivation and therefore well‐being by leveraging ERI development, which has been effectively done through social identity interventions in the U.S. and Europe (e.g., Hoffman & Umaña‐Taylor, 2023; Umaña‐Taylor, 2023).

Limitations of this study include the use of cross‐sectional data, which precludes examination of potential changes in ERI status membership or testing longitudinal associations between ERI status profiles and critical consciousness dimensions. Future research needs to examine and unpack longitudinal associations and chart changes in adolescents' ERI profile membership, as suggested by current theoretical perspectives on identity development, including lifespan model ERI (Williams et al., 2020) and dual identity process model (Crocetti, 2017). Further, future work needs to examine whether and how these changes in ERI profiles inform and correspond with levels and trajectories of critical reflection, motivation, and action. Such work would help to capture how shifts and changes in ERI relate to youths' evolving critical consciousness beliefs during a developmental period where both processes are in great flux. Potential bidirectional associations between ERI and critical consciousness development could also be probed.

Future research should also engage more diverse Latine samples, particularly with respect to national origin and nativity. The current study features Latine participants, primarily of Mexican origin, from a Southwestern U.S. city close to the U.S. Mexico border where the majority of the Latine population is of Mexican origin. We captured ethnic identification (e.g., national origin) but did not assess racial identification (e.g., Black, Indigenous) or other racialized attributes (e.g., skin tone, phenotype) that shape youths' experiences of marginalization; future work should incorporate these constructs to better account for within‐group heterogeneity. Furthermore, testing the hypothesized associations among Latine participants with roots from multiple Latin American countries may reveal how sociohistorical reference points and varied experiences yield distinct ERI profiles and differences in critical consciousness development. In addition, the sample in the present study was mostly comprised of U.S.‐born youth and had very few first‐generation participants (i.e., youth who were themselves immigrants to the U.S.). Future studies with first‐generation Latine youth will help us understand the critical consciousness development of immigrant Latine youth and also whether there might be differences in the extent to which ERI profiles relate to critical consciousness dimensions among these distinct samples. For example, there is some evidence to suggest that among Latine youth, experiencing heightened immigration‐related threat was associated with higher critical reflection, as well as higher critical action, particularly when youth reported that their primary caregivers (e.g., parents) engaged in higher levels of preparation for bias (e.g., socializing youth to prepare for ethnic‐racial discrimination and coping; Gomez Alvarado et al., 2024). Thus, examining critical consciousness development might also require analyzing the potential contributing role of experiences with social injustice in addition to ERI dimensions. Lastly, examining whether these patterns emerge among other ethnic‐racial groups is also a critical direction for future work.

CONCLUSION

ERI and critical consciousness reflect interconnected developmental competencies, but limited research has examined their interrelation. The current study contributes to this important area of research by illustrating how patterns of ERI development were related to differences in critical reflection, motivation, and action among Latine youth, the fastest growing community of youth in the U.S. today. We found that ERI achievement was most supportive of critical motivation and action, but youth in the ERI Achievement, Diffusion, and Foreclosed profiles all engaged in similar levels of critical reflection. Future research should probe these differences further. As we continue to explore ERI and critical consciousness in our research, it is essential that we engage them in parallel to develop a clearer understanding of their connection across youth and across time.
