Spitz Nevus with HRAS Q61R Arising in a Giant Nevus Spilus of the Leg
Authors: Tugba Kevser Uzuncakmak, Narmin Zahedi, Ayse Mine Onenerk, Ovgu Aydın
Published: 2026

Introduction

Spitz nevi developing within nevus spilus represent a rare clinical entity and pose diagnostic and management challenges [1]. We present a case that adds to the limited literature on this uncommon association, particularly highlighting the molecular findings that support the genetic relationship between these lesions (Table 1).

Case Presentation

We present a 15-year-old female with a giant congenital melanocytic nevus spilus involving her left lower extremity, measuring 60×30 cm and present since birth. On skin examination, numerous melanocytic macules and papules (1 mm–1 cm) were distributed over a light brown macular background (Figure 1). The patient had previously undergone excisions of two dysplastic compound nevi from the same thigh, both reported as low-grade dysplasia.

Dermoscopy showed multiple nevi with junctional, compound, and dermal patterns, some displaying dysplastic features (Figure 2). Notably, a lesion on the posterior leg showed clinical asymmetry. An excision was performed to exclude melanoma. Histopathology revealed classical features of Spitz nevus: large epithelioid and spindle melanocytes within a symmetrical compound proliferation. Next-generation sequencing identified an HRAS mutation (c.182A>G, p. Gln61Arg, variant allele frequency 30.13%), consistent with published pathogenic variants for Spitz and congenital nevi (Figure 3) [3]. The case was thus diagnosed as Spitz nevus arising on a giant nevus spilus.

Conclusion

Although nevus spilus is considered benign, its potential for malignant transformation is recognized, particularly when dysplastic nevi or atypical features are present [2]. This risk increases with large or giant lesions, as seen here, and with a prior history of dysplasia. A total of 24 cases of Spitz nevus arising on nevus spilus, both solitary and agminated, have been reported in the literature [3–5]. Management approaches include complete excision for atypical or changing lesions and close monitoring for others.

The presence of a somatic HRAS mutation supports a genetic link reported in prior cases of Spitz nevi on nevus spilus backgrounds [3,5]. Regular follow-up every six months and selective biopsy of morphologically suspicious lesions remain crucial to early detection of malignant change.

In conclusion, Spitz nevus can arise within a giant congenital nevus spilus, complicating clinical management due to overlapping features with melanoma and underlying genetic alterations. Close clinical surveillance and molecular analysis are essential to optimizing outcomes in these complex melanocytic lesions.
