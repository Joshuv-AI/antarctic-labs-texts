Introduction

Obstetric fistulas are abnormal openings created between the vaginal wall and the bladder or the rectum (recto-vaginal fistula) [1]. The most common causes of obstetric fistula are untreated obstructed labor, where the head of the uterus has compressed the soft tissue between the vagina and uterus or between the uterus and rectum, causing ischemia and necrosis [2]. Women with obstetric fistula often experience continuous leakage of urine or feces through the vagina, frequent urinary tract infections, ulceration of the genital area caused by persistent wetness, social discrimination, divorce or separation, absence of sexual intercourse, loss of fertility and amenorrhea, and depression [3–7].

Globally, the burden of the congenital fistula is confined to the “fistula belt”, which extends from Mauritania in the second half of sub-Saharan Africa to Eritrea, the Middle East, and southern Asia including Ethiopia [8, 9]. This can be due to a lack of understanding of complexities, social and cultural barriers, and physical barriers such as mountains, rivers, and lack of transportation, which delay access to care [10]. Globally, the prevalence of obstetric fistula is nearly 2 million with an estimated incidence of 50,000–100,000 new cases registered annually [11]. It is estimated that more than 2 million young women live with untreated obstetric fistula in Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [12]. In Ethiopia, obstetric fistula affects at least 36,000 women, making it a concerning public health problem for the East African nation [13]. It was due to lack of access to skilled personnel creates a danger to mothers and babies due to only 50% of births have access to trained medical staff and the practice of child marriage [14]. It had significant social, economic, and psychological impacts and placed a very large medical and financial burden on victims and their families [15].

Obstetric fistula persists because healthcare systems fail to ensure adequate maternal care, including family planning, emergency obstetric care, skilled delivery, and affordable fistula treatment [16]. However, there is a serious problem with birth defects in Ethiopia; There is no conclusive evidence of recovery time from this condition. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the recovery time for women with obstetric fistula in Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

systematic review and meta-analysis were performed to determine the recovery time of congenital syphilis in Ethiopia. Preferred Reporting Items (PRISMA) guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses were followed [17]. The study protocol was registered in the International Journal of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, registration number: CRD42023270497).

Search strategies and sources of information

PubMed, Hinari, Epistemonikos, and Google Scholar were searched for relevant articles. Search terms were developed using PICO guidelines and applied to these databases. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were employed to combine Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords. The PubMed search strategy was as follows: “Time to recovery” AND “women” OR “Reproductive age group” AND “Obstetric fistula” OR “Rectovaginal fistula” OR “Vesicovaginal fistula” AND “Associated Factors” OR “Determinants” OR “Predictors” AND “Ethiopia.” Additionally, the title “recovery time of obstetric fistula in Ethiopia” was used as a search term.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis included observational studies published in English that examined recovery time from congenital stroke and its predictors in Ethiopia. Studies accessible online were considered through November 8, 2023, for all. Qualitative studies, non-English articles, case studies, case series, cross-sectional studies, and case studies were excluded.

Data extraction

All retrieved studies from the five databases were exported using EndNote X8. After the removal of duplicates, two investigators (YT and LA) independently screened abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. All transcripts of the selected studies were then retrieved and independently screened by two authors (NK and FD) using a standardized data exclusion form adapted from the CHARMS program [18]. Extracted data included author, year of publication, study site, region, study design, survival time in weeks, and follow-up period. For studies not available online, researchers were contacted by email or telephone to request full text.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was critically assessed using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) quality assessment tool for cohort studies [19]. Risk of bias was classified as low (8–14 points), moderate (5–7 points), or high (< 5 points). Four investigators (YT, LA, NK, and FD) independently assessed study quality. To minimize bias, the investigators were blinded to each other’s assessment during data selection, data extraction, and quality assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Overall, the critical appraisal results of eligible studies quality were fair to good (Supplementary file).

Investigation of heterogeneity and publication bias

The heterogeneity among the included studies using the I2 statistics was 97.72%, indicating considerable heterogeneity among the included studies. So, we analyzed it using a random-effects model to adjust for the observed variability. To check publication bias, a Funnel plot and Egger’s regression test were done. The p-value of Egger’s regression test (0.017) was significant and it showed that there was evidence of publication bias.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize evidence on the pooled recovery time of obstetric fistula in Ethiopia. The findings reveal a pooled median recovery time of 4.05 weeks, with considerable heterogeneity among the included studies. The relatively small number of included studies (n = 6) limits the generalizability of our findings. All studies were retrospective cohort studies, which may introduce inherent biases. Additionally, the variation in study design, sample size, and follow-up periods contributed to the observed heterogeneity. The pooled recovery time of 4.05 weeks provides a preliminary estimate of the healing process for obstetric fistula patients in Ethiopia. However, the considerable heterogeneity (I²=97.72%) underscores the need for caution in interpreting this result [25]. Factors such as fistula type, severity, surgical techniques, and postoperative care likely influence recovery time, and further research is needed to explore these variables [26]. This considerable variation across the studies may be due to differences in study settings, patient characteristics, or other factors. The identification of publication bias is a significant limitation of this study. The significant Egger’s test suggests that studies with longer recovery times might be underrepresented in the literature. The prolonged recovery period highlighted in this study emphasizes the significant impact of obstetric fistula on women’s lives. These women often experience social isolation, depression, and diminished quality of life. Comprehensive interventions, including prevention, timely management, and post-operative care, are crucial to mitigate the physical and psychosocial consequences of obstetric fistula. This is supported by a study conducted in low-income countries [27]. This is because; better information increases their awareness and understanding of obstetrics fistula from their husband and social and family support are important to promote health-seeking behavior [28].

Generally, future research should focus on identifying factors associated with longer recovery times, such as fistula type, severity, and surgical techniques. Prospective studies with larger sample sizes and standardized data collection protocols are needed to provide more robust estimates of recovery time and to explore the impact of different treatment approaches.

Strength and limitation

This study contributes to the body of knowledge on this important topic that obstetric fistula is a devastating condition with profound implications for women’s health and well-being. The study provides a pooled estimate of recovery time, which can inform clinical practice, policymaking, and resource allocation and the need for further research to explore potential influencing factors. However, the inclusion of only six studies may limit the generalizability of the findings and reduce statistical power. The considerable heterogeneity among the included studies suggests that the pooled estimate may not accurately represent the recovery time for all women with obstetric fistula.

Conclusion

The findings reveal a pooled median recovery time of 4.05 weeks, with considerable heterogeneity among the included studies. While this estimate provides valuable insights, the presence of publication bias, and significant Egger’s test, underscores the need for caution in interpreting the results. Efforts to address publication bias are essential to enhance the reliability of future meta-analyses by using standardized data collection protocols, larger-scale studies, or the exploration of innovative treatment approaches. By improving access to quality fistula care and addressing the underlying factors contributing to its occurrence, it is possible to reduce the burden of obstetric fistula on affected women and communities in Ethiopia.
