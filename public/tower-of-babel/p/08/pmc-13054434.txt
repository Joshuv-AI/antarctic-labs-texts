Molar extinction coefficients, NMR data, and mass spectrometry data provided for Anabaenopeptin A, C, D and Ferintoic Acid A
Authors: Megan L. Quandt, Blake B. Stringer, Regina G. Szlag Silva, Meher Prakash, Dennis P. Anderson, Nicholas J. Peraino, Jeremy J. Kodanko, Judy A. Westrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article reports a comprehensive dataset of physiochemical properties for four compounds: anabaenopeptin (AP) congeners, AP-A, AP-C, AP-D, and Ferintoic acid A (FAA). All compounds were synthesized and characterized via NMR, high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), UV–Vis, and polarimetry. The identities of each AP were confirmed by 1H, 13C, 2D NMR and HRMS with MS2 fragmentation. High pressure liquid chromatography coupled with photodiode array and mass spectrometry (HPLC-PDA-MS) analysis confirmed each AP was greater than 97% pure. Following identity verification and purity confirmation, UV–Vis profiles, molar extinction coefficients, and specific optical rotations were determined for each AP congener. Due to conflicting limited reports, the reported spectra and physiochemical properties in this work were compared to current literature values. This data will support the standardization of AP reference materials, resulting in improved reliability and comparability of monitoring and research outcomes.

Background

Cyanobacterial harmful algal blooms (cHABs) are prevalent in warm, nutrient rich waters and produce an array of cyanopeptides [1]. Anabaenopeptins (AP) are a class of cyanopeptides and have been shown to be produced by many genera including Dolichospermum, Microcystis, Nodularia, Planktothrix, and Oscillatoria [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Their structures consist of a cyclic core with variable amino acids and a variable ureido side chain [6]. AP congeners have been shown to inhibit carboxypeptidase at sub nanomolar concentrations, with enzyme binding attributed to the ureido side chain [7]. Additionally, APs may be produced in source water at mg/L concentrations [8]. To date, AP research is limited as there are only two commercially available APs, AP-A and AP-B. Due to the lack of standards and difficulty in isolating APs, there are few reports or conflicting reports on physiochemical properties of AP congeners. We synthesized four AP congeners and took physiochemical properties of each. The AP congeners synthesized and tested in this work are AP-A, AP-C, AP-D, and ferintoic acid A (FAA) (Fig. 1A–D). Each AP congener has an identical cyclic pentapeptide core with varying ureido side chains. AP-A, AP-C, AP-D and FAA contain a ureido tyrosine, lysine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan, respectively. 1H, 13C, 2D NMR and high-resolution MS (HRMS) with MS2 fragmentation were used to validate the synthetized chemical structures. A high-pressure liquid chromatography coupled photodiode array and mass spectrometer (HPLC-PDA/MS) were used for purity analysis of each congener to ensure no other cyanopeptides or byproducts were present. Data Description

NMR assignments

Upon synthesis of AP-A, AP-C, AP-D, and FAA, the chemical structures were verified via 1H NMR (Fig. 2A–D). In addition to 1H NMR, 13C NMR and 2D NMR (HSQC, HMBC, COSY, and ROESY) were taken to assist in peak assignments. The 2D NMR provides further spatial proximity and molecular connectivity. The 13C NMR and 2D NMR spectra can be found within this article’s linked repository. The 1H NMR and 13C NMR assignments for each AP congener reported in this work were compared to the current literature values (Table 1A–D). The NMR signals from the cyclic residues follow similar patterns, with shift values and couplings appearing in similar ranges between the different APs. Furthermore, the through-bond and through-space correlations are similar for the residues within the cyclic pentapeptide core, confirming that the ring conformation is conserved between these APs.Fig. 2(A-D). Following the NMR of AP-A, HPLC-MS/MS co-injection was performed as this is the gold standard for confirmation of identical products. The synthesized AP-A was co-injected with the AP-A Marbionics and Enzos analytical standards demonstrating indistinguishable co-elution under a straight-gradient method with matching fragments m/z 84.1, 114.1, 263.1, 362.3, 568.3, 637.3, and 667.4.

The reported 1H NMR for the synthetic AP-C in this work differs from Fujii et al. as their data was acquired with the AP-C dissolved in deuterated methanol, while the data for this work was acquired in DMSO [5]. By differing solvents, small variation in overall conformation of the cyclic peptide is observed. Assignments were determined through analysis of 2D correlations and were consistent with other reported AP congeners. Assignments for the ring and tail Lys residues were found through distinct COSY and HMBC correlations. Though the signals for each are closely spaced, their assignments were unambiguously determined.

As for the synthesized AP-D, the 1H NMR data reported by Fujii et al. match our reported 1H NMR. The 13C NMR signal reported by Fujii et al. are shifted slightly down field compared to our assignments [5]. This could indicate small differences in concentration or water content. An interesting note from the 1H data is that the Phe NH and Lys ε-NH signals persisted with reduced integral values for quite a long period of time after dissolution. This indicates that these amide protons are engaged in hydrogen bonding and are not exposed to solvent, consistent with their location in the interior of the ring structure where exchange is slow. The synthetic AP-D and isolated AP-D NMR assignments are in strong agreement.

The synthetic FAA NMR data reported in this work agrees with Xia et al. though they report FAA d-Trp [11]. Our synthetic route used l-Trp and was not exposed to conditions capable of converting it to d-Trp giving confidence in the reported NMR. The stereochemistry of the Trp could not be determined from 2D NMR analysis due to the lack of mobility of the ureido Trp resulting in limited through-space interactions. Williams et al. were the first to report FAA NMR data, and our reported data is in agreeance but only differs with the α Trp shift [10]. We report 4.17 ppm compared to their 4.42 ppm. Although NMR cannot resolve the stereochemistry of Trp, the reported assignments for synthetic FAA were unambiguous.

UV–Vis profile and molar extinction coefficient

The molar extinction coefficient is an intrinsic property that enables the concentration of a substance in a solution to be accurately measured by UV. After the structure and purity of each AP was confirmed, the UV–Vis profile between 200 and 300 nm was taken where a wavelength maxima (λmax) for each AP was observed at 278 nm. By varying concentration and recording the absorbance at 278 nm, molar extinction coefficients were calculated for each AP (Fig. 8A–D). Each molar extinction coefficient was compared to the current literature and theoretical values (Table 4). AP-A’s molar extinction coefficient has been reported twice previously with varying values. After isolation of AP-A, Steiner et al. report 4190 M−1cm−1 while Harada et al. report 2417 M−1cm−1 [9,12]. For our synthesized AP-A, the molar extinction coefficient was determined to be 2289 M−1cm−1. To our knowledge, AP-C and AP-D molar extinction coefficients are being reported for the first time. The previously reported molar extinction coefficient from isolated FAA of 548 M−1cm−1 does not agree with the observed 5580 M−1cm−1 in this work [10]. Each molar extinction coefficient reported in this work was in general agreement with the theoretical molar extinction coefficient calculated by Expasy ProtParam as seen in Table 4. Expasy ProtParam calculates its theoretical molar extinction coefficients by assuming that the peptides of interest are linear and contain only proteinogenic amino acids. APs have cyclic structures and contain both proteinogenic and non-proteinogenic amino acids [17]. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

NMR assignments

All NMR data except for the carbon experiments were collected on a Bruker Avance NEO 500 NMR instrument using a 5 mm iProbe. The 13C data were collected on a Bruker Avance NEO 600 instrument using a 5 mm Prodigy BBO probe. All data were processed using MestreNova processing software.

Each NMR tube had between 1.5–3.0 mg of each AP. The 1H experiments were collected using 32k complex data points over 4 s of acquisition time. There were 64 transients per FID, which were apodised with 0.25 Hz of line broadening and zero-filled to 64k points. Data were referenced to the residual solvent signal of the deuterated solvent (2.49 PPM for DMSO‑d6 and 3.30 PPM for CD3OD).

A double-quantum filtered version of the COSY experiment was used. There were 4k complex points collected in the t2 dimension, with 50% non-uniform sampling (NUS) used in the t1 dimension for a total of 256 complex points. Either 4 or 8 scans per t1 point were collected. The t1 points were doubled with linear prediction (LP) and zero-filled to 1k points during processing. Cosine squared apodization was applied to both dimensions before analysis.

A multiplicity-edited version of the HSQC experiment was used. There were 4k complex points collected in the t2 dimension, with 50% NUS used in the t1 dimension for a total of 256 complex points. Either 16 or 32 scans per t1 point were collected. The t1 points were doubled with LP and zero-filled to 1k points during processing. Cosine squared apodization was applied to both dimensions before analysis.

For the HMBC experiments, 4k complex points collected in the t2 dimension, with 50% NUS used in the t1 dimension for a total of 256 complex points. Either 32 or 64 scans per t1 point were collected. The t1 points were doubled with LP and zero-filled to 1k points during processing. Sine squared apodization was applied to both dimensions before analysis.

For the ROESY experiments, 4k complex points collected in the t2 dimension, with 512 complex t1 points. Either 8 or 16 scans per t1 point were collected. The t1 points were doubled with LP and zero-filled to 2k points during processing. Cosine squared apodization was applied to both dimensions before analysis.

The 13C experiments were collected using 128k complex data points over 1.8 s of acquisition time. There were 512 or 1024 transients per FID, which were apodised with 1.0 Hz of line broadening and zero-filled to 256k points. Data were referenced to the solvent signal of the deuterated solvent (39.5 PPM for DMSO‑d6 and 49.0 PPM for CD3OD).

HRMS and MS2 sequencing

HRMS and MS2 fragmentation for FAA, AP-A, and AP-D was performed on the Bruker timsTOF Pro II while the Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Elite was implored for AP-C characterization. Each sample is prepared from dryness. Samples are brought up in methanol. 1 µL of stock is diluted into 99 µL of water making a 1:100 dilution. The sample is then transferred to a 2 mL HPLC vial and ready to be injected. The timsTOF is coupled to an Elute+ UHPLC with HPG1300 pumps (Bruker), and the Orbitrap Elite is coupled with an UltiMate 3000 HPLC system (Thermo Scientific). A Thermo Scientific Hypersil GOLD™ C18 column 2.1 × 50 mm (particle size 1.9 µm particle size) was used for both instruments. Solvent A was water with 0.1% formic acid and solvent B was acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was set to 0.350mL/min and the gradients went as follows: 5 – 5% B over 2 min, 5 – 95% B from 2 to 11 min, 95 – 95% B from 11 to 13 min, 95 – 5% B from 13 to 13.1 min, 5 – 5% B from 13.1 to 18 min. Each diluted sample had 10 µL injected.

The MSs were both run in positive mode. The timsTOF used vacuum insulated probe-heated electrospray ionization (VIP-HESI). The end plate offset was at 500 V, and the capillary was at 4500 V. The nebulizer was at 2.0 bar with dry gas at 8 L/min and temperature set to 230 °C. The sheath gas was 400 °C at 4.0 L/min. Compass DataAnalysis was used for analysing the mass spectrum spectra. The MS2 higher-energy collision dissociation energy (HCD) ranged between 44.9–45.9 kV. The Orbitrap Elite used heated ESI with a 300 °C heater temperature. The sheath gas flow rate was set to 55 with the aux gas flow rate set to 5. The spray voltage was 3.5 kV, and the capillary temperature was set to 350 °C. The S-Lens RF level was set to 65%. The MS2 HCD energy was set to 30 kV. Xcalibur qual browser was used to process the mass spectrum spectra. MS2 fragments were compared to literature values and validated in ChemDraw version 22.2. This can be done by deleting varying sites of the parent structure and aligning the ChemDraw reported m/z to the observed m/z.

HPLC-PDA spectra

A Shimadzu 2020EV LC-PDA-MS was used for purity analysis. The samples were prepared the same as shown in section 4.2. The analytical column and solvents were kept the same. The solvent gradient was the same as used for the HRMS fragmentation samples. 10 µL of each sample was injected. DL temperature was set to 200 °C while the heating block was set to 300 °C. Nebulizing gas flowed at 1.5 L/min, and the drying gas flowed at 15 L/min. Shimadzu LabSolutions was used for data analysis of PDA and mass spectrum. The purity was assessed based off the PDA wavelength range of 200–500 nm. 278 nm was investigated in further detail as this is the maxima molar absorptivity for each AP. Peaks that were greater than 0.5% of the parent ion peak were considered real.

UV–Vis profile and molar extinction coefficient

Differential weighing was utilized to obtain an accurate measurement of each AP. Vials were weighed with an analytical balance that had been calibrated to 5 mg. Each AP in methanol was then added to its respective vial and nitrogen evaporated. With the methanol evaporated, the vial and AP weight could be taken and used for the UV–Vis measurements. After differential weighing, AP-A, AP-C, AP-D, and FAA were measured to be 13 mg, 4.2 mg, 3.7 mg, and 7.5 mg, respectively. 4 mL of methanol was added to each vial. Dilutions, typically 1:10 or 1:25, were then made from this stock. A SpectraMax M2e Multi detection Reader was used to determine the UV–Vis profiles and respective molar extinction coefficients. In the SoftMax Pro Version 6.5.1 software the spectrum wavelength range was to 200–300 nm with 1 nm steps. 278 nm was observed as the maximum molar absorptivity. Excel and OriginLab were used for data workup and plotting.

Specific optical rotation

A Jasco P-2000 Polarimeter was used to obtain specific optical rotation measurements. A CG1–100 cylindrical glass cell (3.5 mm x 100 mm) was used to house the APs in 1.5 mL methanol. A tungsten-halogen lamp with a 589 (Na) interference filter was used. The polarimeter is turned on and given a 5-minute stabilization period that must not be skipped. The specific optical rotation measurement is selected in the ‘start menu.’ The liquid sample is ensured to be clear with no suspended particles or air bubbles. The Teflon caps are removed from the cell to allow for the AP solution to be transferred to the cell via pipette. It is ensured that the optical window of the cell is never touched. The cell is placed in the sample compartment and secured. The default 8 mm sample compartment aperture and 2 mm source aperture were used. The parameters were set to Specific O.R. with 10 cycles and 2 s intervals. The milligrams of each AP and volume of methanol are input. After saving the parameters, the instrument is ready to start acquiring data. Before running the AP sample, a “Blank” is run for correction. The results of each run appear on the iRM screen where they can be written or transferred via USB flash drive. Each sample was collected at 23 °C.

Limitations

The quantities of each AP available for characterization were limited (3.7–13.0 mg per compound), which constrained some measurements such as molar extinction coefficients and specific optical rotations to two significant figures. Additionally, molar extinction coefficients were estimated using the Expasy ProtParam tool, which is designed for linear peptides composed of proteogenic amino acids. Therefore, its use for cyclic peptides containing non-proteogenic residues represents an approximation.
