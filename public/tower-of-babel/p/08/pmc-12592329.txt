Relaxation of selective constraint on the sweet-taste receptor gene TAS1R2 in lorisiform primates
Authors: Qinyuan Ji, Min Hou, Muhammad Shoaib Akhtar, Takashi Hayakawa, Yasuka Toda, Akihiro Itoigawa, Hiroo Imai, Takafumi Ishida, Amanda D Melin, Shoji Kawamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent research has revealed considerable evolutionary diversity in umami-taste (amino acids and nucleotides) and sweet-taste receptor TAS1R genes across vertebrate species. To contribute to a growing understanding of how diet shapes taste evolution, we studied TAS1R genes in non-anthropoid primates with highly diverse diets, including members of the Strepsirrhini, comprised of Lorisiformes (lorises) and Lemuriformes (lemurs), as well as members of the Tarsiiformes (tarsiers). We employed a targeted capture (TC) approach specifically probing all the three mammalian TAS1R genes, i.e., TAS1R1 (for sensing umami), TAS1R2 (sweet) and TAS1R3 (required for forming a heterodimer), followed by short-read massive-parallel sequencing for three lorisiform, four lemuriform, and one tarsiiform species. Analyzing together with publicly available whole-genome assemblies (WGAs) of non-anthropoids, we found that TAS1R1 and TAS1R2 of some lorisiform species were disrupted. The relative evolutionary rates in introns and synonymous sites of all the three TAS1R genes, as well as non-genic genome regions, of lorisiforms were higher than those of lemuriforms, a finding consistent with the higher genome-wide mutation rate of the lorisiforms. We found a similar pattern in the amino acid sequences and nonsynonymous sites of the sweet receptor TAS1R2 in lorisiforms. Evolutionary rates of amino acid sequences and nonsynonymous sites in TAS1R1 and TAS1R3 of lorisiforms were as slow as those of lemuriforms. This suggests that functional constraint on sweet sensing has been relaxed in lorisiform primates since their common ancestor. These results shed a new light on understanding evolutionary diversification of umami and sweet sensing in a diverse group of mammals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23648-x.Keywords: TAS1R, Strepsirrhini, Lorisiformes, Lemuriformes, Tarsiiformes, Targeted captureSubject terms: Evolution, Genetics

Perception of umami and sweet tastes plays a crucial role in intake of beneficial foods. The umami taste facilitates the recognition of amino acids and nucleotides, while the sweet taste enables animals to detect sugars1. Sensation of these tastes is mediated by a family of G protein-coupled receptors known as taste receptor type 1 (TAS1R, also referred to as T1R), which includes three members: TAS1R1, TAS1R2, and TAS1R3 in mammals2. The TAS1R genes consist of six exons, encoding a large N-terminal extracellular region containing the Venus flytrap domain and a small cysteine-rich domain, seven transmembrane domains and a C-terminal intracellular domain3. The TAS1R1/TAS1R3 heterodimer functions as the umami taste receptor, detecting L-amino acids and 5′-ribonucleotides, while the TAS1R2/TAS1R3 heterodimer serves as the sweet taste receptor detecting sugars2,4,5.

With the advent of massive parallel sequencing, TAS1R sequences for numerous species have become accessible through public whole-genome assembly (WGA) databases6,7. Although TAS1R genes once appeared to be relatively conserved across vertebrates8, recent studies have uncovered remarkable diversity across taxonomic groups7,9–11. While both mammals and teleost fish have three TAS1R genes, a phylogenetic study revealed that mammalian and teleost genes were not orthologous with respect to TAS1R2 and TAS1R3 due to independent gene loss from an original nine TAS1R genes which were inferred to have arisen at the bony vertebrate common ancestor7. Among mammals, the umami sensory TAS1R1 gene of giant pandas is disrupted, coinciding with their dietary shift from carnivory to bamboo consumption12. Similarly, the sweet sensory TAS1R2 gene of carnivorous felid species is disrupted13.

Primates are among the most diverse groups of mammals, including substantial dietary diversity. Their umami sensory TAS1R1 evolved from sensing nucleotides to sensing glutamate in some taxa. This change appears to be concomitant with a dietary shift from insect-based to leaf-based protein acquisition, which accompanied the evolutionary shift from small to middle/large bodyweight5. While the umami sensory TAS1R1 in the Family Cercopithecidae is inferred to be under positive natural selection, the sweet sensory TAS1R2 is inferred to be under contrasting selective regimes between its two Subfamilies: positive selection in omnivorous Cercopithecinae and relaxation of negative (purifying) selection in folivorous Colobinae primates14.

Among primates, strepsirrhines and tarsiers exhibit diverse ecological traits (e.g., diel activity and dietary habits)15. Strepsirrhines and tarsiers were once regarded as a monophyletic group and were collectively called “prosimians”16. This grouping was largely based on shared anatomical and ecological characteristics, including small body size, nocturnality, and insectivory. However, molecular phylogenetic studies have revealed that the Suborder Tarsiiformes (tarsiers), comprised of a single Infraorder Tarsiiformes, is the sister group to the Suborder Simiiformes (also called as Anthropoidea)17–19. The two Suborders comprise the Semiorder Haplorhini which is the sister group to the Semiorder Strepsirrhini. These two Semiorders comprise the Order Primates. The Semiorder Strepsirrhini is comprised of a single Suborder Strepsirrhini. The Suborder Strepsirrhini is comprised of two Infraorders Lorisiformes (lorises) and Lemuriformes (lemurs and aye-aye)17. Thus, from humans, an anthropoid, strepsirrhines are the most distant and tarsiers are the second most distant primate lineages with deep evolutionary splits. The lorisiform species are widely distributed in Africa and Southern Asia and are nocturnal with diverse dietary habits such as insectivory, gummivory, and frugivory15. The lemuriform species are distributed only in Madagascar but are highly diverse taxonomically and ecologically, containing 13% of living primate species, and diverse diel activity (diurnal, nocturnal, and cathemeral) and dietary habits including frugivory, folivory, gummivory, and insectivory20. The tarsiiform species are nocturnal and primarily insectivorous, distributed across the islands of Southeast Asia15. Collectively, these groups of primates are excellent models for studying evolutionary diversity of chemosensory genes under various ecological scenarios and can offer unique insights into primate evolution and dietary reconstruction efforts for ancestral species.

Regarding TAS1R genes, it has been reported that ring‑tailed lemur (Lemur catta) is highly sensitive to both glutamate and nucleotides, whereas brown greater galago (Otolemur crassicaudatus) and Philippine tarsier [Tarsius (or Carlito) syrichta] are significantly more sensitive to nucleotides than to glutamate5. However, strepsirrhine and tarsiiform species are widely understudied and variation may be substantial5,14. Moreover, publicly available WGA databases for non-model organisms are often incomplete, typically with low sequencing depth and assemblies that contain only a single haploid sequence. While information on the other haploid sequence may be obtainable through accessing sequencing reads and re-assembling them, this generally requires intensive computational investment, limiting feasibility and ease of access. An alternative is targeted capture (TC) of the DNA sequences of interest using RNA baits prior to sequencing, and to thus focus sequencing efforts specifically on these regions. This method typically generates much higher coverage of the genes under study, and higher assuredness in correctly identifying their variants21–26.

In this study, we applied TC and short-read massive parallel sequencing, supplemented with data from WGA databases, to analyze a total of twenty species of non-anthropoid primates with diverse diets from eight Families across the three Infraorders, Lorisiformes, Lemuriformes and Tarsiiformes (Fig. 1, Table S1). Our central hypothesis is that dietary specialization has shaped the evolution of genes underlying taste perception. We predict that purifying selection has largely conserved TAS1R1, the gene unique to the umami taste receptor, in species feeding mainly insects and/or leaves, TAS1R2, the gene unique to the sweet taste receptor, in species feeding mainly fruits, and TAS1R3, the gene shared by both receptors in all species. Conversely, we predict that purifying selection on TAS1R1 and TAS1R2 has been relaxed in species for which those foods are not of importance in their diets. Relaxed selection would allow disrupted alleles to spread through the population or lessen conservancy of amino acid sequences of the gene with respect to relative evolutionary rate among taxonomic groups. We examined whether the TAS1R genes were intact or disrupted in each species. We also evaluated the relative evolutionary rate of the genes among taxonomic groups and inferred when evolutionary rate changed during evolution. These approaches provide insight into whether and when dietary shift occurred in primate evolution.

Materials and methods

Genomic DNA samples

TC of TAS1R genes was conducted for genomic DNA samples from eight non-anthropoid species (one individual each) (Table 1; Fig. 1). One species was from the Tarsiiformes [Sulawesi tarsier (Tarsius tarsier)] and seven species were from the Strepsirrhini: four were from Lorisiformes [Northern lesser galago (Galago senegalensis), West African potto (Perodicticus potto), pygmy slow loris (or pygmy loris) (Nycticebus pygmaeus or Xanthonycticebus sp.) {Note that Xanthonycticebus was recently proposed as a new genus name for Nycticebus pygmaeus and was divided into two species X. pygmaeus and X. intermedius and that a common name of pygmy loris was suggested for the genus27}, and greater slow loris (N. coucang)], three were from the Lemuriformes [black-and-white ruffed lemur (Varecia variegata), black lemur (Eulemur macaco) and ring-tailed lemur (Lemur catta)].

Probe design of TAS1R genes

We designed TAS1R probes for each infraorder using three WGA databases available at the time we started this project: Tarsiiformes: Philippine tarsier [Tarsius (or Carlito) syrichta] Tarsius_syrichta-2.0.129; Lorisiformes: northern greater galago (Otolemur garnettii) OtoGar330; Lemuriformes: gray mouse lemur (Microcebus murinus) Mmur_3.031 (Table S2). We used the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) (BLASTN2.8.1+)32[,33 to search for TAS1R1, TAS1R2 and TAS1R3 in these databases (Table S3−1, Supplementary_data1).

In the Tarsius_syrichta-2.0.1 and OtoGar3 assemblies, the intron sequences of TAS1R1 and TAS1R2 were found to contain undetermined sequences, denoted by ‘n’s. To better capture the TAS1R sequences, we included human TAS1R1 and TAS1R2 sequences in the probe set (Tables S2, S3-1, Supplementary_data1). Because the TAS1R3 sequence was not found in the Tarsius_syrichta-2.0.1 assembly, this sequence was taken from Toda et al. (2021)5. For each TAS1R gene, probes were designed for entire protein-coding exons and introns as well as for 100 base pairs (bp) each of upstream and downstream flanking regions to the first and last coding exons, respectively (Table S3−1, Supplementary_data1).

Probe design of neutral references

As a control reference to evaluate gene copy number and selective neutrality, our previous study21 selected 85 single-locus non-protein-coding sequences (82 autosomal and three X- non-pseudoautosomal) from the human reference genome hg19 assembly using the Neutral Region Explorer34. In brief, the selected regions were without repeat sequences, with a length longer than 1.0 kb, with distance to the nearest gene longer than 0.2 centimorgan (cM) in autosomes or 0.1 cM in the X chromosome, and with a minimum recombination rate of 0.9 cM/Mb under the Neutral Region Explorer. In this study, we aimed to retrieve their orthologous sequences from the non-anthropoid primate WGAs for enabling us to conduct phylogenetic analyses across primate taxonomic groups. This approach assumed that their orthologous regions of other primate genomes were likely to evolve neutrally due to their intergenic location and lack of known conserved regulatory elements.

Using the LiftOver tool (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgLiftOver), genome coordinates were converted from the hg19 assembly to the Tarsius_syrichta-2.0.1, OtoGar3, Mmur_2.0 WGA databases. When abnormal hits were assigned, we performed BLAST+ (ver2.8.1) to find corresponding coordinates. We took the sequences with a reasonably high similarity to human’s (75–90% in Tarsius_syrichta-2.0.1, 73–86% in OtoGar3 and 75–87% in Mmur_2.0) for these non-anthropoid non-protein coding regions. We also confirmed their orthology to the human sequences by reconstructing a phylogenetic tree for each neutral reference (data not presented). Among the 85 regions, 83, 80 and 78 regions were identified from Tarsius_syrichta-2.0.1, OtoGar3, and Mmur_2.0 WGA databases, respectively, and used as probes (Table S3−2, Supplementary_data2). Missing regions in Tarsius_syrichta-2.0.1 were supplemented by human sequences, those in OtoGar3 were by Mmur_2.0 or human sequences, and those in Mmur_2.0 were by OtoGar3 or human sequences (Table S3−2).

Probe synthesis, targeted capture, and massive parallel sequencing

The probe synthesis, TC, and massive parallel sequencing were conducted following Hou et al. (2024)25. Briefly, probe synthesis was outsourced to Biodiscovery, LLC (Ann Arbor, MI) who provided in-solution biotinylated RNA baits (myBaits®)35. Each probe was 120-nucleotides (nt) length and overlapped by 60 nt with adjacent probes (i.e., 2 x tiling). Construction of the DNA sequencing libraries and TC were performed using myBaits® kit v5 following the manufacturer’s protocol (myBaits® user manual version 5.02). Construction of DNA sequencing library, TC, and massive parallel sequencing were conducted by the Centre for Health Genomics and Informatics and UCDNA Service, University of Calgary, Canada. DNA sequencing libraries were constructed by fragmenting genomic DNA into ~ 500 bp. Biotinylated RNA baits were introduced and allowed to hybridize to targets at 65 °C. Bait-target hybrids were then pulled out of the solution with streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. The beads were stringently washed at 65 °C to remove non-hybridized and nonspecifically hybridized molecules. These conditions of hybridization and washing are reported to allow ~ 10% mismatch between baits and targets according to the manufacturer’s protocol (myBaits® user manual version 5.02). Captured DNA library was released from the beads and amplified by PCR targeting the adaptor sequence. The massive parallel sequencer used in this study was the Illumina NextSeq with 300 cycle and 150-bp paired end sequencing.

Identification of TAS1R sequences from WGA databases

We searched for WGA databases of eighteen non-anthropoid primate species for TAS1R sequences (Fig. 1, Table S2). These species cover the three Infraorders (Tarsiiformes, Lorisiformes and Lemuriformes), four Superfamilies (Tarsioidea, Lorisoidea, Daubentonoidea and Lemuroidea), eight Families (Tarsiidae, Galagidae, Lorisidae, Daubentoniidae, Lemuridae, Lepilemuridae, Cheirogaleidae and Indriidae), and eighteen genera (Carlito, Tarsius, Otolemur, Galago, Arctocebus, Perodicticus, Loris, Nycticebus, Daubentonia, Varecia, Eulemur, Lemur, Lepilemur, Cheirogaleus, Microcebus, Indri, Propithecus and Avahi). WGA databases of Nycticebus coucang and Lemur catta provide both principal and alternate haploid assemblies of the diploid genome. We conducted analyses on both assemblies for these species.

We conducted the BLAST search using probe sequences (Supplementary_data1) as query to search for TAS1R1, TAS1R2 and TAS1R3 sequences in these databases. Information on the protein-coding exons in the retrieved sequences is shown in Table S4 and Supplementary_data3. Only TAS1R sequences containing all the six complete exons were included in the subsequent analyses.

Mapping and assemblage of sequencing reads from targeted capture

We conducted mapping and assemblage of sequenced reads from TC by modifying the procedure described in Hou et al. (2024)25 (Fig. 2). We used the PRINSEQ tool for quality control of raw sequencing reads36. For FASTQ-formatted reads, we retained nucleotide positions with a quality score above 20 at both ends of each read. Additionally, we only kept reads with a mean quality score of at least 20 and a length greater than 20 nt.

Flowchart of analytical procedure.

We used TAS1R sequences from the same species or closely related species as reference for the first-round mapping (Table S5). At the time of mapping, six out of the eight targeted species had publicly available WGA databases (Fig. 1). For the first-round mapping, we prioritized using the TAS1R sequences identified from the same species as reference. If the study species lacked available public WGA databases or if complete TAS1R sequences were missing in the WGA databases, we used TAS1R sequences from WGA databases of the most closely related species among available WGA databases as reference (Table S5).

For the first-round mapping of the neutral reference genome regions, the choice of reference sequences was as follows. Tarsius_syrichta-2.0.1 sequences (Table S3−2, Supplementary_data2) were used for T. tarsier. If the sequence was missing for a neutral reference genome region in Tarsius_syrichta-2.0.1, the corresponding human sequence was used (Table S3−2, Supplementary_data2). OtoGar3 sequences (Table S3−2, Supplementary_data2) were used for lorisiform species. If the sequence was missing for a neutral reference genome region in OtoGar3, the corresponding Mmur_2.0 sequence was used (Table S3−2, Supplementary_data2). If Mmur_2.0 sequences were also missing, corresponding human sequences were used. Mmur_2.0 sequences were used for lemuriform species. If the sequence was missing for a neutral reference genome region in Mmur_2.0, the corresponding OtoGar3 sequence was used. If OtoGar3 sequences were also missing, corresponding human sequences were used.

First-round mapping

We mapped the filtered reads using bwa mem tool v. 0.7.17 to the references (Table S5) which were assigned to each TAS1R gene of each study species for the first-round mapping. After the mapping, by applying the MarkDuplicates of the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK)37–39, we identified the read pairs that were likely to have originated from duplicates of the same original DNA fragments through artefactual processes such as PCR amplification. Only a single read pair within each set of duplicates was used for the subsequent analyses.

The HaplotypeCaller of GATK (4.2.5.0) was used to assemble the mapped reads, to find insertion/deletion (indel) sites and heterozygous sites [designated here as single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sites], and to determine haplotype phase among these polymorphic sites based on their physical linkage in a sequencing read or in a paired-reads set. We defined an indel site as the site where insertion or deletion of a nucleotide was detected among reads relative to the reference sequence. We defined an SNP site as the site where two or more nucleotide kinds were present among reads. We did not count nucleotide sites as SNPs where only one nucleotide kind was detected among reads but the nucleotide kind was different from the one in the reference at the site. Two sequences were generated as putative haplotypes by bcftools consensus v. 1.7. If no read is mapped to a region in a reference sequence, the HaplotypeCaller generates chimeras consisting of the sample’s own sequences for the mapped region and the reference sequences for the unmapped region.

Second-round mapping

Less mismatch is expected between a sequence generated from the first-round mapping and a sequencing read from a gene/genome region. To improve the proportion of mapped regions, we conducted the second-round mapping using the chimeric sequences generated from the first-round mapping. For the second-round mapping references, we used one of the two putative haplotype sequences bearing nucleotides from the sample but not from the reference at SNP sites. Mapping was conducted as in the first mapping. Variant calling for each site was conducted with samtools mpileup v. 1.740 to identify unmapped regions. Two sequences were generated as putative haplotypes by bcftools consensus v. 1.7. In the generated sequences, we replaced nucleotides in these unmapped regions with ‘N’s. We evaluated the sequencing depth of the TAS1R genes and of the neutral references using the samtools bedcov40.

Third-round mapping

For the third-round mapping, we used the sequences generated from the second-round mapping including ‘N’ regions as references. There, we used one of the two putative haplotype sequences bearing a smaller number of deletions relative to the second-round reference sequence. We regarded genes/genome regions with low depth (< 10 per site) after the second-round mapping as being absent and excluded them from the third-round mapping to improve its specificity. Mapping was conducted as in the first mapping. The HaplotypeCaller was used to assemble the mapped reads and determine haplotype phase among SNPs and indels as in the first mapping. We further refined variant calls using VariantFiltration of GATK. Any variant sites with a genotype quality score less than 20 were filtered out. Two sequences were generated as putative haplotypes by bcftools consensus v. 1.7. The IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) degenerate nucleotide codes were used to designate unphased SNP sites with more than one nucleotide kinds. We removed the ‘N’ sites from the generated sequences, which makes them consist of only the sample’s own sequences.

After the third-round mapping, we evaluated the sequencing depth of the TAS1R genes in each exon and intron (Table S6−1) and of each neutral reference (Table S6−2) using the samtools bedcov40. When sequencing depth was lower than half of the minimum edge value of lower whisker range in boxplots, we regarded such TAS1R genes and the autosomal neutral references (NR-As) as being possibly absent and excluded them on a species-by-species basis from further analyses. We excluded neutral references shorter than 1000 bp from subsequent analyses. We also excluded from further analyses the NR-As with sequencing depth 1.5 times higher than the maximum edge value of upper whisker range in the boxplots as being potentially duplicated in at least one allele.

Determination of intact and disrupted genes

After the third-round mapping, we used HaplotypeCaller and bcftools41 to generate two putative haplotype sequences (H1 and H2) for further analysis (Fig. 2). We concatenated the sequences of the six coding exons of TAS1R genes into full-length coding sequences for further analysis. The open reading frame (ORF) was identified using EMBOSS getorf42. In TAS1R genes, the seven transmembrane domains are encoded in the sixth exon. If the identified ORF does not reach to the end of the sixth transmembrane domain, i.e., if the ORF could contain at most six transmembrane domains, we regarded such TAS1R sequences as disrupted genes. Based on the lengths of known functional TAS1R gene sequences of mammals, we excluded TAS1R sequences as possibly absent when the total length mapped to coding exons was less than 2340 bp. Among the TAS1R sequences retained, we regarded sequences with maximum ORF length less than 2340 bp (encoding for fewer than 780 amino acids) as disrupted. For TAS1R sequences with a maximum ORF greater than 2340 bp, we further applied DeepTMHMM43 to examine if the TAS1R sequence was capable of forming seven transmembrane (7-TM) domains. Only TAS1R sequences with an ORF of at least 2340 bp and capable of forming a seven transmembrane domain were classified as intact TAS1R genes.

To examine ORF and 7-TM structure, for the TAS1R sequences containing IUPAC degenerate nucleotide codes (indicating SNP sites with undetermined haplotype phases), we assigned tentative haplotype sequences to each pair of H1 and H2 sequences following Hou et al. (2024)25. This was because ORF and 7-TM structure can be disturbed by one amino acid site irrespective of haplotype phases.

If a gene in an individual is homozygous with intact alleles, we consider the gene to be an intact gene. If a gene in an individual is homozygous with disrupted alleles, we consider the gene to be a disrupted gene. If an individual is heterozygous with intact and disrupted alleles, we consider the gene to be a segregating disrupted gene.

Reconstruction of phylogenetic tree

Phylogenetic trees for full-length coding regions of TAS1R genes were reconstructed to confirm their identity by examining if gene sequences obtained by our TC were properly positioned in the trees together with known orthologous sequences in WGA databases. Phylogenetic trees for introns and flanking regions of TAS1R genes, those for neutral references, and those for synonymous nucleotide sites were reconstructed to examine if the mutation rates were different between lorisiform and lemuriform species by comparing branch lengths. Phylogenetic trees for amino acid sequences and non-synonymous sites were reconstructed to evaluate if natural selection has operated to conserve these sequences and to equate branch lengths between lorisiform and lemuriform species irrespective of difference of mutation rate.

We first made alignments for each of the protein-coding exons (20 species, including human as an anthropoid reference, for TAS1R1 and TAS1R3, and 18 species, including human, for TAS1R2 for which full-length sequences were available) and introns (12 species including human) of each TAS1R gene using MUSCLE44, and concatenated the aligned sequences for the exons and for the introns respectively for each gene. For aligning amino acid sequences, the premature stop codons and the insertion-nucleotide site depicted in Fig. 3 were removed from the corresponding concatenated exon sequences before translation. We aligned each of the neutral reference sequences (12 species including human) using MAFFT45 and subsequently concatenated all the neutral reference alignments.

The numbers of nucleotide substitutions per site between each pair of sequences were estimated using the Tamura-Nei model46. The numbers of amino acid substitutions per site between each pair of sequences were estimated for species examined for the exons using the Poisson model47. We examined the number of synonymous nucleotide substitutions per synonymous site and the number of non-synonymous nucleotide substitutions per non-synonymous site between each pair of sequences for species examined for the exons by following Nei and Gojobori (1986)48 with the Jukes-Cantor correction47. Gap sites were excluded in a pairwise manner in these distance calculations.

We reconstructed nucleotide and amino acid phylogenetic trees based on distance matrices for the nucleotide and amino acid substitutions, respectively, estimated above. We reconstructed these phylogenetic trees using the Neighbor-Joining method49 and assessed their reliability through 1,000 bootstrap replicates50. These analyses were conducted using the MEGA11 platform51.

Evolutionary rate test

We applied Tajima’s relative rate test52 in MEGA 11 to evaluate whether the evolutionary distance from Tarsius tarsier as the outgroup between lorisiform and lemuriform species was different in nucleotide or amino acid sequences. Concretely, we tested whether the number of unique differences in a lorisiform species was significantly different from that in a lemuriform species by chi square test with one degree of freedom52.

Neutral reference sequences were also retrieved from all eight study species by TC (Supplementary data5). Heterogeneity of evolutionary rate

We noticed that in the phylogenetic trees of the three TAS1R genes reconstructed using whole coding sequences (Figs. 4A, B, C), branch lengths from the strepsirrhine common ancestor to lorisiform species appeared longer than those to lemuriform species. By applying Tajima’s relative rate test52, this difference was verified to be statistically significant in TAS1R2 and TAS1R3 and marginally not significant in TAS1R1 using these gene sequences of Tarsius tarsier as an outgroup (Tables 6, S9). This trend of longer branches to lorisforms than to lemuriforms appeared more obvious when intron and flanking regions were used for reconstruction of phylogenetic trees of the three TAS1R genes (Figs. 4D, E, F). The same trend was also observed when the concatenated neutral reference sequences were used (Fig. 5). These differences were all statistically significant (Tables 6, S10, S11). While the Tajima’s relative rate test is not applicable to synonymous nucleotide sites of which the numbers vary among sequences48, the same trend of longer evolutionary branches to lorisforms than to lemuriforms was observed for the three genes (Figs. 6A, B, C). This was consistent with a genome-wide diversity study which revealed that mutation rate was generally higher in lorisiform species than in lemuriform species19.

For amino acid sequences, Tajima’s relative rate test verified the longer branch lengths to lorisiform species than to lemuriform species in the TAS1R2 tree with statistical significance using the Tarsius tarsier sequence as an outgroup (Tables 6, S12). Tajima’s relative rate test is not applicable to non-synonymous nucleotide sites as not applicable to synonymous sites48. There was no significant difference in evolutionary distances in amino acid sequences between lorisiform and lemuriform species in TAS1R1 and TAS1R3 using the Tarsius tarsier sequences as an outgroup (Tables 6, S12). When tarsiiform species and human were compared using a lemuriform species as an outgroup, there were no significant differences in any of the three TAS1R genes for amino acid sequences (data not presented).

Because intra-species polymorphism levels vary among species including non-anthropoid primates (Table 3)19, intra-species polymorphism could bring uncertainty to comparison of “fixed” components of nucleotide differences between species which reflects evolutionary rates. To evaluate the effect of intra-species nucleotide polymorphism on the rate test, we reconsidered species for which SNP density data was available (Table 3), i.e., species subjected to TC and species with WGA with principal and alternate haploid data accessible (Table S13 for TAS1R genes, Table S14 for neutral references). From the number of unique differences in a lorisiform species in TAS1R genes’ coding regions and in concatenated neutral references in each lorisiform species [Table S13−1-1-1 (TAS1R1), Table S13−2-1-1 (TAS1R2), Table S13−3-1-1 (TAS1R3), Table S14−1-1 (neutral references)], we subtracted half the number of SNP sites. We subtracted half of the number of SNP sites because the number of nucleotide changes that occurred in the branch from the common ancestral allele to each of current alleles can be approximated by averaging the number of SNP sites between the two alleles. We then evaluated “fixed” unique differences in a lorisiform species [Table S13−1-1–2 (TAS1R1), Table S13−2-1–2 (TAS1R2), Table S13−3-1–2 (TAS1R3), Table S14−1-2 (neutral references)]. In the same way, from the numbers of unique differences in a lemuriform species [Table S13−1-2-1 (TAS1R1), Table S13−2-2-1 (TAS1R2), Table S13−3-2-1 (TAS1R3), Table S14−2-1 (neutral references)], we subtracted half the number of SNP sites of corresponding gene/region in each lemuriform species to deduce “fixed” unique differences in a lemuriform species [Table S13−1-2-2 (TAS1R1), Table S13−2-2-2 (TAS1R2), Table S13−3-2-2 (TAS1R3), Table S14−2-2 (neutral references)].

From these tables, we see that proportion of intra-species nucleotide polymorphism in inter-species nucleotide difference (evaluated as ([B]/[A]) x 100 (%) in these tables) are 0.0 ~ 3.4% in TAS1R genes (Table S13) and 2.4%~4.2% in neutral references (Table S14). We conducted Tajima’s relative rate test using the numbers of unique differences in lorisiform or lemuriform species excluding the intra-species differences (Table S13−1-3 ~ 5, Table S13−2-3 ~ 5, Table S13−3-3 ~ 5, Table S14−3 ~ 5). Results were the same as those we present in Table 6 from our original analyses: the branch lengths leading to lorisiform species were significantly longer than those to lemuriform species in neutral references. In the coding region of TAS1R genes, this trend was statistically significant in TA1R2 and TAS1R3, and marginally not significant in TAS1R1.

Therefore, these results supported that in lorisiforms TAS1R1 and TAS1R3, but not TAS1R2, were under strict functional constraint and purifying selection to maintain amino acid sequences opposing to the high mutation rate while in tarsiiforms and lemuriforms all the three TAS1R genes were be under strict selective constraint. In other words, selective constraint on TAS1R2 is relaxed pan-taxonomically in lorisiform species.

Discussion

In this study, we focused on non-anthropoid primates (Infraorders Lorisiformes, Lemuriformes and Tarsiiformes) to investigate whether their umami/sweet taste receptor genes, TAS1R1, TAS1R2 and TAS1R3, have been kept intact, disrupted or lost during evolution. We also aimed to test whether the intact genes have been under strict functional constraint by examining their conservativeness of evolutionary rates. We not only examined public whole genome assembly (WGA) databases for the three genes of eighteen non-anthropoid species accessible at the time of our study but also conducted targeted capture (TC) of the three genes from genomic DNA samples of eight non-anthropoid species. Many (six) TC species overlapped with the species examined for WGA, enabling us to directly compare the two datasets. Taken together we investigated twenty non-anthropoid primate species with diverse diets (Fig. 1, Table S1).

The sequencing depths achieved through TC were 5 ~ 27 times higher for the TAS1R genes than those of the publicly available WGA databases (Table 2). One to all the three TAS1R genes were partially or completely missing in six out of the eighteen WGA databases, but we retrieved all three genes with their full length in all eight species we examined with TC (Tables 4 and 5). We were also able to retrieve two allele sequences, albeit including phase-unresolved SNPs, for all eight species from TC while we only could retrieve two alleles from WGA with principal and alternate haploid assemblies (greater slow loris and ring-tailed lemur). Thus, our TC successfully complemented the currently missing information in WGAs examined in this study.

Integrating WGA and our TC data, we found that the TAS1R1 gene of West African potto and Northern lesser galago was polymorphic with intact and disrupted alleles (Figs. 3A, B, C). In particular, West African potto has at least two coding-region-disrupted variant alleles. It is currently unclarified if the two variants share a common disruption in the non-coding regulatory region. We also found that TAS1R2 gene of red slender loris was disrupted (Fig. 3D). It remains to be elucidated whether the disruption of TAS1R2 gene of red slender loris is fixed or segregating in the species population. Interestingly, these disruptions were only observed in lorisiform species but not in lemuriforms or tarsiiform species. Intriguingly, in both genes, disruptions appear to be species-specific and do not reflect a pan-taxonomic event. Furthermore, regarding TAS1R1, disruptions are on-going at least in West African potto and Northern lesser galago, thus reflecting possibly recent phenomena which occurred in a species-unique way.

A conventional approach to test relaxation of purifying selection is to compare synonymous and nonsynonymous nucleotide substitutions (“dN/dS” tests). An assumption behind these popular tests is that the mutation rate is the same between the two evolutionary branches being compared. Then, natural selection is evaluated as a deviation from the shared mutation rate (neutral substitution rate). In this study, this assumption was very clearly violated, which complicates interpretations of conventional dN/dS analyses. This is why we took an unconventional approach that examined firstly the difference of mutation rates between the two lineages using supposedly neutral regions (introns and flanking regions, synonymous sites, and neutral references). After verifying the significant difference of mutation rates between lorisiforms and lemuriforms, we then evaluated evolutionary rates of amino acid sequences and non-synonymous sites (i.e., putatively under some form of natural selection) to examine how selection pressures have influenced the difference in mutation rates.

Due to the limited availability of samples, our assessment of intra-species nucleotide polymorphism is relatively poor. However, even with one sample from an individual from each species, the SNP density would reflect the general level of intra-species nucleotide polymorphism. In fact, the order of magnitude of the SNP density values evaluated in this study (0.00 ~ 3.90/1000 bp in TAS1R genes and 2.55 ~ 8.40/1000 bp in neutral references: Table 3) was in the same range as that reported for whole-genome sequencing data of many lorisiforms, lemuriforms and tarsiers (0.5 ~ 6 × 10−3) 19. Our Tajima’s relative rate tests including intra-species polymorphism (Tables 6,S9,S10 ,S11,S12) and those excluding it (Tables S13, S14) were concordant each other. Overall, these analyses confirmed and consolidated our conclusion: selective constraint on TAS1R2 is relaxed pan-taxonomically in lorisiform species.

Relaxation of functional constraint appears to be pan-taxonomic among lorisiform species regarding TAS1R2 based on our comparison of evolutionary rates between lorisiforms and lemuriforms while the gene appears to be maintained in intact forms. It is not currently clear if the disruption of TAS1R2 found in red slender loris WGA is exceptional or not. Further analyses are needed to test if the reactivity of TAS1R2 to sugars is maintained high, reduced or lost among lorisiform species.

Table S1 presents the dietary information of non-anthropoid primate species considered in this study. Although it appears to be not feasible overall to extract clear-cut dietary differences that could explain the species-specific or taxon-specific differences of TAS1R genes found in this study, lorisiform species are overall less dependent on fruits than lemuriform species. The pan-taxonomic relaxation of selective constraint on TAS1R2 among lorisiform species suggests that the relaxation was initiated at the common ancestor of lorisiform species. This would suggest that a dietary shift towards lower frugivory occurred at the common ancestor and that sweet sensing became less important in this lorisiform ancestor than in extant or ancestral lemuriform primates. West African pottos eat mainly fruits and gums and only occasionally eat insects that other species do not15. Supposing that the less-frugivorous feeding habit is an ancestral state of lorisiforms, species-specific frugivorous feeding habits could be derived secondary traits. This secondary dietary shift may have loosened the selective pressure to maintain the functional TAS1R1 gene in a species-specific way. Although further analyses, including ligand assays for the receptors, behavioral evaluations, and assays of non-taste-sensing functions54, are necessary to be conclusive, these results shed a new light on understanding evolutionary diversification of umami and sweet sensing in non-anthropoid primates.
