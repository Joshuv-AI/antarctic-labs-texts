Wilson disease is a single gene disorder – comprehensive analysis of patients with prior negative genetic testing
Authors: Dorte L. Lildballe, Anne Grosen, Peter Ott, Isabelle Mohr, Zoe Mariño, Ayse K. Coskun, Uyen To, Sihoun Hahn, Karl Heinz Weiss, Michael L. Schilsky, Thomas D. Sandahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsWilson disease (WD) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by damaging ATP7B variants. Up to 20% of clinically diagnosed cases remain genetically unexplained. We aimed to analyze patients with WD (Leipzig score ≥4) with zero or one known ATP7B variant using state-of-the-art methods.MethodsAmong 761 patients from four WD cohorts previously screened for ATP7B variants, 44 with zero or one known variant in ATP7B were sequenced by current best practice methods (whole genome sequencing) and the data were analyzed in a stepwise approach: first searching for pathogenic variants in ATP7B, then in 97 copper-related genes, and finally in 4,304 genes linked to liver or neurological diseases. If unsolved, long-read ATP7B sequencing and/or ATP7B protein surrogate peptide analysis was conducted when possible.ResultsA total of 5.8% of patients had zero or only one known variant in ATP7B. Median (range) Leipzig score was 6 (4-11). Primary presentations at diagnosis were hepatic (24), neurologic (12), or mixed (8). Genetic analysis of ATP7B resolved 52% (23/44) of cases: 7 through reclassification of previously reported findings and 16 by whole genome sequencing detecting previously unreported ATP7B variants. ATP7B peptide measurement detected six additional patients with ATP7B protein defects, supporting pathogenicity of variants. Together, these methods confirmed ATP7B dysfunction in 66% (29/44). In our entire cohort, this corresponded to genetic confirmation in 98% (746/761). Comprehensive screening of other genes did not identify alternative genetic causes.ConclusionOur comprehensive data strongly support WD as a single-gene disease. Genetic reevaluation, ATP7B resequencing, and peptide analysis should be performed in unresolved cases.Impact and implicationsThis work addresses the molecular diagnostic gap in clinically confirmed Wilson disease by demonstrating that most genetically unresolved cases can be explained by previously undetected or misclassified ATP7B variants when modern genomic and functional approaches are applied. The findings are particularly relevant for clinicians and patients facing diagnostic uncertainty, as they reinforce Wilson disease as a single-gene disorder rather than a genetic heterogeneous condition. Practically, the study supports routine genetic reevaluation using contemporary whole-genome sequencing and targeted functional assays in unresolved cases, improving diagnostic certainty, genetic counseling, and cascade testing. While some advanced analyses were not feasible in all patients, the results highlight the value of revisiting negative genetic findings before considering alternative diagnoses.

Introduction

Wilson disease (WD; OMIM #277900) is a rare inherited disorder caused by malfunction of the copper transport protein P-type ATPase (ATP7B).1,2 The malfunction or loss-of-function of the ATP7B protein results in impaired copper excretion into the bile, which leads to pathological copper accumulation, primarily in the liver and brain. Consequently, patients may present a spectrum of hepatic, neurologic, and psychiatric symptoms.1 Diagnosis may be straightforward in classic presentations, whereas patients with subtle symptoms may experience diagnostic delay and consequently adverse outcomes. Timely identification of WD is crucial, as early initiation of treatment can prevent and potentially improve symptoms and reduce organ damage, associated disability, and mortality.3,4

No single clinical or laboratory test is adequate for excluding or diagnosing WD. The diagnosis relies on the Leipzig score, which integrates clinical evaluation, biochemical parameters, histologic findings, and genetic testing.2,5,6 According to the Leipzig score, which requires 4 points or more for a definite diagnosis, a clear genetic result with two identified pathogenic variants can lead to a diagnosis, but the diagnosis can also be made in the absence of abnormal genetic findings.

Except for small, often historically isolated populations, the prevalence of WD is estimated to be 1:30,000, which results in a general carrier frequency of 1:90.2 Studies estimating carrier frequency based on genomic data in non-WD populations show a broader carrier range from approximately 1:30 to 1:90.[7], [8], [9], [10] This variation may reflect inconsistency in variant interpretation, i.e. variants of unknown significance (VUS) being interpreted as likely causal or not. It may also reflect reduced penetrance and variability in disease expressivity, which can vary even within families.6

Pathogenic variants in ATP7B are known to cause autosomal recessive WD. Although founder variants exist in different populations,[11], [12], [13], [14] the heterogeneity is large with more than a thousand variants reported as disease-causing in the literature.15 However, clinically confirmed WD (Leipzig score ≥4) with an incomplete genetic explanation of disease can be found in most large WD cohorts. In a mixed international cohort of 1,357 patients with WD, 21.3% had zero or only one pathogenic variant identified,5 while in a British cohort of 181 patients, extensive testing by the Sheffield Diagnostic Genetics Service still left 2% with only one or no disease-causing variant identified.8 This has led to speculation about potential non-ATP7B pathogenic variants as well as possible epigenetic factors causing WD. Identifying the genetic cause of a disease allows for genetic testing in the family and is therefore important for more individuals than just the patient.

In this study, we combined data from four WD registries and identified the minority of patients in whom less than two disease-causing ATP7B variants had been identified. We hypothesized that more disease-causing variants could be identified by using the most recent techniques that allow for detection of variant types, which may be missed by previous methods such as Sanger sequencing.16 Since other very rare diseases can mimic WD17 and even produce diagnostic Leipzig scores,18 we expected that a fraction of the still unsolved cases would be caused by other WD-mimicking inherited diseases.

We employed whole genome sequencing (WGS) to explore whether complete genetic screening of ATP7B would improve the diagnostic yield in these unsolved cases. In patients with WD, where this extended strategy did not provide a genetic confirmation of WD, we looked for possible disease-causing variants in other genes involved in copper and liver metabolism. Further, we measured ATP7B peptides in dried blood spots, as a previous study showed this assay helps reduce ambiguity from genetic test results.19

Patients and methods

Patients

A total of 764 patients from four WD tertiary expert centers were screened: 457 from the University Hospital Heidelberg, Germany; 197 patients from Yale New Haven Hospital, USA; 65 from Hospital Clínic Barcelona, Spain; and 45 from Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark. Three patients did not meet inclusion criteria (2 unaffected family members, and 1 who did not meet Leipzig criteria and whose WD diagnosis was withdrawn). Thus, 761 patients with WD were included with a Leipzig score ≥4 and local genetic testing at the time of clinical diagnosis. Among these, we identified patients diagnosed with WD but lacking genetic confirmation, i.e. with zero or only one identified disease-causing variant. We re-read and verified the original genetic reports to confirm correct registration, and the clinical diagnosis was revisited and verified by WD experts in the center.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from whole blood using standard methods at the participant centers. Extracted DNA was stored at –18 °C or lower and shipped on dry ice to Aarhus.

The genetic analyses were entirely carried out at Department of Molecular Medicine, Aarhus University Hospital, which is ISO 15 189 certified.

SR-WGS and pipeline

DNA sequencing libraries were prepared using Illumina DNA PCR-free library preparation kit. All patient samples were analyzed by SR-WGS. WGS was conducted on NovaSeq 6000 instruments as 2x150 bp paired end sequencing. Demultiplexing, mapping, and variant calling followed GATK best practice. Briefly, reads are mapped to the hg38 reference genome using BWA-MEM,20 and PCR- and optical duplicates are marked using Picard MarkDuplicates.21 Single nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions (<50 bp) are called using GATK HaplotyperCaller,22 and structural variants are called with Manta23 and Delly.24

For all samples, mean (SD) coverage was 4411 X; mean (SD) insert size was 41117 bp; and mean (SD) percentage of genome covered 20X was at least 952 %.

AS-LRS and pipeline

Cases where SR-WGS did not confirm WD with certainty, were analyzed by AS-LRS. Samples with insufficient DNA quality or quantity were excluded.

DNA sequencing libraries were prepared using Nanopore Ligation Sequencing Kit V14. We used 3 μg of DNA as input, performed RNase treatment, followed by bead-based clean-up (AMPure XP beads, Beckman Coulter Life Sciences), and fragmented the DNA in g-TUBES (Covaris) by centrifugation at 2,500 × g to an N50 of ∼10 kb before library preparation. The libraries were sequenced on a PromethION P24 (Oxford Nanopore Technogies) with MinKNOW ver. 24·06·10 (Oxford Nanopore Technogies) on R10·4·1 flow cells (Oxford Nanopore Technogies) with Adaptive Sampling using the human reference genome T2T-CHM13v2·0 for mapping and with enrichment of chromosome 13 as the region of interest. The libraries were sequenced to at least 15 Gb of estimated bases. Sequencing data was aligned to the reference human genome hg38 using Minimap2(25). Structural variants were called using Sniffles226 and CuteSV27 and coverage-based copy number variants were called using HiFiCNV (PacBio). Single nucleotide variants and indels were called using Clair3,28 and variant phasing was performed with LongPhase.27 Methylation information is computed per CpG site using modkit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies), and differentially methylated regions across the two haplotypes are identified using Dispersion Shrinkage for Sequencing (DSS) in R (R-project). For all samples, mean (SD) coverage was 433 X, and mean (SD) read length was 8,618 (720) bp.

Variant filtering

Variant filtering was performed using Varseq 2.6.0 (Golden Helix) with the filter settings: 1) Minor allele frequency gnomAD (all) 4.0.0 <0.01, minor allele frequency (inhouse n = 2,702 samples) <0.02.2) Variant quality: read depths (DP) ≥2, variant allele frequency ≥0.25.

For cases that remained unsolved after the above, we performed expanded ATP7B gene region variant filtering (region chr13(GRCh38): 51,850,000 - 52,100,000) to identify very rare variants (minor allele frequency gnomAD (all) 4.0.0 <0.001). These data are reported in Table S2.

For gene panels in steps 2 and 3 (Fig. 1), we applied additional filters, including variants that met either of the following criteria: 3a) likely pathogenic, defined as REVEL >0.9, Ada >0, ClinVar (likely) pathogenic, or VarSeq ACMG autoclassifier (likely) pathogenic; or 3b) sequence ontology filters, excluding UTR, downstream, intergenic, or intronic variants located >50 bp from exons, and upstream variants unless previously reported as (likely) pathogenic in ClinVar.

Variant classification

Filtered variants were classified according to the ACMG classification system29 based on five classes: benign (Class 1), likely benign (LB, Class 2), variant of unknown significance (VUS, Class 3), likely pathogenic (LP, Class 4), and pathogenic (P, Class 5). When peptide measurements were available, this information was used as a functional criterion (PS3 or BS3).

All variants in this publication have been submitted to ClinVar (submission SUB14694725).

Peptide measurement

For patients, where the results of genetic testing did not fully explain WD, the amount of ATP7B peptide was analyzed in whole blood when available. The method has been described previously.19 Briefly, two different antibodies against ATP7B peptides, ATP7B 1,056 and ATP7B 887, were used for peptide enrichment and the amount of APT7B peptides were measured using liquid chromatography mass spectrometry.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Yale University as part of the Wilson Disease Registry (WDR) study, which includes patients from the US, Germany, and Denmark. WDR participation has been approved by each of the participating institutions separately. Patients participating from Spain were under separate Material Transfer Agreement and Institutional Review Board approval as Spain was not a member of the WDR at the time of study startup (reference: HCB/2024/0352).

The Department of Molecular Medicine, Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark performed the genetic analyses as a service provider under a separate agreement.

All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declaration of Helsinki and the Declaration of Istanbul.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination of our research.

The sequential examination of the 44 unsolved cases is illustrated in Fig. 1. All samples were examined for variants in ATP7B by SR-WGS (summary of findings in Table 2 and S2). This genetic reexamination confirmed previously reported findings in ATP7B in all cases but also provided new information.

From analytical step 1, we sorted the findings into three groups: solved (n = 23; 52%), uncertain (n = 9; 20%), and unsolved (n = 12; 27%).

In the first “Solved” group (n = 23), we were able to identify variants in 16 (69.5%) patients not detected by the initially performed laboratory method. Among the ATP7B variants identified in the patients, 11 variants have not previously been published in the literature (according to HGMD 2025.2). In the remaining seven cases, the previously reported genetics variants in ATP7B were reclassified from VUS to (likely) pathogenic and confirmed the diagnosis. In 11 of these solved cases, the initial method was the so-called “hot-spot” method, where only selected regions of ATP7B were sequenced. Additionally, in one of the cases (case #10), the two variants identified in the current study were in exon 14, which for unknown reasons was left out in the original analysis, although this is considered a “hot spot” region (see Table S2). In a second case (case #11), we identified a complex structural variant where several exons were deleted, and some were inverted. This type of variant could not be detected using the original Sanger sequencing method. Detecting such variants can be challenging with the methods used in this study, as accurate identification of structural variants depends on the bioinformatic tools applied to the sequencing data.

In the “Uncertain group”, we identified 9 (20%) patients with one (L)P variant and one VUS in ATP7B. In the “Unsolved group”, 12 (27%) patients either had none or only one (L)P variant in ATP7B. These 21 patients were all included in the subsequent genetic analysis for genes related to copper metabolism and hereditary liver diseases (Steps 3 and 4 in Fig. 1). However, none of the 21 patients were genetically diagnosed with a possible differential diagnosis. In nine patients, we identified a single (L)P variant in genes associated with autosomal recessively inherited disorders. The unsolved group was also analyzed for the presence of rare variants in the regions (>20 kb) flanking ATP7B, see Table S2. None of these findings could explain the phenotype of the included patients and only disclosed carrier status of rare liver-associated diseases. When sample material was still available, we also analyzed DNA with LRS. This did not result in the identification of any genetic variants that could explain WD.

We had blood samples available for ATP7B peptide testing in 13 of the 21 patients with an uncertain or unsolved genetic confirmation of WD; see results in Table 2. The analysis showed no or significantly reduced peptide levels in six patients. This allowed for subsequent reclassification of ATP7B variants increasing the diagnostic yield to 66% (29/44) in total. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study performing systematic comprehensive genetic sequencing in patients with non-genetically confirmed WD, although these patients had been previously analyzed in expert laboratories.

Most clinical cohorts comprise a fraction of WD cases that cannot be explained by conventional genetic analysis of ATP7B. This raises the question of whether these patients have alterations in the ATP7B gene that we have not been able to detect, or if they, in fact, have other WD-like diseases.30

From our total combined cohort of 761 patients with WD, only 44 were previously genetically unsolved. This relatively low frequency of genetically unsolved cases may be attributable to all included sites being tertiary referral centers with long experience and adequate genetic services. Seven of the 44 (16%) were solved solely by reevaluation of previously reported findings of unknown significance. This is in concordance with large reevaluation studies of VUS in general.31,32 In a clinical routine setting, we therefore recommend reevaluation of previously reported variants before resequencing. Upfront WGS confirmed two disease-causing ATP7B variants in 16 of the 44 (36%) patients. In most cases, these variants had been missed, as expected from known pitfalls of the previous methodologies. This finding supports the use of full ATP7B sequencing in patients with WD in whom previously used methods produce inconclusive results and is in line with general studies analyzing the diagnostic yield of WGS.33

The unsolved group was also analyzed for the presence of rare variants flanking ATP7B (S2). This identified that cases 32 and 35 shared one rare variant (rs9535857) and cases 42 and 43 shared several indicating that they share the same allele. Cases 42 and 43 are not known to be related but originate from the same population. They have different pathogenic variants on the other allele and both show peptide levels within the reference range, indicating that the shared allele is not causal.

Additional comprehensive screening of ATP7B using methods other than SR-WGS, as well as comprehensive screening of other genes known to be associated with liver and/or neurological disease, did not result in genetic confirmation of diagnosis in any of the remaining 21 of 44 patients. To our surprise we did not find any pathogenic variants in genes coding for proteins important for intracellular copper handling, e.g. chaperones or other transporters. This could indicate that malfunction of these proteins is incompatible with life. Furthermore, this adds to the hypothesis that WD is a single-gene disorder.

Among the 21 patients without a certain diagnosis after genetic testing, we tested whole blood ATP7B peptide levels in 13 using a recently published method.19 Of these, six patients showed no or significantly reduced peptide level. This allowed us to further refine the classification of six variants as likely pathogenic. In two patients (#22, #34), we detected two (likely) pathogenic ATP7B variants and measured significantly reduced ATP7B peptide levels, though not zero. This is likely to reflect that one allele is not completely defective, but that its function is significantly reduced compared to the unaffected gene. This may indicate hypomorphic variants and these patients may show a milder phenotype. This is in line with emerging evidence for varying penetrance of WD variants.34 We cannot exclude that peptide analysis would also have confirmed the diagnosis in some of the six patients where samples were not available.

In our study, patient #22 had a gene variant predicted to be synonymous (p.Tyr715=). This variant has previously been suggested to be benign as the variant apparently did not affect splicing.35 However, our peptide measurement shows that the variant affects the peptide level in the blood although the mechanism is unknown. One hypothesis is that alternative codon usage reduces translation efficiency. This synonymous variant changes the tRNA codon from UAC to UAU, and the former is present at a much higher level than the latter, hence it may be limiting the final expression level of the protein.36 In contrast, patient #21 had a different synonymous variant (p.Phe764=) together with the well-known pathogenic variant p.His1069Gln. This patient had a peptide level within the reference range, indicating that this synonymous variant does not affect the peptide level in the blood, although the introduced codon (UUU) is rare for Phe. We consider this variant likely benign, which is also supported by previously published results on this specific variant.35

A 2013 study from UK reported 98% of WD cases being genetically solved8 while a 2019 study based on data from eight different countries solved 78% of cases.5

Importantly, in the UK study, 77 patients were excluded due to insufficient clinical information or the assignment of an alternative diagnosis by the referring physician, raising the possibility of selection bias. In contrast, our study included all patients diagnosed with WD based on clinical criteria (Leipzig score ≥4) regardless of genetic findings, thereby offering a more representative view of variant detection in routine clinical practice. Consistent with previous reports, 5.8% (44/761) of our genetically tested patients lacked a conclusive molecular diagnosis at the time of inclusion.

Unfortunately, our sample set had availability bias, as not all samples were available for testing by all methods. Still, following our comprehensive ATP7B analysis of 761 clinically confirmed WD cases (Leipzig score ≥4), ATP7B defects were identified as the cause in 98% (746/761).

Our data strongly supports the value of functional analysis to further classify ATP7B VUS, in our case using peptide measurement. Many clinical genetic laboratories lack assays that reliably assess the effect of variants on protein level. Our study also emphasizes the need to consider sampling for protein and RNA analysis in future collections of sample material as we do not know the tools of tomorrow.

In summary, our data strengthen the hypothesis that WD is a single-gene disorder. If previous ATP7B sequencing has reported VUS, we recommend the first step should be variant reevaluation. When sequencing, we recommend using a method that allows for detection of variants in the entire ATP7B gene. Moreover, peptide analysis supports the diagnosis even without genetically confirmed diagnosis.
