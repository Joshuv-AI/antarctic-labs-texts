Laboratory cultivated Cordyceps militaris modulates cockroach allergen-induced inflammation in keratinocytes and macrophages dual cell platforms
Authors: Mey-Fann Lee, Jane-Yii Wu, Chi-Sheng Wu, Ching-Chun Lin, Yu-Wen Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCordyceps militaris (CM) is noted for its anti-inflammatory properties and potential efficacy in treating allergic diseases. This research aimed to investigate the anti-inflammatory effect of ethanolic and aqueous extracts from laboratory-cultivated specific Cordyceps militaris on Per a 2-induced keratinocytes and macrophages.MethodsThe effects of aqueous and ethanolic extracts were evaluated in Per a 2-stimulated HaCaT and RAW264.7 cell models. This study encompassed cytotoxicity assays, quantitative real-time PCR, nitric oxide inhibitory assays, Western blot analysis, and cytokine assays.ResultsThe ethanolic and aqueous extracts significantly reduced the Per a 2-induced mRNA levels of IL-8, GM-CSF, and CCL-20 in a dose-dependent manner in HaCaT cells, and the optimal doses of extracts were 20 and 125 µg/mL, respectively. The ethanolic extracts of CM significantly inhibited production of iNOS and NO and downregulated COX-2 in Per a 2-induced RAW264.7 cells. Furthermore, the ethanolic extracts of CM also significantly attenuated secretion of IL-13, IL-2, TNF-α, IL-6, G-CSF, and GM-CSF in a dose-dependent manner in Per a 2-stimulated RAW264.7 cells. High-performance liquid chromatography analyses revealed that the concentrations of cordycepin, adenosine, ergosterol, adenine, uridine, and uracil in the ethanolic extracts of CM were 3.87, 61.24, 44.76, 39.40, 26.68, and 4.66 mg/mL, respectively. The ethanolic extracts of CM, which contain enriched quantities of these compounds, is more effective in terms of anti-inflammation than aqueous extracts of CM.ConclusionsThese important findings reported herein suggest that ethanolic extracts of CM could be further developed as a potential herbal remedy for aeroallergen-induced skin inflammation.Trial registrationNot applicable. Background

The American cockroach (Periplaneta americana) is one of the major indoor allergens in Taiwan [1] and the predominant species present in tropical and subtropical regions [2–4]. Previous studies indicated that the top 3 dominant indoor allergens, house dust mites, cockroaches, and mold could induce allergic airway diseases [3]. In Taiwan, 57.5% of asthmatic patients and 50.7% of rhinitis patients are allergic to cockroaches, which are a major indoor allergen for immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated respiratory diseases [5, 6]. In respiratory allergy, cockroach allergen Per a 2 plays an important role in asthma severity [6]. Previous study findings demonstrated that repeated brushing of Per a 2 on the skin leads to atopic dermatitis (AD) [7]. Our data also indicated that American cockroaches and mite allergens could initiate atopic march through cutaneous contact to induce AD-like symptoms and systemic inflammation in a murine model [8, 9]. AD is generally the first allergic manifestation of atopic march and in patients with progression of AD, it is estimated that approximately one-third will develop asthma and two-thirds will develop allergic rhinitis [10, 11]. However, information on the initiation and activation of innate immunity by cockroach allergens on the skin is also limited.

Keratinocytes are known to be activated by a variety of environmental stimuli and to release several cytokines contributing to the development of AD [12, 13]. The release of cytokines by both T cells and keratinocytes might play a key role in skin inflammation and could drive many AD-related itch and symptoms [12]. Skin barrier dysfunction might be a potential mechanism whereby aeroallergens could initiate allergic dermatitis [14, 15]. Macrophages regulate various inflammatory mediators and enhance pro-inflammatory cytokines to induce an immune response [16]. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α induces an inflammatory reaction via activation of macrophages and T cells as well as enhancement of other pro-inflammatory cytokines. Keratinocytes are also stimulated with TNF-α and IL-17 to release various cytokines and chemokines [17].

Cordyceps militaris, a well-regarded medicinal mushroom with an extensive history in Asian traditional medicine, demonstrates diverse pharmacological functions [18–23]. The primary bioactive constituents in C. militaris, cordycepin and adenosine, are associated with a broad spectrum of biological activities, encompassing anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory effects, as well as antioxidant, antiviral, and antibacterial properties [20, 24, 25]. Herbal compounds and natural plant extracts hold promise for advancement as therapeutic agents or preventive supplements [26–28]. Therefore, exploring herbal remedies that could contribute to novel preventive or therapeutic approaches for skin inflammation or allergic skin diseases is meritorious.

Several studies have also used HaCaT and RAW264.7 cells to examine the therapeutic effects of botanical compounds against the inflammatory and allergic responses of allergic skin diseases or atopic dermatitis [16, 29]. In this pilot study, we utilized cockroach allergen-stimulated HaCaT and RAW264.7 cell models to evaluate anti-inflammatory effects of extract from Cordyceps militaris.

Methods

Cell culture and allergen stimulation

HaCaT (human immortalized keratinocyte) and RAW264.7 (murine macrophage) cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA) at 37 °C under 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Cells were passaged prior to 90% confluence and after 10 passages were no longer used for the treatment. For the allergen-challenged experiments, the cells were seeded in 6-well culture plates at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/mL (HaCaT cells) or 5 × 105 cells/mL (RAW264.7 cells) for 24 h. The cells were then rinsed with dPBS and incubated for another 16 h in a serum-free medium. Subsequently, the cells were treated with lab-prepared endo-free E.coli-expressed recombinant Per a 2 (E-rPer a 2) for 1–24 h [1]. At the end of each experiment, culture supernatants were collected and stored at −70 °C until assay for cytokines. Cell pellets were disrupted immediately by RNAzol reagent (Invitrogen) for RNA extraction or lysed by PRO-PREP protein extraction solution (iNtRON Biotechnology) for protein preparation.

Preparation of crude extracts isolated from specific Cordyceps militaris

We used a specific Cordyceps militaris strain, which was previously identified by Dr. JY Wu and named Cordyceps militaris WU-CMS, deposited and submitted to NCBI GenBank with the accession numbers KM197166 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/670604910) (Supplementary Figure S1). The culture was maintained on potato dextrose agar (Himedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India) slants at 20 °C for 2 weeks and stored at 4 °C. The substrate was prepared by filling a 400-mL glass bottle with 20 g of brown rice and 40 mL of medium (consisting of 20 g/L glucose, 0.5 g/L yeast extract, 1.0 g/L peptone, 1.0 g/L MgSO4 × 7H2O, and 1.0 g/L K2HPO4) and autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min. The substrates were cooled to room temperature and inoculated with 10 mL of inoculum culture. The cultivation of C. militaris WU-CMS fruiting bodies is divided into three stages. In the white mycelial growth stage, rice substrates were prepared by incubating inoculated substrates in a germination chamber at 20 °C in the dark for 2 weeks. In the primordial initiation stage, C. militaris WU-CMS was grown at 18 °C and exposed to light at an intensity of 500 lx for 12 h daily. In the fruiting stage, the temperature was re-adjusted to 22 °C to retain the light pattern required for fruiting body development. At the end of cultivation, the fruiting bodies of C. militaris WU-CMS were cleaned and dried at 50 °C for 2 days.

Then, 10-g samples of dried Cordyceps militaris were powdered and extracted either with 100 mL of 95% ethanol using an ultrasonicator at 50 °C for 30 min or 100 mL distilled water in an autoclave at 121 °C for 30 min, respectively. The insoluble materials were removed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were freeze-dried. The aqueous extract of Cordyceps militaris (W-CM) was dissolved in sterilized water and the ethanolic extract (E-CM) was dissolved in DMSO for cell experiments.

Cytotoxicity assay of extracts from specific Cordyceps militaris

The cell viability was analyzed using Cell Meter™ Colorimetric WST-8 Cell Quantification Kit (AAT Bioquest, CA, USA). The HaCaT or RAW264.7 cells were cultured at a density of 5 × 103 or 1 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates, respectively. The cells were treated with various concentrations of W-CM or E-CM and cultured for 24 h. Then, 10 µL of the WST-8 solution was added to each well and the plates were incubated for an additional 2 h. Finally, absorbance was measured at 460 nm with an ELISA reader (TECAN).

Quantitative real-time PCR

HaCaT (2.5 × 105 cells/mL) or RAW264.7 (5 × 105 cells/mL) cells were plated in a 6-well plate for 24 h. After starvation for 16 h, cells were pre-treated with various doses of E-CM for 1 h. Subsequently, 10 µg of E-rPer a 2 were added to each well and incubated for 4 h. Total RNA was extracted from the treated cells by TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and the cDNA was prepared as described previously [3, 5]. Predesigned primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a StepOnePlus™ system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The gene expression data were imported into an Excel database and expressed as a fold increase between medium alone and allergen-stimulated or co-treated cells after correction by the housekeeping gene β-actin (RAW264.7 cells) or GAPDH (HaCaT cells).

Primers for real-time PCR

Nitric oxide inhibitory assays of ethanolic-CM

Nitric oxide (NO) is a signaling molecule that plays a key role in the pathogenesis of inflammation. The allergic inflammation of RAW264.7 cells was stimulated by recombinant allergen Per a 2 with or without E-CM. Briefly, the RAW264.7 cells were pre-treated with 5–40 µg/mL of E-CM for 1 h in 96-well plates and then treated with 10 µg/mL of E-rPer a 2 for a further 24 h at 37 °C. Then, a 100 µL of Griess reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) was mixed with 100 µL of culture supernatant. The mixed solution was incubated in the dark for 10 min at room temperature, followed by determination of the absorbance at 540 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN). The concentration of NO in the sample was determined from a standard calibration curve prepared from sodium nitrite (NaNO2, from 0 to 100 µM).

Western blotting

The protein expression level of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) was determined by Western blotting. RAW264.7 cells were pretreated for 1 h with E-CM at doses of 5–20 µg/mL and then treated with 10 µg/mL of E-rPer a 2 for a further 24 h. The treated cells were lysed by adding PRO-PREP protein extraction solution (iNtRON biotechnology) and harvested with a cell scraper. Cell suspensions were placed on ice for 20 min and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined by the Bradford method (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) using BSA as standard. Protein samples (20 µg/lane) were separated on 4–12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad). The membrane was blocked with 2% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. After washing with PBST, the membrane was incubated with rabbit anti-COX-2 antibody (1:1,000, Cell signaling) at an appropriate dilution with gentle agitation overnight at 4 °C, followed by addition of HRP-coupled secondary antibody (1:2,000, Millipore). The membrane was developed using an enhanced chemiluminescent solution (ECL) (Millipore, MA, USA) and the signals were recorded using the G: BOX Chemi XX9 gel imaging system (Syngene, Cambridge, UK).

Cytokine assays

RAW264.7 cells were plated in a 24-well plate at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL for 24 h. The cells were pretreated with E-CM and then stimulated with allergen Per a 2 as above (without incubation for 24 h). The culture supernatants were collected and stored at −20 °C until they were used for the cytokine assay. The levels of cytokines IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-3, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-9, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, IL-17 A, Eotaxin, G-CSF, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, KC, MCP-1, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, RANTES, and TNF-α were evaluated using a Bio-Plex mouse 23-plex panel (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., USA). Protein concentrations were determined based on a standard curve described by the manufacturer-provided protocol.

Constitutions analysis of Cordyceps militaris extracts with HPLC

The amounts of constituents in W-CM and E-CM were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to methods described elsewhere with some modifications [24, 30]. All standards were purchased from Sigma Chemical Corporation (St. Louis, MO, USA). Briefly, a reverse phase column (ODS-Hypersil 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) equipped with a UV detector set at 254 nm and a mobile phase consisting of 0.02 M KH2PO4 and methanol (85:15, v/v) were used to determine the amounts of cordycepin, adenosine, adenine, uridine, and uracil. A Phenomenex kinetex Luna column equipped with a refractive index (RI) detector and a mobile phase consisting of 90% acetonitrile solution were used for measuring D-mannitol. Moreover, an SCpak ODS-P column equipped with a UV detector set at 282 nm, and a mobile phase consisting of methanol (100%) were used for the detection of ergosterol. The HPLC profile was detected and the amounts of cordycepin, adenosine, ergosterol, adenine, uridine, uracil, and mannitol in both extracts of W-CM and E-CM were calculated from the standard graph of each compound.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS software version 22 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) with appropriate methods. P-values less than 0.05 were considered to be significant.

Results

Cytotoxicity of extracts from Cordyceps militaris on HaCaT and RAW264.7 cells

Cell viabilities were tested with WST-8 assay to determine the toxicity of E-CM and W-CM in HaCaT (Fig. 1A) and RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 1B), respectively. W-CM and E-CM showed 90.88% and 87.5% cell viability at the highest dose of 1000 µg/mL in HaCaT, respectively. We found that W-CM had no effect on cell cytotoxicity from both cell lines. However, there was little cytotoxic effect of E-CM at concentrations from 31.25 to 1000 µg/mL in RAW264.7 cells.

Cytotoxicity of Cordyceps militaris by water (W-CM) or ethanol (E-CM) extraction in (A) HaCaT and (B) RAW264.7 cells. Cells were treated with serial dilutions of W-CM or E-CM for 24 h. The data were expressed as mean ± SD of triplicate experiments

Evaluation of anti-inflammatory effects of extracts from C. militaris at the transcriptional level in Per a 2-induced HaCaT cells

Previously, we found the major cockroach allergen Per a 2 could induce acute and chronic inflammation in the keratinocytes, which may contribute to the development of AD. Initially, we performed a dose-response evaluation of W-CM and E-CM on Per a 2-stimulated HaCaT cells. As shown in Fig. 2, the mRNA levels of IL-8, GM-CSF, and CCL-20 were significantly reduced in a dose-dependent manner after pre-treatment of each extract for 1 h, followed by stimulation with Per a 2 for an additional 3 h. Dexamethasone is a synthetic glucocorticoid with anti-inflammatory properties. However, the active control treatment of dexamethasone did not affect CCL-20. Taken together, the data revealed that the anti-inflammatory activities of E-CM are better than W-CM, and the optimal doses were shown to be 20 and 125 µg/mL, respectively. Therefore, we performed further experiments with E-CM to investigate its anti-allergic effects in RAW264.7 macrophages stimulated by cockroach allergen.

Inhibitory effects of E-CM on mRNA expression of iNOS and NO production in Per a 2-induced RAW264.7 cells

Free radical NO plays an important role in inflammatory regulation and its production depends on enzyme activity of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). First, we examined whether E-CM could modulate inflammatory mediator NO synthesis in Per a 2-stimulated RAW264.7 cells by measuring iNOS mRNA expression and NO releasing level. When the RAW264.7 cells were treated with Per a 2, the expression of iNOS mRNA was significantly increased (Fig. 3A). The treatment of E-CM at a concentration of more than 10 µg/mL significantly inhibited both iNOS transcription and subsequent NO production (Fig. 3B). Indeed, E-CM strongly inhibited iNOS and NO production to a greater extent than positive control of 1 µM dexamethasone.

Inhibitory effects of E-CM on mRNA expression and protein level of COX-2 in Per a 2-induced RAW264.7 cells

COX-2 is the other important inflammation-associated enzyme with prostaglandin production. Next, the inhibitory effects of E-CM on COX-2 expression in mRNA and protein were examined in RAW264.7 cells stimulated by Per a 2. The treatment of RAW264.7 with E-CM at concentrations of 10 and 20 µg/mL achieved a significant decrease in COX-2 mRNA (Fig. 4A) and protein (Fig. 4B) expression in a dose-dependent manner. The data indicated that 20 µg/mL of E-CM could down-regulate the COX-2 expressions in allergen-activated murine macrophages, achieving the same effect as the dexamethasone control.

Downregulation of IL-13 and pro-inflammatory cytokines by E-CM treatment in Per a 2-induced RAW264.7 cells

RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with various doses of E-CM for 1 h and then incubated with Per a 2 for an additional 24 h to examine the inhibitory activity in terms of the production of IL-13 and pro-inflammatory cytokines. The levels of IL-13, IL-2, TNF-α, IL-6, G-CSF, and GM-CSF in the cell-free culture supernatants were significantly increased in RAW264.7 cells in response to Per a 2 stimulation alone (Fig. 5, red star labeled). E-CM treatment of Per a 2-induced RAW264.7 cells markedly suppressed these six cytokines secretions in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5). We also found the treatment with 1 µM dexamethasone only inhibited IL-2, TNF-α, and GM-CSF, but did not have an effect on IL-13, IL-6, or G-CSF production in Per a 2-stimulated RAW264.7 cells.

The levels of other cytokines, including IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-3, IL-4, IL-5, IL-9, IL-10, IL-12, IL-17 A, Eotaxin, IFN-γ, KC, MCP-1, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, and RANTES, were not significantly elevated in RAW264.7 cells following stimulation with Per a 2 alone. Moreover, treatment with E-CM did not result in a significant suppression of the secretion of these cytokines in Per a 2-induced RAW264.7 cells (Supplementary Figure S2A and S2B). Although MIP-1α and MIP-1β are known to be involved in pro- and anti-inflammatory responses, respectively, the protein levels in our study exceeded the assay’s detection range. As a result, we cannot draw definitive conclusions about their roles.

Chemical constituents of W-CM and E-CM

C. militaris has several biologically important components including cordycepin, ergosterol, mannitol, proteins, essential amino acids, volatile oils, nucleosides, polysaccharides, and sterols [18, 22]. The amounts of 7 major chemical constituents in W-CM and E-CM were determined by HPLC, as summarized in Fig. 6. The amounts of cordycepin, adenosine, ergosterol, adenine, uridine, and uracil in ethanolic extracts of C. militaris were 3.87, 61.24, 44.76, 39.40, 26.68, and 4.66 mg/mL, respectively. These compounds had higher content in the ethanolic extracts of C. militaris than in water extracts, which explained the better therapeutic effects of E-CM on anti-inflammation.

Discussion

One hallmark of AD is an accumulation of inflammatory cells, including different T cell subsets, macrophages, eosinophils, and mast cells in skin lesions [12, 31, 32]. Epidermal keratinocytes are the prominent cell type in the skin, which initiate cross-talk between innate and adaptive immune responses in AD [12, 32]. Our findings indicate that E-CM at a concentration of 20 µg/mL exhibited better anti-inflammatory activities compared to W-CM at 125 µg/mL in keratinocytes. Jo et al. previously documented that the highest concentration (1,250 µg/mL) of W-CM significantly attenuated lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced production of NO, TNF-α, and IL-6 secretion in murine macrophages [29]. However, our results demonstrated that a lower dose of E-CM (20 µg/mL) was more effective than those reported in previous studies at inhibiting Per a 2-induced NO, TNF-α, IL-6, COX-2, IL-13, IL-2, G-CSF, and GM-CSF in RAW264.7 cells. These data suggest that E-CM may exert its anti-inflammatory effects through the downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

IgE sensitization to aeroallergens, food-derived allergens, microbial allergens, and aeroallergens is associated with atopic dermatitis [13, 31]. Epithelial barrier dysfunction may facilitate the epicutaneous penetration of allergens, leading to the development of epicutaneous sensitization for AD [31, 32]. We found that Per a 2 protein exists abundantly in roach feces and in high concentrations in the environment [1]. Previous studies have demonstrated that skin stimulation with Per a 2 triggers Th2-driven inflammation associated with atopic dermatitis, while Per a 2 has also been associated with elevated serum levels of IL-8, MCP-1, GM-CSF, and CCL-20 in patients with asthma [6–8]. In the present study, we demonstrated that Per a 2 upregulates the mRNA expression of IL-8, CCL20, and GM-CSF in HaCaT cells, further supporting its involvement in allergic inflammatory responses. IL-8 serves as a key marker of inflammation severity, while increased levels of CCL20 and GM-CSF are also characteristic features of atopic dermatitis [9, 17, 33, 34]. Notably, E-CM significantly suppressed Per a 2-induced IL-8, CCL20, and GM-CSF in the keratinocytes and might be a candidate to treat allergic skin diseases or atopic dermatitis.

Cyclooxygenase (COX)−2 converts arachidonic acid to produce prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), another inflammatory mediator associated with allergic response [16]. PGE2 facilitates adaptive IL-22 production from T cells and involves the development of atopic dermatitis and allergic contact dermatitis [35]. Moreover, PGE2 and IL-22 related genes were both upregulated in concert in the lesional skin of patients with AD [35]. Many anti-inflammatory drugs act by inhibiting PG synthesis through the suppression of COX-2 production or activity [36, 37]. We found that E-CM significantly inhibited the expression of iNOS and COX-2 in Per a 2-induced RAW264.7 cells, and might be involved in the mechanism underlying the anti-inflammatory response. AD is considered a type 2 immune response and IL-13 has emerged as the primary cytokine involved in AD inflammation [31–33, 38]. We also found that E-CM significantly inhibited the expression of IL-2, IL-6, IL-13, TNF-α, G-CSF, and GM-CSF in Per a 2-induced RAW264.7 cells, and these cytokines are known to be involved in the pathogenesis of atopic dermatitis [12, 13, 39]. Notably, the anti-inflammatory capacity of E-CM on the expression of IL-6, iNOS, and COX-2 was as effective as dexamethasone.

Eiamthaworn et al. determined that the crude ethanolic extract of the fruiting body of C. militaris contained the highest contents of cordycepin and adenosine, and these findings were compatible with our results [24]. Ethanol extract contained more cordycepin to stimulate type-2 immune effects, whereas water extract of C. militaris contained more polysaccharides to stimulate type-1 immune effects [19, 25]. Cordycepin (3’-deoxyadenosine) is an adenosine analog. The mechanism of cordycepin was reported to involve suppression of the production of NO and proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in LPS-stimulated macrophages [20, 40, 41]. We also found that the ethanolic extract of C. militaris had higher amounts of ergosterol. Ergosterol from C. militaris could inhibit the release of inflammatory mediators NO, TNF-α, and IL-12, and was found to be correlated with antiproliferative activity in colon tumor cells [25, 42]. This evidence is consistent with our findings on the anti-allergic and anti-inflammatory effects of the ethanolic extract of C. militaris.

Cordyceps militaris and its bioactive constituents might modulate key molecular signaling pathways, including Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT), and phosphatidylinositol 3‑kinase (PI3K), and protein kinase C (PKC) [19, 20, 41]. These pathways play a crucial role in regulating inflammatory mediators and are implicated in the inflammatory disorders. Previous studies indicated that C. militaris extracts could inhibit LPS-induced expression of iNOS and reduce proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 [20, 21]. Additionally, C. militaris extracts might ameliorate the LPS-induced phosphorylation levels of ERK1/2, JNK1/2, and p38 in the MAPK pathways [20]. Other studies demonstrated that C. militaris effectively suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines through inhibiting NF-κB, and downregulating the iNOS, COX-2, MAPKs and AKT signaling cascades to alleviate LPS induced inflammation in RAW264.7 macrophages [29]. However, the precise immune signaling pathways involved remain unclear. Further studies are needed to elucidate the causal association between the E-CM modulating pro-inflammatory cytokines and targeting signal pathways.

A study conducted in Thailand revealed that aqueous extracts of C. militaris showed a high content of total phenolics and flavonoids relating to higher radical scavenging activity [24]. C. militaris also contains nucleosides, such as adenosine, guanosine, cytidine, uridine, adenine, and uracil, among which, adenosine could be used to treat chronic heart failure by significantly suppressing the release of neurotransmitters in the central nervous system [20, 25]. Our HPLC data also revealed that the ethanolic extract of C. militaris had higher amounts of adenine, uridine, and uracil. More data are needed to gain a better understanding of its biological functions.

When an individual with allergies encounters a typically harmless substance such as dust mites, cockroaches, mold, or pollen, the immune system may overreact by producing IgE antibodies [32]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that skin exposure to indoor aeroallergens, including extract of cockroaches, recombinant Per a 2 and Der p 2, can trigger systemic allergic inflammation [3, 8, 9]. This is a generally observed phenomenon in allergic diseases. Furthermore, we have also proved that epicutaneous sensitization to aeroallergens could lead to nasal and airway symptoms comparable to those of atopic march [8, 9]. In this study, we used allergens rather than LPS to induce AD-like symptoms in order to create conditions that were closer to real-world environmental exposure.

It is important to acknowledge that this study has some limitations. First, AD is a multifactorial inflammatory skin disease, and there remains a considerable translational gap between cell models and human AD [39]. Various in-vitro models have been developed to mimic key features of allergic skin diseases or atopic dermatitis; however, selecting the most appropriate experimental system remains challenging, as it requires careful consideration of the respective advantages and limitations of each model [39, 43]. However, we use dual cell models to screen new compounds for preventive or therapeutic strategies of skin diseases and understand the underlying mechanism. Second, our results showed E-CM in dual cell models was effective; however, more in-vivo data on dosage, systemic absorption, and immune system interactions are needed to evaluate and provide evidence supporting the anti-inflammatory effects on AD. Third, cordycepin possesses a wide range of bioactive properties; however, its clinical application is constrained by cytotoxicity, limited bioavailability, off-target effects, and a lack of sufficient human studies. This study confirms that E-CM contains higher levels of cordycepin compared to W-CM, highlighting potential concerns regarding its associated side effects. Therefore, further safety evaluations are warranted in future research.

Future research may focus on assessing the anti-allergic and anti-inflammatory effects of E-CM using previously established atopic dermatitis (AD)-like mouse model induced by the cockroach allergen Per a 2. Additionally, preclinical studies are essential to evaluate the dose-response relationship, efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and toxicity of E-CM in Per a 2-stimulated cells and mouse models. Looking forward, E-CM holds potential for development as a herbal remedy for the treatment or prevention of allergen-induced skin inflammation. Prospective multi-center clinical trials are also necessary to evaluate the efficacy and safety of E-CM in the treatment of atopic dermatitis and other allergic diseases.

Conclusion

In the present study, we explored the effects of ethanolic (E-CM) and water (W-CM) extracts from Cordyceps militaris and found that E-CM exhibited significant anti-inflammatory effects on Per a 2-induced keratinocytes and macrophages. We provide evidence that these compounds were effective in reducing cockroach allergen-induced skin inflammation. E-CM may be considered as a potential therapeutic herbal remedy, functional food or dietary supplement for the amelioration of allergies. Furthermore, future research will assess the anti-inflammatory effects and safety profile of Cordyceps militaris using the cockroach allergen-induced atopic dermatitis-like murine model that we have developed.
