Objective dyspnea measurement in real time using a forehead wearable and artificial intelligence
Authors: Sabrina Meng, Alexandru Bogdan, Alireza Akhbardeh, Hersh Sagreiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dyspnea is a complex symptom measured using subjective patient-reported ratings. Continuous, automated dyspnea measurements are needed, especially in critical care and trauma settings with impaired patient communication. We prospectively enrolled 54 pulmonary rehabilitation subjects. Participants completed two treadmill walking trials, during which dyspnea measurements were collected at one-minute intervals automatically using physiologic sensors and patient-reported ratings of perceived breathlessness and exertion (RPB and RPE). The sensor data were used to train machine learning models using either 19 or 7 features to generate an objective dyspnea score (ODS). Classification performance was assessed on a held-out test set, compared against patient-reported RPB and RPE. The model trained on 7 features performed best, resulting in a correlation between predicted and actual scores (percent accuracy) of 0.84 (78.7%) for RPE and 0.86 (83.6%) for RPB. Our system incorporating ODS accurately predicts patients’ subjective dyspnea scores and is promising for automated, real-time dyspnea measurement.

Introduction

Dyspnea is a complex symptom that can manifest in variety of diseases, including cardiopulmonary disorders, cancers, and metabolic imbalances1–5. Chronic and acute dyspnea may represent harbingers of a more serious illness and must be treated quickly to avoid the possibility of respiratory failure and/or patient death6. Factors associated with increased dyspnea include female sex, old age, low socioeconomic status, and unemployment7. Dyspnea is highly prevalent, and 8.9% of the population is affected by exertional dyspnea that chronically limits daily activity and reduces work productivity7,8. Nearly half (49%) of the general cancer patient population experiences breathlessness, and lung cancer patients report even higher rates of dyspnea, with up to 84% of patients experiencing shortness of breath3,9. Thus, the development of automated methods capable of providing continuous physiologic monitoring related to dyspnea may complement existing assessment tools and support clinical monitoring, particularly in settings where patient reporting is intermittent or not possible.

Moreover, dyspnea is a primary symptom of both chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and congestive heart failure10,11. Approximately 6% of people over 18 suffer from COPD in the United States, with a stable prevalence in the period between 2011–202112. COPD was the 6th leading cause of death in the United States in 2021, and up to 82% of COPD patients reported experiencing dyspnea12,13. The burden of COPD is high. Approximately 3% of all hospitalizations are related to COPD exacerbations, and readmission rates are frequent, ranging from 2.6–82.2% at 30 days and 17.9–63.0% at 6 months14,15. In addition, over 6.5 million people in the U.S. have heart failure, and nearly one million (960,000) new cases of heart failure are diagnosed yearly16,17. Reports of dyspnea in heart failure patients are known to be as high as 90%18. Similar to COPD, the burden of heart failure-associated hospitalizations is high, with hospitalization rates reaching 4.9 per 1000 US adults in 2017 and 30-day and 90-day readmission rates of 21–22% and 40%, respectively19,20.

Dyspnea is also commonly observed in chest trauma and is a major predictor of mortality in patients suffering from traumatic injury or respiratory illness21,22. Battlefield chest trauma is a substantial contributor to morbidity and mortality in combat casualties, occurring in approximately 15% of war injuries23. Of note, 25% of trauma-related deaths are attributed to chest trauma alone24,25. Additionally, other battlefield exposures, such as chemical warfare agents, may lead to respiratory symptoms including dyspnea26.

Dyspnea is recognized as a subjective, multidimensional experience that includes sensory, emotional, and functional components. Current clinical assessments often rely on patient-reported measures of perceived breathing effort, such as the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) and the Modified Borg Rating of Perceived Breathlessness (RPB)27–31. Additional validated instruments include the Dyspnea-12 (D-12) questionnaire and the Multidimensional Dyspnea Profile (MDP), which capture both sensory and emotional aspects of dyspnea, as well as the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale and the London Chest Activity of Daily Living Scale (LCADL), which assess the impact of dyspnea on daily activities32–35. However, these subjective measures of exertion can be difficult to interpret and apply, especially since they are not measured continuously. While dyspnea is defined as a subjective experience that can only be directly assessed by the individual experiencing it, in clinical practice, providers frequently rely on observational assessments to estimate respiratory distress. Prior studies have shown that agreement between patient-reported dyspnea and provider assessments can be limited, with nurses and physicians underestimating patient dyspnea in 43.5 and 37.9% of cases, respectively36. These findings demonstrate potential discrepancies between subjective patient experience and clinical observation. This highlights the importance of continuous dyspnea monitoring to prevent patient-provider misunderstanding.

Dyspnea assessment may be especially difficult in non-communicative patients who cannot report their experiences; for this reason, tools such as the respiratory distress observation scale (RDOS) have been developed to allow observers to rate dyspnea in non-communicative patients37,38. However, RDOS is limited by discrete, non-continuous monitoring and the need for provider observation, which may not always be time efficient or feasible. Thus, there has been substantial work in developing sensors for automated respiratory monitoring, including devices such as chest belts and patches, clothing sensors, mattresses, radars, and camera systems; one such system has attempted to correlate measurements with self-reported dyspnea scores39,40. However, these devices present significant limitations in dyspnea quantification for multiple reasons: 1) focus on collecting physiologic data (e.g., respiratory rate, heart rate) without interpretation of the measurements; 2) complicated equipment and/or positioning of sensors on the body; or 3) lack of applicability to the symptom of dyspnea itself, which remains a largely subjective measurement.

Given that dyspnea is a subjective experience, patient self-report remains the primary method of assessment. However, identifying physiologic signals that correlate with patient-reported dyspnea may provide complementary information about respiratory distress, particularly in situations where communication is limited. In this study, we therefore explore whether cerebral optical spectrometry signals obtained from a wearable device correlate with patient-reported dyspnea scores. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has identified the prefrontal cortex as a key brain region involved in processing dyspnea41,42. In particular, prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex activation is correlated with patient-reported dyspnea via the visual analog and Borg scales41,43. We hypothesized that we could leverage the relationship between prefrontal cortex activity and individuals’ experience of dyspnea, which is also positively correlated with increased work of breathing44,45. This correlation with dyspnea was most notable for total work of breathing at peak exercise and expiratory work of breathing at rest in COPD patients, as well as total work of breathing at peak exercise in ILD patients45. Thus, we developed a system that utilizes cerebral optical spectrometry data and predictive machine learning algorithms to generate an objective dyspnea score (ODS), which continuously assesses the patient’s work of breathing regardless of their clinical condition or ability to communicate effectively. The objective of this study was to evaluate whether physiologic signals obtained using cerebral optical spectrometry correlate with patient-reported dyspnea scores measured using the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) and Modified Borg Rating of Perceived Breathlessness (RPB) scales.

Methods

We developed a non-invasive method to capture real-time objective dyspnea scores (ODS) utilizing cerebral hemodynamic changes measured by optical spectrometry. ODS measurements are computed using specialized cerebral sensors, with signal processing and predictive machine learning (ML) algorithms yielding an ODS output from 0–10 (Supplementary Fig. 1). The ODS system uses near-infrared and red spectroscopy to sample a cross-section of microvasculature in the brain tissue. Next, the spectroscopic analysis determines changes in oxygenation. Finally, the display software calculates an ODS based on changes in regional hemoglobin oxygenation and hemodynamic response in the prefrontal cortex. The display software is portable and can be used via tablet or laptop, and it connects to the sensor using either USB or Bluetooth.

To validate the ODS system, we collected clinical data via a prospective crossover cohort study at a single center, the John Muir Hospital Pulmonary Rehabilitation Center in Pleasant Hill, CA. Participants completed two treadmill walking tests: one under normal breathing conditions and one using a resistance breathing apparatus designed to increase inspiratory effort. The purpose of including the resistance breathing condition was to expand the range of dyspnea levels experienced during testing and thereby capture a broader range of patient-reported Borg scores. The first trial with normal breathing consisted of walking for 10 minutes on a treadmill at a speed acceptable to the participant. After this trial, participants were allowed a rest period of at least 30 minutes. Then, the second trial with a high-resistance facemask (equivalent to an N95 mask, 10 – 15 cmH₂O/L/sec) was conducted at the same speed. Data collection during the treadmill walking test included continuous physiologic monitoring (oxygen saturation [SpO₂], heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature) via forehead and wrist sensors and perceptual assessments using the Modified Borg Rating of Perceived Breathlessness (RPB) and the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) at one-minute intervals. Observers were blinded to the system’s dyspnea predictions (ODS). Finally, following the completion of the walking trial, participants were allowed to rest for an additional 5 minutes, and the final (recovery) dyspnea scores were recorded. Clinical pulmonary function metrics such as FEV1, 6-minute walk test distance, and quality-of-life measures were not collected in this exploratory protocol, as the study focused specifically on the relationship between spectrometry signals and patient-reported dyspnea during controlled treadmill testing.

From February 2022 to December 2023, 54 pulmonary rehabilitation subjects in maintenance exercise classes at John Muir Health were enrolled. Subjects were selected using convenience sampling. Participants were at least 18 years old, recreationally active (exercising ≤3 times per week), and able to walk on a treadmill for multiple sessions. Exclusion criteria included inability to ambulate independently and any acute medical instability. The cohort had a mean (standard deviation) age of 74.12 (9.28) years with a representative distribution of race and ethnicity. Cohort characteristics are summarized in Table 1. During the treadmill walking test, dyspnea measurements were collected at one-minute intervals both (i) automatically with the ODS system and (ii) using patient-reported Borg values. Cerebral hemodynamic changes and vital signs collected during treadmill walking tests served as our ODS measurements. Data were collected using three specialized cerebral sensors with red and infrared light illumination. Both raw sensor data and derived features were used for prediction, with derived features including metrics such as heart rate and respiratory rate, and vitality estimates such as SpO2. Additionally, we asked subjects to rate their level of dyspnea and breathing effort using the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE; values: 6–20) and the Modified Borg Rating of Perceived Breathlessness (RPB; values: 0–10). The Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale categories were based on the original description of the Borg scale46. The Modified Borg Rating of Perceived Breathlessness (RPB) scale uses a 0–10 numeric scale with verbal descriptors corresponding to increasing levels of breathlessness47. While individual numerical descriptors exist for specific values, there is no universally accepted grouping of categories. For the purposes of the analysis in this study, scores were grouped into broader categories to facilitate statistical comparison across dyspnea levels (Supplementary Tables 1–2). The cutoffs for the RPE score categories were rest (6–7), light (8–11), hard (12–16), and very hard (17–20). The cutoffs for the RPB score categories were light (0–2), moderate (2–4), severe (5–7), and very severe (8–10).

19 features served as inputs to the ML prediction engine, including SpO₂, respiratory rate, heart rate, timing between heartbeats, derived heart rate variability metrics, optical ratios from three infrared/red channels, and the ROPA index (a quantification of physiologic stress using cerebral hemodynamic responses)48. We also performed an analysis using a second prediction engine trained with only 7 vitality features, which include SpO₂, respiratory rate, heart rate, ROPA index, and optical ratios from three infrared/red channels48. The prediction targets were the maximum RPB and RPE dyspnea scores given by the patient within each one-minute interval of the treadmill walking test. A data filtering step was applied to each observation before considering it for use in prediction. Each observation was checked for missing values, data range, and abnormal variations. After filtering, the data consisted of 1717 observations from 54 subjects.

Our ML prediction engines were created using automated machine learning (AutoML) with the Tree-based Pipeline Optimization Tool (TPOT), an open-source Python development tool49. The TPOT library is based on the scikit-learn package, which uses Genetic Programming (GP) to efficiently discover the best model pipeline for a given dataset. Data collected from the sensor was first processed to extract quantitative features, which were then subjected to standard preprocessing transformations (e.g., scaling, dimensionality reduction, and feature selection) prior to input into the machine-learning models. The training pipeline was created with both a pre-processing stage and a machine-learning prediction stage using 5-fold cross-validation. For each pipeline, we use a train/test split ratio of 0.75/0.25. 1287 observations were used for training and 430 observations for hold-out testing in an independent validation set. Data was split via subject-level partitioning such that the subset of patients represented in the training and test sets were distinct, preventing data leakage. In addition, during 5-fold cross validation on the development set, the dataset was also partitioned at the participant level. After training, the accuracy and correlation coefficient of the models were calculated on the test set to evaluate the ability of the ODS to predict and identify clinically meaningful ranges for RPB and RPE. The model was assessed on (i) dyspnea classification into the four classes defined for RPB and RPE and (ii) regression to produce predicted numerical RPB and RPE scores. Several ML prediction and data pre-processing algorithms were considered for this task, summarized in Supplementary Table 3, with the final model chosen automatically by TPOT to optimize performance.

This study was approved by the John Muir Health Institutional Review Committee (IRC) (Approval Number: IRC 22-08-01).

Results

In classification tests on the hold-out test set using the model trained on 19 features, the Gradient Boosting Classifier predicted the RPE with 71.6% accuracy, while the Random Forest Classifier predicted the RPB with 80.0% accuracy (Table 2). Overall, cerebral optical spectrometry signals demonstrated measurable correlations with patient-reported dyspnea scores obtained using the Borg RPE and Modified Borg RPB scales during treadmill exercise testing. These findings support the hypothesis that physiologic signals derived from cerebral spectrometry may reflect changes in perceived respiratory effort.

Discussion

Dyspnea is a common symptom of systemic disease and trauma that is an important prognostic indicator and that may lead to respiratory failure and death if not treated promptly. However, the current standards for assessing dyspnea—the RPB and RPE—are subjective measurements based on patient reporting and do not necessarily correlate well with providers’ understanding of dyspnea severity. Thus, we developed a lightweight, easy-to-use, and non-invasive method to capture real-time ODS by measuring cerebral hemodynamic changes using optical spectrometry. The generated ODS is an indicator of dyspnea, which provides clinicians with additional information needed to diagnose and manage critical care patients in a variety of settings, including acute care settings and on the battlefield. Notably, there is a significant need to quickly assess injuries on the battlefield and during evacuation transport, for which our ODS system is well-equipped.

This study demonstrates that the ODS system can predict and distinguish between clinically relevant categories of dyspnea with high accuracy. For regression tasks, the ODS system achieved peak performance using a Random Forest Regressor trained on 7 features, yielding correlations of 0.84 and 0.86 when predicting the RPE and RPB, respectively. This demonstrates a strong positive association between the predicted ODS and patient-reported dyspnea scores. For classification tasks, the ODS system achieves a peak performance of 78.7% accuracy using a Random Forest Classifier to classify the RPE and an 83.6% accuracy using an Extra Trees Classifier to classify the RPB—all trained on 7 features. Additionally, ROC analysis using the OvR strategy for RPE classification using 7 features yielded AUC scores of at least 0.90 for all classes, while the same analysis for RPB classification yielded AUC scores of at least 0.92 for all classes. These results further demonstrate concordance between categories of dyspnea severity predicted via the ODS system as compared to patients’ self-reported dyspnea severity. Our findings also show that different classifiers are optimized for prediction tasks depending on the type of dyspnea scale assessed (i.e., Random Forest Classifier for RPE and Extra Trees Classifier for RPB). Interestingly, the performance of the ML prediction engine was improved when trained on 7 features compared to 19 features for both classification and regression tasks, which we hypothesize may be due to feature redundancy resulting in overfitting and poor generalizability50. Overall, the ODS system trained using 7 features achieves excellent performance in predicting and classifying RPE and RPB scores, and including more training features does not necessarily lead to an increase in model performance, a trend that has also been observed in other clinical prediction models51.

Currently, several sensors for measuring respiration and detecting respiratory pathologies have been developed. One category of sensors entails abdominal movement-based respiratory monitoring, including chest belts, chest patches, body attachments, clothing sensors, mattresses, radars, and camera systems39,52–57. Respiratory data can also be collected using ECG-derived data39,58,59. However, these sensors are mainly focused on collecting objective respiratory measurements, such as respiratory rate, heart rate, breathing patterns, and related data. Some of these sensors are additionally limited due to complicated equipment and/or positioning of sensors on the chest, which may be especially difficult in settings of thoracic trauma. Another category of respiratory sensors utilizes acoustic data and can perform sound and signal analytics on the measurements to monitor respiratory symptoms like cough, wheezing, snoring, etc. in addition to basic respiratory data39,60,61. Finally, sensors (commonly via a face mask or transcutaneous device) exist for measuring the composition of exhaled and blood gases, such as ammonia and oxygen saturation, to assess respiratory status39,62–64. Although these sensors provide insight into an individual’s breathing patterns and metabolic state—even predicting respiratory pathologies—they are still limited in their ability to relay information about an individual’s perception of respiratory distress.

Thus, our ODS system presents a novel method of assessing dyspnea and respiratory distress that presents several advantages over existing technologies. To our knowledge, our system is the first to objectively quantify subjective perceptions of dyspnea in real-time using a cerebral optical spectrometry system. When comparing ODS-generated to patient-reported RPE and RPB scores, the current standard for dyspnea assessment, our ODS system delivered concordant results with high accuracy for both regression and classification tasks. Additionally, traditional dyspnea assessment tools such as Borg scales and other questionnaires provide intermittent, patient-reported measurements of perceived respiratory effort. In contrast, the cerebral optical spectrometry approach explored in this study provides physiologic signals that can be collected continuously through a wearable device. Such signals may provide complementary information about respiratory distress and could be particularly useful in clinical settings where continuous monitoring or limited patient communication (e.g., cognitive impairment, sleep, or sedation) makes repeated questionnaire-based assessment difficult65. In the future, the ODS system could be adapted to monitor cerebral perfusion during other cardiopulmonary events, such as traumatic brain injury, cardiac surgery, and thoracic surgery66–68.

Study limitations include a sample size of 54 subjects from a single medical center. The possibility of differential performance of the optical spectroscopy sensor in patients with different skin tones and other demographic factors was not assessed. Moreover, the symptom of dyspnea is complex and may involve additional domains beyond physiologic measurements, including the sensory-perceptual experience, affective distress, and symptom impact or burden, which was not directly assessed in our study31. Strengths of our study include rigorous methods of data collection in a standardized environment (i.e., treadmill test), as well as validation of a high-performing ODS system that has opportunities for implementation in respiratory care and management. Moreover, while we collected data from 54 subjects, we gathered many data points from each patient, resulting in a large dataset of 1717 observations mapping ODS measurements to reported RPE and RPB scores.

Future studies may consider evaluating the performance of the ODS system in predicting dyspnea in people at rest and for hospitalized/critically ill patients, as well as training the ODS system to distinguish between differing origins of dyspnea (e.g., pain, anxiety, and fear). Studies investigating how sensory experience, affective distress, and symptom burden intersect with physiologic spectrometry measurements are needed; one way of accomplishing this goal is by building predictive models for other methods of dyspnea assessment such as D-12, MDP, mMRC, and LCADL, which capture various other dimensions of dyspnea. Additional studies examining the effect of therapeutic intervention on scores reported by the ODS system (i.e., whether changes in ODS outputs match the changes in dyspnea ratings provided by patients) may also be of interest. Further research should be done to validate the ODS system in a larger population and to evaluate the performance of the system in patients with different demographic backgrounds. Finally, exploration of additional ODS system applications beyond dyspnea monitoring, such as detecting other cardiopulmonary pathologies or monitoring perfusion during high-risk surgical procedures, is also encouraged.

In conclusion, dyspnea is commonly observed in traumatic chest injuries and systemic disease, serving as an important prognostic indicator. However, there is currently no continuous, automated measure of dyspnea, which is instead quantified based on subjective reports. In this study, we validate a system that utilizes optical spectrometry of the cerebral vasculature and machine learning models to generate real-time objective dyspnea scores. Given the ODS system’s high accuracy on both dyspnea score regression and classification tasks, our ODS system is a promising technology for continuous, real-time dyspnea measurement that can be used in battlefield injuries, acute trauma, and other patient care settings.
