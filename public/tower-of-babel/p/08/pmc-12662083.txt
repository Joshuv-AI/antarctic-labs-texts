Adherence to oral anticancer medications in metastatic versus nonmetastatic breast cancer patients: Insights from a low-middle-income country
Authors: Abdel-Hameed IM Ebid, Rana Elshewy, Mahmoud I Mostafa, Reem Eid, Emad Shash, Mohamed A Mobarez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal treatment outcomes in breast cancer rely heavily on patient adherence to oral anticancer medications (OAMs).AimThis study aimed to assess adherence to OAMs and identify factors associated with nonadherence in metastatic and nonmetastatic breast cancer patients.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study at the National Cancer Institute, Cairo University, 500 breast cancer patients were categorised into metastatic (45.6 %) and nonmetastatic (54.4 %) groups. Adherence was measured using the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8) and the Medication Possession Ratio (MPR), with nonadherence defined as an MMAS-8 score < 8 or an MPR < 80 %. Data collection was augmented by patient interviews and examinations of outpatient pharmacy dispensing records.ResultsAdherence rates were 46.4 % as per MMAS-8 and 71 % according to MPR. Specifically, in the nonmetastatic group, MMAS-8 revealed a 28.3 % adherence rate, MPR showed 75.4 % adherence rate. Metastatic patients displayed significantly higher adherence (MASS-8, 68 %) (p < 0.001). There was a notable inverse correlation between adherence and the severity of side effects like musculoskeletal pain, neurological symptoms, fatigue, and hot flushes (p < 0.001). Additionally, adherence varied with treatment duration, with shorter periods associated with better adherence.ConclusionThis study confirms that adherence to OAMs is significantly affected by both the metastatic status and the severity of side effects. This highlights the urgent need for enhanced educational and management strategies tailored to the specific challenges faced by breast cancer patients, especially those dealing with severe side effects. Ongoing research should focus on developing and testing targeted interventions to improve adherence in these patient groups.Keywords: Breast cancer, Medication possession ratio, Metastatic, Nonmetastatic, Oral anticancer medications, Self-reported adherence, Side effects

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) remains the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women worldwide and is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with over 2.3 million new cases annually.1,2 Oral anticancer medications (OAMs) are crucial in contemporary oncology,1,2 improving clinical outcomes and reducing hospitalisation rates while offering patients more control over their treatment1; however, they also pose considerable challenges, including side effect management, long durations, and adherence issues.3

Medication adherence is a fundamental prerequisite for treatment success but, it is also one of the most significant obstacles in real-world settings.4,5 Nonadherence is associated with negative health outcomes and increased healthcare expenditures.3,6,7 According to clinical trials, 72–94 % of women adhere to their treatment plan.8 Conversely, in standard clinical practice, this number can be as low as 40 %.9, 10, 11 Indeed, recent worldwide estimates imply that 30–60 % of BC patients are nonadherent.10 In Africa, recent evidence highlights nonadherence rates of 4.3–65.4 % for hormonal therapy, 80.9 % for cytotoxic medications, and 32.7 % for combination treatments.12 Moreover, a recent study in Egypt demonstrated that only 40.3 % of BC patients have high adherence to hormonal treatment.13

These discrepancies may be due to the diversity of the adherence definitions. Currently, a universally accepted definition remains challenging.14 Medication adherence is defined by three distinct yet interacting components: initiation, implementation, and discontinuation.15 Treatment persistence refers to the duration from initiation to discontinuation.16 Accordingly, this study focuses on the real-world initiation and implementation phases of adherence.

Adherence behaviours are shaped by a complex interaction of factors, including patient-related, condition-related, and treatment-related factors.17, 18, 19 Considering the seriousness and repercussions of nonadherence, an investigation of the contributing variables and underlying mechanisms of nonadherence is essential. Although several studies have investigated the prevalence of and factors associated with adherence to OAMs globally, data from Africa are scarce, with Egypt being no exception.

This study aimed to assess adherence to OAMs and identify potential risk factors associated with nonadherence in metastatic and nonmetastatic BC patients in clinical practice, thereby informing the development of targeted interventions to enhance adherence in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional survey, coupled with a retrospective study of patient medical and administrative claims data, was performed at the National Cancer Institute, Cairo University, Egypt, over a period of six months from November 2023 to April 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of the National Cancer Institute (Approval No. 2310–105-003 approved on 7/11/2023). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrolment in the study.

Study population

A total of 522 BC patients were enrolled in the study through conventional sampling. The participants were categorised into two groups based on their disease status: metastatic (45.6 %) and nonmetastatic (54.4 %). The inclusion criteria included BC patients aged ≥18 years on OAMs with ≥3 prescription refills following the date of diagnosis. All stages, from I to IV, were included, provided that BC was the primary tumour diagnosis.

The exclusion criteria included refusal to participate, early discontinuation of OAMs (< 3 prescription refills), cognitive impairments that could hinder participation, incomplete questionnaires with a significant number of missing responses (> 10 %), and a high amount of missing data in the medical records (>10 %).

Of the 522 individuals initially assessed for eligibility, 5 declined participation. 10 patients were excluded because of failure to complete 3 prescription refills, and 7 were excluded because of incomplete questionnaires or missing data.

Data collection

Data were collected through patient interviews and examinations of medical and pharmacy record, safeguarding patient confidentiality through a unique identity number in an Excel spreadsheet database. Under the direction of the principal investigator, trained clinical pharmacists utilised a structured questionnaire to gather demographic, clinical, and self-reported adherence data (appendix A). After obtaining participants' informed consent, the researcher conducted face–to–face interviews with each subject for approximately 20 min.

All medical records were retrospectively explored, and all information about diagnosis, disease status, prescription OAM regimens, and suspected side effects of OAMs was noted. Moreover, data from the hospital pharmacy regarding dispensing were reviewed to calculate the Medication Possession Ratio (MPR).

Adherence measurement

Given the advantages and disadvantages of each method, both self-reports and objective measures of adherence were utilised. Self-reports are among the most widely used and practical methods for assessing adherence.20 Self-reported data capture patient-reported behaviours and may reveal adherence-related challenges, such as forgetfulness or deliberate omission, by examining usage patterns.21 However, compared with objective measurements such as pharmacy claims data, it may be susceptible to recall bias.22 While prescription records may offer greater objectivity, they may mistakenly confuse medications dispensed with actual consumption. This discrepancy highlights the challenging nature of adherence and underscores the importance of combining techniques to gain comprehensive insight.23

Adherence to OAMs was assessed via two validated methods:

Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8): An eight-item questionnaire measures self-reported adherence, with scores ranging from 0 to 8. Patients scoring <8 points were classified as nonadherent (permission was obtained from the corresponding author via e-mail).24,25 We employed a validated Arabic version of the MMAS-8 through direct patient interviews as Arabic is Egypt's primary language.26, 27, 28 Furthermore, an additional open-ended question was included to probe reasons for nonadherence beyond forgetfulness (i.e., any other specific reasons the patient mentioned). We categorised non-adherence patterns as intentional or unintentional based on patient responses to the MMAS-8 items and the additional open-ended question. Intentional non-adherence was defined as a deliberate decision by the patient to deviate from the prescribed regime due to reasons such as perceived recovery, side effects, or beliefs about treatment necessity, while unintentional non-adherence referred to instances where doses were missed inadvertently due to forgetfulness, confusion about dosing schedules, or misunderstanding instructions.29

Medication Possession Ratio (MPR): One indirect objective method utilised for assessing adherence is the MPR, which is determined by the following formula:(total number of days supplied – supply of the last refill)/(last refill date - first refill date) (appendix B1–3).30 Most studies have defined adherence as a dichotomous variable, employing a ≥ 80 % MPR cut-off to categorise adherent individuals, as previous studies had shown a correlation between poor adherence (MPR < 80 %) and increased mortality risk.10 Therefore, the result of this calculation was expressed as a percentage, and patients who achieved a value of ≥80 % were classified as adherents.

Assessment of side effects

Side effects were assessed via patient-reported outcomes during the interviews. Rather than being given a predefined list, participants were invited to document any symptom they felt relevant, along with documented side effects in their medical records. Using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0,31 severity was classified as mild, moderate, or severe.

Statistical analysis

The data were analysed via SPSS Win (Statistical Package of Social Science) version 26. Numerical data were summarised as the means ± standard deviations (or medians and ranges) as appropriate, and categorical data are presented as frequencies (percentages). Given that the existing literature on medication adherence to OAMs has predominantly focused on early-stage BC patients, with limited data available specifically for metastatic BC populations,32 we conducted subgroup analyses by metastatic status to account for potential differences in adherence behaviour, clinical characteristics, and treatment regimens between metastatic and non-metastatic patients. Predictors were assessed separately within each group based on clinically meaningful distinctions in disease trajectory. This approach was taken to discern group-specific risk factors that might inform tailored adherence interventions. Group comparisons for categorical variables were performed via Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Adverse events and severity of side effects

Side effects were reported by 368 patients (73 %), with a substantially higher proportion of nonadherent patients (90 %, n = 322/358) than adherent patients (50 %, n = 71/142) (p < 0.001). The most common side effect reported was musculoskeletal pain (51.2 %) (Fig. 1). Additional side effects noted included vaginal dryness and discharge, weight gain, mood swings, and decreased libido, with the severity ranging between grade 0 (no events; 27.4 %), grade 1–2 (mild–moderate; 64 %), and grade 3–4 (severe; 8.6 %).

Adherence to OAMs

•Overall Adherence Rate

Overall Adherence Rate

The overall adherence varies depending on the assessment method and the definition of adherence. When using MPR alone, the adherence rate was 71 %, while MMAS-8 alone yielded a lower rate of 46.4 %. A stricter definition requiring patients to be adherent by both measures resulted in an adherence rate of only 38.2 %, highlighting the gap between prescription fulfilment and actual use.

•Adherence by Therapy Type

Adherence by Therapy Type

Adherence to OAMs varies by treatment type. For tamoxifen and AIs, MPR-based adherence was high (79.1 % and 77.3 %, respectively) but much lower according to MMAS-8 (27.1 % and 41.9 %). In contrast, CDK4/6 inhibitors showed higher adherence by MMAS-8 (66.3 %) than by MPR (40 %). Patients on capecitabine-based regimens demonstrated high and balanced adherence, as measured by both methods. (Fig. 2).

Adherence patterns to OAMs across different therapy types by MPR and MMAS-8.

Patterns and reasons for nonadherence

Nonadherence was further categorised into intentional and unintentional behaviours. 11.2 % of patients reported intentional nonadherence, 8.2 % reported unintentional nonadherence, and 80.6 % reported both, as shown in Table 2. Forgetfulness was the most cited reason (74.3 % of nonadherent patients). Other reasons (e.g., lack of knowledge and financial barriers) were reported by 54.9 % of the respondents. A total of 28.4 % reported that they stopped medication because they felt worse, and 18.7 % reported that they stopped because they felt better.

Disease status

Patients with more advanced disease demonstrated significantly higher adherence rates compared to those with early-stage disease. The overall MMAS-8-based adherence was 68 % in the metastatic group compared to 28.3 % in the non-metastatic group; MPR-based adherence was 71 % versus 75.4 %, respectively. A similar pattern was observed for adherence to endocrine therapy, as shown in Fig. 3.

Impact of disease status on adherence to endocrine treatment using the MPR and MMAS-8.

Adherence by therapy type

In the metastatic cohort, adherence rates were relatively high across all therapy types, although the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.708). While in the non-metastatic group, adherence was higher for patients receiving AIs compared to tamoxifen (32.4 % vs 21.9 %, p = 0.170).

Severity of side effects

Patients exhibiting greater severity of side effects demonstrated significantly reduced adherence. In the non-metastatic group, patients with mild or no side effects (grades 0–1) had significantly higher adherence (50 %) compared to those with more severe side effects (grades 2–3; 9 %) (p < 0.001). Comparable trends were noted in metastatic patients.

Treatment duration

Patients on therapy for ≤2 years showed higher adherence (50 %) compared to those treated for >2 years (41.5 %) based on MMAS-8, though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.067). No significant difference was observed in the metastatic group (p = 0.125).

Menopausal status

In the non-metastatic group, postmenopausal women had higher adherence (34.1 %) than premenopausal women (23.1 %) (p = 0.004). No such difference was observed in the metastatic group (p = 0.423).

Comorbidities

Patients with comorbidities demonstrated lower adherence compared to those without comorbidities, although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.138).

Discussion

BC patients receiving OAMs are frequently presumed to be adhering to their treatment satisfactorily, given the gravity of their disease, coupled with the simplicity of treatment.23 Nevertheless, our findings indicate that over half of our patients are nonadherent. The observed adherence rates align with prior findings reported by Ali et al., 2017, philipovskiy et al.33, 2020 and ElBaghdady et al., 2024, indicating adherence challenges among BC patients, especially in low-middle-income countries such as Egypt; nevertheless, these rates were inferior to those reported in countries such as the USA, Japan, and Brazil.34,35 The variances in adherence definitions, measurement tools, study populations, treatment types, and study designs could all contribute to disparities in adherence levels.36

This study assessed adherence via two validated tools, the MMAS-8 and MPR, with overall adherence rates of 46.4 % by MMAS-8 and 71 % by MPR. Our findings demonstrated a noticeable gap between patients' self-reported adherence and their MPR. This discrepancy suggests that while patients may be collecting their medications regularly, they may not be taking them consistently, possibly due to side effects or low perceptions of benefits.22,37 Interestingly, this trend was reversed in patients on CDK4/6 inhibitors. This could reflect administrative barriers, particularly as the patients were unaware of the renewal process, resulting in delayed refills. However, once patients obtained the medication, they reported high adherence. Overall, these findings highlight the complexity of adherence and emphasize the importance of integrating multiple tools to achieve a holistic understanding.22,37

This study evaluated adherence to OAMs, paying particular attention to differences between the non-metastatic and metastatic groups. While most BC adherence research focuses on early-stage disease and endocrine therapy, our findings provide a more nuanced understanding of how disease stage influences adherence behaviour, highlighting higher adherence in metastatic patients, which aligns with existing literature.35,38, 39, 40, 41 This group likely perceives their treatment as critical for survival, fostering a stronger adherence.42 Additionally, closer monitoring and monthly follow-up likely support adherence in our sample, as metastatic patients typically receive more frequent medical appointments.9,43,44 Furthermore, most metastatic patients are prescribed newer cytotoxic medications, such as palbociclib and capecitabine/lapatinib. According to a recent study, patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors exhibit higher adherence rates than early-stage BC patients due to their recognition of engaging in novel, high-cost treatments.42,45 Moreover, cytotoxic medications are typically administered for a shorter period than endocrine medications, thus enhancing adherence.12

In contrast, non-metastatic early BC patients may regard their therapy as less urgent, exhibiting a diminished perceived necessity for treatment owing to their interpreted favourable diagnosis.9,39 This finding was further confirmed in several systematic reviews showing that a shortage of knowledge about BC or endocrine treatment, alongside the perception of being at low risk for recurrence, was associated with nonadherence.2,9,39,46, 47, 48 According to recent research, women with less advanced illnesses are less likely to experience a “fear of recurrence”.40 This fear may drive adherence to OAMs in certain individuals, therefore partially explaining our observation of low adherence in patients with the least anticipated risk of disease progression.9,40,49 This finding presents an opportunity to target certain subgroups at risk of nonadherence, potentially leading to preventable recurrences.

The present study revealed inverse correlation between the severity of side effects and adherence, which was corroborated by several systematic reviews.12,22,50 This may result from the perceived benefits being overshadowed by the adverse effects of the treatment, as the severity of the side effects plays a critical role in the choice to continue therapy, with several patients reaching a point when they regard the perceived benefits of the drugs to be outweighed by the consequences of the side effects.9,51 Importantly, our findings indicate that this association holds considerable clinical value, as moderate side effects were associated with the lowest adherence rate (30.4 %), reflecting a 38.4 percentage-point decrease compared to patients without side effects (p < 0.001). This magnitude calls for proactive symptom management protocols, including routine toxicity screening and timely intervention. As early intervention, especially during the mild-to-moderate transition phase, can avert the “tipping point” at which symptom burden triggers nonadherence. Therefore, integrating symptom monitoring and patient education about side-effect management strategies into standard care pathways represents an actionable strategy to improve adherence.

Menopausal status also played a role in adherence, particularly in the non-metastatic group. Compared with premenopausal women, postmenopausal women had significantly higher adherence. However, this association was not observed in the metastatic group, where adherence rates were consistent regardless of menopausal status, likely driven by high perceived treatment urgency.42 The adverse effects of endocrine treatments, including hot flushes, vaginal dryness, and musculoskeletal discomfort, might resemble or intensify menopausal symptoms, thereby potentially discouraging younger patients from adhering to treatment.39,52,53 A qualitative systematic study indicated that menopausal symptoms affected women's identity, engendering a perception of premature ageing, which in turn impacted their quality of life and adversely affected their adherence.47 Furthermore, premenopausal patients may harbour apprehensions regarding the effects of treatment on fertility or long-term health, which exacerbates nonadherence.41,50,54

Moreover, adherence was superior in patients on therapy for less than 2 years. Treatment fatigue, cumulative side effects, and diminishing perceived benefits over time may contribute to this trend.44 Given that numerous OAMs have been utilised for up to ten years or until disease progression, sustained adherence is essential for ongoing BC management.51 Nonetheless, the literature indicates that compliance with prolonged OAMs progressively diminishes over time.6,12

Although not statistically significant, patients with comorbidities showed lower adherence. Despite the lack of statistical significance, the observed trend aligns with previously published studies.12,22,55 Polypharmacy, treatment fatigue, and the burden of multiple conditions may reduce OAM adherence, as patients often prioritize more symptomatic or perceived life-threatening diseases over cancer.56

The multidimensional and layered character of nonadherence to OAMs is underscored by the findings of this research, which revealed a variety of nonadherence patterns. Certain patients demonstrated intentional nonadherence by actively discontinuing treatment owing to perceived recovery or side effects, while others unintentionally missed doses, often resulting from forgetfulness or confusion regarding dosing schedules. Forgetting was the most frequently cited reason, but a substantial portion of patients also discontinued therapy after feeling worse or assuming further treatment was no longer needed. Notably, the vast majority of nonadherent patients experienced both intentional and unintentional behaviours, illustrating the multifaceted nature of adherence behaviour.57 This complexity suggests that nonadherence should not be approached with one-size-fits-all solutions.57,58 Patients who forget doses may benefit from behavioral interventions, such as reminders and supportive routines, while those who intentionally stop treatment require more personalized education about the long-term goals of therapy.59,60

Our findings provide insights into real-world adherence behaviours among BC patients in a low and middle-income country, expanding the international literature to populations that are often underrepresented in adherence research. Recognizing the strong association between adherence, side effect severity, and disease stage highlights the importance of timely systematic symptom management and continuous patient education regarding treatment benefits. Integrating multidisciplinary teams and adopting dual-method adherence monitoring into oncology care pathways can facilitate early detection and management of nonadherence. Additionally, targeted interventions should address early-stage, premenopausal patients with treatment fatigue, and medication administrative barriers. Finally, addressing the multifactorial causes of nonadherence necessitates comprehensive personalized patient-centered adherence strategies, such as structured patient counselling, regular follow-up, and the incorporation of behavioral health support into standard oncology care pathways,58,60 to help sustain long-term adherence. Collectively, these findings provide a foundation for scalable adherence-support models in real-world oncology practice that may enhance treatment adherence and optimize clinical outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the diverse patient population and the use of dual adherence measurement methods, which provide robust insights into adherence behaviours. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, self-reported adherence measures, such as the MMAS-8, may be subject to recall bias. Second, MPR was used rather than Proportion of Days Covered (PDC) for pharmacy-based adherence due to its straightforward calculation from our dispensing records. While the PDC is generally preferred in adherence research for its ability to account for overlapping fills, MPR provided an efficient initial measure for this cross-sectional study. Future analyses will incorporate PDC to complement these findings and provide more robust adherence estimates. Third, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw conclusions about causality in terms of the relationship between adherence and associated variables. Given the large sample size, even small numerical differences could reach statistical significance. We therefore emphasize that these findings should be interpreted with caution, considering their potential clinical relevance rather than relying solely on statistical significance. Future longitudinal studies should evaluate whether the observed associations translate into meaningful improvements in treatment adherence or clinical outcomes. Finally, the study was executed at a solitary tertiary care centre, which may restrict the generalisability of the findings to other settings or populations.

Conclusion

Adherence to OAMs is significantly influenced by metastatic status and the severity of side effects. Enhanced patient education and management strategies are critical for improving adherence among BC patients, particularly those experiencing severe side effects. Strategies such as regular side effect management and simplified treatment regimens could mitigate barriers to adherence. The multifaceted character of nonadherence highlights the need for integrated, multidisciplinary care methods since effective adherence support requires a combination of practical tools with empathetic, individualised guidance from the care team. Ongoing research should focus on developing and testing targeted interventions to improve adherence in these patient groups.
