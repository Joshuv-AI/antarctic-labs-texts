Spatial organization of the TNBC tumor microenvironment: multicellular niches, T-cell bottlenecks, and therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Jinpeng Wu, Jingjing Fan, Tong Sha, Hongtao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) lacks stable druggable targets, is highly aggressive, and exhibits marked heterogeneity; consequently, therapeutic variability is often determined not only by tumor cell–intrinsic states but also by the immune–stromal ecology of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and its modes of spatial organization. The TNBC TME is jointly shaped by adaptive and innate immune cells, fibroblasts and the extracellular matrix, the vascular–hypoxia–lymphatic axis, and neural components, whose tissue-scale co-localization and boundary architecture underlie distinct phenotypes such as immune activation, immune exclusion, and immune desert.Main bodyRecent advances in single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and multiplex imaging now enable in situ characterization of cellular composition, functional states, spatial positioning, and local interactions, thereby grounding complex multicellular crosstalk in observable microanatomical patterns. Focusing on recurrent constraints on T-cell entry, intratumoral positioning and productive contact, and the maintenance of effector function, this Review synthesizes the spatial heterogeneity of adaptive immunity and local immune organization, and delineates how myeloid programs and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) subsets and extracellular matrix (ECM) barriers, aberrant vasculature and hypoxia-linked metabolism, and the tumor–nerve signaling axis cooperatively shape immunosuppressive niches that drive progression and therapeutic resistance. We further summarize spatial neighborhoods/niches that are reproducibly identifiable across cohorts in TNBC (e.g. hypoxic niches, the stromal–myeloid axis formed by co-localized CA9+ CAFs and SPP1+ macrophages, and tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS)-associated immune-activated units) and discuss their associations with prognosis and therapeutic response.Conclusions: Finally, integrating progress in TME-targeting clinical studies with window-of-opportunity sampling paradigms, we propose incorporating spatial neighborhood features and interaction signatures into stratification and dynamic assessment, and developing combination strategies centered on relieving myeloid suppression, remodeling stromal barriers, and correcting vascular/metabolic niches, with the goal of improving precision prediction and durable benefit of immunotherapy in TNBC.ConclusionsFinally, integrating progress in TME-targeting clinical studies with window-of-opportunity sampling paradigms, we propose incorporating spatial neighborhood features and interaction signatures into stratification and dynamic assessment, and developing combination strategies centered on relieving myeloid suppression, remodeling stromal barriers, and correcting vascular/metabolic niches, with the goal of improving precision prediction and durable benefit of immunotherapy in TNBC.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) lacks subtype-specific actionable therapeutic targets and is among the most aggressive breast cancer subtypes with the poorest prognosis [1–3]. In current clinical practice, even when surgery combined with conventional multimodal regimens such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy constitutes the therapeutic backbone, long-term outcomes remain suboptimal and treatment efficacy varies markedly across individuals—underscoring the need for a more systematic stratification framework (e.g., integrating immune-related multi-omic features) to explain and predict such differences [4–6]. Meanwhile, although the introduction of emerging therapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors has created new therapeutic windows for a subset of patients, clinical benefit often concentrates within biomarker-defined subgroups, indicating that precise selection and response prediction remain key bottlenecks [7–9]. Thus, tumor cell–intrinsic features alone are insufficient to explain the marked differences in clinical outcomes among patients with TNBC; accumulating evidence suggests that the cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and its spatial organization together shape TNBC progression and therapeutic response/resistance [10–12].

The TNBC TME is composed of immune cells, fibroblasts, and extracellular matrix (ECM); as a complex and dynamic ecosystem, it can substantially influence key biological processes such as tumor growth and metastasis [13]. Single-cell and multi-omic analyses further indicate that the TNBC immune microenvironment exhibits pronounced heterogeneity in cellular composition, interaction networks, and differentiation trajectories, and that such variation correlates with tumor progression and responses to immunotherapy [14]. In this framework, different microenvironmental components are not merely juxtaposed; instead, they form self-reinforcing suppressive circuits through signaling and metabolic crosstalk. For example, CA9+ cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) can strongly co-localize with SPP1+ tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in a hypoxic context accompanied by aberrant angiogenesis and glycolysis, forming a stromal–myeloid axis that promotes immune evasion via interactions such as VEGFA/NRP2, reduces effector T-cell infiltration, and weakens responses to immunotherapy [15]. In parallel, increased regulatory T cells are associated with an immunosuppressive milieu and treatment resistance [16], while nutrient competition between tumor cells and macrophages can spatially remodel antitumor immunity, thereby influencing sensitivity to immunotherapy [17]. Importantly, immune efficacy in TNBC cannot be captured solely by whether immune cells infiltrate: the compartmentalization of immune cells between tumor parenchyma and stroma, ordered structures at the tumor–immune interface, and the resulting spatial immune subtypes can stratify and predict patient survival or treatment response [18–20]. Despite the accumulation of these findings, the molecular basis of stromal activation and tumor–stroma interactions—and their causally testable perturbation-based validation—remains insufficiently elucidated [21].

In recent years, the maturation of single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and multiplex imaging has enabled cell-resolution, in situ characterization of TNBC microenvironmental composition, states, and local interactions, integrating cellular composition, functional states, spatial positioning, and local interactions into a unified analytical framework [4, 12]. More importantly, these advances extend beyond merely cataloging heterogeneity: they reveal reproducible spatial prototypes/niches and their characteristic molecular programs within TNBC tumor–stroma architecture, providing a quantifiable description of tissue organization [22]. On this basis, links between spatial organization and therapeutic response/resistance are being captured with greater precision: the coupling of multicellular spatial architecture with cellular activation states and spatial positioning can distinguish sensitive from resistant tumors early during treatment, supporting the development of spatial metrics for prediction and dynamic treatment adaptation [12, 23]. On this basis, previously intractable complexity is increasingly being translated into testable and potentially actionable organizational principles. This Review examines how the spatial organization of the TNBC microenvironment shapes T-cell entry into tumor tissue, intratumoral positioning and productive contact, and the maintenance of effector function. We discuss the contributions of adaptive and myeloid immunity, CAF–ECM remodeling, vascular, hypoxic, and lymphatic niches, and neural regulation, and then summarize recurrent spatial architectures and their implications for therapeutic stratification and combination strategies. Spatially organized multicellular niches constrain adaptive immunity in the triple-negative breast cancer microenvironment

The triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) tumor microenvironment (TME) comprises interconnected stromal, myeloid, vascular–hypoxic–lymphatic, neural, and lymphoid compartments that collectively restrict antitumor T-cell responses. Cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) states, including myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), and extracellular matrix–producing CAFs (ecmCAFs), support the formation of fibrotic stromal barriers through extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and stiffening. Tumor-derived exosomal miR-9 promotes fibroblast reprogramming through EFEMP1 downregulation, whereas CAF-derived CXCL12 contributes to myeloid recruitment and G protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER) activation supports glutamine supply to tumor cells.

Aberrant vasculature, hypoperfusion, and hypoxia further reinforce immune suppression. Extracellular nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (eNAMPT)-associated angiogenesis and NG2-positive pericyte-enriched vessels can generate structurally abnormal and leaky vascular networks, limiting cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) entry and impairing drug delivery. Hypoxic signaling promotes pro-angiogenic macrophage programs, including the PlGF–VEGFR1 axis and B7–H3^high tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Lymphatic remodeling is supported by the HUMT–YBX1–FOXK1–VEGFC axis and by macrophage-derived TGF-β1, which increases lymphatic dilation and permeability, thereby facilitating lymph-node dissemination and pre-metastatic niche conditioning.

Myeloid populations provide additional layers of suppression. Dendritic-cell (DC) cross-presentation and maturation can be impaired by tumor-derived exosomal CDC37, TIM-3-mediated inhibition of cGAS–STING–type I interferon signaling, and RANKL–RANK-dependent mTOR suppression. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), induced in part by CHI3L1, restrict CD8^+ T-cell access and suppress T-cell activity through NET-associated PD-L1; NET-derived DNA can also activate the CCDC25 pathway in tumor cells. Monocyte recruitment and differentiation into TAMs are promoted by CSF-1 and CCL5. TAMs suppress antitumor immunity through checkpoint-related and soluble mediators, including PD-L1, VISTA, TGF-β, and IL-10, and can impair phagocytosis through the CD52–Siglec-G axis. Reciprocal interactions between SPP1^+ TAMs and ecmCAFs reinforce a fibrotic, immune-excluding stromal–myeloid niche.

Neural signals further influence tumor progression and immune organization. Sensory-neuron-associated NGF–TrkA signaling, CGRP, substance P, and PlexinB3-related programs can promote invasion and nerve-associated migration, whereas sympathetic β2-adrenergic and cholinergic α9-nAChR signaling support stem-like and invasive tumor phenotypes. At a higher spatial level, recurrent microenvironmental configurations include hypoxic niches enriched in dormant-like tumor cells, exhausted T cells, and supportive fibroblasts; CA9^+ CAF–SPP1^+ TAM niches associated with ECM thickening and immune exclusion; and TLS/DC-rich niches containing B cells, DCs, and TCF1^+ stem-like CD8^+ T cells. Together, these interacting compartments constrain T-cell entry, intratumoral positioning and productive tumor–immune contact, and effector function. T-cell bottlenecks in the TNBC microenvironment

Adaptive immunity in TNBC varies substantially across patients and across spatial regions within the same tumor [18, 24]. Similar heterogeneity is also observed in metastatic TNBC lesions [25]. Based on the distribution of T cells relative to tumor nests and stromal regions, TNBC tumors can be broadly classified as immune-desert, immune-excluded, or inflamed. Immune-desert tumors contain few infiltrating T cells; immune-excluded tumors retain lymphocytes predominantly within stromal or peripheral regions; and inflamed tumors show deeper penetration of T cells into the tumor parenchyma [11, 19]. These spatial phenotypes are associated with prognosis and treatment response: tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL)-rich, inflamed tumors generally exhibit more favorable outcomes, whereas immune-desert and immune-excluded tumors are more frequently associated with limited therapeutic benefit [11, 22, 24, 26].

Single-cell sequencing and multiplex profiling further show that these spatial patterns reflect distinct immune ecologies. Some TNBC tumors are enriched for activated cytotoxic T cells and Th1-associated programs, whereas others contain higher proportions of regulatory T cells and suppressive myeloid populations [27–31]. Together, these findings indicate that antitumor T-cell responses can be constrained at several points, including recruitment into tumor tissue, positioning relative to tumor cells and antigen-presenting niches, and maintenance of effector function after infiltration.

T-cell accumulation within TNBC lesions is shaped by stromal architecture, chemokine availability, and tumor-cell antigen-presentation programs. Distinct fibroblast states and their spatial organization can generate stromal regions with restricted lymphocyte access [32–34]. LRRC15^+ CAFs, for example, are associated with desmoplastic and immunosuppressive stromal programs that limit T-cell infiltration [34]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) can provide an additional physical constraint by forming mesh-like structures that restrict T-cell entry into tumor regions [35].

These structural barriers are compounded by variation in inflammatory signaling. Tumor-cell-intrinsic cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) signaling supports the expression of chemokines such as CCL5 and CXCL10, whereas attenuation of this axis is associated with reduced lymphocyte recruitment. In TNBC, MYC-driven programs can suppress interferon-related signaling and weaken this chemokine response, contributing to poorly infiltrated tumor states [36]. Tumor-cell antigen presentation further shapes the immune context. Reduced surface expression of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) impairs immune recognition and weakens CD8^+ T-cell responses [37]. MHC-I-low tumors may become more dependent on NK-cell surveillance while simultaneously evading NK-cell killing through HLA-E–NKG2A signaling, providing a potential rationale for NKG2A blockade [37]. Conversely, tumor-cell expression of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) pathway molecules is associated with greater TIL infiltration and improved prognosis, consistent with a more inflamed immune microenvironment [38].

Even when T cells infiltrate TNBC lesions, including tumor-specific tissue-resident memory-like CD8^+ T cells (T_RM), their cytotoxic activity can remain constrained [39–41]. Immunosuppressive myeloid populations, including tumor-associated macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, release factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β and compete for local nutrients, thereby impairing T-cell proliferation and function [30, 42]. CAF-derived CXCL12 and TGF-β can further restrict productive interactions between T cells and tumor cells while promoting sustained expression of exhaustion-associated molecules [34, 43]. Tumor cells add another layer of suppression through checkpoint-ligand expression and immunoediting-associated loss of antigen-presentation molecules, reducing recognition by tumor-reactive T cells [36, 44].

Under these convergent pressures, infiltrating T cells frequently acquire dysfunctional or exhausted states characterized by persistent expression of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), TOX, and related factors, together with reduced effector cytokine production [42, 44]. However, T-cell dysfunction is not uniform. A T cell factor 1 (TCF1)^+ stem-like subset can retain self-renewal capacity and proliferative responsiveness to checkpoint blockade, in contrast to terminally exhausted TCF1-negative, TOX-high populations [44–46]. These findings indicate that T-cell abundance alone is insufficient to define effective antitumor immunity; intratumoral positioning and functional state must also be considered.

Beyond T-cell abundance and state, adaptive antitumor immunity depends on the participation of immunostimulatory cell types and on whether lymphoid-like organizational units form locally within tumors. Dendritic cells (DCs) are the key antigen-presenting cells that initiate priming of naïve T-cell responses; among them, Batf3-dependent type 1 conventional DCs (cDC1) cross-present tumor antigens and activate CD8^+ T cells. In TNBC, the density and functional state of cDC1 critically influence the quality of T-cell responses [47]. However, DC function in the breast tumor microenvironment is shaped by suppressive factors, and different DC subsets vary in their capacity to adapt to the tumor milieu. Effective T-cell immunity requires coordinated interactions between DCs and other immune cells: for instance, chemokines produced by natural killer (NK) cells can promote cDC1 recruitment into tumors, thereby supporting cDC1-mediated cross-priming and enhancing antitumor CD8^+ T-cell responses [48]. In addition, specific intratumoral CD4^+ T cells (e.g., Th1-like helper T cells) can provide licensing signals that activate cDC1, enabling more efficient induction of CD8^+ T-cell cytotoxicity [49, 50]. On the other hand, some TNBCs develop tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), lymph node–like aggregates within or at the edge of tumors that contain B-cell follicles and T-cell zones. The presence of TLS and the abundance of tumor-infiltrating B cells have been associated with stronger local immune responses and improved clinical outcomes [51–54]. In breast cancer, functionally Th1-skewed follicular helper T cells (T_FH) have been detected and are coupled to B-cell maturation and humoral immunity within TLS, supporting more effective adaptive immunity [52]. By contrast, tumors lacking organized immune architectures such as TLS often show insufficient local immune priming [53, 54]. Notably, these lymphoid-like structures and DC-enriched regions may provide intratumoral niches that support the maintenance of stem-like CD8^+ T-cell populations and support durable immune responses [55]. Thus, the strength of adaptive immunity in TNBC is determined not only by T cells themselves, but also by the integrity of antigen-presenting compartments and local lymphoid-like structures. Taken together, adaptive immunity in TNBC is shaped by the coordinated effects of T-cell recruitment, intratumoral positioning, effector state, antigen presentation, and local lymphoid organization. The following sections discuss how myeloid networks, CAF–ECM remodeling, vascular and hypoxic niches, and higher-order spatial architecture influence these processes and inform potential combination strategies.

Myeloid niches and T-cell suppression

Myeloid cells constitute a central component of the TNBC TME. They can coordinately orchestrate immunosuppression, inflammatory signal transduction, and local tissue remodeling, and under therapeutic pressure they drive dynamic rewiring of immune niches [23, 56]. Spatial multi-omics and single-cell studies indicate that the TNBC immune microenvironment exhibits pronounced spatial heterogeneity; a prototypical immunosuppressive configuration is characterized by enrichment of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) alongside relative scarcity of cytotoxic CD8^+ T cells, and is associated with unfavorable outcomes and therapy-insensitive phenotypes [12, 57]. Such cell-compositional and spatial organizational patterns—already evident at baseline and in the early on-treatment window—can distinguish divergent immune trajectories and are linked to the subsequent shape of immune responses [12, 23]. Given the central and highly plastic role of myeloid cells in initiating inflammation and sculpting antitumor immunity, myeloid populations in TNBC often undergo sustained reprogramming under the combined influence of tumor-intrinsic inflammatory axes (e.g., programs related to IL-1β processing) and therapeutic/metabolic stress, thereby promoting TAM recruitment and the maintenance and expansion of immunosuppressive niches [56, 57].

Although accumulating evidence suggests that other innate immune cell types, such as eosinophils, can also be reshaped by tumor-derived cues and participate in immunosuppression, the following sections will focus on the key functions and spatial organization of macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils in TNBC [58].

Tumor-associated macrophages

TAMs are prominent components of the TNBC microenvironment and contribute to antitumor immune imbalance and tissue remodeling [57, 59]. TNBC cells can recruit macrophages through colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1; also known as macrophage colony-stimulating factor), thereby promoting TAM accumulation within tumors [60]. Beyond the traditional M1/M2 dichotomy, TAMs in TNBC arise from diverse sources, including mammary tissue-resident macrophages and recruited peripheral monocytes [60, 61], and exhibit heterogeneous transcriptional and functional states.For example, single-cell analyses have identified a TAM subset enriched for YTHDC2, an N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-binding RNA helicase. These macrophages display an antitumor inflammatory phenotype, with upregulation of CD80, CD86, IL1B, TNF, and related programs and more active interactions with T cells and NK cells, whereas YTHDC2-low macrophages are enriched for immunosuppression-associated genes [62]. Spatially, distinct TAM subsets preferentially occupy specific tumor niches: for instance, in hypoxic regions and at the tumor nest–stroma interface, a population of SPP1^+ macrophages recruited by tumor cell–derived osteopontin (SPP1) is observed adjacent to matrix-producing fibroblast clusters and exhibits a distinctive transcriptional program [63]. Spatial-omics studies further report that, in some TNBC patients, an immune-desert niche dominated by an endothelial–macrophage–mesenchymal cell circuitry is prevalent and correlates with poorer survival [24]; by contrast, other tumors exhibit prominent T-cell infiltration, underscoring that the spatiotemporal distribution of TAMs and their interaction patterns can steer the immune state of the TNBC microenvironment.

TAMs co-emerge with, and reinforce, immunosuppressive microenvironments and are among the major myeloid populations constraining antitumor immunity in TNBC [57]. Their suppressive effects extend beyond cytokine release and include checkpoint-ligand expression and impaired macrophage-mediated clearance of tumor cells. Both TNBC cells and macrophages can express high levels of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1); in a neoadjuvant immunotherapy study, PD-L1 positivity on either population was associated with pathological response, indicating that TAM infiltration and checkpoint-ligand expression may influence therapeutic outcomes [64]. TAMs can also express the inhibitory receptor V-domain immunoglobulin suppressor of T-cell activation (VISTA). In TNBC models, blockade of macrophage VISTA shifts TAMs toward a more immune-stimulatory state and is accompanied by increased CD8^+ T-cell infiltration and activation [65]. In parallel, tumor-cell CD52 engages the inhibitory receptor Siglec-G on macrophages and limits tumor-cell phagocytosis. Blocking the CD52–Siglec-G interaction restores macrophage phagocytic activity and enhances antitumor immunity [66].

TAMs can also sustain an inflammatory yet immunosuppressive microenvironment in which effective T-cell responses remain limited. TAM-derived cytokines, including TGF-β and IL-10, suppress effector T-cell activity and promote regulatory T-cell accumulation [67]. At the same time, reciprocal interactions between TAMs and tumor cells can amplify inflammatory programs that favor tumor progression. In co-culture models, macrophages increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) and IL-1α expression in TNBC cells. IL-1α subsequently induces EMT-associated and inflammatory genes, including ZEB1, VIM, IL-1β, and IL-8, thereby promoting tumor-cell survival and metastatic potential [68]. The ETS1–caspase-1–IL-1β axis provides a related mechanism: activation of this pathway promotes IL-1β maturation and TAM recruitment, accompanied by reduced infiltration of CD8^+ T cells into tumor nests. Its inhibition redistributes TAMs and increases T-cell access to tumor regions [57].

Tumor cells can further reinforce TAM accumulation and polarization through soluble mediators and extracellular vesicles. Tumor-derived CSF-1 and CCL5 promote macrophage recruitment and contribute to the accumulation of tumor-promoting TAMs in TNBC [59, 60]. Ex vivo cultures of human TNBC tissues further indicate that tumor-derived extracellular vesicles can promote monocyte chemotaxis toward tumor regions and favor differentiation into TAM-like states [69]. These interactions may intensify under therapeutic pressure. Following chemotherapy-induced tumor-cell death, exosomes enriched in CXCL1 can be taken up by macrophages and promote the accumulation of immunosuppressive PD-L1^+ TAMs, thereby supporting immune evasion and metastatic progression of residual tumors [70].

TAM polarization is also shaped by systemic metabolic conditions and tumor-intrinsic signaling programs. In obesity-associated mammary adipose tissue, metabolically activated macrophages secrete factors that enhance the invasive potential of TNBC cells, linking local inflammation to tumor progression [71]. Tumor-cell expression of MCT-1 provides an additional example: by increasing IL-6/IL-6 R signaling through miR-34a downregulation, MCT-1 promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition and stem-like features in TNBC cells while favoring M2-like polarization of neighboring macrophages [72].

Beyond direct effects on immune cells, TAMs also cooperate with CAFs and the ECM to establish a physical immune barrier at the tumor–stroma boundary. In the dense-stroma subtype of TNBC, TAMs and CAFs are tightly coupled, driving a fibrotic microenvironment that is difficult for immune cells to penetrate [59, 63]. For instance, integrated single-cell and spatial analyses have identified an osteopontin-high TAM population (SPP1^+ macrophages) that frequently localizes near ECM-producing CAF (ecmCAF) subsets. Under hypoxic conditions, these macrophages secrete elevated levels of TGF-β1, IL-1β, and SPP1, promoting the differentiation of normal fibroblasts into ECM-depositing ecmCAFs and thereby exacerbating stromal fibrosis and T-cell exclusion [63]. Tumor cell–derived osteopontin can also act as an upstream amplifier: in TNBC models, manipulating tumor osteopontin secretion alters TAM recruitment, and recruited/activated TAMs in turn release factors that promote CAF activation and collagen deposition (e.g., TGF-β), indirectly driving stromal fibrosis; this process may also involve division of labor among TAM subsets with distinct functions [59]. Collectively, TAM–CAF/ECM crosstalk constitutes an important basis for the immune-excluding, densely fibrotic architecture observed in TNBC.

Although TAMs frequently adopt tumor-promoting immunosuppressive states, their functional programs remain plastic and can be redirected toward antitumor activity through therapeutic intervention. Several preclinical strategies have sought to exploit this plasticity.One approach is to reduce TAM recruitment or survival by targeting the CSF-1–colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF-1 R) axis. For example, delivery of a CSF-1 R inhibitor to TAMs using polymeric nanovesicles reduced immunosuppressive macrophage features and promoted a more inflammatory phenotype [73]. A complementary strategy is to alter TAM polarization through targeted delivery systems. Biodegradable nanoparticles guided by an M2-macrophage-affinity peptide and loaded with curcumin (M2pep-Cs-Cur NP) selectively accumulated in CD206+ macrophages within TNBC tumors and attenuated their immunosuppressive polarization [74]. This intervention not only inhibited TNBC cell proliferation and invasion, but also increased tumor sensitivity to PD-L1–directed immunotherapy. Innate immune activation provides another route to reshape myeloid responses. In TNBC models, the combination of paclitaxel and the STING agonist cyclic GMP–AMP (cGAMP) enhanced antitumor activity through Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-dependent reprogramming of macrophages and other myeloid cells [75]. Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling represents another potential target for modulating TAM states. In TNBC mouse models, Formosanin C reduced STAT3 phosphorylation, decreased the intratumoral abundance of M2-like macrophages, and suppressed tumor progression [76]. The CD52–Siglec-G interaction also represents a potential therapeutic target. CD52 blockade restores macrophage phagocytic activity and, when combined with anti–PD-1 therapy, enhances immune clearance [66]. Lipid-derived mediators may also modulate tumor–macrophage interactions. In vitro, the omega-3 derivative docosahexaenoyl ethanolamide (DHEA) reduced CCL5 secretion by TNBC cells and thereby attenuated macrophage recruitment [77]. Together, these findings indicate that TAMs are not irreversibly fixed in immunosuppressive states and that selected interventions can partially restore antitumor macrophage functions.

Overall, TAMs contribute to immune evasion, invasive progression, and fibrotic stromal remodeling in TNBC, while retaining sufficient plasticity to remain therapeutically tractable [57, 63, 66, 76]. Their suppressive effects are reinforced by other microenvironmental constraints, including impaired antigen presentation and dense stromal barriers. Effective microenvironment-directed strategies will therefore likely require coordinated modulation of multiple interacting TME components.

Dendritic-cell dysfunction and impaired T-cell priming

In TNBC, DCs within tumors often display low infiltration, impaired maturation, and functional dysfunction, thereby constituting a bottleneck at the initiation stage of antitumor immunity [78, 79]. DC abundance and activation status are tightly linked to T-cell infiltration and the overall immune landscape: for example, an increased proportion of activated PD-L1^+ DCs in tumors is frequently accompanied by greater CD8^+ T-cell infiltration or clonal expansion [80]. Conversely, high tumor-cell expression of cell division cycle 37 (CDC37) can impair DC cross-presentation, accompanied by reduced infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and poor responses to immune checkpoint blockade [78]. Multiple mechanisms in the TNBC microenvironment can impair DC function. Tumor-derived exosomal CDC37 binds heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) in DCs and restricts antigen translocation from endosomes to the cytosol, thereby compromising cross-presentation [78]. Receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) signaling through receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK) on DCs and plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) can suppress mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) activity and maintain a tolerogenic, incompletely mature phenotype characterized by reduced co-stimulatory molecule expression and increased expression of CCR7, ILT3, and ICOSL [81].

DC-intrinsic inhibitory receptors provide an additional layer of regulation. Elevated TIM-3 expression on tumor-infiltrating cDC1 limits the uptake of extracellular tumor DNA and suppresses cGAS–STING activation, reducing the production of type I interferons and CXCL9 and thereby impairing T-cell recruitment and activation [82]. Local glucocorticoid production can further promote tolerogenic DC states, whereas genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of tumor-intrinsic steroidogenesis restores a more mature DC phenotype [83].

Treatment-associated and lymph-node contexts also influence DC priming. Radiotherapy can induce ecto-5′-nucleotidase (CD73) expression in tumor cells and increase adenosine accumulation, limiting the infiltration of Batf3+ cDC1 into tumors; CD73 blockade can partially reverse this effect and promote systemic antitumor immune activation [84]. In tumor-draining lymph nodes associated with immune-cold tumors, mature DCs spatially co-cluster with Th2 cells in regions enriched for IL-4+ mast cells, suggesting a bias toward Th2 rather than effective Th1/CTL responses [79].

Distinct DC lineages and activation states coexist in TNBC and associated lymphoid organs, including classical type 1 DCs (cDC1; canonical markers such as XCR1^+/CD103^+) specialized for cross-presentation, classical type 2 DCs (cDC2) biased toward CD4^+ T-cell priming, plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) that predominantly produce type I interferons yet can be rendered tolerogenic by the tumor milieu, and mature/migratory DC subsets expressing trafficking molecules such as CCR7 (e.g., LAMP3^+ migratory DCs that transport antigen to lymph nodes) [79, 85]. In TNBC, defects in antigen uptake, processing, and presentation can limit the capacity of DCs to prime tumor-specific T-cell responses. Tumor-infiltrating DCs may remain incompletely mature, with reduced expression of MHC molecules and co-stimulatory signals and limited production of immunostimulatory cytokines [81]. Impaired transfer of internalized antigen from endosomes to the cytosol, as observed in the tumor-derived CDC37 mechanism, further restricts cross-presentation and the priming of cytotoxic T lymphocytes [78].

DCs also contribute to the spatial organization of antitumor immunity. Under effective immune conditions, DC-derived CXCL9 and CXCL10 recruit CXCR3+ effector T cells, while CCR7-dependent migration enables antigen presentation in draining lymph nodes [79, 85]. In TNBC, elevated TIM-3 signaling reduces DC production of chemokines and IL-12, contributing to limited intratumoral CD8+ T-cell accumulation [82]. In parallel, DC-associated Th2 polarization in tumor-draining lymph nodes may weaken effective Th1 and cytotoxic T-cell responses [79]. Together, impaired antigen presentation, chemokine production, and migratory function can constrain the initiation and maintenance of antitumor T-cell immunity.

Therapeutic modulation of DC-suppressive mechanisms can partially restore antitumor immunity in TNBC. Delivery of engineered exosome-based immunomodulators to tumor-draining lymph nodes promotes local DC maturation, increases the abundance of activated CD80+CD86+ DCs, and enhances CD8+ T-cell infiltration in tumors and lymph nodes [86]. RANKL blockade provides another potential approach: neutralization of RANKL reverses the tolerogenic phenotype of tumor-infiltrating pDCs, promotes DC maturation, and reduces regulatory T-cell (Treg) accumulation, accompanied by decreased tumor growth and pulmonary metastatic burden in TNBC models [81]. Together, these findings indicate that DC abundance, maturation, and spatial distribution influence the quality of antitumor T-cell responses and may provide targets for microenvironment-directed combination strategies.

Neutrophils and neutrophil extracellular traps

In TNBC, neutrophils tend to adopt immunosuppressive and pro-metastatic behaviors, with NET formation representing a key effector program [58, 87]. Neutrophil infiltration and NET abundance correlate with CD8^+ T-cell exclusion and lung metastasis [35, 88]. Multiple factors can drive this phenotype: tumor metabolic dysregulation (e.g., lipid accumulation) can reprogram neutrophils toward an immunosuppressive state [58]; tumor-secreted vesicles or soluble factors can induce NETs and confer immunosuppressive characteristics [35, 87]; chronic stress and tumor exosomes remodel the pre-metastatic lung niche and recruit pro-metastatic neutrophils [89, 90]; and programs involving c-FOS and CTSC can promote NET formation and neutrophil recruitment, facilitating lung metastatic colonization [88, 91].

NETs contribute to both immune exclusion and metastatic dissemination in TNBC. TNBC-derived chitinase-3-like protein 1 (CHI3L1) induces NET formation that limits CD8+ T-cell infiltration, whereas PD-L1 displayed on NET surfaces directly suppresses T-cell activity [35, 87]. NET-derived DNA can also be sensed by coiled-coil domain-containing protein 25 (CCDC25) on tumor cells, activating the integrin-linked kinase (ILK)–β-parvin pathway and promoting lung metastasis. In parallel, tumor-derived ROS–p38–c-FOS signaling and the CTSC–PR3–IL-1β axis further enhance NET formation and neutrophil-associated metastatic progression [88, 91, 92].

Neutrophil-associated immunosuppressive and metastatic programs are potentially targetable. Inhibition of NET formation, including through PAD4 blockade, or induction of ferroptosis in tumor-associated neutrophils can attenuate neutrophil-mediated immunosuppression and suppress tumor progression [92–95]. Neutrophil-mimetic delivery systems provide an additional therapeutic approach. These nanoparticles can disrupt tumor–neutrophil interactions to reduce metastatic progression or deliver a STING agonist together with a photothermal agent to induce immunogenic tumor-cell death and enhance antitumor immunity [96–98]. Modulation of broader innate immune programs, including through ERβ activation, may also restrain metastasis by altering neutrophil responses [99]. Overall, neutrophils and NETs contribute to both immune exclusion and metastatic dissemination in TNBC. Targeting neutrophil-associated immunosuppressive programs, particularly NET formation, may provide opportunities to limit tumor progression and metastasis [58, 87].

CAF–ECM niches and immune exclusion

Within the TNBC microenvironment, CAFs differ from their normal counterparts in their secretory profiles and matrix-remodeling activities. CAFs can deposit and reorganize ECM components and release soluble mediators that influence neighboring tumor and immune cells. However, the consequences of CAF–ECM remodeling are not uniform, but vary across fibroblast states, matrix compositions, and local microenvironmental conditions [100, 101]. Single-cell and spatial-omics approaches have revealed CAF heterogeneity and spatial patterning, which can be characterized along at least three axes: transcriptional states, marker-defined phenotypes, and cellular lineage/origin [5]. Overall, CAFs share core functions such as ECM remodeling, yet specific states/subsets can also exert immunosuppressive or therapy-resistance–promoting effects [101]. These subtypes are neither mutually exclusive nor unique to cancer (they can also arise in settings such as wound healing or aging), exist along continua, and display dynamic, plastic interconversion [100, 102]. Accordingly, the transition from normal fibroblasts to tumor-promoting CAF states may be reversible, raising the possibility of therapeutically redirecting CAF states rather than indiscriminately depleting fibroblasts.

Transcriptional CAF states

Single-cell RNA sequencing has revealed at least two transcriptionally differentiated CAF states in TNBC: a myofibroblastic, ECM-depositing state (myCAF) and an inflammatory state (iCAF) characterized by the expression of diverse growth factors and immunomodulatory molecules [103]. A third antigen-presenting CAF state (apCAF), characterized by MHC-II-related programs, has also been reported, with its proportion often increasing in later-stage tumors [104], although nomenclature varies across studies. myCAF-like CAFs produce large amounts of ECM and can form fibrotic stromal barriers that impede T-cell infiltration; in hypoxia-associated microenvironments, they can be driven by signals from SPP1^+ macrophages toward even more ECM-enriched states, thereby exacerbating fibrosis and immune exclusion [63]. One study further found that ECM-myCAF–derived factors activate the tumor-cell SRC–G0S2 pathway, promoting chemotherapy resistance in TNBC [101]. In contrast, iCAF-like CAFs secrete abundant inflammatory mediators and chemokines that shape the immune milieu: chemokines such as CXCL12 can recruit bone marrow–derived cells and, together with matrix metalloproteinases, facilitate tumor invasion [105]; in BRCA1-mutant TNBC, this subset interacts with endothelial cells via CXCL and VEGF programs to drive aberrant angiogenesis and is associated with poor chemotherapy response [106]. In addition, IL-34 secreted by certain inflammatory CAFs can induce polarization of neighboring macrophages toward an M2 state, fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment and correlating with unfavorable outcomes [107]. Notably, CAF transcriptional states remain plastic and can shift in response to microenvironmental cues and therapeutic perturbations. Blockade of TGF-β/SMAD signaling can suppress differentiation toward myofibroblastic programs and promote a more inflamed immune microenvironment [108]. These observations underscore the need to define which CAF states can be selectively modulated to improve therapeutic responses.

Protein and cell-surface markers of CAF heterogeneity

In TNBC, specific myofibroblast-like CAF subsets are enriched and can promote an immunosuppressive milieu by recruiting and inducing regulatory T cells [43]. Because no single marker captures the full diversity of CAF states, combinations of markers such as α-SMA, FAP, PDGFR, PDPN, and CD26 (DPP4), together with lineage exclusion, are commonly used to distinguish CAF populations [109]. In breast cancer, two major CAF populations, S100A4^+ and PDPN^+ CAFs, have been described, and their relative proportions correlate with patient outcomes; this association appears particularly pronounced in BRCA-associated TNBC [104]. Meanwhile, the stromal factor Asporin can inhibit TGF-β1–related signaling and is associated with tumor-suppressive effects and favorable prognosis [110]. An AKAP12-marked CAF subset can promote M2-like polarization via an IL-34/CSF1R-related mechanism and shape an immunosuppressive niche [107]. CAF-derived AEBP1 can bind CKAP4 and upregulate PD-L1, inducing T-cell dysfunction and correlating with poor prognosis [111]. Senescent CAFs can restrict NK-cell cytotoxicity by secreting ECM and can predict recurrence risk at the DCIS stage [102]. Therefore, stratifying CAFs using actionable protein markers may improve prognostic assessment and provide a basis for targeted strategies [112].

Cellular origins and plasticity of CAFs

CAF origin represents an additional dimension of heterogeneity in TNBC. Transplantation and single-cell studies suggest that CAFs can arise from tissue-resident mammary fibroblasts, with CD26− and CD26+ precursor populations preferentially contributing to myCAF- and iCAF-like states, respectively [105]. These findings suggest that part of CAF heterogeneity may reflect lineage-associated biases already present within the resident fibroblast pool.Co-culture models show that breast cancer cells can induce lung fibroblasts into CAF-like states and, through short-range LF→TNBC signaling, expand a dormancy-like subpopulation and autophagy-dependent drug resistance [113]. A defined combination of microRNAs carried by TNBC-derived extracellular vesicles can reprogram normal fibroblasts toward CAF-like states by targeting PATZ1, thereby altering the fibroblast secretome and promoting tumor-cell migration and invasion [114]. Additional extracellular-vesicle-associated microRNAs can induce CAF-associated markers, increase fibroblast motility, and enhance collagen contraction, consistent with the acquisition of a more motile and matrix-remodeling CAF-like phenotype [115]. In addition, tumors can recruit adipose stromal cells (ASCs) that contribute to the stromal compartment. D-CAN, a pro-apoptotic peptide designed to selectively target ASCs, reduced spontaneous lung metastasis in breast cancer models and enhanced the antitumor effects of cisplatin [116].

Reciprocal tumor–CAF interactions and ECM remodeling

Normal mammary fibroblasts can restrain tumor growth, whereas tumor-associated fibroblast states can acquire tumor-promoting properties through reciprocal interactions with TNBC cells [117]. TNBC cells can promote the acquisition of CAF-like features in neighboring fibroblasts. For example, tumor-derived exosomal miR-9 downregulates Fibulin-3 (EFEMP1) in fibroblasts, increasing their migratory and matrix-remodeling capacities; fibroblast-derived factors can in turn enhance chemoresistance in TNBC cells [118]. CAFs can also provide metabolic support to TNBC cells. Estrogenic stimulation activates the G protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER) in CAFs, increasing glutamine synthetase expression and glutamine release; uptake of CAF-derived glutamine by TNBC cells promotes survival, migration, and drug resistance [119]. Inflammatory CAFs can remodel both immune and stromal compartments. In TNBC, these cells express high levels of CXCL12, which promotes myeloid-cell recruitment, and facilitate matrix metalloproteinase-mediated ECM degradation, thereby supporting tumor-cell invasion [105].

TNBC tumors can exhibit a desmoplastic phenotype characterized by CAF enrichment and excessive ECM deposition. Activated CAFs contribute to matrix accumulation and remodeling, which can increase tissue stiffness, impede drug penetration, and restrict immune-cell access to tumor regions [120]. Sensory-neuron-driven CAF activation has also been shown to promote dense extracellular matrix formation and immune exclusion in the breast cancer tumor microenvironment [121]. However, ECM remodeling does not uniformly promote tumor progression. In breast tumor models, an ECM profile characterized by increased collagen and reduced fibronectin has been associated with attenuated tumor growth and metastasis, indicating that the biological effects of CAF-driven remodeling depend on matrix composition [122]. Matrix-producing CAF states are also associated with immune exclusion. Single-cell analyses have identified an ECM gene-enriched ecmCAF population that is enriched in residual tumors from immunotherapy non-responders and is associated with fibrosis and T-cell exclusion [63]. In preclinical models, αvβ3-targeted therapy reduced the abundance of CAFs and aberrantly angiogenic endothelial cells, decreased intratumoral collagen, alleviated hypoxia, and improved drug delivery. Reduced lysyl oxidase (LOX) secretion may also have contributed to the accompanying decrease in metastasis [123].

Overall, CAF states and the ECM programs they support influence TNBC progression through context-dependent effects on stromal architecture, immune-cell access, and therapeutic delivery. Future strategies should distinguish the specific CAF states and matrix components that contribute to treatment resistance and evaluate their selective modulation in combination with existing therapies.

Vascular, hypoxic, and lymphatic niches

Hypoxia responses are prominent in the TNBC tumor microenvironment. Hypoxia can induce a series of adaptive changes (e.g., upregulation of hypoxia-associated genes and activation of pro-angiogenic programs) and is also linked to invasion and metastasis [124]. In TNBC mouse models, tumors can secrete high levels of pro-angiogenic factors such as extracellular NAMPT, inducing extensive angiogenesis enriched for NG2^+ pericytes; however, these neovessels are frequently structurally aberrant and highly leaky and are associated with increased metastasis, indicating that increased vessel density does not necessarily translate into improved functional perfusion [125]. Administration of troxerutin to TNBC model tumors can transiently normalize disorganized vasculature and improve tumor oxygenation, providing proof-of-concept that modulating the vessel–hypoxia axis can support radiotherapy-based combinations [126]. These findings indicate that increased vascular density does not necessarily translate into improved perfusion or vascular function in TNBC. Endothelial metabolic dysregulation may contribute to this functional impairment and warrants further investigation [127].

Hypoxic regions can support pro-angiogenic TAM states in TNBC. In transplantation models, TAMs enriched in peritumoral hypoxic regions exhibit activation of the PlGF–VEGFR1 axis; pharmacological inhibition of HIF-1α shifts these macrophages toward a more inflammatory phenotype and attenuates their pro-angiogenic features [128]. In patient samples and functional models, B7–H3-high TAMs are associated with adverse outcomes and promote stromal remodeling and angiogenesis, contributing to an immunosuppressive vascular niche that facilitates tumor dissemination and restricts T-cell infiltration [129].

Lymphatic remodeling provides an additional route for metastatic dissemination in TNBC. The long non-coding RNA HUMT is enriched in TNBC cells with high lymphatic invasive capacity and is associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Mechanistically, HUMT recruits a YBX1-containing transcriptional complex to increase FOXK1 expression, thereby upregulating VEGFC, promoting lymphangiogenesis, and facilitating lymph-node metastasis [130]. Beyond the primary tumor, TNBC-derived signals can remodel lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) at distant sites before metastatic colonization. In mouse models, tumor-secreted factors induce CCL5 expression in LECs and promote local angiogenesis in pre-metastatic niches, including the lung and lymph nodes, thereby creating a microenvironment that supports the recruitment and colonization of circulating tumor cells [131].

Preclinical strategies have explored both hypoxia-associated microenvironmental modulation and vascular normalization in TNBC. The carbonic anhydrase IX inhibitor p-toluenesulfonamide (PTS) attenuated HIF-1α-related signaling and altered the expression of pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF in hypoxia-mimicking experiments and mouse models; its combination with PD-1 blockade has also been evaluated [132]. In parallel, the integrin-targeting peptide AXT201 reduced aberrant vascular features and increased pericyte coverage in TNBC mouse models, accompanied by decreased Treg and myeloid-derived suppressor cell abundance and enhanced T-cell effector activity [133].

Blood and lymphatic vessels have context-dependent roles in TNBC progression. Although functional vasculature supports immune-cell trafficking and drug delivery, vascular and lymphatic remodeling can also facilitate metastatic dissemination and immune exclusion. In TNBC models, macrophage-derived TGF-β1 induces lymphatic dilation and increases lymphatic permeability, thereby promoting tumor-cell invasion through lymphatic vessels [134]. Chronic stress-associated signaling can also support perivascular immunosuppressive macrophage niches that restrict T-cell infiltration. In model systems, β-adrenergic blockade reduces these suppressive macrophage populations and promotes T-cell entry, including when evaluated in combination with oncolytic virotherapy [135]. Further characterization of vascular niche heterogeneity and its interactions with immune and lymphatic compartments may help guide microenvironment-targeted strategies in TNBC.

Neural regulation of the TNBC microenvironment

Neural remodeling has emerged as an additional feature of the TNBC microenvironment. Altered nerve-fiber infiltration and organization have been observed in TNBC tissues and may contribute to tumor progression [136]. Clinical observations have associated persistent moderate-to-severe pain in patients with breast cancer with poorer outcomes, whereas effective pain control has been associated with longer survival [137]. Although the causal mechanisms remain incompletely defined, accumulating evidence indicates that neural signals can influence tumor cells, stromal components, and immune populations. Preclinical studies further suggest that modulation of sympathetic signaling may enhance antitumor responses [138].

Multiple peripheral nerve-fiber populations, including sensory and sympathetic fibers, have been detected in TNBC tissues [135, 136]. NGF–TrkA signaling has been implicated in tumor-cell migration and metastasis, potentially through a TrkA/CD44v3-associated complex [139]. Sensory neurons may also facilitate tumor invasion: TNBC cells can adhere to and migrate along nerve fibers through axon-guidance programs involving PlexinB3 [136]. In addition, sensory neuropeptides, including CGRP and substance P, can promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition and invasive phenotypes in TNBC cells, whereas activation of the endogenous κ-opioid pathway may partially counteract these effects [137].

Chronic stress can activate sympathetic signaling in TNBC. Catecholamine-mediated stimulation of β2-adrenergic receptors on tumor and stromal cells promotes invasion-associated and stem-like phenotypes through downstream pathways that include cAMP/PKA and intracellular Ca2+ signaling [140, 141]. In preclinical models, blockade of β-adrenergic signaling with propranolol reduced metastatic burden and improved tumor control during anthracycline treatment [138]. Sympathetic signaling can also alter the immune microenvironment by promoting the accumulation of immunosuppressive perivascular macrophages and limiting T-cell-mediated antitumor responses [135]. Cholinergic signaling may also influence TNBC progression. Nicotine exposure increases the expression of the α9 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α9-nAChR) and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) in tumor cells, promoting stem-like and invasive phenotypes [142]. In experimental models, inhibition of α9-nAChR-mediated signaling suppresses TNBC-cell proliferation and growth [143].

Neural regulation also intersects with stromal and immune programs in TNBC. CAF-derived brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and placental growth factor (PlGF) can influence invasion-associated phenotypes in TNBC cells through paracrine signaling [144]. Plexin-B1 signaling has also been implicated in remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment and altering CD8+ T-cell infiltration [145].

Tumor-cell expression of neural receptors provides an additional mechanism linking neural signaling to TNBC progression. High expression of nerve growth factor receptor (NGFR) is associated with mammosphere formation, anoikis resistance, enhanced tumor growth and metastasis, and poorer survival. NGFR can also promote metastatic signaling through interaction with TrkC [146]. Together, current evidence indicates that neural signals can influence tumor-cell behavior, stromal remodeling, and immune organization in TNBC. However, the clinical generalizability and relative contributions of these mechanisms require further validation.

Spatial architecture of the TNBC tumor microenvironment

Spatial organization in TNBC has been investigated using complementary technologies that resolve different features of the tumor immune microenvironment. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) links regional gene-expression programs to tissue morphology and enables the identification of tumor–stroma compartments, lymphoid-rich regions, and recurrent spatial archetypes [22, 32, 147]. Imaging mass cytometry (IMC) generates highly multiplexed single-cell protein maps, allowing cellular neighborhoods and their relationships with vascular structures to be characterized [148]. Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) and related image-based analyses quantify the localization, proximity, co-aggregation, and exclusion of defined immune-cell populations relative to tumor nests [20, 149–151]. Whole-slide image–based single-cell morphological and topological profiling further extends these analyses to tissue-scale ecotypes [152]. Integrating single-cell RNA sequencing with ST and mIF can connect spatial patterns with functionally defined cell states, as illustrated by the co-localization of CA9+ CAFs and SPP1+ macrophages [15].

The TNBC microenvironment exhibits marked spatial heterogeneity in its cellular composition and functional organization [32]. These patterns vary with both host-related features and tumor-intrinsic programs. For example, tumors from younger premenopausal patients can display increased T- and B-cell infiltration and upregulated antigen-presentation pathways [1], whereas basal-like tumor-cell clusters with EMT activation preferentially localize to stromal regions and are associated with chemoresistance [153]. Spatial profiling studies have further identified recurrent multicellular structures within TNBC tissues. Single-cell imaging mass cytometry analysis identified ten recurrent cellular neighborhoods with distinct combinations of tumor cells, B cells, fibroblasts, and other microenvironmental populations. Neighborhoods enriched in B cells and fibroblasts and characterized by perivascular immune activation were associated with longer survival [148]. Spatial transcriptomic clustering has also identified nine spatial archetypes in TNBC, some of which are associated with prognosis and potentially targetable biological features [22].

These complementary approaches reveal TNBC organization across multiple spatial scales. At the tissue scale, whole-slide image-based single-cell profiling has identified breast cancer ecotypes, including inflammatory ecotypes characterized by focal immune-cell accumulation and associated with immune activation and favorable treatment outcomes in TNBC [152]. At the regional scale, spatial transcriptomics has shown that tumors achieving pathological complete response (pCR) contain intermingled tumor and lymphocyte-rich regions accompanied by interferon-pathway activation, whereas non-responders exhibit enhanced angiogenic and oxygen-dependent metabolic programs [147]. At shorter spatial ranges, reduced distances between T cells and tumor cells are associated with improved responses to neoadjuvant therapy [149]. In TIL-rich tumors, CD8^+ T cells and CD20^+ B cells also co-aggregate near tumor nests, consistent with coordinated local B- and T-cell immunity [20].

Spatial proximity and exclusion patterns provide information that cannot be inferred from immune-cell abundance alone. Intratumoral and peritumoral CD8+ T-cell clusters are associated with favorable outcomes [154], whereas exclusion of immune cells from tumor regions is associated with limited therapeutic benefit [150]. In tumors from patients with relapse-free survival, intratumoral B cells show a more dispersed distribution, and small CD8+CD20+ lymphocytic aggregates localize closer to tumor islands [151]. Together, these findings suggest that the location and local organization of lymphocytes, rather than their presence alone, may contribute to effective immune surveillance.

Conversely, immunosuppressive spatial niches can counteract these favorable immune arrangements. Dormant tumor cells have been reported within hypoxic niches enriched in exhausted T cells and tumor-supportive fibroblasts [155]. Integrated single-cell and spatial analyses have also identified the co-localization of CA9+ CAFs and SPP1+ macrophages within tumor regions, defining a stromal–myeloid niche associated with reduced effector T-cell abundance and poorer outcomes [15]. These findings indicate that spatially restricted microenvironments can concentrate hypoxic, stromal, and myeloid programs that collectively reinforce immune dysfunction.

Together, these studies indicate that spatial organization provides information beyond cellular abundance alone. Recurrent cellular neighborhoods, proximity and exclusion patterns, and localized immunosuppressive niches are associated with prognosis and therapeutic response in TNBC, supporting their evaluation as candidate features for patient stratification. However, their reproducibility across independent cohorts and their incremental value beyond established biomarkers require further validation. Spatial neighborhood features and cell–cell interaction signatures may therefore complement existing stratification approaches and help identify tumors in which microenvironment-directed interventions could be prioritized. Candidate targets include immunosuppressive niches such as CA9+ CAF–SPP1+ macrophage regions and dormancy-associated hypoxic niches, although their clinical utility requires prospective validation.

Therapeutic opportunities targeting the TNBC microenvironment

Clinical and translational studies have increasingly examined how TME features influence therapeutic response in TNBC and whether selected microenvironmental components can be targeted in combination strategies [3, 8]. Dense collagen deposition and stromal stiffening can restrict immune-cell entry into tumor regions and contribute to immune-excluded spatial phenotypes [156]. In preclinical models, inhibition of the Hedgehog–CAF axis reduced fibrillar collagen-associated stromal barriers and improved chemosensitivity, supporting further evaluation of stromal remodeling strategies [157]. Clinical studies have evaluated anti-angiogenic agents, including vascular kinase inhibitors, as well as their combination with immunotherapy to modulate tumor vasculature and the surrounding microenvironment [158, 159]. However, the patient populations most likely to benefit and the optimal combination regimens remain to be defined, and vascular modulation alone may not be sufficient to overcome the broader mechanisms of treatment resistance.Residual lesions after immunotherapy can exhibit increased TGF-β signaling and enrichment of ECM-related features [159]. These observations support the evaluation of combination strategies that target stromal and vascular programs alongside immune-directed therapies, rather than relying on modulation of a single microenvironmental component. Several immunotherapy-centered combination strategies have been explored in TNBC [176]. Short-course induction therapy before immune checkpoint blockade may alter the tumor immune microenvironment and influence subsequent treatment responses. In the GeparNuevo trial, a window-of-opportunity phase of durvalumab was administered before neoadjuvant chemotherapy, enabling evaluation of immune biomarkers after brief immune exposure [164]. Similarly, the TONIC trial used short induction treatments before PD-1 blockade and identified post-induction changes in inflammation-related transcriptional programs and T-cell infiltration [165].

Vascular modulation represents another potential combination approach. Anti-angiogenic and VEGF-pathway-targeted agents have been evaluated alongside immunotherapy with the aim of improving vascular function and immune-cell access to tumor regions [160]. However, treatment-associated microenvironmental adaptation remains a challenge. Even in tumors with relatively high baseline immune infiltration, TAM-associated suppressive niches can emerge and limit therapeutic responses [177]. These findings support mechanism-informed combinations and biomarker-based patient stratification rather than reliance on modulation of a single microenvironmental pathway [178].

Clinical experience with immune checkpoint blockade in TNBC has expanded from monotherapy to combination regimens. Early studies of PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitor monotherapy in advanced TNBC reported heterogeneous clinical activity [179, 180]. Randomized trials subsequently evaluated immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)–chemotherapy combinations in advanced disease, with PD-L1 status used to characterize differences in treatment benefit across patient subgroups [9, 167, 168]. In high-risk early TNBC, neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy trials have extended this strategy to earlier disease stages using pathological response and event-based outcomes [169, 170]. Beyond chemotherapy-based regimens, early-phase studies have explored combinations designed to modify vascular and innate immune constraints. Anti-angiogenic or VEGF-pathway-targeted agents have been combined with immunotherapy, with safety, clinical activity, and translational biomarkers evaluated across early-phase studies [161, 181]. Myeloid-directed strategies are also being investigated. Eganelisib, a PI3K-γ inhibitor, has been combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors and chemotherapy to modulate TAM-associated immunosuppression, with exploratory analyses including PD-L1-negative disease [2]. Oleclumab, an anti-CD73 antibody, has similarly been evaluated with chemo-immunotherapy to counter adenosine-mediated immunosuppression [162]. Personalized neoantigen DNA vaccines have also shown feasibility and the capacity to induce neoantigen-specific T-cell responses in patients with TNBC, supporting further evaluation in combination strategies [175]. Clinical outcomes cannot be readily extrapolated across immunotherapy regimens or TNBC populations. KEYNOTE-119 compared pembrolizumab monotherapy with investigator-choice chemotherapy in metastatic TNBC and incorporated analyses according to the PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) [172]. IMpassion131 evaluated first-line paclitaxel with or without atezolizumab using PD-L1 stratification and hierarchical testing [173]. Together, these studies underscore the need to prespecify biomarker-defined subgroups and to validate each treatment backbone directly rather than extrapolate efficacy across regimens.

Translational studies are beginning to identify biomarkers that may refine patient stratification in TNBC. Analysis of baseline and early on-treatment samples from a randomized neoadjuvant ICI trial showed that integrating tumor-cell MHC-II expression with tissue features, including proliferating CD8+TCF1+ T cells detected early during treatment, improved prediction of ICI response [12]. Intratumoral tertiary lymphoid structures and enrichment of antigen-presenting mast cells have also been associated with greater benefit from anti–PD-1 therapy [163]. These findings suggest that spatial immune organization and selected immune-cell states may complement existing biomarkers in future stratification strategies.The timing of immunotherapy initiation may also influence treatment response. In early TNBC, a clinical study compared a lead-in schedule of PD-1 blockade before neoadjuvant chemotherapy with concurrent initiation of the two treatments, using pathological complete response (pCR) as the primary endpoint and evaluating safety across the two schedules [166]. Such designs may help clarify how treatment timing shapes therapeutic outcomes and early microenvironmental responses.Treatment evaluation may also benefit from endpoints beyond pCR alone. An exploratory analysis from KEYNOTE-522 used residual cancer burden (RCB) to quantify residual disease after neoadjuvant therapy and examine its association with event-free survival [171]. In the NeoPACT phase 2 trial, pretreatment samples were also classified using prespecified cutoffs for the 44-gene DNA damage immune response (DDIR) signature and the 27-gene tumor immune microenvironment (TIM) signature, allowing their associations with pathological response to be evaluated [174]. Together, pathological assessment and molecular biomarkers may provide a more refined characterization of treatment response and residual risk.Future trial design may be strengthened by integrating high-dimensional immune profiling with patient-derived model systems. Transcriptomic and immunophenotypic atlas analyses may help identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from specific treatments [182], whereas TNBC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) biobanks can support mechanistic validation and preclinical screening of candidate regimens [183]. Together, these approaches may inform more individualized treatment strategies. Future TNBC trials should further evaluate mechanism-informed stratification, longitudinal biomarker assessment, and composite endpoints that integrate pathological, molecular, and clinical outcomes [184, 185].

Conclusions and future perspectives

Advances in spatial omics, single-cell imaging, and multi-omic integration have shown that the TNBC microenvironment cannot be understood solely from the abundance of individual cell populations. The spatial arrangement of tumor, immune, and stromal cells can generate local niches with distinct effects on antitumor immunity. Reported immunosuppressive configurations include hypoxic niches containing dormant tumor cells, exhausted T cells, and tumor-supportive fibroblasts, as well as tumor regions in which CA9+ CAFs co-localize with SPP1+ macrophages. These spatial features are associated with reduced effector T-cell abundance and adverse outcomes. Future studies should determine whether selective remodeling of suppressive niches, together with preservation or restoration of immune-supportive structures such as tertiary lymphoid structures, can improve T-cell entry, intratumoral positioning and productive contact, and effector function.

A major unresolved question is how suppressive spatial niches evolve during tumor progression and in response to therapy. Myeloid-cell states, CAF–ECM remodeling, vascular dysfunction, and hypoxia-associated metabolic programs may change over time and generate distinct patterns of immune exclusion or ineffective inflammation. However, current evidence is derived largely from single time-point analyses, which cannot adequately capture the temporal evolution of these microenvironmental programs. Neoadjuvant and window-of-opportunity studies provide opportunities to collect paired pre- and post-treatment samples and to evaluate spatial changes using integrated spatial transcriptomics, whole-tissue imaging, and multi-omic profiling. Longitudinal analyses should also compare primary tumors, metastatic lesions, and pre-metastatic niches, including vascular and lymphatic alterations, to distinguish shared dependencies from site-specific programs and identify microenvironmental features with translational potential.

Combination strategies targeting myeloid suppression, adenosine signaling, hypoxia, and abnormal vasculature warrant further evaluation in TNBC. Clinical and preclinical studies of PI3K-γ inhibition with eganelisib, CD73 blockade with oleclumab, carbonic anhydrase IX inhibition with PTS, and AXT201-mediated vascular remodeling illustrate several candidate approaches, although their clinical value remains to be established in biomarker-defined populations. Future studies should integrate spatial neighborhood features and cell–cell interaction signatures into longitudinal assessments to determine whether the emergence of suppressive myeloid, stromal, or perivascular niches precedes treatment resistance. Such analyses may help refine microenvironment-directed combination strategies and improve the durability of therapeutic responses.
