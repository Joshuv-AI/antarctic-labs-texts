Reasonable layout range of lower mining roadways in the close and variable interval coal seams mining: a case study
Authors: Nan Wang, Dapeng Yang, Yuliang Wang, Yang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the context of close-distance coal seam mining, the stress transfer mechanism following the extraction of the upper coal seam is crucial for the layout of roadways in the lower coal seam. This study utilizes ground-penetrating radar (GPR) to analyze the stability and plastic zone extent of residual coal pillars after upper seam extraction. A theoretical model for the stress distribution of multiple goaves and coal pillars was established. Based on a case study with variable inter-seam spacing, the stress distribution and transfer mechanisms of the floor were analyzed. The results indicate that during the transition from the center of the coal pillar to the goaf, stress concentration initially decreases significantly and then gradually returns to the original rock stress, forming a distinct stress gradient distribution. As the depth of the floor strata increases, the weight of the overlying strata is more evidently transferred through the coal pillars to the goaf. Finally, a reasonable layout range for the lower seam mining roadways under different inter-seam spacings is proposed, providing a reference for optimizing roadway layout in similar geological conditions.Keywords: Reasonable layout range, Closely spaced coal seams, Variable interval, Multiple panels, Multiple goaf areasSubject terms: Coal, Energy science and technology

Introduction

In China, coal fields are mostly distributed through multiple coal seams1–3. Among the proven and recoverable reserves, multiple coal seams in close distance account for 43% of the total coal resources, which is the foundation of flexible supply. The mining of coal seams at close distances often adopts a downward mining method4,5. Due to the close distance among the coal seams, the mining of the upper coal seams will affect the lower coal seams, leading to a complex stress evolution in the surrounding rocks6. Moreover, the strength and stress distribution for the roof of the lower coal seam will change7–9.

In recent years, significant attention has been devoted to studying the impact of coal pillars in coal seam mining. Du Feng et al.10 examined the overburden structural characteristics and shield adaptability in stratified mining of thick coal seams, deriving a formula for calculating shield working resistance within the stress arch beneath sectional coal pillars, with a focus on cooperative extraction of these pillars and the underlying coal seam. Tan Yunliang et al.11 investigated the failure and instability mechanisms of residual coal pillars and rock strata during multi-seam mining, identifying key controls such as residual pillar spalling, safety factor, local mine stiffness, and post-peak stiffness, and defining the conditions under which progressive and dynamic failures occur. Wu Haoshuai et al.12 analyzed the stability of rib pillars in high wall mining under dynamic and static loads, establishing a load-bearing model and deriving equations for the plastic zone width and compression of rib pillars. Kang Qingtao et al.13 investigated the attenuation characteristics of concentrated stress under coal pillars in close coal seams, developing a theoretical model and simulation to determine the optimal roadway layout in the lower coal seam. Song Zhengyang et al.14–16 investigated the mechanical responses and acoustic emission behaviors of coal under compressive differential cyclic loading (DCL) using a 3D heterogeneous particle model, highlighting the impact of loading and unloading rates on stress-strain relations and damage mechanisms. Chai Jing et al.17 investigated the reasonable layout parameters of working faces based on the concept of harmonic extraction to minimize damage to coal seams awaiting upward mining, establishing a theoretical framework and numerical methods to optimize the coordinated mining of multiple coal seams. Fu Zhiyong and Zhang Wei18 conducted a study on the stress change and deformation monitoring of coal pillars between two longwall coal faces, developing a method to optimize the width of coal pillars. Chen Dingchao et al.19 investigated the progressive instability mechanisms of special-shaped coal pillars and developed stability control measures for roadways under mining influence, utilizing numerical simulations and field experiments to optimize support strategies for enhanced safety and efficiency. Gamal Rashed et al.20 conducted a comprehensive monitoring study on coal rib deformation and stress changes in a room-and-pillar retreat mine, developing insights into optimizing rib support at various depths and geological conditions to enhance safety and efficiency. Frith and Reed21 explored the limitations and potential design risks of applying empirically derived coal pillar strength equations to real-life mine stability problems, highlighting the problem-specific nature of these equations and proposing a modified design model that considers coal pillars as reinforcing a horizontally-stressed overburden. Ajit Yadav et al.22 developed a machine learning model to evaluate the stability of chain pillars in deep longwall workings in India, focusing on the influence of various geological and mining parameters to optimize pillar design. Wang Kai et al.23 investigated the influence of coal pillars on the stress and permeability of mining-disturbed coal seams for CBM drainage, developing a model to optimize coal pillar design for enhanced gas extraction efficiency and safety.

Extensive research has been conducted on the characteristics of overburden structures, the applicability of supports, and the stability of coal pillars. These studies provide valuable insights for explaining the mechanisms of coal pillar failure and optimizing roadway layouts. However, the applicability of some findings may be limited to specific geological conditions. Additionally, there is a relative paucity of research addressing the variability in the distance between coal seams in multi-seam mining. The stress distribution environment under multi-seam, multi-goaf coal pillar structures is complex, necessitating further investigation into coal pillar stability and stress transfer mechanisms. Such research is essential for providing scientific references for the layout of subsequent mining roadways.

This paper undertakes field measurements to evaluate the stability of residual coal pillars in the upper coal seam. A stress distribution model for multi-goaf and multi-pillar structures was developed through theoretical analysis and calculations. Case studies were utilized to elucidate the characteristics and transmission mechanisms of stress distribution. Subsequently, rational layout schemes for mining roadways with varying coal seam spacings were proposed. This research provides valuable insights for optimizing roadway layouts in mining operations involving closely spaced and variably distanced coal seams, thereby demonstrating significant engineering practical value.

Methods

Study area

Mining area No. 23 is bounded by the DF11 and DF3 faults in the southwest and southeast respectively, and by the mine boundary in the north. The mining area No. 23 as a whole is a triangular area with an area of about 7.88–11.9 km2, as shown in Fig. 1a. At present, the main coal seam in mining area No. 23 is coal seam No. 2, with an average thickness of 5.56 m. The immediate roof is siltstone, with an average thickness of 4.12 m. The main roof is the gritstone, with an average thickness of 7.69 m. The immediate floor is the siltstone, with a thickness range from 1 to 20 m. Immediately below the bottom siltstone is coal seam No. 3, with an average thickness of 3.48 m. The integrated rock column of mining area No. 23 is pictured in Fig. 1b. There are four panel faces between DF11 and DF1 faults in mining area No. 23, from north to south, they are No. 232,206, 232,205, 232,203 and 232,202, the length of the panel face is 300 m, and the width of the residual coal pillar between two working faces is 60 m, as pictured in Fig. 1c. Due to the proximity of coal seam No. 3 and coal seam No. 2, as well as significant variation in the thickness of the interlayer rock strata, the extraction of the upper coal seam will have an impact on the mining environment of the lower coal seam. Therefore, it is of great significance to investigate the stress distribution in the lower coal seam after the extraction of the upper coal seam and determine the optimal positioning of the panel face in the lower coal seam for safe and efficient mining.

Field measurement

Based on the mining conditions in the study area, the stability of coal seam No. 3 is primarily influenced by the overlying coal seam pillars. Therefore, early detection of the stability of coal seam No. 2 pillars is essential to analyze their impact on the stress distribution in coal seam No. 3 under varying interlayer distances.

The ZTR12 intrinsically safe ground penetrating radar (GPR) system, equipped with a 200 MHz antenna, was employed for this detection. Under the control of the monitor, the antenna emits high-energy electromagnetic pulses downward, receiving echoes from the subsurface and converting them into slowly varying electromagnetic signals. These signals are then transmitted back to the main unit for sampling, storage, and further processing. The GPR system and measurement principles are illustrated in Fig. 2a.

Field measurement plan.

The measuring line was arranged in the tailgate of panel No.232,206 near the 60 m coal pillar side. The total length of the measuring line is 40 m, with markings at 5 m intervals. The location of the measuring line and the field measurement are shown in the Fig. 2b,c.

The two-zone constraint theory was proposed by A.H. Wilson26 in 1983. Through coal pillar loading tests, it was found that the stress of the coal pillar varies during loading. From the stress peak of the coal pillar to its boundary, the stress of the coal body exceeds its yield point and flows to the goaf to a certain extent. This region is called the yield zone or plastic zone. Within the coal pillar, the region where the peak stress begins to decrease is known as the elastic zone. Results

Instability of the coal pillar

Drawing from the measured results shown in Fig. 8, three areas, A, B, and C, can be identified. Area A exhibits a clear signal reflection zone, indicating that the coal pillar in this area is affected by the advanced abutment pressure, and the stress in this area is relatively high. The range of this strong reflection zone is about 25–30 m. The signal in area B is different from that in areas A and C, indicating that the coal pillar in area B has already been affected by mining stress, which is significantly lower than the stress in area A. The coal pillar in area B exhibits an elastic recovery state under stress, while the coal pillar in area C is subjected to less stress because it has not been affected by the abutment pressure from panel face No. 232,205.

Field measurement result.

As the results indicate, the influence range within the coal pillar exhibits a generally symmetrical distribution. After the remaining panel is mined, the stability of the coal pillar will not be compromised, and an elastic zone will remain within the coal pillar. The residual coal pillar will transmit the weight of the overlying strata, significantly impacting the mining of the underlying coal seam.

Stress distribution characteristics under the structure of “goaf–coal pillar–goaf”

The stress distribution under the multi-goaf and multi-coal pillar structure exhibits complex characteristics. Figure 9 illustrates the vertical stress distribution within the floor strata beneath multiple goaves, as calculated by Eq. (14). The stress distribution reveals significant stress concentration at the base of the coal pillars, particularly beneath the central residual coal pillars, forming a “V” shape. These areas exhibit stress values significantly higher than the surrounding rock mass, forming stress concentration zones. The maximum stress beneath the central remnant coal pillars reaches approximately 50 MPa, while the stress beneath the boundary coal pillars peaks at 35 MPa. Beneath the goaves, due to coal extraction, the originally borne stress is released, forming stress relief zones where the stress values are relatively low, less than 5 MPa. Additionally, during the transition from the coal pillars to the goaves, the stress values decrease and then gradually recover, forming a distinct stress gradient. This gradient reflects the supporting role of the coal pillars on the roof strata and the compaction phenomenon of the collapsed rock blocks within the goaves.

Vertical stress distribution.

Figure 10 depicts the horizontal stress distribution in the floor strata beneath multiple goaves, calculated using Eq. (14). Similar to the vertical stress distribution, the horizontal stress is higher at the base of the coal pillars, particularly beneath the central residual coal pillars, reaching up to approximately 45 MPa. The horizontal stress beneath the boundary coal pillars peaks at 30 MPa, while it is lower beneath the goaf areas, generally ranging between 10 and 15 MPa. Additionally, during the transition from the coal pillars to the goaf areas, the horizontal stress decreases and then gradually recovers. This stress gradient helps maintain the stability of the coal pillars during load-bearing, preventing sudden stress release and failure. Unlike the vertical stress distribution, the horizontal stress beneath the central goaf areas is significantly higher than that beneath the lateral goaf areas. Furthermore, when the horizontal stress beneath the residual coal pillars drops below 25 MPa, the horizontal stress contours exhibit a double-peak trend, forming an “M” shape. This indicates that beyond a certain depth beneath the coal pillars, the horizontal stress shifts to the sides of the pillars, reducing local stress concentration.

Horizontal stress distribution.

Figure 11 presents the distribution of shear stress within the floor strata under multiple goaves, calculated according to Eq. (14). In the figure, the positive and negative values of shear stress represent the direction of the shear force, with positive indicating downward and negative indicating upward, while the numerical values denote the magnitude of the shear force. At the interface between the coal pillars and the goaves, the shear stress exhibits a pronounced concentration, forming a “00” shape distribution. The maximum shear stress on both sides of the central residual coal pillar reaches approximately 12 MPa, while the maximum shear stress on the side of the boundary coal pillar adjacent to the goaf reaches about 9 MPa. This phenomenon is attributed to the significant shear force borne by the rock mass at the interface between the coal pillars and the goaves after the formation of the goaves. In regions with high shear stress, the rock mass is more likely to enter a state of plastic deformation, forming plastic zones.

Shear stress distribution.

Stress distribution characteristics with different depth

The vertical stress distribution at different depths beneath multiple goaves and coal pillars is shown in Fig. 12. As seen in the figure, with increasing depth into the floor strata, the vertical stress beneath the coal pillars decreases, while the vertical stress beneath the sides of the pillars gradually increases. This phenomenon indicates that the vertical stress, as the coal pillars support the overlying strata, gradually transfers downward and laterally toward the goaf areas. In addition, in shallower regions (< 5 m), the vertical stress beneath the coal pillars is higher, forming a double-peak distribution, which suggests that the pillars can effectively support the overlying strata within this depth range. However, as the depth increases, the load-bearing capacity of the coal pillars weakens, and the vertical stress distribution evolves into a single-peak pattern. This indicates that at greater depths, the support function of the coal pillars diminishes, and the stress progressively shifts toward the sides of the pillars.

Vertical stress distribution with different depth.

The distribution of horizontal stress curves at different depths beneath multiple goaves and coal pillars is illustrated in Fig. 13. As the depth of the floor strata increases, the horizontal stress beneath the coal pillars exhibits a significant variation trend. Under the central residual coal pillars, the horizontal stress in the shallow region shows a “∧” shape distribution, which gradually transitions to a “M” shape with increasing depth. This phenomenon indicates that as the depth of the floor strata increases, the weight of the overlying strata extends from the center of the residual coal pillars to the sides. Due to the load of the overlying strata, the stress directly beneath the boundary coal pillars in the shallow region is relatively concentrated, while the stress on the goaf side is relatively small. However, with increasing depth, the bearing capacity of the floor strata gradually enhances, and the stress beneath the coal pillars transfers towards the goaf side, leading to an increase in horizontal stress on the goaf side. When the depth of the floor strata exceeds 10 m, the stress on the goaf side surpasses that beneath the coal pillars, indicating a change in the stress transfer path within the deep floor strata under the load of the overlying strata.

Horizontal stress distribution with different depth.

Furthermore, the distribution of shear stress curves at different depths under multiple goaves and coal pillars is illustrated in Fig. 14. As shown in the figure, the shear stress beneath the boundary coal pillars exhibits a “∧/∨” pattern. In contrast, under the central residual coal pillars, due to the combined effect of multidirectional loads, the shear stress is concentrated on both sides of the coal pillars, while the middle part shows relatively lower stress, forming an “N” shape. This indicates that the contact interface between the coal pillars and the goaf is a stress concentration zone. As the depth of the floor strata increases, the shear stress in these areas decreases, but the reduction is limited. These regions require special attention and reinforcement in support design to prevent instability or failure of the rock strata caused by shear stress concentration. Moreover, the concentration of shear stress on both sides of the coal pillars suggests that the stress transfer at the interface between the coal pillar and the floor is non-uniform, which could potentially lead to localized pillar failure or shear slippage of the floor.

Discussion

The residual coal pillars left after the extraction of the upper coal seam play a crucial role in bearing and transmitting stress. The stability of these coal pillars and the pattern of stress transmission are vital for the safety of the entire goaf. Based on the measured stability of the coal pillars and the theoretical analysis of stress transmission patterns, a reasonable layout scheme for the lower coal seam mining roadway has been proposed, considering the specific geological conditions and coal seam characteristics of the study area. Layout position of lower seam mining roadway.

In this study, through an in-depth investigation of the stress distribution patterns in the “goaf - coal pillar - goaf” structure, a more accurate understanding of the stress transmission patterns beneath the coal pillars has been achieved. This deepens the understanding of the mechanisms of coal pillar bearing and stress transmission. The findings provide a theoretical basis and scientific guidance for optimizing roadway layout schemes, effectively avoiding stress concentration areas, ensuring the stability of roadways during the lower coal seam mining process, reducing roadway maintenance costs, and achieving the goal of safe and efficient mining.

Despite the progress made in revealing the stress transmission patterns of coal pillars and optimizing roadway layout, there are still some limitations. Firstly, the study mainly relies on the measured stability of coal pillars in the upper coal seam and theoretical analysis. Since the lower coal seam has not yet been mined, there is a lack of field measurement data for the lower coal seam, which may limit the applicability and accuracy of the results. Secondly, the geological conditions and coal seam characteristics of the study area have certain peculiarities, and the applicability of the research results to other mining areas needs further verification. Future research should strengthen the collection and analysis of field measurement data to verify and improve the theoretical analysis results. Additionally, more geological and engineering parameters, such as coal seam thickness and the lithology of the roof and floor, should be considered to enhance the accuracy and applicability of the theoretical analysis model. Through interdisciplinary research, the understanding of the mechanisms of coal pillar bearing and stress transmission can be further deepened, providing more scientific and reliable guidance for coal mining under different geological conditions. These improvements aim to better ensure the safety of the goaf, reduce roadway maintenance costs, and achieve the goal of safe and efficient coal mining.

Conclusions

Through theoretical analysis, a computational model for the stress distribution in the floor of a multi-goaf and multi-coal pillar structure has been established. By incorporating specific geological parameters, this model can determine the stress distribution in the floor under the combined influence of complex environments, providing a theoretical basis for the layout of subsequent coal seam mining roadways.

By incorporating specific geological parameters, this model can determine the stress distribution in the floor under the combined influence of complex environments, providing a theoretical basis for the layout of subsequent coal seam mining roadways.

The stability of residual coal pillars was rigorously evaluated through field measurements. Post-extraction analyses of coal seam No. 2 revealed a damage range of 25 to 30 m within the residual coal pillars, with the identification of an elastic zone significantly impacting stress distribution in the lower coal seam.
