Brain network dynamics during rumination relate to relapse of depression
Authors: Su Shu, Yumeng Ju, Mi Wang, Wenwen Ou, Mohan Ma, Qianqian Zhang, Mei Huang, Hairuo He, Wentao Chen, Hui Liang, Aoqian Deng, Sirui Gao, Fanyu Meng, Kangning Li, Yilin Peng, Mei Liao, Li Zhang, Bangshan Liu, Jin Liu, Lingjiang Li, Yan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Patients with remitted depression were guided into an active rumination state using a rumination induction fMRI paradigm.•Dynamic network properties including flexibility and integration were compared between rumination and distraction states.•Reduced dynamic integration between the FPN and the DAN was observed during rumination compared to distraction.•Dynamic network properties predicted relapse of depression within 2 years but not 6 months.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar depression (BD) are debilitating mood disorders characterized by high relapse rates, with at least one-third of patients experiencing recurrence after their first remission (Hett et al., 2023, Kishi et al., 2021, Moffitt et al., 2010, Richards, 2011). The risk of relapse increases with each episode, exacerbating neurocognitive impairments and imposing profound medical and economic burdens (Semkovska et al., 2019, Van Rheenen et al., 2020). Despite the clinical urgency to prevent relapse, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying depression recurrence remain poorly understood, highlighting the necessity to explore vulnerability factors and objective neural markers.

Rumination, characterized by repetitive, negative, and self-focused thinking, is a transdiagnostic factor linked to the onset, maintenance, and recurrence of various mood disorders (Egan et al., 2025, Ehring, 2021). Notably, research has demonstrated that rumination persists in patients with MDD and BD during the remission phase, and has further established its significant association with increased relapse risk and shorter time to relapse (Kovacs et al., 2020, Michalak et al., 2011, Van der Gucht et al., 2009). Earlier studies based on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) found that an increased brain activity during rumination involved widespread brain regions, including posterior cingulate cortex, anterior medial prefrontal cortex, dorsal medial prefrontal cortex, amygdala and insula (Lemogne et al., 2009, Vanderhasselt et al., 2011). In recent years, advances in network neuroscience have deepened the understanding of rumination’s neurocognitive processes (Bassett and Sporns, 2017, Thomas et al., 2024, van der Velden et al., 2023). The integration between brain regions of different brain systems underpins higher-level cognitive functions. Abnormalities in this integration may cause disruptions in cognitive-affective processes (e.g., excessive rumination), which may persist during remission of depression (Burkhouse et al., 2017, Lois and Wessa, 2016, Petersen and Sporns, 2015, Seguin et al., 2023).

Previous fMRI studies have identified networks associated with rumination across both unipolar and bipolar depression, particularly in the default mode network (DMN), fronto-parietal network (FPN), dorsal attention network (DAN) (Apazoglou et al., 2019, Chou et al., 2024, Jia et al., 2023, Tozzi et al., 2021). The DMN, central to self-referential processing, exhibits hyperactivation and aberrant functional connectivity (FC) in both MDD and BD during rumination (Ghaznavi et al., 2023, Hamilton et al., 2015, Zhou et al., 2020). The FPN, critical for cognitive control and attentional redirection, shows disrupted FC with the DMN and DAN, reflecting a bias toward internal mentation over external engagement, suggesting overlapping network-level dysfunction in both disorders (Kaiser et al., 2015, Lois et al., 2017). However, despite the important findings, most of research on network integration were based on a static perspective. Emerging evidence reveals the dynamic characteristics of brain functional network, that is, the communication between brain regions is an inherently time-varying process (Allen et al., 2014, Hutchison et al., 2013). The dynamic nature of integration represents a fundamental property of the human brain, underpinning resting, perceptual, and cognitive processes (Saggar et al., 2022, Shine et al., 2016). Investigating the network integration from a dynamic perspective is thus crucial for advancing a more comprehensive understanding of rumination. Significant insights have already been achieved based on dynamic functional connectivity (DFC) methodologies. For instance, Kim et al. used DFC features derived from resting-state fMRI data to successfully predict trait rumination (Kim et al., 2023). Chen and Yan identified hypostability in the DMN and hyperstability in the FPN during rumination in healthy individuals (Chen and Yan, 2021). Nevertheless, although rumination is a known vulnerable factor in remitted depression, the temporal dynamics of brain networks during rumination in these patients remain unclear. Moreover, although rumination is consistently linked to relapse of depression, it remains uncertain whether network dynamics underlie the neural mechanisms of relapse.

In the present study, we utilized a multilayer community detection approach (Braun et al., 2015, Daws et al., 2022, Mucha et al., 2010) to investigate the dynamic interactions of large-scale brain networks during rumination in remitted depression. Within this framework, brain regions were modeled as nodes, and their interactions as time-varying edges, enabling the identification of densely interconnected communities whose integration dynamically fluctuates during rumination. We used the rumination induction fMRI task, with distraction as a contrast. Two hypotheses were formulated: (i) Patients would demonstrate distinct dynamic network patterns during rumination compared to the distraction state; (ii) The dynamic features during rumination would be associated with relapse of depression.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Thirty-nine patients with remitted MDD and 7 patients with remitted BD were recruited from the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. Diagnostic and inclusion criteria are detailed in the Supplementary information. Participants with excessive head motion (mean frame to frame displacement (FFD) > 0.2 mm, 4 patients) during rumination or distraction states were excluded. Eventually, 42 patients (35 MDD, 7 BD) were included in the present study. Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected via self-reported questionnaires. Depression severity was evaluated using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD17), manic symptoms with the Young Mania Rating Scale. Rumination was evaluated by the 22-item Ruminative Responses Scale (RRS), encompassing Brooding, Reflection, and Depression subscales.

After baseline assessment, patients completed 2-year follow-ups. During this period, 16 patients experienced relapse (15 MDD, 1 BD). Notably, over half of these relapses (9 cases; 8 MDD, 1 BD) occurred within the first 6 months. All participants provided their informed consents, with their anonymity being preserved. The study was approved by the medical ethics committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given that the paradigm involved recalling negative personal experiences, participants were carefully monitored throughout the study, asked to report any discomfort after scanning, and provided access to clinical support if needed. No participants reported significant distress following the study.

Data acquisition and task paradigm

MRI data were acquired on a 3 T United Imaging scanner (uMR 790, China) using a 32-channel head coil. A T1-weighted image was obtained using the magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo sequence with the following parameters: TR = 6.97 ms, TE = 3.1 ms, flip angle = 10°, FOV = 230 × 256 mm2, matrix size = 192 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm, slice number = 200. The fMRI data was obtained using a multi-band echo planar imaging sequence: TR = 700 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 52°, FOV = 210 × 210 mm2, matrix size = 84 × 84, slice thickness = 2.5 mm, slice number = 63, with a simultaneous multi-slice acceleration factor = 7. A total of 514 volumes were collected for each state, with axial slices aligned to the AC-PC line. The acquisition covered the whole brain.

The fMRI paradigm comprised six consecutive scans: resting state I, negative autobiographical state, rumination state, mindfulness state, distraction state, and resting state II. At baseline, participants generated four personalized keywords related to negative life events. Each condition (except for resting state) included four stimuli. In the negative autobiographical condition, participants vividly recalled and re-experienced these events to evoke self-focused negative memories. In the rumination condition, sentences like “Analyze your personality to understand why you felt so depressed in the events you just recalled” were presented to induce rumination. These instructions were adapted from the RRS and have been widely used and validated for effectively inducing rumination (Chen et al., 2020, Kuehner et al., 2009). In the mindfulness condition, instructions will be presented to guide participants to adopt a non-judgmental awareness of their thoughts and emotions to facilitate mindfulness. The distraction condition used prompts such as “Imagine the expression on Mona Lisa’s face” to help participants focus on objective scenes, serving as a comparison to rumination. After each condition, participants completed a questionnaire assessing their thought content and phenomenology (details of the questionnaire are provided in Table S1). Responses were rated on a Likert 4-point scale, where 1 represented “not at all” and 4 represented “all the time”. More details are provided in the Supplementary information.

MRI data analysis

Preprocessing

Preprocessing was conducted with the Freesurfer (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) and Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI, https://rfmri.org/-dpabi) software suite (Yan et al., 2016). Detailed steps are provided in the Supplementary information. Briefly, spatial preprocessing included slice-timing correction, realignment to the mean image, covariate regression (with linear trends, motion confounds, mean cerebrospinal fluid signal, the mean white matter signal, and global signals, binary regressors representing the four sets of stimuli), bandpass filtering and smoothing. After preprocessing, the mean time series from regions of interests (ROI) defined by the Brainnetome parcellation (Fan et al., 2016) were extracted. This parcellation consists of N = 246 ROIs, and can be divided into 9 large-scale networks according to Yeo et al. (Yeo et al., 2011), including the salience network (SN), DMN, FPN, DAN, ventral attention network (VAN), visual network (VIS), somatomotor network (SMN), subcortical system (SUB) and limbic system (LIM) (Table S2).

Intra-layer adjacency matrices of FC were estimated using Pearson’s correlation between each pair of brain regions, with negative correlations set to zero (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). For each subject, an N × N × T matrix was constructed, where N represents the number of brain regions and T the number of ordered layers, serving as input for subsequent multilayer community analysis.

Multilayer community detection

We applied multilayer modularity maximization using the generalized Louvain-like community detection algorithm (Mucha et al., 2010), which optimizes the modularity quality function to assign brain regions into modules across temporal layers. The formula follows Eq. (2) in Mucha et al. (2010), with detailed parameters provided in Supplementary.

Given the heuristic nature of the algorithm, each independent run produces slightly different partitions of nodes into communities. To address this variability, we repeated this procedure 100 times per subject. Node flexibility was calculated as the normalized frequency of a brain region changing its community assignment between consecutive layers, divided by the maximum possible changes (i.e., T-1) (Braun et al., 2016). For each subject, we calculated each brain region’s flexibility as the average flexibility over the 100 repetitions, and network flexibility was then defined as the average node flexibility within a given large-scale network.

Module allegiance and network integration

Multilayer community detection assigns each brain region to a module at every layer. Based on these assignments, we computed an N × N module allegiance matrix M for each subject (Bassett et al., 2015). Specifically, for each of the 100 repetitions and within each layer, we generated an N × N binary matrix where elements indicated whether two nodes were assigned to the same module. The final module allegiance matrix was obtained by averaging all these binary matrices, with each element Mij calculated as the number of layers where node i and j were assigned to the same module divided by the total possible assignments (i.e., 100 × T, where 100 is the number of repetitions).

Statistical analysis

Between state comparison

Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB R2019b (The MathWorks, USA). Paired t-tests were employed to compare head motion (mean FFD) between rumination and distraction states, and behavioral results (self-reported sadness and self-focus ratings) between rumination and distraction/resting states. Dynamic metrics between rumination and distraction states were compared using the repeated-measure general linear model (GLM), with head motion as a covariate. Results passing false discovery rates (FDR) correction (PFDR < 0.05, one-tailed) were considered significant. Nominally significant findings (Puncorrected < 0.05) with 0.05 ≤ PFDR < 0.1 were also reported as exploratory.

Correlation with rumination traits

We explored associations between metrics showing significant differences in the rumination-distraction comparison and rumination traits. Specifically, we computed the partial Spearman correlations between RRS scores (i.e., Brooding, Reflection and Depression) and significant metrics from the rumination state, controlling for age, gender, education years, and head motion.

Univariate and multivariate regression analyses

Univariate logistic regression was utilized to assess the relationship between relapse of depression and each rumination score, and was further employed to examine the associations between relapse and each dynamic metric showing significant differences between states. Moreover, a logistic elastic net regression model was employed to identify relapse-predictive features in a data-driven manner. All dynamic metrics (network flexibility, total integration, and between-network integration in both states, totaling 108 features) were included. The regularization parameter α was set as 0.5 to balance L1 / L2 penalties, and λ was optimized via leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). The area under the curve (AUC) was computed as a measure of model performance and its 95 % confidence intervals were estimated using bootstrap method with 5,000 resampling iterations. Features with non-zero coefficients were examined to evaluate their contributions to relapse prediction. These predictive analyses were conducted separately for two outcomes: overall relapse within the 2-year follow-up period and early relapse occurring specifically within the first 6 months.

Validation analysis

To ensure the robustness of our results, two additional window sizes, 42 TR and 54 TR (corresponding to T = 23 and 18 windows, respectively), were employed in the sliding time window process and all the analyses were reconducted. Moreover, to assess whether the main findings were altered by including patients diagnosed with BD, we excluded these patients and reconducted the analyses.

Correlation with RRS traits

We computed the correlations between the metrics showing significant between-states differences (here, the integration between FPN-DAN and VIS-DAN) and each RRS score, respectively. We found that weaker integration between FPN-DAN during rumination was marginally correlated with higher Brooding score (r = −0.35, Puncorrected = 0.033, PFDR = 0.100; ntests = 3). No significant correlations were observed for VIS-DAN integration metric with RRS scores.

Rumination traits and network dynamics contributed to predicting relapse

Sixteen patients (38.1 %) experienced relapse within two years. Univariate logistic regression revealed that Reflection (OR = 1.3, 95 % CI: 1.1–1.7, PFDR = 0.038; ntests = 3) and Depression (OR = 1.1, 95 % CI: 1.0–1.2, PFDR = 0.038; ntests = 3) were significantly related to relapse, with Brooding showing marginal significance (OR = 1.2, 95 % CI: 1.0–1.4, Puncorrected = 0.071, PFDR = 0.071; ntests = 3). When combined, these three scores predicted relapse with 69.0 % LOOCV accuracy, balanced accuracy of 65.4 %, and an AUC of 0.68 (95 % CI: 0.51–0.84; Fig. 3). Among these relapsed patients, 9 cases (56.3 %) recurred within six months. We found that predictive models performed poorly for early relapse: rumination traits showed no significant associations with six-month recurrence (all PFDR ≥ 0.27), the combined three-score model yielded week discrimination (AUC = 0.57, 95 % CI: 0.32–0.80), and elastic net regression also exhibited limited performance (AUC = 0.61, 95 % CI: 0.41–0.80). Validation analysis

Validation analyses using alternative window sizes (42 and 54 TRs) and samples excluding patients with BD largely replicated the main findings. The FPN total integration and FPN-DAN integration were lower during rumination compared to distraction, while VIS-DAN integration were consistently higher. The FPN-DAN integration showed significant negative correlations or non-significant trends with Brooding scores. Elastic net regression demonstrated similar two-year relapse prediction performance to the main results (detailed results are provided in the Supplementary information).

Discussion

This study elucidates the dynamic interactions of putative network modules underlying rumination in remitted depression. Through multilayer community analysis, we revealed significant differences in time-varying interactions of large-scale networks between rumination and distraction states. Results demonstrated that the FPN-DAN integration were decreased during rumination. Both the rumination traits and dynamic network metrics demonstrated moderate predictive values for long-term relapse outcomes (2-year), though not for short-term (6-month) prediction. These findings deepen our understanding of the intricate network dynamics during rumination and their relation to relapse of depression, emphasizing the need to consider rumination and associated brain networks in relapse prevention strategies.

During rumination, the FPN showed reduced integration, particularly with the DAN, compared to distraction. Moreover, FPN-DAN integration during rumination showed a negative correlation with individual rumination traits; however, this association did not survive multiple comparisons. While this preliminary finding may suggest a neurocognitive link between state rumination (i.e., task-induced) and trait rumination (measured by self-reported scales) (Wei et al., 2024, Whitmer and Gotlib, 2013), future studies with larger samples and directed hypotheses are needed to further examine this relationship. The DAN is recognized as a brain system responsible for maintaining and guiding attention (Petersen and Posner, 2012). Whereas FPN enables the redirection of attention to external tasks or stimuli when the brain shifts from internal processing, ensuring focus on the current task or action (Eysenck et al., 2023, Gratton et al., 2018). The continuous adaptation and interaction between the control network with the attention network may serve as the neural foundation underlying the processes allowing the brain to flexibly shift its focus from internal contemplation (e.g., rumination) to external tasks or stimuli. Therefore, reduced FPN-DAN integration may be implicated in sustained rumination. Interventions incorporating mindfulness or distraction may have the potential to induce changes in network interactions, enhance cognitive adaptability and mitigate rumination (Raugh and Strauss, 2024, Wolgast and Lundh, 2017).

During rumination, we observed increased VIS–DAN integration, suggesting that visual systems are stably engaged with attentional control and may serve internally generated imagery that sustains self-focused thought (Bressler et al., 2008, Vetter et al., 2014). At the individual level, higher VIS flexibility during rumination predicted greater relapse risk. This finding may imply that unstable and frequently shifting interactions of the visual system foster intrusive imagery that reinforces maladaptive rumination (Kosslyn et al., 2001). These results indicate that state-level coupling of VIS with attentional systems characterizes rumination, whereas individual differences in VIS instability within this state track vulnerability to relapse.

Although these measures did not predict 6-month relapse in our cohort, they showed moderate predictive value for 2-year relapse. This pattern accords with accounts that repetitive negative thinking accrues risk over time by prolonging stress-related physiological activation and sensitizing stress responses, thereby increasing vulnerability to recurrence (Kaiser et al., 2015, Monroe and Harkness, 2005, Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008). Among the predictive features of dynamic network metrics, SN flexibility during distraction emerged as the most robust marker of relapse risk, consistently contributing across analytic settings. Although network flexibility is often viewed as a marker of adaptive reconfiguration (Braun et al., 2015), higher SN flexibility during distraction may reflect unstable engagement of the salience system and difficulty in sustaining external attention (Safron et al., 2021). Given the SN’s critical role in orchestrating shifts between internally oriented and externally oriented processing (Seeley et al., 2007, Uddin, 2015), excessive switching of the SN during distraction could reflect a reduced ability to maintain stable allocation of attention to the external environment. This instability might, in turn, increase susceptibility to re-engage ruminative self-focus and thereby elevate relapse vulnerability (Christoff et al., 2016, DeJong et al., 2019, Koster et al., 2011). Besides, we found that greater integration between the DMN and the DAN during rumination was associated with lower relapse risk. We speculate that higher DMN–DAN integration may reflect a preserved capacity of attentional control systems to regulate self-referential processing, thereby supporting disengagement from repetitive negative thought (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2014, Dosenbach et al., 2008, Momeni et al., 2025, Whitfield-Gabrieli and Ford, 2012). A previous study has also found that most of the brain functional features associated with recurrence of depression were distributed in the DMN connected to other networks (van Kleef et al., 2023), underscoring the central role of DMN-centered integration in relapse vulnerability. Notably, while the model’s AUC (0.70) with a lower confidence bound above 0.5 indicates statistical validity, the breadth of its confidence interval (0.54–0.85) underscores its promising yet preliminary value, necessitating further validation for clinical application.

Several limitations should be noted in our study. First, the modest sample size and inclusion of both unipolar and bipolar depression may limit the generalizability of the findings. Despite the shared clinical features and overlapping network dysfunction of rumination between two depressions, the inclusion of both groups may have introduced subtle differences in brain dynamics. Further research employing larger and well-balanced cohorts is essential to more precisely investigate the disorder-specific and transdiagnostic neural signatures of rumination. Second, the data collection duration in our study is limited to 6 min for each state. Despite the relatively high temporal resolution used in the current fMRI acquisition, the number of time windows through the scan was not large. Future fMRI studies could extend data collection periods to more thoroughly capture the dynamic changes associated with the targeted phenomenon. Third, some of the significant effects reported in main findings did not survive multiple comparisons correction. We acknowledge that these marginal effects should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

The present study investigated the dynamic interactions of brain networks during rumination using a time-resolved community detection approach. Our results demonstrated the decreased dynamic integration between FPN and DAN during rumination. Both rumination traits and dynamic network metrics demonstrated moderate predictive values for two-year relapse, highlighting their potential as clinically useful biomarkers. These findings provide new insights into the persistent neural patterns that contribute to vulnerability and potential relapse during the period of depression remission.
