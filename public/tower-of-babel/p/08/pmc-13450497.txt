Unveiling the molecular basis of arsenic resistance in Dietzia maris isolated from arsenic-contaminated soil
Authors: Shahid Sher, Rosny Jean, Zaman Khan, Abdul Rehman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe highly toxic nature and uncontrolled release of arsenic from anthropogenic activities into ecosystems create a huge health concern globally. Being a class 1 carcinogen, arsenic is affecting billions of people in both developed and developing countries.ResultsDietzia maris strain sha9 was isolated from industrially contaminated soil, screened phenotypically and genetically, and investigated for its potential in arsenic resistance and bioremediation. Minimum inhibitory concentration assay showed that it can tolerate < 36 mM arsenite and < 185 mM arsenate along with other multiple heavy metals and metalloids, including lead (5 mM), chromium (15 mM), cobalt (10 mM), selenium (7 mM), cadmium 6 mM), mercury (5 mM), and nickel (5 mM). The key genes for arsenic and other heavy metals were identified through whole-genome sequencing, and these genes include aioB, arsC, Acr3, arsR, csoR, copZ, and CzcD. Brownish precipitation upon reaction with AgNO3 confirmed its oxidizing potential, and the arsenite oxidation potential assay showed 93% oxidizing capacity after 96 h. The experiment with wastewater and Milli-Q samples over time demonstrated the functional role of the strain in bioremediation, and the biomass of heat-inactivated cells also has a role in arsenic biosorption. Biochemical characterization highlighted higher intracellular glutathione levels and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities such as catalase under arsenic stress, demonstrating the triggering of oxidative-stress defense mechanisms in the bacterium. The qPCR analysis further confirmed substantial induction of the aioB gene copy number and expression in the cells exposed to arsenite (almost threefold relative to control), and it is consistent with its pivotal role in the oxidation of arsenic.ConclusionCollectively, these observations proved D. maris strain sha9 is a metabolically adaptable and genetically equipped arsenite-oxidizing bacterium with robust capability for arsenic bioremediation from contaminated sites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01171-9.

Introduction

Arsenic is a highly toxic and ubiquitous metalloid [1]. It is present as an insoluble sulfosalt (Realgar, Orpiment, Tennantite, and Lollingite) and sulfides in soil [2]. Arsenic is classified as a heavy metal due to its high atomic density and mass, and it is in Group V of the periodic Table [3]. Anthropogenic activities by humans and naturally occurring geochemical processes are major contributors to arsenic contamination in the subsurface and terrestrial environments [4]. It has been reported that most of the subsurface water worldwide is contaminated with arsenic [5].

The human body requires some metal ions as enzyme cofactors, such as iron, cobalt, nickel, and copper in trace amounts, while other metal ions, such as lead, arsenic, mercury, and chromium, are lethal for the human body even in trace amounts [6]. It was found that by the year 2016 in Myanmar, the arsenic concentration in drinking water was > 50 ppb [7]. People exposed to arsenic are at high risk for cancer [8].

Toxic compounds of organic and inorganic nature, comprising heavy metals, are present in waste that enter the environment without proper treatment. Industrial release contained high concentrations of heavy metals [9]. These metals reside in the environment for a longer half-life; the trivalent form of arsenic is a hundred times more toxic than the pentavalent form [10]. Heavy metals can enter the food chain and remain there, which makes it more lethal for humans when they ingest contaminated food [11]. Bioremediation is the effective and eco-friendly tool for removing heavy metals from contaminated sites without producing secondary compounds, which are typically generated in the chemical treatment of metals [12].

Microorganisms have developed a variety of responses to arsenic toxicity, which entails the coordinated phenotypic, biochemical, and genetic changes [13]. Bacteria are also tolerant at the phenotypic level, which is manifested in growth modulation and metal resistance functions [14]. Arsenic causes oxidative stress, resulting in the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are neutralized by antioxidant pathways such as glutathione, non-protein thiols, and enzymes like catalase and superoxide dismutase, biochemically [15]. At the molecular level, arsenic resistance is largely mediated by genes in the ars operon (e.g., arsC, arsR and efflux transporters including Acr3), which facilitate reduction and extrusion of arsenic and the aio operon (aioA/aioB), which is involved in oxidizing arsenite [16]. These combined processes not only allow bacteria to survive in arsenic-contaminated conditions, but also make them potentially useful in bioremediation.

Considering the ubiquitous, highly toxic nature of arsenic and multiple health effects on the human body, this study aimed to isolate an indigenous arsenic-resistant bacterium from industrially contaminated soil. The isolated bacteria were characterized morphologically, biochemically, and genomically for their phenotypic and taxonomic traits determinants. MIC of the bacterium was checked against arsenic and other multiple heavy metals. The effect of arsenite on the bacterium was determined, and the arsenite-oxidizing test was performed. Lab-scale experiments in media and wastewater proved their arsenic-oxidizing and biosorption efficiency. Genomic sequencing identified the aioB gene for arsenic oxidation, and qPCR demonstrated the significant induction of aioB gene in the presence of arsenite. The genomic and phenotypic traits demonstrated that the bacterium in the current study is an ideal candidate for the decontamination of arsenic and heavy metals from metal-enriched sites.

Materials and methods

Study site, sampling, and physicochemical parameters

For this study, soil samples were collected from an enriched industrialized district named Sheikhupura. Being the 16th largest city of Pakistan, it has a long history of waste release from the industrial sector into the open environment. The geographical coordinates for this district are 31° 42′ 59.9796″ north and 73° 59′ 6.0828″ east with an altitude of 679 feet. Sheikhupura is a large industrial region of Punjab, with a great variety of industries, including textile processing, chemical manufacturing, leather tanning, pharmaceuticals, food processing, pesticide production, pulp and paper mills. It is known that these industries release the untreated or partially treated effluents, which contain heavy metals and toxic compounds in the environment. Specifically, chemical industries, tanners, and production units of pesticides are known sources of arsenic pollution. Leaching of arsenic and other heavy metals into the ground water systems is caused by the release of industrial wastewater into the canals and the soil. During sampling of soil, topsoil and subsoil were collected with an already sterilized spatula (metal), mixed, manually removed large particles, and packed in a plastic bag as per standard protocol. The sealed soil bag was shipped to the laboratory for further analysis and stored at 4 °C until analysis. The physicochemical parameters were measured for the sample, which include temperature, pH, and electrical conductivity, total suspended/dissolved solids (TSS/TDS), dissolved oxygen (DO), biological oxygen demand (BOD), and chemical oxygen demand (COD). In the list of heavy metals, different heavy metals such as arsenic, nickel, chromium, copper, cadmium, and lead were determined in the sample. A multiparameter (HANNA, HI 9829) was used for physicochemical parameters. The DO, BOD, and COD were measured for the soil sample as already described [17]. A heavy metals analysis from soil sample was carried out as per the already reported method [18, 19]. An atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS) (Agilent Technologies, 200 Series, Model No. 240AA) was used for the analysis of heavy metals.

Isolation and characterization of arsenic-resistant bacteria

The soil was diluted (tenfold) in autoclaved milli Q water, and different dilutions (103 and 105) were further spread on LB-agar plates, which were already supplemented with arsenite (10 mM). The plates were incubated at 37 °C, and after 24 h, morphologically distinct colonies were selected for further confirmation on 20 mM LB-agar arsenite-containing plates, which showed that only a single colony dubbed as sha9 was used for further analysis of arsenic resistance insights after quadrant streaking to make it purified. Furthermore, to assess the optimum growth conditions (temperature and pH), the isolated bacterial strain was grown in LB broth containing 10 mM arsenite at various temperatures (20 ℃, 25 ℃, 30 ℃, 37 ℃, and 42 ℃) and pH (3, 5, 7, 9, and 11) for 24 h and absorbance was measured for each flask through spectrophotometer at 600 nm. The arsenic effect on an isolated bacterium was analyzed, and the study strain grew aerobically at 37 °C in LB broth with and without arsenic stress (10 mM). During the incubation period, absorption was measured at intervals of every 4 h at 600 nm, followed by plotting a graph of time and absorbance. The morphology (shape, size, margin, and elevation) of the colony was observed along with biochemical testing, which includes Gram-staining, catalase, oxidase, citrate utilization, carbohydrate formation, production of H2S, nitrate reduction, urease, indole, and Voges-Proskauer tests as per the method [20]. The bacterial strain sha9 was submitted to First Culture Bank of Pakistan (FCBP), in the Department of Agricultural Sciences, University of the Punjab, Lahore, Pakistan. Under accession number FCBP-B-734.

Heavy metals resistance capacity and arsenic oxidation

The bacterial MIC against arsenite and other heavy metals was determined in MS broth as described previously [21]. The flasks having MS-broth were supplemented with different concentrations of arsenite (5 to 50 mM) and arsenate (5 to 275 mM) with 5 mM increments, inoculated with bacteria, and incubated at 37 °C with continuous shaking for 48 h. Following incubation, 1 mL of an aliquot was taken and absorbance was measured at 600 nm against the blank. The bacterium was tested against other heavy metals, which include arsenate, zinc, cadmium, copper, chromium, lead, and mercury. The qualitative test for arsenic oxidation capacity of the isolated bacterial strain was checked on acetate minimal agar with silver nitrate solution (1 M) [22].

Arsenite oxidizing potential

For arsenic oxidizing potential, the bacterium was grown aerobically in 100 ml LB-broth in a flask (250 ml). For each condition, 15 flasks were used, including a control. In this experiment, the following conditions were maintained: temperatures (25, 30, 37 and 42 ℃), pH (3, 5, 7, and 9), and arsenic concentrations (250, 500, 750, 1000, and 1250 mg/L (in mM, its ranged 3.34–16.68 mM)). The initial inoculum concentration was maintained 10⁷ cells/mL, ensuring consistent starting cell density across all treatments. The duration of the experiment was kept for 96 h, and the bacterium’s arsenic oxidizing potential was checked at intervals of 24 h. Each time, 2 ml of bacterial culture was drawn, followed by centrifugation (8000 rpm for 10 min) to harvest cells, and supernatants were processed for metal estimation through ICP-MS.

Bioremediation of wastewater

The sha9 strain was further applied for the bioremediation of arsenic from wastewater in a laboratory at room temperature. A total of nine containers were taken, three for each condition: wastewater with bacterial cells, milliQ water with bacterial culture along with 250 mg/L (3.34 mM) arsenic stress, and wastewater as a control. This experiment was conducted for 8 days, and 2 ml of the sample was drawn at intervals of 2 days, centrifuged (8000 rpm for 10 min), and the supernatant was analyzed for arsenic estimation.

Bacterial biomass for arsenic biosorption

For this experiment, bacterial non-living biomass was obtained from culturing the bacteria in large volumes. The bacterial biomass was dried in an oven at 70 °C for approximately 24 h (overnight) until a constant dry weight was achieved. To ensure complete inactivation of cells, the dried biomass was tested for viability by plating on LB agar, and no colony growth was observed after incubation, confirming effective cell inactivation [23]. An arsenic solution was made (1000 mg/L) and inoculated with 1 g of bacterial cells, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 10 h in aerobic shaking conditions. The effect of different factors on arsenic biosorption was determined, which includes temperature (20, 30, 37, 45, and 55 ℃), pH (3, 5, 7, 9, and 11), and biomass dosage (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 g/L).

Glutathione, non-protein thiol contents, and antioxidant enzymes

The bacterium was exposed to arsenite (10 mM) to assess intracellular glutathione levels reduced (GSH and GSSG), and other non-protein thiols (NPSH) intracellularly (Shamim and Rehman, 2015). Four kinds of antioxidant enzymes, e.g., catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POX), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), were estimated under arsenite (10 mM) and without arsenite [25]. All the experiments were performed in triplicate.

Genome sequencing

DNA was extracted from the bacterium through the Zymo Research kit as per the manufacturer’s guidelines, followed by DNA purification through the DNA Clean and ConcentratorTM-5 kit (Zymo Research). The extracted DNA was run on a 1.0% agarose standard gel, and quality was assessed through a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer, and quantity was measured through Qubit. Libraries were prepared for Miseq through the Nextera XT DNA library prep kit as per manufacturer guidelines, followed by bead cleaning (AMPure XP beads). 250 paired-end sequencing was carried out, and raw reads were checked for quality. Assemblies were produced through Flye. The genome annotation was performed through RAST based pipelines called BV-BRC.org, and the homologs of arsenic resistance genes were found. The 16 S rRNA sequence was obtained and blasted for species identification. The taxonomic analysis of bacteria was carried out by uploading the data of the genome sequence to the Type Strain Genome server (TYGS), which is a freely available platform of bioinformatics (https://tygs.dsmz.de). The ANI of the isolated bacteria was determined by using the https://www.ezbiocloud.net/tools/ani. The draft genome sequence was deposited to GenBank with Biosample accession SAMN53331104 and BioProject ID: PRJNA1367099. The submission ID of the genome sequence is SUB15785080.

Analysis of aioB

Initially, three different independent primer sets were designed through primer3 for aioB gene amplification (Table S1) and generated amplicon sizes of 214, 242, and 79 bp. The PCR products from all three sets of primers were resolved on 1% agarose gel along with the ladder of sizes in the range 100–10,000 bp. After successful amplification of the aioB gene through conventional PCR and band confirmation on agarose gel, only the primer producing 214 bp was further utilized in qPCR for the aioB gene expression analysis.

The quantification of aioB through qPCR was observed in bacterial culture, treated and untreated with arsenite. Total RNA was extracted from three independent biological cultures per condition using the RNA isolation kit named Total RNA Mini (A & A Biotechnology, innovating life science), followed by synthesis of cDNA through reverse transcription. Total RNA was treated with DNase I (RNase-free) prior to cDNA synthesis to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a Bio-Rad real-time PCR system. The reaction included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20s. A melt curve analysis was performed at the end of the amplification to verify the specificity of the PCR products. The primers targeting the aioB gene, aioB_F2_214-5-CTTCATCGGTCTCGGCATCG-3 and aioB_R1_214-5-GAACAGCGAGTTGCGGATCA-3, were added to the reaction mixture and run with melt curve analysis to verify the specificity of the product. The cDNA/ng was measured, and a single value for the biological replicate was obtained after getting averaged from technical replicates. The Ratio of mean copy number under both conditions was used for the derivation of fold change/log2 fold change. The gyrB with stable Ct value (20.0 ± 0.2) gene was used for normalization of the expression analysis of the aioB gene. The ΔCt, ΔΔCt, and relative expression (2⁻ΔΔCt) were measured for each well.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were conducted in independent replicates, and the mean and standard error of the means (SEM) were determined. qPCR data were analyzed through an unpaired two-tailed t-test as per the means of biological replicates. The qPCR aioB gene expression and absolute abundance were plotted on a log10 scale for the visualization of transcription difference in control and treated groups. The R scripts used for statistical analyses and figure generation are publicly available in a GitHub repository (https://github.com/Shahid-sher/Dietzia-maris-SHA9-R-scripts).

Results

Physicochemical characteristics

The temperature of the soil sample was 33 °C, with a pH of 7.7 at the time of sampling. The other physicochemical parameters, which include electrical conductivity (µS/cm), turbidity (NTU), total suspended solids, total dissolved solids, dissolved oxygen, biological oxygen demand, and chemical oxygen demand (mg/L), were 8312, 12.9, 512, 3321, 0.72, 3123, and 8037. The total heavy metals concentration (mg/kg) determined through AAS was in the following concentrations: arsenic (72), cadmium (28), chromium (89), copper (95), lead (212), and nickel (24).

The wastewater sample was evaluated for color (light dark), temperature (27℃), pH (7.5), electrical conductivity (1060 µS/cm), turbidity (14.2 NTU), total dissolved solid (687 µg/ml) and arsenic concentration (0.674 µg/ml).

Morphological, biochemical characteristics, and metals MIC

The bacterium colony was in circular form with a glistering surface, color was orange with an entire margin, elevation was convex with opaque opacity, and no motility was observed for the bacterium. Gram staining showed a gram-positive result with catalase, citrate, and nitrate reduction positive test, while the rest of all biochemical tests we performed were negative, which include oxidase, Coagulase, Urease, Lactose fermentation test, H2S production test, Nitrate reduction test, Indole production test, Methyl red test, Voges-proskauer test, and Mannitol salt agar test. The isolated bacterial strain showed MIC 36 mM against arsenite. Dietzia maris strain sha9 was tested against other heavy metals to assess its cross-metal resistance, which we found to be 185 mM for arsenate, 5 mM for lead, 6 mM for cadmium, 15 mM for chromium, 5 mM for mercury, 7 mM for selenium, 10 mM for cobalt, and 5 mM for nickel. The order of cross-metal resistance is Arsenate> Arsenite> Chromium> Cobalt> Selenium> Cadmium> Lead> Mercury> Nickel.

Effect of arsenite on bacterial growth and arsenic oxidation

The effect of arsenite on the growth of the isolate was evaluated under optimum growth conditions, which include temperature (37 °C) and pH (7). The growth of the isolated bacterium was low under exposure to arsenic compared to the unexposed control. An extension in the lag phase was observed when there was arsenic in the media. It was noted that overall bacterial growth was decreased due to exposure to arsenite (Fig. 1). For the oxidation potential of arsenic, the 48-h bacterial culture on LB-agar plates supplemented with arsenite was flooded with 0.1 M AgNO3, and it showed brownish color precipitation, which is an indication of arsenic oxidation. The arsenic-oxidizing ability was observed for 4 days with an interval of 24 h. The bacterium’s arsenic oxidizing capacity was 30, 49, 79, and 93% after 24 to 96 h with an interval of 24 h (Fig. 1).

Effect of different factors on arsenite processing ability

The arsenic oxidation capacity was strongly influenced by the temperature in the isolated bacterium; across all time points, the maximum activity was observed at temperature 30 to 37 °C. The sharp peak for oxidation efficiency was obtained at pH 7, while in the range of acidic (3–5) and alkaline pH (9–11), the oxidation activity was significantly reduced. The oxidation performance progressively decreased when the initial metal concentrations increased. The lowering of oxidation performance with increasing concentration of arsenite is an indication of substrate inhibition at higher levels of arsenite (> 750 mM) (Fig. 2). Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA showed a significant difference among time points (F = 160.1, p < 0.0001), indicating a time-dependent increase in arsenite oxidation.

Arsenic removal from industrial wastewater

The isolated bacterial strain sha9 showed a steady increase in percentage of arsenic removal from both wastewater and MilliQ water for the 8 days of incubation. The removal of arsenic from MilliQ water was consistently higher, and by day 8, it was ~ 93%, while at the same time, it was ~ 71% in wastewater (Fig. 3). The arsenic removal from wastewater was slower in the beginning (~ 15% after day 2), but later on, the increase was progressive, which is an indication of bacterial detoxification in a complex matrix. The high arsenic removal in wastewater suggests that organic matter and other heavy metal ions have a role in the partial reduction of bioremediation capacity by the isolated strain sha9.

Biosorption through heat-inactivated biomass

The Bioremediation efficiency of bacterial biomass steadily increased for the incubated period, rising from ~ 30 to ~ 68% after 2 and 10 h, respectively (Fig. 4). After 6 h, the enhancement was pronounced, which is an indication of accelerated arsenic removal under the conditions tested. The arsenic removal capacity was strong, and it was time-dependent; even more than 2-fold improvement was observed compared to the initial measurement. The bacterial bioremediation efficiency varied markedly with different factors such as temperature, pH, and heat-inactivated biomass of the bacteria (Fig. 4). At 37 °C, the arsenic removal was ~ 68%, which was maximum for the incubated time. There was a huge decline observed at a temperature of 55 °C, and this time, ~ 21% bioremediation efficiency was observed. Like temperature, the bioremediation activity was high (~ 68%) at neutral pH, and this was markedly reduced in both acidic and alkaline pH ranges. The increasing concentration of bacterial biomass also increased the bioremediation efficiency, and the maximum efficiency was achieved at maximum biomass (68% at 2.5 units) (Fig. 4).

Genome analysis

The 16 S rRNA gene sequence analysis was performed to determine the taxonomic affiliation of strain sha9. The 16 S rRNA gene sequence of strain sha9 showed highest similarity Dietzia maris strain AUCM A-593 with 99.93% identity. These results confirm that strain sha9 belongs to the genus Dietzia, with the closest affiliation to Dietzia maris-related taxa. The dDDH analysis showed values of 72.8% (d₀) and 81.4% (d₄) with Dietzia maris IMV 195, exceeding the 70% species threshold, confirming that strain SHA9 belongs to D. maris. ANI was calculated (https://www.ezbiocloud.net/tools/ani), it was 97%.

The draft genome of the isolated bacterium strain sha9 is shown in a circular graphical annotated form (Fig. 5A). The circular form of the genome, from outer to inner, includes contigs, coding sequences on forward, coding sequences on reverse strands, genes for RNA, known metal resistance homolog CDS, Known virulence factors homolog CDS, GC content, and skew, respectively. A comprehensive set of metal resistance determinants was found, and these were associated with arsenic resistance dominantly along with other heavy metal genes. The isolated strain harbored aioB, which is responsible for the oxidation of arsenite. Besides arsenic oxidation genes, other determinants involved in arsenic resistance, efflux from intracellular to outside, and regulator genes named as arsC, Acr3, and ArsR were identified after nucleotide blast on NCBI. The Phylogenetic analysis of the isolated strain sha9, based on the whole genome sequence through type strain genome server (TSGS), found its closest phylogenetic relationship with Dietzia maris strain sha9 (Fig. 5B). Along with a single arsC gene encoding the canonical thioredoxin-dependent arsenate reductase. In addition to arsenic, the resistance genes complement other heavy metals, which include P-type ATPases for lead, zinc, cadmium, and mercury efflux. The transporter genes for cobalt, chromium, magnesium, nickel, and iron-uptake systems are present in the genome of the bacterium. The multiple copper homeostasis systems (CsoR, CopD, CopZ, and multicopper oxidases) are also an integral part of the genome. The major efflux component Acr3, which exports arsenite from the cytoplasm, was also identified, confirming a complete arsenic detoxification module. Additionally, nine ArsR family transcription regulators were detected; although this protein family is expanded across bacterial genomes, only a subset is expected to regulate the arsenic operon, while the remaining members control other metal-stress responses. Beyond arsenic, the genome encoded a diverse complement of heavy-metal resistance genes, including P-type ATPases for lead, cadmium, zinc, and mercury efflux; multiple copper-homeostasis systems (CsoR, CopZ, CopD, multicopper oxidases); and transporters for cobalt, nickel, chromium, magnesium, and iron uptake systems. The number of multiple metal resistance genes across the contigs of the genomes (Fig. 5C). This extensive compilation of detoxification and metal-homeostasis genes focuses on the bacterium’s robust adaptive potential and capability to bear dense metal-rich environmental settings. Contig 4 (37914.79.0004) of the genomic imaging unearthed various arsenic-resistance genes distributed around the intact contig, each bounded by a packed cluster of neighboring genes (Fig. 5D). The well-known dispersal of these arsenic genes implies that arsenic resistance elements are amalgamated throughout the contig rather than limited to a specific operonic region. The draft genome sequence was deposited to GenBank with Biosample accession SAMN53331104 and BioProject ID: PRJNA1367099. The submission ID of the genome sequence is SUB15785080.

BLASTp analysis revealed that the predicted protein shares highest similarity with cytochrome bc1 complex Rieske iron–sulfur subunits from Actinobacteria, exhibiting 100% query coverage, E-value = 0.0, and 89.17% amino acid identity, with a protein length of 358 aa.

To verify the annotation of Acr3, we performed BLASTp analysis against the NCBI protein database. The predicted Acr3 protein showed very high similarity to annotated Acr3 family arsenite efflux transporters from Dietzia maris, with 100% query coverage, E-value = 0.0, and 99.19–100% amino acid identity. The predicted protein length was 370 aa, further supporting correct annotation. These results have now been added to the revised manuscript.

The predicted ArsC protein showed similarity to arsenate–mycothiol transferases from Actinobacteria, including Micrococcus species, with 100% coverage, E-value = 2e − 67, and 61.11% identity. The protein length (~ 227 aa) is consistent with mycothiol-dependent ArsC enzymes, supporting correct annotation.

BLASTp analysis of the predicted ArsR protein showed highest similarity to ArsR/SmtB family transcriptional regulators from high G + C Gram-positive bacteria, including Dietzia species, with 100% query coverage, E-value = 4e − 73, and 96.55% amino acid identity, and a protein length of 115 amino acids.

InterProScan analysis confirmed that the predicted ArsR protein (116 aa) belongs to the HTH-type metal-responsive transcriptional regulator (ArsR/SmtB family). The presence of a conserved helix–turn–helix (HTH) DNA-binding domain supports its role as an arsenic-responsive transcriptional regulator.

InterProScan analysis identified the predicted protein (370 aa) as an Acr3 family arsenite efflux transporter, belonging to the bile acid: sodium symporter/arsenical resistance protein family. The presence of conserved membrane-associated domains supports its role in arsenite efflux and arsenic resistance.

InterProScan analysis identified the predicted protein (228 aa) as an arsenate reductase (ArsC), with conserved phosphatase-like domains characteristic of mycothiol-dependent ArsC enzymes. The domain architecture supports its role in arsenate reduction and arsenic detoxification.

InterProScan analysis identified the predicted protein (354 aa) as a Rieske iron–sulfur protein containing a conserved [2Fe–2 S] cluster domain and cytochrome bc1 complex–associated features. The domain architecture indicates that this protein belongs to the respiratory Rieske (bc1 complex) family rather than a canonical arsenite oxidase AioB.

We performed multiple sequence alignment of the predicted proteins with characterized homologs from Actinobacteria using Clustal Omega. The alignment revealed conservation of key functional features, including the catalytic cysteine residue in ArsC, the helix–turn–helix DNA-binding motif in ArsR, conserved regions in Acr3 arsenite efflux transporters, and the characteristic Cys–His residues involved in [2Fe–2 S] cluster binding in the Rieske protein. These conserved motifs support the functional annotation of the identified arsenic resistance-related proteins.

Expression analysis of the aioB gene

For amplification of the aioB gene, the primer was designed, and a product size of 214 bp was obtained (Figure S1). The gyrB gene was selected as a reference gene based on its stable Ct values across control and arsenite-treated conditions, showing minimal variation. The aioB gene transcription abundantly increased when the culture was grown in arsenite exposure. The absolute quantification confirmed that the copy number (per ng cDNA) increased from the control mean, and the induction level was ~ 2.9-fold higher in treated samples (p = 0.036). aioB expression remained significantly elevated after normalization to the stable reference gyrB gene with a mean 2⁻ΔΔCt relating to a log2 fold-change of 1.52 (~ 2.87-fold high with a value of p = 0.0066). These results mutually support the fact that arsenite effectively upregulates aioB expression at the level of the transcription process (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The development of industrialization has changed the life of humankind, but at the same time, its waste is creating health-associated problems when it comes to the open environment without significant treatment [26]. Physical and chemical methods of treatment are the options to treat industrial waste before its disposal, but these have become ineffective due to the production of secondary waste products and high costs. The economical and eco-friendly approach for cleaning of heavy metals, including arsenic from polluted sites, is bioremediation. In the bioremediation system, microorganisms, including bacteria, use different strategies, which include biotransformation, biosorption, bioaccumulation, and biomineralization [27].

In the present study, we isolated an arsenic-resistant bacterial strain, Dietzia maris strain sha9, which was isolated from industrial-contaminated soil in the industrial-rich city of Pakistan, named as Sheikhupura. The morphological and biochemical properties of the tested strain showed that it is gram-positive and belongs to the genus Dietzia. Tolerance to arsenite and arsenate of D. maris strain sha9 is similar or higher than that of a few environmental isolates. For instance, arsenite-oxidizing strains of Bacillus and Aneurinibacillus have been shown to tolerate arsenic in the range of hundreds to thousands of ppm, while Halomonas elongata exhibits tolerance up to 25 mM arsenite. Likewise, multi-metal resistance is observed with other environmental bacteria like Klebsiella pneumoniae and Micrococcus luteus, though at relatively lower concentrations of individual metals. Comparisonwise, the current strain has been shown to be highly tolerant to arsenite, arsenate as well as various heavy metals, which indicates the potential of a high adaptive fitness in polluted backgrounds [2, 10, 28, 29, 30].

The brownish precipitation after the application of AgNO3 is an indication of the presence of arsenite on culture plates, which proved its oxidizing capacity. The same sort of observation has been observed in other independent studies [21, 31]. The growth curve under arsenic stress revealed the lag phase extension, while the same observation was reported for K. pneumoniae under arsenic stress [32].

The arsenite-oxidizing efficiency was 93% after 4 days for the isolated bacterial strain. The oxidizing potential was maximum at optimum conditions under 250 mM stress, and it decreased while increasing arsenic stress in the media. The continuous increase in bioremediation efficiency suggests that the bacterial strain remained metabolically active and capable of sustained arsenic transformation throughout the experiment. The sharp improvement after the first day may reflect the induction of metal-handling pathways or increased biomass activity under optimal conditions. These results highlight the potential of this isolation for practical applications in rapid arsenic bioremediation systems.

The industrial wastewater treatment experiment showed that 71% adsorption of the isolated strain. The same sort of experiment in another study found 66% adsorption efficiency by an immobilization mixture of three different strain K1, K7, and Chlorella [33]. The heat-inactivated biomass showed 68% biosorption after 10 h. The progressive increase in arsenic removal by heat-inactivated biomass indicates that biosorption, rather than metabolic activity, was the dominant mechanism. This is probably carried out by physicochemical reactions between the arsenic species and functional groups on the bacterial cell surface such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, amino, and phosphate groups. These groups enable the binding of metals by ion exchange, surface complexation and electrostatic attraction. The fact that these functional groups remain after heat treatment justifies the high biosorption capacity of non-viable cells, and they can be used as safe and stable biosorbents to remove arsenic. In one of the studies, no biosorption was shown by the biomass of Enterococcus faecalis, Escherichia coli, and Bacillus subtilis even after three days [34]. While in other studies, two bacterial strains, biomass KG1D and PF14, showed 30 and 28 mg/g arsenic biosorption [22].

Under arsenic stress, the strain sha9 showed a significant increase in glutathiones, non-protein thiols, and catalase, an antioxidant enzyme. These heavy metals produce stress, and as a result, it leads to the production of reactive oxygen species, which are toxic for microorganisms [35]. The increased catalase activity implies increased hydrogen peroxide, one of the most important reactive oxygen species produced in response to metal stress. Conversely, the relatively lower or no change in the activity of SOD and POX can be due to sensitivity of enzymes to the long-term exposure to arsenic or a change in regulation in favor of catalase-mediated detoxification routes. Moreover, elevated intracellular thiols like GSH probably aid in the redox buffering and chelation of metals, which further raise cell protection against oxidative injuries caused by arsenic. A multiple heavy metal resistance bacterium isolated from wastewater showed 94 and 175% in in GSH, and the ratio of GSH/GSSG under 100 mg/L cadmium stress, and the same bacterium showed 41.37% increase in non-protein thiols [36]. It has been reported that ROS scavenging enzyme catalase and SOD showed higher activity in bacterial culture treated with cadmium in comparison with the control culture [37].

The presence of aioB, ArsC, and the arsenite efflux permease Acr3 indicates that this strain influences a complete and functional pathway for arsenic detoxification. In bacteria, arsenite oxidation is usually catalyzed by the arsenite oxidase enzyme complex (AioBA) which is a heterodimeric system with aioA (the catalytic large subunit) being a molybdopterin-containing catalytic small subunit and aioB (the electron transfer small subunit) being a Rieske iron-sulfur subunit. In sha9 genome, only one copy of the aioB gene was identified, suggesting a single arsenite oxidase system in this strain. Regarding its role in biosorption, aioB is not directly involved in arsenic biosorption. Instead, it contributes to arsenic transformation by oxidizing the more toxic and mobile As(III) into the less toxic As(V), which can subsequently bind more readily to cell surfaces or environmental matrices. Therefore, its role is indirect, facilitating arsenic immobilization rather than adsorption itself.

Even though aioA was not also found in our annotation results in the genome, its absence could be attributed to potential limitations in annotation, or sequence divergence; instead, functionally similar oxidoreductases might play a role in arsenite oxidation. Additionally, according to homology with previously described arsenite-oxidising bacteria, the AioBA complex is probably localised in the periplasmic space, whereby arsenite can be detoxified before it enters the cytoplasm [38, 39]. All these observations indicate that strain sha9 uses a classical but possibly flexible arsenite oxidation system, which involves electron transfer mechanisms with cellular detoxification.

A study found the aoxB gene in the genome of the bacterium, and this gene has a role in arsenic oxidation [40]. The expansion of ArsR-family regulators, together with multiple efflux transporters for copper, cadmium, zinc, lead, and mercury, indicates a broad regulatory and physiological capacity to sense and respond to diverse metal stresses. Such a robust metal-resistance repertoire likely enhances ecological fitness in contaminated or metal-rich environments. Overall, these gene clusters suggest strong adaptation to harsh environmental conditions where multi-metal exposure is common. The role of these genes has been summarized in Table 3. It has been reported that the arsenic resistance transcriptional regulator (ArsR) is responsible for both microbial arsenic identification and the requirement of intercellular inorganic arsenic [41].

The consistent increase observed in both absolute transcript abundance and normalized expression directs a strong arsenite-responsive regulation of aioB. Because gyrB Ct values were highly stable across treatment groups, the observed upregulation is attributable to true transcriptional activation rather than variation in RNA quantity or quality. The ~ 3-fold increase in aioB aligns with its functional role in arsenite oxidation and suggests that the organism swiftly increases arsenite detoxification pathways upon exposure. The understanding linking copy-number data and 2⁻ΔΔCt analysis supports the confidence in the dependability of the qPCR measurement, encouraging aioB as a key biomarker for arsenite response in this strain. Previous studies exhibited that aioB expression is tightly controlled by the alternative sigma factor RpoN, which is essential for As(III) oxidation in Agrobacterium tumefaciens [42]. In Thiomonas arsenitoxydans, the regulation of the aioBA operon is unusually complex, involving the arsenic-binding protein AioF, which directly interacts with the promoter region only when loaded with As(III) or As(V). This advanced guiding architecture, linked with convergent aioX/aioB, emphasizes that arsenic stringently regulates aioB expression, consistent with our observed induction of aioB under arsenite exposure [43].

Conclusion

This paper explains that strain sha9 of Dietzia maris has a strong physiological, biochemical, and genetic profile that allows efficient tolerance and detoxification to arsenite. Its robustness to metal stress is demonstrated by its high metal tolerance, its efficiency in removing arsenite in water and wastewater, and its increased antioxidant responses. The drastic increase in aioB helps to activate the arsenite oxidation pathway and indicates the presence of an active arsenic-resistance system. Altogether, strain sha9 is a promising bioremediation agent of arsenic and has a high possibility of being used in environment and mechanism in the future.
