GIMIC: smoothed graph-image representation of microbiome samples induce an optimal distance
Authors: Oshrit Shtossel, Yoram Louzoun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An essential tool in microbiome is the distance between samples. This serves to detect groups of similar samples. We have recently shown that combining the cladogram with the abundance of each microbe into a sorted normalized abundance tree can improve the accuracy of machine learning and differential analysis. We show that smoothing these tree-based images to produce GIMIC (Smoothed Graph IMages of the MICrobiome) leads to an interpretable visualization of microbiome sets and that the difference between the smoothed tree-based images is a better metric than the current state-of-the-art in a wide array of tasks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03822-0.

Background

Microbial compositions are associated with multiple aspects of their host’s health and environment [1]. These compositions are often characterized using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which generates amplicon sequence variant (ASV see Table S1 for acronyms) counts for each sample. Each ASV can be associated with a taxonomy.

In practice, microbiome analysis is often conducted either at the single taxon or the sample level. At the single taxon level, specific taxa can be associated with the host condition [2–8]. At the sample level, two main approaches are often used: applying machine learning (ML) methods to each sample to predict a condition or defining a distance between samples and testing whether nearby samples (low-distance) have similar conditions [4, 6, 7, 9]. While informative at the sample level, these methods are not easily interpretable and are often centered on the most abundant taxa. As such, a method that can on the one hand produce a good metric (as further defined) and on the other hand can lead to some interpretation would improve our understanding of the relation between the microbiome and the host condition.

A good representation of microbiome samples would address the microbial sequencing characteristics. Those include its sparsity, with only a few species or genera being shared between samples [10, 11] (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A), the wide-tailed distribution of ASV ranging over multiple scales [12] (Additional file 1: Fig. S1 B) and the varying taxonomic resolution of ASV. An additional limitation of microbiome analysis methods is the difference often observed between different experiments and the need for meta-analyses of multiple cohorts with the same phenotype [13–15]. Single analyses applied to separate cohorts often result in a minimal overlap in results, with even the initial overlap between microbes observed in different experiments being very low without considering significance (22.5% average pair overlap over 27 cohorts). This issue also hampers whole-microbiome analyses, as taxonomies in different cohorts do not match, limiting the possibility of combining full-sample methods.

To address that, we propose to combine the logic of our previously developed iMic ML framework and miMic differential expression analysis to produce a GIMIC (Smoothed Graph IMages of the MICrobiome)—a novel representation of full microbiome samples as tree-based images. We show that GIMIC can be used to compare the microbiome composition between experiments and that the difference between such images is a better metric than the current State Of The Art (SOTA).

Before digging into the details of GIMIC, let us focus on existing distance metrics for the microbiome (often denoted beta diversity or dissimilarity of two communities; here two microbial populations). Multiple diversity measures were proposed, each addressing different challenges of microbiome sequencing data (see Table 1 and Table S2 for a summary). We here shortly describe the most common ones (see Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Both indices produce quantitative and qualitative similarity/distance measures, respectively, based on assumptions that may not hold for microbiome data. This leads to artifacts in subsequent analysis steps such as dimensionality reduction and classification [18]. These assumptions include among others, treating all organisms as equally related while ignoring the phylogeny, assuming non-compositional data, assuming dense data while ignoring the sparsity and the different scales, assuming rarefaction or another method to adjust for sampling variation, and treating samples as independent.

A crucial step toward the solution to these limitations was the UniFrac distance metric (weighted and unweighted). Both weighted and unweighted UniFrac overcome the naive approach of giving equal weights to all the taxa in the distance by incorporating the phylogenetic information [19, 20]. However, the unweighted UniFrac ignores all the compositional data by using only the phylogenetic structure. The weighted UniFrac necessitates rarefaction, presupposes data density (it ignores sparsity and different scale values), and neglects the compositional aspect of the data. Both methods are quite computationally expensive.

An alternative approach that solves other limitations is the Aitchison distance [21]. The Aitchison distance utilizes the centered log-ratio transformation (CLR) [22] to account for the compositional nature of the data and its different scales. However, it still ignores the internal microbial relations, assuming the data is dense, requiring the imputation of missing values.

A recent attempt to combine the advantages of Aitchison and UniFrac is PhyloRPCA [23]. PhyloRPCA accounts for the evolutionary relationships among the microbes present within a sample (using the phylogenetic structure) and the missing data problem using the RCLR (robust centered log ratio). PhyloRPCA also copes with the sparsity and compositional nature of the data. However, even PhyloRPCA does not handle well the low overlap between species among different samples.

To address the challenges in microbiome analysis mentioned above, GIMIC transforms the logged ASV counts into tree-based images using the iMic (image microbiome) method, which maps the ASV counts to an image according to their phylogenetic structure [9], and based on the abundance similarity between taxa. To further refine the images, GIMIC applies a low-pass filter based on a fast Fourier transform (FFT). These images are the representation of a sample or a subset of samples in a cohort when averaged over samples.

To translate GIMIC to a distance, we use two popular image metrics between pairs of GIMIC images: the mean squared error (MSE) and the Spectral Angle Mapper (SAM), on the microbial images. For further explanation regarding the technical terms, see the Additional file 1: ML Nomenclature section.

GIMIC is designed to address the challenges above, including different scales (by using the logged values, Additional file 1: Fig. S1B), sparsity (by the log normalization and translating the data to image and the smoothing), and inner microbial interactions (by using the cladogram structure for the translation to tree-based images). In addition, we show that GIMIC is fully unsupervised, but it still produces a metric that is more associated with the host phenotype and less sensitive to the fine details of specific species/strains (those may be important at the single taxon analysis, but may hide some larger patterns). Results

Smoothed Graph IMages of the MICrobiome (GIMIC)

We propose GIMIC (Smoothed Graph IMages of the MICrobiome)—a robust, smooth representation designed to handle the inherent variability in microbiome data without being overly sensitive to specific microbes. GIMIC integrates taxonomy and taxa log frequency into a combined representation. This representation is used for a comprehensive meta-analysis tool (Fig. 1 right) and a metric (Fig. 1 left), facilitating the discovery of consistent patterns across multiple cohorts with the same phenotype. GIMIC represents microbiome samples as smoothed images created through a series of stages. Initially, microbial data are processed by MIPMLP [24] to combine ASV belonging to the same taxa and log normalizing them (see the “Methods” section). The frequencies of each taxon are propagated through their hierarchy (cladogram) via iMic [9], where the cladogram is transformed to images. These images are subsequently smoothed using an FFT-based low-pass filter, producing refined images that are not sensitive to specific microbe frequencies (for a detailed methodology, see Fig. 1, left gray frame, and methods). The logic of GIMIC stages

The first step includes log-normalization. This prevents highly abundant taxa from dominating the analysis, ensuring that rare taxa also contribute meaningfully to distance calculations. It also ensures that the distance is based on the ratio of frequencies in different samples (difference in log), and not on the absolute frequency. This approach is grounded in the understanding that low-abundance taxa can have significant biological effects on the phenotype [25–28].

The next step is the propagation of log frequencies through a cladogram, where internal nodes are the average of their direct descendants. This preserves the log-normalization effect, ensuring that distances are not dictated solely by highly abundant microbes. Additionally, this process stabilizes the representation of sparse data by smoothing across sister taxa—taxa that share similar taxonomy and abundance levels. This smoothing minimizes sensitivity to the absence of any specific taxon, reducing noise while maintaining biological relevance. Unlike traditional phylogenetic-based distances such as UniFrac, GIMIC does not rely on direct phylogenetic distance calculations. Instead, it captures taxonomic similarities and structural patterns within the microbiome, emphasizing consistent differences across taxonomic levels. Finally to further refine the resulting image, a low-pass filter is applied via FFT. This filtering process preserves large-scale variance at higher taxonomic levels while reducing fine-grained noise. In biological terms, this smoothing removes excess variance within each taxonomic level, particularly among sister taxa.

To quantify the effect of smoothing, we measured variance at each taxonomic level before and after smoothing. As expected, smoothing significantly reduced variance (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B–D), yet diversity measures before and after smoothing remained strongly correlated (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A–C), confirming that the overall diversity structure was preserved.

To further assess the biological impact of smoothing, we examined its role in emphasizing taxonomic similarity. Specifically, we compared pairwise distances between sister taxa (taxa sharing a common ancestor) to the distances between randomly selected taxa within the same taxonomic level. Before and after smoothing, we computed the average pairwise distance between sister taxa and compared it to the overall average distance at that level. Our results show that the ratio of normalized distances in smoothed images was significantly lower than in non-smoothed images (Fig. 3A and B).

Cross-cohort visualization using GIMIC

The GIMIC images can be utilized as an informative and interpretable meta-analysis tool. For such a comparison, we add a statistical test layer to GIMIC. We use the miMic framework [2]—a differential abundance analysis (DAA) tool that applies statistical tests to the same microbial hierarchical trees (see Fig. 1, right).

An important issue in understanding the association between microbes and phenotypic conditions is the appropriate taxonomy level to understand such associations. For that, we detect patterns consistent among cohorts at different taxonomic levels. First, averaged smoothed images for all positive and negative samples are produced for each cohort (Fig. 2A, images next to the blue (positives) and red (negatives) groups of people, respectively). The difference between these two averages is computed for each cohort. These difference maps cannot be compared across cohorts since the typical paired overlap between taxa at species and genus levels in different cohorts is low (typically less than 22.5% for species and 30% for genera between any pair of cohorts). The species or genera overlaps may decrease even more as we increase the number of cohorts tested per intersection. To enable such a comparison, we leverage the high class taxonomic level overlap between cohorts (56.65% average overlap). We produce class-averaged difference images (computed by averaging all the class members’ values) for each cohort (Fig. 2A, second lowest difference image). To reduce the noise level, only significant differences (translated to their z-scores with p-value < 0.05) are presented (Fig. 2A, lowest difference images). Finally, the significant differences across all cohorts for a phenotype are summed to create a comprehensive visualization (Fig. 2B).Fig. 2Cross-cohort visualization using GIMIC. GIMIC as a metric

The GIMIC representation is naturally amenable to produce a metric. Specifically, any distance metric can be applied to the GIMIC smoothed graph images. Here, we utilized mean squared error (MSE) and Spectral Angle Mapper (SAM) distances (see the “Methods” section and Fig. 1 left distance metric). However, other distances can be used at will.

To show that the GIMIC representation produces a metric that is more appropriate than current metrics for microbiome analysis, we used a wide variety of tasks, such as KNN-based prediction, unsupervised classification performance (clustering), visualization based on dimension reduction, robustness, and association with the host biology (Fig. 1 left optional usages) to compare GIMIC (pink) to current metrics. These metrics included Aitchison (gold), Bray-Curtis (orange), Jaccard, Unweighted UniFrac (further referred to as 1D-structure, in light blue), Weighted UniFrac (blue), PhyloRPCA (royal blue), image-based metric, such as MSE or RMSE (root mean squared error) on the raw iMic images with no external smoothing (further referred to as 2D-log(composition), in light purple), and SAM on the raw iMic images with no external smoothing (referred to as 2D-SAM in purple).

Bray-Curtis is the most popular metric, as measured by the fraction of citations reported via Google Scholar scanning more than 20,000 citations, has 64.3% of the citations. This is followed by 1D-structure methods such as Jaccard and UniFrac, which together account for more than 25% of citations (Additional file 1: Fig. S4).

The tests were performed over 40 cohorts based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing and 8 cohorts based on WGS (Additional file 1: Table S3).

To determine the accuracy of sample classification based on the different metrics, each metric was used to calculate distances between samples in the dataset. To avoid results that are sensitive to the machine learning process, we used a simple KNN (K-nearest neighbors with 5 neighbors) classifier trained on 80% of the cohort to predict the labels of the 20% test samples. The labels were all the external conditions available for each sample (e.g., IBD positive or negative). The accuracy was measured by the average test AUC (area under the ROC curve) over the tests of a 10-fold cross-validation of the KNN. GIMIC’s AUC was significantly higher (p-value < 0.05) than all the other metrics’ AUCs in most of the cohorts (34/40 in the 16S rRNA gene-based and 5/8 in the WGS-based, Fig. 3C–D and Additional file 1: Figs. S5 and S6) and is most likely to be ranked in the first 2 places. GIMIC’s average AUC (AUC = 0.736±0.15 for 16S, and AUC = 0.713±0.11 for WGS) was higher by 0.1595 (Fig. 3C-D) than the best other metrics’ (Aitchison distance, Bray-Curtis, 1D-structure (Unweighted UniFrac and Jaccard), Weighted UniFrac, PhyloRPCA) AUCs over the 16S rRNA gene-based cohorts (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A–R and ZA), and by 0.105 over the WGS-based cohorts (Additional file 1: Fig. S5S–Z). GIMIC: enhance cluster separation, reliability and visualization performances

Another important application of distance metrics is clustering. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of distance-based clustering algorithms using GIMIC and the SOTA metrics above, we applied the Louvain clustering algorithm [30] via scikit-learn [31] (similar results were obtained with other distance-based clustering algorithms, such as spectral clustering, data not shown) based on the computed distances with each measure. The Silhouette score [32] was used to assess the quality and consistency of the clustering results. The Silhouette score is a widely used measure that evaluates the appropriateness of sample assignments to their respective clusters.

As was the case for the other measures above, GIMIC had a significantly higher Silhouette score compared with all SOTA metrics with an average of 0.743 ±0.23 for 16S and an average of 0.413±0.09 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p\text {-value}\ < 0.001$$\end{document}p-value<0.001) for all 16S rRNA gene-based comparisons and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p\text {-value}\ < 0.05$$\end{document}p-value<0.05) for all WGS-based comparisons, Fig. 3G–H), with an average gain of 0.31 in the 16S rRNA gene-based cohort and 0.241 in the WGS based cohort (Fig. 4G, H, K, L, and Additional file 1: Fig. S16 and S17). These substantial improvements highlight GIMIC’s efficacy in capturing meaningful patterns within the microbial data.

We next tested the visualization quality of the GIMIC metric, based on dimension reduction. We utilized UMAP to project distance metrics into a two-dimensional space. Although there is no absolute measure for the visualization quality, GIMIC often produced visualizations that exhibited clearer boundaries between different phenotypic groups, highlighting again its ability to capture meaningful variations in the microbiome composition associated with specific phenotypic outcomes.

Optimal smoothing level

As mentioned in the Background, GIMIC incorporates two levels of smoothing: image smoothing and a low-pass filter. The first step is the image smoothing step. It involves populating the cladogram of means, where the leaves retain their original values and their ancestors are assigned the average of their descendants, thus smoothing over coarser levels. The second step is a low-pass filter. It is applied through the removal of high frequencies following an FFT. We tested the effect of the frequency threshold on the classification accuracy, by comparing the KNN test set accuracy with different filter thresholds. A threshold of 1 is no smoothing. The optimal threshold is task-dependent, and different tasks may require different cutoff values. Strong association between the GIMIC metric and biological correlates

We further tested the direct association between GIMIC and the biological features of each sample. To illustrate this, we computed GIMIC and other metrics between every pair of samples in the GGMP (Guangdong Gut Microbiome Project) cohort (N = 7008) and tested if this distance could be predicted using the difference between the biological features. For categorical variables, a one-hot (a process of transforming categorical variables into a vector per sample with the number of categories, with 1 if the sample is from the category, and 0 otherwise) definition was used. For instance, we predicted the microbial distance between two samples based on their BMI, metabolite, and heart disease distances. The distance was predicted using an XGBOOST (R2 = 0.4, SCC = 0.6, Additional file 1: Fig. S23).

We analyzed the feature importance (FI) of various biological outcomes. Remarkably, GIMIC consistently exhibited significant (ANOVA followed by paired t-test) higher FI values with a p-value of 0.005 compared to the other methods (Fig. 5A, indicated by the size of circles). Furthermore, the GIMIC distances had a positive correlation with the distance between the biological features consistently over all features. This is in contrast with all other methods (Fig. 5A, indicated by the darker color of circles in comparison to other methods).

Simulations to test GIMIC robustness

To comprehensively evaluate the robustness of GIMIC, we conducted a series of simulations addressing different sources of variability. First, to assess the impact of missing rare species, we randomly removed species-level information in increasing proportions (0%, 20%, 50%, and 80%) within each cohort, while retaining the genus-level associations (see the “Methods” section). We then computed distances using each metric and measured the AUC of a KNN classifier. GIMIC consistently exhibited the highest robustness among all methods tested (Additional file 1: Fig. S24). For comparison, we also tested a taxonomy restricted to the genus level without species information; this approach yielded substantially lower KNN accuracy than the original species-based classification (Additional file 1: Fig. S25).

Next, to evaluate sensitivity to sequencing depth, we systematically reduced read counts by randomly removing reads at increasing proportions (0%, 20%, 50%, and 90%) in each raw dataset. For each downsampled dataset, we applied GIMIC and other SOTA methods using their respective pipelines, and we assessed robustness by averaging the test AUC scores over 10 runs of a KNN classifier (Additional file 1: Fig. S26). In an additional test, we used one IBD dataset (N = 257, IBD vs. healthy ground truth) to explore performance under both a homogeneous setting (where all samples were downsampled) and a heterogeneous setting where only 50% of reads were downsampled.

Across all these simulations, GIMIC maintained superior predictive performance, even under extreme rarefaction (Additional file 1: Fig. S27), demonstrating its resilience to missing species-level detail and variations in sequencing depth.

Discussion

Distance metrics for ASV face several challenges, including inner taxonomic relationships, sparsity, varying scales of abundances, and variations in species presence across samples. Moreover, such distances are often hard to interpret beyond the mere question of similarity between samples. We have here proposed a new microbiome sample representation as smoothed images of cladograms populated with the log ASV frequencies, denoted GIMIC.

This image-based representation is easily visualizable, at the single sample level, as well as the full cohort. We have shown that such visualization can highlight taxa associated with a host condition across cohorts.

We have further shown that the distance between two GIMIC visualizations with various distance metrics, such as SAM and MSE outperforms current SOTA metrics in diverse applications, including KNN-based class prediction, phenotype-based distances, clustering, dimension reduction, and visualization.

A key concept in GIMIC is that the relative frequency of the same microbe between samples (performed through the difference in log concentrations) is more important than its absolute or relative frequency. The second key concept is that similar taxa behave similarly. As such, if a taxon is missing from a sample, taxonomically similar (sister) taxa can be used. For that, two steps of smoothing are applied: smoothing during image generation, and the low pass filter. This smoothing approach achieves a balance between variance reduction and structural consistency: it reduces variance at each taxonomic level while preserving the overall diversity structure. Moreover, it sharpens biologically meaningful differences, particularly by emphasizing distinctions between sister taxa and non-sister taxa, ensuring a robust and biologically coherent representation. A more minor and non-intuitive concept is that when treating a broad taxon (e.g., a genus or a family), GIMIC averages the log of its descendants, rather than simply summing the total number of reads associated with this taxon. In other words, GIMIC propagates information up the cladogram and not counts.

While GIMIC handles many limitations of existing methods, it still has important limitations and arbitrary decisions. For example, we proposed a standard frequency cutoff of 0.8, which performed best in most cases and was used in our comparisons across 48 different human datasets, including 16S and WGS gut and vaginal data. However, optimizing this threshold for each specific task or study may further improve results. Similarly, following filtering, there was no clear preference for MSE or SAM. We cannot give a definite choice of metric between the two.

GIMIC is conveniently accessible in GitHub, available online at https://github.com/oshritshtossel/SAMBA and as a PyPi package at https://pypi.org/project/samba-metric/.

At a broader level, the comparison between microbiome samples is typically done either at the single taxon level or as a distance between samples. The latter approach is typically done by comparing the distances of taxa between different samples with or without their frequency being taken into consideration. The results here suggest that it is better to compare smoothed descriptions of the log samples than the actual frequencies.

This can be the result of the large number of species/strains that may hide the differences at a broader level, the limitations of the sequencing—the same microbe is associated with different yet genetically similar taxa, or can be biological—in different hosts, similar, yet different taxa occupy the same ecological niche. Note as a side note that during the development of GIMIC, we tested whether any specific taxonomic level is more important than the others (based on the KNN accuracy) but found no consistent signal. As such GIMIC combines all taxonomic levels equally.

Conclusions

GIMIC provides a novel and effective approach for representing and comparing microbiome samples, as well as cohorts by transforming ASV data into smoothed tree-based images. GIMIC highlights consistent patterns across different cohorts.

More importantly, the difference between two GIMIC images is a novel metric that outperforms existing metrics in a wide array of tasks such as dimension reduction, visualization, prediction, and unsupervised classifications over metagenomic as well as 16S datasets, both within and between cohorts.

Overall, GIMIC offers an essential tool for microbiome research, enhancing the ability to analyze and interpret complex microbial communities. Its availability on GitHub https://github.com/oshritshtossel/SAMBA [33] and as a PyPi package https://pypi.org/project/samba-metric/ makes it accessible for researchers, facilitating its adoption and application in various microbiome studies.

Methods

Data

The metrics were tested both on 16S rRNA gene sequence datasets (40 cohorts and Whole Genome Shotgun sequencing (WGS) datasets (8 cohorts). For more information about the datasets, see Additional file 1: Table S3 [1, 4, 34–48].

Preprocessing

We preprocessed the 16S rRNA gene sequences (some downloaded using YAMAS https://github.com/YarinBekor/YaMAS [49]) and the WGS of each dataset using the MIPMLP pipeline [24]. The preprocessing of MIPMLP contains 4 stages: merging similar features based on the taxonomy, scaling the distribution, standardization to z-scores, and dimension reduction. For the image metric analysis, we merged the features at the species taxonomy by Sub-PCA. We performed log normalization on the patients. For all other metric analyses, we merged the features at the species taxonomy level by summing them, followed by the standard preprocessing stages reported for each method.

Sub-PCA merging in MIPMLP. A taxonomic level (e.g., species) is set. All the ASVs that are consistent with this taxonomy are grouped. A PCA (Principal component analysis) is performed on this group. The components which explain more than half of the variance are added to the new input table. This was applied to the data analyzed by the image-based metrics.

Log normalization in MIPMLP. Image-based metrics

Generating tree-based images

Microbial ASV vectors, preprocessed by MIPMLP, are transformed into 2D images using the iMic algorithm [9]. In this approach, observed taxa are positioned at the leaves of a cladogram based on their observed taxonomic level, with their values set to the corresponding preprocessed frequencies. Deeper levels below the leaves are assigned zeros. Each internal node is computed as the average of its direct descendants. If taxa exist both at a given taxonomy level and at its descendant levels, we first compute the contribution of the direct descendants and then average the combined descendants and the current level. The cladogram is then mapped onto a 2D matrix, where each row represents a taxonomic level. Within each row, taxa are ordered using recursive hierarchical clustering of sample values, ensuring that sister taxa are placed in adjacent columns and grouped according to their similarity while preserving the tree’s structure.

Distance metrics

We used 2 SOTA image metrics: (1) MSE (mean square error) and (2) SAM (Spectral Angle Mapper) [52] which determines the spectral similarity between given reference spectra (r) and the spectra found at each pixel (t). The result of the comparison is reported as the angular difference (in radians) between the two spectra according to the equation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} cos^{-1}\left( \frac{t \cdot r}{||t||\cdot ||r||}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}cos-1t·r||t||·||r||

Spectral filter

We smoothed the images using a FFT algorithm that transforms the image from the space into the frequency domain. The FFT was performed on each row (taxonomy level) by itself. Then a cutoff was used and all frequencies above the cutoff were removed. Then an inverse FFT was performed to translate back the results to the space domain. The cutoff beyond which the FFT coefficients were truncated or filtered out was a hyperparameter. This filter results in a smoother image with less detail.

Applying MIDASim simulation

We conducted additional artificial simulations using the MIDASim package in R [29]. We started with control samples from a reference IBD dataset (N = 120) and created a duplicate control group. In one of the duplicates, we randomly selected 20 taxa—10 of which were multiplied by a constant and the other 10 divided by the same constant. We repeated this process using scaling factors of 5, 10, and 100. The modified dataset was then processed through MIDASim [29] to generate artificial microbial data for simulation. We applied all tested methods to these artificial datasets and evaluated their performance through KNN accuracy.

Generating the meta-analysis image

To perform the meta-analysis, we first translated all cohorts with the same phenotype into separate 2D images. For each cohort, we averaged the samples that were positive for the phenotype and, in parallel, averaged the samples that were negative for the phenotype. We then computed the difference between the positive and negative averages.

To enhance the overlap between cohorts, we averaged the values in the images across all members of each class at each taxonomic level. This resulted in an image with dimensions corresponding to the number of taxonomy levels multiplied by the number of classes. Any missing classes from the union of all cohorts were padded with zeros.

Each pixel in these images was then normalized using z-score normalization, and only significant pixels (with a p-value < 0.05) were colored, while non-significant differences were set to zero.

Each cohort thus generated an image representing these differences. We then summed all these cohort-specific images over the different cohorts. The color of each circle in the final image reflects the summed value, while the size of each circle indicates the number of cohorts in which the difference was consistent.

Statistics and validation

Visualization

One of the main roles of metrics is a dimensional reduction to a low dimension of 2 or 3 to enable visualization. Each dataset was represented in a dimension of 2 by the UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction) approach [53] according to the SOTA metrics in microbiome, such as Bray-Curtis, Aitchison, Jaccard, Unweighted UniFrac, Weighted UniFrac, PhyloRPCA, and 2D-image-based metrics. Then the data was visualized such that the controls were in red and the conditions in blue.

Clustering

Louvain clustering was applied according to the distance matrices of the different metrics. Then the clustering performance of each metric was evaluated according to its average Silhouette score [32]. The higher the average Silhouette score the better the clustering. The average Silhouette score was calculated by scikit-learn in Python [31]. Another way to compare the classification quality according to the different metrics was by calculating the average out/in distance. The “out distance” was defined as the average distance between samples within clusters. The “in distance” was defined as the average distance between all the samples that belonged to the same cluster (same label). The higher the ratio between the out/in distance the more separated the clusters were according to the metric tested.
