ACOXL-AS1’s pan-cancer dynamics and its proliferative impact on endometrial endometrioid carcinoma
Authors: Hongrong Wu, Ruilin Lin, Liangli Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are critically involved in carcinogenesis; however, the pan-cancer significance and functional mechanisms of ACOXL-AS1 remain largely uncharacterized. This study comprehensively investigates the expression landscape, prognostic value, and biological impact of ACOXL-AS1 across multiple malignancies, with a specific focus on its pro-proliferative role in endometrial endometrioid carcinoma (EEC).MethodsWe analyzed ACOXL-AS1 expression and its association with clinical outcomes using pan-cancer RNA-seq data from TCGA and GTEx databases. Functional validation was performed in EEC cell lines (HHUA, HEC-1 A) via lentivirus-mediated overexpression, followed by CCK-8, colony formation, and transwell migration/invasion assays. Mechanistic insights were investigated using integrated bioinformatics approaches.ResultsACOXL-AS1 was significantly downregulated in most cancers, and higher expression correlated with improved prognosis. Elevated ACOXL-AS1 levels were associated with smaller tumor size, lower disease stage, reduced metastasis, and decreased tumor mutational burden (TMB)/microsatellite instability (MSI). It also modulated the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Critically, ACOXL-AS1 overexpression significantly inhibited EEC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor growth in xenograft models. Mechanistically, it may regulate RPA4 expression through the homologous recombination pathway in EEC cells.ConclusionsACOXL-AS1 may play a role as a tumor suppressor in endometrial endometrioid carcinoma (EEC), suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and a candidate for further exploration as a therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15005-y.Keywords: ACOXL-AS1, Endometrial carcinoma, Cancer progression, Immune evasion

Introduction

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as pivotal regulators in tumorigenesis due to their diverse roles in transcriptional regulation, chromatin remodeling, and post-transcriptional modification [1]. Increasing evidence links lncRNAs to fundamental cancer hallmarks, including uncontrolled proliferation, resistance to cell death, and enhanced migration and invasion [2–5]. While significant progress has been made in elucidating the functions of individual lncRNAs within specific cancer types, a comprehensive understanding of their roles across multiple tumor lineages remains limited.

In recent years, pan-cancer analysis has become an essential strategy in oncology research, offering a global view of gene expression, mutation profiles, and pathway alterations across various cancer types [6–8]. This approach facilitates the discovery of novel biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets that may exhibit conserved or tissue-specific patterns of activity. However, key challenges persist, including the integration of heterogeneous datasets, normalization across platforms, and the interpretation of context-dependent gene functions. Identifying lncRNAs with consistent oncogenic or tumor-suppressive roles across different cancers remains particularly difficult due to their complex regulation and often tissue-specific expression.

Robust bioinformatic tools and large-scale datasets, such as those provided by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and GTEx, now enable pan-cancer expression profiling and survival analyses with unprecedented precision [9]. These computational approaches play a critical role in uncovering meaningful correlations between gene expression and clinical outcomes, laying the groundwork for downstream mechanistic investigations.

ACOXL-AS1 (ACOXL antisense RNA 1) is an RNA gene associated with the class of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs). Although the specific functions of ACOXL-AS1 might not be fully elucidated, our previous research has preliminarily suggested that ACOXL-AS1 may play a role in the progression of endometrial cancer [10–16]. But its precise biological functions and mechanistic pathways remain unclear. Given its potential role in EEC, a better understanding of ACOXL-AS1’s pan-cancer dynamics could uncover novel insights into its function and prognostic relevance.

To this end, we performed an integrative analysis combining pan-cancer bioinformatics and experimental validation, aiming to elucidate the expression patterns, prognostic implications, and functional roles of ACOXL-AS1, with a particular focus on its proliferative impact in EEC.

Methods and materials

Data acquisition and pan-cancer expression analysis

RNA-sequencing data and corresponding clinical annotations were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases via the UCSC Xena platform (https://xenabrowser.net/) [17]. Expression profiles of ACOXL-AS1 across 33 cancer types were normalized and analyzed using the log2(FPKM + 1) transformation. Tumor versus normal differential expression analysis was conducted using the “limma” R package [18, 19]. ACOXL-AS1 expression in tumor vs. normal tissues was visualized and statistically compared using the Sangerbox tool [20].

Genomic location and transcripts of ACOXL-AS1

To query the genomic location and transcripts of a gene, several major online bioinformatics resources and tools can be utilized. We used NCBI Gene (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/) to identify the chromosomal location, exon structure, and annotations for multiple transcripts of the gene. the Ensembl web tool(www.ensembl.org) display the chromosomal location, exon structure, and annotations for multiple transcripts of the gene [21].

Clinicopathological correlation

Associations between ACOXL-AS1 expression and key clinicopathological features—including tumor stage, lymph node status, metastasis, and molecular subtype—were evaluated using chi-square tests and logistic regression. Tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI).

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to assess the overall survival of patients stratified by high and low expression levels of ACOXL-AS1. The optimal cutoff value for high and low expression groups was determined using median expression levels. Log-rank tests were conducted to compare the survival differences between high and low expression groups. The hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Using the GEPIA2 tool [22], we assessed the impact of ACOXL-AS1 expression on overall survival in general cancer cohorts.

Tumor microenvironment and immune cell infiltration profile

In this study, immune cell and stromal cell infiltration levels were estimated using the TIMER and ESTIMATE algorithms. For TIMER, RNA-Seq data were first normalized, and immune cell infiltration was assessed based on the expression of specific marker genes, such as CD8A for CD8 + T cells. TIMER estimated the abundance of immune cell types, including B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, with high or low immune infiltration defined by the median expression level of the respective marker genes. For ESTIMATE, RNA-Seq data were utilized to calculate the Immune Score and Stromal Score, which represent the relative content of immune and stromal cells within the tumor. The ESTIMATE score, derived by combining both the Immune and Stromal Scores, was used to evaluate the overall tumor microenvironment. A threshold for categorizing high or low immune and stromal content was established using the median of the respective scores, allowing for the classification of samples into distinct groups based on immune and stromal cell infiltration. These algorithms provided a comprehensive analysis of the immune and stromal components of the tumor microenvironment.

These scores, derived using the R-based ESTIMATE package, have been shown to correlate with overall survival and clinicopathological features in various cancers [23–25]. ACOXL-AS1 expression and TME-related gene signatures were assessed using established R methodologies [26].

For immune landscape analysis, CIBERSORT was used to estimate the abundance of 22 immune cell subtypes from gene expression data [27]. The association between ACOXL-AS1 expression and these immune subtypes was examined. To assess correlations between ACOXL-AS1 expression and immune checkpoints, we employed the ggplot2 R package. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to analyze the data, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied for multiple comparisons to control false discovery rate (FDR). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of pan cancer

The hallmark gene set file in “gmt” format was procured from the Molecular Signature Database, ensuring a comprehensive and standardized collection of gene sets for analysis. Utilizing clusterProfiler package in R [28], we assessed the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to determine their normalized enrichment scores (NES) and associated false discovery rates (FDR).

Cell culture and lentiviral transfection

HEC-1 A and HHUA cell lines were selected to model different biological subtypes of endometrial carcinoma. HEC-1 A represents moderately to poorly differentiated tumors with stable growth and moderate invasiveness, while HHUA is a well-differentiated line enriched in cancer stem-like cells, exhibiting high proliferative and invasive potential, and strong expression of markers such as Ki67. Their distinct characteristics allow for a comprehensive assessment of ACOXL-AS1 function across varying tumor phenotypes.

HEC-1 A, a human endometrial cancer cell line sourced from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, was utilized in this study. HHUA cells were graciously provided by the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College. HEC-1 A and HHUA cell Lines were cultured in appropriate media supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics. HEC-1 A and HHUA cell Lines were maintained in culture using appropriate growth media supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics.

For transfection experiments, cells were transfected with either an empty vector or a vector containing the ACOXL-AS1 overexpression construct. Specifically, to overexpress ACOXL-AS1, the recombinant full-length complementary DNA (cDNA) was cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector from Guangzhou Aiji Biotechnology Co., LTD. The pcDNA3.1 empty vector was used as a negative control. Primary human HEC-1 A and HHUA cells were cultured in polybrene-containing medium and infected with lentivirus harboring the ACOXL-AS1-expressing sequence for 48 h. Following viral infection, cells were transferred to fresh complete medium with puromycin (2.5 µg/mL) for selection of stable cells, which continued for 5–6 passages. The overexpression of ACOXL-AS1 was consistently verified in the established stable cell populations (“ACOXL-AS1”). Control experiments involved cells infected with lentivirus carrying an empty vector (“vec”), following an analogous selection process.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol, reverse transcribed into cDNA, and ACOXL-AS1 expression was quantified via qRT-PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix. β-actin served as the internal control, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method (Table 1).

Cell proliferation, migration, and invasion assays

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo). HEC-1 A and HHUA cells were seeded at a density of 5,000 cells per well in 96-well plates. Cells were transfected with either si-NC (negative control), si-ACOXL-AS1#1, or si-ACOXL-AS1#2 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell viability was measured at 0-, 24-, 48-, and 72-hours post-transfection by adding 10 µL of CCK-8 solution to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h and measurement of absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

For colony formation assays, transfected cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well and cultured for 14 days under standard conditions. Colonies were then fixed with 4% methanol for 20 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, and washed with PBS. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted manually. Colony formation efficiency was compared across transfection groups.

Cell migration and invasion abilities were evaluated using Transwell chambers (8.0 μm pore size, Corning) without (migration) or with (invasion) a Matrigel coating. For the migration assay, 5 × 10⁴ transfected cells suspended in 200 µL serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers. For the invasion assay, 1 × 10⁵ cells were seeded into Matrigel-coated upper chambers. In both assays, the lower chambers were filled with 600 µL of medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, non-migrated/invaded cells on the upper membrane surface were removed with a cotton swab. Cells on the lower surface were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a light microscope in five randomly selected fields per insert.

In vivo tumor growth assay

Twelve 4-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (weighing 11–15 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of Shantou University Medical College. Mice were allowed to acclimate for one week prior to experimentation. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shantou University Medical College (Approval No: SUMCXM2024-088).

For euthanasia procedures

Preoperative anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg body weight), a dose established through previous pharmacokinetic studies to achieve surgical-level anesthesia in rodents.

Depth of anesthesia was confirmed by absence of pedal withdrawal reflex and corneal reflex prior to proceeding with tumor resection. Following tumor collection, euthanasia was completed by cervical dislocation while animals remained under deep anesthesia. This two-step method (chemical anesthesia followed by physical disruption of CNS function) complies with AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Edition) which recognizes sodium pentobarbital overdose combined with secondary physical methods as an acceptable euthanasia protocol for rodents.

For the xenograft tumor model, HEC-1 A cells transfected with either pcDNA3.1 vector (control group) or pcDNA3.1-ACOXL-AS1 (overexpression group) were suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) mixed 1:1 with Matrigel (Corning). A total of 1 × 10⁷ cells in 100 µL suspension was injected subcutaneously into the right dorsal flank of each mouse. Each group included six mice. Tumor growth was monitored every three days by measuring tumor length (L) and width (W) using calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the standard formula: Volume = (L × W²)/2.

At the end of the experiment (day 28 post-injection), mice were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), and the depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal withdrawal and corneal reflexes. Tumors were then surgically resected, weighed, and photographed. While under deep anesthesia, mice were humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation. This two-step euthanasia method complies with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Edition), combining chemical anesthesia with physical disruption of central nervous system function to ensure minimal suffering.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

Single-cell transcriptomic data were obtained from the NCBI SRA database (accession number: SRP349751), which includes five primary tissue samples derived from patients with endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (EEC) [29]. Raw sequencing data were processed using the Seurat package (v4.0) in R. After quality control filtering (cells with high mitochondrial gene content or low feature counts were excluded), data normalization and identification of highly variable genes were performed. Dimensionality reduction was conducted using principal component analysis (PCA), followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for visualization.

Cell clustering was achieved using a graph-based approach, and cell type annotation was performed via the SingleR package by comparing cluster-specific expression profiles to reference human cell type datasets. Canonical markers including EPCAM, COL1A1, CD14, and CD68 were used to validate annotation accuracy.

The expression pattern of the long noncoding RNA ACOXL-AS1 across different cell types and pathological states (normal, atypical endometrial hyperplasia [AEH], and EEC) was visualized using DotPlot and FeaturePlot functions. Data integration was performed to ensure comparability across conditions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using R (v4.2.1) with packages including “Maftools,” “ggplot2,” “limma,” “survminer,” “dplyr,” “plyr,” “survivalROC,” “clusterProfiler,” “ggplotify,” “cowplot,” “Hmisc,” “gridExtra,” “GSVA,” “corrplot,” “VennDiagram,” “pheatmap,” and “tidyverse.” Wilcoxon tests were used for two-group comparisons, and ANOVA for three-group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (* p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001).

Results

ACOXL-AS1 expression in pan cancer

ACOXL-AS1 exhibits a distinct expression profile across various human tissues. Notably, it is highly expressed in the testis, thyroid, and prostate, while its expression is comparatively lower in most other tissues (Supplemental Fig. 1 A). To investigate its potential involvement in tumorigenesis, we performed a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis by integrating RNA-seq data from TCGA and GTEx. This analysis revealed that ACOXL-AS1 is significantly downregulated in a broad spectrum of malignancies, including glioblastoma (GBM), low-grade glioma (LGG), breast cancer (BRCA), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), and endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) (Supplemental Fig. 1B). These results suggest a potential tumor-suppressive role for ACOXL-AS1 across multiple cancer types.

Importantly, among the cancer types analyzed, the most pronounced and consistent reduction in ACOXL-AS1 expression was observed in UCEC and LGG, indicating potential tissue-specific functional relevance. Given the particularly striking downregulation of ACOXL-AS1 in UCEC, we further examined its expression specifically in endometrial endometrioid carcinoma (EEC), the most common histological subtype of UCEC. Comparative analysis between tumor and normal endometrial tissues revealed a marked decrease in ACOXL-AS1 levels in EEC samples (Fig. 1A), supporting its putative role as a tumor suppressor in this context. This focused analysis provides a rationale for selecting EEC as a model to further explore the biological functions and regulatory mechanisms of ACOXL-AS1 in endometrial carcinogenesis.

Genomic mapping confirmed that ACOXL-AS1 is located on chromosome 2 (chr2:110732573–111184925) in antisense orientation to ACOXL (Supplemental Fig. 1 C). Transcript annotation identified three lncRNA isoforms, with ENST00000376593.2 being the longest (3026 bp) (Supplemental Fig. 1D). Transcript-level analysis showed widespread downregulation of both ENST00000418615.1 and ENST00000376593.2 in multiple tumor types (Supplemental Fig. 1E), further corroborating the tumor-suppressive potential of ACOXL-AS1.

Prognostic value of ACOXL-AS1

To evaluate the prognostic significance of ACOXL-AS1 in tumor patient outcomes, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were conducted. High ACOXL-AS1 expression was associated with improved overall survival in most cancer types (Supplemental Fig. 2A–L). In general cancer cohorts, elevated expression of ACOXL-AS1 (ENSG00000204581) and ENST00000418615.1 correlated with better overall survival (p < 0.05, Supplemental Fig. 2A–B). This association was particularly evident in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD, p < 0.001), low-grade glioma (LGG, p < 0.001), cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC, p = 0.002), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM, p = 0.008), and mesothelioma (MESO, p = 0.015). Conversely, high ACOXL-AS1 expression was linked to poorer overall survival in kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC, p = 0.001), thymoma (THYM, p = 0.002), thyroid carcinoma (THCA, p = 0.005), adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC, p = 0.019), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that ACOXL-AS1 may serve as a robust prognostic biomarker across multiple malignancies.

Relationships between ACOXL-AS1 and clinicopathological features

A clinical correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the role of ACOXL-AS1 in tumors. ACOXL-AS1 expression was significantly reduced in advanced stages (e.g., BLCA, KIRP, LUSC, BRCA; Supplemental Fig. 3A–D) and exhibited similar trends in LUAD, TGCT, ACC, and CHOL (Supplemental Fig. 3E–H). Lower expression was also observed in lymph node metastases (e.g., LUAD, THCA, KIRC, LUSC; Supplemental Fig. 3I–L) and distant metastases (KICH; Supplemental Fig. 3M). Additionally, larger tumors showed decreased ACOXL-AS1 expression (e.g., PAAD, LUAD; Supplemental Fig. 3P–Q), with similar patterns in THCA and CHOL (Supplemental Fig. 3N–O). These findings suggest ACOXL-AS1 as a potential tumor suppressor and biomarker for cancer progression.

In UCEC, ACOXL-AS1 expression was significantly higher in tumors with < 50% myometrial invasion compared to those with ≥ 50% invasion (p = 0.0379; Fig. 1B; Table 2), implying a role in limiting deeper invasion. While no significant associations were found for other features, trends indicated increased expression with tumor dedifferentiation (Fig. 1A), slight decreases from Stage I to later stages (Fig. 1C), and marginally higher expression in tumors without distant or lymph node metastasis (Fig. 1D–E), suggesting a protective role in tumor progression. Notably, tumors with ≥ 10% necrosis exhibited slightly higher ACOXL-AS1 expression (Fig. 1F), indicating a potential link to tumor microenvironment stability.

Relationship between stromalscore and immunescore with the expression levels of the ACOXL-AS1

To explore the potential role of ACOXL-AS1 in the tumor microenvironment (TME), we examined its association with StromalScore and ImmuneScore metrics across a variety of cancer types. The results revealed consistently negative correlations in several malignancies. Specifically, in bladder cancer (BLCA: R = −0.33 for StromalScore and R = −0.38 for ImmuneScore), adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC: R = −0.32 and R = −0.26), mesothelioma (MESO: R = −0.31 and R = −0.38), ovarian cancer (OV: R = −0.23 and R = −0.19), sarcoma (SARC: R = −0.23 and R = −0.20), thyroid cancer (THCA: R = −0.28 and R = −0.14), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP: R = −0.20 and R = −0.19), and kidney chromophobe (KICH: R = −0.25 and R = −0.27) (Supplemental Fig. 4A-P). These findings suggest that elevated StromalScore and ImmuneScore are linked to reduced ACOXL-AS1 expression, indicating that ACOXL-AS1 may modulate the TME by interacting with stromal and immune components, potentially influencing tumor progression and immune evasion mechanisms.

Correlation of ACOXL-AS1 expression with immune cell infiltration analysis, and immune checkpoint-associated genes across various cancer types

We utilized the ‘ESTIMATE’ package to calculate StromalScore and ImmuneScore, which quantify stromal and immune cell infiltration within tumor samples. Additionally, CIBERSORT was employed to estimate the abundance of 22 immune cell subtypes. Significant correlations between ACOXL-AS1 expression and immune cell infiltration were observed in multiple cancer types, including KIRP, BLCA, THCA, LUAD, BRCA, LGG, TGCT, UCEC, HNSC, STAD, LUSC, THYM, and CESC, with all associations showing low p-values (Supplemental Fig. 5 A). Specifically, in KIRP, ACOXL-AS1 expression was negatively correlated with M1 macrophages (R = −0.45, p = 1.1e-14, Supplemental Fig. 5 C), while in TGCT, it was inversely associated with activated NK cells (R = −0.41, p = 1.1e-07, Supplemental Fig. 5D). Similarly, in THCA, ACOXL-AS1 showed a negative correlation with activated dendritic cells (R = −0.32, p = 1.5e-10, Supplemental Fig. 5E), and in LGG, it was negatively correlated with M1 macrophages (R = −0.28, p = 9.1e-08, Supplemental Fig. 5 F). These findings suggest that higher ACOXL-AS1 expression is associated with lower levels of specific immune cell subtypes in these cancers. Furthermore, co-expression analysis revealed that ACOXL-AS1 was correlated with several immune checkpoint-associated genes. In particular, CD160 and BTNL2 exhibited a positive correlation with ACOXL-AS1 expression across multiple cancer types (Supplemental Fig. 5B). Statistical significance for all analyses was determined using Pearson’s correlation, with correction for multiple testing applied.

Figure 1H-I showed that ACOXL-AS1 expression was predominantly negatively associated with immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment of uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC). Tumors with high ACOXL-AS1 expression exhibited a significant reduction in immune-activating cells, including CD8 + T cells, and M1 macrophages cells, as evidenced by both fraction analysis and correlation coefficients (e.g., M1 macrophages, R = −0.17, p = 0.023; CD8 + T cells, p = 0.022). This negative association between ACOXL-AS1 and most immune cell subtypes underscores its potential role in fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which may contribute to immune evasion and tumor progression.

ACOXL-AS1 expression with TMB, MSI and GSEA across various cancer types

ACOXL-AS1 exhibited correlations with TMB and MSI in certain cancer types. In UCEC, for example, ACOXL-AS1 showed a negative correlation with TMB, indicating a potential role in tumor mutational processes (Supplemental Fig. 5G). Similarly, the correlation with MSI in other cancers suggests a link between ACOXL-AS1 and genomic instability (Supplemental Fig. 5H). These associations highlight the potential of ACOXL-AS1 as a predictive marker for immunotherapy response, as both TMB and MSI are critical indicators of immunogenicity.

To further investigate the role of ACOXL-AS1 in cancers, we performed GSEA on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between low and high ACOXL-AS1 expressing samples across various pan-cancer subsets. Enrichment results revealed associations with several key immune-related biological pathways in low ACOXL-AS1 expressing samples, including TNFα signaling via NF-κB, interferon gamma response, interferon alpha response, inflammatory response, IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Supplemental Fig. 5I). These results indicate that ACOXL-AS1 may play a crucial role in regulating tumor progression by influencing various immune processes within tumor tissues. Importantly, the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway, which is critical for cancer metastasis and invasion, was also closely related to ACOXL-AS1.

ACOXL-AS1 downregulation and its inhibitory effects on endometrial adenocarcinoma cell proliferation

ACOXL-AS1 was downregulated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues within the cohorts in TCGA EEA, with a p-value of 0.0021, indicating a potential role in tumorigenesis (Fig. 2A). The diagnostic value of ACOXL-AS1 expression was further evaluated through ROC curve analysis, yielding an AUC of 0.74, which suggests moderate efficacy in distinguishing between normal and tumor tissues (Fig. 2B). Subsequent experiments confirmed that ACOXL-AS1 was overexpressed in both HEC-1 A and HHUA cell lines following transfection, as evidenced by the qRT-PCR results (Fig. 2C). This transfection-induced overexpression of ACOXL-AS1 led to significant inhibition of cell proliferation in these cell Lines, observed at 48 h and 72 h post-transfection, highlighting its potential anti-proliferative effects (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, colony formation assays revealed a marked reduction in the number of colonies formed by ACOXL-AS1-overexpressing cells compared to the vector controls, emphasizing its inhibitory impact on cellular growth and proliferation (Fig. 2E-F). Collectively, these findings suggest that ACOXL-AS1 functions as a tumor suppressor in endometrial adenocarcinoma, with important implications for its potential use as both a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target.

ACOXL-AS1 inhibits cell migration and invasion in HEC-1 A and HHUA cells

To investigate the role of ACOXL-AS1 in cell migration and invasion, wound healing and transwell assays were performed on HEC-1 A and HHUA cells transfected with either ACOXL-AS1 or vector control. In the wound healing assay, HEC-1 A cells transfected with ACOXL-AS1 exhibited reduced wound healing rate compared to vector controls (*P < 0.05, Fig. 3A). Similarly, HHUA cells showed a decrease in wound healing rate upon ACOXL-AS1 transfection (**P < 0.01, Fig. 3B). ACOXL-AS1 reduces tumor growth in vivo

In an in vivo setting, the overexpression of ACOXL-AS1 resulted in a significant reduction in both tumor weight and volume in HEC-1 A xenografts when compared to the vector control (Fig. 4A-C). These findings underscore the potential of ACOXL-AS1 as a therapeutic target, suggesting that its upregulation may exert inhibitory effects on tumor growth in endometrial cancer models.

ACOXL-AS1 is specifically expressed in epithelial cells and progressively downregulated during endometrial carcinogenesis

As shown in Fig. 5, a comprehensive analysis of five EEC samples revealed distinct cellular landscapes. Figure 5A presents a UMAP projection identifying over 30 annotated cell populations, encompassing epithelial cells, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, tissue stem cells, mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), and diverse immune subsets including T cells, B cells, NK cells, and macrophages. The accuracy of these annotations is validated in Fig. 5B through the expression of classical marker genes, for example, EPCAM and KRT7 for epithelial populations, and CD14 and CD68 for myeloid lineages. Figure 5C highlights the cell-type specificity of ACOXL-AS1, showing its expression to be highly restricted to epithelial cells, with little to no expression detected in stromal or immune compartments. Furthermore, Fig. 5D demonstrates a progressive decline in ACOXL-AS1 expression: it is robust in normal epithelial cells, markedly reduced in atypical endometrial hyperplasia (AEH), and nearly absent in EEC tissues, indicating a loss of ACOXL-AS1 during malignant transformation.

ACOXL-AS1 inhibits homologous recombination by targeting RPA4

GSEA analysis of the high-expression group of ACOXL-AS1 using the “c3.all.v2025.1.Hs. symbols” gene set library revealed a significant enrichment of the homologous recombination pathway (Supplemental Fig. 6 A). Subsequent analysis indicates that ACOXL-AS1 may regulate this pathway by targeting RPA4, thereby potentially modulating its activity in tumor cells. γ-H2A.X, which is a crucial signal for initiating DSB signaling and repair mechanisms [30]. RPA4, as part of the RPA complex, binds to single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) at the site of DSBs. This binding is crucial for protecting the ssDNA from nucleolytic degradation and preventing the formation of secondary structures that could impede repair processes [31].

Discussion

Our pan-cancer analysis of ACOXL-AS1 expression across multiple tumor types revealed a consistent downregulation of this lncRNA in most cancer tissues compared to corresponding normal tissues, suggesting a potential tumor-suppressive function. Notably, elevated ACOXL-AS1 expression was associated with favorable clinical outcomes, including improved overall survival in several malignancies, supporting its utility as a prognostic biomarker. However, the inverse pattern—reduced ACOXL-AS1 expression correlating with poor prognosis—was observed in certain cancer types, implying that the role of ACOXL-AS1 in tumorigenesis is both complex and tissue-specific. These findings highlight the need for deeper investigation into the contextual regulation and downstream mechanisms of ACOXL-AS1 across diverse tumor types.

Among the tumor types analyzed, uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) demonstrated one of the most pronounced differences in ACOXL-AS1 expression between tumor and normal tissues. This striking dysregulation, combined with its strong association with key clinicopathologic features such as tumor stage, metastasis, and patient survival, formed the central rationale for focusing our functional studies on UCEC. By concentrating on this type of cancer, we aimed to provide mechanistic insights that are both biologically meaningful and clinically relevant. Although our findings across the pan-cancer landscape provide a broad overview of ACOXL-AS1’s potential significance, the UCEC-specific data justifies a more targeted investigation into its role in endometrial cancer progression.

Our in vitro and in vivo experiments in endometrial carcinoma cell lines demonstrated that overexpression of ACOXL-AS1 suppressed tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, reinforcing its proposed tumor-suppressive function. Mechanistically, ACOXL-AS1 may modulate the homologous recombination (HR) DNA repair pathway by regulating the expression or activity of RPA4, a critical protein involved in the response to DNA double-strand breaks. GSEA analysis of high ACOXL-AS1–expressing groups revealed significant enrichment of the HR pathway, and accompanying Western blot assays showed decreased γ-H2A.X and RPA4 expression upon ACOXL-AS1 overexpression. These results suggest that ACOXL-AS1 may promote genomic stability by enhancing DNA repair fidelity, thereby suppressing oncogenic transformation.

Additionally, ACOXL-AS1 is located on chromosome 2q13, a genomic region often affected by chromosomal alterations that may influence cancer progression through the deregulation of key non-coding RNAs [32–34]. Structural changes such as deletions, amplifications, or translocations involving this locus may lead to aberrant expression of ACOXL-AS1 and contribute to tumor development. Although our current study did not directly evaluate chromosomal alterations, future genomic and epigenomic analyses could clarify the upstream regulatory mechanisms affecting ACOXL-AS1 in UCEC and other cancers.

Although our data indicate that ACOXL-AS1 expression is inversely correlated with immune cell infiltration—particularly CD8⁺ T cells and M1 macrophages—in uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), this does not necessarily contradict its tumor-suppressive role. The immune microenvironment in cancer is highly context-dependent, and reduced immune infiltration does not universally imply a pro-tumor state. In certain settings, immune infiltration may be accompanied by chronic inflammation, immune exhaustion, or regulatory cell dominance, all of which can paradoxically support tumor progression. Moreover, tumor-suppressive mechanisms may also involve limiting aberrant or dysregulated immune activation that promotes immune escape, stromal remodeling, or angiogenesis. It is therefore plausible that ACOXL-AS1 may exert anti-tumor effects not only by impairing cancer cell proliferation and DNA damage repair but also by modulating the tumor immune landscape toward a less inflammatory, less immunosuppressive, or more stable state. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the precise nature of ACOXL-AS1’s influence on the tumor-immune interface remains to be fully elucidated and warrants further experimental investigation. These findings were derived using bioinformatics tools such as TIMER and ESTIMATE, and thus require validation through experimental approaches, including flow cytometry-based immune profiling and cytokine secretion assays.

Tumor mutational burden and microsatellite instability are critical indicators of tumor immunogenicity and have been increasingly recognized for their predictive value in immunotherapy response [35].We also observed that low ACOXL-AS1 expression was associated with elevated tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) in certain cancer types, further suggesting its role in maintaining genomic integrity. Since TMB and MSI are established biomarkers for predicting immunotherapy efficacy, our findings raise the possibility that ACOXL-AS1 may influence not only tumor biology but also responsiveness to immune-based treatments. These insights warrant further exploration, particularly in the context of immunotherapy for UCEC, which has seen increasing clinical relevance with the approval of immune checkpoint inhibitors for advanced-stage disease.

Despite these promising findings, we acknowledge several limitations in our current study. Given these differential expression patterns, we emphasize the need for further studies to explore the tissue-specific mechanisms underlying ACOXL-AS1 regulation.

Most notably, knockdown experiments were not conducted, due to the intrinsically low expression of ACOXL-AS1 in endometrial cancer cells, which posed a challenge for generating effective CRISPR knockout models. To address this, we are currently exploring alternative approaches such as RNA interference and inducible knockdown systems. Furthermore, our in vitro results were derived from only two UCEC cell lines, which may not fully capture the heterogeneity of endometrial tumors. Expanding future studies to include primary cell cultures and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models will be essential for validating the generalizability of our conclusions.

These insights are preliminary, and the study is exploratory in nature, relying on existing datasets that may not fully capture the complexity of tissue-specific regulatory networks. Therefore, further experimental validation is essential.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides both pan-cancer and tumor-specific evidence supporting ACOXL-AS1 as a tumor suppressor with important implications in UCEC. These results underscore the need for further mechanistic and translational research to fully elucidate the potential of ACOXL-AS1 as a biomarker and therapeutic target in cancer.
