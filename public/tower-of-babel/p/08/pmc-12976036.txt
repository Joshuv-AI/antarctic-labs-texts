Cybersecurity in connected medical devices: a policy agenda for the NHS
Authors: Oliver Toparti, Kunal Rajput, Ara Darzi, Saira Ghafur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConnected Medical Devices (CMD) are redefining care within the NHS but exposing it to bi-directional cyber-physical threats that traverse physical, network and cloud layers. These vulnerabilities blur the boundary between technology and patient safety. This Comment argues that the MHRA should elevate cybersecurity to a clinical-safety mandate, enforcing a unified socio-technical framework with security-by-design, cross-layer risk assessment and continuous post-market vigilance.Subject terms: Engineering, Health care

Introduction

The recent Darzi review of the National Health Service (NHS) warned that, without full-scale digitisation, the NHS cannot deliver the transformation needed for a modern, resilient health system1. Amid growing systemic strain, technological stagnation, and health inequities in the NHS, the UK government recently published its 10-year NHS strategy. This plan establishes the drive to reform the NHS by digital transformational change2. As the NHS advances its digital agenda, technologies such as Artificial Intelligence (AI), the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), and digital twins are progressively being integrated into the NHS. One notable example of this transformation is the growing adoption of connected medical devices (CMD), which are becoming more interconnected and interdependent on the NHS’s infrastructure. As these digital advancements accelerate, they also expose the NHS to greater cybersecurity risks, particularly due to the increasing interconnectivity and dependency of CMD on critical health infrastructure.

Bi-directionality

Since most CMDs are considered to be part of the IoMT, they exist within a larger interconnected ecosystem known as the connected medical device infrastructure (CMDI). Previous research has concentrated on securing individual mechanisms within this infrastructure, often examining components in isolation from their broader operational context. However, modern cyber-attacks are known to traverse bi-directionally across components and layers, often originating through multiple entry points (EP). Given this complexity, vulnerabilities and their associated risks must be examined collectively, treating the CMDI as a unified ecosystem rather than a collection of silos. This rationale is particularly evident in connected diabetes management devices (CDMD) (Fig. 1), where attacks can originate from the physical, network or cloud layer. An exploit in any layer can lead to effects that surpass the boundaries of the initially affected component. Such breaches often cascade system-wide, jeopardising confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

Layers and entry points in CMDI.

A bi-directional attack (Fig. 1) can manifest through multiple vectors across the CDMI. One example is a Man-In-the-Middle (MITM) attack, targeting the controller of a CDMD. As an example, this can occur between a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) and an insulin pump (IP), where a malicious actor intercepts the communication between the two devices and impersonates both the sender and the receiver. This gives the attacker control over the data flow without either device detecting the breach. The attack usually begins at the network layer, often by exploiting an unencrypted data channel. From there, it can extend to the physical layer, for example, by jamming the CGM’s sensor or sending fake commands to the IP. It can also upward-layer propagate, with the attacker sending false data to the cloud, which may affect monitoring systems or automated decisions. Although this represents a theoretical construct, bi-directional threats have materialised in practice.

The 2017 WannaCry ransomware attack illustrates how malware introduced at the cloud-level can propagate downwards to disrupt physical-layer systems, forcing NHS hospitals to cancel over 13,500 outpatient appointments, divert emergency services, and revert to manual workflows across radiology and admissions3. Conversely, the 2017 MedJack (Medical Device Hijack) ransomware attack began with the exploitation of vulnerable physical-layer devices, such as radiology workstations and infusion pumps, and escalated upward into hospital cloud networks through insecure interfaces and insufficient segmentation. Bidirectional threats do not arise solely from deliberate cyberattacks but can also result from negligence. The 2024 CrowdStrike outage illustrates how a faulty update in widely deployed cybersecurity infrastructure can propagate downwards from cloud-based systems to critical patient-facing technologies, disabling imaging platforms, electronic health records (EHR), and clinical monitoring systems across hundreds of hospitals4. To this end, all events underscore the need to approach CMD security as a multilayered challenge rather than a collection of isolated concerns, one that must be addressed through a unified framework spanning the physical, network, and cloud layers.

Risks at the physical and network layer

The physical layer extends to a broad range of CMD, any of which exhibits varying degrees of vulnerabilities. Examples include smart insulin pens, smart inhalers, smart hearing aids, wearable Electrocardiogram (ECG) monitors, implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs), and connected ultrasound devices, among others. However, the risk of one of these CMD vulnerabilities being successfully exploited and cascading to patient harm is influenced by a combination of contextual, technical, and physical variables.

The contextual variable refers to the specific environment in which a medical device is deployed. Settings such as hospitals, outpatient clinics, remote care facilities, or domestic environments each introduce distinct exposure and control measures. For instance, a smart infusion pump installed in an Intensive Care Unit (ICU) may be hard-wired to the hospital’s secured Ethernet network, thereby presenting a relatively moderate level of cyber risk. In contrast, a device operating entirely offline via removable media in a rural clinic may face significantly higher risks because of the broader limitations of the rural or low-resource setting, including reduced oversight, maintenance, and cybersecurity infrastructure.

The technical variables relate to how CMD connects and exchanges data. Close-range proximity methods, such as low-energy Bluetooth (BLE), reduce remote attack risk but may be vulnerable to nearby interference. Wider-range proximity methods like Wi-Fi and Cellular increase functionality but also expand the potential for external threats. Regardless of proximity, both types of connectivity can introduce varying levels of vulnerabilities, from weak authentication to unencrypted data transmission. One notable close-proximity attack is the 2019 Medtronic MiniMed insulin pump vulnerability, where the manufacturer recalled thousands of devices due to insufficient protection mechanisms against authentication attacks. An attacker within wireless range could intercept, modify, or inject data, altering the pump settings to control insulin delivery, representing a serious risk to patient safety. Indeed, the potential impact of exploiting this vulnerability, otherwise code-named CVE-2019-10964, was formally proven highly severe, as demonstrated by the 8.8 high common vulnerability scoring system (CVSS) score assigned by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) via the National Vulnerability Database (NVD)5. To date, CVSS is used to quantify the severity of software and hardware vulnerabilities based on standardised metrics, including exploitability, impact on confidentiality, integrity, and availability, and the level of user interaction or privileges required. Vulnerabilities are scored on a scale from 0.0 to 10.0, with severity ratings classified as none (0.0), low (0.1–3.9), medium (4.0–6.9), high (7.0–8.9) and critical (9.0–10.0).

The physical variable includes device invasiveness, which refers to the extent to which a device is physically integrated with the patient’s body. The greater the level of integration, the more severe the potential consequences of a security compromise. For example, while a smart pill bottle entails minimal direct physical risk, a pacemaker, which is surgically implanted and essential for sustaining cardiac function, presents a significantly higher threat in the event of exploitation.

Risks at the cloud layer

The cloud layer acts as a shared central node for collecting, processing, and routing CMD data. It interfaces with the physical layer, via the network layer, using integration protocols, typically implemented as application programming interfaces (API) or on-premises gateways (OPGs). While experts often frame vulnerabilities in cloud environments as issues of software, firmware, or hardware, rooted in architectural design, computational limitations, or proprietary systems, the likelihood of cloud-layer compromise, and its capacity to propagate downstream into patient harm, is more accurately understood as a socio-technical problem. This rationale refers to the interdependent relationship between people (e.g., developers, security operatives, or high-level executives), organisational processes (e.g., software supply chain verification), and technologies (e.g., identity management systems and notably connected medical devices).

As organisations such as the NHS are confronted with the growing complexity of cloud infrastructure, one of the most prevalent risks they may face is cloud-level misconfiguration (CLM). This often manifests as vulnerabilities in authentication and access control mechanisms, allowing attackers to gain unauthorised access to private integration protocols, such as the CMD API. In such cases, security mechanisms are bypassed, potentially exposing sensitive patient data at the cloud layer. The underlying causes of CLM, especially at larger CMD vendors, are less attributed to the absence of broken user authentication and more about broader organisational challenges, including a shortage of skilled professionals and outdated practices.

Identity and access management (IAM) failures are another major risk in modern cloud environments, referring to the inability to adequately control which users can and cannot access sensitive data, applications, and other resources. A common manifestation of this is excessive account permissions (EAP), where users or services are granted access that is broader than required. While the principle of least privilege (PoLP) offers a well-established framework for mitigating such risks by restricting access strictly to what is necessary, its implementation is often inadequate. Additionally, organisational culture and attitudes, such as complacency or negligence, can further undermine the security of the cloud layer.

Cross-domain threats are also a significant concern to cloud infrastructure, as they often come from outside the organisation through compromised vendors, third-party tools, or shared identity systems, making traditional perimeter defences less effective. The recent Synnovis cyber-attack impacting the NHS is a clear example, where a third-party supplier was targeted with ransomware and caused significant disruptions to hospital and primary care services6. As the NHS becomes increasingly reliant on interconnected services and external platforms, the ability to detect and respond to threats across domains becomes crucial for maintaining clinical continuity.

Recognising these risks, NHS England and the Department of Health and Social Care launched a new supplier engagement programme in January 2026. The voluntary Cyber Security Supply Chain Charter strengthens collaborative assurance across key controls, including patch management, access control, and backup resilience7. While this represents a positive step in addressing supply chain vulnerabilities, it must be complemented by robust regulatory frameworks to ensure accountability across the CMD ecosystem.

Recommendations to the MHRA

The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) has recently taken significant steps to strengthen the cybersecurity and safety of medical devices, including the ongoing implementation of the Future Regulation of Medical Devices (FRMD) programme to reform the post-Brexit Medical Devices Regulations 2002 (as amended)8. The programme applies to all categories of medical devices but places particular emphasis on digital or connected technologies, where cybersecurity is a key concern. Phase 1 took effect in June 2025 and strengthens post‑market surveillance, so devices continue to operate safely once on the market, with closer monitoring of performance, reliability and cybersecurity. Phase 2 started in 2024 and focuses on pre‑market requirements. It aims to ensure a device is safe enough to be sold by improving how products are assessed, classified and approved before reaching patients.

Currently, these standards primarily assess risks within individual layers and do not require systematic analysis of cascading or bi-directional threats across interconnected components. Manufacturers of CMDs should be required to submit explicit cross-layer security risk assessments as part of the approval process. An adequate cross-layer assessment would evaluate how vulnerabilities and dependencies interact across the physical, network and cloud layers. This involves mapping how an exploit in one layer could affect another, such as a network breach leading to unauthorised firmware modification or a cloud configuration error altering device data.

Existing international standards, including ISO 14971 on risk management, provide general guidance for identifying and mitigating cybersecurity risks. Whilst the U.S. FDA does not use the term cross-layer security risk assessment as a discrete artefact, its latest pre-market cybersecurity guidance moves in this direction. It expects submissions to include “security architecture views” that show the device in its wider system, including networks and cloud connections, and to explain how controls mitigate risk across that system9. It calls for a “Global System View” that identifies update infrastructure, healthcare-network impacts and cloud connections.

Embedding security from the outset is far more effective and sustainable than retrofitting it later. This has been acknowledged by the U.S. Health Care Industry Cybersecurity Task Force, which highlighted the substantial challenges associated with replacing legacy medical devices and proposed a “Cash for Clunkers” style incentive model to accelerate their safe and systematic replacement10. However, to reduce the economic and operational burden of such transitions, the MHRA should focus on embedding secure-by-design principles through mandatory Secure Product Development Frameworks (SPDF) for all new CMDs as a condition of pre-market approval. An SPDF is a set of formal processes that integrate cybersecurity throughout the product lifecycle, ensuring that it is ‘built in’ to a device and not ‘bolted on’ after the fact, as emphasised in the FDA’s pre-market guidance9. The FDA also expects manufacturers to provide a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) as part of this process, giving visibility into all software components and dependencies used within a device. The MHRA does not currently require SBOMs. Incorporating them as part of SPDF evidence would enhance supply chain transparency and align the UK with international practice.

The MHRA should strengthen governance by clarifying accountability at the operational level. Current UK regulation does not define who is responsible, accountable, consulted, or informed (RACI) for key activities such as patching, access control, recovery, or vulnerability management once a connected medical device is deployed. This gap blurs the line between the product domain, which is the manufacturer's responsibility, and the operational domain, which lies with healthcare providers. Addressing this requires overcoming structural barriers where regulators, like the FDA, oversee devices but lack the remit to regulate hospital networks. Without coordination, strict security rules may accidentally block new medical technology from the UK without actually improving the system’s safety. The MHRA should align manufacturer requirements with NHS operational capabilities to ensure transparency tools, such as SBOMs, are actionable to deliver whole-system improvement in cybersecurity. The MHRA could address this by establishing a formal RACI framework that explicitly links product-level obligations to operational responsibilities.

Together, these measures would establish a coherent regulatory foundation for CMD security in the United Kingdom. By requiring cross-layer risk assessment at the point of market entry, embedding secure-by-design principles throughout product development, and defining clear lines of accountability once devices are deployed, the MHRA can move beyond fragmented oversight toward a unified model of safety that treats cybersecurity and patient safety as one.
