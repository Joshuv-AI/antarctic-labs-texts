Efficiency and economic performance of honey producers in southwest Nigeria: a comprehensive empirical analysis
Authors: Justin Orimisan Ijigbade, Sunday Oluwadare Wright Toluwase, Taiwo Michael Agbede, Oluwaseun Adetarami, Olufunke Olufunmilayo Ilemobayo, Aruna Imoru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the profitability and efficiency of honey production in Southwest Nigeria, with emphasis on technical, economic, and allocative efficiencies. A multistage sampling technique was used to select 114 honey producers. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, budgetary analysis, the Stochastic Frontier Model, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), and the Relative Importance Index (RII). The results demonstrate that honey production is male-dominated, with an average household size of five persons. Budgetary analysis confirms that honey production is economically viable, generating a gross margin of ₦452,201.65, a net profit of ₦371,428.58, and a capital turnover ratio of 3.70, indicating a return of ₦3.70 for every ₦1.00 invested. Stochastic frontier estimates reveal that honey price and depreciation costs significantly increased output at the 1% level, while labor, herbicides, and transportation costs significantly reduced output at the same level of significance. The mean technical efficiency of 0.92 suggests that producers are operating close to the production frontier. However, the mean economic and allocative efficiencies (0.43 each) indicate substantial cost inefficiencies and suboptimal resource allocation. The RII identifies the major constraints affecting productivity and profitability as limited access to modern technology, outdated honey extraction methods, human disturbances, and high beehive costs. The findings imply that while honey production is profitable and technically efficient, improving resource allocation and adopting modern production technologies would significantly enhance overall economic performance.

Introduction

Apiculture plays a strategic role in sustainable agriculture and rural development by generating income, enhancing food security, and providing essential ecosystem services such as pollination. In many developing regions, especially in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia, honey production plays a significant role in livelihood diversification among smallholder farmers due to minimal entry barriers, its capacity to enhance household income and food security, and increasing consumer demand for natural products1,2. Despite this potential, honey production systems are often characterized by sub-optimal productivity and limited economic performance, suggesting inefficiencies in the use of inputs and the adoption of modern technologies2.

Across Africa, prolific honey-producing countries such as Ethiopia, Tanzania, and Kenya still rely heavily on traditional beekeeping practices that limit productivity per unit input. For example, comprehensive reviews on African apiculture document widespread reliance on outdated production techniques, limited access to modern equipment, and insufficient extension services, all of which contribute to persistently low honey yields and weak value chain integration2,3. These structural barriers not only constrain production efficiency but also reduce the competitiveness of honey in both domestic and international markets.

Empirical studies in various African countries reinforce this narrative. In Southern Ethiopia, research using stochastic frontier analysis shows that honey producers operating improved hive technologies exhibit higher technical efficiency compared to those using traditional hives, indicating that technological adoption can reduce inefficiency and increase output4. However, even with improved technologies, average technical efficiency often remains below optimal levels, suggesting that multiple factors beyond technology adoption—such as training, market access, and input availability—influence performance outcomes.

In Nigeria, honey production has received scholarly attention in recent years, yet empirical evidence remains fragmented and regional. Studies in northern states reveal that honey production systems generate positive economic returns but are hampered by inefficient resource use and poor adoption of productivity-enhancing practices. For instance, a study of honey and beeswax producers in Kaduna State reported an average technical efficiency score of 56.3%, indicating a sizeable gap between actual and potential output under existing technologies and management practices5. Similarly, research on honey productivity in Kaduna State identified sub-optimal productivity among 66% of beekeepers, highlighting constraints such as high costs of modern technology, inadequate capital, limited extension services, and poor access to credit6. These inefficiencies translate into forgone income and restrict the socioeconomic impact of apiculture in rural communities.

While recent literature provides important insights into profitability and farm-level performance in specific Nigerian states, comprehensive assessments that integrate technical, economic, and allocative efficiency measurements are sparse, particularly for the Southwest region. Studies focused on honey processing in Southwest Nigeria have documented profitability and operational efficiency among processors but do not directly address production efficiency at the farm level, which is critical to understanding how producers allocate inputs and manage costs to maximize output7. Moreover, existing research often relies on descriptive profitability measures without the rigorous decomposition of inefficiency effects or exploration of allocative distortions in input use.

Beyond Nigeria, research across Sub-Saharan Africa also points to similar gaps. Profitability studies in Nepal, for instance, show that beekeeping can be efficient and profitable when modern practices are adopted, with mean technical efficiency estimated at 81%8. However, these findings from Asia underscore the need for region-specific analyses, as socioeconomic conditions, market integration, and production environments differ significantly across contexts. In Rwanda, investigations into market determinants demonstrate that access to markets and transaction costs significantly influence economic outcomes for honey producers, indicating that structural characteristics beyond production technology affect efficiency and profitability9,10.

Despite its favourable ecological conditions, honey production in Southwest Nigeria has not attained its full commercial potential. Nigeria still relies partly on honey imports to meet domestic demand, indicating possible inefficiencies within local production systems. Although previous studies have examined profitability and production constraints—particularly in northern Nigeria—comprehensive efficiency analyses that jointly estimate production and cost frontiers remain scarce in the southwest region. This limits a deeper understanding of whether the area’s ecological advantages translate into technical, economic, and allocative efficiency.

Moreover, existing studies often focus on descriptive profitability measures without applying robust econometric approaches capable of identifying sources of inefficiency and productivity gaps. There is therefore insufficient empirical evidence that integrates stochastic frontier production and cost models with economic performance indicators to provide a holistic assessment of honey producers’ performance in Southwest Nigeria. The absence of such integrated analysis constrains policy efforts aimed at improving productivity, reducing inefficiencies, and strengthening value capture in the apiculture subsector.

In light of national policy priorities on agricultural diversification, rural income enhancement, and reduction of import dependence, a rigorous evaluation of efficiency and economic performance in this region is both timely and necessary. Accordingly, the objective of this study is to empirically assess the technical, economic, and allocative efficiencies of honey producers in Southwest Nigeria using stochastic frontier production and cost models, while identifying the key determinants of inefficiency and major structural constraints affecting their economic performance.

Results and discussion

Socio-economic characteristics of the respondents

The results in Table 1 revealed that 84.7% of the producers were male, implying that honey production in the study area is male-dominated. This pattern reflects the gendered nature of apiculture in many developing countries, where beekeeping activities—especially hive management and harvesting—are perceived as physically demanding and are therefore predominantly undertaken by men. Similar male dominance has been reported in recent studies in Nigeria, Burkina Faso and Tanzania, where over 80–90% of beekeepers were male, highlighting gender disparities in participation across the beekeeping value chain11–13. The findings also revealed that 81.9% of the respondents were married. The likely reason might be because most married people have dependents, and therefore, require a steady and additional source of income to meet their family’s needs14. This observation aligns with findings that married households are more actively engaged in beekeeping activities compared to single producers, as evidenced by a recent study in Tanzania where a large majority of honey producers were married and more involved in production and hive management compared to unmarried individuals¹³. The mean age of honey producers in our study was approximately 50 years, indicating that the majority were still within the economically active age group. This age range is typically associated with agility and productivity, essential qualities for successful honey production. These findings are consistent with recent studies showing that most beekeepers are in the productive adult age cohort15,16. Moreover, our study revealed that honey producers had an average of 13.8 years of schooling, suggesting a relatively high level of literacy among the beekeeping community. This higher level of education implies that honey producers may possess a greater understanding and a higher propensity to adopt new technologies in their practices, a relationship supported by recent research showing that formal education positively influences technology adoption among beekeepers17. Additionally, our study indicated that 76.4% of honey producers had family sizes ranging from 1 to 5 members, with a mean household size of 5 persons. This suggests that honey production in the study area is largely undertaken by moderately sized households. Similar findings have been reported in Ethiopia, where beekeeping households had a mean family size of approximately 5.7 members18. However, our findings contrast with other studies which observed relatively larger household sizes among rural agricultural households engaged in beekeeping activities19. These variations may reflect differences in socio-economic structures and labour allocation patterns across regions. The study revealed that only 27.8% of the producers had access to credit/loan in the study area revealing that many of the producers did not have access to credit facilities in order to finance their businesses, thereby causing expansion limitation. It can be deduced that capital were raised through personal savings or from relatives that might not charge interest on the money borrowed. These findings are in consonance with Vaziritabar and Esmaeilzade20, who reported that about 82% of the beekeepers do not have access to credit facilities in their studies carried out in Karaj State in Iran. The results showed a value of 9.5 mean years of business experience by the honey producers. The findings ascertained that a great number of the honey producers have been in the business for a long time, and could have learnt from their past mistakes through experience. It is also assumed that the producers can predict changes in climatic and economic factors that could influence the level of efficiency of the business. This is because the more experienced a producer is, the more efficient he/she is and vice versa as reported by Jagiso et al.21 among honey producers in Damot, Gale District in Southern Ethiopia. The result also indicated that 69.4% of the producers belonged to trade associations which could imply that majority of the respondents have access to credit facilities through cooperative organizations. This is supported by the findings of Jiboye et al.22, who reported that membership of cooperatives is a vehicle for entrepreneurial development since they provide informal loans to the members. The finding is also consistent with Vaziritabar and Esmaeilzade20, who also reported that many honey producers belong to one association or another. As presented in Tables 1 and 70.8% of the honey producers in the study area used hired labour, while 15.3% depended on family labour. Also, the results revealed that most (93.0%) of the respondents produce honey from their apiary in the study area.

System of beekeeping by honey producers

Results from Fig. 2 indicated that 94.5% of the honey producers practiced conventional and modern method while 5.6% still practiced the traditional method of honey production. The high rate of use of modern (41.7%) and conventional (52.8%) methods of honey production practices could be attributed to high output and ease of production methods.

The traditional system typically involves the use of rudimentary hives such as hollowed logs, clay pots, or woven baskets placed in trees or forest locations. This method requires minimal capital investment but offers limited control over colony management, resulting in lower productivity and greater difficulty during harvesting.

The conventional system represents an intermediate approach, commonly involving transitional hives such as the Kenyan Top Bar Hive (KTBH). This system allows better colony inspection and management than traditional methods while remaining relatively affordable for small-scale producers. It improves honey yield and reduces colony disturbance during harvesting.

The modern system, on the other hand, utilizes standardized movable-frame hives such as the Langstroth hive. This method enables systematic colony management, disease control, efficient harvesting, and higher productivity. It also facilitates the use of modern accessories such as smokers, protective clothing, and mechanical extractors. The relatively high adoption rate of conventional and modern systems observed in this study may be attributed to their higher output potential, improved honey quality, and greater ease of management compared to the traditional method.

The predominance of improved systems suggests a positive transition toward commercialization and productivity enhancement in the study area, which may contribute to the relatively high technical efficiency recorded among producers.

Method of honey extraction

Figure 3 shows that 56.9% of the respondents used the mechanical (hydraulic) method of honey extraction. This method involves the use of a mechanical device to press or spin honey out of the comb after harvesting. In contrast, 43.1% of the honey producers relied on the manual method of extraction. The higher adoption of the mechanical method may be attributed to its efficiency and relative ease of operation. Mechanical extraction typically results in faster processing and higher honey yield compared to the manual method, which is more labor-intensive and time-consuming.

Channels of output sales by honey producers

Figure 4 shows that 41.7% of honey producers sold their products at the point of production to processors, marketers, or fellow producers. In comparison, 29.2% marketed their honey through local markets, 22.2% through urban markets, and only 6.9% accessed international markets. The predominance of farm-gate sales reflects limited market integration and a preference for immediate cash transactions, lower transportation costs, and the availability of ready buyers. It also suggests that small-scale producers tend to rely on direct sales channels due to infrastructural limitations and marketing constraints. Additionally, buyers’ ability to identify trusted producers and verify product authenticity may have encouraged direct purchases, particularly amid concerns about honey adulteration in open markets.

Costs and returns analysis to honey production

The results of the costs and returns, as presented in Table 2, show that the total cost incurred in the enterprise per annum was ₦137,351.42, while the total revenue accrued from the business was ₦508,780.00. Again, the results of the gross margin and profit were ₦452,201.65 and ₦371,428.58, respectively. These positive values indicate that honey production is a profitable business in the study area. The value of the capital turnover of 3.70 implies that honey production will realize/return ₦3.70 on every ₦1 expended on the enterprise. A high capital turnover ratio indicates the honey producers’ ability to achieve maximum sales with a minimal amount of capital employed. The value of Expense Ratio (ER) of 27.0 indicates that the fixed cost incurred in the business is greater than money expended on variable cost by 73.0%. The result of return on investment further confirms the viability of the business as it has the capability of repayment of capital, as well as the interest on any loan borrowed and invested in the business. All these profitability measures confirmed and reiterated the profitability of honey production in the area. The result is in line with the findings of Vaziritabar and Esmaeilzade20, who reported that the average annual household earnings from beekeeping was relatively high (₦53,699,568.00), and contributes to an average of 26.64 ± 28.95% of the total annual income of beekeepers which shows that beekeeping plays a vital role in increasing and diversifying the incomes of rural communities.

Estimation of stochastic production function for honey producers

The Maximum Likelihood Estimates (MLE) of the stochastic frontier production function for producers is presented in Table 3. The high and significant value of 1.226 for sigma-square (σ²) indicates the goodness of fit of the model and supports the correct specification of the assumed composite error terms distribution23. The Gamma (Γ) of 0.908 for producers shows that 90.8% of the variations in the outputs of honey production were due to technical inefficiency. The results revealed that the coefficients of apiary size, depreciation on fixed items and honey price had significant and positive effect on the honey output of the producers in the study area. This implies that increase in these variables would increase the output of producers in honey production. The unit price of honey has positive and strong relationship with honey output at 1% significant level. This means that a naira increase in the unit price of the honey leads to more output of honey production by 1.2 L. The coefficient of apiary size had positive correlation with the honey output at 10% level of significance. Thus, the result is consistent with a priori expectation. The result here implies that a unit increase in the apiary size will lead to about 0.15 increases in the honey output, ceteris paribus.

On the other hand, the results also indicated that the coefficients of labour, herbicide, and transportation costs had negative relationship with the output of producers in the study area. This implies that increase in these variables would lead to decrease in the output of the producers and consequently reduce their accruable income.

Technical, economic, and allocative efficiencies. Table 5 shows that the average technical, economic and allocative efficiencies of honey producers in the study area were 0.923, 0.433 and 0.434, respectively. This suggests that an average of about 92.3%, 43.3% and 43.4%, respectively of potential maximum output were gained due to production efficiency, while the short fall (discrepancy between observed output and the frontier output) could be attributed to inefficiencies. The mean technical efficiency of 0.923 indicates that honey producers operate close to the production frontier, consistent with findings from studies conducted in Nigeria, Burkina Faso, and Tanzania11–13. However, the relatively lower economic efficiency (0.433) and allocative efficiency (0.434) values suggest suboptimal use of inputs and higher production costs, a pattern similarly reported in other developing countries. Positive effects of honey price, apiary size, and depreciation costs highlight the benefits of market incentives and investment in infrastructure, while negative effects of labour, herbicide, and transportation costs show how rising operational expenses can limit productivity. Nigerian studies confirm that improved hive management, larger apiaries, and favorable market conditions enhance output and profitability6,11,16.

Conclusion

The study found that honey production in the area is male-dominated, with producers in their economically active years and experienced in the trade. The enterprise is profitable, with notable gross margin, profit, and capital turnover. Key determinants of productivity include labour, herbicide, transportation, honey price, and depreciation, while allocative efficiency is influenced by age, sex, household size, experience, colonization period, beehive type, and extraction method. Major constraints confronting producers include limited access to modern technologies, high costs of improved hives, inefficient extraction methods, and human disturbances. Recommendations include access to modern beekeeping equipment, promotion of improved hive technologies, providing targeted education and training, encouraging youth participation, facilitation of affordable credit schemes to reduce capital constraints, bulk procurement of modern hives by cooperative societies to lower acquisition costs, and strengthening extension services to improve technical and allocative efficiencies and to sustain profitability in the honey production enterprise.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Southwest Nigeria, which comprises Ondo, Ekiti, Lagos, Ogun, Osun, and Oyo States (Fig. 5). The area lies between 2° 31′ and 6° 00′ East longitude and 6° 20′ and 8° 37′ North latitude. It encompasses the derived savannah and humid forest zones, characterized by a tropical wet and dry climate with mean annual rainfall of approximately 1,500 mm and 2,000 mm, respectively. The region experiences two distinct seasons: the rainy season, from April to October, and the dry season, from November to March.

Fig. 5Map of the study area.

Map of the study area.

Southwest Nigeria was selected for this study because of its ecological suitability and strategic importance to apiculture development in the country. The region lies within the humid forest and derived savanna zones of the forest–savanna transition belt, characterized by relatively high annual rainfall, diverse vegetation, and abundant nectar-producing plants that support strong honeybee colony performance and year-round foraging. It also hosts a mix of traditional and modern apiary systems, organized beekeeper associations, cooperative societies, and emerging agribusiness enterprises, making it an appropriate setting for evaluating production efficiency and economic performance. Furthermore, the presence of agricultural research institutions and extension services enhances its relevance for analysing productivity dynamics. Despite these advantages, honey production in the region has not fully met domestic demand, as Nigeria still relies partly on honey imports—suggesting potential inefficiencies in local production systems. While previous studies have largely focused on profitability and constraints in other parts of the country, comprehensive efficiency analyses integrating stochastic frontier production and cost models remain limited in Southwest Nigeria. The selection of this region therefore addresses a clear empirical gap and provides policy-relevant insights to strengthen apiculture as a viable livelihood and agribusiness strategy.

Data collection

Data used for the study were collected from the primary source. Again, the data were sourced through a direct personal interview and well-structured questionnaire to obtain pertinent information on socioeconomic characteristics of the respondents, costs, and returns involved in the honey production as well as the constraints faced in the business. The questionnaire (Code: ESKU/AAE/300) was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Ekiti State University, Ado-Ekiti, Ekiti State, Nigeria.

Validity of instrument

The validity of the content and construction of the instrument (questionnaire) was determined by expert judgment of some selected practicing apiculturists in Ondo and Ogun States, top management staff of Apicultural Unit of Ondo State Agri-business Empowerment Centre (OSAEC) and Lecturers of the Department of Agricultural Economics and Extension Services of Ekiti State University, Ado-Ekiti.

Sampling technique and sample size

The study focused on honey producers within a specific geographical area. A multistage sampling approach was employed to select respondents for the study. In the first stage, three states were purposively chosen from the six states in southwest Nigeria. Ondo, Ekiti, and Ogun States were selected based on preliminary investigations that indicated widespread beekeeping activities in those areas. In the second stage, six local government areas were purposively selected from each of the three states to make a total of eighteen local governments for the study. In Ondo State, the selected local government areas were Odigbo, IleOluji/Okeigbo, Ondo West, Akure South, Owo, and Ose. For Ekiti State, the chosen local government areas were Ise/Orun, Ikere, Ikole, Oye, Ado, and Ijero. In Ogun State, the selected local government areas included Abeokuta North, Odeda, Ijebu-East, Ijebu Ode, Odogbolu, and Yewa North. Lastly, at the third stage, eight communities were randomly selected from each of the eighteen local government areas chosen to make a total of 144 respondents for the study.

Analytical tools

Data collected were analyzed using a combination of statistical and specialized software to ensure accuracy and clarity. Descriptive statistics—including frequency counts, percentages, mean, and standard deviation—were computed using SPSS version 25.0. Budgetary analysis and the construction of graphs and figures were performed using Microsoft Excel 2019, ensuring that all figures were generated in high resolution with clear labeling, legible legends, and consistent formatting to improve visual quality. The technical efficiency of honey production was measured using a stochastic frontier production function, while cost efficiency was assessed through a stochastic frontier cost function, both estimated using Frontier 4.1 software. The technical and allocative efficiency models followed the parameterization of Alem (2018)24, where gamma (γ) and sigma-squared (σ²) were estimated using the maximum likelihood method.
