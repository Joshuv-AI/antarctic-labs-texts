Anisakid larvae in the organs of Atlantic mackerel, Scomber scombrus (L.): Host or parasite superiority?
Authors: Bahram Sayyaf Dezfuli, Giovanni Bernacchia, Morena De Bastiani, Martina Pinca, Emanuela Franchella, Luca Bellini, Giuseppe Castaldelli, Luisa Giari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMembers of the Anisakidae family, in particular the genus Anisakis are among the most common seafood-borne parasites which may lead to anisakidosis, a zoonotic disease. Anisakis simplex (s.s.) (Rudolphi, 1809) can infect humans by means of the ingestion of raw or undercooked fish harbouring the third-stage larvae of this nematode. Histopathological and ultrastructural investigations were performed on the visceral organs and flesh of Atlantic mackerel Scomber scombrus (L.) from the Bay of Biscay. Results showed the presence of a high infection level of third-larval stages of an anisakid species in the visceral organs and rarely in the flesh of the host. A subsample of 15 live nematode larvae from 5 fish were genetically identified by mtDNA Cox2 gene sequencing and found to belong to the species Anisakis simplex (s.s.). Of 41 Atlantic mackerel examined, 36 individuals (88 %) harbored A. simplex (s.s.) L3 larvae, with an intensity of infection ranging from 5 to 650 larvae per fish (86.97 ± 17.26, mean ± standard error). A total of 3131 larvae were counted and the number of calcified larvae was significantly higher than that of live ones (p < 0.01). The most parasitized sites were the serosa covering the intestine and the pyloric caeca as well as the mesenteries which encircled the gonads. In most infected organs, a granuloma surrounded the calcified and live larvae with a more intense response around the former. Within the granuloma epithelioid cells, fibroblasts and collagen fibres were recognized, outside the granuloma, mast cells (MCs), melano-macrophage aggregates (MAs) and macrophages were frequent and to a lesser degree rodlet cells (RCs). This is the first study that has investigated the cellular immune responses in the visceral organs and flesh of Atlantic mackerel against A. simplex (s.s.) larvae.Keywords: Atlantic mackerel, Anisakis simplex (s.s.), Histopathology, Innate immune cells, Zoonotic disease

Introduction

Nematodes belonging to the family Anisakidae occur at the third larval stage in numerous marine teleost species in several regions of the world (Shamsi and Barton, 2023). Species of Anisakidae use planktonic or pelagic crustaceans as intermediate hosts, several fish species (Levsen and Berland, 2012) and squid serve as paratenic hosts for Anisakis spp. (Cipriani et al., 2024a) with sea mammals as definitive hosts (Cipriani et al., 2024a, Cipriani et al., 2024b; Levsen and Berland, 2012; Mattiucci et al., 2018) and humans act as accidental hosts. Nematodes of the genus Anisakis Dujardin, 1845 are marine parasites with a worldwide distribution, so far the genus includes six species of heteroxenous parasites (Bao et al., 2022). Among the members of the above genus, Anisakis simplex (s.s.) is the most frequent and extensively investigated species and the dominant species in northern North Eastern Atlantic waters (Mattiucci et al., 2018). A recent bibliometric analysis study was published by Aydemir et al. (2024) and a review on the risk factors of Anisakidosis to humans at the global level appeared in Fujisawa et al. (2025). The infective larval stage of A. simplex (s.s.) can be found within the flesh of several sea fish species and cephalopods, and the consumption of infected raw or undercooked seafood may lead to anisakidosis which is a zoonotic disease (Cipriani et al., 2024a; Mattiucci et al., 2018). Anisakidosis is still significantly underreported and misdiagnosed globally, making it a disease of rising public health concerns (Fujisawa et al., 2025; Shamsi and Barton, 2023). The Atlantic mackerel, Scomber scombrus ranks among the most valuable fish species in Europe, but systematic and concerted epidemiological studies of zoonotic parasites especially Anisakis species in this fish from European fishing grounds have been largely lacking (Levsen et al., 2018).

Concerning fish paratenic hosts of Anisakis spp., the larvae invade various tissues and organs of the animal, some frequent sites of infection are the digestive tract, gonads, liver, somatic musculature, peritoneum and body cavity (Cipriani et al., 2025; Moravec, 1994). Anisakid species larvae reside encapsulated in or on the visceral organs of the fish and often several larvae may migrate from the viscera into the host flesh (Cipriani et al., 2024a, Cipriani et al., 2024b; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021a, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2025). Larval migration of A. simplex in three fish paratenic hosts, Clupea harengus, Scomber scombrus and Micromesistius poutassou from NE Atlantic fishing areas was detected by experimental assay and results revealed that both intra-vitam migration and post-mortem migration of parasite from the viscera into the flesh is related to different storage time and temperature (Cipriani et al., 2024b).

The main goals of this study were to highlight the immunohistopathological response of S. scombrus organs against A. simplex (s.s.) larvae and verify wherther the host or the parasite has more success.

Materials and methods

Fish

Samples of Atlantic mackerel, S. scombrus (N = 41, of which 17 females and 24 males) were obtained during research cruises in March 2025 from the Bay of Biscay (45°30′N 4°24′W, FAO Zone 27 VIIIB). Fish were processed immediately after catch on board the vessel, they received a lethal dose of the anaesthetic MS222 (tricaine methanesulphonate, Sandoz, Basel, Switzerland), and after severing the spinal cord, were weighed, measured and sexed. Total length of fish ranged from 30.00 to 44.50 cm (36.76 ± 0.58, mean ± standard error), body weight ranged from 200 to 738 g (398.68 ± 17.91, mean ± standard error).

Fish dissection and histological processes

Each specimen was opened ventrally and the stomach, pyloric caeca, intestine, liver, gonads, spleen, kidney and flesh were examined first by visual inspection then under stereomicroscope for the occurrence of anisakid larvae, and photographed.

All visceral organs, after visual inspection were removed and put in separate Petri dishes in order to search for parasites and examined under a dissecting microscope. With reference to the gonads, after recording the number of larvae, the organs were opened longitudinally and observed under a stereomicroscope in order to search for nematodes located internally. After removal of the visceral organs, the skeletal muscle was cut in very thin fillets including the belly flaps and observed first by visual inspection, and then under a stereomicroscope. With reference to detection of parasites in host flesh, authors assume that a slight underestimation of the number of larvae present. A subsample of 43 calcified larvae were isolated from fish organs and dissected by tweezers and needles under stereomicroscope to verify the contents.

Pieces of infected organs (approx. 15 × 15 mm) containing anisakid larvae, were excised, and then fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin for 24 h. Afterwards, the formalin was substituted with 70 % ethanol and the samples were prepared by means of routine histological techniques. Each organ sample was embedded in paraffin wax and cut. Multiple 5 μm sections were taken from each tissue block, stained with either Alcian Blue- Haematoxylin and Eosin (AB/HE), Alcian Blue 8 GX pH 2.5 and Periodic Acid Schiff's (AB/PAS), Masson's Trichrome and Giemsa, examined and photographed using a Nikon Microscope ECLIPSE 80i (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistics on epidemiological data

As data on the number of parasites did not satisfy the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances even after being transformed, non-parametric statistics were applied. The Mann Whitney Test was used to compare the total numbers of larvae (live + calficied) between male and female hosts and the Wilcoxon test for paired data was applied to identify differences in the number of calcified parasites and live ones. The relationship between intensity of infection, including live and calcified larvae, and mackerel size was investigated by linear regression after square root transformation of the numbers of parasite larvae. All analyses were performed in RStudio software. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Molecular characterization for parasite species identification

Genomic DNA was extracted from 15 live nematodes isolated from 5 different S. scombrus specimens, that had been cleaned from host tissue by tweezers and needles in a Petri dish with saline solution then stored in 96 % (v/v) ethanol using the NucleoSpin DNA Insect kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany), quantified by microspectrophotometer (Bio-Spec Nano, Shimadzu) and analysed by gel electrophoresis. PCR amplifications of the mtDNA Cox2 gene were performed in accordance with Mattiucci et al. (2014) using the primers 211F and 210R (Table 1). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was carried out at the following conditions: 94 °C for 3 min, 35 cycles at 94 °C for 40 s, 46 °C for 40 s, 72 °C for 7 min, followed by post-amplification at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR amplifications for the genomic rDNA region (spanning the ITS-1, the 5.8S subunit and the ITS-2) were performed with primers (Table 1) as described by D'Amelio et al. (2000). PCR amplification conditions were: 94 °C for 3 min, 35 cycles at 94 °C for 40 s, 58 °C for 40 s, 72 °C for 60 s, 72 °C for 7 min. PCR fragments were analysed by gel electrophoresis, purified and sequenced at BMR Genomics (Padua, Italy) by Sanger sequencing using specific primers (Table 1). All calcified larvae encysted on visceral organs were tightly encircled by fish cells and it was impossible to separate host and parasite tissues. Due to this evident parasite contamination, molecular investigations did not include this group of larvae. Therefore, all calcified larvae might be considered putative A. simplex (s.s.).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For (TEM), several pieces mostly up to 7 × 7 mm in size of all infected tissues-organs were fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 3 h at 4 °C. The fixed tissues were post-fixed in 1 % osmium tetroxide for 2 h, rinsed, and stored in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer containing 6 % sucrose for 48 h. Thereafter, the tissue pieces were dehydrated using a graded acetone series and embedded in epoxy resin (Durcupan ACM, Fluka). Semi-thin sections (1.5 μm) were cut using a Reichert Om U2 ultramicrotome and stained with toluidine blue. Ultra-thin sections (90 nm) were stained with 4 % uranyl acetate solution in 50 % ethanol and Reynold's lead citrate and then examined using a Talos L120C transmission electron microscope.

For both light microscopy and TEM, corresponding pieces of visceral organs and flesh of all 5 uninfected fish were also processed so that a direct comparison with the parasitized material could be made.

Results

Parasite epidemiological data

Quantitative infection assessment focused on nematode prevalence and mean intensity as defined in Bush et al. (1997). The epidemiological parameters considered were: prevalence (P%), mean intensity (mI) with standard error (±SE) and range of infection (min – max). Of 41 specimens of Atlantic Mackerel S. scombrus (17 females and 24 males), 36 were infected (prevalence 88 %). The intensity of infection ranged from 5 to 650 larvae per fish (86.97 ± 1817.26, mean ± standard error) and did not differ significantly between male and female mackerel (W = 173, p > 0.05). The number of nematode larvae was positively correlated with the mackerel total length (R2adj = 0.17, p < 0.01) and weight (R2adj = 0.09, p < 0.05).

In the vast majority of infected mackerel, co-occurrence of live and calcified larvae of anisakid in visceral organs of the same host was documented. A total of 3131 larvae were counted of which 1858 were calcified. The percentage of calcified larvae in each infected mackerel ranged from 23 % to 100 % of the total larvae with the mean number of calcified larvae significantly higher than that of live larvae (W = 90, p < 0.01).

Anisakid species identification

PCR fragments derived from Cox2 and rDNA loci were obtained and sequenced from 15 live intact larvae collected in 5 different S. scombrus specimens. All the fragments derived from the mithocondrial Cox2 locus were identical among each other (one representative sequence was deposited in GenBank with PX267000 accession number) and their sequence matched >99.6 % with sequences previously deposited in GenBank from Anisakis simplex (s.s.) (for Cox2 for example LT883270). On the other hand, their similarity was lower (<96 %) with sequences from A. pegreffii Cox2. The sequence analysis of the 15 PCR fragments derived from the genomic rDNA locus (one representative sequence was deposited in GenBank with the PX233707 accession number) matched >99.6 % with sequences previously deposited in GenBank from A. simplex (s.s.) (for example PV523525). 13 out of the 15 sequences obtained contained the A. simplex characteristic nucleotides (Ts) at the positions described by Costa et al. (2016), while only two PCR sequences appeared to contain one A. simplex/A. pegreffii heterozygous nucleotidic position (T and C) at the first position described by Costa et al. (2016). Taken together these data would suggest that all the parasite samples studied belong to the A. simplex species.

Parasitological examination and histopathology

After gutting the Atlantic mackerel, a variable number of live and calcified worms co-occurred on the surface of the visceral organs or were encysted partially inside them (Figs. 1A-D). The serosa covering the host's digestive tract was one of most infected regions of host body (Figs. 1A-C), less infected organs were the liver (Fig. 1D) and to a considerably lesser degree spleen and flesh. No parasites were encountered in the kidney. It should be noted that in each host, in all of the infected organs (with the exception of flesh) a variable number of larvae were calcified (Figs. 1D, 2A-C): the percentage of calcified larvae in each infected mackerel ranged from 23 to 100 % of the total larvae. During visual inspection and then under the stereomicroscope, the majority of the A. simplex (s.s.) larvae on the surface of the visceral organs appeared as flat, tight spirals with a diameter measuring from 2.5 to 6 mm (Figs. 1A-D, 2B-C). Only in 8 S. scombrus, were anisakid larvae (from 1 to 5) observed to be encysted in the flesh (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. A.2E-F). The occurrence of many larvae in the serosa of the fish intestine and the differences in size and color between live and calcified nematode larvae is also evident (Figs. 1D, 2B-C). Moreover, co-occurrence of live and calcified larvae over and inside the mesenteries which surround the female and male gonads is appreciable (Fig. 2C). Live larvae appeared transparent in color and with higher dimensions, whereas, calcified larvae were much smaller in size and intense beige in color (Figs. 2A-C). In some parasitized gonads of males and females, hemorrhagic signs were noticed and the occurrence of free live larvae on the surface of organs was very common (Figs. 1C, D).

Observations of the histological sections revealed the interface region between parasite larvae and host organs, for instance, in some gonads, the larvae were encysted deep inside the organ very close to the oocytes and spermatocytes and substituted gonadic tissue (respectively, Fig. 3A, E) or in proximity to the surface of the organ (Figs. 3B, C). In female infected gonads, recruitment of numerous mast cells (MCs), macrophages, macrophage aggregates (MAs) and rodlet cells (RCs) in close proximity to the Anisakis simplex (s.s.) larva was documented, often some of these cells co-occurred together (e.g., MCs-MAs, Figs. 3B-C, MCs-RCs, Fig. 3D). Recruitment of the MCs and MAs is visible around the calcified larva attached to the surface of the female gonad (Fig. 3C). Conversely, in uninfected female gonads the number of immune cell types was very low (Supplementary Fig. A.1C).

A. simplex (s.s.) larvae also infected male gonads and in the site of infection, the parasite replaced the gonadic tissue (Fig. 3E), nevertheless, in adjacent areas, the organ appeared intact with numerous spermatocytes and spermatozoa (Supplementary Fig. A.1F). In some parasitized male gonads, live and calcified larvae (Fig. 3E) were found on the outer surface of the testis and in the interface region, the presence of very few RCs was noticed. Indeed, near worms encysted inside the testis, the occurrence of numerous MCs in mesenteries was documented (Fig. 3F). Nonetheless, the calcified larvae in testis were encircled by MCs and clusters of MAs (Supplementary Fig. A.1E). In parasitized testis, near the nematode site of attachment, the gonad presented a normal aspect with numerous spermatocytes and mature spermatozoa (Supplementary Fig. A.1F).

Dissection under the stereomicroscope of a subpopulation of calcified larvae isolated from different organs revealed the presence of undefinable hard material (Supplementary Fig. A.1E) and in most cases the material was hard when touched with tweezers.

In all infected visceral organs, live and calcified larvae were surrounded by granulomas in which epithelioid cells, fibroblasts, collagen fibres, MCs, macrophages and MAs were recognized (Figs. 4A-F). In many infected individuals, nematode larvae occurred on the surface and between the pyloric caeca which were interposed by the pancreatic tissue (Fig. 4A), around the parasite numerous MCs were documented (Fig. 4B). In some instances, the nematode with apical part of the body was encysted inside the caecum and near the granuloma, presence of high number of MCs and MAs was encountered (Fig. 4C). Frequently, the larvae were found on the surface of the intestine (Fig. 4D). As it was mentioned earlier, pyloric caeca and the intestine with its three regions were among the most infected organs of the Atlantic mackerel (respectively Figs. 4A- C and D). MCs were numerous in the different layers of the infected intestine (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Fig. A.1 A) and calcified larva on intestinal surface was also encircled by MCs and macrophages (Supplementary Fig. A.2B).

The liver was one of less infected organs, live and calcified larvae were encysted on the surface and inside the liver and were encircled by granulomas (Figs. 4E, F). The type of innate immune cells within the granuloma and outside were the same as in other parasitised organs. The parasitized liver lost its normal architecture, hepatocytes had eccentric nuclei with cytoplasmic rarefaction and cell swelling (Fig. 4D), conversely in uninfected livers the normal feature of the organ were observed, accordingly, the hepatocytes had spherical central nuclei with no rarefaction of the cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. A.2C). Histological sections of several calcified larvae isolated from different organs confirmed the presence of undefinable material encountered during dissection (Fig. 4F, Supplementary Fig. A.2B).

In 8 Atlantic mackerels, from 1 to 5 live A. simplex (s.s.) larvae were encysted in the host's flesh (Supplementary Fig. A.2E). Parasite-induced interruption of branches of the myofibres of the skeletal muscle (Figs. 4G, H, Supplementary Fig. A.2F) and around the nematode intense MCs recruitment and occurrence of several macrophages were observed (Figs. 4G, H). In contrast, in uninfected muscle, the normal aspects of myofibres were visible with no innate immune cells scattered among them (Supplementary Fig. A.2D).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Light microscopical observations of all infected organs revealed that the granulomas around A. simplex (s.s.) larvae presented very similar features. Accordingly, in both male and female infected gonads, the granuloma around the A. simplex (s.s.) larva consisted of three layers (Fig. 5A). An inner layer in close vicinity to the parasite body was made up by epithelioid cells, frequently these cells were in rarefaction. MCs and fibroblasts were abundant in the middle layer and among them collagen fibres were scattered (Figs. 5A-C). The external layer was often constituted by macrophages, some fibroblasts and both were scattered in a loose mesh of collagen fibres, frequently the outer part of the granuloma was in contact with the surface of the mesenteries which surround and connect the gonads to the abdominal wall (Fig. 5A). Within the mesenteries which encircle the female gonad, sometimes MCs and macrophages were visible (Fig. 5C). These two cell types as documented with light microscopy were very close to the oocyte (Fig. 5D), in some instances, the MC granules were in rarefaction (Fig. 5E). MC appeared as big round-shape cells, with cytoplasm containing numerous small electron-dense granules (Figs. 5B, E), rarely some electron-lucent vesicles also occurred, with an intact nucleus with peripheral heterochromatin. RCs were also noticed inside the mesenteries and frequently in its periphery (Fig. 5F). Rodlet cell in some instances presented apoptotic nucleus with condensed electron-dense chromatin, indeed, the cytoplasm was vacuolated and rodlets were small in size and uncommon in aspect (Fig. 5F).

Discussion

Anisakidae species infect numerous marine fish in different regions of the world (Mattiucci et al., 2018). As a genus, Anisakis is found world-wide, but Anisakis spp. are differentially distributed geographically and utilize different host species and these species are important due to their medical, veterinary and environmental significance (Fujisawa et al., 2025; Levsen and Berland, 2012; Shamsi and Barton, 2023). The results provide evidence on the presence of Anisakis larvae in Atlantic mackerel. A total of 15 live larvae, as rappresentative of all live nematodes, were utilized for molecular characterization and attributed to A. simplex (s.s.).

Numerous accounts have been published on infection due to A. simplex (s.s.) larvae in visceral organs and musculature of several fish species (Cipriani et al., 2024a, Cipriani et al., 2025; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021a, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2025) but to date, there is no systematic and concerted epidemiological investigation of zoonotic parasites such as Anisakis species in Atlantic mackerel from European fishing grounds (Levsen et al., 2018). Thus, the current study was designed to begin an assessment on occurrence of A. simplex (s.s.) larvae in S. scombrus from Bay of Biscay and to document the immune response in different infected organs.

High prevalence and mean abundance of Anisakis spp. in Atlantic mackerel was recorded by (Cipriani et al., 2024b). S. scombrus from Atlantic fishing grounds showed markedly higher Anisakis spp. infection levels than their Mediterranean congeners (Levsen et al., 2018). Furthermore, a positive relationship between the intensity of infection of A. simplex (s.s.) and Atlantic mackerel size was reported in Abaunza et al. (1995), whereas, some papers found no relationship between A. simplex (s.s.) infection and Atlantic mackerel size or even a negative correlation (e.g., Levsen and Berland, 2012).

Our results on the low presence of larvae in host flesh tally with the suggestion provided by Smith and Wootten (1975), namely at the first hours of capture the worms did not have enough time to migrate to host muscle. Furthermore, also in S. scombrus from the Atlantic Portuguese coast and from 5 different geographical sites in NE Atlantic the mean intensity of infection by Anisakis spp. in fish fillet was 1 and around 2 larvae (respectively Santos et al., 2017; Levsen et al., 2018). Current data on low intensity of A. simplex (s.s.) larvae in host fillet are in agreement with above mentioned records. Nonetheless, we consider the results on infected flesh could be slightly underestimated due to the method of inspection.

Herein we found the number of calcified larvae was significantly greater than live ones and in some hosts, only calcified larvae were observed. Moreover, different types of host innate immune cells were documented in close proximity or on surface of the putative A. simplex (s.s.) calcified larvae as well as near live A. simplex (s.s.) L3. Such observations might lead to the following hypotheses on calcified larvae: (i) there was a good efficacy of S. scombrus immune response against them; (ii) these larvae were the older ones and host immune cells had enough time to sequester and calcify them.

With reference to the effects of nematodes on fish reproductive organs, a range of disorders (e.g., hemorrhage, inflammation, edema, pre-necrotic and necrotic changes and follicular atresia), which resulted in poor development of oocytes and affected host fecundity, have been observed (Hine and Anderson, 1981). Conversely, no significant correlation between A. simplex larvae intensities and Scomber australasicus gonad development was encountered, accordingly infection status did not interfere with host fertility despite the high number of parasite larvae observed on fish gonads (Chou et al., 2011). Our results concur with data of Chou et al. (2011), because all infected fish were mature individuals with a high quantity of gametes in their gonads.

Several accounts dealt with adverse health effects of larval nematodes on marine fish species (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2025). Formation of granulomas on the surface of internal organs or in the skeletal musculature, is a common response to larval nematodes in fish (López-Verdejo et al., 2025; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021a, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024). Granulomas are focal chronic inflammatory lesions that appear as nodules in tissues and organs of the host (Ferguson, 2006; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024). Cellular immune responses in fish generally involve the following cell components: epithelioid cells (Gauthier et al., 2004; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2025), macrophages and macrophage aggregates (MAs)(Agius and Roberts, 2003; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021a, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2025), fibroblasts (Gauthier et al., 2004; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024), neutrophils (Speirs et al., 2024), mast cells (MCs) (Reite and Evensen, 2006; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b), and rodlet cells (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024). Most of above cells were noticed in infected organs of S. scombrus, we will therefore examine in turn each type of cell involved in the response to A. simplex (s.s.).

The inner layer of the granuloma nearest to the pathogen mainly formed dark necrotic epithelioid cells and non-necrotic epithelioid cells formed desmosomes with each other (Guathier et al., 2004; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b). Epithelioid cells, so called because of their histologic resemblance to true epithelial cells, are transformed macrophages (Ferguson, 2006; Guathier et al., 2004). In the present study, epithelioid cells documented in granulomas in different infected organs presented the same features noticed in other fish-nematode systems (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021a, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024).

Macrophages and their precursor monocytes are known as primary phagocytic cells, and key innate immune cells in vertebrates, indeed, they are crucial to bridging the innate and adaptive arms of the vertebrate immune response (Speirs et al., 2024). Macrophages are large cells with an irregular outline and frequently contain pigments like melanin, lipofuscin and hemosiderin (Agius and Roberts, 2003; Stosik et al., 2019) and can be organized in groups known as melano-macrophage centers or macrophage aggregates (MAs)(Agius and Roberts, 2003; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021a, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b). Our results on the presence of macrophages and MAs within the thickness of the granuloma around the A. simplex (s.s.) in all parasitized organs of Atlantic mackerel tally with similar accounts on infected organs of other fish species (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021a, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b).

Within the granulomas on infected organs of S. scombrus, presence of fibroblasts scattered among other immune cells and collagen fibres was documented. Fibroblast layers in the granuloma aligns with a known process in the development of a chronic inflammation (Ferguson, 2006; López-Verdejo et al., 2025) and could suggest that encapsulation and isolation of high number of live nematode larvae might be a strategy of the host to reduce the effects of parasite (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024).

Neutrophils are among the first cell types to arrive at the site of infection, are involved in the inflammatory process during the initial period of pathogen challenge (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b). Neutrophils have an essential role in the innate immune defence against all types of pathogenic microorganisms (Buchmann, 2022). Based upon this statement and the fact that a nematode larva is much bigger in size than a microorganism, scarce presence of neutrophils in infected organs of S. scombrus might appear reasonable.

Mast cells (MCs) comprise a heterogeneous cell population and are important components of adaptive and innate immunity and have their origin from CD34+pluripotent progenitor cells which reside in the bone marrow (Wang et al., 2024). MCs release a range of inflammatory mediators as discussed by Sayyaf Dezfuli et al. (2024). In the current study, an impressive number of the MCs was documented in infected gonads, intestine and flesh of S. scombrus in close proximity to the A. simplex (s.s.) larvae. Evidence for the role of MCs tryptase in different fish-parasite systems reinforces its active role in tissue repair after worm damage (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b).

Rodlet cells (RCs) are unique cells exclusive to teleosts and are found in different types of tissues (Reite and Evesen, 2006; Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b). RCs are a type of immune cell closely associated with other piscine inflammatory cells, such as epithelioid cells, mesothelial cells and MCs (Reite and Evesen, 2006). Herein, occurrence of the RCs was noticed mainly as a cluster in female infected gonads. Nonetheless, evidence on their defensive function as part of the innate immune system continue to grow (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021b, Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024).

The present study demonstrated that in all infected organs of Atlantic mackerel, A. simplex (s.s.) live larvae and putative A. simplex (s.s.) calcified larvae were encircled by granulomas which might be considered to act as a protective barrier for fish to impede further damage to its organs (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2024). Accordingly, the occurrence of different types of the immune cells in granulomas around all larvae reveals important ongoing activity of the immune response in chronic inflamed organs due to anisakid larvae.

Conclusion

In our previous investigations on other fish-anisakid systems like flounder-Anisakis simplex (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2025), blue whiting-A. simplex (Sayyaf Dezfuli et al., 2021a), brown trout-A. simplex (Kumas et al., 2025), we did not encounter high numbers of calcified nematode larvae as observed in the present study. This result suggests that S. scombrus is more successful in its ability to overcome the impact of the parasite with respect to other host species.
