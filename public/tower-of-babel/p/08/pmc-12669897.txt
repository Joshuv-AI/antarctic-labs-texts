Systematic review and meta-analysis of brain neuroimaging abnormalities in COVID-19 patients and survivors
Authors: Peng Li, Shuyu Ni, Xiao Lin, Zengbo Ding, Na Zeng, Yimiao Zhao, Huan Mei, Xuan Chen, Nan Gao, Hanliang Wei, Tong Li, Yingbo Yang, Beini Yang, Ye Tian, Norimichi Hara, Tao Wang, Jinyuan Zhang, Wei Yan, Junliang Yuan, Ying Han, Kai Yuan, Le Shi, Jie Shi, Yanping Bao, Lin Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccumulating evidence indicates that COVID-19 may cause neurological complications detectable on brain imaging. Yet, the overall prevalence, modality-specific characteristics, and clinical implications of these neuroimaging abnormalities have not been systematically summarized through comprehensive quantitative synthesis.MethodsWe searched the PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane Library, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases, and Wanfang for original articles published up to August 5, 2025. The pooled proportions of brain-imaging findings on computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and electroencephalography (EEG), including hemorrhage, microbleeds, ischemia, stroke, encephalitis, background activity abnormality, periodic or rhythmic activity, and epileptiform discharge, were estimated using a random-effects model. This study was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines.ResultsEighty-three eligible studies that included 9466 COVID-19 patients were included in the meta-analysis. Pooled results from 27 studies, including 3081 patients, showed that more than two-fifths (42.60%) of patients who underwent CT/MRI had objective brain abnormalities. The most frequently reported abnormalities on CT/MRI were changes in white matter and non-specific stroke. Twenty-five EEG studies, including 1273 patients, reported epileptiform discharges in one-fifth (20.54%) of cases. The systematic review of long-term brain imaging manifestations in COVID-19 survivors also found common changes in brain microstructure and function.ConclusionWhile these findings offer insights into the potential pathological mechanisms of neuroimaging abnormalities in COVID-19 patients, the high heterogeneity and variability across studies highlight the need for cautious interpretation. It will be necessary to conduct large-scale longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods in order to validate these neuroimaging findings and clarify the long-term neuropsychiatric consequences of COVID-19.Keywords: COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2, CT, MRI, EEG, brain, imaging

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 19 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has infected 613 million people and caused more than 6.5 million deaths globally (Zhou et al., 2020; Johns Hopkins University, 2022). Emerging evidence indicates that neurological sequelae may persist well beyond the acute phase of COVID-19, with recent longitudinal studies reporting cognitive deficits and microstructural brain changes (Guan et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2020; Han et al., 2021) detectable months after infection. COVID-19 has also been proposed to target central neural systems (Desforgeset al., 2014; Prakash et al., 2021). Given the well-documented neurological manifestations of COVID-19—including headache, nausea, vomiting, myalgia, dizziness, chemosensory disturbances (hypogeusia/hyposmia), and impaired consciousness—research evidence indicates that SARS-CoV-2 infection leads to central nervous system (CNS) dysfunction (Ali et al., 2020; Mao et al., 2020; Han et al., 2021; Misra et al., 2021). Long-term neurological sequelae, including brain fog, ischemic stroke, seizures, cognitive impairment, and Moyamoya disease, have also been reported in COVID-19 survivors (Ellul et al., 2020; Shelley, 2020; Boldrini et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021).

Previous studies investigated the characteristics and possible mechanisms of acute symptoms and sequelae in the nervous system in COVID-19 patients (Rogers et al., 2021). The number of studies on objective brain imaging findings related to COVID-19 continues to increase (Hemasian and Ansari, 2020; Moriguchi et al., 2020; Poyiadji et al., 2020; Reichard et al., 2020). Four reviews summarized brain abnormalities, including seizures (Asadi-Pooya et al., 2021; Kubota et al., 2021) cerebral hemorrhage, infarction, and encephalitis/encephalopathy (Choi and Lee, 2020; Kandemirli et al., 2020) using computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and electroencephalography (EEG). Despite these important findings, several critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the prevalence, progression trajectories, and clinical significance of COVID-19-related neurological abnormalities. Current research faces several major limitations: (i) substantial heterogeneity in imaging protocols across studies complicates cross-study comparisons; (ii) published reviews only focused on one imaging technique and were usually based on studies with relatively small sample sizes; and (iii) The sensitivities of these imaging methods varied across outcomes, and the minimal comparative analyses with non-COVID respiratory infections obscure the specificity of the observed neuropathological patterns This systematic review and meta-analysis address these critical gaps by conducting a comprehensive synthesis of multi-modal neuroimaging evidence (CT, MRI, EEG) from 93 studies encompassing 10 176 patients. We aim to provide robust pooled estimates of abnormality prevalence across different imaging modalities and establish evidence-based recommendations for standardized clinical neuroimaging protocols to improve diagnostic consistency and patient management. Through this multifaceted approach, our work tried to advance the understanding of the neurological impact of COVID-19 while offering practical guidance for clinicians and researchers.

Methods

The present review was conducted using parameters consistent with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The study was registered in PROSPERO (registration no. CRD42022322501).

Search strategy and selection criteria

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we searched PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure databases (CNKI) databases, and Wanfang for studies published before August 5, 2025. The following search terms were used: ((Coronavirus) OR (COVID-19) OR (SARS-CoV-2) OR (severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2)) AND ((EEG) OR (Encephalograph) OR (Encephalography) OR (Encephalographic) OR (Encephalogram) OR (CT) OR (computerized tomography) OR (computed tomography) OR (DWI) OR (diffusion weighted imaging) OR (SWI) OR (susceptibility weighted imaging) OR (DTI) OR (diffusion tensor imaging) OR (MRI) OR (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) OR (Magnetic Resonance Angiography) OR (fMRI) OR (Functional magnetic resonance imaging)) AND (brain). The entire search strategy is shown in Appendix 1.

The screening of titles and abstracts was performed by two of the authors independently based on the following eligibility criteria: (i) population (COVID-19 patients and survivors under brain imaging), (ii) brain imaging (CT, MRI, and EEG), (iii) outcomes [for CT and MRI, any abnormalities, including hemorrhage, microbleeds, ischemia, stroke, encephalitis, and white matter abnormalities; for EEG, any abnormalities, including background activity abnormalities, generalized periodic discharge (GPD), lateralized periodic discharge (LPD), generalized rhythmic delta activity (GRDA), lateralized rhythmic delta activity (LRDA), epileptiform discharge (ED), seizure, and status epilepticus (SE)], (iv) study design (cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, and case series studies with sample sizes >5), (v) language (English), and (vi) non-gray literature (excluding preprints with full text available). The full texts were reviewed in a second screening. We excluded reviews, case reports, letters, and studies without available full text or data. If the same sample was used in more than one publication, then only the dataset with the most comprehensive information was included to avoid data duplication in the meta-analysis.

Data extraction and quality evaluation

Three researchers independently extracted data using standardized formats. Any disagreement was resolved with a third researcher. The following information was extracted from the included studies: (i) basic information (first author, year published, country of origin, survey time, and study design), (ii) demographic and clinical characteristics [patient type, mean age with standard deviation (SD) or interquartile range (IQR), sex distribution, the severity of patients, sample size, imaging modality, imaging specifications, and the number of neuroradiologist reviewers involved in diagnosis), and (iii) outcomes (a proportion or a positive number of brain imaging findings meeting our inclusion criteria and location of findings if available). Severe COVID-19 was defined as meeting the diagnosis guidelines (from the World Health Organization or government), ICU admission, having critical performance (e.g. acute respiratory distress syndrome), or requirement for other special treatment (e.g. oxygen therapy and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation). If more than one brain imaging technique was used in one study, we only included results with the largest sample size unless there was no inclusion relationship between samples.

To assess study quality, we used the 9-star Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for cohort studies and Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality methodology checklist for cross-sectional studies. Two reviewers independently performed quality assessments of the included studies by consensus.

Statistical analysis

We calculated the pooled proportion and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of each brain imaging finding among COVID-19 patients from all included studies using an inverse variance method. A random-effects model was used, depending on high heterogeneities across studies (evaluated by I2, I-squared is a measure of heterogeneity in a meta-analysis, indicating the percentage of variation across studies that is due to heterogeneity rather than chance). It is important to note that we only included studies that report an overall abnormal proportion when estimating the CT/MRI abnormalities and EEG abnormalities (i.e. a study that reported the rates of hemorrhage and ischemia alone would not be included for this outcome).

We performed several subgroup analyses and meta-regressions to explore potential sources of high heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were performed based on the mean or median age of patients (<60 years vs. ≥60 years), sex ratio (male <50% vs. ≥50%), proportion of severe COVID-19 patients (<50% vs. ≥50%), acute patients and discharged survivors, and income region (low- and middle-income regions vs. high-income regions according to World Bank standards). Proportions of brain imaging findings and their 95% CIs in different subgroups were provided. Primary outcomes (any CT or MRI abnormality, hemorrhagic manifestation, microbleeds, ischemic manifestation, leukoencephalopathy, and encephalitis for CT and MRI; ED, seizure, and SE for EEG) were included in the subgroup analysis unless there was only one study in a subgroup. Meta-regressions of COVID-19 severity (percentage of severe patients), sex ratio (percentage of male patients), the mean or median age of the sample, and proportion of patients with a history of epilepsy (only in EEG studies) were conducted for primary outcomes. Publication bias for each outcome was assessed by funnel plots and Egger’s test. We also excluded each of the studies from the pooled estimate to identify the influence of individual studies on pooled estimates as sensitivity analyses. All analyses were performed using R 4.0.5 software and the meta package, version 5.0-1.

Results

Literature search

An overview of the selection process of studies from the literature search is depicted in Fig. 1. The initial literature search identified 31 600 articles (18 206 from PubMed, 6023 from Scopus, 2284 from Web of Science, 133 from Cochare library, 39 from Embase, 4532 from CNKI, and 383 from Wanfang). After removing duplicates and screening the titles and abstracts, 289 full-text articles were screened for eligibility. In total, 196 studies were excluded after full-text screening (28 case reports/series, 12 reviews, four meeting abstracts, two letters, 111 articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria, 37 articles with irrelevant content, and two non-English articles). A total of 93 studies met the eligibility criteria. Eighty-three studies of COVID-19 patients in the acute phase were included in the meta-analysis, and 10 studies of long-term brain imaging findings in COVID-19 survivors were summarized in the systematic review.

Flow chart of the study selection.

Among 83 studies in the meta-analysis, a total of 9466 patients were included in the final analysis. A total of 8193 patients from 58 studies were included for CT/MRI, and 1273 patients from 25 studies were included for EEG. Among 58 studies with CT/MRI data, 31 reported hemorrhagic manifestations, 27 reported any abnormality, 20 reported microbleeds, 18 reported ischemic manifestations, 14 reported encephalitis, and seven reported leukoencephalopathies. In addition to these, 10 specific outcomes were also included: subarachnoid hemorrhage (17 studies), ischemic stroke (17 studies), acute/subacute infarct and ischemia (14 studies), parenchymal hemorrhage (eight studies), subdural hemorrhage (nine studies), non-specific white matter lesions (nine studies), white matter microbleeds (eight studies), hemorrhagic stroke (seven studies), non-specific stroke (five studies), and chronic infarct and ischemia (four studies). In the 25 EEG studies, 12 outcomes were reported: ED (18 studies), seizure (16 studies), abnormal background activity (15 studies), GPD (15 studies), SE (14 studies), any abnormality (13 studies), general slowing (12 studies), GRDA (11 studies), focal slowing (11 studies), discontinuity/burst suppression or attenuation (10 studies), LPD (nine studies), and LRDA (eight studies). These studies covered 22 countries, including the USA (28 studies), France (14 studies), Germany (three studies), Italy (seven studies), Iran (four studies), Mexico (four studies), Brazil (three studies), India (four studies), China (five studies), Egypt (four studies), Spain (two studies), Canada (two studies), Turkey (five studies), UK (two studies), Pakistan, Costa Rica, Japan, Poland, Switzerland, Saudi Arabia, Croatia, and Sweden (one study each). Most studies were retrospective and case series. The sample sizes ranged from eight (Pilato et al., 2020) to 697 (Yea et al., 2024), and the mean or median age ranged from 35 months (range, 3–138 months) (Li et al., 2024) to 73.3 years (SD = 14.9 years) (Jegatheeswaran et al., 2021). Nearly all of these studies included more males than females, except seven (Alonazi et al., 2021; CORAZZA et al., 2021; Günbey et al., 2021; Kelsch et al., 2021; Usta and Bulut, 2022; Bosak et al., 2023; Nada et al., 2024) (Table 1). Details of studies included in the meta-analysis are summarized in Table S2, and the characteristics of the 10 studies included in the systematic review are shown in Table S3.

CT and MRI findings in COVID-19 patients

Figure 2 shows pooled estimations of multiple abnormalities of imaging performance, including hemorrhagic manifestations, ischemic manifestations, nonspecific stroke, microbeads, white matter changes/leukoencephalopathy, and encephalitis on brain CT and/or MRI neuroimaging examinations. In 27 of the included studies, 42.60% (95% CI, 31.35–54.27%; I2 = 97.3%) of the COVID-19 patients exhibited at least one abnormality. The pooled proportion of hemorrhagic manifestations was 10.41% (31 studies; 95% CI, 7.37–13.91%; I2 = 93.7%), and the pooled proportion of microbleeds was 11.80% (20 studies; 95% CI, 4.61–21.73%; I2 = 94.5%). Ischemic manifestations were found in 15.02% of patients (95% CI, 8.49–23.00%; I2 = 93.7%) from 18 studies. A total of 30.44% of patients (seven studies; 95% CI, 13.82–50.27%; I2 = 96.4%) exhibited white matter lesions, and 11.1% of patients (seven studies; 95% CI, 5.13–18.99%; I2 = 84.9%) exhibited leukoencephalopathy. Encephalitis was reported in 4.43% of patients (14 studies; 95% CI, 1.63–8.53%; I2 = 92.9%).

Proportion of CT/MRI and EEG findings among COVID-19 patients.

We further explored the proportion of several specific manifestations. For hemorrhage, the most frequent finding was hemorrhagic stroke (seven studies; 5.44%, 95% CI, 2.07–10.28%), followed by parenchymal hemorrhage (eight studies; 4.08%, 95% CI, 2.51–6.01%), subarachnoid hemorrhage (17 studies; 1.73%, 95% CI, 1.15–2.43%), and subdural hemorrhage (seven studies; 1.58%, 95% CI, 0.70–2.80%). For ischemia, acute/subacute infarct and ischemia were found in 17.19% of patients (14 studies; 95% CI, 0.99–24.44%). Chronic infection and ischemia were found in 18.86% of patients (four studies; 95% CI, 10.14–29.51%). Ischemic stroke was found in 18.49% of patients (17 studies; 95% CI, 12.28–25.63%). Five studies reported nonspecific stroke in COVID-19 patients, with a pooled proportion of 21.58% (95% CI, 11.02–34.51%), and 15.26% of patients (eight studies; 95% CI, 9.04–22.75%) exhibited white matter microbleeds (Fig. 2). Forest plots and funnel plots of CT/MRI outcomes are shown in the Supplementary Data.

Electroencephalography findings in COVID-19 patients

In 13 studies, the proportion of any EEG abnormality was 93.74 (95% CI, 83.17–99.31%). Abnormal background activity (15 studies; 91.06%, 95% CI, 79.87–97.99%) was common among COVID-19 patients. The pooled proportion of general slowing and focal slowing was 92.81% of patients (12 studies; 95% CI, 80.82–99.20%) and 18.52% of patients (12 studies; 95% CI, 13.53–24.09%). GPD and LPD were found in 17.29% (15 studies; 95% CI, 13.24–21.75%) and 3.49% (nine studies; 95% CI, 1.32–6.63%) of these patients. The pooled proportion of ED was 20.54% of patients (18 studies; 95% CI, 11.52–31.37%). A total of 4.89% of these patients (16 studies; 95% CI, 1.52–10.04%) exhibited seizures, and 2.73% of patients (14 studies; 95% CI, 1.22–4.83%) exhibited SE. Other EEG findings of our meta-analysis are summarized in Fig. 2. Forest plots and funnel plots of EEG outcomes are shown in the Supplementary Data.

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression analysis

A subgroup analysis was only conducted for primary outcomes with at least two studies in each subgroup (Fig. 3). In the subgroup analysis of CT and MRI abnormalities and hemorrhagic manifestations, studies with younger patients (mean age <60 years) reported a significantly higher proportion than studies with a mean age >60 years (75.39% vs. 31.99%, P = 0.01, and 17.89% vs. 9.75%, P = 0.01, respectively). Moreover, the proportion of hemorrhagic manifestations was higher in studies that included more severe patients (severity ≥50%, 15.13% vs. severity <50%, 6.20%, P = 0.04). None of the other subgroup analyses showed a significant difference in each outcome measurement (Figs S11–S13)

Subgroup analyses of primary imaging findings across variables.

The meta-regression suggested that severe COVID-19 was associated with a higher proportion of microbleeds (P = 0.002), and younger age was associated with CT and MRI abnormalities (P < 0.001) and seizures (P < 0.001) on EEG. When more male participants were included in the studies, the proportion of microbleeds (P = 0.01) and ischemic manifestations (P = 0.02) was higher, whereas the proportion of seizures on EEG was lower (P = 0.005). The meta-regression of SE showed that studies with more patients with a history of epilepsy reported a higher proportion of SE (P < 0.001). We did not find any relationship between COVID-19 severity, age, sex ratio, epilepsy history, and other outcomes (Table S4).

Long-term brain imaging sequelae of COVID-19

Impairments in the central and peripheral nervous systems can also lead to long-term neurological sequelae. Cognitive impairment was recently included in the definition of the post-COVID-19 condition as a major symptom (Soriano et al., 2022). A study of 33 COVID-19 patients reported a reduction of cognitive impairment and delta-band EEG connectivity at a follow-up of 10 months (Cecchetti et al., 2022). Another study from Poland investigated the macroscopic and biochemical status of the brain in patients who reported brain fog and found a significantly higher concentration of glutamine in deep cerebral gray matter. Subtle changes in brain metabolites were similar to patients who had an insufficiency of brain blood circulation (Sklinda et al., 2021).

A recent meta-analysis showed that 15% and 10% of survivors had olfactory alterations and gustatory dysfunctions, respectively, 12 months after discharge from the hospital (Zeng et al., 2023). An MRI study reported a decrease in gray matter volume in the olfactory bulb 10–12 months after infection (Güney et al., 2021). Furthermore, a recent study of olfactory MRI, diffusion tensor imaging, and functional MRI found a decrease in olfactory bulb volume and a decrease in white matter tract integrity in olfactory regions in COVID-19-related anosmia, but this was not as pronounced as other post-infectious olfactory dysfunction (Yildirim et al., 2022). A recent study investigated brain changes in 401 cases with a positive diagnosis of COVID-19 from the UK Biobank. A more significant reduction of gray matter thickness and tissue contrast was found in the orbitofrontal cortex and parahippocampal gyrus, which play a role in the processing of olfactory inputs, at an average of 141 days after infection compared with baseline before infection (Douaud et al., 2022). Long-COVID patients exhibited brain hypometabolism in the right parahippocampal gyrus and thalamus based on [18F] fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (Sollini et al., 2021). The reported abnormalities in limbic regions may be the main biological basis of consistently reported post-infection olfactory dysfunction.

In the acute phase of COVID-19, hemorrhagic lesions and extensive and isolated white matter microhemorrhages have frequently been reported. Similar lesions were found in discharged survivors and one multi-organ imaging study (and its brain-focused follow-up) in 51 previously hospitalized COVID-19 patients, suggesting modest abnormalities in T2* (which is commonly used in clinical settings to study diverse pathological conditions, such as traumatic brain injury, stroke, and hemorrhage) of the left and right thalami compared with matched controls (Griffanti et al., 2021). A long-term follow-up imaging cohort study of COVID-19 quantitatively compared data across subjects based on gray matter morphology, cerebral blood flow (CBF), and volumes of white matter tracts. Differences in cortical thickness were dynamic and eventually returned to baseline. For cortical CBF, hypoperfusion in severe cases observed at 3 months tended to recover at 10 months. Changes in subcortical nuclei and white matter showed various trends, including recoverable and long-term unrecovered differences (Tian et al., 2022). These findings support the hypothesis that regional gray matter and white matter microstructure could mediate the relationship between cognitive dysfunction and its pathological sequelae. Still, the trend toward recovery needs to be confirmed in future long-term studies.

In addition to cognitive dysfunction, olfactory alterations, and changes in brain microstructure, brain function also presents some alterations in long-COVID patients. In a study of 19 COVID-19 patients, an increase in the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation values was found in the hippocampus at 1-year follow-up (Du et al., 2022). Functional connectivity analyses revealed a significant decrease in connectivity in the anosognosic group compared with the nosognosic group, within and between the following networks: left default mode, bilateral somatosensory motor, right executive control, right salient ventral attention, and bilateral dorsal attention. A significant decrease in connectivity was also found in the right lobules IV and V of the cerebellum (Voruz et al., 2022). In 42 COVID-19 survivors, widespread lower diffusivity along the main axis of white matter tracts was found, and abnormal functional connectivity among resting-state networks was identified 3 months after COVID-19 (Benedetti et al., 2021). A recent large-scale cohort study utilizing US Veterans Affairs healthcare data (n = 154 068 COVID-19 cases) systematically evaluated 12-month neurological outcomes following SARS-CoV-2 infection. The findings revealed significantly increased risks of multiple post-acute neurological manifestations, including cerebrovascular complications (ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke), cognitive impairment, peripheral neuropathies, episodic neurological disorders (migraine and seizures), movement disorders, psychiatric conditions, and encephalopathy. The composite analysis demonstrated a hazard ratio of 1.42 (95% CI 1.38–1.47) for any neurological sequela, with an attributable burden of 70.69 (95% CI 63.54–78.01) cases per 1000 persons at 1-year follow-up (Xu et al., 2022). However, the underlying neural mechanisms of the reported neurological symptoms are still unclear. Post-COVID syndrome might also result from neuroinflammatory events in the brain (Crook et al., 2021). Another possible cause of brain injury is a higher propensity for blood clotting attributable to infection (Crook et al., 2021).

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

High heterogeneity was found in most analyses, except for a few subgroups. Studies of CT and MRI abnormalities, hemorrhagic stroke, ischemic manifestations, microbleeds, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and acute/subacute infarct and ischemia showed publication bias (Fig. 2). In contrast, studies of other CT and MRI and EEG outcomes did not. Sensitivity analysis showed that the results of primary outcomes were stable (Figs S23–S28)

Discussion

This study systematically elucidates the acute and long-term neurological impacts of COVID-19 through comprehensive multimodal evidence (Fig. 4). Our meta-analysis demonstrates that acute SARS-CoV-2 infection may be associated with neurological changes, with structural abnormalities (CT/MRI) observed in more than 40% of cases and functional disturbances (EEG) in approximately 93% of patients with clinical symptoms. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the high heterogeneity in study designs and imaging protocols. These acute-phase manifestations predominantly involve white matter microstructural damage, cerebrovascular events, and neuroinflammatory responses. Notably, subgroup analyses revealed these effects to be most severe in young male patients with critical disease progression. Longitudinal data demonstrate persistent neurological sequelae at 12-month follow-up, including cognitive impairment, olfactory/gustatory dysfunction, and cerebrovascular sequelae. The underlying neuropathology involves olfactory bulb gray matter atrophy, limbic system metabolic abnormalities, and thalamic–white matter microstructural changes. While emerging evidence suggests that SARS-CoV-2 infection may be linked to both transient and potentially lasting neurological effects, the available data, particularly from longitudinal studies, remain limited and preliminary. Given the methodological constraints and the absence of large-scale, long-term studies, further research is crucial to validate these findings and to fully understand the long-term impact of COVID-19 on cognition, sensory functions, and cerebrovascular health.

The framework of brain neuroimaging abnormalities and neuropsychiatric manifestations in COVID-19 patients and survivors.

Cerebrovascular pathology appears to be a common neurological manifestation of COVID-19, with findings such as hemorrhage (10.41%), microbleeds (11.80%), and ischemia (17.19%) on CT/MRI examinations. The pathogenesis of these vascular complications involves multiple interconnected mechanisms. (i) SARS-CoV-2 infection triggers a systemic inflammatory cascade through immune hyperactivation, leading to cytokine storm syndrome characterized by elevated inteleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and other proinflammatory mediators (Malik et al., 2021). (ii) The virus induces endothelial dysfunction via ACE2 receptor-mediated entry, resulting in widespread microangiopathy, thromboinflammation, and a procoagulant state marked by elevated D-dimer and fibrinogen levels (Connors and Levy, 2020; Baig and Sanders, 2020; Libby and Lüscher, 2020). (iii) Neurovascular unit disruption occurs through blood–brain barrier breakdown, mediated by viral entry into cerebral endothelial cells and subsequent tight junction protein degradation. This can result in both microvascular and macrovascular complications (Guerrero et al., 2021; Caldeira et al., 2023; Faghy and Ashton, 2023). Upon entering the cerebral circulatory system, SARS-CoV-2 may trigger a hypercoagulable state, thereby raising the risk of thrombotic stroke. These pathological processes are further exacerbated by treatment-related factors, particularly in critically ill patients receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation who develop thrombocytopenia and platelet dysfunction (Jiritano et al., 2020). Clinically, these vascular insults manifest as acute neurological symptoms (headache, dizziness, impaired consciousness) and contribute to long-term cognitive sequelae (“brain fog”), with cerebral infarction volume showing particular correlation with persistent cognitive deficits (Kühne et al., 2022). These findings underscore the importance of early neurological monitoring, judicious anticoagulation strategies, and long-term neurocognitive follow-up in COVID-19 patients.

Changes in white matter, including leukoencephalopathy and nonspecific lesions, such as microbleeds, are notable. These findings may reflect acute demyelination processes, with emerging evidence supporting SARS-CoV-2-induced immune-mediated demyelinating disease (Mahalakshmi et al., 2021). Alternative mechanisms include direct viral central nervous system invasion, as demonstrated by detectable viral RNA in cerebrospinal fluid (Espíndola et al., 2021), as well as blood–brain barrier dysfunction and hypoxic injury contributing to cerebral microbleeds and white matter hyperintensities (Caress et al., 2020). Longitudinal studies reveal a dynamic evolution of these white matter changes: while initial reports suggested gradual normalization within 1 year (Huang et al., 2022), recent data indicate persistent abnormalities lasting up to 2 years after infection that correlate with cognitive deficits (Huang et al., 2023). Notably, since leukoencephalopathy constitutes a hallmark of normal brain aging that typically remains asymptomatic, we emphasize the need for future studies to: (i) implement standardized assessment using the Fazekas scale, (ii) provide detailed topographic characterization of white matter lesions, and (iii) incorporate longitudinal neuroimaging with comprehensive neuropsychological evaluations. This approach will enable differentiation between COVID-19-related and age-related white matter changes while facilitating robust meta-analyses. Further research is warranted to elucidate the reversibility of these white matter injuries and their precise clinical correlates.

Abnormal findings on EEG suggest diffuse brain dysfunction among COVID-19 patients. The high proportion of abnormal EEG may be consistent with common neurological manifestations in COVID-19 patients, including fever, headache, and acute confusion/delirium during hospitalization. Epileptiform discharges were present in one-fifth of patients who underwent EEG. Probable causes include acute symptomatic seizure, de novo epileptogenicity, and pre-existing epilepsy with or without exacerbation. Acute symptomatic status epilepticus after ischemic stroke is significantly associated with the long-term risk of epilepsy (with a 10-year risk reaching 81%) (Sinka et al., 2023). COVID-19 may exacerbate cerebral ischemia through vascular endothelial damage, microthrombosis, or hypoxia, thereby triggering epileptic events (Garcao et al., 2023). Systematic reviews that have posed similar clinical questions have also been previously reported (Antony and Haneef, 2020; Roberto et al., 2020; Asadi-Pooya et al., 2021). Although there might be EEG ordering bias in patients with a history of epilepsy, these results still suggest that epilepsy-related symptoms might not be as rare as previously thought. Previous studies reported that the proportion of seizures/seizure-like events and status epilepticus was as low as <1% (Emami et al., 2020; Mao et al., 2020).

Brain imaging findings in the acute phase can undoubtedly continue until after hospital discharge. These abnormalities may be associated with long-term neuropsychiatric sequelae that affect the patient’s overall recovery, a clinical phenomenon now recognized as post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC) or “long COVID.” However, only a few studies have focused on mechanisms and longer-term (e.g. >1 year) outcomes. Besides, the existing studies often focus on survivors with specific symptoms, such as anosmia. Particular attention should be paid to vulnerable populations who may be at increased risk for prolonged neurological sequelae, including older adults, those with pre-existing cerebrovascular disease, and patients experiencing severe acute infections. Our study revealed higher hemorrhage rates in patients under 60 compared to older individuals, probably due to clinical screening biases where older patients received more routine neuroimaging. Emerging evidence (Narayanan et al., 2024) suggests acute neurological complications may contribute to long COVID development. Younger patients with hemorrhagic manifestations could potentially face higher risks of persistent neurological symptoms, though this requires further investigation. Longitudinal studies with longer follow-up time and a more comprehensive view of long-term brain imaging abnormalities and their neuropsychiatric mechanism are still lacking. Future research must also address whether early neuroimaging findings can predict long-term neuropsychiatric outcomes, potentially enabling targeted interventions to mitigate permanent neurological damage. Only through such rigorous, systematic investigation can we fully understand the natural history of COVID-19’s neurological impact and develop evidence-based management strategies for this growing public health challenge.

The present systematic review and meta-analysis has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. (i) There was high heterogeneity across most studies, reflecting uncontrolled confounding by differing research areas, institutions, times, and subjective factors of researchers between studies. Potential sources of heterogeneities were only found in a few subgroups and publication bias was also found in several outcomes. (ii) All included studies performed brain imaging of COVID-19 patients based on clinical need rather than protocol-driven research purposes. The pooled proportions were calculated in patients with specific indications (e.g. neurological manifestations) and cannot necessarily be generalizable to general COVID-19 patients, especially those never hospitalized. The availability of the COVID-19 vaccine may also change the detectable rate of abnormal brain imaging in COVID-19 patients. (iii) We found publication bias in several outcomes, including hemorrhagic stroke, microbleeds, subarachnoid hemorrhage, CT and MRI abnormalities, ischemic manifestations, and acute/subacute infarct. (iv) Methodological limitations of the primary studies constrain our conclusions. Most studies were retrospective case series with small sample sizes and potential selection biases. (v) We were unable to perform meta-analyses of long-term neuroimaging outcomes or functional correlates due to insufficient available data. This is a critical knowledge gap, given the growing recognition of post-acute neurological sequelae. As SARS-CoV-2 continues to evolve and population immunity changes through vaccination and prior infection, the epidemiology and clinical manifestations of COVID-19-related neurological complications may undergo significant temporal shifts (Cai et al., 2021). Future research should prioritize prospective, protocol-based neuroimaging studies with standardized methodologies and longer follow-up durations to better characterize both acute and chronic neurological effects across the spectrum of COVID-19 severity.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that neuroimaging abnormalities are frequently reported in COVID-19 patients with clinical brain symptoms and are also observed among some survivors. However, substantial between-study heterogeneity, clinical-need-based imaging (rather than protocolized screening), and evidence of publication bias limit the certainty and generalizability of the pooled estimates. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as signals rather than definitive rates. Standardized, prospective, protocol-driven studies with appropriate comparison groups and longer follow-up are needed to clarify prevalence, mechanisms, and prognostic significance across variants and vaccination contexts, and to determine whether and how neuroimaging can inform clinical management.
