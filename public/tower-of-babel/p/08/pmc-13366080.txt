14th International Festival of Public Health
Authors: Greg Williams, Gethin Hopkin, Elena Petelos, Tina Purnat, Laura Swain, Christine Greehalgh, Abin Thomas, Joanne Elliott, Jedidah Mould, Gary Clough, Arpana Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis year’s International Festival of Public Health marks our 14th year, and with the introduction of a dedicated supplement in the European Journal of Public Health, it seems appropriate to reflect on those years and the purpose of the Festival, including its place in the current world. What started off in 2012 as our team inviting others beyond our group and university to attend a visit by the then Faculty of Public Health President, Professor Lindsey Davies, has since grown to become an annual event attended by people from all over the world.Our ethos has always been to try and have an inclusive event where anyone with an interest in public health can feel welcome. Over the years we’ve been lucky enough to bring in some world-leading experts in their field to give keynote speeches. At the same time, we have striven to provide a platform for our public engagement groups, for students and young professionals alike, and for all those who might just wish to share their own personal experiences of some of the vast array of topics we discuss at the event. Quite simply, the Festival’s International Scientific Committee believe that knowledge is for everyone and sharing that knowledge is vital in our endeavour to improve population health. Whilst this is a conference held at an academic institution, we hope that it goes some way to breaking down those academic silos and barriers that so many academics talk about breaking down but never actively do.This conference is free. For various reasons in the past, we have had to charge to cover some costs, but we have tried to keep this cost to a minimum (and providing us with an excuse as to why you can’t afford the slight fee would generally get you put on the “free” registration list anyway). It can be tough identifying ways to put this on for minimal cost (and covering lunch!) but we have a remarkable team who deliver the Festival on top of their day-to-day jobs, from within the University and beyond.We cannot help but observe the irony that as a discipline we spend our lives describing, researching and trying to minimise inequalities, whilst simultaneously accepting and endorsing inequalities in access to learning through professional conferences. In this day and age, a conference registration fee alone is hundreds (nearing thousands) of pounds (or relevant currency). This is before you factor in travel and accommodation. Many academics, researchers and institutions simply cannot afford these costs, particularly in the current climate. The model almost restricts these platforms to the public health “elite” (we acknowledge that this is an issue that extends beyond public health). For those starting out their careers, it can be near impossible to find the funds to take advantage of these learning and presenting opportunities. We often talk about things like decolonising our curriculums, whilst at the same time continuously platforming the same people and ideas. Blocking public health researchers and academics from participating is not just an access issue, it is also a serious equity issue. When only well-resourced institutions can contribute, important perspectives, populations, and contexts are left out of the evidence base.It’s also worth considering the purpose of these events. Where limited funding may be available to attend them, institutions will often impose conditions to gain the funding such as strict requirements to have work presented at an event. Whilst this is a tremendous opportunity for people to disseminate the results of their hard work, it’s restrictive. Having a conference presentation on your CV can be valuable, but what about people who want to go just to learn, such as those on teaching-focused contracts? These people can have the opportunity to spread this knowledge to the next generation of public health professionals, yet often find themselves excluded from conferences because they lack a research focus to their work.We don’t claim to be a perfect conference, but we hope that we demonstrate that there is some capacity to go beyond the normal conference model and provide access to knowledge for those who may not have other opportunities to attend such events. At each Festival we cover a wide range of themes that we hope can provide insight into new areas for some and expand the knowledge for others who may have more experience in the field.For this year’s Festival, we have five main themes, which we summarise here before considering each in more detail in the theme abstracts. It’s worth highlighting that abstract submission to the Festival is not confined to these themes, and no weighting is applied to them during the abstract review process.We turn first to Global Public Health, considering that in an interconnected world, public health challenges increasingly transcend local, national and regional borders. As an international conference, we hear from voices across the world and provide a platform to exchange ideas to tackle these interconnected challenges. In-keeping with our goal of hosting an inclusive conference, we highlight the fundamental importance of Research Inclusion as our second theme. We hear from presenters who have designed, conducted and interpreted research so that the voices of patients, public and communities are sought, heard and amplified. For our third theme, we consider Women’s Health, learning from people whose research and practice addresses historic and ongoing challenges and inequities in this too often under-researched and under-funded area. Continuing the idea of tackling structural inequity and the role of public health, our fourth theme addresses the Commercial Determinants of Health. We ask how the public health community can come together to offer a collective challenge to commercial interests when they undermine health, as well as work constructively with those powerful interests and actors to promote good health. In our final theme we look to the future and consider how Artificial Intelligence can contribute meaningfully to public health in 2026 and beyond, while recognising its potential to harm equity and the environment, among myriad other challenges.Fourteen years after that first decision to open an event out beyond the University, we continue to strengthen the International Festival of Public Health as a platform for tackling deep-rooted structural inequities. Through both the structure and function of the conference as well as the research and practice we elevate, we provide an inclusive and engaging Festival for learning, building relationships, and exchanging knowledge across traditional boundaries of public, professions and disciplines. We would like to thank the Division of Medical Education and the School of Medical Sciences here at The University of Manchester for their ongoing support in the organisation of the event, as well as our partners at the European Public Health Association who help us share our common values with colleagues across Europe and beyond. We would like to thank all of our speakers, our chairs, moderators, stall holders, the University of Manchester’s Conference and Events team including house services and administrative teams. Finally, to our delegates, without whom this annual event would not be possible, and especially our Friends of the Festival who have attended and supported us since the beginning.
