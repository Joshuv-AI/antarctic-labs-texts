Epigenetic Regulation of G6PD Drives Metabolic Reprogramming in Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Yusuke Nakano, Miwa Tanaka, Takeharu Sakamoto, Masahiro Hashimoto, Taro Tobo, Hideyuki Saito, Tadashi Abe, Tomohiko Ikehara, Takashi Ofuchi, Koto Kawata, Takaaki Masuda, Takayuki Ogino, Mamoru Uemura, Hidetoshi Eguchi, Yuichiro Doki, Koshi Mimori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is a highly aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis and significant molecular heterogeneity. This study investigates the role of tumor‐specific enhancers in metabolic reprogramming, focusing on glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). Using native elongating transcript–cap analysis of gene expression and single‐cell RNA sequencing, tumor‐specific enhancers driving G6PD overexpression were identified in ICC tumor epithelial cells. Functional assays demonstrated that G6PD promotes tumor proliferation by enhancing PPP activity and maintaining redox homeostasis, which provides NADPH to counter oxidative stress. Enhancer knockdown disrupted G6PD expression and PPP activity, increasing reactive oxygen species levels and reducing the NADPH/NADP+ ratio. These metabolic changes impaired tumor cell proliferation and sensitized ICC cells to cisplatin, emphasizing the dual therapeutic potential of targeting G6PD to inhibit tumor growth and overcome chemoresistance. Survival analyses showed that high G6PD expression correlates strongly with poor overall survival in ICC patients. While previous studies have recognized the roles of G6PD and PPP in cancer metabolism, this study uniquely links enhancer‐mediated regulation to these processes in ICC, offering novel insights into epigenetic mechanisms driving metabolic reprogramming. Moreover, the findings highlight tumor‐specific enhancers as critical epigenetic drivers of ICC progression, with potential as therapeutic targets. Future research should explore the integration of enhancer profiling into precision medicine frameworks and the development of novel enhancer‐targeting strategies. These efforts could uncover additional metabolic vulnerabilities and provide effective treatments for this highly aggressive cancer.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is a highly aggressive liver malignancy and the second most common primary hepatic tumor, characterized by poor prognosis, high mortality, and an increasing global incidence [1, 2]. This malignancy poses significant clinical challenges due to its late diagnosis, molecular heterogeneity, and resistance to conventional therapies, including chemotherapy and targeted treatments [3, 4]. Advances in molecular profiling have revealed the complex biology of ICC, but effective therapeutic strategies remain limited, emphasizing the urgent need for novel approaches to better understand and target its underlying mechanisms.

A growing body of research highlights the role of metabolic reprogramming as a hallmark of ICC progression [5, 6, 7]. Metabolic reprogramming enables tumor cells to adapt to nutrient‐deprived and hypoxic microenvironments, maintain biosynthetic activity, and sustain rapid proliferation under adverse conditions [6, 7]. Among the metabolic pathways altered in several cancers, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) has emerged as a critical driver [8, 9]. This pathway provides ribose‐5‐phosphate for nucleotide biosynthesis and generates NADPH, a key reducing agent required for neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and maintaining redox homeostasis [8, 9]. Elevated PPP activity supports tumor cell survival and growth, highlighting its potential as a metabolic vulnerability in cancer [8, 9].

At the center of the PPP is glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), the rate‐limiting enzyme that catalyzes the first step of the pathway [10]. G6PD plays a vital role in tumor metabolism by producing NADPH to counter oxidative stress, repair damage caused by ROS, and maintain cellular redox balance [10]. Aberrant G6PD expression has been reported in several cancers, where it is associated with aggressive phenotypes, enhanced resistance to chemotherapy, and poor clinical outcomes [11, 12, 13]. However, the roles of G6PD in the development and chemoresistance of ICC remain unclear. Deciphering these mechanisms is crucial for understanding how G6PD contributes to ICC biology and for identifying new therapeutic opportunities.

Epigenetic regulators are emerging as key players in various biological processes [14, 15]. Enhancers are key cis‐regulatory elements within the epigenetic regulatory network that bind transcription factors and coactivators, playing a crucial role in driving cell‐type‐specific gene expression and the transcriptional control of genes [16, 17]. Tumor‐specific enhancers, which exhibit unique activity patterns in cancer cells, are increasingly recognized for their role in reprogramming gene expression to meet the metabolic and biosynthetic demands of aggressive tumors [18, 19]. Despite their established role in other cancers, the involvement of tumor‐specific enhancers in regulating metabolic pathways remains largely unexplored in ICC. Investigating this relationship could provide novel insights into the epigenetic control of tumor metabolism and identify potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.

In this study, we investigated the epigenetic regulation of G6PD in ICC and identified tumor‐specific enhancers driving its overexpression. These enhancers promoted PPP activity, redox homeostasis, and tumor proliferation, while their inhibition reduced tumor growth and enhanced chemotherapy sensitivity, highlighting their therapeutic potential.

Materials and Methods

ICC Multiomics Data Utilization

The ICC multiomics data used in this study were derived from a previous analysis of ICC samples [5]. This dataset included genome, transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome analyses obtained through multiregional sampling of primary ICC cases. Specifically, we utilized the data from 11 ICC cases (88 samples) for whole‐transcriptome sequencing, 10 cases (49 samples) for proteomic analysis, and 10 cases (49 samples) for metabolomic analysis.

NET‐CAGE Analysis for Nascent RNA

The native elongating transcript–cap analysis of gene expression (NET‐CAGE) analysis was conducted by Revorf Co. Ltd., following the methodology previously described [20]. Cell pellets of SSP‐25, RBE, HCCC9810, HuH‐28, and IHBEC cells were prepared, and total and nascent RNA were extracted from the cell pellets. RNA quality was assessed based on RNA Integrity Number (RIN), A260/A280 ratio, and yield. For CAGE library preparation, 5 μg of nascent RNA was used as the input. Libraries underwent intermediate and final quality checks to ensure integrity, including assessments of ssDNA yield and rRNA contamination. Sequencing was performed on a NextSeq500 platform with a 75‐cycle single‐read protocol, targeting 20 M reads per sample. Raw bcl files were converted to fastq format using Illumina's cloud service. Reads were evaluated using FastQC v0.11.9, and adapters and low‐quality reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39. Reads were mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR v2.7.9a. Coverage at the 5′ read ends was calculated using bedtools v2.27.1, and resulting bedgraph files were converted to bigwig format for mapping to promoter and enhancer regions from the FANTOM5 database [21]. De novo enhancer regions were identified following the method previously described. Bidirectional transcription clusters within 400 bp were extracted and validated against previously annotated CAGE‐defined promoter and enhancer regions, as described in the FANTOM5 project [21].

Generation of Enhancer Knockdown ICC Cells

The lenti‐EF1a‐dCas9‐KRAB‐Puro was a gift from Kristen Brennand (Addgene plasmid #99372; http://n2t.net/addgene:99372; RRID: Addgene_99372) [22]. Lentiviral particles were generated by transfecting 293FT cells with 2.5 μg of the lenti‐EF1a‐dCas9‐KRAB‐Puro plasmid, 1.8 μg of ViraPower Lentiviral Packaging Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's instructions. The transfected 293FT cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ for 48 h. After incubation, 2 mL of viral supernatant was collected and used to infect SSP‐25 cells by adding 1 mL of viral supernatant to each well containing SSP‐25 cells. Following 24 h of transduction, the medium was replaced, and the cells were selected with puromycin (Sigma, Cat. No. P9620) at a concentration of 2 μg/mL for 3 days. For further experiments, the SSP‐25 cells stably expressing dCas9‐KRAB were transduced with lentiviral particles containing either one of three gRNA pairs (g1, g2, or g3) or a scramble control gRNA. These gRNAs were designed and obtained from VectorBuilder (Chicago, IL, https://en.vectorbuilder.com/). Lentiviral particles for gRNA transduction were generated by transfecting 293FT cells with 2.5 μg of the pLV[2gRNA]‐Bsd‐U6 plasmid containing the corresponding gRNA sequences, 1.8 μg of ViraPower Lentiviral Packaging Mix, and Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. The transfected 293FT cells were incubated under the same conditions as described above, and the viral supernatant was collected after 48 h. The viral supernatant was then used to transduce the SSP‐25 cells stably expressing dCas9‐KRAB. After 24 h of transduction, the cells were selected with blasticidin (InvivoGen, Cat. No. ant‐bl‐1) at a concentration of 5 μg/mL for 7 days to ensure stable integration. Nonclonal cell populations were used to maintain heterogeneity. The successful establishment of dCas9‐KRAB‐expressing cells and subsequent gRNA‐directed transduction was validated through western blot analysis.

The gRNA sequences used in this study were as follows:

g1: 5′‐GCACATAGGTCCCATGAGGA‐3′, 5′‐AGACAGCGATTGGGTACTTC‐3′.

g2: 5′‐CTCGGTGCGGGAGGTAGGCA‐3′, 5′‐CCTACCTCCCGCACCGAGTG‐3′.

g3: 5′‐ATCAGGCGTGGAAGAAGCCT‐3′, 5′‐AGATGGGAGGTGGCGTGCTC‐3′.

Scramble: 5′‐GTGTAGTTCGACCATTCGTG‐3′, 5′‐GTTCAGGATCACGTTACCGC‐3′.

Tumor Growth Assay

Experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees of Kansai Medical University, and the experiments were conducted following the institutional ethical guidelines for animal experiments and the safety guidelines for gene manipulation experiments. BALB/c nude mice were purchased from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan) and maintained under specific pathogen‐free conditions. The tumorigenicity of the cells was examined using 8‐week‐old male BALB/c nude mice as previously described [23]. Briefly, 1 × 107 SSP‐25 cells suspended in 0.1 mL 50% Matrigel (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA)/phosphate buffer saline were injected subcutaneously into the dorsal side of the mice. Subsequently, the implanted tumors were measured with calipers on the indicated days, and their volumes were calculated using the formula V = (L × W 2)/2, where V is the volume (mm3), L is the largest tumor diameter (mm), and W is the smallest tumor diameter (mm).

G6PD Activity Assay

G6PD activity was measured utilizing a Glucose‐6‐Phosphate Dehydrogenase Activity Colorimetric Assay kit (Abcam, ab102529) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 2 × 106 cells were rapidly homogenized in an equivalent volume of G6PDH Assay Buffer. 50 microliters of lysis were added to a 96‐well plate with the addition of another 50 μL reaction mix. Absorbance at OD 450 nm before and after incubation at 37°C for 30 min was recorded. G6PD activity was calculated using the NADH standard curve.

NADP/NADPH Assay

NADP/NADPH Assay Kit‐WST (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) was used for the assay. The assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. SSP‐25 cells were used for experiments in which G6PD expression was suppressed through enhancer knockdown, and the assays were performed in triplicate.

ROS Assay

ROS Assay Kit‐Highly Sensitive DCFH‐DA (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) was used for the assay. The assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. SSP‐25 cells with G6PD expression suppressed by enhancer knockdown and control cells (1.0 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into a 96‐well plate and incubated for 1 night in an incubator set at 37°C and equilibrated with 95% air and 5% CO₂. After the culture medium was removed, the Highly Sensitive DCFH‐DA Dye working solution was added. The cells were then cultured for 30 min under the same conditions as above. Following this, the cells were washed twice with HBSS (Hank's Balanced Salt Solution). For some wells, 100 μmol/L hydrogen peroxide in HBSS was added, and the cells were cultured again for 30 min under the same conditions. The cells were then washed twice with HBSS, and all wells were refilled with HBSS. Fluorescence signals were measured using a Multi‐Label Counter ARVO MX (PerkinElmer Japan) operated with Wallac 1420 Workstation software (Version 3.00 Revision 2).

Metabolism Quantification by scMetabolism and Enrichment Analysis

Metabolic activity analysis at the single‐cell resolution was performed using the R package, scMetabolism, as previously described [24]. The R package of scMetabolism is available on GitHub (https://github.com/wu‐yc/scMetabolism) and can be directly installed. We evaluated metabolism gene signature scoring by imputing the scRNA‐seq matrix data using the R package Seurat v5.0.1. We used the gene set from the KEGG database and the metabolism quantification using VISION, the default method. Cluster‐based detection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test and Benjamini–Hochberg method to correct for multiple comparisons (scanpy.tl_rank_genes_groups). DEGs with an adjusted p < 0.01 and log2‐fold change > 1 were evaluated. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses of the DEGs were performed using scapy.queries.enrich.

Statistical Analysis

Variations among variables were compared using either the Mann–Whitney U test, Student's t‐test, or Fisher's exact test where applicable. The Kaplan–Meier method was utilized to estimate overall survival (OS), and comparisons between survival curves were made using the log‐rank test. The statistical analyses were performed using R software v4.4.2 and Python v3.9.16. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For further details regarding other materials and methods used in this study, please refer to the Doc. S1.

Results

Identification of Tumor‐Specific Enhancers via NET‐CAGE Analysis

First, to identify tumor‐specific enhancers in ICC, we performed NET‐CAGE analysis on four tumor cell lines (HuH28, HCCC9810, SSP25, and RBE) and a normal biliary epithelial cell (IHBEC) (Figure 1A and Table 1). We compared the transcriptional activity patterns of promoters and enhancers in the identified enhancers, finding that promoters exhibited ubiquitous transcriptional activity, whereas enhancers displayed cell‐type‐specific transcriptional activity patterns (Figure S1A). Next, we compared the numbers of enhancers identified in tumor and normal cells, identifying 18,866 tumor‐specific enhancers and 803 enhancers in normal cells (Table S1), which highlights the distinct gene regulatory activities present in tumor cells (Figure 1B). The enhancers identified in tumor cells overlapped significantly with those identified using conventional enhancer identification methods (Figure S1B). Additionally, we evaluated the proportion of tumor‐specific enhancers shared across four tumor cell lines and found that most were specific to a single cell line, while some enhancers were shared among multiple cell lines (Figure 1C). These data suggest that tumor‐specific enhancers exhibit distinct patterns of activity, with a significant subset shared across multiple tumor cell lines. This finding underscores the existence of conserved regulatory elements in tumor cells, despite their heterogeneous landscapes.

Pathway Enrichment Analysis of Tumor‐Specific Enhancer

Next, we identified target genes associated with tumor‐specific enhancer regions using the Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool (GREAT) [25] and performed pathway enrichment analysis with Enrichr [26]. We found significant enrichment of the glucose 6‐phosphate (G6P) pathway in 2435 genes nearest tumor‐specific enhancers and observed pathways related to NADP+ metabolism as significantly enriched when focusing on 755 highly expressed tumor‐specific genes (Figure 1D). The enrichment of pathways involving G6P and NADP+ suggests enhanced metabolic activity through the PPP.

Identification of Tumor‐Specific Metabolic Pathway and Related Genes

We identified 32 KEGG metabolic pathways specifically enriched in tumor cells (Table S2) and extracted 120 highly expressed genes associated with these pathways in tumor cells (Figure 2A and Table S3). The scRNA‐seq dataset contained 33,991 cells, which were annotated according to the original dataset and classified into 10 cell types (Figure 2B). We observed particularly prominent enrichment of the PPP and pathways related to glutathione metabolism among the metabolic pathways significantly enriched in tumor cells (Figure 2C). These pathways were also enriched in tumor epithelial cells compared to normal epithelial cells (Figure 2C).

Glucose‐6‐Phosphate Dehydrogenase is Highly Expressed in Tumor Epithelial Cells

From the results of tumor‐specific enhancer analysis (Figure 1D), scRNA‐seq, and scMetabolism (Figure 2C), PPP enrichment was consistently observed. Therefore, we focused on G6PD, the rate‐limiting enzyme of this pathway. G6PD expression was higher in tumor epithelial cells compared with other cells, with significantly higher expression than in normal epithelial cells (Figure 2D). In the TCGA dataset, G6PD mRNA expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Figure 2E). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that G6PD was localized in the nuclei of tumor cells, whereas G6PD staining was weak in normal epithelial cells (Figure 2E). Based on the median expression level of G6PD, pathway analysis revealed that the high expression group showed significant enrichment of pathways related to glutathione metabolism, the PPP, and cellular responses to metabolic and environmental stimuli (Figure 2F). To validate these findings, we analyzed the public ICC scRNA‐seq dataset GSE189903, containing 36,123 cells across seven annotated cell types (Figure S2A). Similar to the GSE138709 cohort, G6PD expression was significantly higher in tumor epithelial cells than in normal epithelial cells (Figure S2B), and pathway analysis revealed enrichment of pathways related to glutathione metabolism, the PPP, and cellular stress responses (Figure S2C). These findings suggest that G6PD is involved in tumor metabolism and cellular stress responses through its overexpression and associated pathway activations.

Distinct Expression Patterns of PPP Components in ICC Tumor Tissues

The results so far suggest significant enrichment of the PPP in the tumor epithelial cells of ICC. To further understand the findings of this analysis, it is necessary to perform a detailed investigation of the transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic expression patterns of PPP components. In our cohort's multisampling data, G6PD, a key enzyme in the oxidative pathway (Figure 3A), exhibited significantly higher expression patterns in tumor tissues (Figure 3B). Similar results were observed in the validation cohorts GSE76297, GSE26566, and GSE32225 (Figure S3A–C). Furthermore, at the protein level, G6PD showed significantly higher expression patterns in tumor tissues (Figure 3C). Metabolite analysis also revealed distinct expression patterns between tumor and normal tissues, with a particularly notable increase in the NADPH/NADP+ ratio in tumor tissues (Figure 3D). Moreover, correlation analysis demonstrated that G6PD mRNA expression positively correlated with intracellular levels of G6P and NADPH/NADP+ ratio, suggesting that elevated G6PD contributes to redox balance in ICC (Figure 3E). We examined Transketolase (TKT), a key enzyme in the nonoxidative pathway (Figure 3A), and found significantly higher expression in tumor tissues, consistent with validation cohorts and in our multisampling data (Figure S3A–E).

Activity of Pentose Phosphate Pathway Correlates With Case Survival in ICC

To investigate the association of PPP activity with prognosis in ICC cases, we analyzed matched transcriptome data and cohort survival information from 103 ICC tumors generated for previous studies (Shibata cohort) [27], as well as data from the TCGA dataset. In the analysis, we selected 27 genes related to PPP in the KEGG pathway database, and the activity of the PPP was calculated for each case. Based on the score values, the cases were divided into two groups, and the prognosis was compared. The results suggested that the group with higher scores tended to have lower OS rates (Figure S4A–B).

High G6PD Expression Promotes ICC Proliferation and Poor Prognosis

We investigated the impact of G6PD overexpression on the proliferation of ICC cell lines using MTT and colony formation assays. SSP‐25 and HCCC‐9810 cells with overexpression of G6PD were established (Figure 4A,B). MTT assays revealed that the overexpression of G6PD significantly increased cell proliferation in both SSP‐25 and HCCC‐9810 cells (Figure 4C). Colony formation assays showed that the overexpression of G6PD significantly increased colony formation in both SSP‐25 and HCCC‐9810 cells (Figure 4D). The prognostic relevance of G6PD mRNA expression in ICC was evaluated. Survival rates were assessed based on G6PD mRNA expression levels in patients with ICC. The OS rate was significantly lower in patients with high vs. low G6PD mRNA expression in our cohort (Figure 4E). These results suggest that G6PD promotes the proliferation of ICC cells in vitro, and increased mRNA expression of G6PD is linked to a poorer prognosis in patients with ICC.

Tumor‐Specific Enhancer‐Mediated Regulation of G6PD Expression in ICC

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms of G6PD expression, we first confirmed that mutations, promoter methylation, and copy number variations of G6PD were rare or not correlated with expression (Figure S5A–C). To further explore the possibility of enhancer‐mediated regulation of G6PD expression, we investigated tumor‐specific enhancer regions near the G6PD locus identified by NET‐CAGE. These regions overlapped with enhancer regions identified through ChIP‐seq in ICC cell lines and ATAC‐seq in ICC samples (Figure S6A), Hi‐C analysis also revealed physical interactions between these enhancer regions and the G6PD promoter (Figure S7A). To experimentally validate these in silico findings, we targeted these regions with dCas9‐KRAB to knock down the enhancer activity (Figure 5A,B and S7A). Because this region is located within an intron of G6PD and proximal to the alternative promoter of the long isoform of IKBKG (Figure S7A), knockdown significantly reduced G6PD expression (Figure 5B) and also decreased the expression of both long and short isoforms of IKBKG (Figure S7B), suggesting that this element functions as a shared regulatory hub for both genes. To further explore upstream regulation, motif analysis of this region revealed significant enrichment of the NRF2 binding motif (Figure S7C), and public ChIP‐seq data for NRF2 and H3K27ac confirmed a distinct binding peak overlapping the region (Figure S7A). Collectively, these data indicate that NRF2 contributes to the activation of this regulatory region. The complete list of motif analysis results is provided in Table S4.

Functional Role of Tumor‐Specific Enhancer in G6PD Regulation and ICC Progression

To further investigate the functional role of tumor‐specific enhancers in regulating G6PD expression and its impact on tumor behavior, we conducted a series of experiments using enhancer knockdown cell lines. First, RNA‐seq analysis was performed, revealing significant enrichment of the PPP among significantly downregulated DEGs (Figure 5C). Functionally, enhancer knockdown cell lines exhibited reduced proliferation as assessed by MTT assays (Figure 5D) and decreased colony formation in colony formation assays (Figure 5E). Additionally, in vivo experiments demonstrated that mice injected subcutaneously with enhancer knockdown cells showed significantly shorter tumor engraftment times compared to controls (Figure 5F). These findings suggest that tumor‐specific enhancer not only regulates G6PD expression but also plays a critical role in promoting tumor growth and survival, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets in ICC.

G6PD Expression Modulates Redox Homeostasis and Chemotherapy Resistance

To investigate the functional consequences of G6PD regulation and its role in tumor biology, we conducted experiments using both enhancer knockdown cells and G6PD overexpressing cells. G6PD activity assays demonstrated a significant reduction in activity in the enhancer knockdown cells (Figure 6A). ROS assays revealed a marked increase in ROS levels in the knockdown cells (Figure 6B), while NADPH/NADP+ assays showed a significant decrease in the NADPH/NADP+ ratio (Figure 6C). These findings indicate that enhancer‐mediated regulation of G6PD directly impacts redox homeostasis in tumor cells. Additionally, drug sensitivity to cisplatin was assessed. G6PD‐overexpressing cells exhibited reduced sensitivity to cisplatin (Figure 6D), whereas enhancer knockdown cells showed increased sensitivity (Figure 6E). To further assess therapeutic relevance, we tested the G6PD inhibitor 6‐aminonicotinamide (6‐AN). 6‐AN significantly suppressed tumor cell proliferation (Figure 6F) and enhanced cisplatin sensitivity in tumor cells (Figure 6G). These findings indicate that G6PD contributes to cisplatin resistance through its role in redox regulation and support the potential of tumor‐specific enhancers as therapeutic targets in ICC (Figure 6H).

Discussion

This study demonstrates the critical role of tumor‐specific enhancers in regulating metabolic pathways in ICC. Using NET‐CAGE analysis, we identified enhancers with cell‐type‐specific transcriptional activity, highlighting their potential as epigenetic regulators of tumor biology. These enhancers were significantly enriched in pathways such as the PPP and glutathione metabolism, which are vital for maintaining redox homeostasis and supporting tumor proliferation. Prior research has shown that enhancers play essential roles in transcriptional control in aggressive cancers [28, 29, 30], aligning with our findings in ICC.

G6PD overexpression provides NADPH required for oxidative stress management, a mechanism previously linked to tumor survival and progression in aggressive cancers [11, 12, 13]. Our functional assays revealed that G6PD enhances tumor cell proliferation and growth in ICC. Moreover, survival analyses demonstrated a strong association between high G6PD expression and poor overall survival, underscoring its clinical importance as both a marker of aggressiveness and a potential therapeutic target. In addition to elevated expression levels, immunohistochemical analysis revealed nuclear localization of G6PD in ICC. Previous reports have described nuclear G6PD in both malignant and nonmalignant cells, where it supports NADPH‐dependent chromatin remodeling and DNA repair [31, 32, 33]. This noncanonical localization suggests that G6PD may also participate in epigenetic regulation in ICC.

To investigate the role of tumor‐specific enhancers in regulating G6PD, knockdown experiments targeting enhancers near the G6PD locus were conducted, which significantly reduced G6PD expression. Although various reports have been made regarding the regulation of G6PD expression [34, 35, 36], this study is the first to demonstrate regulation via enhancers. This regulatory region resides within an intron of G6PD and is adjacent to the alternative promoter of the long isoform of IKBKG. Knockdown reduced the expression of both genes, indicating that it may function as a shared regulatory region. As IKBKG primarily acts in NFκB signaling and is not directly linked to metabolic pathways such as the PPP [37], the observed PPP suppression and redox imbalance are most likely explained by reduced G6PD expression. Moreover, NRF2 motif enrichment and ChIPseq data support that this region is activated by NRF2, a key regulator of enhancer activity [38].

Pathway analysis based on RNA‐seq of enhancer knockdown cells revealed significant enrichment of the PPP, suggesting reduced PPP flux and feedback‐mediated downregulation of PPP‐related genes [39, 40]. Notably, enhancer knockdown increased ROS levels and decreased the NADPH/NADP+ ratio, disrupting the redox balance. These metabolic disruptions inhibited tumor growth and enhanced cisplatin sensitivity, suggesting a dual therapeutic potential in targeting G6PD and highlighting the utility of targeting enhancers. Consistently, G6PD inhibition with 6AN also suppressed tumor cell proliferation and sensitized cells to cisplatin. These results further validate G6PD as a therapeutic target and suggest that G6PD inhibition may help overcome chemotherapy resistance in ICC. While the role of G6PD in chemotherapy resistance has been previously reported [41], this study establishes the link between enhancer‐mediated G6PD regulation and ICC chemoresistance.

The field of epigenetic drug discovery has advanced significantly in recent years. The potent bromodomain and extra‐terminal domain inhibitor (BETi) has emerged as a promising candidate for cancer therapy [42]. Despite the early successes of BET inhibitors across various malignancies, previous studies have highlighted that drug resistance may limit their clinical application [43]. Unlike BETi, which broadly targets enhancers, the tumor‐specific enhancer regions identified in this study represent precise genomic sequences, potentially offering a more targeted and effective therapeutic strategy. However, further investigation is essential to translate these findings into clinical applications.

In conclusion, this study reveals a novel epigenetic mechanism in which tumor‐specific enhancers regulate G6PD expression to drive metabolic reprogramming in ICC. Enhancer‐mediated G6PD overexpression was shown to promote tumor proliferation, redox homeostasis, and chemoresistance, positioning tumor‐specific enhancers as promising therapeutic targets.
