Risk factors for venous thromboembolism after knee arthroscopy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Wei Zheng, Xing Jin, Mingming Yao, Zhiting Dong, Wei He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOver the past few years, a growing number of knee arthroscopy (KA) procedures have been associated with an escalating incidence of postoperative venous thromboembolism (VTE), posing a significant threat to patient well-being. Nevertheless, the purpose of this study is to evaluate and synthesize the risk factors associated with VTE following KA, thereby establishing a scientific foundation for developing evidence-based clinical preventive measures.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive search across the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases for relevant literature spanning from January 1, 2019, to January 1, 2025. We meticulously reviewed all English-language observational studies about the risk factors associated with VTE after KA. The quality of the selected studies was critically appraised utilizing the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Subsequently, odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were determined to assess the relationship between various risk factors and the incidence of VTE after KA.ResultsA total of 11 observational studies were included, all of which demonstrated moderate to high methodological quality. The studies involved 875,099 patients who underwent arthroscopic KA. The meta-analysis results indicated that the risk factors for venous thrombosis after KA include age over 30 years (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: [1.20, 2.12], P < 0.001), body mass index (BMI) over 25 (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: [1.07, 1.41], P = 0.004), and D-dimer over 0.62 mg/L (OR = 5.69, 95% CI: [1.41, 22.89], P = 0.014).ConclusionAge over 30 years, BMI over 25, and D-dimer over 0.62 mg/L are significant risk factors for VTE following KA. Limited evidence also suggests an association between oral contraceptive use and a higher risk of VTE following KA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12959-025-00790-0.Keywords: Knee arthroscopy, Venous thromboembolism, Meta-analysis, Risk factors

Introduction

Over the past few years, there has been a steady rise in the number of patients opting for arthroscopic knee procedures, paralleled by notable enhancements in the arthroscopic methods used. For instance, the refinement of minimally invasive tools like the 1.9 mm needle arthroscope has significantly diminished patient trauma [1]. Additionally, virtual reality (VR) simulation training has significantly enhanced surgeons’ skills and proficiency [2]. However, the increase in the volume of KA surgeries has also led to a rise in venous thromboembolism (VTE) cases. Reports indicate that the incidence of VTE after KA ranges from 0.16% to 14% [3]. VTE is one of the serious complications following KA and is the third leading cause of death globally [4]. It primarily includes deep venous thromboses (DVTs) and pulmonary emboli (PEs) [5].

Postoperative VTE following KA includes several risk factors, such as age >30 years, obesity, oral contraceptive use, and D-dimer levels >0.62 mg/L [6–8]. Most previous studies have analyzed the risk factors for VTE following primary KA, but the conclusions remain controversial. For example, Sugimoto et al. [9] found no association between elevated D-dimer levels and the risk of thromboembolism, while Wu et al. [10] identified elevated D-dimer as a risk factor for thromboembolism.

Currently, the recommendations for the prevention of VTE in patients after KA are based on the consensus published by the International Consensus Meeting on VTE (ICM-VTE) in 2022. The international experts at ICM-VTE unanimously agreed that the overall risk of VTE after KA is low, and routine prophylaxis for VTE is unnecessary. The impact of routine VTE prophylaxis on the occurrence of VTE after KA can be considered negligible [11].

Given the high incidence, mortality, and resource expenditure associated with VTE events, understanding and preventing this complication remains crucial for patient safety [12]. However, there is still controversy regarding the association between various risk factors and VTE, as well as whether routine thromboembolism prophylaxis should be performed during the perioperative period. This study conducts a systematic review and meta-analysis of the risk factors for VTE after KA, providing evidence-based support for clinicians to identify high-risk patients and develop individualized prevention strategies, aiming to reduce the incidence of thromboembolism-related complications during the perioperative period.

Methods

Ethics and registration

This study protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13]. It was also registered in PROSPERO with the registration number CRD420250652721.

Literature search strategy

We searched the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases for literature from January 1, 2019, to January 1, 2025, to identify studies suitable for meta-analysis. The keywords used for the search were “Knee Arthroscopy,” “Venous Thromboembolism,” and “Thromboembolism, Venous.” Additionally, we manually searched the references of other related articles to find eligible studies. Eligibility criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) The included studies must explicitly aim to investigate risk factors for VTE following KA as their primary objective. (2) The case group must consist of patients diagnosed with VTE after KA, and the control group must consist of patients who underwent the same procedure but did not develop VTE. (3) Only cohort studies or case–control studies will be included. (4) The included studies must provide at least one of the following: OR with 95% CI; hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CI; or sufficient information to construct a 2 × 2 contingency table, with at least one reported case of VTE.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Excluded are reviews, letters, editorials, case reports, conference abstracts, unpublished articles, pharmacological experiments, efficacy or pharmacokinetic studies, etc. (2) Studies that evaluate outcome indicators only as secondary results of complications without including data on venous thrombosis after KA. (3) Patients with severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, unstable vital signs, or other conditions that could affect outcome measures. (4) Literature with missing data or statistical method errors. (5) Non-English literature.

Data extraction

Two authors(WZ, XJ) independently conducted the literature screening, initially reviewing the titles and abstracts to perform a preliminary screening, excluding studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria. They then read the full texts to determine final inclusion. Any contentious issues were resolved through discussion, with third-party intervention if necessary. Data extraction was carried out independently by two researchers ༈WZ, XJ༉using a pre-defined electronic form. The following data were extracted and recorded: first author, publication year, study type, region, total number of participants, number of cases, number of controls, sex, outcome indicators, and Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) score.

Quality assessment

Two authors(WZ, XJ) independently assessed the quality of eligible studies using the NOS [14]. This scale includes eight items divided into three dimensions: selection, comparability, and exposure. The quality score on the NOS ranges from 0 to 9, with studies scoring above 8 classified as high quality, studies scoring between 6 and 7 classified as moderate quality, and those scoring 5 or below classified as low quality.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The association between different risk factors and VTE after KA was assessed by calculating OR and 95% CI. The heterogeneity of the studies was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic, with P < 0.1 or I² >50% indicating significant statistical heterogeneity. In cases of significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was employed. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the stability of the results. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and quantified with the Egger test; P < 0.05 indicated significant bias, which was corrected using the trim-and-fill method.

Results

Literature screening process

Figure 1 displays the flowchart of the article screening process and the detailed selection procedure. The initial search yielded 1,240 relevant articles from electronic databases. After removing 261 duplicate articles, a preliminary screening was conducted on the remaining 979 articles, leading to 22 articles being reviewed in the next phase. Full-text screening according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria resulted in the exclusion of two articles due to incomplete data and nine articles due to a mismatch in subjects and objectives, ultimately including 11 articles for the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Basic information of included studies

All included studies were published in English between 2019 and 2025. These 11 studies [3, 6–8, 10, 12, 15–19] collectively included a total of 875,099 cases of KA. Among them, four studies were from the United States [7, 12, 17, 18], three from Japan [8, 15, 19], two from China [6, 10], one from Sweden [3], and one from South Korea [16]. There were six cohort studies [3, 7, 12, 17–19] and five case-control studies [6, 8, 10, 15, 16]. Risk factors such as age over 30 years, BMI over 25, D-dimer over 0.62 mg/L, oral contraceptive use, hypertension, diabetes, surgical duration, smoking, and gender were reported in two or more studies. The 11 included studies were assessed for quality using the NOS [14], and the results indicated that all included studies were of moderate to high quality (Table 2). Meta-analysis results

Association between age over 30 and VTE risk

A total of seven studies [3, 6, 7, 10, 15, 17, 19] reported the relationship between age and the occurrence of venous thrombosis after KA. Heterogeneity analysis indicated significant heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 82.3%, P < 0.001); thus, a random-effects model was employed. The pooled results showed that patients over 30 years of age were more likely to develop venous thrombosis after KA (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: [1.20, 2.12], P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Forest plot for association between age over 30 and VTE risk

Association between BMI over 25 and VTE risk

A total of six studies [6–8, 12, 18, 19] reported the association between BMI over 25 and the incidence of venous thrombosis after KA. Heterogeneity analysis indicated heterogeneity among the studies (I²=73.5%, P = 0.002); therefore, a random-effects model was used. The pooled results showed that patients with a BMI over 25 are more likely to develop venous thrombosis (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: [1.07, 1.41], P = 0.004) (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of association between BMI over 25 and VTE risk

Association between D-dimer over 0.62 mg/L and VTE risk

A total of three studies [6, 10, 15] reported the association between D-dimer and the occurrence of venous thrombosis after KA. Heterogeneity analysis indicated significant heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 88.2%, P = 0.001); thus, a random-effects model was employed. The pooled results showed that patients with D-dimer levels greater than 0.62 mg/L were more likely to develop venous thrombosis after KA (OR = 5.69, 95% CI: [1.41, 22.89], P = 0.014) (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of association between D-dimer over 0.62 mg/L and VTE risk

Association between other assessment factors and VTE risk

Our meta-analysis found an association between oral contraceptive use and a higher risk of VTE following KA. However, this finding is based on limited studies and should be interpreted with caution. Meanwhile, we found no evidence that surgical duration, diabetes, smoking, gender, or hypertension are risk factors for VTE after KA (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis results

To evaluate the robustness of the meta-analysis findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding each study individually. No significant changes were observed in the associations of age, BMI, and D-dimer with VTE following KA, demonstrating the stability of the results (Figs. 5, 6 and 7).

Results of publication bias analysis

To assess publication bias in our meta-analysis, we used funnel plots and Egger’s test [20] for factors with three or more studies. The results showed that the age funnel plot was asymmetrically distributed, with Egger’s test (P = 0.009) indicating publication bias. After applying the trim-and-fill method to include 4 virtual studies, the software performed a meta-analysis of all studies, showing Q = 45.930, P < 0.05, with substantial heterogeneity. The LogOR (95% CI) was 0.342 (0.039, 0.645), P < 0.001. After trim-and-fill adjustment, publication bias was no longer significant, suggesting that the original results may have been affected by publication bias, though the overall findings remain robust (Figs. 8, 9 and 10). The BMI funnel plot showed a generally symmetric distribution with Egger’s test (P = 0.053), indicating no publication bias (Figs. 11 and 12). The D-dimer funnel plot was essentially symmetrically distributed with Egger’s test (P = 0.100), suggesting no publication bias (Figs. 13 and 14).

Discussion

VTE is one of the most common complications after KA. Our meta-analysis found that age over 30 years, BMI over 25, and D-dimer over 0.62 mg/L are significant risk factors for VTE following KA. Limited evidence also suggests an association between oral contraceptive use and a higher risk of VTE following KA.

The meta-analysis revealed that patients undergoing KA who are over 30 years old had a 1.59-fold higher risk of postoperative venous thrombosis compared to those under 30. Hashimoto et al. [15] identified age as a risk factor for venous thrombosis following KA. Previous studies have reported that the cumulative incidence of VTE increases with advancing age [21]. Firstly, progressive degenerative changes in the vascular system occur with aging, characterized by the formation of atherosclerotic plaques. These pathological processes lead to vascular wall thickening, reduced elastic fibers, luminal narrowing, and decreased vascular compliance. Such structural alterations disrupt hemodynamics by elevating local shear stress and blood viscosity, significantly increasing blood stasis. This promotes platelet activation and fibrin deposition, ultimately driving pathological thrombus formation [22]. Secondly, aging is associated with abnormal blood rheological parameters, including significant age-dependent increases in whole blood viscosity and plasma viscosity. Concurrently, upregulated expression of platelet activation markers on platelet membranes enhances their propensity for aggregation and occlusion [23]. Aging is accompanied by heightened activity of key intrinsic coagulation factors (e.g., Factors VIII and IX), while anticoagulant mechanisms such as antithrombin III (AT-III) activity and the protein C system exhibit functional decline [24, 25]. Finally, advancing age is often correlated with decreased physical activity and functional decline in vascular systems, further elevating thrombotic risk [26, 27]. Although biological mechanisms and most studies indicate that VTE risk increases with age, there was significant heterogeneity in the critical age thresholds among our included literature. Madeleine et al. [17] used 50 years as the critical threshold, while Wu et al. [10] employed 32 years. This inconsistency in threshold values likely contributed to the observed publication bias. Given that there are currently no standardized guidelines for establishing these critical values, and based on current research that commonly uses 30 years as the age threshold [7, 28], we recommend that patients over 30 years of age undergoing KA should be closely monitored for the risk of VTE.

The meta-analysis revealed that patients undergoing KA with BMI over 25 had a 1.23-fold higher risk of postoperative VTE compared to those under 25. Previously, Kanaya et al. [8] considered a BMI over 25 to be a risk factor for VTE. Zhong et al. [7] also pointed out that a BMI over 25 is a risk factor for VTE. Firstly, patients with a BMI over 25 often have lipid metabolism disorders, such as increased triglycerides and decreased high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels, while HDL has an antithrombotic effect [29, 30]. Secondly, those with a BMI over 25 frequently exhibit increased platelet activity and elevated levels of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), leading to decreased fibrinolytic function, making the blood more prone to clotting. Inflammation from adipose tissue is believed to contribute to increased thrombosis [31]. Finally, patients with a BMI over 25 often have an abnormal accumulation of visceral fat, particularly characteristic changes associated with abdominal obesity, which can lead to impaired venous return from the lower limbs due to mechanical compression. Additionally, the reduced exercise tolerance associated with obesity significantly decreases daily activity levels, resulting in decreased skeletal muscle pump efficiency, thereby increasing the risk of thrombosis [32, 33].

This meta-analysis demonstrated that a postoperative D-dimer over 0.62 mg/L served as an independent predictive factor for VTE following KA. While Wu et al. [10] identified elevated postoperative D-dimer levels as a significant risk determinant for VTE with 86.5% sensitivity at the 0.62 mg/L threshold, Sugimoto et al. [9] failed to establish D-dimer elevation as a significant thromboembolic risk indicator. D-dimer, a specific byproduct of fibrinolytic system activation, quantitatively reflects the degradation of cross-linked fibrin. Its elevated levels were validated as a predictive biomarker for venous thrombus formation in multiple clinical validations [34]. Despite variations in D-dimer cutoff values across relevant literature, it is noteworthy that multiple studies have confirmed that elevated D-dimer levels are significantly associated with the occurrence, progression, and severity of VTE [35–37]. Therefore, patients with D-dimer >0.62 mg/L should be closely monitored for VTE formation.

The meta-analysis found an association between oral contraceptive use and a higher risk of VTE following KA. Meaidi et al. [38]and Traven et al. [18] also found that oral contraceptives are a risk factor for VTE following KA. Current evidence indicates that oral contraceptives can promote a hypercoagulable state by activating genetic susceptibility in carriers of the Factor V Leiden mutation and by increasing the activity of procoagulant proteins such as prothrombin (Factor II), thereby raising the risk of VTE [38, 39]. In addition, combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP) use increases VTE risk in the general population by elevating plasma fibrinogen activity and enhancing platelet aggregation [40, 41]. Although our meta-analysis results are supported by extensive literature, the limited sample size inevitably introduces bias into our findings. Nevertheless, we must remain attentive to the risk of VTE in patients taking oral contraceptives who undergo knee surgery. Given the limited amount of evidence, it is currently difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding oral contraceptives as a risk factor for VTE following KA. Future studies with standardized exposure definitions, adequate control of key confounding factors, and complete perioperative management information are needed to verify this association and provide higher quality evidence for clinical practice.

Our meta-analysis found no evidence that surgical duration, diabetes, smoking, gender, or hypertension are risk factors for VTE after KA. Regarding the influence of diabetes, research conclusions are divergent. Hu et al. [42] identified it as a risk factor, while Joo et al. [16] reached conclusions consistent with ours, finding no significant correlation between diabetes and postoperative VTE risk. Notably, although diabetic patients face increased risk of vascular diseases due to chronic hyperglycemia and various pathophysiological changes, aggressive pharmacological intervention can effectively improve vascular outcomes, and some anti-hyperglycemic treatment regimens may modulate VTE risk [43]. Concerning gender factors, Linnemann et al. [44] suggested that female sex might be a risk factor for VTE after KA, which differs from our findings. A possible explanation is that hormone replacement therapy and pregnancy may indeed increase postoperative VTE risk [45, 46], but it should be noted that elective surgical interventions are typically not recommended during pregnancy. Regarding hypertension, our findings align with those of Nazarzadeh et al. [47], neither confirming a correlation between hypertension and increased post-operative VTE risk. Furthermore, our analysis failed to establish significant correlations between surgical duration or smoking and VTE risk following KA. The interpretation of these results should consider the minimal differences in cutoff values across studies, the lack of standardized criteria, and the interference of various subjective factors that may affect outcome assessment.

Recent work on enhanced visualization techniques and advancements in anterior cruciate ligament repair during KA have shown how refined arthroscopic procedures can achieve faster mobilization and shorter surgical duration, both of which can impact the risk of VTE [48, 49]. Regarding the prevention of VTE after KA, different guidelines provide varying recommendations. The current guidelines published by the American College of Chest Physicians and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons suggest that routine thromboembolic prophylaxis is not recommended for patients without a known history of VTE, while patients with a history of thrombosis may receive prophylaxis [50]. In contrast, European guidelines advocate for pharmacological prophylaxis for KA even in patients who are not at high risk for VTE [51]. Currently, there is still controversy in the medical field regarding the routine provision of thromboembolic prevention after KA. The use of medications for thrombus prevention may increase the heterogeneity of outcome measures. Additionally, existing literature often excludes patients with a history or predisposition to thrombosis, and different studies employ various mechanisms to reduce risk bias. Therefore, the results of meta-analyses remain of significant reference value for the prevention of VTE after KA. Furthermore, in recent years the application of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) models in predicting postoperative thromboembolism in orthopedics has improved the accuracy of VTE detection and prediction [52, 53]. This makes it particularly important to understand the risk factors for postoperative VTE after KA in order to identify, predict, and prevent VTE in high‑risk patients.

This study is a comprehensive analysis of risk factors for VTE following KA. A thorough search across four databases and manual searches ensured the complete inclusion of relevant studies. However, this research still has several limitations. Firstly, many studies only provided data from univariate analyses rather than multivariate analyses, meaning the results might be influenced by confounding factors. Secondly, differences in surgical types across studies, the choice of cutoff values for independent risk factors, variations in surgical techniques among different surgeons, and the impact of surgical duration on the diagnosis of VTE may contribute to increased heterogeneity. Finally, the inclusion of five case-control studies in this analysis could also contribute to higher heterogeneity, and compared to cohort studies, case-control designs have weaker causal inference capabilities.

Conclusions

Age over 30 years, BMI over 25, and D-dimer over 0.62 mg/L are significant risk factors for VTE following KA. Limited evidence also suggests an association between oral contraceptive use and a higher risk of VTE following KA. Although this meta-analysis investigated several risk factors for venous thrombosis after knee arthroscopy, the limitations of the included studies necessitate a cautious interpretation of these findings. Further research with larger sample sizes and improved study designs is warranted to validate these results.
