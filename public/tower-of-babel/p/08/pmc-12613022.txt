Detection method for CRISPR-Cas9 edited tomato with single nucleotide deletion using multiplex probe-based real-time PCR and confirmation of non-transgenicity: A case study

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Genome editing has emerged as a globally recognized technology for development of gene-edited (GE) food crops and products using specialized enzymes to mutate, insert, replace or remove a part of DNA from the targeted site with high level of specificity and precision1. It allows the genetic improvement in plants by removing undesirable chromosomal DNA, up- or down- regulating the expression pattern of endogenous genes, or by knock-out/ knock-in modifications. Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) is being widely employed for gene editing due to its efficiency, ease-of-use, comparatively low cost and ability of introducing edits at multiple sites in the targeted genomes in a single procedure2. CRISPR combined with the Cas9 enzyme is an important tool for gene editing that allows for the targeted modification of specific genes in plants, enabling breeders to enhance various agronomic traits3. CRISPR is an integral part of a bacterial defense system, which forms the basis of the CRISPR-Cas9 system. The system comprises of two key components: the Cas9 endonuclease and a single guide RNA (sgRNA), which includes a 20-nucleotide spacer sequence that specifically targets the gene of interest and non-variable Cas-9 binding sites.

GE plants can be classified into three major categories based on the gene edits using site-directed nucleases (SDN): SDN-1, SDN-2 and SDN-32, 4, 6, 5. SDN-1 and SDN-2 techniques typically produce changes based on the natural DNA repair mechanisms, while SDN-3 involves the insertion of foreign genes, which may result in the formation of new proteins. Though SDN3 technique involves insertion of foreign gene as in the case of genetically modified (GM) crops, however, the insertion of DNA is targeted to a predefined region of the genome in SDN3 mediated gene editing. Therefore, the hazards associated with the disruption of genes and/or regulatory elements in the recipient genome are minimized4).

Globally, the adoption of CRISPR technology in crop development is rapidly increasing. Countries such as India, the United States, Japan, and China have made significant progress in developing and testing GE crops. The regulatory process for GE crops is generally more favourable than for GM crops in several countries. Canada and the United States are regulating GM and GE products according to a product-trigger mechanism on a case-by-case basis, irrespective of the technology used to develop it. However, countries such as India and European Union (EU)-based countries follow a process-based approach5.

In India, although commercial cultivation of Bt cotton is permitted, no GM food crops have been approved for cultivation or for use as food to date7. However, GE crops (SDN-1 and SDN-2) are deregulated in the country, while SDN-3 crops follow the same regulatory procedure as that of GM crops. In India, genetically modified organisms (GMOs) are regulated in accordance with the Rules for the Manufacture, Use/ Import/ Export and Storage of Hazardous Micro Organisms/ Genetically Engineered Organisms or Cells, 1989 notified under the Environment Protection Act, 1986 (EPA, 1986; Rules, 1989). As per the Guidelines for Safety Assessment of Genome Edited Plants, 2022 issued by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEF&CC), GE plants falling under categories SDN-1 and SDN-2 are exempted under Rule 20 of Rules 19898. A food/ feed and environmental safety assessment may not be required for SDN-1 or SDN-2 types, provided that the technology developer submits sufficient molecular and other documentary evidence demonstrating that the GE plants carry the intended mutations, show no biologically relevant off-target genomic changes, and show phenotypic equivalence if necessary on a case-by-case basis for SDN-1, along with additional phenotypic equivalence and trait efficacy through appropriate contained and/ or confined field trials for SDN-2 category9. The developer shall also ensure that GE plant (SDN-1, SDN-2) intended for environmental release does not contain any foreign gene/ DNA or sequences of vector and other components used in the editing process.

With the relaxation in the regulation of SDN-1 and SDN-2, India has marked a transformative move toward sustainable, high-yield crops developed without the insertion of foreign DNA by approving the first GE rice varieties, namely, Pusa Rice DST110 and DRR Dhan 100 developed using the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology. Pusa Rice DST1, developed by the ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI), exhibits enhanced tolerance to drought and salinity. These genome-edited rice lines exemplify a promising path forward in Indian agriculture—delivering robust performance while aligning with sustainability and regulatory acceptance, as they do not fall under the category of GMOs (https://www.nature.com/articles/d44151-025–00078-2).

In a scenario where different countries operate under varied regulatory frameworks, robust molecular approaches for detecting GE crops are essential to facilitate global trade of GE materials/ products while ensuring compliance with the respective national regulations. Since 1998, ICAR-National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (NBPGR), New Delhi facilitates the import of transgenics for research purposes after the technical clearance by Review Committee on Genetic Manipulation (RCGM)12, 11. GM detection laboratory at ICAR-NBPGR has developed/ validated GM diagnostics for the imported transgenics, as a part of regulatory research. Though the SDN-1 and SDN-2 are exempted under Rule 20 of Rules 1989, regulatory research related to the development of detection methods for GE plant material is required for the verification of the editing as claimed by the developer. For the confirmation of editing at an early phase of the experimentation followed by the detection of edited gene in the subsequent generations, development of detection methods for GE crops (referred to as GE detection in this study) has recently been initiated at ICAR-NBPGR.

In tomato, an important agricultural commodity in the world, CRISPR-Cas9 technology has been utilized for desirable traits including shelf-life, fruit characters, agronomic traits and nutritional enhancement17, 15, 13, 16, 14. This technology has also been employed for tomato improvement for fruit domestication traits in parthenocarpic fruits18, 19, For biotic stress resistance, genes such as CP and Rep for resistance against Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus20, MLO1 for powdery mildew resistance21, JAZ2 for bacterial speck disease resistance22; and for abiotic stress resistance traits such as BZR1, CBF1, MAPK3 for tolerance to heat, chilling and drought25, 23, 24have been targeted.

GE detection is challenging because GE plants may exhibit smaller modifications, such as single or few nucleotide substitutions, insertions, or deletions, and may lack transgenic elements commonly found in GM crops6. Among the DNA-based methods, real-time PCR in Arabidopsis, canola, maize, rice, sorghum27, 26, digital droplet PCR in rice and rapeseed29, 28, high throughput sequencing in rice30 have been reported for detection of mutations or gene editing at the targeted loci. The detection protocols for four commercial GE products, namely, Calyno™ soybeans, Waxy maize, tomato with high γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and herbicide-tolerant oilseed rape lines of Cibus have also been reported31. Real-time PCR is being commonly employed for detecting and quantifying expression profiles of selected genes, as well as in the detection of GM crops. Gene editing with short sequence changes, i.e., substitutions or indels of one or a few nucleotides can be detected using a specific probe. Alternatively, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), an isothermal amplification method, is being used with wider applicability in diagnostics including rapid on-site GM detection32, 37, 34, 35, 36, 33.

In the present study, a stepwise strategy is designed and used for GE detection: (a) screening at early phase targeting Cas9 protein gene, and (b) verification of single nucleotide mutation as deletion in Solanum lycopersicum pectate lyase (SlPL) gene in GE tomato line. We report (a) rapid LAMP assay targeting Cas9 protein gene, in addition to conventional PCR assay, and (b) multiplex-real time PCR for verification of single-point mutation as a deletion in the SlPL gene in tomato line edited by SDN-1 approach. The visual LAMP assay specifically targeted the Cas9 protein gene, allowing edited lines to be easily identified in an early phase through a visible colour change. This rapid and simple method facilitates the early confirmation of successful gene editing, thereby accelerating further experimentation. In addition, a multiplex real-time PCR assay using fluorescent-labelled dual probes was employed to verify the single nucleotide deletion in the GE line. This technique allowed detection of GE tomato with targeted single point deletion with detection limit up to 0.1 %. Furthermore, it was also ensured that the edited line did not carry transgenic elements as commonly present in globally approved GM tomato events38 employing real-time PCR targeting P-35S promoter and T-nos terminator.

Material and methods

Plant material and DNA extraction

Tomato DNA samples of wild type GAT5 variety (WT) and gene-edited line (GE) with single base deletion in the SlPL gene developed at Anand Agricultural University, Gujarat15were provided by late Dr. Akarsh Parihar. The GE line represented SDN-1 type and DNA sample used in the study represented the initial generation, that is, T0. Approximately 1 g seeds of each variety were homogenized to fine powder using pestle and mortar. From 200 mg of the seed powder, genomic DNA was extracted and purified using DNeasy® Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of DNA extracts were estimated by measuring UV absorbance using a Spectrophotometer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). DNA extracts were diluted to a concentration of 50 ng/μl for further use.

Amplifiability of DNA extracts was confirmed by PCR assay targeting plant-specific chloroplast t-RNA (cp-tRNA) gene39. PCR was performed in a thermal cycler (Biometra, AnalytikJena, GmbH + Co. KG) in 20 μl reaction volume containing 200 ng of template DNA, 1x polymerase buffer, 1.5 mM of MgCl2, 200 μM of dNTP mix, 0.25 μM each of forward and reverse primers, and 0.5 U of Taq DNA polymerase (ThermoFisher Scientifc, MA, USA). PCR conditions used were 94 °C for 10 min, and 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 7 min. The electrophoretic analysis was performed to resolve amplified products on 2.0 % (w/v) agarose gel (G-Biosciences) in 1xTAE running buffer. The amplification pattern of products was then visualized using UV Gel Documentation Imaging System (Alpha Innotech, SL, USA).

Strategy for detection of gene-edited (GE) tomato

A stepwise strategy was designed and used for GE detection (Fig. 1):

(i)Preliminary screening at an early phase for selection of transformants: This was accomplished using a LAMP assay targeting the Cas9 protein gene to verify the gene-editing in the GE line at the initial stage. Alternatively, a PCR assay targeting Cas9 was also employed.

(ii)Detection of the desired mutation: An assay specific to the sgRNA-guided desired target/ loci can be used to detect the modification. In the present case, where a single-point deletion occurred, a negative selection approach was employed for detection. Duplex TaqMan® real-time PCR was used, with one probe targeting the edited sequence where presence of a mutation could be determined by absence of signal and another probe outside the edited region where the signals could be detected in both the WT and GE lines. Assay through the use of probe outside the edited region would serve as an internal control of the experiment. As a result, in the WT line, both the probes would bind, generating two amplification curves. However, in the GE line, the single nucleotide mutation would disrupt the binding of probe targeting the edited sequence, resulting in only one amplification curve.

Oligonucleotide primers and probes

LAMP primers including forward and reverse primers (F3, B3), forward inner and backward inner primers (FIP, BIP) specific to the Cas9 sequence present in the GE tomato were designed with LAMP Primer Designing Software ‘PrimerExplorer V5″ (https://primerexplorer.jp/e/). For real-time PCR, primers targeting the SlPL gene (GenBank Accession No. NC_015440.3) and Probe 2 targeting the unedited sequence were designed using Primer3 (v. 0.4.0) (https://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3-0.4.0/;40, 41. The probe (P1) targeting the edited region was labelled with 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM) at the 5′ end and TAMRA at the 3′ end. The probe P2 was labelled with hexachlorinated derivate of the fluorescent dye fluorescein (HEX). The primers and probes were synthesized from M/s Pivotal Marketing, India. LAMP and PCR assays targeting Cas9 protein gene

Visual LAMP assay was performed in a 25 µl reaction volume containing 200 ng of template DNA, 1x Isothermal Amplification Buffer, 6 mM MgSO4, 1.4 mM dNTP mix, 0.2 µM each of F3 & B3 primers, and 0.4 µM each of FIP & BIP primers, and 3.2 units of Bst 2.0 WarmStart® DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Inc., MA). The reaction mixture was incubated for 1 h at 60 °C in a thermal cycler (Biometra). Once the reaction was completed, LAMP products were visually observed after addition of 0.1 µl of 10,000x SYBR® Green I (Sigma Aldrich) into the reaction mixture. Amplification in LAMP is indicated by the change in colour from orange to green upon addition of SYBR® Green I dye, whereas no change in colour is observed in the absence of LAMP amplification. Change in color is attributed to the binding of double stranded DNA intercalating dye SYBR® Green I to specific LAMP products formed by the amplification of template DNA by binding with LAMP primers for a particular target.

Alternatively, PCR was performed using same set of F3 and B3 primers in the thermal cycler (Biometra) in a 20 μl reaction volume containing 200 ng of template DNA, 1x polymerase buffer, 1.5 mM of MgCl2, 200 μM of dNTP mix, 0.25 μM each of forward and reverse primers, and 0.5 U of Taq DNA polymerase (ThermoFisher Scientifc, MA, USA). PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, and 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 50 s, 72 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 7 min. The amplified products were resolved on 2.0 % (w/v) agarose gel in 1xTAE running buffer by horizontal electrophoresis. The amplification pattern of products was then visualized using UV Gel Documentation Imaging System (Alpha Innotech).

Multiplex TaqMan® real-time PCR assay

Multiplex real-time PCR using TaqMan® chemistry was performed in a real-time PCR system (qTOWER3, AnalytikJena) in a 25 μl reaction containing 300 ng of a template DNA, 12.5 μl Probe Master Mix (Qiagen), 0.4 μl of each primer (100 μM), and 0.4 μl probe (100 μM) (for unedited sequence) and 0.2 μl probe (100 μM) (for edited sequence). The step-cycle program was 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C, and 1 min at 59.3 °C. The real-time PCR data was analyzed using qPCRsoft 4.1 software (AnalytikJena).

For determining the sensitivity of the developed assay, the DNA sample of wild type and gene-edited tomato lines was serially diluted with molecular grade water to obtain 10, 1.0, 0.1 and 0.01 % (v/v) of template DNA. These dilutions in two replications each, were subjected to the optimized conditions of multiplex TaqMan® real-time PCR. Sensitivity of the assay was determined as limit of detection (LOD) referring to the lowest amount of targeted region could be reliably detected with all the replicates of corresponding dilution series positive. Further statistical analysis for the perimeters including mean Ct values, standard deviation (SD), amplification efficiency (E), and value of regression coefficient (R2), was performed to assess the performance of developed assay.

Confirmation of non-GM status of SlPL-edited tomato line

On the basis of previously reported GMO matrix of globally approved GM tomato events, P-35S promoter and T-nos terminator together cover 100 per cent of the GM coverage in all these events7, 38. The absence of these elements would ensure the non-GM status of the SlPL-edited tomato line. Real-time PCR for these targets was performed using appropriate positive and negative controls in qTOWER3 real-time PCR thermal cycler (AnalytikJena) in a 25 μl reaction volume containing 200 ng of a template DNA, 12.5 μl Master Mix (Qiagen), 0.4 μl of each primer (10 μM), and 0.2 μl probe (10 μM) corresponding to P-35S and T-nos. The step-cycle program was 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C, and 1 min at 60 °C. The amplification curves were analysed using qPCRsoft 4.1 software.

Results and discussion

Gene editing technologies such as CRISPR-Cas9 have enabled precise modifications in plant genomes, including the introduction of small insertions, deletions, or single nucleotide substitutions. With the advancement of gene editing for crop improvement, the discussion on regulatory regime with respect to GE plants throughout the globe is overwhelming. As GE crops are proceeding towards the phase of commercialization, there is an increasing need for reliable, rapid, and regulatory-compliant detection methods, particularly in the countries that require traceability of such modifications. Given the global significance of tomato in trade and the differing regulatory frameworks for SDN-1, SDN-2, and SDN-3 products across the countries, we selected an SDN-1 gene-edited tomato with a single nucleotide deletion in pectate lyase (SlPL) gene as the study material to develop GE detection methods. We herein reported on the development of LAMP assay for quick verification of Cas9 protein gene, as an alternative to PCR and multiplex-real time PCR for detection of single-point mutation as a deletion in SlPL gene in tomato line edited by SDN-1 approach. For the regulatory purpose, the non-GM status of GE line was further confirmed to ensure absence of any foreign gene.

Preliminary screening of GE line targeting Cas9 protein gene

The genetic constructs for the gene editing comprises essential single guide (sg) RNA, the Cas9 nuclease gene and the regulatory elements of the CRISPR/Cas system. Cas9 derived from Streptococcus pyogenes is most widely used Cas nuclease for CRISPR-based gene editing43, 44. In this study, a stepwise strategy was designed for GE detection (Fig. 1) comprising assays targeting Cas9 protein gene for initial screening purpose and further specific detection of desired mutation. PCR and LAMP assays targeting Cas9 were developed, which could be utilized to verify the delivery of genetic construct for gene editing at an initial stage of transformation process. The specificity of the assays was verified by checking the amplification of Cas9 protein gene in WT (non-edited control) and other tomato varieties along with the GE line. Before specificity studies, DNA samples of GE line and other varieties were first verified for their amplifiability using cp-tRNA gene specific universal primers for plant (Fig. 2a).

On LAMP assay with Cas9 specific designed primers (Fig. 2b), positive amplification in edited line was visually detected by the change in colour from orange to green whereas no amplification was detected in WT and other tomato varieties, thereby confirming specificity of Cas9 based detection system to target the edited lines (Fig. 2c). LAMP, being a fast method with the field level applicability, could serve as a useful tool for continuous monitoring and auditing, which is crucial for upholding regulatory control at all stages of development, from research to commercialization. Further using PCR with F3 and B3 primers, specificity of products was verified where positive amplification as product of 172 bp was detected in the GE line only (Fig. 2d).

Due to conserved sequence, the screening method for detecting Cas9 in GE line could be easily adapted for use in other plant species like maize, wheat, rice, and soybeans, where CRISPR-based gene editing is frequently employed. Because of the universal applicability of the CRISPR/Cas system, these assays can be tailored to detect specific genetic constructs in different crops with minimal adjustments to the overall approach. Additionally, these assays are essential for differentiating between transformed and non-transformed edits, which enables adherence to the regulations that may allow GE crops but not the transgenics.

Detection of single nucleotide deletion in GE tomato using multiplex TaqMan® real-time PCR

The single point deletion introduced using CRISPR-Cas9 falls under the SDN-1 category of gene editing. While SDN-1 plants are exempted from regulation in the countries like India after the research phase, the detection method of such edited lines would be required for verification purpose.

Real-time PCR, especially probe-based assays, offers high specificity and sensitivity, making it a valuable platform for GE detection. In this study, a multiplex real-time PCR method was developed, utilizing primers targeting the SlPL gene, which includes the edited region, along with two labelled probes: one probe (P1) targeting the edited sequence and another (P2) targeting the unedited sequence for detection of single-point deletion in edited tomato. Both the probes P1 and P2 were differentially labelled with FAM and HEX so that the respective amplified products could be simultaneously detected in a single reaction. Probe P2 served as an internal experimental control, the product of which would be detected in both the GE and WT samples. The probe P1 specifically targets the deleted base of the edited sequence, which would allow the negative selection of GE line as no amplification of P1 would occur in the edited sample due to the deletion of a base. The detection principle is outlined in Fig. 3.

Target sequence of designed primers and differentially labelled probes P1 and P2 simultaneously targeting edited and unedited regions and principle behind the detection system allowing the negative selection of edited line where the presence of a mutation is determined by absence of signal.

Given the complexity of the reaction mixture involving primers along with Probe 1 & 2, optimization of factors such as concentration of Probe 1 & 2, concentration of DNA template, followed by the annealing temperature (Tm) for amplification of the desired product was performed (Fig. 4). As the amplification did not occur at Tm of 60 °C, therefore, the Tm was standardized using gradient real-time PCR with various temperatures during the annealing-extension step, and optimum amplification for both the probes was noted at 59.3 °C (Fig. 4). Proper amplification was obtained when using 300 ng of template DNA and 0.2 µl of P1 and 0.4 µl of P2 from 100 µM stock (Fig. 4).

Following optimization of multiplex real-time PCR, as expected, real-time PCR results showed amplification with both probes P1 and P2 in the wild-type line, with mean Ct values of 25.668 ± 0.61and 30.562 ± 0.67, respectively. In the edited line, only P2 produced amplification, with a Ct value of 22.652 ± 0.48 (Fig. 5a). The real-time PCR was repeated six times (Fig. 5a) and the amplification profile for the 1st repetition was depicted in Fig. 5b. These research findings confirmed that the single-point deletion could be comparatively detected using the multiplex TaqMan® real-time PCR system. The difference in Ct values between the wild-type and GE lines was observed for probe P2 (targeting unedited region), i.e., specifically the higher Ct in wild-type, which might be attributed to the assay design and the amplification dynamics involving P1 and P2 probes. The detection was based on the fluorescence generated from the binding of both the probes P1 and P2 at the adjacent sites on the wild type template DNA, simultaneously targeting edited and unedited regions, respectively. Thus, binding of both the probes in proximity in case of wild-type target DNA may be affected by the steric hindrances or distortion changes in the DNA by binding of second probe to an adjacent site. This might have caused delay in fluorescence detection, resulting in a higher Ct value in wild-type DNA.

Furthermore, sensitivity of the assay was determined using 10, 1.0, 0.1 and 0.01 % (v/v) of template DNA concentration of wild type and gene edited lines in two replications each. The amplification for both the probes P1 and P2 was detected in the dilution series 10, 1 and 0.1 % of wild type, whereas no amplification was observed in 0.01 % dilution series (Fig. 6a-b). Similarly, amplification for probe P2 was detected in the dilution series of edited line up to 0.1 % (Fig. 6c). The data of two replications (Table 2) was used to calculate the mean Ct values and to generate the calibration curves for each set (Fig. 6). The R2 value for each calibration curve was found > 0.987 and the amplification efficiency was within the acceptable range of 90–110 % (Table 2, Fig. 6). The standard curve for probe P1 in wild type with a range from 10-0.1 % produced an amplification efficiency of 94 %, with an R2 value of 0.997 and a slope of − 3.49. Similarly standard curve for probe P2 in wild type showed an acceptable efficiency of 103 % with R2 of 0.986 and slope of −3.24. Furthermore, standard curve for probe P2 in gene edited line with a range from 10-0.1 % showed an amplification efficiency of 93 % with R2 of 0.981 and slope of −3.5. These findings confirmed the acceptability of the assay up to an LOD of 0.1 % with accuracy. This acceptance criteria are in line with the recent findings reported in context of EU for detection of single nucleotide variation in grf1-3 gene in Arabidopsis using real-time PCR45.

Real-time PCR for detecting gene editing have been employed across different plant species. For instance, GE detection using real-time PCR has been demonstrated in Arabidopsis, maize, rice and sorghum by Peng et al.27, Moreover, single nucleotide variation in the acetolactate synthase gene was detected in commercially available GE herbicide-tolerant canola26, and the chlorophyllide a oxygenase 1 (CAO1) gene was successfully targeted in GE rice (Zhang et al. 2021). Given the growing use of SDN-1 gene editing in crops worldwide without majorly affecting the genetic makeup, detection systems such as the reported multiplex real-time PCR could play an important role in monitoring the presence of edits across different genetic loci, ensuring both research and commercialization align with international regulatory standards. This method provides a proof of principle for detecting single-point deletions in a tomato edited region and could contribute significantly to the standardization of GE detection practices across various plant species.

Although the real-time PCR is a well-established technique for molecular diagnostics, but the novelty of our approach lies in the specific application and design of a TaqMan®-based assay tailored for detection of edited sequence, particularly for single nucleotide changes introduced via CRISPR-Cas9. In this study, SlPL allele-specific probes were designed that discriminate between wild-type and edited lines with single-nucleotide mutation, which are difficult to resolve reliably using standard PCR or mismatch cleavage assays like T7Endonuclease I. The developed real-time PCR based method offers advantage over the existing methods of GE detection using digital PCR (dPCR) or sequencing in terms of cost-effectiveness and rapidity. The study aimed to the development of a rapid and cost-effective, and high-throughput detection system for GE plants using multiplex real-time PCR. TaqMan®-based real-time PCR assay offers high specificity and sensitivity due to the use of sequence-specific probes. Unlike next generation sequencing (NGS), which requires library preparation and extensive bioinformatics analysis, real-time PCR provides results within a few hours. Additionally, real-time PCR is significantly more affordable than both NGS and dPCR due to less expensive set up and running cost of real-time PCR in the laboratory, making it a practical choice for routine screening in regulatory and breeding programs. While NGS offers an unbiased and comprehensive approach to detecting genetic modifications, it has certain limitations, including being time-intensive, requiring sequencing library preparation, high-depth sequencing, and extensive data analysis. The high per-sample cost further limits its feasibility for large-scale screening. Sequencing is sometimes outsourced so the developed real-time PCR could be done with more authenticity of results at the laboratory level. Similarly, dPCR provides absolute quantification without the need for standard curves and offers superior sensitivity in detecting low-frequency mutations. However, dPCR is more expensive, requires specialized instrumentation that may not be readily available, and has lower throughput compared to real-time PCR, making it less suitable for large-scale screening of GE plants. The proposed real-time PCR assay achieves comparable sensitivity while maintaining greater scalability and cost-efficiency. While this study primarily focuses on developing a robust multiplex real-time PCR assay, future research involving direct experimental comparisons with NGS and dPCR would further validate its accuracy and applicability across different settings.

Ensuring the absence of foreign transgenic construct in GE tomato

In the countries like India, where stringent regulatory frameworks govern the commercialization of SDN3 and transgenic crops, it is critical to ensure that GE plants (particularly SDN-1 and SDN-2 categories) do not contain any foreign gene sequences, including those from vectors or transgenic constructs. As a part of the regulatory requirement, GE tomato line was subjected to real-time PCR analysis, targeting common transgenic screening elements such as P-35S promoter and T-nos terminator. These two screening elements were selected because they are commonly present in a wide range of GM crops, providing 100 % coverage for the detection of globally approved GM events in tomato, as highlighted by the GMO matrix reported by Singh et al.38. In this study, real-time PCR results showed no amplification for the P-35S promoter and T-nos terminator in the GE tomato line, confirming that no foreign transgenic constructs were present. As a control, amplification was observed in respective positive controls, with Ct values of 22.02 for the P-35S promoter and 24.11 for the T-nos terminator (Fig. 7). By screening for these elements, the absence of unintended foreign DNA can be conclusively demonstrated, which is a requirement for SDN-1 and SDN-2 plants that are not classified as GMOs. The absence of foreign transgenic sequences in GE plants is essential not only for regulatory compliance but also for public acceptance and market access. Countries that differentiate between GE crops and traditional GMOs often have specific requirements to ensure no remnants of vector or transgenic DNA are left in the final product.

Conclusion

In the context of global regulatory framework associated with GE plants and the complexity therein, accurate, rapid and robust GE detection tools are of paramount importance. In this context, the present study is quite relevant. This study underscores development of molecular assays for the detection of gene editing in SlPL gene in tomato using multiplex TaqMan® real-time PCR. The absence of foreign transgenic constructs was confirmed through real-time PCR targeting common screening elements (P-35S promoter and T-nos terminator), ensuring compliance with regulatory requirements for GE crops. Thus, this work bridges a critical methodological gap between gene editing research in tomato and its regulatory oversight. The multiplex real-time PCR using differentially labelled TaqMan® probes simultaneously targeting edited and unedited sequences can also be applicable for detection of GE plants modified through addition of base(s), provided one of the probes targeting the specific gene-edit, i.e., the edited sequence with insertion of base(s). The developed method was based on the negative selection of edited region where the presence of a mutation was determined by absence of signal when compared to the wild-type. However, the unedited region would be well amplified in edited line, serving as an internal control of the assay. Furthermore, the techniques developed can be adapted to other crops and gene editing platforms, offering a valuable tool for research, regulatory verification, and product differentiation in the emerging field of gene editing, thereby contributing to the safe commercialization of GE plants.
