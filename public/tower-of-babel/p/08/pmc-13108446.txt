Effect of leucine-enriched essential amino acid supplementation combined with different exercise regimen on appendicular skeletal muscle mass and muscle performance in older adults: an open label randomized controlled trial
Authors: Chintrai Thavonlun, Jirawat Limsapjaroen, Praesiri Yoosuk, Chantas Mahaisavariya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcopenia in older adults increases fall risk and frailty. Improving muscle mass and function enhances balance and supports basic activities of daily living. Leucine-enriched essential amino acid supplementation combined with resistance training has been shown to enhance muscle performance. The present study evaluated muscle performance outcomes after 6 mo of these treatments. In an open-label randomized controlled trial, 66 participants were randomized equally to control and intervention groups (n = 33 each). Both groups consumed daily leucine-enriched essential amino acid supplements after exercise. The intervention group performed resistance training, and the control group followed regular walking and strengthening exercises. Primary outcomes were appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI), gait speed, and hand grip strength. Safety was assessed by adverse event reporting and laboratory tests. The results of this study show no significant improvement in ASMI in either group after 6 mo. Both groups demonstrated significant gait speed improvements, with no between-group difference. Hand grip strength improved only in the non-dominant hand of the control group. No serious adverse events occurred, and kidney function remained stable. In conclusion, 6 mo of daily leucine-enriched essential amino acid supplementation combined with exercise did not increase ASMI among healthy older adults. Gait speed showed a positive association with supplementation and daily exercise regardless of regimen. Improvement in hand grip strength was observed only in the non-dominant hand, warranting further investigation to clarify the effect of supplementation combined with exercise. Additional clinical studies are required to establish the effectiveness of this regimen in preserving muscle mass and enhancing functional performance. Clinical trial registration: This study has been registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (ID: TCTR20210824002).

Introduction

Sarcopenia in older adults causes decreased muscle strength and size, leading to frailty, increased fall risk, and associated health economic burdens.1 Fragility fractures such as hip and vertebral fractures occur after falls in osteoporotic patients, with high comorbidity, mortality, and reduced ability to walk or perform basic activities of daily living.2–4

Improving skeletal muscle function reduces frailty incidence in older adults.5 Enhanced lower-extremity strength improves gait and balance, while greater muscle mass and force enhance bone quality and preserve bone density.6,7 Therefore, resistance training combined with adequate nutritional support is recommended for older adults to increase muscle power and mass.8

Leucine-enriched essential amino acid supplements increase muscle protein synthesis.9 Some studies also report improved muscle function in sarcopenic patients following such supplementation.10 However, the long-term effects on appendicular skeletal muscle mass remain unclear. This study evaluated the combined effect of leucine-enriched amino acid supplements and resistance training on appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) and muscle function in healthy older adults. We hypothesized that combining these supplements with specific resistance exercises would increase the ASMI in this population.

Materials and methods

We conducted a single-center, open-label, randomized controlled trial at the Golden Jubilee Medical Center from September 2024 to May 2025. The trial received approval from the affiliated university institutional review board. Randomization used a computer-generated sequence and opaque-seal envelopes to ensure allocation concealment.

We enrolled healthy older adults, both men and women, aged ≥65 yr and obtained written informed consent after explaining trial details. Exclusion criteria were prior stroke; poor standing balance; stage 4 CKD per estimated glomerular filtration rate; high fall risk by Thai Fall Risk Assessment Tool score; or impaired mental status by Thai Mental State Examination.

After randomization, a physician opened sealed envelopes on the first visit in this open-label trial to assign participants to the control group (regular exercise) or the intervention group (resistance training). Both groups consumed a sachet of leucine-enriched essential amino acids (LEAAs) (AminoL40 (R); containing 40% leucine and 60% other essential amino acids) after daily exercise. On non-exercise days, participants took the supplement in the late morning or afternoon between meals. Each sachet provided 3 g leucine-enriched amino acid protein, 2 g carbohydrate, and 10 mg potassium dissolved in 100-200 mL water at room temperature. Participants consumed the supplement daily for 180 d.

Participants in the control group performed standing and walking exercises: at least 30 min of walking, 5 d per week, plus quadriceps strengthening at the same frequency. The intervention group completed 5 specific resistance exercises with identical frequency: hip raises in the supine position; side straight-leg raises in the lateral decubitus position; seated quadriceps strengthening; single-leg standing; and 10-min marching.

Follow-up visits occurred at 3 and 6 mo. Laboratory tests at enrollment and at the final visit comprised measurements of blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and albumin. Kidney function was calculated as the estimated glomerular filtration rate using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula. ASMI was assessed at both visits using a bioelectrical impedance analysis device routinely employed in the hospital’s geriatric clinic.

Muscle performance was evaluated at each visit. Gait speed, calculated over a clinic-marked distance, was reported in meters per second. Hand grip strength was measured twice on each hand, and the highest value for the dominant and non-dominant hand (in kg) was recorded using an analog dynamometer. A registered nurse holding a Master of Nursing Science degree in Gerontological Nursing performed all evaluations. The Barthel Index and exercise frequency were assessed via an exercise diary and a standard questionnaire. Adverse events were monitored at each visit and reported to the physician, who could withdraw participants to prevent serious harm.

The primary outcome was change in ASMI after 6 mo. The secondary outcomes were improvements in gait speed, hand grip strength, quality of life, and adverse events related to supplementation or exercise. A third-party analyst blinded to group allocation performed all data analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29 (IBM Corp). Sample size was based on the mean ASMI of 5.99 ± 0.72 kg/m2 in Thai women aged ≥65 yr. We hypothesized that the intervention would increase ASMI by approximately 1 SD from the population mean. To achieve 90% power at α = .05, 27 participants per group were required. Allowing for a 20% dropout rate, 33 participants per group were enrolled.

Demographic variables were summarized as means with standard deviations for continuous data and as percentages for categorical data. Within-group comparisons of continuous outcomes vs baseline used the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test, based on distribution. Between-group comparisons of outcome improvements employed the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate.

Results

Sixty-six participants were enrolled and randomized equally. Both groups had 1 participant who did not complete the study. Two participants randomized to the resistance-training group were later discovered—due to an initial screening error—to have been 64 yr old at study completion; they were therefore excluded to uphold the ≥65-yr enrollment criterion. One regular-exercise participant as withdrawn at 3 mo due to supplement-related hyperglycemia. A resistance-training participant was also withdrawn due to proteinuria and underlying stage 3A CKD. Ultimately, 60 participants were analyzed: 29 in the resistance-training group and 31 in the regular-exercise group (Figure 1). Change in ASMI

The ASMI was measured in all participants using the bioelectrical impedance analysis device. At baseline, the mean ASMI was 6.54 ± 1.27 kg/m2 in the regular-exercise group and 6.80 ± 1.15 kg/m2 in the resistance-training group. No statistically significant change was observed between groups after 6 mo of the clinical trial (−0.14 ± 1.59 kg/m2 vs +0.10 ± 1.58 kg/m2, p = .877; Table 2). Moreover, the ASMI did not show statistically significant improvement from baseline after 6 mo of supplementation combined with exercise in either group (Figure 2, Table 3).

Change in gait speed

Gait speed showed no statistically significant difference between the regular-exercise and resistance-training groups after 6 mo (1.75 ± 0.24 m/s vs 1.73 ± 0.24 m/s, p = .83). The improvement in gait speed was similar between groups, with no statistically significant difference (0.63 ± 0.29 m/s vs 0.61 ± 0.27 m/s, p = .802; Figure 3, Table 2). However, improvement in gait speed from baseline was observed in both groups at 3 and 6 mo (Table 3).

Change in hand grip strength

One regular-exercise participant lacked baseline hand grip data and was excluded, leaving 30 participants for this analysis. There was no statistically significant difference in hand grip strength improvement between the regular-exercise and resistance-training groups after 6 mo of the clinical trial (Table 2; Figures 4 and 5).

Subgroup analysis compared within-group changes separately for the non-dominant and dominant hands. The regular-exercise group showed statistically significant non-dominant hand strength gains at each visit compared with baseline. In contrast, the resistance-training group improved at 3 mo but not at 6 mo (Table 3; Figures 4 and 5).

For the dominant hand, only the regular-exercise group improved at 3 mo, with no sustained change at 6 mo; the resistance-training group showed no statistically significant changes (Table 3; Figures 4 and 5).

Adverse events

Two participants experienced adverse events. One reported elevated blood glucose on a routine home test; no serious side effects occurred, but the participant withdrew to achieve better control diabetes. Another participant developed proteinuria at the 3-mo visit. Although this participant’s CKD stage remained unchanged, the estimated glomerular filtration rate decreased from 49 mL/min/1.73 m2 at baseline to 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 at month 3. The participant was withdrawn for safety.

No serious adverse events related to supplementation or exercise occurred.

Quality of life

Quality of life, assessed by the Barthel Index, remained at 100 points from baseline to 6 mo. Seven participants (11%) lost their exercise diaries, resulting in incomplete exercise frequency data and precluding analysis of the relationship between exercise frequency and outcomes (Figure 6).

Exercise diary compliance and data completeness caption: proportion of participants with complete exercise diaries over 6 mo. Overall diary loss was 12%, limiting the power to assess the relationship between exercise frequency and outcomes.

Changes in laboratory investigations

All participants underwent laboratory testing according to protocol (Table 4). No significant differences were observed in any domain, and no participant’s underlying disease worsened. Estimated glomerular filtration rate and creatinine remained stable. To avoid reporting bias, values from the withdrawn proteinuria participant were included: the mean estimated glomerular filtration rate was 80.75 vs 77.41 mL/min/1.73 m2 (p = .129), and the mean creatinine was 0.87 vs 0.90 mg/dL (p = .336).

Discussion

Reduced muscle mass and function contribute to frailty in older adults.11 As global populations age, frailty and sarcopenia incidence has risen, increasing morbidity and mortality.1,12 Elevated fall risk in these individuals may result in osteoporotic vertebral or hip fractures,5 impairing basic activities of daily living or causing some to become bedridden.2–4 Enhancing muscle strength, gait stability, and preserving muscle mass can mitigate sarcopenia and its complications.13

Adequate nutritional intake combined with regular resistance training improves muscle function.8,10,14,15 In healthy older Japanese women, 24 wk of resistance exercise plus whey protein supplementation increased grip strength and gait speed.16 Additionally, daily intake of 20 g whey protein enhances muscle anabolism,8,17 but may induce early satiety and abdominal discomfort in older adults.9,18

Leucine-enriched essential amino acid supplementation shows clinical evidence supporting its potential to enhance muscle anabolism. Wilkinson et al. reported elevated muscle anabolism biomarkers associated with increased muscle mass synthesis.18 However, the long-term effects of combined leucine supplementation and resistance training remain unclear.

The present study examined the effects of 6-mo administration of leucine-enriched essential amino acid supplementation combined with either regular or resistance-training exercise. The results showed no improvement in ASMI with either type of exercise combined with supplementation. Although ASMI showed only a maintenance effect, muscle function improvement was established—statistically significant gains in gait speed were observed in both exercise groups.16,19 This evidence suggests a positive effect of supplementation combined with daily exercise regardless of regimen. Nevertheless, further study is needed to demonstrate the true effect of leucine supplementation on mobility because the trial was not powered for gait speed and did not compare supplementation alone without exercise.

Hand grip strength increased predominantly in the non-dominant hand, which is less influenced by daily activities and exercise, because both regimens targeted lower-extremity exercises to prevent falls. This pattern suggested that supplementation may have more influence on hand grip strength improvement than exercise alone. However, the study was underpowered for this outcome. Moreover, the upper-extremity exercise program was neither controlled nor restricted, which may confound interpretation of the relationship between leucine-enriched essential amino acid supplementation and hand grip strength. Further research is needed to confirm the independent effect of supplementation on hand grip strength.

Optimal gains in muscle size and function require adequate nutrient intake and good nutritional status. Older adults often have reduced appetite and stomach capacity, limiting meal volume; thus, improving dietary quality is preferable. Bhukari et al. found that 3 g of leucine-enriched essential amino acids produces an anabolic effect on muscle synthesis comparable to 20 g of whey protein.9 A smaller supplement dose may suit older adults better, as it is easier to consume and causes less abdominal discomfort.9,18 However, nutritional status did not improve in this study, as serum albumin levels remained unchanged.

Clinical application of leucine-enriched supplements remains limited, with most evidence derived from in vitro or short-term studies. Our trial involved healthy older adults rather than patients with sarcopenia, which may limit generalizability. Although the present study did not demonstrate improvements in muscle mass, the results revealed a positive association between supplementation combined with exercise and improved muscle function. Furthermore, the supplementation was well tolerated, with no observed decline in renal function as measured by estimated glomerular filtration rate. More clinical trials in patients with sarcopenia or fragility fractures are warranted, prioritizing safety and patient benefit.

The current study has several limitations. First, participants were healthy older volunteers with above-average baseline muscle function, possibly inflating observed improvements. Second, although the intervention focused on specific resistance exercises, both groups received hip and leg-strengthening guidance to reduce fall risk, which may have minimized between-group differences in gait speed. Third, verifying exercise sequence was difficult because it depended on participant recall, and lost or forgotten diaries rendered frequency data unreliable. Finally, neither participants nor evaluators could be fully blinded. We selected ASMI as the primary outcome because it is objective, operator-independent, and represents the first long-term assessment of this supplementation strategy. However, ASMI may not fully capture functional gains. Moreover, the hydration status of participants was not controlled in the study protocol, which may have affected ASMI results obtained by bioelectrical impedance analysis. Future studies with larger samples and functional primary outcomes are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

Six months of daily leucine-enriched essential amino acid supplementation combined with exercise did not increase ASMI among healthy older adults. Gait speed showed a positive association with supplementation and daily exercise regardless of regimen. Improvement in hand grip strength was observed only in the non-dominant hand, warranting further investigation to clarify the effect of supplementation combined with exercise. Additional clinical studies are required to establish the effectiveness of this regimen in preserving muscle mass and enhancing functional performance.
