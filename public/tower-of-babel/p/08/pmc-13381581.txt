Plasticity of subfornical organ astrocyte structure and supraoptic nucleus hormone expression following extreme dehydration in the desert rodent Gerbillus tarabuli
Authors: Nadir Benhafri, Soumia Remana, Amina Derbouz Rouibate, Zahra Simone Thirouin, Aicha Dekar‐Madoui, Charles W. Bourque, Saliha Ouali‐Hassenaoui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe central nervous system plays a pivotal role in the regulation of water and electrolyte balance, largely via the circumventricular organs (CVOs) and the hypothalamo‐neurohypophysial system. Gerbillus tarabuli, a desert‐dwelling rodent capable of surviving in arid environments, remains poorly characterized with respect to the neuroendocrine mechanisms underlying its hydromineral adaptation In the present study, we examined the effects of long‐term dehydration (4 weeks) in G. tarabuli. We focused on glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression in the subfornical organ (SFO), as well as vasopressin (VP) and oxytocin (OT) expression in magnocellular neurons (MCNs) of the supraoptic nucleus (SON) using immunohistochemistry. Physiological parameters, including body mass, plasma osmolality, sodium concentration, hematocrit, and glycemia, were also assessed. In dehydrated gerbils, the observed changes in body mass, plasma osmolality, natremia, and glycemia were not significantly different from those of euhydrated gerbils. In contrast, hematocrit was significantly increased (5.53%), corresponding to a 15.97% reduction in plasma volume. Long‐term dehydration induced a marked increase in GFAP immunoreactivity within the SFO, concomitant with a significant increase in VP and OT immunoreactivity and enlargement of magnocellular soma size in the SON. Additionally, the SON of G. tarabuli showed a mediolateral extension in the dorsolateral region of the optic chiasm (OC). Together, these findings identify G. tarabuli as a valuable model for investigating the neuroendocrine mechanisms underlying hydromineral homeostasis and the central adaptations supporting survival in arid environments.

INTRODUCTION

Desert environments are marked by extreme heat, sharp day–night temperature fluctuations, very low humidity, and scarce, unpredictable rainfall. Under such harsh conditions, both food and water are often limited or unavailable. 1 Small desert mammals, particularly rodents, have developed an impressive suite of adaptations that enable them to survive without access to free water. 2 These animals rely mainly on dietary water, metabolic water production, and on highly efficient renal and endocrine mechanisms to maintain hydromineral balance. 3 , 4 Such strategies allow them not only to survive but also to remain active within the extreme and unpredictable constraints of arid environments. 5 Water equilibrium relies strongly on the hypothalamo‐neurohypophysial system, where the release of vasopressin (VP) and oxytocin (OT) from magnocellular neurons (MCNs) of the supraoptic nucleus (SON) and paraventricular nucleus (PVN) play fundamental roles in controlling water conservation, natriuresis, and osmotic stability. 6 In addition, the circumventricular organs, such as the subfornical organ (SFO) and Organum vasculosum lamina terminalis (OVLT), are specialized sensory structures that lack a blood–brain barrier. These regions regulate magnocellular system activity by integrating circulating osmotic and hormonal signals, thereby initiating a coordinated neuroendocrine response to dehydration. 7 Among circumventricular organs, the subfornical organ (SFO) was specifically targeted in this study due to its privileged position as a primary integrative hub for circulating osmotic and hormonal signals and its strong functional connectivity with hypothalamic magnocellular neurons controlling vasopressin and oxytocin release, making it particularly relevant for investigating long‐term dehydration‐induced neuroendocrine and glial adaptations. In parallel, glial cells, particularly astrocytes, exhibit morphological plasticity associated with change in glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression, a major intermediate filament protein that contributes to the organization of astrocytic processes 8 which modulates interaction with neuronal and neural vascular circuits, leading to significant functional consequences. 9 Under physiological conditions, such as lactation or dehydration, the distal processes undergo reversible structural changes that alter coverage of neurons and synapses, ultimately influencing neuroendocrine activity. 10 , 11

Despite their remarkable tolerance to arid conditions, the neurobiological mechanisms supporting dehydration resistance in North African desert gerbils Gerbillus tarabuli remain poorly documented. Understanding how this species withstands prolonged water deprivation offers a unique opportunity to identify adaptive features of neuroendocrine and glial systems involved in hydromineral homeostasis.

In the present study, we aimed to characterize the effect of long‐term dehydration in the desert rodent G. tarabuli, with particular focus on state‐dependent glial remodeling within the SFO and changes in vasopressin and oxytocin expression in MCNs of the SON. To achieve this, we combined immunohistochemical analyses with physiological measurements including plasma osmolality, sodium concentration, hematocrit, and glycemia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Adult male and female nocturnal rodents of the Gerbillinae family G. tarabuli classified as young adults based on body weight (33–51 g). Animals were captured in the wilaya of Beni Abbas, southwestern Algeria (30°4′48″ N 2°6′0″ W), a region characterized by an arid climate. Gerbils were transported to the laboratory in Algiers and housed individually in separate cages (25 × 46 × 20 cm) under controlled room temperature (23 ± 2°C), relative humidity (50%–60%), and with a 12/12 h light–dark cycle. The animals had free access to water and standard dried rat pellets. Prior to the initiation of the experiment, the animals were acclimated to these conditions for a period of 2 weeks. The present study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the “Association Algérienne des Sciences en Expérimentation Animale” (http://www.aasea.asso.dz/).

Experimental procedure

The gerbils were randomly divided into two experimental groups and subjected to different hydration conditions for 28 days: each experimental group comprised three males and either three or four females depending on availability; euhydrated gerbils group (n = 6): received standard dried rat pellets with free access to water; dehydrated gerbils group (n = 7): received only standard dried rat pellets.

One animal from the dehydrated group died during the experimental period and was excluded from the final analysis. As a result, the final sample size for this group was n = 6. Food was provided ad libitum in both groups.

Body weight

Animals were weighed on a daily basis between 10:00 and 12:00 a.m. for 28 consecutive days. Weight changes were calculated by the formula ∆BW(%) = [(BWfinal − BMinitial)/BMinitial] × 100.

Blood parameters

Blood was collected at the beginning of experiment (day 0) and at the end (day 28) by retro‐orbital puncture. Hematocrit levels were determined directly using capillary tubes centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min (Hematokif‐210), and blood glucose levels were measured using a glucometer. The change in plasma volume was calculated according to the formula % PV = [100 × (Htbefore − Htafter)/Htbefore (100 − Htafter)] × 100, 12 where %PV is the percentage of variation of plasma volume; Htbefore, the hematocrit at day 0; Htafter, the hematocrit at day 28. The plasma osmolality, collected in heparinized tubes, was analyzed by cryogenesis freezing point measurement using an osmometer (Loeser type 6 M). The sodium concentration was estimated using the following equation Osmp = 2 × [Na+] + [glucose]. 13

Tissue preparation

At the end of 4‐week experimental period, the animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 25% urethane (1 mL/100 g) and subsequently fixed through intracardiac perfusion using 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) (0.1 M, pH 7.4). Brains were carefully removed and post‐fixed in the same fixative solution for 48 h. Tissues were then washed in water, dehydrated through graded ethanol series, treated with butanol and embedded in paraffin. Serial coronal sections of the sample (20 μm thick) were sliced using a microtome (HistoCore BIOCUT, Leica Biosystems). Sections containing the subfornical organ (SFO) and the supraoptic nucleus (SON) were collected and mounted onto glass slides.

Immunohistochemistry

Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through decreasing concentrations of ethanol and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X‐100 for 10 min followed by blocking in 2% goat serum for 1 h. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: vasopressin (VP) guinea pig antibody 1/1000 (403,004, SySy), mouse oxytocin antibody (OT) 1/50 (PS38), kindly provided by Prof. Harold Gainer (Bethesda, Maryland, USA, NIH). For GFAP immunolabeling, two validated primary antibodies raised in different host species, mouse Anti‐GFAP (GA5) 1/1000 (3670T, Cell Signaling Technology) and chicken anti‐GFAP 1:1000 (Ab4674, abcam) were available and selected according to the combination of primary antibodies used in each immunofluorescence experiment. This approach provided flexibility in antibody selection by allowing the use of species‐specific secondary antibodies when different combinations of primary antibodies were required, thereby avoiding cross‐reactivity and fluorochrome overlap. In the experiments presented in Figure 3, GFAP expression was assessed exclusively by single immunolabeling. No GFAP co‐immunostaining data are presented in the current study. The next day, sections were rinsed in PBS (3× 10 min), permeabilized again by 0.25% Triton X‐100 for 10 min, then incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h: Alexa fluor 488 Goat Anti‐guinea pig 1/500 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. A‐11073, RRID AB_2534117, Waltham, Massachusetts), Alexa fluor 647 Goat anti‐mouse 1/500 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. A‐21236, RRID AB_2535805, Waltham, Massachusetts) and Alexa fluor 568 Goat anti‐Chicken 1/500 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. A‐11041, RRID AB_2534098, Waltham, Massachusetts). Finally, sections were rinsed in PBS and cover‐slipped using Prolong Gold antifade reagent (Invitrogen).

Imaging and quantification

Sections were visualized and imaged using an FV 3000 inverted confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus Canada Inc., Richmond Hill, Ontario) with 10× and 20× objectives. Image analysis was performed with ImageJ software (NIH) to measure the soma area of MCNs and to quantify the mean intensity of VP and OT in SON. Images were acquired from both the left and the right sides of the nuclei in three animals per group. Thus, for each experimental group, data points represent six SONs (three animals × two hemispheres). The co‐localization of VP/OT was quantified in the SON of three animals per group. In addition, GFAP in SFO was quantified in four animals of each group. A confocal plane was selected in which the core of SFO was visible. A threshold intensity value was determined, and the image was converted to grayscale and binarized. A region of interest (ROI: 100 × 100 μm) in the SFO core was drawn, and the mean intensity was measured, and its relative percentage coverage was calculated (Rel% = mean/max). The mean intensity values are reported in arbitrary units. The maximal intensity value (max) was defined as the brightest pixel intensity within the same confocal image, allowing normalization across sections.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (Graph‐Pad software). Unpaired t‐test was used to compare the mean intensity of GFAP, VP, OT, and co‐localization VP/OT in the euhydrated and dehydrated gerbils. Two‐way ANOVA was applied to compare blood parameters and weight of the two groups, followed by Tukey's post‐hoc test for multiple comparisons when significant main effects or interactions were detected. All data are expressed as the means ± SEM. Differences were considered statistically significant when *p < .05.

RESULTS

Body weight

Over a period of 28 days, a non‐significant increase in body weight was observed in hydrated gerbils (6.85%). In contrast, dehydrated gerbils displayed a non‐significant decrease in body weight (−2.08%). However, there was a highly significant effect of time on body weight of dehydrated animals (p < .0001) compared to hydrated animals, independent of hydration status (Figure 1).

Body weight evolution over 28 days. No significant difference in body weight was observed between euhydrated and dehydrated gerbils at any time point (two‐way ANOVA, effect of hydration status: p > .05; time × hydration interaction: p > .05). A significant main effect of time was detected (p < .0001), reflecting daily fluctuations. The slight increase in body weight of dehydrated gerbils from day 14 onward was not significant compared to day 0 (post‐hoc Tukey, p > .05). Values are expressed as mean ± SEM.

Blood parameters

In the end of the experiment, the dehydrated group's hematocrit level had increased by 5.53%, which corresponds to 15.97% reduction in plasma volume without significant difference (p > .05). In contrast, the euhydrated group's hematocrit level had decreased by 8.16%, representing 25.38% increase in plasma volume, which also no statistically significant (p > .05). However, comparison of hematocrit variation between the two groups of gerbils revealed a statistically significant difference (p < .05) (Figure 2A). Plasma sodium concentration [Na+], did not differ significantly between the two groups (p > .05). A non‐significant increase of 3.90% in [Na+] was observed for the dehydrated gerbils, while values remained stable in the hydrated animals. (Figure 2B). Similarly, plasma osmolality showed no significant difference (p > .05) with a slight increase of 0.46% in euhydrated and 4.28% in dehydrated gerbils. (Figure 2C). In addition, blood glucose level increased by 19.36% in the euhydrated and 17.08% in the dehydrated gerbils. This variation was significant over time (p < .05), regardless of hydration status (Figure 2D).

Immunocytochemistry

The glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is a major component of the cytoskeletal architecture of the astrocyte and a histochemical marker. 14 , 15 Immunolabeling of GFAP showed a high protein intensity throughout the SFO, and around blood vessels in the dehydrated gerbils, compared to the euhydrated group. Intensity and coverage increase significantly (p < .01) by 80% and 86% respectively with pronounced increase around the blood vessels (yellow arrowhead) (Figure 3).

VP and OT expression in MNCs of the SON

Analysis of coronal sections of the SON revealed large extensions of the nucleus in the dorso‐lateral part of the optic chiasma (OC). Compared with the euhydrated group, animals subjected to 28 days of dehydration demonstrated a significant increase in soma area and immunoreactivity of VP magnocellular neurons by 51.39% (p < .01) and 91.19% (p < .05), respectively (Figure 4). A similar significant increase (p < .05) was observed in the soma area and immunoreactivity of OT neurons by 53.48% and 48.96% respectively (Figure 5). Furthermore, analyses of frontal SON sections revealed magnocellular neurons that exhibited a significant 18% increase in dual immunofluorescence in dehydrated gerbils (p < .05) (Figure 6).

DISCUSSION

Desert rodents have evolved physiological adaptations that enable them to maintain water and electrolyte homeostasis despite prolonged droughts and the absence of reliable sources of free water. 16

Body weight

The present study showed a non‐significant decrease in body weight in dehydrated G. tarabuli compared to euhydrated gerbils. Similar observations have been reported in several semi‐arid and arid rodent species subjected to acute or chronic water deprivation. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Desert rodents can partially compensate for water availability by oxidizing fatty acids, thereby producing metabolic water. 22 In our study, body weight in dehydrated gerbils increased slightly from day 14, without reaching statistical significance, and remained below baseline until the end of the experiment. A comparable pattern was observed in the Australian desert rodent Notomys alexis after the 7th day of dehydration. 23 Under laboratory conditions, several arid‐zone rodents increase their food intake when water is restricted. 24 , 25 Dietary intake has been identified as an important source of metabolic water in these species. 3 , 26 , 27 , 28 Taken together, these observations indicate that G. tarabuli is able to maintain a relatively stable body mass during prolonged dehydration.

Blood parameters

At the end of the experiment, hematocrit was significantly increased in the dehydrated group indicating a hypovolemic state compared to euhydrated gerbils. This finding is consistent with hematocrit's role as a reliable marker of plasma volume and indicator of body fluid loss. 29 , 30 Similar findings have been reported in small desert and semi‐desert rodents subjected to dehydration 17 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 as well as in rats 12 , 35 , 36 , 37 and large desert‐adapted mammals such as camel. 38 , 39 In contrast, other small desert rodents subjected to water deprivation did not show significant hematocrit changes. 17 , 30 , 40 , 41 , 42 Plasma sodium levels remained stable between the two groups of gerbils, consistent with observations in certain desert rodents species, 23 , 30 whereas increases in sodium during dehydration have been described in other rodent species and rats 33 , 43 , 44 as well in camel. 45 Similarly, osmolality did not differ significantly between the two experimental groups, consistent with findings in Notomys alexis, 46 Steatomys caurinus, Taterillus gracilis, 32 whereas other rodents, including Dipodomys spectabilis, 31 Meriones libycus, 33 Jaculus spp, 42 , 47 rats 29 , 48 and camel 38 exhibit marked increases in osmolality under dehydration. Glucose levels were not statistically significant, consistent with previous studies in rats 49 and Notomys alexis, 23 despite reports of altered glycemia in other rats models and in vitro studies 50 , 51 , 52 and camel. 39 Overall, these results indicate that G. tarabuli maintains key plasma parameters during prolonged dehydration, reflecting effective hydromineral homeostasis. Taken together, this physiological profile supports a volume‐dominant regulatory strategy in this species, in which hypovolemia constitutes the primary internal signal driving adaptive responses, while osmotic parameters remain tightly controlled.

Overall, this comparative analysis reveals a continuum of adaptive strategies to dehydration across mammals. Desert‐adapted rodents such as G. tarabuli and Notomys alexis maintain stable plasma osmotic parameters despite hypovolemia, indicating a predominant reliance on volume‐dependent regulatory mechanisms. Meriones libycus exhibits an intermediate phenotype, combining partial osmotic adjustment with hemoconcentration. In contrast, rats display marked increases in plasma sodium and osmolality, reflecting a pronounced osmotic disturbance and limited dehydration tolerance. Jaculus spp. and the dromedary camel (Camelus dromedarius) represent progressively more osmotic‐tolerant strategies, characterized by elevated plasma sodium and osmolality. At the neuroendocrine and cellular levels, vasopressin upregulation appears conserved across species, whereas oxytocin and astroglial responses show greater interspecies variability. Together, these findings support a graded spectrum of hydromineral regulation ranging from strict homeostasis to extreme osmotic tolerance.

Immunohistochemistry

Glial remodeling within SFO

The subfornical organ plays a central role in hydromineral regulation due to its strategic location outside the blood–brain barrier and its function as a critical osmosensory structure that detects changes in circulating signaling molecules such as sodium concentration and osmolality. 53 , 54 In the present study, however, the absence of significant changes in these parameters indicates that classical osmotic stimuli are unlikely to account for SFO activation during prolonged dehydration. Instead, the significant increase in hematocrit supports the presence of hypovolemia, suggesting that the SFO in G. tarabuli preferentially responds to volume‐related signals under these conditions. This interpretation is consistent with activation of the renin–angiotensin system, as hypovolemia is known to elevate circulating angiotensin II, a potent activator of SFO neurons. 55 , 56 , 57 Although angiotensin II was not directly measured in this study, the physiological context is consistent with its involvement. Similar volume‐dominant neuroendocrine adaptations have been reported in other desert rodents including N. alexis where prolonged dehydration induces volume‐dependent adjustments without major osmotic disturbances. 23 Together, these findings suggest that the SFO primarily integrates hypovolemia‐related hormonal signals rather than direct osmotic changes in this model. In this context, angiotensin II‐dependent signaling pathways likely constitute a key upstream driver of SFO activation, overriding the relatively stable plasma osmotic environment. Thus, rather than functioning primarily as a classical osmotic sensor in this species, the SFO appears to operate as a volume‐sensitive neuroendocrine integrator, preferentially coupling hypovolemic signals to hypothalamic output pathways. At the cellular level, this integrative function likely involves the recruitment of astrocytic signaling and angiotensin II‐responsive pathways, enabling the encoding of circulating volume status independently of stable osmotic parameters.

Consistent with this framework, we observed a marked, state‐dependent remodeling of GFAP‐immunoreactive astrocytes within the SFO. In euhydrated gerbils, GFAP immunolabelling was largely confined to the dorsolateral region of the SFO, in agreement with previous reports in rat, 58 , 59 whereas dehydration induced a robust and widespread increase in GFAP immunoreactivity throughout the structure. The increase in GFAP immunoreactivity is consistent with astroglial activation under dehydration, and with previous studies describing the dynamic structural plasticity of astrocytes including GFAP reorganization and remodeling of astrocytic processes under physiological conditions 60 , 61 , 62 with or without astrocytic hypertrophy. 63 GFAP intermediate filaments are highly dynamic and capable of polymerization and depolymerization. 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 In addition, sodium‐sensitive Nax channels have been identified in SFO astrocytes, where they are proposed to contribute to the regulation of neuronal activity via metabolic signaling pathways, 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 potentially enabling the integration of local ionic cues independently of systemic osmolality. 72 Although not directly investigated here, such mechanisms may contribute to fine‐tuning SFO responsiveness during dehydration. In dehydrated gerbils, the increased GFAP immunoreactivity in both parenchymal and perivascular regions suggests structural remodeling of astrocytic end‐feet, potentially modulating neurovascular coupling and the integration of circulating osmotic and hormonal signals. 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 In this context, astrocytic remodeling may also influence neuronal excitability through modulation of extracellular ionic homeostasis and metabolic coupling. Collectively, these data support a shift in the classical osmoregulatory paradigm toward a volume‐driven mode of central fluid homeostasis in G. tarabuli.

Regardless of the primary nature of the signal, osmoreceptive information arising from circumventricular organs, including the SFO and the OVLT, 78 , 79 , 80 is transmitted to the SON via synaptic inputs and integrated within magnocellular neurons. 81 , 82 , 83 In this context, circulating angiotensin II, a key signal of hypovolemia, enhances SFO neuronal activity and potentiates synaptic transmission toward hypothalamic targets, thereby amplifying excitatory drive onto SON magnocellular neurons. 84 The convergence of hormonal and afferent inputs ultimately regulates vasopressin and oxytocin secretion, contributing to the maintenance of systemic hydromineral balance. 85 , 86 , 87 In this framework, the SFO–SON axis can be viewed as a functional continuum through which volume‐related signals are translated into coordinated neuroendocrine outputs.

Vasopressinergic adaptation

The supraoptic nucleus (SON) acts as a major hypothalamic output node within the SFO‐driven osmoregulatory network, integrating convergent glutamatergic and angiotensin II‐mediated inputs to regulate magnocellular neurosecretory activity. 88 , 89 , 90 Within the SON, we observed a large medio‐lateral extension in the dorso‐lateral region of the optic chiasm, as previously reported in other Gerbillinae family, such as Psammomys obesus and Meriones libycus 33 , 91 and Jaculus spp. 47 In contrast, in rats, the SON is largely confined to the lateral border of the optic chiasm. 92 Prolonged dehydration for 28 days was associated with a significant increase in VP, OT immunoreactivity, indicating enhanced synthesis and storage of these hormones, potentially supporting increased hormone release from the posterior pituitary gland. 93 Regarding vasopressin, similar upregulation of VP immunostaining during dehydration, water deprivation or dry diet has been reported in several desert rodents 17 , 18 , 33 , 94 , 95 as well as in rat. 96 Increased hypothalamic VP expression has been documented in Mongolian gerbils exposed to high‐salt drinking water. 97 Numerous studies have shown that plasma VP concentration increases proportionally with the rise of osmolality and reduced plasma volume. 31 , 98 In situ hybridization combined immunohistochemistry studies has shown that dehydration and salt loading increase the transcription of VP gene in magnocellular neurons 99 , 100 and/or decrease degradation of VP mRNA, thereby prolonging its half‐life. 101 , 102 Early studies using liquid hybridization assay, nuclear run‐on transcription assay and Northern blot analyses similarly demonstrated transcriptional upregulation of the VP gene in response to various osmotic stimuli. 103 , 104 , 105 , 106 , 107 Electrophysiological study of rats subjected to salt loading for 7 days demonstrated reduced osmoresponsiveness of magnocellular neurons, while VP mRNA levels and neurohypophysial hormone content remained stable. 108 Maintenance of neurohypophysial hormone stores during prolonged salt loading and dehydration has also been described in rats and desert rodents. 109 , 110 , 111 Collectively, these observations support the view that vasopressin acts as a key effector of volume‐preserving mechanisms in G. tarabuli, integrating both osmotic and non‐osmotic inputs to maintain hydromineral balance.

Oxytocinergic adaptation

Prolonged dehydration induced a marked increase in OT immunoreactivity within the supraoptic nucleus of G. tarabuli. Similar findings have been reported in Australian desert rodent Pseudomys australis under water deprivation, 112 whereas Meriones lybicus exhibits a significant decrease under comparable condition. 33 In rats subjected to salt loading, plasma OT concentrations positively correlate with osmolality, a pattern analogous to the vasopressin response. 113 OT synthesis is known to increase in response to hypovolemia and osmotic stimuli. 114 Molecular studies using in situ hybridization and Northern blot analysis have demonstrated that prolonged salt loading, water deprivation, or lactation in rats is associated with increased OT mRNA expression. 104 , 114 , 115 Similarly, intra‐peritoneal injection of hypertonic saline or polyethylene glycol which induce osmotic and hypovolemic challenges, respectively, leads to increased hypothalamic OT peptide content and OT mRNA levels within SON in a transgenic rat model with hypovolemic stimuli eliciting a more pronounced response than hyperosmotic stimulation. 116 Functionally, early studies in rats have shown that dehydration induces a natriuretic response accompanied by sodium depletion, 117 and that either dehydration or oxytocin administration promotes a natriuretic effect by inducing renal sodium excretion. 112 , 118 , 119 , 120 Therefore, the increase in OT immunoreactivity in G. tarabuli is consistent with the absence of a significant rise in plasma sodium levels during prolonged dehydration.

Morphological plasticity of MCNs

Our study revealed a significant increase in cross‐sectional surface soma area of both VP and OT magnocellular neurons following dehydration. This finding is consistent with previous observations in SON magnocellular neurons of rat and desert rodent subjected to water deprivation. 95 , 121 Such structural changes are generally considered indicative of increased metabolic and secretory demand, reflecting elevated transcriptional activity, proteins and neuropeptide synthesis, and expansion of organelles involved in osmotic stimulation. 122 , 123 , 124

Co‐localization of vasopressin and oxytocin within MCNs

In dehydrated G. tarabuli, numerous magnocellular neurons within the SON exhibited dual immunoreactivity for VP and OT. This co‐labelling indicates that these neuropeptides can be co‐synthesized and potentially co‐released from individual neurons. Similar observations have been reported in mice, rat, and desert rodent exposed to dehydration conditions. 33 , 125 , 126 Consistent with our findings, in situ hybridization studies in rats subjected to hyperosmotic stress have demonstrated co‐expression of VP and OT mRNAs in magnocellular neurons. 127 Furthermore, combined electron microscopy and in situ hybridization studies in lactating rats revealed the simultaneous presence of VP and OT mRNAs along with their corresponding peptides within SON neurons. 128

The concurrent synthesis of vasopressin and oxytocin in G. tarabuli aligns with their complementary roles to water conservation and sodium homeostasis. The observed co‐expression in MCNs during dehydration is therefore consistent with coordinated neuroendocrine mechanisms contributing to fluid and electrolyte regulation. Although based on a limited number of biological replicates, this observation is consistent with previous reports and suggests a functional convergence that may enhance adaptive efficiency under dehydration conditions.

Overall, these findings position G. tarabuli within a continuum of dehydration tolerance strategies, characterized by the preservation of osmotic stability alongside a pronounced reliance on volume‐sensitive regulatory mechanisms. This integrated response highlights the coordinated involvement of astroglial plasticity and magnocellular neuroendocrine activation in maintaining internal homeostasis under extreme environmental constraints.

Collectively, these findings support a hierarchical model of hydromineral regulation in G. tarabuli, in which hypovolemia‐related signaling constitutes the dominant driver of central neuroendocrine and glial adaptations, superseding classical osmotic control mechanisms.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Circulating vasopressin and oxytocin levels and renal functional parameters were not measured, limiting physiological interpretation. Key molecular markers within the SFO (c‐Fos, Nax channels, and AT1 receptors) and other osmosensory structures (OVLT, area postrema) were not assessed, restricting mechanistic insight into central osmoregulatory pathways. GFAP expression was not evaluated in the SON due to methodological constraints, precluding direct regional comparison of astroglial responses. Behavioral parameters, including feeding and drinking activity, were not monitored. The relatively small sample size reflects the constraints of working with wild‐caught desert rodents. Finally, the study is observational and does not establish causal relationships between astroglial remodeling and neuroendocrine adaptation. Future studies integrating molecular, electrophysiological, and functional approaches will be required to further elucidate SFO–SON interactions during dehydration.

CONCLUSION

Desert‐dwelling rodents have evolved physiological, morphological and behavioral adaptations that enable them to conserve water and thrive under harsh environmental conditions. 129 G. tarabuli exhibits distinct neuroendocrine and glial adaptations that support resilience during prolonged periods of dehydration. Our findings show that long‐term water deprivation induces structural plasticity in astrocytes within the SFO and enhances VP and OT expression in MCNs of the SON, while plasma osmolality, sodium levels and glycemia remain stable. The increase in hematocrit is consistent with hypovolemia, supporting a contribution for non‐osmotic (hypovolemic) signals to central dehydration responses. These findings support a model in which desert‐adapted rodents prioritize volume‐preserving neuroendocrine strategies over classical osmotic regulation. Together, these results highlight G. tarabuli as a valuable model for investigating integrated neuroendocrine and glial mechanisms underlying hydromineral homeostasis. They provide new insights into central and peripheral adaptations that support survival in xeric environments, advancing the understanding of hydromineral regulation in desert‐adapted mammals by identifying a functional shift toward volume‐driven central integration, redefining the role of circumventricular organs as dynamic interfaces between vascular status and neuroendocrine output under extreme dehydration.
