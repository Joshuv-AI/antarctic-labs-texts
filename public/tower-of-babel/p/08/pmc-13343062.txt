Introduction

Although polypharmacy lacks a standard definition, it is often defined as the routine use of five or more medications and represents a significant and growing global public health concern, particularly within the older adult population.1 2 This trend is primarily driven by the increase in life expectancy and the growing rate of chronic conditions. These conditions commonly require the administration of various medications for symptom control and reducing complications.3

A study conducted among the European older adult population reported an overall polypharmacy prevalence of 36.2%, with a marked increase corresponding to age: 30.0% at 65–74 years, 41.1% at 75–84 years and 49.4% at ≥85 years.4 Critically, South Korea has a significantly higher prevalence of polypharmacy among its older adults than other developed nations. The rate of use of five or more medications among those aged ≥75 years substantially surpasses the mean value documented across Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development member countries.5 6

The use of multiple medications elevates the danger of unfavourable health outcomes and mortality; this risk demonstrates a positive correlation with the number of prescribed medications.7 Ultimately, polypharmacy leads to a diminished quality of life and other negative health outcomes, contributing to rising societal healthcare costs.8 Furthermore, patients with frailty commonly suffer from an elevated incidence of chronic conditions, making them particularly susceptible to polypharmacy; therefore, interventions focused on this demographic may be more effective.9

Deprescribing, which encompasses the planned and supervised reduction or cessation of medication dosages, serves as a critical element in optimising a patient’s drug regimen.10 11 This process involves reassessing whether medications remain appropriate and safe in light of a patient’s changing health status, functional level, preferences and goals of care, thereby providing treatment tailored to their individual needs.12 Evidence from various studies demonstrates that deprescribing is both practicable and secure and does not lead to a notable rise in either adverse drug events or mortality.13

Frail older adults often have a diminished capacity for medication self-management due to factors such as cognitive decline, limited health literacy and physical decline.14 Consequently, the active participation of caregivers plays a critical role in their medication management.15,17 As caregivers’ beliefs and attitudes can significantly influence the success of deprescribing initiatives, understanding their perspectives is of paramount importance.18 19 A comprehensive assessment of caregiver attitudes, motivated by varying degrees of involvement, requires consideration of several domains: burden, appropriateness, concerns about stopping and involvement. Merely assessing their ‘willingness to deprescribe’ is insufficient to capture the complete picture. Therefore, a tool capable of comprehensively evaluating multifaceted attitudes is required.

Originally developed and validated in Australia, the revised Patients’ Attitudes Towards Deprescribing (rPATD) questionnaire serves as a tool for evaluating the perspectives of older individuals and their caregivers regarding deprescribing.20 The rPATD questionnaire has been translated and officially validated in several languages, including Portuguese, French, Spanish and Romanian, demonstrating its cross-cultural applicability.21,25 In South Korea, despite the growing prevalence of polypharmacy and the importance of deprescribing, there is a lack of validated instruments to assess the attitudes of Korean caregivers towards this process. Although a Korean version of the rPATD questionnaire for older adults exists, a version for caregivers has not yet been developed or validated. The primary goal of this study was to translate and culturally adapt the caregiver rPATD questionnaire into Korean. We also sought to rigorously assess the psychometric properties of this version, focusing on internal consistency (reliability) and construct validity among Korean caregivers.

Methods

Questionnaire

The initial English version of the rPATD questionnaire for caregivers comprised 19 distinct items, structured into four core factors and two global questions. The scale is structured around four primary factors: burden, appropriateness, concerns about stopping treatment and involvement. The two global questions, ‘If their doctor said it was possible, I would be willing to stop one or more of my care recipient’s medicines’ and ‘Overall, I am satisfied with my care recipient’s current medicines’, were omitted from the factor analysis.

Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The response options were as follows: strongly agree (5), agree (4), neutral (3), disagree (2) and strongly disagree (1). Factor scores were calculated as the mean of the summed scores of the relevant items, with a score range of 1–5 for each factor. Elevated scores typically signify an increased perceived burden, heightened concerns about cessation and stronger engagement in medication-related choices. However, the appropriateness factor was scored in the opposite direction. Consequently, a higher mean value indicates a stronger conviction regarding the suitability of the care recipient’s current medication.

Translation and cultural modification procedure

The caregiver rPATD questionnaire was meticulously translated and culturally adapted from English to Korean. The aim was to achieve conceptual, semantic and functional equivalences. The four-phase translation protocol recommended by the WHO was adopted for this procedure. The steps included forward translation, harmonisation via expert review, subsequent back translation and finalisation after pre-testing.26 Prior to the translation process, ethical permission for the use of the rPATD questionnaire (originally developed in 2016) was obtained from the original developer.

Forward translation

The original rPATD questionnaire was translated from English to Korean by a team of Korean researchers specialising in clinical medicine. Subsequently, a professional translator refined the wording to ensure that the expressions were easily understood and appropriate for the target population, resulting in a preliminary Korean version.

Expert review and harmonisation

The preliminary Korean version was reviewed by an expert panel comprising six physicians, including five geriatric specialists and one medical communication specialist. This panel reviewed items for translational errors, inappropriate language use and conceptual inconsistencies. The panel provided revision feedback, and all discussion points and decisions were thoroughly documented to reach a consensus on the harmonised Korean version.

Back-translation

An independent bilingual translator whose first language was English subsequently translated the harmonised Korean version back into English. The translator was unaware of the original English questionnaire. The expert panel subsequently compared the back-translated English version with the original rPATD questionnaire. This was done to identify and resolve any persisting semantic, cultural or conceptual inconsistencies.

Pre-testing and finalisation

To evaluate clarity and comprehensibility, we used a sample of 20 individuals from the target population to pre-test the reconciled Korean questionnaire. These participants were excluded from the final analyses. Following the completion of the preliminary survey, a final adjustment phase was conducted based on survey responses to finalise the Korean version of the rPATD questionnaire, ensuring its suitability for use in the Korean cultural context.

Research design and study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted between July and September 2025 among 1000 caregivers of older adult residents in long-term care facilities (LTCFs) in South Korea.

Participants were recruited through convenience sampling via the online consumer research panel of a professional survey company (Global Research, Seoul, South Korea). Details of the research goals, participant eligibility criteria and study timeline were disseminated via advertisements on the same platform. The inclusion criteria for this study were (1) adults aged 19 years or older and (2) individuals acting as a caregiver for a resident currently admitted to an LTCF. Individuals who did not meet these criteria were excluded; specifically, respondents who answered ‘no’ to the initial caregiver screening question were automatically directed out of the survey, and incomplete responses were excluded through the survey platform’s automated programming. The survey was distributed to panel members who met the inclusion criteria, and data collection continued until 1000 complete and valid responses were obtained. As recruitment was conducted through a closed panel managed by an external research firm, the total number of individuals approached and the overall response rate could not be determined.

Caregiver status was strictly verified through a mandatory screening question at the beginning of the survey. Respondents were first asked whether they were a caregiver for an LTCF resident; if a respondent answered ‘no’ to this initial screening question, the survey was automatically terminated. Subsequently, respondents were provided with a clear definition of a ‘primary caregiver’ (defined as the individual most knowledgeable about the resident’s medical history, medication use and functional status) and asked to identify their specific role for demographic characterisation purposes. Both primary and non-primary caregivers who passed the initial screening were allowed to proceed to the main questionnaire.

The data collection instrument comprised a demographic profile section and the Korean adaptation of the rPATD questionnaire. Specifically, the survey elicited details about caregivers (eg, kinship to the resident, sex, age, educational background and socioeconomic status) and residents (eg, duration of institutionalisation and current medications).

Sample size estimation

The minimum required sample size was initially calculated using Slovin’s formula based on the total number of institutional long-term care benefit recipients in 2023 (n=1 99 592) yielding a minimum of 384 respondents at a 95% CI with a 5% margin of error.27 The final sample of 1000 participants also satisfies psychometric criteria for factor analysis. Standard guidelines recommend a minimum subject-to-item ratio of 10:1; with 19 items, a minimum of 190 participants was required.28 Furthermore, Comrey and Lee classify a sample size of 500 as ‘very good’ and 1000 as ‘excellent’ for psychometric validation.29 Therefore, the present sample of 1000 participants substantially satisfies the recommended psychometric criteria for factor analysis and considerably exceeds sample sizes reported in other rPATD validation studies internationally, which ranged from 60 to 320 participants.22,25

Statistical analysis

Item analysis involved confirming the response distribution, mean and SD for each item and examining skewness and kurtosis to assess whether the responses were excessively skewed or exhibited a highly peaked distribution. We also confirmed the presence or absence of missing values across all items to check for data completeness.

Cronbach’s alpha values were computed to ensure the internal coherence of the measurement tools. This process encompassed the aggregate score and the four underlying factors (burden, appropriateness, concerns about stopping and involvement), confirming the stability of the assessment.30 We interpreted Cronbach’s alpha coefficients according to standardised grading: excellent (α≥0.90), good (0.80–0.89), acceptable (0.70–0.79), questionable (0.60–0.69) and weak (under 0.60). Furthermore, the corrected item–total correlation was calculated to evaluate how consistently each item measured the overall scale. A coefficient of≥0.30 was considered acceptable. Additionally, alpha-if-item-deleted analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of individual item removal on subscale reliability.

To evaluate the construct validity of the Korean rPATD questionnaire, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to test the hypothesised four-factor structure (burden, appropriateness, concerns about stopping and involvement), consistent with the original rPATD instrument.20 Model fit was assessed using the following indices: Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). CFI and TLI values of ≥0.90 were considered indicative of acceptable fit, and RMSEA and SRMR values of ≤0.08 were considered acceptable.31 Standardised factor loadings of ≥0.40 were considered acceptable. CFA was performed using the lavaan package (V.0.6) in R (V.4.6.0).32

Statistical processing of the data was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (V.27.0; IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) and R (V.4.6.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Outcomes of translation and cross-cultural validation

To ensure that the instrument was appropriately adjusted for the Korean-speaking population, the rPATD caregiver questionnaire was developed in accordance with the aforementioned translation and cultural modification protocols. The final Korean version of the questionnaire is provided in online supplemental table 1.

Sociodemographic profiles of study participants

The study sample consisted of 1000 caregivers, with a sex distribution of 55.9% males and 44.1% females. The most common relationship among older adults residing in LTCFs was with a child or spouse (73.7%). The largest proportion of respondents was aged in their 40s and 50s (65.2%), and 87.9% had a bachelor’s degree or higher.

The sex distribution of older adults admitted to the facilities was 45.3% male and 54.7% female, with the largest proportion of them aged in their 80s (46.0%). The most common duration of residence in LTCFs was 1–2 years (33.4%). Residents taking <5 medications accounted for 43.8%, while 47.9% were taking 5–10 medications. The mean values for the 19 analysed items ranged from a minimum of 3.05 (question (Q) 11) to a maximum of 3.77 (Q4 and Q14). Both skewness and kurtosis for all items were within 1.0, indicating an overall response pattern that approximated a normal distribution (table 2). This data distribution differs from that of previous studies, which reported sharp, skewed distributions for the 19 items used in the analysis.22 An analysis of missing values revealed that no missing data occurred for any of the items, which signifies the high completeness of the collected data.

Internal consistency reliability

The overall Cronbach’s alpha for the 17-item Korean rPATD questionnaire was 0.881 (after omitting the two global items, Q18 and Q19), indicating high internal consistency. As the rPATD captures four distinct dimensions, this value reflects general scale coherence rather than unidimensionality.

A summary of the corrected item–total correlations and Cronbach’s alpha values for each subfactor is provided in table 3. The burden (0.781) and appropriateness (0.766) factors showed acceptable reliability (>0.70). However, the Cronbach’s alpha for the concerns about the stopping factor was 0.563, indicating a relatively lower (weak) internal consistency, which suggests that the items within this factor have weaker cohesion regarding the measured construct.

Construct validity (CFA)

To evaluate construct validity, CFA was conducted to test the hypothesised four-factor structure of the Korean rPATD questionnaire, consistent with the original instrument. The CFA model specified four latent factors (burden, appropriateness, concerns about stopping and involvement) with their respective indicator items, allowing the four factors to correlate freely.

The overall model fit indices were as follows: χ2(113)=1111.375, p<0.001; CFI=0.830; TLI=0.795; RMSEA=0.094 (90% CI 0.089 to 0.099); SRMR=0.074. While CFI, TLI and RMSEA did not meet conventional thresholds (≥0.90 and ≤0.08, respectively), the SRMR value of 0.074 was within the acceptable range (≤0.08).

All standardised factor loadings were statistically significant (p<0.001), providing support for the item-factor associations specified in the model. Factor loadings ranged from 0.283 (Q17) to 0.744 (Q3), with most items exceeding the 0.40 threshold. However, Q17 of the involvement factor showed a notably low loading (0.283), and Q12 of the concerns about stopping factor showed a borderline loading (0.393). Discussion

The Korean version of the rPATD caregiver questionnaire was established with a focus on maintaining conceptual, semantic and functional equivalence, following a rigorous process of linguistic translation and cultural adjustment. The item analysis results showed that all items exhibited favourable skewness and kurtosis values (−1.0<skewness<1.0; −1.0<kurtosis<1.0), indicating a near-normal response distribution. Furthermore, the complete absence of missing values across all items confirmed the high quality of the collected data and suggested low concerns regarding response bias in the data. The psychometric properties of the Korean rPATD were evaluated across its four distinct dimensions (burden, appropriateness, concerns about stopping and involvement), consistent with the multidimensional structure of the original instrument.20 The overall internal consistency and factor structure were generally supported, though performance varied across subscales. Furthermore, the instrument was well received by participants, indicating high feasibility.

The overall internal consistency of the Korean rPATD was satisfactory, as evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.881, indicating that the instrument demonstrates general scale coherence across its four dimensions. The reliability of the four Korean factors (burden, appropriateness, concerns about stopping and involvement), when compared with other validated versions, such as the French (mostly≥0.70) and Spanish (0.683–0.879) versions, suggests that these dimensions of attitudes towards deprescribing show a degree of cross-cultural consistency, though performance varied across subscales.23 24

The low internal consistency of the concerns about the stopping factor (0.563) was comparable to the findings of other validation studies, such as the Spanish caregiver version (0.683) and the French version (0.600).23 24 One possible explanation in the Korean context may relate to cultural characteristics associated with high healthcare accessibility, low out-of-pocket drug costs and a generally high level of medication adherence.33 34 However, it should be noted that this explanation is speculative, as the cultural factors influencing this subscale were not empirically tested in the present study. The lower reliability of this subscale may reflect cross-cultural characteristics of the construct and sensitivity to national healthcare systems, a pattern observed across multiple language versions of the rPATD questionnaire.

The analysis of the corrected item–total correlations showed a pattern generally consistent with the Cronbach’s alpha results. Most items in the burden, appropriateness and involvement factors displayed good correlation coefficients of ≥0.40, suggesting adequate item-factor associations. However, Q17 of the involvement factor showed a borderline corrected item–total correlation (0.244), falling below 0.30, and the alpha-if-item-deleted analysis confirmed that removal of Q17 would increase the involvement factor alpha from 0.637 to 0.684. Despite this, Q17 was retained, as the improvement was marginal and the item covers a conceptually important domain of caregiver involvement in medication management, consistent with international validation studies of the rPATD questionnaire.

To evaluate construct validity, CFA was conducted to test the hypothesised four-factor structure of the Korean rPATD questionnaire. While the overall model fit indices indicated marginal fit (CFI=0.830, TLI=0.795, RMSEA=0.094, SRMR=0.074), the SRMR value was within the acceptable range (≤0.08) and all factor loadings were statistically significant (p<0.001). The relatively conservative fit indices may be partially attributable to the large sample size (n=1000), which is known to increase the sensitivity of χ2-based fit statistics to minor model misspecifications.31 These findings broadly support the four-dimensional framework of the original rPATD instrument, which has been consistently identified in French and Spanish validation studies, suggesting that the fundamental dimensions of attitudes towards deprescribing show a degree of cross-cultural consistency.23 24

Analysis of the standardised factor loadings indicated that most items demonstrated acceptable to good loadings. Items within the burden factor (Q1–Q4) showed loadings ranging from 0.594 to 0.744, and items within the appropriateness factor (Q5–Q9) showed loadings between 0.466 and 0.721. Items within the involvement factor (Q14–Q16) showed loadings between 0.586 and 0.682, while Q17 showed a notably low loading of 0.283, consistent with its lower corrected item–total correlation observed in the reliability analysis. Items within the concerns about stopping factor (Q10–Q13) showed loadings between 0.393 and 0.579, with Q12 showing a borderline loading of 0.393, which is consistent with the relatively lower internal consistency of this subscale (α=0.563).

Despite the lower Cronbach’s alpha values for the concerns about stopping (0.563) and involvement (0.637) factors and the borderline corrected item–total correlation for Q17 (0.244), no items were removed. This decision was based on the following rationales: first, the lower reliability of these factors has been consistently observed across other cultural validation studies, including the Spanish and French versions, suggesting a degree of cross-cultural consistency in these psychometric limitations.23 24 Second, these factors and Q17 cover conceptually important domains in the caregiver’s medication decision-making process. Third, alpha-if-item-deleted analysis showed that removal of Q17 would result in only a marginal improvement in the involvement factor alpha (0.637→0.684), and Q17 demonstrated a statistically significant CFA loading (β=0.283, p<0.001). Finally, all items met the minimum acceptable criteria for an initial validation study. These limitations have been explicitly acknowledged in the Strengths and limitations section, and future studies should further examine the psychometric performance of these subscales.

The validated Korean version of the rPATD questionnaire may provide a valuable tool for researchers and clinicians in Korea to evaluate caregivers’ attitudes towards deprescribing. Understanding these attitudes may help develop tailored educational interventions and communication strategies to facilitate shared decision-making during the deprescribing process. This instrument allows for the identification of specific barriers (eg, great concerns about stopping) or facilitators (eg, high perceptions of appropriateness) among caregivers, enabling a more personalised approach to deprescribing.13 Further exploration using the Korean version of the rPATD questionnaire to examine the influence of various situational factors (eg, specific chronic diseases, healthcare settings and cultural beliefs about medicine) on caregiver attitudes will provide deeper insights into this issue.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to develop and validate a Korean version of the caregiver rPATD questionnaire, addressing a critical evidence gap in deprescribing research in South Korea. This study also benefited from a large-scale dataset comprising 1000 participants, which enhanced analytical rigour. However, as recruitment was conducted through an online panel, the sample may not be fully representative of the broader caregiver population in South Korea, and the generalisability of findings should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, our study focused on caregivers, a critical yet under-researched population in the deprescribing field. Finally, our study demonstrated acceptable psychometric stability, with properties observed across the four core factors (burden, appropriateness, concerns about stopping and involvement) being generally consistent with international rPATD questionnaire validation efforts.

This study had some limitations. First, the low Cronbach’s alpha value for the concerns about the stopping factor (0.563) and the weak reliability for the involvement factor (0.637) indicate relatively limited internal consistency for these subscales. These findings may affect the interpretability of results derived from these two dimensions and suggest that caution is warranted when using them independently in clinical or research contexts. Second, Q17 of the involvement factor showed a low corrected item–total correlation (0.244), suggesting borderline conceptual coherence with the other items within that factor. Although Q17 was retained based on its conceptual importance and statistically significant CFA loading (β=0.283, p<0.001), future studies should further examine its psychometric performance. Third, as recruitment was conducted through an online panel, the sample was skewed towards individuals with higher education and income levels, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to populations with lower socioeconomic status. Future validation studies should incorporate more diverse recruitment strategies to enhance representativeness. Fourth, several important psychometric properties were not assessed in the present study, including test–retest reliability, convergent and discriminant validity and longitudinal validity. These omissions limit the comprehensiveness of the psychometric evaluation. Future studies should incorporate validated comparison tools such as the Beliefs about Medicines Questionnaire,35 administer the questionnaire at two time points to assess temporal stability and examine the responsiveness of the instrument to changes in caregiver attitudes over time. Finally, the CFA indicated a marginal model fit (CFI=0.830, TLI=0.795, RMSEA=0.094, SRMR=0.074), suggesting that the hypothesised four-factor structure may benefit from further refinement. The relatively conservative fit indices may be partially attributable to the large sample size (n=1000), which increases the sensitivity of χ2-based statistics to minor model misspecifications. Future studies should explore alternative model specifications and examine measurement invariance across demographic subgroups (eg, gender, age and caregiver type) to improve model fit and ensure the validity of cross-group comparisons.

Conclusions

The CFA provided partial support for the four-factor structure of the Korean rPATD questionnaire, with all factor loadings statistically significant (p<0.001) and an acceptable SRMR value (0.074), though CFI, TLI and RMSEA indicated marginal model fit. These findings are broadly consistent with the four-factor architecture of the original rPATD established in French and Spanish studies, suggesting a degree of cross-cultural consistency in the underlying dimensions of attitudes towards deprescribing.23 24

The Korean rPATD demonstrated generally acceptable internal consistency and partial structural validity across the four primary dimensions, though performance varied across subscales. These findings underscore the potential of the questionnaire as a useful resource for evaluating the multifaceted attitudes of Korean caregivers towards medication optimisation and deprescribing, while acknowledging that further psychometric refinement may be warranted.

This study expands the academic discourse on deprescribing by highlighting the pivotal role of caregiver perspectives in improving medication management for Korea’s older adult population. Our results provide a foundational basis for integrating caregiver perspectives into clinical decision-making. The availability of a Korean version of the rPATD questionnaire with acceptable psychometric properties may contribute to deprescribing research and clinical application in South Korea. By quantifying caregiver attitudes, systematic evaluation of interventions and better communication among patients, caregivers and healthcare providers can be facilitated, ultimately improving medication management in older adults.
