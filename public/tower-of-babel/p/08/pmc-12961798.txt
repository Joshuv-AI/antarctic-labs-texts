Citrate accumulation during CRRT: the impact of diagnostic criteria
Authors: Fabian Perschinka, Michael Joannidis
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the recent publication by Müller and colleagues on citrate accumulation during continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). We congratulate the authors on their valuable work and commend them for addressing the important and complex issue of citrate accumulation during regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) in critically ill patients.

In their study, Müller et al. [1] reported a comparatively high incidence of citrate accumulation in critically ill patients treated with continuous veno-venous hemodialysis (CVVHD) or continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF) using RCA. Citrate accumulation was defined by the ratio of albumin-corrected total calcium to ionized calcium (tCa/iCa) > 2.5. Citrate accumulation occurred in 17% of the study population, with 64% of affected patients receiving CVVHD and 36% CVVHDF. This rate appears higher than previously reported [2–4].

We would like to offer some considerations that might help contextualize these unexpected findings. One aspect relates to the use of an albumin-corrected tCa/iCa ratio, although the use of the uncorrected tCa/iCa ratio is generally considered standard practice [5, 6]. Bohr et al. demonstrated that albumin is interactively associated with the tCa/iCa ratio, with higher albumin levels leading to an increase in the ratio [7]. As circulating calcium is mainly protein-bound or ionized, changes in albumin levels directly affect the tCa/iCa ratio. Correcting total calcium for albumin may therefore shift some patients above the diagnostic threshold of 2.5 who would not exceed it using the uncorrected ratio. Since common correction formulas increase calculated total calcium in hypoalbuminemia, this approach may have influenced the reported incidence.

A second aspect concerns the diagnostic criteria, as citrate accumulation was defined solely by an elevated tCa/iCa ratio. However, clinically relevant citrate accumulation is usually accompanied by metabolic acidosis and systemic hypocalcemia requiring increasing calcium substitution [5, 6]. An isolated elevation of the tCa/iCa ratio without increased calcium requirement or metabolic acidosis may not necessarily reflect clinically relevant citrate toxicity, which could partly explain why citrate accumulation in this cohort was not associated with increased mortality. Other studies have reported substantially higher mortality rates when more comprehensive diagnostic criteria were applied. In the analysis by Khadzhynov et al. [8] a decrease in systemic iCa (< 1.1 mmol/L) despite adequate calcium supplementation and at least two of the following criteria were required to diagnose citrate accumulation: a concomitant increase in tCa and thus an elevated tCa/iCa ratio; relevant metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.2 and/or base excess < − 5 mmol/L); or an increased anion gap (> 11 mmol/L). Although no multivariable analysis was performed, the mortality among patients meeting these criteria was strikingly high: all affected patients died during their ICU stay. Most deaths occurred shortly after the diagnosis of citrate accumulation (median 22 h), and only a third of patients survived beyond 48 h. Notably, the severity of illness in this cohort was comparable to that reported by Müller et al., who demonstrated significantly lower mortality for their cohort showing citrate accumulation.

Third, a higher incidence of citrate accumulation in patients treated with CVVHD compared with CVVHDF was observed. As also discussed by Wang and Li [9], this association may reflect differences in the citrate protocols used rather than an inherent effect of the CRRT modality itself. CVVHDF was predominantly performed using predilution with low-concentration citrate solutions, allowing effective anticoagulation at a lower absolute citrate dose. In contrast, CVVHD was typically conducted using high-concentration citrate infused pre-filter without relevant predilution, necessitating a higher citrate dose to achieve comparable post-filter ionized calcium targets. Given that citrate clearance by dialysis or filtration is equally efficient, the higher incidence of citrate accumulation observed in CVVHD is more plausibly explained by the higher prescribed citrate load which was also calculated by the authors themselves [10] rather than by differences between CRRT modalities [9]. As citrate accumulation reflects an imbalance between citrate delivery and the patient’s metabolic capacity to metabolize citrate to bicarbonate, a sustained increase in citrate load over time may therefore represent a relevant risk factor. The citrate dose at the time point when diagnostic criteria are met may not adequately reflect the cumulative imbalance, which often develops over several hours. This could partly explain the absence of a difference in citrate load at the time point of citrate accumulation. In clinical practice, rising tCa/iCa ratios are typically addressed by reducing citrate delivery, as described for CVVHD in patients with impaired liver function [11]. Consequently, evaluation of the temporal course of citrate load over the preceding 24–48 h might provide additional insight into the observed findings.

In summary, we believe that the reported incidence of citrate accumulation invites further reflection on how methodological aspects – particularly the use of albumin-corrected tCa/iCa ratios and the absence of additional metabolic criteria – may influence reported rates. We hope that these considerations may contribute to the ongoing discussion and help refine future analyses, ultimately supporting the optimization and safety of RCA protocols in critically ill patients.
