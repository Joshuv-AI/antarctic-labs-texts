Surgical reconstruction of invasive external cervical root resorption with perforation and associated periodontal defect using novel bioceramic materials and cone beam computed tomography as a diagnostic adjunct- A case report
Authors: Vikrant Thakur, Shweta Verma, Munish Goel, Vijay Kumar, Baljeet Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Tooth resorption; both internal and external, occasionally occurs as an inflammatory response of the dentoalveolar apparatus to infection. Depending on the type of resorption, etiology, and the stage at which root resorption defect is detected, different treatment regimens have been proposed. This case report demonstrates the management of extensive inflammatory combined internal and external root resorption by nonsurgical endodontic therapy using a Bioceramic sealer (BioRoot RCS) and surgical reconstruction of resorptive defect using Biodentine. On follow-up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, clinical and radiographic evidence revealed signs of periapical healing, arrested root resorption, and periodontal reattachment. The inclusion of cone beam computed tomography plays a crucial role in diagnosis, decision-making, and treatment planning and thus successful management of such cases having Endodontic-Periodontal involvement. As supported by clinical and radiographic findings at 12-month follow-up, treatment of cases with extensive root resorption and associated involvement of periodontium can be successfully done using bioceramic materials.

INTRODUCTION

Root resorption cases present as a diagnostic and treatment decision challenge to the dentists worldwide. The clastic activities result in the loss of dental hard tissues and hence lead to root resorption. The root resorption of permanent teeth is invariably an inflammatory, pathological event, which, if left untreated, might result in premature tooth loss. The other potential etiological factors are dental trauma, pulp infection, orthodontic treatment, periodontal injury, and idiopathic causes.[1]

Depending on its location, the resorption can be classified as internal resorption (seen on the walls of pulp space) and external resorption (if present on the external cemental root surface).[2]

In the present case, the patient presented with the combined root resorption defect involving the internal surface of the root as well as its external cervical surface along with accompanying periodontal attachment loss and periradicular pathology. The most challenging part was establishing the correct location, size, and nature of the resorptive defect. The use of cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) for the current case allowed the detailed three-dimensional assessment of the root resorptive defect and adjacent anatomy, ensuring accurate diagnosis and establishment of the correct treatment plan. The diagnosis established was external cervical root resorption with perforating internal resorption.

The present case was managed using combined nonsurgical endodontic therapy and surgical reconstruction of the resorptive defect. This case report highlights the successful management and treatment outcome of extensive External Cervical Resorption (ECR) along with perforating internal resorption and attachment loss due to advanced periodontal ligament involvement, using novel bioceramic materials, namely BioRoot RCS and Biodentine. CBCT proved to be an indispensable adjunct in this case management.

The recent bioceramic-based materials have exhibited excellent bioactivity, antimicrobial activity, biocompatibility, and high mechanical strength. All such effects of bioceramics result in the remineralization of periradicular tissues. Furthermore, due to their hydrophilic profile; they set in humid environments and possess wettability to ensure the formation of hermetic seal when in tissue contact.[3]

CASE REPORT

A 52-year-old male patient reported to the department of conservative dentistry and endodontics with the chief complaint of recurrent dull pain, redness, and swelling in gums, with respect to upper left front tooth for the past 3 months. There was a history of trauma 10 years back, and the patient had undergone surgical endodontic therapy of the adjacent teeth around 5 years back.

Clinical and radiographic examination

The patient was very co-operative and otherwise in good health at the time of examination. Medical history was noncontributory. Intraoral examination revealed redness, swelling, and tenderness on palpation over gingivae in relation to maxillary left canine, tooth #23 [Figure 1a]. The tooth was tender on percussion and gave a negative response on electric pulp testing. Careful probing of the gingival sulcus was done with the help of William’s Probe which showed 5 mm clinical probing depth on the middle aspect of the buccal surface of tooth # 23 [Figure 1b]. The other associated findings were yellowish discoloration and distobuccal rotation of tooth # 22, anterior deep bite, and proclined maxillary anterior teeth [Figure 1a].

Diagnostic clinical photographs and radiographs of tooth #23; (a) Photograph showing discolored and rotated tooth #22 and presence of gingival inflammation with tooth #23; (b) Clinical probing in tooth #23 depicts pocket depth of 5mm; (c) Intraoral periapical radiograph (IOPAR) depicting perforating root resorptive defect in the cervical portion of the root of tooth #23 along with periapical widening of lamina dura, also tooth #22 shows previous surgical endodontic treatment performed; (d) Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) image (Axial view) of tooth #23 showing buccal resorptive defect and communication of 2.6 mm dimension; (e) CBCT image (Sagittal view) showing extension of resorption

Preoperative radiographs of the region revealed root resorption involving mainly cervical third of the root of tooth #23 with large communication between the root canal and periodontium [Figure 1c]. The tooth also had periapical radiolucency along with widening of periodontal ligament space.

To confirm the extent of resorptive defect and ascertain the level of communication between the root canal and periodontium, a CBCT with small field of view 40 mm × 40 mm and voxel size 0.125 mm was performed, and it revealed that the resorptive defect was present on cervical third of buccal surface of root of tooth # 23 and also ascertained a large communication between pulp space and periodontium measuring 2.6 mm in its maximum dimension [Figure 1d and e]. The clinical findings were suggestive of inflammatory combined external cervical and internal root resorption and chronic apical periodontitis with tooth #23.

Treatment

The three main treatment objectives were to arrest the resorption process, restore the healthy root architecture, and to ensure that no future resorption takes place. Considering the location and size of the defect, the treatment plan was to perform conventional nonsurgical endodontic treatment, followed by surgical repair of the resorbed root and its reconstruction with Biodentine (Septodont, Saint Maur des Fosses, France). This treatment plan was consistent with European Society of Endodontology recommendations for external cervical resorption.[4] Before planning the procedure, an informed consent was obtained from the patient. The patient’s hemogram and other relevant blood investigations were done which were found to be within normal limits. The entire treatment was executed in four phases.

Phase I: Nonsurgical endodontic treatment

After administration of local anesthesia and rubber dam isolation, root canal treatment of tooth #23 was started. Access cavity was prepared using Endo Access bur (Dentsply, United States), and working length was determined using an electronic apex locator (J Morita Root Zx II, 680 Higashihama Minami-cho, Japan) and was then confirmed with the intraoral periapical radiograph [Figure 2a]. Following this, apical preparation was done up to #50 K-file (Mani, Inc. Japan), and biomechanical preparation was completed using step-back technique. Calcium hydroxide (Prime Dental, India) intracanal dressing was given for 2 weeks [Figure 2b].

Intra-operative radiographs and clinical photographs of tooth #23; (a) Working length determination; (b) Placement of intracanal calcium hydroxide dressing; (c) Orthograde repair of resorptive lesion using Biodentine and sectional obturation; (d) Incisions given (e) Rectangular full thickness mucoperiosteal flap raised; (f) Surgical root reconstruction with Biodentine; (g) Flap repositioned and sutures given

Sectional obturation with lateral condensation technique was done apical to the resorptive lesion using customized gutta-percha and BioRoot RCS, a bioceramic sealer.

Phase II: Orthograde repair of the resorptive lesion using Biodentine

Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) carrier was used to fill the internal resorption defect with Biodentine, and amalgam condenser was used to condense the material well [Figure 2c]. Considering the large size of the communicating defect present between the root canal and periodontium, reconstructive surgery was planned to thoroughly clean and properly seal the defect.

Phase III: Surgical root reconstruction periodontal regeneration with Biodentine

Two vertical incisions involving one teeth each on the mesial and distal of #23 and sulcular/interdental incisions were given under local anesthesia [Figure 2d]. A full thickness flap was raised to gain access to the resorptive defect and associated periodontal lesion [Figure 2e]. Thorough debridement of the defect was done to remove the granulomatous tissue. Removal of the granulomatous tissue attached to the defect helps in the disruption of blood circulation of the resorptive cells, which may prevent the recurrence of the resorption. 90% aqueous solution of trichloroacetic acid was topically applied to the resorptive lesion.

The resorptive lesion was carefully packed, and the communication was sealed with Biodentine; the original anatomic contour of the root was restored [Figure 2f]. Biodentine was left undisturbed for around 15 min for proper setting and to achieve adequate hardening.

Following the complete setting of Biodentine, the flap was repositioned and sutured back using 3-0 black silk sutures [Figure 2g]. An immediate postoperative radiograph was taken [Figure 3a], and the patient was called after 1 week for clinical evaluation and suture removal.

Phase IV: Prosthetic rehabilitation and follow-up

One week postoperatively, sutures were removed and initial healing at the site was evaluated. The patient was recalled thereafter at 1-month, 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year interval [Figure 3b–e]. A careful clinical and radiographic examination was performed at each follow-up visit. The patient was advised for orthodontic consultation for the treatment of associated findings but he declined. Two months postoperatively, a porcelain fused to metal crown was fabricated for the discolored and rotated tooth #22, in a way to properly align it with the arch [Figure 3f].

Careful and gentle gingival sulcus probing was again performed after 6 months of surgery. It measured 3 mm, which is the depth of normal healthy gingival sulcus [Figure 3g], indicating the healing of the associated periodontal lesion as well. Furthermore, the radiographic examination of the area showed healthy bone and periodontal structures, with no signs of progression of the resorptive defect.

DISCUSSION

The process of root resorption is an irreversible phenomenon resulting in the loss of tooth if timely detection and treatment is not performed.[5] The major causative factors of root resorption include dental trauma, orthodontic tooth movement, bleaching, and periodontal therapy. External cervical resorption begins at the cervical area of the tooth. This condition many a times goes unnoticed because it typically progresses as a painless lesion, shows no obvious visual signs until and unless there occurs pulpal or periodontal involvement.[6] Typically, during early stages, teeth affected by external cervical resorption are asymptomatic and show no obvious visual signs. Probing depth >3 mm and chronic gingival abscess are occasionally noted at mature stages. Internal resorption is a clinical entity that involves progressive resorption of intraradicular dentin along the pulp space. It continues to cause necrosis of pulpal tissue apical to resorptive lesion and results in apical periodontitis subsequently.[7] Continuous resorption of the canal wall can lead to perforation and communication between the root canal and periodontium. In this case, the lesion was completely asymptomatic for a long time until there was periodontal involvement, resulting in swelling and tenderness of gingiva in the region of the involved tooth. The early detection of resorptive lesions is usually done while obtaining routine dental radiographs, but they fail to reveal the true anatomic extent of the lesion. To overcome these limitations, CBCT is being employed as an invaluable tool for diagnosis as well as an adjunct in treatment planning and decision-making process. It ensures 3D appreciation of the tooth,[8] the resorptive lesion, and adjacent hard and soft tissues. A CBCT scan was requested in this study case, and it was of unequivocal help to ascertain the exact location of the defect and provide the most appropriate treatment to the patient. Surgical repair alone is performed in cases of mild external cervical resorption, while surgical or nonsurgical repair combined with endodontic treatment is indicated in cases where the lesion approaches pulp and in case of pulp and periapical pathoses.

Heithersay recommended topical application of a 90% aqueous solution of trichloroacetic acid. It causes coagulation necrosis of the ECR resorptive tissue and no damage to adjacent periodontal tissue. It also infiltrates the small channels and recesses of ECR that would otherwise be inaccessible to mechanical instrumentation.

The selection of suitable materials to treat and restore the weakened tooth structure is also very crucial for the long-term success of the treatment imparted. The major concern is invasion of the biological width because the restoration is placed close to the bone margin. Biodentine was used for restoration and surgical root reconstruction in this case, because of its biocompatibility, bioactivity, and antimicrobial activity. This material is easy to handle, closely resembles human dentin in its properties, reinforces the tooth structure, promotes an excellent biological response, and maintains periodontal health as it comes into direct contact with this tissue.[9]

Due to calcium ion release, it produces favorable alkaline environment for osteoblastic activity which results in the regeneration and mineralization of lost root dentin structures.[10]

BioRoot RCS was used as a root canal sealer in this case, because of its excellent properties in terms of biocompatibility, antimicrobial activity, flowability, and wettability which results in the formation of a very good hermetic seal. Its hydrophilic nature enables it to set in a humid environment. And it also has the capability to induce the mineralization of periradicular tissues. All these properties help in reducing and eliminating the inflammation and infection, resulting in strengthening of root and peri-radicular apparatus.

Due to the formation of periodontal pocket, regeneration and reattachment of the periodontal tissue was vital for the success of this case since it was a combined endodontic-periodontal lesion. However, in this case, the authors avoided using the bone graft material and membrane, as a study by Rahimi et al.,[11] concluded that mineralized bone graft materials negatively affect the surface microhardness of Biodentine. In this case, the microhardness of Biodentine was of vital significance to give strength to root structure because, presence of a large resorptive defect resulted in considerable loss of root structure.

CONCLUSION

The use of CBCT for examination in endodontics has resulted in easier detection of position and extent of root resorption defect, which in turn ensures framing of the correct treatment plan for each case. Treatment of extensive root resorptive defect and communication between root canal and periodontium with Biodentine was considered successful, as evidenced by clinical examination, clinical probing, and radiographic findings after 12 months.
