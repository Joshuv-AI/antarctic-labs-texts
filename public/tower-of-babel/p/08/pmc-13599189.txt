Biomonitoring-informed neonicotinoid mixture exposure reveals modest cytotoxicity and cell-type-specific molecular responses in human cell lines
Authors: Li Ma, Xiuying Lin, Yan Wu, Zengheng Xiong, Long Cheng, Shuhai He, Huan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonicotinoids (NEOs) are widely detected in environmental matrices and in humans, raising concerns about potential health risks. However, most toxicological studies have focused on single compounds and/or concentrations far exceeding biomonitoring-relevant exposure ranges.MethodsWe developed a UPLC-MS/MS method to quantify eight NEOs and five metabolites in human urine. Using environmental monitoring and urinary biomonitoring data from Hainan Island, China, we constructed exposure-informed NEO mixture profiles based on surface water, tap water, and urinary concentrations, and evaluated their cytotoxicity in four human cell lines (A2780, HEK293T, HeLa, and HepG2). Toxicity was assessed via cell viability, oxidative stress, DNA fragmentation, and integrated multi-omics analyses, with the urinary-biomonitoring-informed mixture evaluated across four cell lines and the surface/tap-water mixtures assessed as an initial transcriptomic screen in HEK293T cells.ResultsEnvironmentally relevant mixtures (surface/tap water) elicited only limited transcriptomic changes (≤ 0.34% of genes). In contrast, biomonitoring-informed mixtures (≈ 0.1 µM ΣNEOs) induced modest but detectable dose-dependent cytotoxicity, with viability reductions of 3–8%, modest oxidative stress, and limited increases in DNA fragmentation, with effects distinct from individual compounds at matched concentrations. Transcriptomic analysis revealed cell-type-specific responses converging on three core modules: innate immune-like inflammatory signaling, ER stress/unfolded protein response, and metabolic reprogramming. Metabolomic profiling identified two dominant perturbation patterns—arginine/nitrogen-centered rewiring (A2780 and HEK293T) versus membrane lipid remodeling (HeLa and HepG2).ConclusionsBiomonitoring-informed NEO/metabolite mixtures elicited measurable, cell-specific molecular perturbations within biomonitoring-relevant concentration ranges, engaging coordinated stress and adaptation programs. These findings support the incorporation of mixture composition and biomonitoring-relevant concentration ranges into future toxicological evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40360-026-01179-z.Keywords: Neonicotinoids, UPLC-MS/MS, Cytotoxicity, Transcriptomics, Metabolomics

Introduction

Neonicotinoids (NEOs) are a major class of insecticides characterized by high insecticidal efficacy and preferential targeting of insect nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) [1]. Structurally, they typically contain chloropyridinyl or chlorothiazolyl moieties and can be classified into nitromethylene, N-cyanoamidine, and N-nitroguanidine subtypes (Fig. S1) [2]. By persistently activating insect nAChRs, rather than being rapidly terminated as in acetylcholine signaling, NEOs induce prolonged excitation that ultimately leads to paralysis and insect mortality [3].

NEOs are widely used in agriculture to control insect pests, and their application is subject to regulatory evaluation and residue management frameworks intended to keep residues in food and the environment below established safety thresholds. Nevertheless, because of their relatively high water solubility and chemical stability, NEOs and their metabolites may persist after application and be transported into environmental compartments. Accordingly, they have been detected in water [4–8], sediments [9–10], soils [10], and atmospheric particulates [11]. NEOs and their metabolites have also been detected in diverse human biological matrices, including urine [12, 13], breast milk [14], hair [15], and teeth [16], as well as in food items such as fruit juices [17] and vegetables [18]. These findings indicate that human exposure to NEOs can occur through multiple routes, although exposure levels and associated risks should be interpreted in the context of regulated agricultural use, residue limits, and population-specific exposure patterns.

In recent years, biomonitoring studies have consistently reported widespread NEOs exposure in the general population. In Japan, urine samples from adults without occupational exposure (n = 52) showed detection rates close to 100% for thiamethoxam (THM) and dinotefuran (DNT), while imidacloprid (IMI) and clothianidin (CLO) both exceeded 95% [19]. Urinary measurements in Japanese young children (n = 223) further suggested lower concentrations than in adults and a seasonal pattern with higher levels in summer than in winter [12]. In Greece, agricultural activity was associated with higher exposure, as reflected by a markedly greater detection rate of IMI in hair from rural residents (n = 32, 65.6%) than from urban residents (n = 26) [20]. A large-scale U.S. study (n = 3032) reported the major metabolite of acetamiprid (ACE), N-desmethyl-acetamiprid (DM-ACE), in 37% of urine samples, with higher concentrations in children and the elderly [21]. In a Chinese general population (n = 324), six NEOs and five metabolites were detected with detection rates ranging from 63% to 99% [13]. These data suggest that human exposure to NEOs is widespread, although it varies across populations and regions.

Accumulating evidence suggests that NEOs may pose risks to human health. Following ingestion, most NEOs are efficiently excreted via urine; however, a small fraction, particularly biologically active metabolites, may undergo systemic distribution and accumulate in tissues, potentially eliciting adverse effects. In vitro studies (Table S1) have shown that NEOs exposure can induce multiple toxic effects in mammalian cells [22–27]. Reported mechanisms include DNA damage, disruption of calcium signaling and mitochondrial function, and induction of oxidative stress, ultimately reducing cell viability. Epidemiological studies have further linked long-term NEO exposure with congenital heart defects, anencephaly, and autism spectrum disorders [28–30]. However, most existing studies, including those cited above, have relied on exposure concentrations exceeding realistic human exposure levels and/or have examined only individual compounds or simplified mixtures of two to three NEOs, which may limit their relevance to real-world exposure scenarios.

To address this gap, we investigated the cytotoxicity of NEO/metabolite mixtures informed by measured concentrations in environmental water samples and human urine across four cell lines. Urinary exposure biomarkers were measured using a sensitive UPLC-MS/MS method developed for the simultaneous quantification of eight NEOs and five metabolites in human urine and applied to samples from participants in Hainan Island, China. Associations between urinary exposure indicators and demographic characteristics were examined as exploratory analyses. Based on the types and concentrations of NEOs identified in environmental water samples (tap water and surface water) from Hainan Island in our previous studies [31–32], as well as in the present urinary biomonitoring analysis, representative mixtures were constructed and evaluated in human cell lines (A2780, HEK293T, HeLa, and HepG2). Toxicity was assessed using multi-level endpoints, including cell viability, DNA fragmentation, and oxidative stress, under both single and mixed NEOs/metabolites exposure conditions. In addition, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were performed to explore molecular responses associated with NEO/metabolite exposure.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

The analyte standards, including IMI, CLO, DNT, THM, ACE, nitenpyram (NTP), imidaclothiz (IMZ), thiacloprid (THA), imidacloprid-urea (IMI-U), N-desmethylthiamethoxam (DM-THM), desnitro-imidacloprid hydrochloride (DN-IMI), 5-hydroxyimidacloprid (5-OH-IMI), imidacloprid-olefin (IMI-O), and the standard for calculating recovery, chlordimeform-d6 (CDM-d6), were all purchased from First Standard (Tianjin, China). Stock solutions were stored at -20℃.

The ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.7 μm) was purchased from Waters (USA), the L-column2 ODS column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2 μm) was purchased from CERI (Japan). The cells and culture media were purchased from Fenghui Biotechnology (Hunan, China). β-glucuronidase was purchased from Maclin (Shanghai, China).

Urine sample collection and storage

A total of 38 participants aged 23–79 years who had not received chemotherapy or radiotherapy were recruited from the Hainan Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Morning urine samples were collected from participants after fasting for at least 10 h. All samples were stored at -20 °C. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hainan Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine and conducted in accordance with approved guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Quantitative detection methods for NEOs in urine

Urinary NEOs/metabolite concentrations were determined using a standard addition method with non-isotopic internal standard correction to minimize inter-individual urine matrix effects. Creatinine and specific gravity were not measured in the present study; therefore, urinary concentrations were not adjusted for urine dilution.

The detailed sample preparation and LC-MS/MS analysis procedures were as follows. A 2.5 mL aliquot of a room-temperature-thawed urine sample was spiked with 10 µL of CDM-d6 internal standard (1 mg/L) and 200 µL of β-glucuronidase solution (100 units/mL), followed by enzymatic hydrolysis at 37 °C and 200 rpm shaking for 12 h. The hydrolysate was purified by solid-phase extraction (SPE) using Oasis HLB cartridges (WAT094226, Waters), followed by elution with dichloromethane-acetonitrile (70:30, v/v) and filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane. After drying, the eluate was reconstituted either in 0.1% formic acid in water or in 0.1% formic acid in water containing a concentration gradient of non-isotopic internal standards.

Separation of NEOs was performed on an L-column2 ODS column. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (Phase A) and acetonitrile (Phase B). Gradient elution was applied as follows: 0–1 min, 80% A; 1–4.5 min, 80–10% A; 4.5–7 min, 10% A; 7–7.1 min, 10–80% A; and 7.1–10 min, 80% A. The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 55 °C. Target analytes were detected in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode using an AB Sciex Triple Quad 7500 mass spectrometer, with detailed transitions listed in Table 1. Three urine samples were used to assess the precision of the method by calculating the relative standard deviation (RSD) of the measurement results. Each sample was analyzed in six replicate injections at five spiking levels. The formula for precision is: (standard deviation / mean) × 100%.

Cell culture and NEOs exposure groups

HEK293T, HeLa and HepG2 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) mixture. A2780 cells were grown in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, and 2 mM glutamine. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere until they reached the logarithmic growth phase.

These cell lines were selected to provide a panel of commonly used human cell models with different cellular backgrounds, including ovarian carcinoma-derived cells (A2780), kidney-derived cells (HEK293T), cervical carcinoma-derived cells (HeLa), and hepatocellular carcinoma-derived cells (HepG2), allowing comparison of cell-line-dependent responses to NEO/metabolite mixtures.

To construct exposure-informed in vitro treatment conditions, cells were treated with NEO/metabolite mixtures at the respective highest detected concentrations measured in surface water (SW), tap water (TW) from Hainan Island, and urine (UR) from participants, as listed in Table 2. NEOs/metabolites were initially prepared in DMSO and subsequently diluted in culture medium for cell treatment. The final DMSO concentration was maintained below 0.1% (v/v), and control cells received an equivalent volume of DMSO alone.

Cytotoxicity test

Cell viability, apoptosis, and intracellular ROS levels in NEOs/metabolites-treated and untreated cells were assessed using a CCK-8 assay kit (G4103, Servicebio), a TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (C1086, Beyotime), and a ROS assay kit (S0033S, Beyotime), respectively. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured for 24 h under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing either NEOs/metabolites or 0.1% DMSO (control group), followed by an additional 48 h of incubation. Cells were subsequently subjected to the respective assay procedures according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Transcriptomics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the treated and control group cells using Trizol reagent and quantified using an IMPLEN NanoPhotometer (NP80, Germany). For the environmental-water-informed RNA-seq experiment, HEK293T cells exposed to surface-water- or tap-water-informed NEO mixtures and the corresponding controls were analyzed with three biological replicates per group and were sequenced by Huayan-bio (Wuhan, China). For the urinary-biomonitoring-informed RNA-seq experiment, cells exposed to the urinary NEO/metabolite mixture and the corresponding controls were analyzed with three biological replicates per group for each cell line and were sequenced by HaploX (Shenzhen, China). For all RNA-seq samples, RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, followed by standard library construction and paired-end sequencing (150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, CA, USA). An average of 40 million raw reads per sample was generated.

RNA-seq data quality was assessed at both the RNA and sequencing-read levels. Only RNA samples with acceptable integrity, defined as RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7 as evaluated by the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, were used for library construction. Raw reads were filtered to remove adapter sequences, reads containing ambiguous bases, and low-quality reads. The quality of clean reads was evaluated based on Q30 values, read depth, and mapping rate to the human reference genome. Samples with RIN ≤ 7, insufficient read depth, poor sequencing quality, low mapping rate, or abnormal clustering in sample-level quality assessment were considered low-quality and excluded from downstream analysis. No samples failed these quality-control criteria.

Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p13) using HISAT2, and gene-level counts were quantified with featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (version 1.34.0) in R. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with an absolute |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p value (padj) < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted to interpret the functional significance of DEGs, and enrichment results were evaluated using Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p values where applicable.

qRT-PCR was conducted to verify the transcriptomic findings. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Code No. RR047A). The qPCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq Ⅱ (Tli RNaseH Plus) (TaKaRa, Code No. RR820A) on a FQD-96 A real-time PCR system (Bioer Technology, China). β-Actin was used as the internal reference gene. All primers (sequence information provided in Table S2) were designed via PrimerBank and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed with six biological replicates per group for each cell line. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing either NEOs/metabolites or 0.1% DMSO (control group), followed by an additional 48 h of incubation. After removing the culture medium, the cells were lysed by adding 1 mL of ice-cold LC-MS grade extraction solvent (methanol: acetonitrile: water = 2:2:1, v/v) with repeated pipetting. The resulting suspension was subjected to ultrasonication (Covaris S220) under the following program: peak power: 150, duty factor: 20, cycles: 200, duration: 20 min, while maintaining the temperature between 5 and 9 °C. To precipitate proteins, the lysate was stored at -20 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, the sample was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane, and finally lyophilized under vacuum. The dried extract can be stored at -80 °C until reconstitution and UPLC-MS analysis. For each analytical batch, pooled quality control (QC) samples were prepared by mixing equal aliquots from all samples within the corresponding group. Three QC injections were included at the beginning, middle, and end of each analytical sequence to monitor LC-MS stability and reproducibility.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer. Separation of metabolites was performed on an UPLC BEH Amide column. For positive and negative ionization modes, the mobile phases consisted of 0.08% formic acid and 25 mM ammonium formate in water (Phase A) and 0.08% formic acid in 95% acetonitrile (Phase B), and 25 mM ammonium acetate in water (pH = 8) (Phase A) and 95% acetonitrile in water (Phase B), respectively. Gradient elution was applied as follows: 0 min, 0% A; 5 min, 0% A; 22 min, 60% A; 27 min, 60% A; 27.1 min, 0% A. The flow rate was set at 0.2 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 36 °C. Signals were acquired in both positive and negative ion scan modes, with a mass scan range of m/z 70–800; electrospray ionization (ESI) source, positive ion voltage 3400 V, negative ion voltage 3000 V, ion source heating temperature 320 °C, and collision energy of 20–60 V.

The Compound discoverer 3.3.2.31 software was used to process the raw data for peak alignment and metabolite quantification. Metabolite features were putatively annotated by MS/MS spectral matching against mzCloud (2024), NIST (2023 MS/MS Library), and KEGG databases. Data analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 software [33]. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Metabolites/features with |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p value (padj) < 0.05 were defined as differential metabolites/features between the two groups. KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of differential metabolites/features were also evaluated using Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p values.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Excel, Prism and Origin software, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). For cytotoxicity, ROS, TUNEL, qRT-PCR, and other non-omics assays, p< 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). ROS and TUNEL fluorescence signals were quantified using ImageJ (v1.54f). Fluorescence images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, and a uniform threshold was applied to separate signals from background. Fluorescent particles meeting pre-defined size criteria were automatically counted using “Analyze Particles,” and integrated fluorescence density was recorded per field of view. All data are shown as mean values with error bars from at least three independent experiments.

Results and discussion

Precise determination of NEOs in urine

To quantify NEOs and their metabolites in human urine, an UPLC-MS/MS method was developed and its analytical performance was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 1A, satisfactory separation of eight NEOs and five metabolites was achieved within a short analytical runtime (5 min). The standard addition method was used for absolute quantification. Real urine samples were spiked with a concentration gradient of standard solutions (Fig. 1B). Calibration curves were constructed using the differences in signal intensity between spiked and non-spiked samples. The signal intensities of the real samples were then interpolated into the calibration curves to obtain the actual concentrations (Fig. 1C). Although this approach is relatively time-consuming, requiring six injections per sample for quantification, it effectively minimizes matrix effects. Moreover, chromatographic separation was completed within 5 min, resulting in a total quantification time of less than 30 min per sample, which is considered acceptable. The concentrations measured for each participant, as well as the calibration curve, recovery, and signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio, are summarized in Table S3. Table S4 shows that the instrument exhibited good precision and reproducibility, with relative standard deviations (RSDs) below 5% for all conditions.

Characteristics of urinary NEOs/metabolites distribution in participant from Hainan island

The established UPLC-MS/MS method was applied to analyze urinary NEOs/metabolite concentrations in participants at Hainan island (Table S3). Widespread exposure was observed, with at least one and up to five NEOs/metabolite detected in the urine samples. The maximum concentration of ΣNEOs increased from approximately 1.5-2 nM in the aquatic environment to ~ 111 nM in urine (Table 2), representing an approximately 55- to 74-fold difference between measured water concentrations and urinary biomarker levels. The UR mixture was constructed using the maximum detected urinary concentration of each analyte across participants and therefore represents a conservative composite exposure scenario rather than an actual exposure profile observed in a single individual. This comparison indicates that urinary concentrations can be substantially higher than environmental water concentrations, but should not be interpreted as direct estimates of systemic or target-organ exposure. Although our primary aim was to characterize the concentration ranges of urinary NEOs in residents of Hainan Island, we also performed an exploratory correlation analysis between urinary NEO levels and participant characteristics. Analytes without detectable signals in the unspiked urine sample were treated as non-detects, which may reflect concentrations below the detectable range under the present workflow and/or sample-specific urine matrix effects. For the exploratory correlation analyses, participants with non-detectable values for the corresponding analyte were excluded from that specific analysis rather than assigned a numerical concentration. Given the relatively small sample size (n = 38), the lack of urinary dilution adjustment, and the absence of a predefined biological hypothesis for some variables, particularly height, these associations should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Urinary IMI and its metabolite 5-OH-IMI, a cytochrome P450-mediated hydroxylation product of IMI, did not show significant associations with age or height in the participants (Fig. 2A-E). Nominal correlations were observed between IMI-U and age (Fig. 2C; r = 0.9591, p < 0.0001), IMI-U and height (Fig. 2F; r = -0.7660, p < 0.01), DNT and age (Fig. 2G; r = 0.9334, p < 0.001), and ACE and body weight (Fig. 2H; r = -0.9631, p < 0.01). For the remaining NEOs, no significant associations were detected with age (Fig. S2), height (Fig. S3), body weight (Fig. S4), or sex (Fig. S5).

Previous biomonitoring studies have reported heterogeneous associations between urinary NEO levels and demographic variables. In Shanghai school children, THM was more frequently detected in girls [34]. A subsequent pediatric study reported mixed associations with adiposity, with CLO tending to track higher BMI indices whereas ACE and its metabolites showed an opposite trend at higher exposure levels [35]. In a nationwide Chinese survey, males exhibited higher urinary IMI, ACE, and ΣNEOs than females [13]. NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) (2015–2016) revealed that the main factors associated with 5-OH-IMI were season and race/ethnicity, rather than age or sex [21]. A Japanese adult study also noted demographic associations but cautioned potential dietary confounding [36]. Overall, the literature suggests that demographic associations of urinary NEOs can vary across compounds and populations. In the present study, most urinary NEOs showed no convincing associations with age, body size, or sex (Fig. S2-S5). Because of the limited sample size and the absence of a clear biological mechanism, the observed correlations should not be over-interpreted. Larger studies with detailed dietary, occupational, and environmental exposure information are needed to determine whether these associations are reproducible.

Transcriptomic effects of NEO/metabolite mixtures detected in surface and tap water on HEK293T cells

To evaluate cellular responses under environmentally relevant exposure conditions, we first examined the transcriptomic effects of NEO/metabolite mixtures on HEK293T cells after 48 and 72 h of exposure. These mixtures comprised the specific compounds and their respective maximum concentrations detected in surface water (SW) and tap water (TW) on Hainan Island in our previous studies (Table 2). As shown in Fig. 3A-D, although DEGs were detected in both the TW and SW groups, their numbers were highly limited, with the maximum proportion of DEGs accounting for less than 0.34% of all expressed genes (Table S5). We further performed qPCR validation for two selected DEGs. Only one gene (NFATC2) showed consistent expression changes (Fig. 3E-F), whereas the other (RP11-545E17.3) could not be reproducibly validated (data not shown). Collectively, our findings indicate that low concentrations of NEOs present in aquatic environments exert very limited transcriptomic effects on cells. The reproducible regulation of NFATC2 - an established regulator of inflammatory/immune-associated gene programs - suggests that low-level NEOs/metabolites exposure may engage signaling pathways relevant to immunomodulation, consistent with prior reports describing immune alterations following NEOs exposure [37–39].

In vitro assessment of modest cytotoxic responses under urinary-biomonitoring-informed NEO/metabolite exposure

As shown in Table 2, the maximum urinary concentration of ΣNEOs/metabolites detected in our population was only ≈ 0.1 µM (on the order of tens of µg/L), which is substantially lower than the concentrations used in the in vitro studies summarized in Table S1 (1µM to mM levels). This comparison highlights the importance of testing NEOs within biomonitoring-relevant concentration ranges.

Here, we conducted cytotoxicity assessments using urinary-biomonitoring-informed NEO/metabolite mixtures (Table 2, UR group) in human cell lines. Because urine is a major excretion pathway for NEOs and their metabolites [40], urinary concentrations provide useful biomonitoring indicators of exposure. However, they should not be interpreted as direct surrogates for systemic tissue concentrations or target-organ doses.

Cell viability

We treated cells with NEO/metabolite mixtures at 1×, 10×, 100×, and 1,000× the urinary concentrations and found that cell viability was inhibited in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4A). HeLa and HepG2 cells showed slightly greater viability reductions following exposure to the NEO/metabolite mixture, whereas A2780 cells showed relatively smaller changes. Given the limited magnitude of the effect, these differences should be interpreted as modest cell-line-dependent variations rather than definitive evidence of differential biological sensitivity. We also found that exposure to single compounds was sometimes more toxic than the mixture, even when the total concentration was the same (HEK293T cells, Fig. 4B). Overall, exposure to the NEO/metabolite mixture at urinary-relevant concentrations reduced cell viability by approximately 3–8%.

DNA fragmentation

The urinary NEO/metabolite mixture induced more DNA fragmentation than the control, although the increase remained within 3%, as shown by cell staining (Fig. 5A) and quantified in the statistical analysis (Fig. 5B). For DNA fragmentation, HepG2 cells showed relatively larger increases following NEO/metabolite exposure among the tested cell lines. IMI, IMI-U and DNT showed weaker DNA fragmentation-inducing effects than the mixture, whereas ACE and 5-OH-IMI showed stronger effects (Fig. 5C).

ROS generation

The urinary NEO/metabolite mixture also induced higher ROS levels in all four cell lines (Fig. 6A), with the increase remaining within 1.2% based on the quantitative analysis (Fig. 6B). HepG2 cells showed relatively larger ROS increases under the tested conditions, although the magnitude of change was small and should be interpreted cautiously. In terms of ROS induction, most single NEOs did not differ significantly from the NEO/metabolite mixture (Fig. 6C).

Taken together, exposure to the urinary NEOs profile resulted in modest but statistically significant reductions in cell viability and increases in DNA fragmentation and ROS levels. The toxic effects differed between the NEO/metabolite mixture and individual compounds, and the magnitude of the responses varied across cell lines.

In vitro transcriptomic responses to urinary-relevant NEOs exposure

In contrast to the waterborne NEO profile, 48 h exposure to the urinary NEO profile induced detectable and cell type-specific transcriptional changes across all four cell lines, with substantial differences in both the number of DEGs (Fig. S6A-D) (A2780: 293 DEGs; HEK293T: 312 DEGs; HepG2: 872 DEGs; HeLa: 237 DEGs) and functional enrichment patterns (Fig. S7, Fig. 7). The magnitude of transcriptomic changes varied across cell lines, with HepG2 showing larger DEG numbers than the other tested models.

(A-D) KEGG pathway enrichment results for DEGs identified between the NEO/metabolite-mixture-exposed and control groups in four cell lines: (A) HepG2, (B) HeLa, (C) A2780, and (D) HEK293T. The x-axis indicates the gene ratio, dot size represents the number of DEGs enriched in each pathway (count), and dot color denotes the adjusted enrichment p-value (padj)

We examined the specific genes underlying the KEGG-enriched terms across the four cell lines (Table S6) and found that exposure to urinary-relevant NEOs was associated with cell-type-specific transcriptional responses that converged on three recurrent response modules: innate immune-like inflammatory signaling, proteostasis/ER stress, and metabolic rewiring. HepG2 cells showed a liver-related response characterized by xenobiotic detoxification and redox/ER stress programs (CYP1A2, UGT2B10, GSTA2/GSTT4; HSPA family members; HERPUD1; PPP1R15A), together with innate immune-like signaling centered on TLR2, IKBKE, FADD/TRADD, and MAPK components, supporting a model in which NEOs elicit inflammatory stress signaling while engaging hepatic biotransformation and proteostasis mechanisms. In HEK293T cells, enrichment highlighted NF-κB-linked inflammatory signaling (MYD88, CXCL2) and stress-response regulators (GADD45B/G), accompanied by metabolic homeostasis cues (GYS1, MTHFR), suggesting activation of a TLR-MYD88-NF-κB axis with concomitant adjustment of energy buffering and one-carbon metabolism. In A2780 cells, despite a smaller enriched gene set, the signal was concentrated in receptor/ligand modules and metabolic control points, including a neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction signature featuring a nicotinic acetylcholine receptor subunit (CHRNA10) and multiple GPCRs (e.g., ADRA1D/ADRA2C, NTSR1, SSTR5), coupled to glycolytic regulation (PFKFB4, ALDOA) and an AMPK-related node (PFKFB4, MLYCD), consistent with early perturbation of signaling inputs that links to metabolic adaptation. In HeLa cells, several KEGG “disease” terms were driven predominantly by histone genes (H2A/H2B/H4), suggesting chromatin/nucleosome-related stress modules rather than bona fide disease programs; HeLa cells also displayed an inflammatory lipid-remodeling signature involving IL1B together with membrane lipid mediators (PLA2G4B, ASAH2, GGT5) and an inflammatory cell-death-associated signature, implicating phospholipid remodeling and sphingolipid/arachidonic acid pathways as potential effectors of cytotoxicity. Collectively, these data support a model in which NEOs, even at urinary-relevant exposure levels, are associated with cell-type-specific transcriptional response patterns that converge on (i) innate immune-like inflammatory signaling, (ii) ER stress/UPR-driven proteostasis disruption, and (iii) metabolic rewiring spanning glycolysis/one-carbon flux and membrane lipid mediator pathways, thereby providing a framework for interpreting cellular stress-response pathways associated with NEO/metabolite exposure.

To validate the reliability of RNA-seq-derived expression changes, we selected genes across different cell lines for qRT-PCR confirmation. As shown in Fig. S6E, although the fold-change magnitudes differed between qRT-PCR and RNA-seq, the directions of regulation (up- or down-regulation) were consistent, supporting the robustness of our transcriptomic conclusions.

In vitro metabolomic responses to urinary-relevant NEOs exposure

To define the functional metabolic landscape elicited by urinary-relevant NEO/metabolite mixtures, we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling across all four cell lines. PCA showed clear separation between NEO-exposed and control groups in both positive- and negative-ion modes, with high cumulative contribution rates (positive: 91.5–99.3%; negative: 85.4–99.9%), indicating a clear separation between exposure and control groups in the metabolomic profiles (Fig. S8). Consistent with the volcano plots, the magnitude of metabolic perturbation varied markedly by cell type (A2780: 262 differential metabolites (DMEs); HEK293T: 243 DMEs; HepG2: 92 DMEs; HeLa: 276 DMEs), with HepG2 showing the fewest number of differential metabolites (Fig. S9). The metabolomic data revealed a model-specific pattern, with HepG2 showing a more selective metabolic response despite clear pathway-level remodeling.

KEGG enrichment analysis further revealed two major metabolic response patterns (Fig. 8; Table S7). A2780 and HEK293T cells were dominated by amino acid metabolic rewiring, particularly within arginine-related pathways. A2780 and HEK293T commonly showed enrichment in arginine biosynthesis, arginine and proline metabolism, and histidine metabolism, and A2780 cells displayed elevated L-arginine, L-ornithine, L-citrulline, and argininosuccinate, consistent with altered urea-cycle/arginine flux.

In contrast, HeLa and HepG2 cells showed a lipid-centered metabolic phenotype. HeLa cells were enriched in sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism, with altered sphingosine-, ceramide-, and sphingomyelin-related metabolites, consistent with membrane lipid remodeling. HepG2 cells also showed glycerophospholipid pathway enrichment, together with enrichment in riboflavin and purine metabolism, indicating a more selective metabolic response linked to lipid remodeling and redox/cofactor metabolism.

Integration of transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis

Using an integrative comparison of metabolomic and transcriptomic outcomes, we observed concordance at the pathway level. Metabolomic signatures segregated into two major response patterns - arginine/nitrogen-centered rewiring (A2780 and HEK293T) versus membrane lipid remodeling (HeLa and HepG2) - and these patterns aligned closely with RNA-seq-derived pathway themes. In HeLa, the lipid-dominant phenotype (sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid enrichment) mirrored transcriptomic alterations in lipid mediator and membrane-associated genes (e.g., PLA2G4B, ASAH2) together with pro-inflammatory signaling (IL1B), suggesting an inflammatory lipid-remodeling axis that may contribute to the observed cellular responses; this interpretation is consistent with prior multi-omics studies reporting prominent lipid-pathway perturbations following NEOs exposure in vitro [41]. In HepG2, lipid-centered metabolomic changes were compatible with a liver-like transcriptomic program featuring xenobiotic/detoxification and ER/redox stress signatures, in line with evidence that NEOs/metabolites exposure can engage hepatic detox-like transcriptional responses and broader stress adaptation programs [42], as well as metabolomic disturbances in liver-related pathways in vivo [43]. More broadly, oxidative stress has been repeatedly implicated as a core mechanistic axis of NEO toxicity [44], providing a possible link between the proteostasis/stress signaling observed here and the downstream metabolic rewiring captured by our metabolomic analysis. Finally, our module-level convergence across omics is consistent with emerging mixture-focused multi-omics studies showing that NEO-containing cocktails can produce coordinated pathway disruptions encompassing lipid dysregulation, inflammation, and oxidative/stress responses [45]. These results provide biomonitoring-informed multi-omics evidence that urinary-concentration-based NEO/metabolite mixtures are associated with distinct, cell-type-dependent molecular response patterns, including nitrogen/arginine rewiring and membrane lipid remodeling, within the tested concentration range. Together, these findings link monitoring-informed mixture profiles to coordinated molecular response patterns in human cell-line models.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and applied a quantitative analytical workflow to determine urinary neonicotinoid insecticides (NEOs) and used urinary biomonitoring data, together with environmental monitoring data, to guide in vitro toxicological evaluation. By integrating multi-dimensional readouts, including cytotoxicity phenotyping and multi-omics profiling, we show that urinary-biomonitoring-informed NEO/metabolite mixtures can elicit measurable, although generally modest, cell-specific molecular perturbations within the tested concentration range (≈ 0.1 µM), far lower than those used in previously reported artificial exposure studies (Table S1). Importantly, when tested under the same experimental settings, the water-environment NEOs profile examined in parallel produced no (or only minimal) global molecular changes, underscoring that concentration profiles informed by urinary biomonitoring may provide complementary information beyond environmental occurrence alone.

Across the tested models, the observed responses consistently pointed to a coordinated stress-and-adaptation program that links inflammatory-like signaling, proteostasis imbalance, and metabolic remodeling, providing a coherent molecular-response framework for interpreting the cellular effects of NEO/metabolite mixtures. Collectively, our findings highlight that NEOs exposure assessment should move beyond single-compound testing and consider mixture composition and biologically relevant concentration ranges derived from biomonitoring. The combined analytical and molecular-profiling framework established here provides experimental evidence that may support more comprehensive future toxicological evaluation of NEOs and may help connect human biomonitoring data with mechanistically informed toxicological interpretation.

Several limitations should be noted. First, although several cytotoxicity-related endpoints reached statistical significance, the absolute changes in cell viability, DNA fragmentation, and ROS levels were small; therefore, their biological impact at the tested concentrations may be limited. Second, the urinary concentrations used to construct the UR mixture provide biomonitoring-informed exposure indicators but should not be interpreted as direct surrogates for systemic tissue concentrations or target-organ doses. Because many NEOs and their metabolites are primarily excreted through urine, renal concentration processes may cause urinary levels to differ substantially from tissue exposure levels. Moreover, absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion were not modeled in the present study. Therefore, the tested concentrations should be considered biomonitoring-relevant concentration ranges rather than fully characterized real-world internal-exposure profiles. Future studies incorporating physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling or in vitro to in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) approaches will be needed to estimate target-organ doses more accurately. In addition, the urinary biomonitoring analysis was based on a relatively small sample size from one geographic region, and the selected cell lines represent only a limited set of in vitro models. Another limitation is that most metabolites in the untargeted metabolomics analysis were putatively annotated based on MS/MS spectral matching rather than confirmed individually using authentic standards. These factors limit the generalizability of the exposure distribution, demographic associations, and cell-type-specific responses. Future studies using larger populations and additional physiologically relevant models will be needed to further validate these findings.
