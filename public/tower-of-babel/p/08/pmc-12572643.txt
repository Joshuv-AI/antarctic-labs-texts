Rasch analysis and differential item functioning of the marital happiness scale in Oman
Authors: Ehab M N Omara, Mustafa Ali Khalaf, Manal Khasib Al Fazari, Mahmood Salim Almaawali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarital happiness is an important variable in family life in Oman as a coherent Muslim society. Recent societal attention to marital happiness and divorce rates in Oman underpins the need for a valid and reliable instrument to precisely measure the levels of marital happiness among Omani spouses. However, a close look at the measurement of marital happiness reveals the scarcity of reliable and valid instruments developed using item response theory assumption. To this end, we used data from a nationally representative sample of Omani couples to develop and validate the marital happiness scale in light of the Rasch rating scale model. Using Winsteps software version 3.80.1 (https://www.winsteps.com/index.htm), we examined unidimensionality, rating scale functioning, item infit and outfit statistics, item polarity, reliability and separation indices of items and persons, the item hierarchy, and DIF across genders. The findings indicated that marital happiness is a single unidimensional construct and that the scale has adequate psychometric properties in Oman despite the existence of DIF in certain items. Limitations and further research topics concerning cross-cultural validation of the scale and qualitative refinement of the items showing DIF were discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21736-6.Keywords: Marital happiness, Rasch analysis, DIF, Scale development, Scale validation, Omani couplesSubject terms: Psychology, Health care

Introduction

Throughout history, happiness has consistently stood as the paramount objective for humans, surpassing all other goals1. A marital relationship characterized by tranquillity, love, and mercy is a sign of God’s blessing; however, divorce rates in Omani society are increasing2. With the divorce rate doubling, there is an increasing demand for counselling and therapy to increase psychological vitality3,4. Moreover, studies in Oman have focused mainly on the theoretical background, reasons, factors underlying marital happiness, and negative effects of marital maladjustment as well as its relationship with other variables5,6. Recently, researchers have explored the factors leading to marital happiness due to its impact on Omani family cohesion highlighting the need for a better understanding of marital happiness mechanisms and determinants especially in the Omani context7,8.

In other Asian contexts, Chen and Hu9 explored the variations in how each spouse values financial contributions and household responsibilities owing to the importance of financial aspects in family life stability. Marital happiness is influenced not only by financial support but also by social support10. Marital happiness affects different aspects of family life; for example, there is a positive association between marital happiness and parenting satisfaction. Moreover, some scholars have argued that marital happiness is the driving force of positive parenting experiences11.

Globally, Ipsos Group, a multinational market research and consulting firm in Paris, conducted a survey using 22,508 adults aged 18–74 years from 32 countries worldwide, including the USA, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Japan, and reported that 76% felt loved by their partner/spouse, 84% were satisfied with their relationships, and 63% were satisfied with their romantic or sex life12.

Regionally, very few studies have been conducted on marital happiness in Arab Gulf countries5,13. To complicate matters further, many existing studies tend to be exploratory in nature and lack the utilization of carefully designed and validated measurement tools. As a result, prior researchers7,8 have emphasized the need for valid measures of marital happiness developed in the Omani context. The limited attention given to the marital happiness of Omani incumbents motivated the authors of this study to develop a robust measure of marital happiness via responses from Omani citizens.

In Oman, a recent study revealed that the overall level of marital happiness among Omani couples was high8. However, the increasing rates of divorce and family breakdown have increased public concern about the severity of marital and family problems14. Research has shown that marital happiness follows a U-shaped curve, indicating that it decreases at the beginning of marriage and increases in later years15. However, other studies have shown that younger couples are more maritally adjusted than older couples5,16. With respect to the association between education level and marital happiness, individuals with higher levels of education were found to exhibit greater levels of marital happiness10.

Relatedly, a lack of marital happiness leads directly to emotional detachment. Some couples suffer from emotional detachment despite remaining married. The claim that emotional disengagement may be a precursor to formal divorce is not exaggerated. For example, Al Shahrani and Hammad17 reported that 78.4% of married couples (n = 305) experienced moderate to severe levels of emotional divorce. This instability in marital status might lead to mental health problems such as depression and alexithymia. Compared with their peers, women with longer periods of marriage and who have more than five children exhibit higher levels of alexithymia13,17. Previous studies have indicated that sexual and interpersonal factors and feelings of expression have positive impacts on marital happiness17.

Problem

According to the National Centre for Statistics and Information (NCSI)18 in Oman, divorce rates have substantially increased since 2013, with 3426 divorce cases recorded in 2020. The highest divorce rate was reported in Muscat Governorate, with 796 cases, whereas Musandam Governorate had the lowest number of divorce cases, with only 18 cases7. Given that 8% of marriages in Oman have ended in divorce, as seen from data over the past decade18, these statistics indicate the presence of various problems experienced by married couples in Oman. They also serve as a significant indicator of the lack of marital stability and happiness, leading such a large proportion of couples to resort to divorce.

Numerous studies have assessed marital satisfaction across various countries, including the UAE13, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia17, and Oman7,8. Importantly, however, the measurement tools employed in these studies were not developed via item response theory (IRT). A significant portion of the literature has predominantly relied on the assumption of classical testing theory (CTT) when validating measures related to constructs overlapping or intertwined with marital happiness, such as marital adjustment (Locke & Wallace, 1959)19, marital quality3,20, authentic happiness21, relationship evaluation22, relationship flourishing23, partnership quality24, and marital satisfaction25.

Some prior studies in Oman and Arabic-speaking countries did not provide any evidence of score validity and instead relied on the validity indicators reported in previous studies that used the same scale. The small number of participants used in computing validity and reliability imposes serious concerns as well. In the literature on psychometrics, five individuals should be selected for each item on a 5-point Likert scale26,27. For example, if the scale consists of 20 items, the standardization sample should include at least 100 individuals.

None of the previous studies in the Omani setting5–8 verified the measurement invariance (MI) across gender (male‒female), culture, or age, despite the use of a scale to compare these groups. The lack of verification of the prerequisite assumptions of MI sheds doubts on the results of these differences and the inability to determine their source. It is unclear whether these differences are due to the differential performance of the scale items (scale bias), whether they are genuine differences, or whether they are nonsubstantial differences that occur by chance. Other studies of marital happiness compared age and gender groups in terms of marital happiness without verifying measurement invariance, even though this is a prerequisite before making any cross-cultural, gender, or age group comparisons28.

Although previous studies have provided good work to clarify the factors affecting marital happiness in Oman, the valid measures constructed to assess the latent trait of marital happiness are not well developed, as presented in the next section. This study fills this gap by providing a valid and reliable IRT-based instrument of marital happiness that could be used for research and practice not only in Oman but also in Arabic-speaking countries. Additionally, this work contributes to the literature on the marital happiness of Omani citizens, not people from other nationalities, residing in Oman by providing new evidence from pure Omani couples because other studies have dealt with participants living in Oman, whether they are Omani citizens or just residents in Oman.

Drawbacks of previous measures

Notably, several limitations exist in the translation procedures of some marital happiness scales used in previous research. Many researchers did not use the back translation method and instead relied solely on translation by an English language specialist. This procedure alone does not guarantee accurate conveyance of the intended psychological content of the items, especially if the translator is not specialized in psychology. If the methodological procedures of the measurement tools are questioned, their psychometric properties (validity, reliability, and fairness) are invalidated29, potentially limiting their usefulness in practice and restricting its utility in research and decision-making purposes.

A review of the literature aimed at constructing new measures of marital happiness indicated that various scales consisted of a limited number of items, a psychometric deficit known as construct underrepresentation29,30. Many of the available measures of marital happiness validated in Oman use item–total correlation to ensure score validity, even though item–total correlation is known as the discrimination index and is not utilized as a validity indicator31. Many of these measures exhibit either excessive length (101 items and 300 items) or a lack of direct alignment with the measurement of marital happiness. Moreover, they have been constructed and validated in Western culture. Notably, within the context of the Omani context, no study has utilized any of the IRT models to construct a scale specifically designed for the direct measurement of marital happiness among Omani couples.

Some authors are satisfied with computing the item‒total correlation as an indicator of validity, whereas this procedure is called the item discrimination index in the literature31. Other studies highlighted that item‒total correlation is just an indicator of the internal validity of the measurement tool32. The internal consistency reliability by Cronbach’s alpha for some measurement tools of marital happiness was lower than the acceptable value of 0.7033. This weak reliability calls into question the data collected by those tools.

Despite the recommendation of psychometricians to replace Cronbach’s alpha with McDonald’s Omega reliability coefficient due to the limitations of the former26,28, or at least to use it alongside Cronbach’s alpha, all previous studies of marital happiness have used Cronbach’s alpha and have not utilized McDonald’s Omega reliability coefficient, except for one study.

Categorical omega is recommended for evaluating the reliability of measures, especially those employing ordinal data, as it has demonstrated unbiased score reliability estimates29. Notably, existing measures of marital happiness have relied solely on Cronbach’s alpha reliability. Nevertheless, certain psychometric experts have warned against using the alpha alone as an indicator of internal consistency reliability34,35. Therefore, it is advisable to compute an additional reliability coefficient alongside the alpha, such as MacDonald’s omega, to provide a more robust reliability index.

Some researchers have verified the construct validity of marital happiness scales. However, when the authors examined the methodology procedures of those studies, it became clear that they considered construct validity as the correlation between the dimensions and the total score. This is a serious methodological issue. However, psychometric robustness can guarantee measurement integrity and quality36.

Concerning the unidimensionality of the marital happiness scale, some studies have dealt with it as a unidimensional construct, whereas others have argued that it is a multidimensional one. With respect to the reliability estimates, nearly all the previous studies utilized Cronbach’s alpha, although no study except7,8 which used McDonald’s omega despite the frequent shortcomings of the alpha coefficient indicated in the literature, as the alpha coefficient is not an optimal measure of reliability35. Despite the availability of many instruments regarding marital adjustment and satisfaction5–8117, no measure has been constructed using IRT assumptions.

Given the importance of marital happiness in Omani families and the threats and negative impacts of marital unhappiness on the future of children coupled with the scarcity of standardized measures of marital happiness at the national level within the Omani context, the current study aims to construct and validate a marital happiness scale on a sample representative of Omani society.

Literature review

The Omani family and marital happiness

The Sultanate of Oman is “a member of the Gulf Cooperation Council States (GCC) and it shares several common features like language, religion, and culture with these countries” (p120)2. The Omani family is essentially built on Islamic principles and is highly cohesive37. The Omani family can be regarded as highly cohesive, as it is strongly influenced by the power of Islamic heritage side-by-side with the great renaissance of Oman, the ethics and traditions of tribalism that support the sustainability of the Omani family. In 1995, a national survey of family health indicated that urban females tended to marry later, women with lower education levels were more inclined to be involved in polygamous marriages, and illiterate women were more likely to be divorced and more likely to marry again than women who held high school education certificates or university degrees. With respect to childbearing, highly educated Omani women stop living beyond the age of 35 years. Globalization and the swift revolution in information and communication technology will continue to significantly influence and challenge the Omani family2. Different factors influence marital happiness in Oman, such as financial income, polygamy, and education level. Recently, polygamy was associated with lower levels of marital happiness in Oman7,8.

Unsurprisingly, access to education opportunities and high-quality healthcare services has empowered Omani women to postpone marriage and childbearing, leading to a decline in both fertility rates and average family size. Nevertheless, divorce is a straightforward path crafted by the Islamic religion to end toxic marriage relationships2.

Measuring marital happiness in the light of CTT versus IRT

A plethora of literature exists on marital happiness and its correlates via measures developed in light of classical testing theory (CCT). Few or even no studies have been conducted using the assumption of modern testing theory, namely, item response theory (IRT). The use of IRT in the development and validation of measurement tools has led to the availability of more robust and precise instruments29,38–40. Unfortunately, in Arabic-speaking countries, there is no appropriate valid instrument constructed using IRT for the purpose of the direct assessment of marital happiness, especially in Oman. Hence, this study addresses this gap in the literature. In this study, we adopted the Rasch rating scale to calibrate a newly developed measure of marital happiness using data from a nationwide representative sample across the Sultanate of Oman.

Rasch rating scale model

The Rasch rating scale model is the most widely used model among IRT models38. It is widely used in psychology studies to validate measurement tools with high levels of precision and quality41,42. Owing to its psychometric robustness and multifunctionality, it has been properly adopted in various disciplines other than psychology and education40,43–45. IRT is based upon robust fundamental assumptions, including unidimensionality, local independence and item characteristic curves40. In accordance with Bond and Fox46, the Rasch rating scale model is used when the distances between response categories are interpreted as equivalent. Therefore, we utilized it in this study.

Differential item functioning (DIF)

Differential item functioning implies that individuals with the same level of the latent trait do not respond equally to the same items depending on their membership in a certain group (e.g., age group, gender, race)47,48. Establishing measurement invariance is crucial prior to conducting comparisons across cultures, genders, or age groups38. Notably, DIF is a threat to the validity of measurement tools48. In this study, we utilized Rasch–Welch’s t and Mantel–Haenszel chi square indices to detect potential item bias across both genders.

Gender differences in marital happiness

Gender is a significant positive predictor of marital adjustment in Omani society5. Marital happiness tends to be lower among couples where the wife earns more than the husband does9. In recent studies in Oman, men reported higher levels of happiness and adjustment than females did6–8. Some authors argue that household tasks, job burdens and children bearing responsibility drive women to feel less happy than males do5. Given that trustworthy results based on valid and reliable instruments can inform policy, the current study examined the psychometric properties of the marital happiness scale in Oman. Method

Instrument

The marital happiness scale

The Arabic version of the Marital Happiness Scale7 was used to measure perceived marital happiness among Omani married couples. The scale consists of 32 items measuring two subscales: emotional and practical involvement (19 items, α = 0.922) and marital relationship tension (13 items, α = 0.859). A three-point response category format was used per the instructions of the psychometric literature27, ranging from never = 1 to always = 3. The factorial validity and internal consistency reliability were adequate. It is noteworthy that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Participants

A total of 1137 Omani individuals responded to the questionnaire after they were selected randomly from all governorates of the country. Most of them were females (67.6%), whereas males represented 32.4% (see Fig. 1). More than half of the participants (56.6%) held a bachelor’s degree, while 25.2% had completed high school (see Fig. 2). A smaller proportion were postgraduate students (13.8%), whereas 4.4% had presecondary education. A total of 96% reported having one wife, and 4% had two wives. The most common revenue category was above 2600 USD (41.3%), followed by 1300 and 2600 USD (35.4%), with 14.5% less than 1300 USD (see Fig. 3). Notably, 99 participants (8.7%) did not provide information about their income. A total of 55.3% were married to nonrelatives, whereas 44.7% were married to relatives (see Fig. 4). 46 (4%) of husbands were married to two wives (see Fig. 5). The participants were selected from different governorates (see Fig. 6), with the largest number from Al Batinah South (24.8%), Muscat (22.2%), and Al Batinah North (16.4%). Other governorates included Al Dakhiliyah (9.1%), Al Dhahirah (7.6%), Al Sharqiyah South and North China (each 6.5%), and a smaller percentage from Al Buraymi, Dhofar, Musandam, and Al Wusta. The names of the respondents were not collected to help them remain anonymous and reduce social desirability bias, as recommended by Yang et al.36.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Unidimensionality

The assessment of dimensionality was first carried out at the overall scale level and then for each factor separately. At the total scale level, the Rasch dimension explained 21.2% of the raw variance (39.8% of total variance), while the first contrast in standardized residuals reached 2.7 eigenvalue units (5.0%). This value falls below the critical value 3.0 typically used as an indicator of secondary dimensions49. When factors were analyzed independently, Factor 1 explained 17.1% of the raw variance (47.4%) with a first contrast of 2.0 units (5.5%), and Factor 2 explained 8.0% (38.0%) with a first contrast of 1.8 units (8.5%). These results support the assumption of unidimensionality for both subscales and justify analyzing them as distinct constructs rather than combining them into a single global scale. Local independence

The largest standardized residual correlation, known as Q3, is typically used in the literature to assess whether the local independence assumption is violated. In this analysis, the Winsteps results showed that residual correlations ranged from − 0.24 to 0.27, indicating the presence of local independence, as these values did not exceed the commonly reported cut-off criterion of 0.30. In some studies, the critical standards have been reported as high as 0.50 or even 0.70.

Rating scale functioning

The observed count of the three response categories ranged from 2422 to 21,360, exceeding the cut-off value of 10 for each response category and indicating that the three-point Likert scale functioned well in this measure. Additionally, the values of the outfit MNSQ (0.76–1.50) were acceptable[50,51]. Moreover, the average measure ranged from 0.39 to 2.68, and the category thresholds increased with the response level, indicating the participants’ ability to distinguish between the 3-point Likert scale categories. Table 4 indicates that the fit statistics of most items fall within the accepted range for the MNSQ (0.6–1.4), suggesting an overall good fit for the Rasch model. However, items 7 (infit = 1.59; outfit = 2.01) and 27 (infit & outfit = 1.54) present potential misfit statistics. Items 10 (outfit = 1.45), 15 (outfit = 1.34), and 19 (outfit = 1.46) also show moderate misfit, with MNSQ values exceeding 1.4. On the other hand, several items have MNSQ values below 0.7, including item 2 (infit = 0.71, outfit = 0.68), item 24 (infit = 0.75, outfit = 0.69), and item 25 (outfit = 0.54). Various studies have proposed different acceptable ranges for infit statistics. According to Jong et al.58, items with an MNSQ Jong et al. (2015) considered values greater than 0.60 and up to 1.40 to be acceptable and values exceeding 0.60–1.40 are just “noisy” but not detrimental to the scale and do not deform its structure; they should be retained and not recommended to be removed as per the instructions of both developers of the Rasch Model59. The negative impact of noisy items is minimal and does not significantly affect the results of the scale. The items that should be eliminated are those whose infit and outfit MNSQ exceed the value of 2.

Reliability and separation

Indicators of measurement precision further confirmed the quality of the scale. Person separation indices ranged from 1.76 to 2.83, with reliability values between 0.76 and 0.89, suggesting sufficient ability to distinguish between respondents with different levels of the latent trait. Item separation was exceptionally high, ranging from 9.35 to 14.01, with reliability values of 0.99 across all analyses. These findings demonstrate excellent stability of the item hierarchy and wide construct coverage. Taken together, the reliability and separation indices indicate that the scale and its subscales provide consistent and precise measurement.

According to Park & Park60,, “The larger the separation index, the greater the extent to which the test can distinguish the measurement function level”. In this study, the following criteria was used to accept the person separation index of 3.00 which represents an excellent level of person separation. The Pearson separation index in our study was 2.83 while item separation index was 11.90 exceeding the desirable limit.

Differential item functioning (DIF) across genders

DIF analysis yielded significant DIF values for five items in the first factor of the scale and three items in the second factor. This implies that a total of 8 items (2, 7, 10, 15, 19, 25, 27, 28) show moderate to high DIF (see Table 5). These differences suggest that most items function similarly across genders, with only a few requiring closer examinations for potential bias. Items showing DIF can be deleted from the scale because the psychometric quality of the measurement tool is negatively affected by the existence of DIF36,57.

Figure 10 illustrates the DIF across genders (male vs. female) for items of the first factor of the marital happiness scale. Most items cluster around the zero DIF line, but some items show significant DIF, especially for females, indicating that females may have found these items easier or more favourable than males did.

Discussion

Considering the scarcity of a psychometrically robust instrument of marital happiness, the current study analysed the item-level psychometric properties of a newly developed tool in Oman via the Rasch rating scale model. Although recent studies e.g7,8., have indicated that marital happiness is high in Omani society, divorce rates have increased2. Given these two distinct phenomena, there is doubt about the ability of existing measures to capture the latent trait of marital happiness. Some of those scales target marital satisfaction or adjustment, and others measure constructs very similar to the underlying concept embedded in this terminology. This study aims to construct and validate a measure of marital happiness via a nationally representative sample to address this gap. The results of this study supported the unidimensionality of the marital happiness scale, indicating that a single underlying construct accounts for the greatest amount of the variance in the marital happiness scores.

All instruments of marital happiness available in the Omani context are developed via classical testing theory assumptions. Owing to the miscellaneous drawbacks in the existing instruments, several researchers have accordingly argued against their utility and preciseness. Therefore, the present study used the Rasch rating scale model to validate a 32-item marital happiness scale and verify differential item functioning. Using Winsteps software, we computed the mean square (MNSQ) and standardized fit statistics (ZSTDs) for both the infit and outfit measures. The findings of the Rasch analysis indicated that all infit and outfit values ranged between 0.6 and 1.4, falling within the acceptable criteria for Likert-scale items recommended by Bond and Fox46.

In our analysis, item difficulty ranged between − 1.55 and 1.68. This range of item difficulty estimates indicated that higher ability estimates implied higher levels of the latent trait (marital happiness). According to Falegnami et al.45, “item difficulty estimates place each item on the latent trait continuum” (p.7). In the current study, the existence of DIF in 8 items (2, 7, 10, 15, 19, 25, 27, 28) (See Table 5) requires further revision through cognitive interviews with experts, who should assess why these problematic items function differently and check them for clarity, relevance, and redundancy or completely remove them if necessary to improve the psychometric properties of the scale.

For example, items 15, 27, and 28 have very large DIFs. Upon closer scrutiny of these items, it appears that these items contain implicit reference to sexual satisfaction and direct mention of jealousy and spying on spouses, either wives or husbands. Notably, these topics may be considered sensitive or culturally inappropriate to discuss openly within the conservative context of Omani society. As a result, participants’ responses were influenced by social desirability, or they may have misunderstood the intent of the item statements. It is also possible that inherent gender differences play a role in the occurrence of significant DIF. It is possible that females are too sensitive to issues of jealousy and spying on spouses. Both males and females have different concepts for satisfaction, whereas males inherently emphasize sexual satisfaction, females, on the other hand, are more responsive to romantic satisfaction and love speech. These results reflect the variations in how physical or emotional satisfaction is perceived and expressed by both genders in Oman. To conclude, the overall findings demonstrate the feasibility of this instrument, after DIF items are removed, for the purposes of research and practice (e.g., marital counselling).

Conclusion

One strength of this study is the large sample size intended to be more representative of the general population than many validation studies use. We did not recruit traditional aged college students or a specific clinical population; rather, a nationwide representative sample was selected. Given that most marital adjustment problems tend to appear during the early years of marriage, it is essential to educate youth about the fundamentals of marital life and effective ways to handle potential problems that arise between couples, especially newly married couples. Such education and marital counselling can help individuals avoid both emotional and formal divorce.

It is difficult to compare the results of the current study with previous research because earlier studies developed marital happiness scales using classical test theory, whereas this study applied item response theory. For example, some previous studies e.g6–8., created marital happiness scales with Omani participants, but they did not calibrate the items using the Rasch rating scale model. Another study e.g14., examined the effectiveness of a marriage enrichment program in the Omani context and developed a tailored measure to assess specific components of the intervention, such as communication, problem-solving, commitment, relationships with family and friends, financial management, and emotional intimacy.

Although the items in that scale were clearly phrased and concise, it was not developed using item response theory. To make matters worse, only face validity and Cronbach’s alpha were reported to support its psychometric properties. Despite including 64 items across six subscales, the study relied on responses from only 50 participants to compute validity and reliability evidence. This small sample raises concerns about the robustness of the scale and its ability to accurately capture the underlying construct of marital happiness.

Limitations and future research

This study is not without limitations that may affect the generalizability of its findings. For example, data collection relies only on a single method, namely, a self-report questionnaire. This approach is negatively influenced by the tendency toward social desirability bias. Accordingly, future research should utilize a mixed-methods approach that integrates both quantitative and qualitative data. The imbalance of the distribution of participants among different governorates might impose a restriction on the scope of the generalization of findings as well. The small number of participants from certain governorates such as Alwusta is recognized as a challenge in previous research e.g62.,.

Other factors paramount to marital happiness and family life stability, but less investigated, are the sexual satisfaction and financial contribution of the working wife, which are typically related to the husband’s contentedness. If it is intended to be used in other Arab Gulf area countries, this instrument should be verified with data from cross-cultural contexts to ensure its measurement invariance across cultures. Although the overall results indicate general measurement equivalence across males and females, one obvious limitation is the presence of differential item functioning in eight items in the measure on the basis of gender. This finding raises some concerns about measurement invariance. To improve the psychometric robustness and applicability of the scale, future studies should reword or refine these items during cognitive interviews with participants or expert reviewers63.

Given that 8 items show significant DIF, future studies should qualitatively investigate the statements of those items and refine the scale to guarantee its validity and fairness. DIF was examined merely across genders, and a potential direction for future research could involve checking other demographic variables, such as urban/rural and high-income/low-income groups. In its original version, scale validity was computed via exploratory factor analysis and, in this analysis, by Rasch measurement unidimensionality and DIF, future studies might collect evidence of validity via external constructs and concurrent validity.
