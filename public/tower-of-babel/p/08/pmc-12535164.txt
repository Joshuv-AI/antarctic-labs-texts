International accreditation in Iranian universities of medical sciences: a qualitative analysis of challenges and solutions
Authors: Asgar Aghaei Hashjin, Enayat Shabani, Ali Tahmasebi, Pouria Farrokhi, Hediye Seval Akgün
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInternational accreditation of universities is a comprehensive evaluation process conducted by reputable external bodies to ensure adherence to quality and effectiveness standards in higher education institutions. This study aimed to identify the challenges and strategic solutions related to the international accreditation of Iranian universities of medical sciences (IUMS).MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in 2023 across IUMS. Data were collected through purposive semistructured interviews with 36 individuals, including administrators, faculty members, and experts from various university departments under the supervision of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MOHME). The interviews continued until data saturation was achieved. The data were analyzed using the conventional content analysis approach proposed by Graneheim and Lundman.ResultsA total of 52 challenges were identified, the most critical of which were cultural barriers, weak implementation processes, and inappropriate organizational structures. In parallel, 57 solutions were classified into seven categories, including training and education, infrastructure development, communication enhancement, policymaking and planning, self-assessment, improvement of standards and indicators, and process optimization.ConclusionsThis study identified major barriers to international accreditation in IUMS and proposed practical solutions. Progress requires joint action: universities should strengthen training, infrastructure, documentation, and internal quality assurance, while accrediting bodies and national authorities must provide supportive regulations, adequate resources, and context-sensitive standards aligned with global frameworks. Coordinated efforts at both levels are essential for enhancing accreditation readiness, improving quality, and increasing global competitiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08031-8.Keywords: International accreditation, Iranian universities of medical sciences, Higher education institutions, Quality assurance

Introduction

International accreditation is an external evaluation process that ensures higher education institutions meet specific quality standards [1]. It improves academic performance, institutional credibility, and teaching quality while promoting strategic planning and continuous improvement [2]. This process also boosts student satisfaction, international mobility, and global competitiveness [3, 4]. However, accreditation also has challenges, such as high resource demands, excessive documentation, and difficulty balancing local and international quality requirements. While it can increase faculty engagement, it may also create more administrative work [5].

International accreditation is crucial for ensuring the quality and global recognition of higher education institutions, particularly medical universities. Prominent international bodies such as the World Higher Education Database [6], the International Network for Quality Assurance Agencies in Higher Education [7], the Council for Higher Education Accreditation (CHEA) [8], the European Association for Quality Assurance in Higher Education [9], and the European Quality Assurance Register for Higher Education [10] provide essential frameworks and standards. These organizations maintain databases and support international evaluation practices.

The accreditation process begins with a university’s application and self-assessment, which results in a self-study report. This is followed by an external evaluation, including site visits, document analysis, and stakeholder interviews. The evaluation team then submits a detailed report to the accrediting body, which makes an accreditation decision based on the institution’s compliance with established standards. Understanding this process is key to examining the challenges and pathways to improving international accreditation in IUMS [11, 12].

Despite these global benefits, the context of Iran presents unique structural, political, and cultural constraints that complicate the adoption of international accreditation. Issues such as centralized governance, sanctions limiting collaboration with reputable international agencies, and misalignment between national and international standards hinder sustainable progress. These challenges not only affect the readiness of medical universities but also underscore the necessity of a focused investigation tailored to the Iranian context [13, 14].

The international accreditation of universities in Iran is a relatively recent development. Based on field research, Tehran University of Medical Sciences was the first Iranian institution to undergo international institutional evaluation. It received institutional accreditation in 2013 from the Accreditation Service for International Schools, Colleges & Universities [15]. Globally, accreditation frameworks not only verify compliance with standards but also promote continuous quality enhancement through self-evaluation and stakeholder engagement [1, 5]. In Iran, aligning these frameworks with the dual academic and clinical missions of medical universities is challenging owing to structural and coordination issues [16, 17].

Although localized medical education standards have been introduced, there is still limited integrated evidence on the specific barriers to and practical solutions for advancing international accreditation in this context [13, 17]. Previous research in Iran has identified fragmented challenges in accreditation. Yarmohammadian et al. reported issues such as unsustainable processes and weak survey instruments [17], while Khan et al. emphasized excessive governmental control and approval of underperforming institutions, raising concerns about credibility [18]. Gandomkar et al. developed national standards based on the World Federation for Medical Education but noted limitations in optimization and evaluability, underscoring difficulties in contextual adaptation [19].

While international accreditation offers significant benefits—such as enhanced quality, global collaboration, and improved rankings—there is a notable knowledge gap concerning the specific challenges and opportunities for IUMS. Limited research has explored the unique context of accreditation within Iran’s academic system. To address this gap, the present study investigated the key challenges and identified practical solutions for advancing international accreditation among Iranian universities of medical sciences.

Methods

Design and setting

This exploratory qualitative study, conducted in 2023, drew on semistructured interviews and used conventional qualitative content analysis for data interpretation. Participants were selected purposively and through snowball sampling from three main groups: (1) policymakers from the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MOHME), (2) university administrators and deputy directors responsible for accreditation and quality assurance, and (3) faculty members with direct involvement in accreditation processes. Additional participants were recruited using the snowball sampling method.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were a minimum of five years of work experience, sufficient knowledge of accreditation or quality assurance, and at least a bachelor’s degree. The participants’ expertise was evaluated based on their organizational positions and academic resumes. We conducted interviews only after the participants confirmed their ability to respond to the questions. Consequently, individuals who lacked sufficient knowledge or experience in accreditation, as well as those who declined participation, were excluded from the study.

Data collection

We collected data through in-depth, individual interviews. We developed an interview guide containing both closed-ended demographic questions and open-ended main questions aligned with our research objectives (Supplementary file 1). The interview guide was developed based on a literature review and expert input. Three faculty experts confirmed its face and content validity, and a pilot test with two eligible participants was conducted to ensure clarity and relevance before final use. The interviews were scheduled in advance, each lasting an average of 55 min. Owing to geographical dispersion, we used online platforms (e.g., Skyroom, Skype, and Google Meet) for remote interviews.

The interviews continued until we reached data saturation, meaning no new concepts emerged. In total, we conducted 36 interviews with experts from the MOHME and several universities of medical sciences. We asked all participants two key open-ended questions: “What are the most critical challenges that IUMS face in achieving international accreditation?” and “What strategies can improve the implementation of international accreditation in IUMS?”

Data analysis and trustworthiness

We analyzed the data via a conventional content analysis method with an inductive approach, following a 5-step process [20]. We transcribed the interviews verbatim immediately after each session to identify concepts. We then reviewed the text to extract relevant codes. Next, we assigned labels to meaning units and grouped codes with similar concepts. We abstracted codes into main concepts and created new categories through constant comparison. Finally, we distilled latent content into overarching categories.

We used MAXQDA version 12 to manage the data. Data trustworthiness was established using Guba and Lincoln’s criteria [21]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement, member checking, and peer debriefing. Dependability was ensured by maintaining an audit trail and double coding. Confirmability was supported by reflexive memoing and external audits, while transferability was addressed by providing thick descriptions of the context and participants. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts. Any discrepancies in coding were discussed and resolved through consensus. In cases of disagreement, a senior faculty member in health services management acted as an adjudicator to ensure consistency and rigor in the coding process.

In addition to thematic coding, we assessed the relative priority of the identified challenges and solutions. For each main theme and its subthemes, we calculated the number of participants (n) who explicitly mentioned it, expressing this count as a percentage (%) of the total sample (N = 36). Based on these values, we classified the salience of each theme as high (≥ 50% of participants), moderate (25%−49%), or low (< 25%).

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Iranian National Committee for Ethics in Biomedical Research (IR.IUMS.REC.1402.546). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the interviews. Participation was voluntary, and the participants were assured that they could withdraw at any time without consequences. To protect confidentiality, we anonymized the transcripts by using participant codes instead of names and stored all data securely with access limited to the research team.

As shown in Table 2, cultural barriers, inappropriate organizational structures, and weak implementation processes emerged as high-salience challenges, each mentioned by more than half of the participants. Legal and regulatory constraints, inadequate standards and indicators, and inefficient evaluation processes were classified as moderate-salience themes.

Challenges at the level of universities

Cultural barriers

Many participants emphasized that an inadequate accreditation culture within universities—at both organizational and individual levels—remains a major barrier to achieving international accreditation. Strengthening shared commitment and cross-unit coordination was viewed as essential for success.

“In my experience, even with national accreditation—which I’m sure applies to international accreditation, too—there’s no real unity among different parts of the university. It’s like every unit just looks out for itself. When you ask another department for a file or something, they delay it as much as they can. Some think that if our department scores low, it somehow benefits them. But the truth is, we’re all supposed to be working toward the same goal.” (HCM-DEP-01).

International accreditation should function as a catalyst for embedding continuous quality improvement into institutional processes, rather than as a standalone certification target. When pursued solely as an endpoint, it risks undermining sustained progress and may lead to post-accreditation declines in standards. Lasting improvement depends on integrating quality enhancement into the institution’s core operations.

“Unfortunately, based on my personal experience, I’ve come to realize that managers engage in improving processes and documentation only when an accreditation is underway. Once it’s over, everything goes back to how it was.” (PHARM-DEP-01).

Strengthening individual culture in alignment with organizational values is vital for advancing international accreditation. Targeted strategies, including capacity-building initiatives, can raise awareness among all staff about its importance and benefits.

“Usually, the toughest part of the accreditation process falls on lower-level employees, so we’ve developed a kind of phobia toward it. If there’s any reward, it’s the managers who get it; if there’s punishment, it’s usually the staff who suffer.” (MED-ADM-08).

Inappropriate organizational structure

The participants emphasized that structural deficiencies—particularly excessive bureaucracy—hinder effective coordination and communication, thereby limiting progress toward international accreditation. An optimized organizational structure can strengthen transparency, accountability, and cross-departmental collaboration.

“During the accreditation process, time is one of the most critical factors, but unfortunately, excessive and often unnecessary paperwork slows down documentation and timely reporting.” (MED-FAC-01).

Inefficient documentation, including delays, data inaccuracies, and limited adoption of electronic systems, was identified as a significant barrier to effective accreditation processes.

“We struggle for timely reporting. Often, we don’t even realize some reports are missing until we’re already involved in processes like accreditation. Documentation of university activities should be done on time, without bias, and through electronic systems.” (HCM-ADM-01).

Insufficient infrastructure was highlighted as a key structural challenge, encompassing misaligned university facilities, outdated technologies, and suboptimal information systems.

“Sometimes a unit is created just for show—and after the accreditation process, it disappears.” (MED-DEP-01).

“To be completely honest, sometimes we resort to temporary, misleading, and cosmetic actions just to fill the gaps—like setting up broken equipment for appearance’s sake or submitting filtered data to the inspection team.” (HP-FAC-01).

Weak implementation

The participants identified multiple challenges during the implementation of international accreditation, with a key concern being the misalignment between internal evaluation standards and international criteria, which generates inefficiencies and increases the burden on universities during external assessments.

“If national accreditation standards matched the international ones, we wouldn’t have to go through so much trouble during external evaluations. We’d get better results with fewer resources spent.” (HCM-FAC-08).

Another participant highlighted the practical difficulties of applying some standards in the local context, particularly in the infrastructure of teaching hospitals: “Some standards just don’t work here because of local conditions. For example, the basic architecture of a university-affiliated hospital doesn’t match its intended function. There’s no lecture hall, no well-equipped lab, and no proper library in the hospital—especially when it comes to medical education.” (HCM-FAC-04).

Another concern was the insufficient financial support for universities seeking international accreditation, which may reduce motivation and hinder effective implementation of the process.

“A lot of what we do for international accreditation isn’t even considered in the university’s budget. That means staff don’t get fairly compensated, and the required resources aren’t provided on time.” (PH-FAC-02).

Another participant echoed this concern, emphasizing resource limitations: “Maintaining accreditation standards takes serious resources—time, money, people. From personal experience, I can say that most of the time, these resources just aren’t there.” (MED-ADM-03).

A final challenge was the shortage of qualified personnel, as newly established roles related to international accreditation often lacked staff with relevant expertise.

“We’ve created new roles and departments for accreditation, but many of them are filled by people without the right qualifications. At the very least, the International Affairs office should have experts in this field, but often times, people with unrelated degrees are handling critical tasks.” (MED-ADM-04).

Legal and regulatory constraints

Legal and policy-related constraints constituted another major category of university-level challenges. Key issues included the absence of unified policies to coordinate departmental efforts, leadership instability, limited evidence-based decision-making, and the impacts of sanctions.

“International accreditation has become politicized and driven by personal agendas. One university president may pursue accreditation just to secure their position, while others don’t consider it a priority. So, there’s no shared vision or common goal.” (MED-ADM-02).

A critical barrier highlighted by the participants was legal and political restrictions on collaboration with reputable international agencies.

“We face restrictions on establishing relationships with top accreditation bodies in countries like the U.S. or the U.K. owing to prevailing legal and political issues. For example, the CHEA is one of the most reputable organizations, but we can’t partner with them. Even if we get internal approval, the agency might still reject us because of sanctions.” (PHARM-DEP-02).

“Each country has its own legal and regulatory framework for higher education, which can make the accreditation process even more complicated.” (HCM-FAC-01).

Additionally, the participants highlighted the absence of an appropriate framework for continuous evaluation, including irregular internal assessments, lack of scheduled periodic reviews, and the absence of an independent, empowered unit for self-assessment.

“Higher education institutions must consistently meet standards to ensure their performance. That requires ongoing monitoring and alignment of curriculum and resources with accreditation requirements.” (HCM-FAC-05).

Challenges at the level of accrediting institutions

Inadequate standards and indicators

The participants highlighted that current standards often overlook the dual academic–clinical mission of medical universities, with uniform criteria failing to address their specific operational realities.

“Most accrediting agencies apply the same set of standards to all universities, but the structure and function of medical universities are fundamentally different from those of other institutions.” (HCM-FAC-02).

Another participant used a metaphor to emphasize this misalignment: “Using the same standards for every university is like trying to fit everyone into the same suit. It doesn’t work. The missions and functions of universities vary, so the standards should be more specialized—especially for medical universities.” (MED-FAC-03).

The participants further highlighted that while the accreditation process itself may be similar across institutions, the indicators must differ: “The process of international accreditation might be generally the same, but the indicators for medical universities should be different. These universities operate in both educational and clinical domains. They train a large number of students directly in the field, at health care centers—not just in classrooms. Medical services are more complex in terms of delivery, outcomes, and procedures compared with other academic disciplines.” (MED-ADM-05).

In addition to content-related concerns, the participants noted practical issues such as the excessive volume of data required, which can overcomplicate the evaluation process. Poorly calibrated weighting of different standards and dimensions was also cited as a problem, potentially leading to unfair scoring.

“The amount of data we have to prepare for accreditation is overwhelming. Most of it will probably be reviewed randomly, which could lead to inaccurate assessments of an institution.” (HP-FAC-03).

Evaluation process

The participants noted concerns in the evaluation phase, including potential evaluator bias (influenced by external perceptions) and a declining perceived value of accreditation certificates among leading universities, both of which may affect the credibility and impact of the process.

“Twelve years ago, when a university earned recognition from an international accrediting agency, it was considered a major achievement. But that sentiment has faded. For example, leading global universities used to proudly display accrediting agency logos and certificates on their websites. Now, they don’t—because they feel their status has surpassed that of the evaluators.” (HCM-FAC-07).

The participants reported procedural challenges, such as compressed timelines, inefficient workflows, outdated data collection methods, and limited availability of evaluators with domain-specific expertise.

“Sometimes we actually have all the documentation the evaluators require, but we just don’t have enough time to prepare and submit everything properly.” (NUR-DEP-01).

Another participant highlighted a more serious concern regarding ethical integrity and transparency in the evaluation process: “Unfortunately, some agencies are willing to overlook shortcomings—or skip the evaluation entirely—in exchange for financial incentives. In effect, they’re selling accreditation to certain universities.” (DEN-DEP-02).

The proposed solutions for improving the international accreditation process in IUMS were categorized at two levels: universities and accrediting agencies. At the university level, five major thematic areas emerged: training and education, infrastructure development, communication enhancement, policymaking and planning, and self-assessment. At the accrediting agency level, the most significant solutions were grouped into two main themes: standards and indicators improvement and process optimization (Table 3).

As shown in Table 3, capacity building and staff training, infrastructure development, and strengthening intersectoral collaboration emerged as high-salience strategies, each mentioned by at least half of the participants. Moderate-salience solutions encompassed monitoring and evaluation, policy reforms, digitalization, and resource allocation. While participants prioritized human resource development, collaboration, and contextual adaptation of standards, they also acknowledged the role of systemic and technological reforms in sustaining international accreditation.

Solutions at the level of universities

Training and education

Key training strategies included curriculum revision, capacity building, and integration of accreditation standards into educational programs with support from local and international expertise.

“You can’t expect everyone to be already familiar with accreditation standards and indicators. I think one of the first steps should be holding classes and workshops to get people up to speed.” (MED-ADM-01).

The participants also emphasized the value of expert-led instruction across all levels of the university and health service system: “Bringing in international specialists to train people—from senior decision-makers and administrators all the way to frontline staff—could really help.” (HCM-DEP-02).

The use of modern educational tools and technologies was also identified as a way to improve training delivery and ensure more effective learning.

“It’s important to use proper teaching methods. It shouldn’t just be one person talking and everyone else listening. I think it works better if it’s more hands-on—like showing examples of actual indicators and how to fill out the relevant forms.” (HCM-FAC-03).

Building strong infrastructure is essential for international accreditation in universities, as it enables alignment with global standards and improves overall quality.

“A university that wants to go for international accreditation and be globally recognized at least needs the basic infrastructure. In our country, even these basics aren’t always available. Just take access to research databases like Scopus or Web of Science—we have access for one month and then we lose it for two.” (HP-FAC-02).

Fostering a supportive organizational culture is essential for aligning institutional attitudes, values, and behaviors with international standards.

“The leadership needs to create an environment where continuous improvement becomes part of the culture—not something that only happens when we’re up for accreditation.” (HCM-FAC-06).

Technological infrastructure plays a vital role as well. Using modern data management systems can greatly enhance data collection, analysis, and timely reporting.

“Regular, short, and visual reports for managers help them stay updated and act sooner. We shouldn’t wait until the last minute to fix things.” (MED-ADM-07) Another participant added, “We have loads of data, but no one’s really using it because no one has time to go through it all. We need to move toward digital platforms; they make both data entry and analysis way easier.” (HI-FAC-01).

Finally, adequate funding was identified as a cornerstone of success. As one of the participants explained, “At the end of the day, everything needs financial support. Universities not only need to be equipped before the accreditation process starts but also need financial backup to address urgent gaps during the process.” (MED-ADM-06).

Enhancing communication

Effective cross-departmental communication enhances coordination, ensures consistent application of accreditation standards, and strengthens the quality of education and research.

“When we realize that accreditation success is a shared goal, then everyone needs to cooperate and help each other. I think cross-departmental collaboration really helps—and sharing insights and suggestions can keep people motivated.” (DEN-DEP-01) Another participant added, “Accreditation is really a team effort. Units need to exchange data and reports so that, in the end, we can pull everything together into a complete and consistent picture.” (NUR-FAC-01).

International engagement facilitates access to global expertise, partnerships, and academic resources, thereby strengthening universities’ capacity to achieve accreditation.

“To succeed in this, we need to see what successful universities have done. I think we should build more connections with other institutions and be present at international conferences and workshops to stay updated on global accreditation trends and standards.” (MED-DEP-02).

Policymaking and strategic planning

Strategic planning and clear policies are vital for aligning universities with global accreditation standards.

“I think university leaders and policymakers should consider accreditation in both their long-term and short-term plans. As much as possible, strategic goals should be aligned with the dimensions and aims of accreditation.” (MED-FAC-02).

The participants also emphasized the importance of data-driven planning. For instance, a participant stated, “University leaders and vice chancellors need to be aware of the current situation, and they should have plans to address the gaps—which means getting timely reports and proper data analysis is key.” (PH-FAC-01).

Effective policymaking can also help coordinate activities, allocate resources more efficiently, and enhance the quality of education and research.

“If a university wants to succeed in international accreditation and earn respected certifications, then its policymaking needs to regularly incorporate accreditation domains, dimensions, and standards. We should be able to see traces of international accreditation in the university’s mission, vision, goals, and outcomes.” (MED-ADM-08).

Establishing and enforcing supportive and transparent rules aligned with global standards can help build a sustainable structure for improving performance and facilitating international accreditation.

“Rules and regulations need to be clear and aligned with accreditation standards. If the policies are vague or get in the way, they’ll just hold people back and reduce effectiveness.” (MED-ADM-07).

Conducting self-assessments

Self-assessment is essential for identifying gaps, ensuring alignment with international standards, and guiding continuous improvement in accreditation processes.

“Before applying for accreditation, it’s better for a university to do a self-evaluation. We can use the standards that are already available or those published by recognized accrediting bodies.” (HCM-FAC-05).

Another participant emphasized the value of external perspectives in this process: “If self-assessments involve external evaluators who have no conflicts of interest with the university, the results can be much more helpful.” (HCM-FAC-01).

Solutions at the level of accrediting agencies

Enhancing standards and indicators

Tailoring accreditation standards to better reflect the nature of medical universities can significantly improve the accuracy and credibility of evaluations.

“Most accrediting agencies, as far as I know, use the same indicators for all universities, but universities of medical sciences are fundamentally different.” (MED-ADM-05) Another participant added, “When these universities are evaluated, the different areas should be weighted differently—especially clinical medical education, which often gets overlooked in most evaluations.” (MED-FAC-02).

The participants also highlighted the importance of educational workshops and greater clarity around the standards to help universities better meet expectations.

“I think it would be really helpful if a representative from the accrediting agency could come and run a workshop or class to clarify what they actually mean by some of these indicators.” (HCM-FAC-02) Another participant commented, “Some standards just aren’t really feasible for our universities. So it would make sense to coordinate with the agency to adjust the evaluation forms accordingly.” (HCM-FAC-08).

Improving the evaluation process

The evaluation process conducted by accrediting institutions can benefit from several enhancements. Key strategies identified include collaboration with local evaluators, adopting modern technologies for data collection and analysis, ensuring fairness and impartiality, and employing a holistic approach to evaluation.

“For a more accurate assessment, accrediting bodies should exchange knowledge and information with the universities they’re evaluating. Since many of the standards and indicators are generalized across countries and institutions, incorporating local context and feedback from internal evaluators can really help tailor the indicators and their weighting.” (MED-FAC-01).

Another important suggestion was simplifying the evaluation reports and providing a clear summary of the university’s status along with actionable recommendations.

“Usually, after an accreditation review, they just tell you whether the university passed or not. But regardless of the outcome, it would be much more helpful if we received a clear, simple summary that includes strengths, weaknesses, threats, and opportunities from the evaluators’ perspective—something we could actually use to improve our performance.” (HCM-FAC-07).

Discussion

This qualitative study explored the multifaceted challenges and solutions related to international accreditation in IUMS. The findings revealed six major categories of challenges—cultural barriers, inappropriate organizational structure, weaknesses in implementation, legal and regulatory constraints, misalignment of standards, and evaluator-related issues—along with seven categories of solutions, ranging from training and capacity building to process optimization. The participants’ perspectives varied based on their institutional roles; while senior managers emphasized policy and structural constraints, faculty members often focused on operational and cultural issues, and administrative staff highlighted workload, insufficient training, and lack of resources.

Challenges in achieving international accreditation

Cultural barriers

Organizational culture emerged as a critical factor influencing accreditation success. Resistance to change, often rooted in traditional academic norms, was consistently reported as a barrier [22, 23]. Some participants perceived accreditation as a one-off goal rather than a continuous improvement process, reflecting a lack of institutionalization of quality assurance. Similar cultural resistance has been reported in the U.S [24]. and Vietnam [22]. Practical implications include fostering a shared vision, linking accreditation to career incentives, and embedding quality culture in daily academic and administrative routines.

Organizational structure limitations

Bureaucratic, centralized, and politicized structures in IUMS hinder agility, coordination, and responsiveness [25]. Participants noted that excessive paperwork and fragmented communication slow down the process. Comparable findings in Vietnam [22] and contrasting experiences in the U.K [26]. —where flexible structures and cross-unit collaboration facilitated accreditation—highlight the need for decentralization and streamlined processes.

Weaknesses in implementation phase

Insufficient familiarity with international standards, limited preparation time, inadequate funding, and shortage of qualified personnel were recurrent issues. These mirror challenges documented in Pakistan [27] and the Middle East [2, 28]. Overemphasis on documentation, as noted in multiple contexts [27], risks diverting attention from substantive quality improvements. Addressing these issues requires targeted training, realistic timelines, and resource allocation aligned with accreditation demands.

Legal and regulatory constraints

Policy instability, inconsistent enforcement, and restrictive legal frameworks were identified as barriers. Several participants highlighted the impact of international sanctions, which limit engagement with reputable accrediting bodies, particularly those in the U.S. and U.K. Such constraints echo challenges in other sanctioned or politically isolated contexts. Beyond restricting evaluator visits and joint programs, sanctions also affect access to essential databases and training opportunities [29, 30]. Policy responses must therefore prioritize alternative partnerships, regional accreditation schemes, and digital capacity-building.

Misalignment of standards and indicators

The participants in our study stressed that generic standards do not reflect the dual academic and clinical missions of medical universities. Similar misalignments have been documented in Saudi Arabia [31] and the U.S [32]. Tailoring indicators, especially those related to clinical education, and assigning appropriate weights can improve the relevance and fairness of evaluations [32, 33].

Evaluator bias and integrity issues

Concerns included negative perceptions, overemphasis on deficiencies, and even corruption in certification processes. Such issues have been reported in Pakistan [27], Saudi Arabia [31], and Qatar [34]. To mitigate these risks, we need transparent evaluation protocols, joint local–international assessment teams, and oversight mechanisms.

Proposed solutions and policy implications

Training and capacity Building

Building institutional capacity is a prerequisite for successful accreditation, as human resources are the main drivers of quality improvement. Structured workshops for faculty, administrators, and staff can enhance accreditation readiness [27, 32]. Policies should institutionalize periodic training, including simulation-based accreditation exercises, and link participation to professional development credits.

Infrastructure and resource development

Improving physical facilities, digital data systems, and access to international databases is critical. Evidence from African contexts shows that infrastructure gaps impede compliance with standards [35, 36]. Policies should prioritize sustained funding mechanisms and infrastructure modernization as part of national higher education strategies.

Enhancing internal and international communication

Strengthening interdepartmental collaboration improves coordination, while international networking enables knowledge exchange [37, 38]. Under sanctions, universities should invest in South–South collaborations, virtual joint programs, and participation in regional accreditation networks. Comparative experiences from countries such as Cuba suggest that alternative alliances can partially offset geopolitical constraints [39, 40].

Strategic planning and policymaking

Effective strategic planning provides the foundation for aligning institutional priorities with accreditation requirements. Integrating accreditation standards into university strategic plans and aligning them with national higher education policies can ensure coherence [26, 37]. Policymakers should develop unified accreditation roadmaps and establish interuniversity taskforces to share resources and best practices [38, 41].

Continuous self-assessment and monitoring

Continuous self-assessment is essential for bridging the gap between periodic external evaluations and everyday quality improvement. Regular internal reviews, guided by international benchmarks, can identify gaps early [42]. Establishing independent quality assurance units with the authority to enforce corrective actions ensures that improvements are sustained between external evaluations.

Improving standards and evaluation processes

Establishing a strong foundation through context-specific standards and evidence-based practices is essential before implementing collaborative evaluation measures. Collaborating with accrediting bodies to adapt indicators to local contexts increases feasibility and acceptance [37, 43, 44]. Policies should mandate joint indicator development committees involving both domestic and international experts [19, 38].

This study contributes to the literature by moving beyond broad descriptions of accreditation challenges. Based on stakeholders’ perspectives, it offers a dual-level analysis that encompasses both university- and national/regulatory-level issues, as well as concerns related to accrediting bodies. By linking these challenges to context-specific and actionable solutions, and by clarifying the locus of responsibility, the findings provide practice-oriented guidance that can inform both policy development and institutional implementation.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. As the research was confined to IUMS, the findings may primarily reflect the context of these institutions, and generalizing the results to other universities or countries should be approached with caution. Owing to the qualitative nature of the study, the participants’ responses were largely based on their professional experiences, which may have led to the omission of key factors beyond their knowledge or expertise. Additionally, given the wide geographic distribution of the target population across the country, we conducted some interviews remotely (online), which may have influenced the level of interaction and depth of responses. To enhance the generalizability and applicability of findings, future research is recommended to adopt a multi-phase mixed-methods approach that combines qualitative and quantitative techniques. Such designs would allow for a more systematic identification and prioritization of challenges and solutions, facilitate comparison across different institutional and regional contexts, and enable longitudinal assessment of the impact of implemented strategies on international accreditation readiness.

Conclusions

This study explored the challenges of and solutions for achieving international accreditation in IUMS. The findings showed that universities face several barriers, including cultural issues, inappropriate organizational structures, weak implementation processes, legal and regulatory constraints, misaligned standards and indicators, and inefficient evaluation processes. To address these challenges, the participants proposed a range of solutions, such as strengthening training and education, improving infrastructure and documentation, enhancing communication and collaboration, and developing more context-sensitive standards and indicators. Implementation of the proposed strategies requires action at multiple levels: at the institutional level by university administrators, faculty leaders, and quality assurance units; at the national level by the MOHME through supportive policies, sustainable funding, and coordination among universities; and at the global level by building partnerships with international accreditation bodies, engaging in benchmarking, and aligning national standards with global frameworks. Clearly defining responsibilities at each level will facilitate effective execution, enhance readiness for international accreditation, and strengthen the global competitiveness of IUMS.
