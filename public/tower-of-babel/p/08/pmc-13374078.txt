HA-UCMSCs as an innovative therapy for treating multiple organ dysfunction syndrome
Authors: Jin-Xiu Hu, Ye Li, Meng Gao, Zi-An Li, Xiao-Juan Zhao, Meng-Die Chen, Li Ye, Qiang Li, Qian-Qian Ye, Qiang Wang, Jie He, Xinghua Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) is highly lethal and lacks effective therapies. Umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs) regulate immunity, suppress inflammation, and promote tissue repair, suggesting potential benefit for MODS. However, despite over 1400 MSC-related clinical studies worldwide, inconsistent outcomes reflect the lack of potent, standardized products. Our group established highly active UCMSCs (HA-UCMSCs) with enhanced proliferative and reparative capacity. This study evaluated their efficacy in MODS and explored underlying mechanisms.MethodsMODS was induced in tree shrews by combining hemorrhagic shock, simulated infection, and hind limb compression. HA-UCMSCs were isolated, expanded, and characterized for their nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, proliferation rate, multilineage differentiation capacity, and expression of mesenchymal and embryonic stem cell surface markers. After intravenous infusion, therapeutic efficacy was assessed via hematological and biochemical parameters, histopathological analysis, and serum proteomics using data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry.ResultsHA-UCMSCs exhibited enhanced biological features compared to conventional UCMSCs, including high proliferative capacity and ESC marker expression. In vivo, HA-UCMSCs homed to injured organs, alleviated systemic inflammation, facilitated tissue regeneration, and maintained hematopoietic homeostasis. Treatment significantly reduced mortality and long-term disability in MODS. DIA proteomic analysis identified 18 candidate serum proteins associated with disease progression and treatment efficacy.ConclusionThis study introduces a clinically relevant tree shrew model of MODS and demonstrates the multi-organ protective effects of HA-UCMSCs. These findings highlight HA-UCMSCs as a promising stem cell-based therapy for MODS and propose novel serum biomarkers for treatment monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05004-6.

Introduction

MODS is a syndrome characterized by the systemic inflammation initiated by proinflammatory cytokines following severe stress injuries such as infection, inflammation, trauma, shock, and major surgery, leading to simultaneous or sequential damage and dysfunction of two or more organs. The pathogenesis of MODS is complex and depends on the number of affected organs and systems, the severity and duration of the disease, as well as the type of damage and combination of affected organs. According to the German Society of Traumatology statistics, 20% of polytrauma civilian patients with septic complications develop multiple organ failure, especially in cases of war-related injuries, such as gunshot wounds, explosive injuries resulting from rocket-propelled grenades, fuel–air explosives, high-tech weapons, and improvised explosive devices (IEDs). These war-related injuries are more severe and complicated, leading to a poorer prognosis [1]. MODS initially represents a protective stress response to the body's response to trauma and infection. However, once the cytokine storm is triggered and not effectively controlled, sustained systemic inflammation can lead to damage to multiple tissues and organs. Ultimately, due to immune dysregulation, a cytokine storm develops, resulting in multi-organ damage [2]. If the body's inflammatory and immune balance can be modulated, and endogenous tissue repair mechanisms activated, MODS patients can survive and gradually recover. Nevertheless, effective treatment methods for MODS are still lacking, and the mortality rate for MODS can range from 20 to 100% [3–9]. Therefore, establishing a model that simulates the complexity of MODS and finding more effective treatment methods for MODS remain new challenges for medical researchers.

Furthermore, traditional clinical parameters are inadequate to directly assess and monitor the disease progression and predict the therapeutic efficacy of HA-UCMSCs in the treatment of MODS. Finding reliable biomarkers to reflect the effects of HA-UCMSCs in reducing inflammation, repairing multiple damaged organs, and promoting tissue regeneration is urgently needed. DIA is an effective tool for exploring complex biological processes, and proteins detected by DIA have the potential to become candidate markers for the diagnosis and evaluation of the HA-UCMSCs' therapeutic effects on MODS.

Various MODS models have been established both domestically and internationally [10–12]. However, there is a significant gap between the genetic background and biological characteristics of mice and humans. Therefore, research results obtained from mouse MODS models are difficult to replicate in clinical settings. Establishing an animal model for MODS that closely resembles humans is essential for clinical relevance. Shrews are experimental animals that are genetically and physiologically closest to humans, second only to primates [13]. Shrews have been domesticated and bred for research purposes in Yunnan, Guangxi, and other regions, and they have obtained permits for artificial breeding and use. Therefore, this study pioneered the establishment of a shrew MODS model that simulates the complexity of MODS.

Based on the mechanism of MODS development and the biological characteristics of MSCs, we hypothesize that MSCs may have better efficacy in preventing and treating MODS. MSCs are found in various tissues and possess self-renewal capabilities, multipotent differentiation potential, and bidirectional regulation of inflammation and immunity [14]. They are particularly effective in suppressing intense inflammatory responses, as demonstrated in clinical research on diseases such as COVID-19 and sepsis. MSCs are known for their low immunogenicity, ease of sourcing, secretion of cytokines, and their ability to promote tissue repair through paracrine effects [15–18]. In theory, MSCs could have multiple beneficial effects in the treatment of MODS, including inflammation suppression, prevention of secondary inflammatory damage, and promotion of tissue repair. However, the existing challenge is the variability in the quality, biological activity, and stability of MSCs prepared in vitro, leading to variable treatment outcomes in clinical practice [19]. Therefore, the development of more homogeneous and efficient MSCs remains a crucial topic for stem cell researchers. To address this, our laboratory has independently established a technical system for the preparation, identification, and quality control of HA-UCMSCs, resulting in standardized HA-UCMSCs products. Preliminary research has shown that these cells have smaller size, higher nuclear-to-cytoplasm ratio, and superior proliferative, homogenous, and multipotent differentiation abilities compared to MSCs obtained through traditional culture systems. While we have successfully isolated and stably expanded HA-UCMSCs from human umbilical cords, rat bone marrow, and macaque bone marrow, we have not yet obtained them from shrew umbilical cords.

Therefore, in this study, we successfully established a MODS tree shrew model using a combination of hemorrhage, simulated infection, and hind limb compression. With the HA-UCMSCs preparation and identification methods developed in our laboratory, we isolated tree shrew HA-UCMSCs for the first time. We conducted research to investigate the efficacy and mechanisms of HA-UCMSCs in treating MODS. Our goal is to discover the unique therapeutic effects of HA-UCMSCs, including the activation of extramedullary hematopoiesis (EMH) in the short term to maintain hematopoietic system homeostasis and long-term repair of the musculoskeletal system, among other multidimensional therapeutic effects. In addition to traditional evaluation indicators, we aimed to identify potential biomarkers through serum proteomics to assess the effectiveness of HA-UCMSCs in treating MODS.

Methods

Establishment and evaluation of a MODS model in tree shrews

Healthy adult female tree shrews (Tupaia belangeri chinensis), 8 months of age and weighing 120–140 g, were used for model establishment. A MODS model was induced by a combined procedure involving hemorrhage, comminuted fracture of the left femur, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) administration. Under anesthesia and immobilization, 30% of the estimated total blood volume (7% of body weight) was withdrawn from the left femoral vein, followed by intravenous injection of LPS at 50 mg/kg. A comminuted fracture of the left femur was then created using a compression clamp method, and fracture severity was firmed by X-ray examination (n = 3 tree shrews). Under standard housing conditions, survival and rectal temperature were dynamically monitored (n = 8 tree shrews). At 48 h post-induction, arterial blood was collected from five surviving animals for hematological and biochemical analyses. Major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, stomach and small intestine, were harvested, sectioned and stained for histo pathological examination. Model evaluation criteria included: consistent severity of femoral fractures across animals; mortality rate > 20% at 48 h; signs of lethargy; significant elevation of inflammatory cells and C-reactive protein in the blood; and evidence of injury involving at least two organs, as determined by biochemical parameters and histopathology. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Cell preparation and identification

In this research, HA-UCMSCs were isolated from tree shrew umbilical cords under strict sterile conditions. The umbilical cord tissues were carefully dissected, minced into small fragments and subjected to explant adherent culture. After primary attachment, the cells were expanded through serial passaging. To ensure high stemness and culture consistency, LN521 in DPBS (Ca2⁺/Mg2⁺) was used as the coating matrix, HA-UCMSCs were cultured in TeSR-E8 medium throughout the expansion process and UCMSCs in DMEM with 10% FBS. After 24 h, eGFP-labeled HA-UCMSCs were suspended at 2.5 × 106 cells/mL and stored at 4 °C. The 'OLYMPUS Provi CM20' tracked cell count and fusion for seven days. Flow cytometry assessed cell cycle and surface marker expression, while Western Blot analyzed Nanog, OCT-4 and SOX2 levels. A Luminex assay evaluated VEGF, IL-10 and HGF secretion after 24-h starvation culture. Both cell types were differentiated into adipogenic, osteogenic, chondrogenic lineages and stained with Oil Red O, Alizarin Red and Alcian Blue for examination. HA-UCMSCs and UCMSCs were placed on LN521 and gelatin-coated slides, exposed to three-germ layer induction medium, and immunostained for FOXA2, SOX17, Brachyury (T), CXCR4, Nestin, and PAX6 to quantitatively assess differentiation potential. Clonal growth of HeLa, MRC-5, HA-UCMSCs and UCMSCs in soft agar was monitored for three weeks, considering clusters over 16 cells as a clone.

HA-UCMSCs treatment and experimental grouping

In the treatment group, high active umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (HA-UCMSCs) were administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 1.5 × 10⁷ cells/kg. The sham group received an equivalent volume of normal saline at the corresponding time point, whereas the normal control group received no intervention. For the 30-day survival assessment, ten healthy tree shrews were randomly selected as the normal control group. Animals subjected to the MODS model were randomly assigned into the sham and treatment groups, with 20 animals in each group. For the 72-h survival evaluation, six animals were randomly selected from each of the normal control, sham and treatment groups (n = 6 tree shrews per group). For the 72-h therapeutic efficacy and mechanistic investigations, eight animals were randomly selected from each group (normal control, sham, and treatment; n = 8 tree shrews per group). In addition, samples were randomly obtained from all three groups for data-independent acquisition (DIA)-based proteomic sequencing.

Evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy of HA-UCMSCs

Post HA-UCMSCs treatment, a 30-day continuous monitoring was executed to evaluate the therapy's effect on mortality. At 72 h after HA-UCMSCs administration, CT scans assessed damaged organ conditions, rectal temperatures were taken with a mercury thermometer and blood samples from the abdominal aorta were collected for cell counts and biochemical analysis. Cervical dislocation was performed to euthanize the tree shrews, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, stomach and small intestine tissues were harvested for histopathological study, with additional samples reserved for mechanistic analysis. CT scans were repeated after 30 days to examine bone fracture site repair.

The mechanism research of HA-UCMSCs

Post 72-h HA-UCMSC treatment, tissue eGFP-positive cell tracking was conducted. "Fusion SoloS.EDGE" chemiluminescence imaged eGFP distribution in fresh tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, stomach, small intestine and muscles), followed by fluorescence microscopy on frozen sections. Apoptosis and proliferation were assessed via TUNEL and Ki67 staining, while CD133, MPO, CD71 and CD14 stainings evaluated hematopoietic factors. Serum DIA proteomics sequenced protein expression differences, analyzed by Mfuzz to categorize proteins into two regulatory pattern groups (Group1: down-then-up, Group2: up-then-down). GO and KEGG analyses enriched these groups. Pearson correlation linked proteins with P < 0.05 to blood counts and biochemical indicators, visualized in a heatmap. Proteins with an AUC of 1 were flagged as potential MODS biomarkers and HA-UCMSCs treatment efficacy indicators.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired t tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. Homogeneity of variances was assessed using the F test (two groups) or Brown–Forsythe/Bartlett’s tests (multiple groups). Normality was assumed due to the small sample size, when assumptions were not met, nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis) were applied. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, P < 0.05), while “ns” indicated no significant difference.

Results

Establishment of a MODS model through combined hemorrhage, simulated infection, and hind limb compression

After inducing a comminuted fracture in the left femur's mid-upper shaft through compressive force, immediate X-ray analysis confirmed consistent injury location and severity across all midshaft femur fractures (Fig. 1a). By 48 h post-injury, disheveled hair and inflammation at the injury site were noted (Fig. 1b), with a significant rectal temperature drop over 1℃ (Fig. 1c), correlating with a 37.5% mortality rate in the model group (Fig. 1d). Comparative blood analysis showed reduced RBC, HGB, and LYM% (P < 0.01), and increased WBC, NEU%, and NRBC counts (P < 0.01) in the model group, indicating systemic inflammation (Fig. 1e). Elevated acute-phase protein CRP, liver markers TBIL, ALT, ALP (P < 0.01), and AST (P < 0.05), along with cardiac enzymes CK and CK-MB (P < 0.01), suggested cardiac and hepatic dysfunction in the model group (Fig. 1f). The normal control group presented no abnormalities in major organs. In the model group, minor myocardial cell vacuolar degeneration, widespread moderate hepatocyte degeneration with cytoplasmic pallor (black arrows), increased EMH in liver sinusoids (purple arrows), scattered multinucleated giant cells (orange arrows), reduced lymphocytes in the spleen, mild alveolar wall thickening in the lungs, and mucosal edema with lymphocyte infiltration in the intestines were observed, with no kidney or stomach abnormalities (Fig. 1g). Preparation and characterization of HA-UCMSCs

HA-UCMSCs showed a higher nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio and smaller cell size compared to UCMSCs (Fig. 2a). Over 7 days, both cell types demonstrated logarithmic growth, with HA-UCMSCs maintaining higher cell numbers and fusion rates, indicating better proliferation and density (Fig. 2b). HA-UCMSCs had a larger G0/G1 cell fraction (73.2%) than UCMSCs (60.8%), suggesting higher proliferative potential (Fig. 2c). Both cell types were positive for CD90, CD73 and CD105, and negative for CD34 and CD45, with HA-UCMSCs also expressing ESC markers SSEA-3, SSEA-4 and TRA-1-60 (Fig. 2d). HA-UCMSCs expressed SOX2, OCT4, and Nanog, unlike UCMSCs which only expressed OCT4, indicating HA-UCMSCs as a more primitive cell type (Fig. 2e). HA-UCMSCs secreted higher levels of IL-10, VEGF and HGF than UCMSCs, enhancing their potential for anti-inflammatory effects and tissue repair (Fig. 2f). HA-UCMSCs showed greater adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation, evidenced by higher rates of lipid droplets, calcium nodules and chondrocytes (Fig. 2g). Both cell types could differentiate into three germ layers, with HA-UCMSCs exhibiting higher potential (Fig. 2h). HA-UMSCs treatment significantly improves MODS outcomes

After 72 h of infusion, the mortality rate in the Sham group was 50%, while in the Treated group, it was 16.7%. HA-UCMSCs treatment reduced the mortality rate within 72 h of MODS (Fig. 3a). The Sham group exhibited gradual deterioration in mental status, with hunched posture, disheveled and dull fur, sinus formation and pus discharge at the site of femoral fracture, or visible unabsorbed hematoma clots. In contrast, the Treated group showed gradual improvement in mental status, with almost normal behavior, alertness, glossy and smooth fur, and no signs of pus or blood discharge at the femoral fracture site. Thus, HA-UCMSCs treatment improved the mental status, reduced the inflammatory response, and salvaged local tissue damage in MODS (Fig. 3b). In the Sham group, there was no significant increase in rectal temperature in surviving animals, while in the Treated group, the rectal temperature significantly increased (Fig. 3c). Compared to the Sham group, the Treated group showed a decrease in WBC to near-normal control levels (P < 0.01) and an increase in nucleated NRBC (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3d). In addition, acute inflammatory markers such as CRP, liver function indicators like ALT and ALP (P < 0.01), as well as TBIL and AST (P < 0.05), decreased to levels close to the normal control group. Furthermore, cardiac function indicators including CK and CK-MB decreased to levels close to the normal control group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3e). HA-UCMSCs treatment reduced acute inflammatory response, improved liver function, and cardiac function. In the Sham group, myocardial cells exhibited vacuolar degeneration, the liver showed EMH, the spleen displayed unclear red-white marrow boundaries with areas of necrosis, lungs exhibited thickened interstitial tissue, and the small intestine showed edema. In contrast, the Treated group showed repair of tissue damage in the heart, small intestine, and liver tissues. The liver showed EMH, the spleen displayed clear red and white marrow boundaries with occasional cell necrosis, lung interstitial thickness was reduced compared to the Sham group, and no significant abnormalities were observed in the kidneys and stomach tissues (Fig. 3f). HA-UCMSCs treatment promoted the repair of damaged organ tissues. In both the Sham and Treated groups, there were fractures in the midshaft of the left femur. However, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of bone and muscle trauma in the short term (Fig. 3g). When the observation period was extended to 30 days, the mortality rate in the Sham group was 55%, while in the Treated group, it was 10%. HA-UCMSCs effectively reduced the long-term mortality rate in MODS (Fig. 3h). In the Sham group, the left femur showed discontinuous bone structure with multiple bone fragments visible, while in the Treated group, the midshaft of the left femur had irregular morphology and uneven density. HA-UCMSCs treatment promoted the repair of damaged bone and muscle tissue in the skeletal muscle system (Fig. 3i). HA-UCMSCs promote tissue repair by enhancing damaged cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and stimulating extramedullary hematopoiesis

The optimal Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) for eGFP transfection into HA-UCMSCs was determined to be 150, with a transfection efficiency of approximately 80%, and it did not affect cell morphology or proliferation capacity (Fig. 4a). In the Treated group, eGFP-positive cells were observed in fresh tissues of the heart, liver, lungs, kidneys, small intestines, and at the site of muscle injury, while no positive cells were found in the spleen and stomach (Fig. 4b). Frozen tissue sections of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys and stomach also showed the presence of eGFP-positive cells, while the small intestine did not (Fig. 4c). HA-UCMSCs were capable of homing to damaged tissue organs. In comparison to the Sham group, the Treated group exhibited reduced apoptosis in the heart, liver, spleen and small intestine (P < 0.05), as well as increased proliferation in these organs (P < 0.05). There was an increase in proliferation in the lungs, although it was not statistically significant (P ≥ 0.05) (Fig. 4d, e). HA-UCMSC therapy inhibited tissue apoptosis while promoting proliferation. In summary, HA-UCMSC therapy halted disease progression and promoted the repair of damaged heart, liver, spleen, lungs and small intestine tissues. Both the Treated and Sham groups exhibited activated EMH. The Treated group expressed endothelial progenitor cell surface marker CD133, while both groups expressed myeloid cell surface markers (granulocyte and monocyte surface marker MPO, red blood cell surface marker CD71). A total of 1405 proteins were detected in the normal control group, Sham group and Treated group, and they clustered separately into distinct groups. Notably, the Treated group exhibited a clustering pattern intermediate between the normal control and Sham groups, closer to the former. Significant differences in protein expression were observed between the Sham and normal control groups, while the differences between the Treated group and the normal control group were relatively minor (Fig. 5a). In comparison to the normal control group, the Sham group showed upregulation of 174 proteins and downregulation of 57 proteins, while the Treated group exhibited upregulation of 40 proteins and downregulation of 56 proteins when compared to the Sham group (Fig. 5b). Both the Sham group and Treated group displayed significant inter-group differences, good intra-group repeatability and distinct clustering patterns, similar to the Sham vs. normal control comparison (Fig. 5c). Over the transition from the normal control group to the Sham group and then to the Treated group, protein expression changes displayed eight distinct trends, with Cluster1, Cluster6 and Cluster8 generally showing a trend of downregulation followed by upregulation, whereas Cluster3 exhibited a trend of upregulation followed by downregulation (Fig. 5d). Functional enrichment analysis of Group1 proteins revealed significant enrichment in functions related to lipoprotein biosynthetic processes, gas transport, positive regulation of acute inflammatory responses, establishment of T cell polarity, and organ- or tissue-specific immune responses, along with the KEGG pathway enrichment predominantly associated with complement and coagulation cascades, riboflavin metabolism, intestinal immune network for IgA production, and longevity regulating pathways (Fig. 5e). For Group2 proteins, the functional enrichment analysis highlighted functions related to acute inflammatory responses, protein activation cascades, positive regulation of immune responses, and negative regulation of endothelial cell apoptotic processes, along with KEGG pathway enrichment primarily associated with Complement and coagulation cascades, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, Apoptosis—multiple species, and Platelet activation pathways (Fig. 5e). These functional enrichments suggest involvement in immune responses, metabolic processes, substance transport, and responses to environmental stress. Discussion

Constructing animal models that more closely mimic the human MODS development process is fundamental to exploring new and effective clinical treatment methods for MODS. According to literature, the dosage of LPS used to establish MODS animal models ranges from 1 to 30 mg/kg [20–23]. However, due to species differences, the LPS dose for tree shrews reaches 50 mg/kg to meet the criteria for MODS model establishment. The MODS tree shrew model we constructed exhibits typical characteristics such as high mortality, multiple organ dysfunctions, and widespread tissue damage, reflecting the complex development of MODS observed in clinical settings. This provides a reliable experimental animal model for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy and mechanism of HA-UCMSCs in treating MODS [24].

Since the first discovery of MSCs in 1970 , scientists have successively isolated MSCs from various tissues such as umbilical cords, bone marrow, fat, embryos and umbilical cord blood. Although MSCs cultured in existing systems exhibit notable effects in immunomodulation and tissue repair, they fall short in comparison to induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) in aspects like proliferative activity, multi-lineage differentiation potential, uniformity and in vivo survival time. The potential immunogenic rejection and animal disease infection risks associated with the use of animal-derived serum in MSCs culture systems are also shortcomings. On the other hand, iPSCs, which have the advantages of high proliferative activity and strong differentiation potential, pose a high risk of tumorigenicity. Our laboratory has cultivated HA-UCMSCs from human and rat umbilical cords, and rhesus monkey bone marrow, using a serum-free TeSE-E8 medium supplemented to prevent induced senescence and maintain cell growth, and LN521 to preserve pluripotency [25]. In this study, HA-UCMSCs were cultivated for the first time from tree shrew umbilical cords. Their stable expansion in culture demonstrates the reliability of this optimized culture system.

Comparing HA-UCMSCs with UCMSCs from the same batch, HA-UCMSCs demonstrate a higher nucleocytoplasmic ratio and superior cell proliferation capabilities than UCMSCs. HA-UCMSCs also show a higher proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase of the cell cycle, indicating greater differentiation potential [26, 27]. Both cell types highly express UCMSC surface markers [28] such as CD90, CD73, and CD105, and low levels of CD45 and CD34. However, HA-UCMSCs additionally ESC surface markers [29, 30] like SSEA-3, SSEA-4, and TRA-1-60. Although 31.3% SSEA-4 (+) expression was detected in UCMSCs, this may be induced by the sialylated glycosphingolipids in the fetal bovine serum added to the UCMSCs culture medium [31, 32]. The pluripotency transcription factors Nanog, OCT4, and SOX2 interact to maintain pluripotency and self-renewal in embryonic stem cells (ES) [33]. HA-UCMSCs express all three of these proteins, indicating their pluripotency and self-renewal capabilities. UCMSCs express only OCT4, suggesting their self-renewal ability. Both HA-UCMSCs and UCMSCs demonstrate the potential to differentiate into endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm, but HA-UCMSCs show significantly stronger differentiation. In adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic induction differentiation assays, HA-UCMSCs also exhibit stronger differentiation capabilities, suggesting greater plasticity compared to UCMSCs. This exogenously cultured HA-UCMSCs, in addition to its advantages over UCMSCs, shows similar expression to the endogenous natural stem cells found in connective tissues of almost all organs, known as Muse cells. Both cell types can express pluripotency transcription factors to maintain multipotency and self-renewal, can differentiate into three germ layers, have a high proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase of the cell cycle, and are non-tumorigenic. Moreover, HA-UCMSCs show stronger expression than Muse cells. Research has confirmed that endogenous Muse cells can be mobilized in the context of bodily tissue damage to exert paracrine, anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and anti-apoptotic effects [34, 35]. Therefore, these findings highlight that HA-UCMSCs possess enhanced stemness that is closer to endogenous reparative stem cell populations, such as Muse cells, which are activated during tissue injury to mediate anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and anti-apoptotic effects.

Although conventional UCMSCs have been widely reported to exert beneficial effects in MODS through paracrine, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-repairing functions, our study focused on HA-UCMSCs because they represent an enhanced next-generation stem cell product. In addition, the therapeutic mechanisms of UCMSCs in MODS have been extensively documented in previous studies, and repeating such experiments would provide limited new insights while increasing animal use. Therefore, only HA-UCMSCs were selected for in vivo efficacy and mechanistic evaluation, which aligns with both scientific rationale and ethical principles. UCMSCs have been proven to play a positive role in repairing various tissue damages in MODS, which may be related to the paracrine functions of UCMSCs. This beneficial effect could be due to their secretion of HGF, which promotes the repair and regeneration of damaged tissues, thereby alleviating tissue and organ damage. Moreover, in MODS cases with accompanying acute heart failure (AHF), the damage and dysfunction of various organs usually increase mortality risk [36]. UCMSCs-derived exosomes (UCMSCs-exo) repair cardiac injuries through immunomodulation, and UCMSCs-derived extracellular vesicles improve heart function and repair cardiac tissue damage through anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory properties [37]. In this study, HA-UCMSCs significantly reduced cardiac function indicators CK and CK-MB, repairing damaged cardiac tissue structures. Liver dysfunction following sepsis in MODS is an independent risk factor for predicting mortality and inducing death [38, 39]. Liver damage repair is critical in MODS treatment. Studies have confirmed that UCMSCs can colonize and differentiate into hepatocytes, promote liver regeneration, reduce liver inflammation, and secrete protective cytokines through paracrine effects [40]. MODS following septic liver injury often accompanies small intestinal inflammation due to elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, persistent bacteria, and metabolic product accumulation [39]. In this research, HA-UCMSCs effectively lowered liver function indicators AST, ALT, and ALP, improved liver tissue structure, and reversed the damage to normal in the small intestine. Immunohistochemical analysis of spleens from MODS patients who died in the ICU following sepsis with spleen damage revealed widespread depletion of CD4, CD8, and HLA-DR cells [41]. In this study, the HA-UCMSCs treatment group largely preserved the red and white pulp structures of the spleen, though lymphocyte depletion did not return to normal levels. The sham treatment group showed depleted lymphocytes and significant structural damage to the spleen, suggesting that HA-UCMSCs have a compensatory effect on lymphocyte depletion and reparative effects on spleen tissue structure. UCMSCs have been proven in multiple studies to effectively repair lung injuries [42–44]. Additionally, in this study, HA-UCMSCs significantly reduced WBC and CRP levels in the MODS model, indicating effective control of systemic inflammatory responses. This effect might be partly attributed to the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 secreted by HA-UCMSCs, playing a key role in mitigating systemic inflammation in the MODS model. The multi-organ protection observed suggests that HA-UCMSCs exert broader and more potent immunomodulatory and reparative effects, likely attributable to their enhanced stemness and paracrine activity, which may be particularly advantageous in the multi-hit pathophysiology of MODS.

UCMSCs possess properties of immunomodulation, anti-inflammation, anti-apoptosis, anti-microbial activity, and anti-oxidative stress. In this study, TUNEL staining revealed that HA-UCMSCs effectively reduced apoptosis in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and small intestine, while Ki67 staining showed significant proliferation in these tissues following HA-UCMSCs treatment. EMH was observed in both the model and treatment groups in the liver [45]. EMH, often triggered by hematopoietic disorders, insufficient bone marrow compensation, or infection under disease conditions, should be evaluated based on its actual effects. In our MODS model, blood loss, infection, and femoral fractures could induce EMH. 72 h post-treatment, EMH was observed in both groups, but their effects were different. The liver's EMH cells in the treatment group had more Ki67 positive cells than the model group, indicating that HA-UCMSCs stimulated the proliferation of EMH cells. Additionally, endothelial progenitor cells in the treatment group expressed the surface marker CD133, effectively supplementing depleted bone marrow hematopoietic function and enhancing liver EMH compensatory ability. The treatment group also showed higher CD71 expression in erythroid precursor cells compared to the model group, suggesting that HA-UCMSCs might compensate for acute blood loss-induced anemia by promoting the proliferation of these cells, consistent with increased nucleated red blood cells in peripheral blood. The treatment group's liver EMH had more myeloid cells expressing the enzyme MPO than the model group, and CD14 expression on monocyte lineage cells in the treatment group suggests that HA-UCMSCs treatment strengthened myeloid and monocyte lineage cells in liver EMH, resisting further development of infection and inflammatory response. These results indicate that in the short term, HA-UCMSCs treatment enhances liver EMH, maintaining hematopoietic homeostasis and regulating immune system balance to cope with MODS development. However, liver EMH is not a long-term solution for maintaining immune defense and hematopoietic stability. Previous studies have shown that UCMSCs' homing effect and migration ability allow them to be recruited to damaged and inflamed tissues, promoting the survival of injured cells [45, 46]. In this study, using two different methods to trace eGFP-labelled HA-UCMSCs, eGFP positive expression was observed in the heart, liver, lung, small intestine, muscle, kidney, and spleen at 72 h. Chemiluminescence imaging showed HA-UCMSCs were largely recruited to the site of comminuted fractures and damaged muscle tissues, confirming that HA-UCMSCs can target damaged tissues. Different organs tested positive in the two tracing methods, likely due to differences in sectioning locations and detection sensitivities. These results confirm that HA-UCMSCs can home and migrate to damaged and inflamed tissues in MODS. Extending the observation period to 30 days, CT imaging showed fracture healing and callus remodeling in the treatment group. In vitro experiments indicated that HA-UCMSCs can differentiate into osteoblasts and secrete VEGF, a key regulator of vascular development and angiogenesis. VEGF induces neovascularization, providing necessary nutrients and oxygen to the fracture site, directly promoting the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts, accelerating the coupling of osteogenesis and angiogenesis, and hastening the formation and maturation of the callus, which is crucial for bone repair [25, 47]. The repair of the musculoskeletal system, in rest oring normal hematopoietic homeostasis, reduces long-term EMH or has beneficial effects.

The DIA results revealed distinct protein expression patterns among the normal control, Sham, and treatment groups. Notably, after HA-UCMSCs treatment, the protein expression pattern in the treatment group tended to align with that of the normal control group. This suggests that specific proteins facilitated the transition from the pathological state of MODS to a normal physiological state. This not only indicates the efficacy of HA-UCMSCs treatment but also confirms the presence of proteins that can serve as biological markers for monitoring the progression of MODS and evaluating treatment responses. Subsequent trend analysis focused on two sets of proteins: those that were first downregulated and then upregulated, and those that were first upregulated and then downregulated, from the normal control group to the Sham group to the Treated group. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that proteins in these trends are consistent with the reported functions of UCMSCs treatment. They participate in various biological processes and pathways, including immunomodulation, inflammatory response, tissue injury repair and regeneration, and metabolic processes [48, 49], reflecting the complex, multifaceted biological effects of HA-UCMSCs treatment in MODS. These two groups of proteins were then correlated with peripheral blood cell counts and biochemical markers. The analysis identified 6 proteins in the first trend (first downregulated then upregulated) and 12 proteins in the second trend (first upregulated then downregulated) with AUC values of 1. This suggests that these 18 proteins are potential candidate biomarkers for monitoring the progression of MODS and evaluating the efficacy of HA-UCMSCs treatment. Integrating specific protein biomarkers with routine clinical tests could become a comprehensive assessment method. This approach may be crucial in clinical practice for controlling key time windows, adjusting treatment plans timely, improving patient survival rates, enhancing treatment outcomes, and potentially advancing precision medicine in the field of critical care medicine.

Conclusions

The isolation system of high-purity HA-UCMSCs is an effective means to address the homogeneity issue of stem cell products, providing a new option for the treatment of MODS. The screening of 18 candidate biological indicators for monitoring the occurrence, development, and efficacy of diseases provides a basis for the diagnosis and treatment of MODS.
