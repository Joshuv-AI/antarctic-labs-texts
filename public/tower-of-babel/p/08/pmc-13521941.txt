Whole transcriptome sequencing and ceRNA regulatory network in diabetic peripheral neuropathy
Authors: Qi Yan, Ming Zhang, Li-Juan Xu, Jing Ma, Jing Wang, Xian-Min Wang, Li Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is a serious complication of diabetes, its molecular causes are still unclear. The aim of this study was therefore to further investigate the pathogenesis of diabetes through whole transcriptome sequencing. Methods:We performed whole transcriptome sequencing on sciatic nerve samples from Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats. Based on the whole transcriptome sequencing data, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the DPN and the control groups were identified, and functions and pathways in which DEGs were enriched were analyzed. Thereafter, the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was built, and the top 20 hub genes were screened via STRING algorithm. In addition, enrichment analysis for the top 20 hub genes was subsequently performed. Finally, the competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network was created. Results:There were 597 DEGs between the DPN group and the control groups. The top 20 hub genes were Fos, Tnf, Jun, Cd3e, Cd79a, Cd27, Cd79b, Ptprc, Cd22, Cd40lg, Cd19, Cr2, Sell, Il6, Pdlim3, Actn2, Myom2, Tnni2, Ldb3, and Myh7. According to the enrichment analysis, DEGs and hub genes were enriched into the following: ‘B cell receptor signaling pathway’, ‘TNF signaling pathway’, ‘T cell receptor signaling pathway’ ‘Th17 cell differentiation’, ‘IL-17 signaling pathway’, ‘primary immunodeficiency’, and ‘toll-like receptor signaling pathway’. The ceRNA network finally contained 9 mRNAs, 23 miRNAs, and 23 lncRNAs. Conclusions:This study was able to determine that hub genes such as Fos and Jun may be involved in the progression of DPN by regulating signaling pathways like the B cell receptor signaling pathway, the TNF signaling pathway, and the IL-17 signaling pathway. The findings of this study could provide novel insights into the roles of DEGs in the pathogenesis of DPN.Keywords: Diabetic neuropathy, Whole-transcriptome sequencing, Hub genes, Bioinformatics analysisSubject terms: Medical research, Outcomes research

Introduction

Diabetes is a global chronic disease, and its related neurological complications have brought a great burden to patients and social medical resources. According to the research of the Global Diabetes Federation, about 10.5% of the adults in the world suffer from diabetes 1. Of these patients, more than half of the patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes suffer from diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) 2. According to many studies, the prevalence of DPN is positively correlated with the patients’ age and time of illness 3–5 and it is the main cause of lower limb ulceration and amputation 3. According to research, neurological dysfunction and cell death in DPN are caused by metabolic imbalance related to diabetes. Hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia and/or insulin resistance promote the activation of protein kinase C(PKC), polyols, advanced glycation end products (AGEs), poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) and hexosamine pathways. It also leads to the loss of insulin signal transduction, which eventually leads to harmful effects on mitochondrial function and gene expression, as well as to inflammation and oxidative stress 6. However, after several years of research into the clinical treatment of DPN, except for optimizing patients’ lifestyle and blood sugar control, there is no new treatment for DPN, and all clinical experiments expected to improve the progress of diabetic neuropathy have failed 7. Therefore, a breakthrough in DPN therapy may require further exploration of molecular mechanisms. At present, a large number of molecular pathways, including NF-κB signaling transduction, TNF-α signaling transduction, and interleukins, are being studied 8. The correlation between DPN and pathogenesis has been explored, but it has not been fully clarified. Therefore, exploring the molecular mechanism of diabetic neuropathy for the prevention and treatment of this disease is of great significance.

RNA-Seq-based whole transcriptome sequencing has been widely employed in recent years for diagnosing various monogenic hereditary diseases 9, and it is recognized for its ability to identify rare pathogenic sequences 10,11. In hereditary dermatosis, it is considered to be a convenient and economical first-class diagnosis method 9. For breast cancer 12, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma 13, head and neck cancer 14 and other cancers it provides help in treating the diseases. Therefore, whole transcriptome sequencing still has great potential in exploring known and unknown disease genes. However, in the literature there is no report of this technology being used in the exploration of the molecular mechanism of DPN. This study employed a DPN rat model and conducted whole transcriptome sequencing to gain deeper insights into the key genes and pathways playing crucial roles in diabetic peripheral neuropathy. This contributes to a reference basis for the early diagnosis of DPN patients and the development of novel treatment methods.

Materials and methods

This study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments).

Animal model construction and sample collection

In this study, 8-week-old clean healthy male SD rats weighing 200 ~ 250 g were selected. They were provided by the Animal Experimental Center of Xinjiang Medical University. All rats were raised under the condition of clean grade (SPF grade): 5 per cage, constant temperature (20 ± 2℃) feeding room, natural light, free drinking water, adaptive feeding under the condition of free feeding for 1 week. The rat feed was a standard pellet feed provided by Xinjiang Experimental Animal Research Center. Rats in the blank group were fed with common feed; the model rats were fed with high-sugar and high-fat feed for 4 weeks (including 70% conventional feed, 20% sucrose and 10% lard). Insulin resistance was induced: after a low dose of streptozotocin (STZ) of 50 mg/kg (STZ was dissolved in 0.1 mol/l citric acid-sodium citrate buffer with pH 4.5 and made into a 1% solution, which was filtered and sterilized by a 0.22 μm microporous membrane). The rats were fasted for 14 h and injected intraperitoneally. The blank group was only given citric acid-sodium citrate buffer for parallel experiments. After 72 h, blood was collected from the tail vein to measure random blood glucose levels, and where the random blood glucose value of the tail vein was determined to be ≥ 16.7 mmol/L, these rats were confirmed as DM rats and included in the experiment. The animals in the blank group (n = 3) and the successful model group (n = 3) were raised in cages respectively, and they were free to eat and drink. After the diabetic rats were successfully established, they were kept in a constant temperature (20 ± 2℃) feeding room with natural light and free drinking water. They continued to be fed with high sugar and high fat feed for 6–8 weeks under the condition of free feeding, The sciatic nerve of one rat in each group was stained with HE, and if the pathological structure had changed accordingly, it could be judged as a successful DPN rat model. After the experiment, the left sciatic nerve of 3 rats in the blank group and the model group were taken for sequencing. The scheme was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University (Ethics approval number: IACUC-20210805–5). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All operations were performed under anesthesia, the surgery was performed under anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (0.3 ml/100 g), and every effort was made to minimize the discomfort and stress of the animals.

Whole transcriptome sequencing and identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the disease and the control groups

Library construction was carried out using the removal of ribosomal RNA, and the insert size (250 bp < insert size < 350 bp) of the libraries was measured by an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. To confirm the integrity of the sample library, the effective concentration of the library (effective concentration of the library > 2 nM) was then precisely quantified using qPCR. Illumina PE150 was employed to sequence. Afterwards, we filtered the raw data to assure the accuracy and dependability of the data. The clean reads after quality control were compared to the reference genome via Hisat2. The readings were converted into transcripts based on the findings of the comparison to the genome, and they were measured by Stringtie software. Finally, the DEGs between the disease and control groups were identified via edgeR software with a P < 0.05. GOseq and KOBAS (Version 2.0) were utilized to carry out the biological functional enrichment analysis of DEGs, based on Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (P < 0.05)15–17.

Screening of hub genes

Firstly, the protein–protein interaction network (PPI) for DEGs was created via STRING database (http://string.embl.de/). Subsequently, the STRING algorithm was employed to identify the top 20 hub genes in the PPI network, and the enrichment analysis for hub genes was carried out on the basis of GO and KEGG.

Construction of competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network

To further investigate the mechanisms underlying the DEGs in DPN, we established the ceRNA network. The miRNAs regulating the DEGs were predicted using the Miranda database (http://www.microrna.org/microrna/home.do) and RNAhybrid database (http://bibiserv.techfak.uni-bielefeld.de). Then, the miRNAs obtained through the two databases were intersected to gain common miRNAs that were used to create the ceRNA network. Subsequently, we similarly predicted lncRNAs with regulatory interactions with common miRNAs through these two databases, and the lncRNAs gained from the two databases were intersected to gain intersecting lncRNAs to create the ceRNA network. Finally, the Cytoscape software was utilized to visualize the ceRNA network.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded sciatic nerve sections from rats in the control and DPN groups were subjected to immunofluorescence staining using anti-Cd3e antibody (diluted 1:200, Proteintech, Cat. No. 17617–1-AP) and anti-Cd19 antibody (diluted 1:200, Proteintech, Cat. No. 27949–1-AP), respectively. Images were visualized and captured using a fluorescence microscope (SOPTOP, Model CX40) with the following excitation and emission wavelengths: DAPI (excitation: 330–380 nm, emission: 420 nm, blue fluorescence); Cd3e (excitation: 465–495 nm, emission: 515–555 nm, green fluorescence); and Cd19 (excitation: 510–560 nm, emission: 590 nm, red fluorescence).

Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, with Mean Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) as the primary quantitative metric. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, with unpaired two-tailed t-tests used to compare groups. Statistical significance was denoted as *p < 0.05.

Acquisition and enrichment analysis of hub genes

The top 20 hub genes were obtained, namely Fos, Tnf, Jun, Cd3e, Cd79a, Cd27, Cd79b, Ptprc, Cd22, Cd40lg, Cd19, Cr2, Sell, Il6, Pdlim3, Actn2, Myom2, Tnni2, Ldb3, and Myh7 (Fig. 3A). Of the GO results, the top 20 hub genes were enriched into 121 GO items that consisted of 100 GO BP items, 10 GO CC items, and 11 GO MF items (Table S1). The top 5 items of each type are displayed in Fig. 3B, and included ‘sarcomere organization’, ‘external side of plasma membrane’, and ‘cytoskeletal protein binding’. Of the KEGG results, 44 KEGG pathways were enriched (Table S2). The top 15 KEGG pathways are shown, and they belong to environmental information processing (‘TNF signaling pathway’), organismal systems (such as ‘T cell receptor signaling pathway’, ‘Th17 cell differentiation’, ‘IL-17 signaling pathway’), and human diseases (such as ‘Chagas disease’, ‘Epstein-Barr virus infection’, ‘primary immunodeficiency’) (Fig. 3C).

Thereafter, we contrasted the KEGG pathways enriched by DEGs with the KEGG pathways enriched by the top 20 hub genes, and found that ‘B cell receptor signaling pathway’, ‘Th17 cell differentiation’, ‘T cell receptor signaling pathway’, ‘TNF signaling pathway’, ‘IL-17 signaling pathway’, ‘primary immunodeficiency’, and ‘toll-like receptor signaling pathway’ were all enriched. Moreover, for the top 20 hub genes, except for ‘primary immunodeficiency’, Jun and Fos were involved in the remaining six pathways. According to our sequencing results, compared with the control group, Jun and Fos in the DPN group were significantly down-regulated.

The ceRNA network establishment

The ceRNA network contained 9 mRNAs, 23 miRNAs and 23 lncRNAs (Fig. 4). For instance, the regulatory relationships included LOC500007-rno-miR-125a-3p-Ldb3, LOC681006-rno-miR-214-3p-Cd3e, LOC100362783-rno-miR-702-5p-Cd19, LOC498276-rno-miR-540-3p-Cd22, LOC100909840-rno-miR-667-3p-Jun, LOC690045-rno-miR-383-3p-Cd27, LOC100911837-rno-miR-370-3p-Cd40lg, LOC100362783-rno-miR-23a-5p-Tnf, and LOC100911042-rno-miR-296-3p-Myh7. Moreover, rno-miR-30c-2-3p and rno-miR-34a-5p could be regulated by Ldb3 and Cd3e. Ldb3 and Cd19 could be regulated by rno-miR-330-5p. Additionally, there were regulatory relationships between rno-miR-324-3p and Jun or Cd27. Rno-miR-540-3p and rno-miR-702-5p might regulate Cd19 and Cd22. Cd27 and Cd19 could be regulated by rno-miR-188-3p.

Validation of DEGs

In this study, we used qRT-PCR to confirm our research results. We contrasted the top 20 KEGG pathways enriched by DEGs with the top 15 KEGG pathways enriched by the top 20 hub genes, and found that ‘B cell receptor signaling pathway’, ‘Th17 cell differentiation’, ‘T cell receptor signaling pathway’, ‘TNF signaling pathway’, ‘IL-17 signaling pathway’, ‘primary immunodeficiency’, and ‘toll-like receptor signaling pathway’ were all enriched. Moreover, of the top 20 hub genes, except for ‘primary immunodeficiency’, Jun and Fos were involved in the remaining six pathways. Based on this observation, we selected Fos and Jun as key candidate genes. In the ceRNA network, we selected LOC681006-rno-miR-214-3p-Cd3e in the same regulatory axis for verification according to the results of previous studies. The complete results are shown in Fig. 5A.

Validation of key genes by qRT-PCR. (A) Expression levels of Fos, Jun, and Cd3e (selected from hub genes and ceRNA network) in the control and the DPN groups. (B) Expression levels of Junb and Mmp3 (core genes in TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways) in the two groups. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, with statistical significance indicated as **p < 0.01 and ****p < 0.0001 (n = 3 per group), consistent with RNA-seq results.

To further validate key regulatory molecules in the TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways, we selected core pathway genes Junb and Mmp3 for qPCR verification (primer sequences are provided in Table S3). The results demonstrated (Fig. 5B) that compared with the control group, Mmp3 expression was significantly upregulated (p < 0.0001), while Junb expression was markedly downregulated (p < 0.01) in the DPN group. These findings are consistent with our sequencing data.

Presence of immune cell infiltration in DPN rats

Several of the enriched hub genes are associated with immune function. Paraffin-embedded sciatic nerve sections from rats in the control and DPN groups were subjected to immunofluorescence staining. Results demonstrated that the mean fluorescence intensities of both Cd3e and Cd19 in the sciatic nerves of the DPN group were significantly elevated compared to the control group (Fig. 6A–B). This finding confirms the presence of immune cell infiltration in the DPN model.

Discussion

DPN is the most common complication of diabetes. The serious final results of this disease, such as amputation and disabling neuropathic pain, greatly affect patients’ lives and increase the social medical burden 3. At present, the drugs on the market used to prevent and treat DPN have general curative effects and serious side effects 18. We therefore need to explore new treatment strategies. We obtained 20 hub genes by creating the PPI network of DEGs, among which it is worth noting that Jun and Fos participated in six important pathways. Fos and Jun are well-known to researchers in the field of early tumor research. They are a group of oncogene transcription factors whose expression is induced by various extracellular stimuli related to mitosis, differentiation and neuronal depolarization 19. Moreover, studies have shown that the down-regulation of c-Fos and c-Jun leads to the decrease of the expression of important tumor suppressor gene p53, thus promoting the progress of tumors 20,21. As a basic leucine zipper transcription factor of the AP-1 family, c-Jun has been proved to be an important regulator of Schwann cell myelination in DPN research 22. In the subsequent research, it was further confirmed that the intersection between Hsp70 and c-Jun signals was important in preventing demyelination 23. c-Fos is a proto-oncogene and was first discovered in 1987. Its stimulation of primary sensory neurons leads to the expression of c-Fos protein-like immunoreactivity in the nucleus 24. Later, this was the basis of much research on c-Fos in the sensory symptoms of diabetes. Megan Johnson found that the number of Fos-positive cells in the dorsal horn of diabetic animals began to decrease obviously after 3 weeks of illness, which led to the loss of peripheral axons and sensory loss, which may be the reason for the early sensory loss symptoms of DPN patients 25. Moreover, the stimulation of various external factors also increased the number of Fos immunoreactive (Fos-IR) neurons. It can therefore be inferred that higher baseline neuronal activity may be the basis of DPN sensory abnormality 26. While these past studies suggest potential regulatory roles of Fos and Jun in DPN, their specific functions in DPN pathogenesis require further validation. Importantly, the gene expression changes observed in this study may reflect secondary compensatory mechanisms or adaptive responses to neural damage during disease progression, rather than direct causative factors driving DPN initiation. Future functional gain/loss experiments (such as gene knockout or over expression models) are needed to establish causal relationships.

When comparing 597 DEGs with 20 hub genes, we found that B cell receptor signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, primary immunodeficiency pathway and toll-like receptor signaling pathway were all enriched. A large number of studies have shown that the aggravation of inflammation is the main cause of diabetic complications 27. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) belong to another major family of pattern recognition receptors and play an important role in the T cell receptor signaling pathway of the immune system 28. After binding to autoantigens (soluble or binding to antigen presenting cells) derived from apoptotic particles or other disease-specific targets, autoreactive B-cell receptors (BCR) transport nuclear antigens to toll-like receptors (TLR7) and (TLR9), leading to initial B-cell activation, which responds to integrated BCR-dependent and TLR-dependent signal transduction 29. One study also found that the expression levels of TLR4 and TLR7 in the TLR family were much higher in patients with DPN 30. Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) acts through two receptors, TNFR1 and TNFR2, and participates in various biological activities including inflammation 31. It stimulates monocytes and endothelial cells to secrete inflammatory factors, such as IL-6 and IL-7, which are toxic to neurons and glial cells, leading to demyelination 32. Hyperglycemia, insulin resistance and dyslipidemia are the main factors in the pathogenesis of DPN 33. It has been proved that TNF-α is related to these factors 34,35. A meta-analysis also showed that the serum TNF-α level of DPN patients was significantly higher than that of the DM control group, and the serum TNF-α level of painful DPN was more significant than that of painless DPN 36. The Th17 Cell Differentiation Pathway has been well-studied due to the phenomenon of T cells, particularly effector T helper cells, secreting IL-17 and other inflammatory cytokines 37. It plays a major role in autoimmune diseases, with the retinoic acid-related orphan receptor (ROR) γt identified as a key transcription factor in the Th17 cell differentiation process 38. While previous research considered IL-17 as a pro-inflammatory factor, increasing evidence also demonstrates its importance in maintaining homeostasis and tissue repair 39,40. In the nervous system, IL-17 is involved in corneal nerve regeneration following injury 41. Additionally, in the submucous plexus of the intestines (SMG), IL-17 participates in the activation of the NF-κB downstream pathway and plays a crucial role in neuronal growth 42. One study has even identified the IL-17 signaling pathway as a major therapeutic target downstream of Th17 cells43. The IL-17 family comprises six cytokines: IL-17A, B, C, D, E, and F. Research has found a decrease in IL-17A in the nerves of diabetic rats 44. Interestingly, IL-17A is primarily produced by Th17 cells 45, and enhances nerve damage by increasing the effects of TNF-α 46.

In the ceRNA regulatory network, studies suggest that four miRNAs (miR-125a-3p, miR-30c-2-3p, miR-214-3p, and miR-702-5p) may serve as important predictive factors for identifying and assessing early diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN). For example, rno-miR-125a-3p was upregulated in pRLA (polyethylene glycolated rat leptin antagonist) rats fed a high-fat diet (HFD) 47. MiR-30c-2-3p was upregulated in the brown adipose tissue (BAT) of C56BL/6 J mice after HFD feeding 48. Margaritis Avgeris also found that miR-214-3p was downregulated in high-BMI, high-blood-lipid diabetic patients 49. Furthermore, miR-214-3p was demonstrated to improve diabetes-induced nerve damage in STZ-induced rats by regulating Nav1.3 and TLR4 50. It is worth noting that HFD, high blood lipids, and high BMI are all major risk factors for DPN development 51. MiR-702-5p improves diabetic encephalopathy (DE) by inhibiting 12/15-LOX 52. Additionally, LOC500007, LOC681006, LOC100362783, and Ldb3, Cd3e, Cd19, as ceRNA regulators and regulated genes of these miRNAs, have not been explored in the field of DPN before. According to our sequencing results, LOC500007, LOC681006, LOC100362783, miR-125a-3p, Cd3e, and Cd19 were upregulated in the DPN group compared to the control group, while miR-30c-2-3p, miR-214-3p, miR-702-5p, and Ldb3 were downregulated. These results were further confirmed by PCR.

In summary, through the analysis of whole transcriptome data, we were able to identify differentially expressed mRNAs between DPN and healthy rats, discover seven pathways related to DPN, and to select 20 hub genes through PPI. We have also established, for the first time, an lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA ceRNA regulatory network related to DPN and have explored new mechanisms at the transcriptional level. Based on PCR validation, we speculate that LOC500007-rno-miR-125a-3p-Ldb3, LOC681006-rno-miR-214-3p-Cd3e, and LOC100362783-rno-miR-702-5p-Cd19 are potential ceRNA regulatory pathways involved in DPN regulation. Fos and Jun may serve as potential biomarkers for DPN. This provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms of diabetic neuropathy and offers new avenues for the treatment of diabetic neuropathy patients.

However, this study still has its limitations. For instance, the sample size of rat sciatic nerve used for sequencing was relatively small. Additionally, we did not find a sufficient number of sequencing results related to DPN rats in public databases for a comprehensive joint analysis with our sequencing results. Moreover, our investigation focused solely on observing the transcriptional level changes of DPN-associated lncRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs in the results. We did not delve deeper into their exploration. What’s more, expression changes might represent consequences rather than causes of the disease, and their clinical relevance requires further validation. Based on the findings of this study, we plan to conduct further in vivo or in vitro experiments on animal models to validate these crucial regulatory genes and pathways. And future studies will involve expanding the sample size to enable deeper mechanistic exploration. These may indeed become a primary direction for our future research endeavors.
