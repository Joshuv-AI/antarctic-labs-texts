Assessment of a conductive component of heat transfer in selected insulation systems: an experimental study
Authors: Aleksandra Skowronek, Michał Pluta, Tomasz Darocha, Tomasz Sanak, Paweł Podsiadło
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-traumatic hypothermia increases morbidity and mortality. Preventing heat loss plays an important role in improving outcomes of trauma patients. The purpose of the study was to compare selected thermal insulation systems regarding heat loss by conduction. An experimental study was conducted. We simulated the scenario mimicking a patient lying on a cold surface. A copper manikin of a human torso was placed on a cold water mattress. The manikin temperature was recorded every 1 min for 2 h. Tested insulation systems (Ultrathin reflective foil, LESS, HPMK, Blizzard, Mediwrap, Ferno) were placed consecutively between the manikin and the mattress. Values of heat balance associated with each insulating system as well as with a reference model without insulation were compared. A total of 21 measurements were included in analysis. Heat balance differed significantly between the analyzed systems (p < 0.05). The highest heat loss was observed for the ultrathin reflective foil (Q= -1994.16 kJ; SD ± 97.8), and the lowest for the Ferno system (Q= -527.48 kJ; SD ± 56.7). The value of heat loss correlated with the thickness of the insulation system (r = 0.72) without statistical significance. A single layer of ultrathin metalized foil does not reduce significantly the heat loss by conduction.

Introduction

Post-traumatic hypothermia (PTH) is defined as a drop in a patient’s core temperature below 36 Celsius degree (ACS 2018). PTH increases mortality, blood loss and transfusion requirements, length of hospital stay, and causes a higher incidence of infection compared to patients in normothermia (Shafi et al. CS 2005; Arthurs et al. 2006; Klauke et al. 2016; Rösli et al. 2020). In addition, patient discomfort caused by cold provokes unnecessary movements which are undesirable in certain types of injuries, increases anxiety, and intensifies pain sensation (Lundgren et al. 2011). Therefore, preventing heat loss in trauma victims plays an important role in improving outcomes of trauma patients (Jurkovich et al. 1987; Wang et al. 2005; Shafi et al. 2005).

The study by Lundgren et al. highlighted the importance of a conductive heat loss in patients lying on spineboards (Lundgren et al. 2004). Scoop stretchers and spineboards commonly used in prehospital settings usually are not equipped with an insulation layer. Therefore, insulation blankets may play a role of thermal insulation from the cold surface of evacuation devices. To the best of our knowledge, however, no study compared insulation systems regarding heat loss by conduction toward the cold ground.

The aim of our study was to compare commonly used insulating systems with regard to the conductive component of heat flow.

Methods

Study design

An experimental study was conducted. The conditions of a victim lying on a cold surface were simulated. A heat transfer system was developed: a thermal manikin was placed on a cold water mattress, and the tested insulation systems were placed between them.

Equipment

A torso-shaped manikin was used, described in detail and evaluated in a study conducted by Podsiadło et al. (Podsiadło et al. 2020). This is a cooper container filled with 43.3 L water. Its total mass is 54.77 kg and the surface in contact with the ground is 0.2443 m2. The manikin has a surface emissivity of 0.99. The container is equipped with a water heater and a stirrer to ensure that the temperature is evenly distributed over the whole surface of the manikin. The temperature was measured using a thermometer / temperature-logger (Termio 2, Termoprodukt, Bielawa, Poland) which probe is placed at the manikin’s geometric centre. The circulating water mattress (Hico water pad - Intensive, Hirtz & Co. Hospitalwerk, Cologne, Germany) was maintained in stable temperature conditions with the heat exchanger Stockert Heater-Cooler System 3T (Sorin Group Deutschland GmbH). The manikin’s walls, apart from the lower one, were protected against excessive heat loss with a 5 cm thick styrofoam cover. A styrofoam plate was also placed between the mattress and the floor (Fig. 1). Since this thermal barrier was identical in all measurements, we assumed that the only way of manikin heat loss is this towards the mattress, and its value depends only of the type of tested system. Moreover, since each tested system was compressed between the manikin and the mattress with a pressure of 224.2 kg/m2, without moisture or air space, it was assumed that conduction was the dominant mechanism of heat flow. Thickness of the insulation materials was measured with a caliper under a pressure of 224.2 kg/m2 (i.e. generated by the manikin) with an accuracy of 0.1 mm.

Study protocol

At the beginning of every measurement, the manikin was heated to temperature of 37 ˚C, then placed on a circulating - water mattress which was kept at a constant temperature of 10 ˚C during the whole measurement process. The manikin’s temperature was recorded every 1 min for 2 h. Initial measurements were taken without insulation between the manikin and the mattress as a reference model. Taking into account the temperature change graphs, measurements affected by technical factors were rejected. They were not included in the calculations. The measurements were repeated in order to obtain three technically correct results. The tests were carried out each time under the same conditions, at an ambient temperature of 20˚C in an air-conditioned room.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistica v.13.3 software (TIBCO Software Inc.). The heat loss of the manikin (Q), expressed in kJ, was calculated for every measurement separately, using the following equation: Q = m × c × ΔT, where m denotes the mass of manikin components, c denotes their specific heat, and ΔT denotes temperature change of the manikin. A drop of 1˚C in the manikin temperature corresponds to a heat loss of 185.7 kJ. Data distribution was assessed with Shapiro-Wilk test. Insulating systems and a reference model were compared by analysing their Q values with ANOVA test. We also performed a post-hoc analysis with Tukey test in order to assess the differences between particular systems. The correlation between the thickness of the thermal insulation systems and heat loss was calculated using linear correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 32 measurements were performed. Eleven records were rejected due to technical artifacts. In the final analysis, 21 measurements were included.

Discussion

In this experimental study, we showed that the conductive heat transfer through URF does not differ from no-insulation scenario. All remaining tested systems reduced heat loss significantly better.

Several studies compared the effectiveness of insulation systems at low temperatures. However, in these studies, additional insulating layers were placed under the patients, so heat loss by conduction towards the cold ground was limited (Grant et al. 2002; Henriksson et al. 2009, 2015). Our results may have important practical implications because URFs are widely available in the prehospital settings and their misuse based on one’s own beliefs and the myth of a “survival blanket” is likely (Ennemoser et al. 1988; Karlsen et al. 2013; Henriksson et al. 2017; Podsiadło et al. 2017).

From the thermodynamic point of view, the main feature of reflective surfaces is reducing heat flow by reflecting infrared radiation. However, in prehospital settings, other ways of patient heat loss such as evaporation, conduction and convection seem to play a more important role (Kosiński et al. 2022). Focusing on the heat flow between patient’s body and the cold ground, conduction seems to be the most important. Airspaces encapsulated in insulation fabrics, their shape and thickness play a crucial role in heat flow reduction (Mert et al. 2017). This is consistent with our findings on the relationship between thickness of insulating systems and their thermal resistance, similarly to the results of the study by Henriksson et al. (Henriksson et al. 2009). However, in our experimental scenario, the pressure exerted by the manikin was even on the entire surface. An increased local compression, e.g. under the scapula or the sacral bone of an actual patient, may lead to reduction or elimination of these airspaces. This phenomenon is less likely in fabrics containing closed airspaces. e.g. bubble wrap (LESS), styrofoam (vacuum mattress) or foam-based camping sleeping mats, while it seems more probable in open-airspaces fabrics (e.g. Blizzard, sponge). The URF may effectively contribute in thermal insulation when used as one of multiple insulating layers containing air gaps. When the patient is separated from the cold ground with a single layer of URF, no air gaps are present and reflection of infrared radiation is not sufficient to reduce heat flow significantly.

Another factor that can alter thermal conductivity in prehospital settings is the moisture (Henriksson et al. 2012, 2015). Thermal conductivity of water is 23-times higher than this of the air (0.61 vs. 0.026 W/m*K) (Lienhard V and Lienhard IV 2019). Water (e.g. from rain, melting snow or wet ground) and other fluids (e.g. blood) may penetrate into an insulating material eliminating airspaces. Our study was conducted in dry conditions, insulating systems we tested were thus not exposed to moisture. In real rescue settings with rainy weather, soaking the fibrous filling of Ferno blanket or similar insulating pads is likely, which can diminish their thermal resistance.

Limitations

Our study suffers from some limitations. First, our study protocol covered only one scenario regarding initial temperature gradient between a manikin and the ground. Testing the insulating systems in more extreme conditions could bring other differences between tested systems. Second, we assumed that the weight of our manikin corresponds to the weight of an average adult patient’s trunk. In actual patients with extreme body weights (e.g. children and obese persons), proportions of heat loss values of particular systems may be different. Third, we arbitrarily selected six commonly used insulation materials for our study. Finally, our study scenario does not eliminate a reflective component of heat flow reduction (e.g. in URF), but regarding our results it is only a theoretical speculation. Clinical applicability of our results should be thus limited to these systems.

Conclusion

In our study, ultrathin metalized foil did not reduce heat loss when compared to no-insulation scenario. All tested multilayer insulating systems performed significantly better. Metalized foils should not be used as the only insulation of a patient from cold ground.
