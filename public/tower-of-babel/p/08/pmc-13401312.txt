Regulatory mechanisms of high-polyphenol dietary intervention on exercise-induced immunosuppression during high-intensity training periods in adolescent athletes: a systems biology analysis based on immune-metabolic pathways
Authors: Qinshan Huang, Lei He, Jiahong Wu, Shun Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExercise-induced immunosuppression poses significant health risks for adolescent athletes during intensive training. This study investigated whether high-polyphenol dietary intervention could maintain immune homeostasis through immunometabolic reprogramming.MethodsEighty adolescent athletes (aged 15—17) were randomized to receive either high-polyphenol supplementation (1200 mg/day: quercetin, EGCG, resveratrol, curcumin) or placebo during a 12-week high-intensity training period. Multi-omics profiling (transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, microbiomics) was performed at baseline, weeks 4, 8, and 12.ResultsThe high-polyphenol group maintained CD4 + /CD8 + ratios above clinical thresholds (1.65 ± 0.29 vs. 1.38 ± 0.42, p < 0.001) at peak training. Regulatory T cells increased 17.2% while Th17 cells remained stable, yielding favorable Treg/Th17 ratios (3.09 ± 0.82 vs. 1.58 ± 0.54, p < 0.001). Multi-omics integration revealed 1,847 differentially expressed genes converging on NF-κB suppression, AMPK-mTOR activation, and Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses. Gut microbiome analysis showed doubled butyrate-producing bacteria and twofold increased short-chain fatty acid production. Salivary IgA declined only 6.5% versus 29.3% in placebo (p < 0.001).ConclusionsHigh-polyphenol dietary intervention effectively prevents exercise-induced immunosuppression through coordinated immunometabolic reprogramming, establishing a practical strategy for maintaining immune resilience in adolescent athletes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01132-1.Keywords: Exercise-induced immunosuppression, Polyphenol intervention, Adolescent athletes, Systems biology, Immunometabolism, Gut microbiome, Multi-omics, High-intensity training, Regulatory T cells, Short-chain fatty acids

Introduction

Exercise-induced immunosuppression represents a significant clinical challenge in adolescent athletes undergoing intensive training, with documented increases in upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) incidence during periods of high training loads. Epidemiological evidence indicates that elite young athletes experience significantly higher infection rates compared to age-matched non-athletes during competition seasons, resulting in substantial training time loss and compromised performance development. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as the "open window" hypothesis, describes the transient period of impaired immune surveillance following prolonged or intensive physical exercise [41].

The immunological vulnerability of adolescent athletes warrants particular attention due to the unique intersection of developmental physiology and training stress. Recent advances in exercise immunology have highlighted the critical importance of understanding age-specific immune responses, with particular emphasis on the monitoring of pubertal stages and biological maturation in pediatric sport populations [9]. De Almeida-Neto et al. demonstrated that repeated sprint exercise elicits markedly different immunological responses between adult and adolescent athletes at various stages of biological maturation, suggesting that chronological age alone is insufficient for characterizing immune function in young athletes [9]. Longitudinal investigations have further established that intensified training periods correspond with compromised immune function and increased URTI susceptibility in young athletes. Notbohm et al. reported that long-term physical training in adolescent swimmers induces substantial alterations in circulating T and NK cell populations, with these changes correlating with inflammatory markers such as soluble ICAM-1 [29]. Blume et al. demonstrated clear relationships between training load, immune status, and clinical outcomes through a controlled prospective study [5]. Furthermore, Wilson et al. identified distinct patterns of circulating T-regulatory cells in elite adolescent swimmers, demonstrating immunoregulatory responses that differ fundamentally from both adult athletes and sedentary peers [43]. These age-specific immune characteristics underscore the necessity for targeted intervention strategies in this population.

Current strategies to mitigate exercise-induced immunosuppression encompass periodization of training loads, optimization of recovery, and various nutritional interventions. Among nutritional approaches, carbohydrate supplementation during prolonged exercise has demonstrated efficacy in attenuating cortisol responses, while probiotic supplementation has shown promise in reducing URTI duration, though effects on immune biomarkers remain inconsistent. Natural bioactive compounds and antioxidant supplementation have garnered increasing attention as potential countermeasures. Spirulina supplementation combined with high-intensity interval training has been shown to favorably modulate inflammatory markers in physically active populations [44]. Similarly, spinach-derived thylakoid supplementation augments the metabolic and inflammatory benefits of high-intensity training [35]. The combined effects of antioxidant supplementation with exercise training on inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α have been demonstrated across multiple studies [34, 37], suggesting that plant-derived bioactive compounds may enhance the anti-inflammatory adaptations to physical training. However, these interventions exhibit notable limitations: single-nutrient approaches often target isolated parameters without addressing the interconnected immunometabolic networks disrupted by intensive training, and most studies have been conducted in adult populations with limited evidence in adolescent athletes.

Polyphenolic compounds have emerged as particularly promising candidates for combating exercise-induced immunosuppression due to their pleiotropic effects on inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune cell function [36]. These plant-derived bioactive compounds share common structural features—multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups—that confer antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties through convergent mechanisms. A systematic review by Fernández-Lázaro et al. demonstrated consistent anti-inflammatory effects of curcumin supplementation in physically active populations [12]. Bagheri et al. showed that green tea extract enhances the anti-inflammatory effects of exercise through catechin-mediated pathways [2]. Resveratrol attenuates exercise-induced interleukin-6 elevations through SIRT1 activation [39]. Notably, quercetin reduces illness incidence in athletes following intensive exercise [26, 27], with Konrad et al. confirming acute anti-inflammatory effects in runners [16]. These polyphenolic compounds target distinct yet interconnected nodes within the immunometabolic network, suggesting potential for synergistic effects when combined.

The emerging field of immunometabolism provides a framework for understanding the intersection of nutrition, metabolism, and immune function in exercise contexts. Padilha et al. highlighted the plasticity of immune-metabolic coupling according to physical fitness status [31], suggesting that nutritional interventions may reprogram immune cell metabolism during training stress. The gut microbiome represents an additional mechanistic pathway through which polyphenols may exert immunoprotective effects. Polyphenols undergo extensive microbial metabolism, producing bioactive metabolites while modulating microbiome composition toward increased short-chain fatty acid production, which directly influences immune cell differentiation through epigenetic mechanisms.

Despite these advances, critical knowledge gaps persist in our understanding of how polyphenol interventions can be optimized for adolescent athletes. First, the majority of studies have employed single compounds in adult populations, leaving the efficacy of multi-polyphenol formulations in adolescent athletes unexplored. Second, while individual pathways affected by polyphenols have been characterized, the systems-level integration across transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and microbiome dimensions remains uncharacterized. Third, the temporal dynamics of polyphenol-mediated immunoprotection across different training phases have not been systematically investigated. Finally, the identification of predictive biomarkers for personalized intervention strategies represents an unmet need.

This study addresses these gaps through a comprehensive investigation of high-polyphenol supplementation effects on exercise-induced immunosuppression in adolescent athletes during a 12-week high-intensity training period. By integrating multi-omics profiling with systems biology analysis, we aimed to: (1) evaluate the efficacy of combined polyphenol supplementation in maintaining immune homeostasis across training phases; (2) elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying polyphenol-mediated immunoprotection; (3) characterize the contribution of gut microbiome modulation to observed effects; and (4) identify candidate biomarkers for predicting individual responses.

Materials and methods

Study design

This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluated high-polyphenol dietary intervention effects on exercise-induced immunosuppression in adolescent athletes (n = 80) during a 12-week high-intensity training period. This study has been registered with the ISRCTN registry, registration number ISRCTN89562119.

Participants were stratified by sex, sport discipline, and baseline immune function before 1:1 randomization to high-polyphenol (1200 mg/day) or placebo groups. The intervention spanned three periodized training phases with distinct physiological demands, as detailed in Table 2. The preparatory phase (weeks 1—4) emphasized aerobic base building with moderate intensity and progressive volume increases. The competition phase (weeks 5—10) represented the highest training stress, characterized by increased intensity, sport-specific skill work, and simulated competition. The recovery phase (weeks 11—12) involved reduced training loads to facilitate physiological restoration. Training load was monitored daily using session rating of perceived exertion (sRPE) multiplied by training duration (minutes) to calculate arbitrary units (AU) [13]. Heart rate-based training impulse (TRIMP) was recorded during all sessions using chest-worn monitors (Polar H10, Polar Electro, Finland). Weekly training characteristics across phases are summarized in Table 1. Comprehensive assessments occurred at baseline (T0), week 4 (T1), week 8 (T2), and week 12 (T3), including blood, saliva, urine, and fecal sample collection for multi-omics analyses.

Primary endpoint was CD4 + /CD8 + T cell ratio change from baseline to week 8. Secondary endpoints included inflammatory cytokines, salivary IgA, NK cell activity, metabolomic signatures, and upper respiratory tract infection incidence. Sample size was calculated based on the primary endpoint (CD4 + /CD8 + T cell ratio change from baseline to week 8). We assumed a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.8) based on previous polyphenol intervention studies in athletes reporting effect sizes of 0.65—0.95 for T-cell parameters [26, 27]. With α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and statistical power of 0.80 (β = 0.20), a minimum of 26 participants per group was required. To account for an anticipated 20% dropout rate and to ensure adequate power for secondary multi-omics analyses, we enrolled 40 participants per group (total n = 80). Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). Compliance was monitored through electronic logs and urinary polyphenol metabolite assessment. Statistical analysis employed mixed-effects models with intention-to-treat principles. Quality control measures ensured data integrity throughout the investigation period.

Study participants

Eighty adolescent athletes (aged 15—17 years) were recruited from three provincial sports academies in Jiangsu Province, China, between September 2024 and November 2024. Participants represented diverse disciplines including swimming (n = 28), track and field (n = 26), and basketball (n = 26), with balanced sex distribution (42 males, 38 females). Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) minimum three years systematic training experience; (2) provincial-level competition participation within previous 12 months; (3) training volume ≥ 10 h/week; (4) Tanner stage III-V pubertal development. Exclusion criteria included: (1) acute infection within four weeks; (2) chronic inflammatory or autoimmune conditions; (3) regular medication or supplement use affecting immune function; (4) history of overtraining syndrome; (5) musculoskeletal injury preventing normal training.

All participants resided at their respective sports academies, ensuring standardized nutrition and recovery conditions. Baseline characteristics showed homogeneous distribution: mean age 16.2 ± 0.8 years, training experience 5.3 ± 1.4 years, VO₂max 52.4 ± 4.6 mL/kg/min (males) and 46.8 ± 3.9 mL/kg/min (females). The China General Administration of Sport approved recruitment procedures, with written informed consent obtained from participants and guardians.

Intervention protocol

The high-polyphenol dietary intervention delivered 1200 mg daily of four synergistic bioactive compounds targeting complementary immunometabolic pathways, as detailed in Table 1. The designation "high-polyphenol" reflects comparison with typical dietary intakes: this dose represents approximately 4—6 times the estimated average daily polyphenol intake in Western populations (200—300 mg/day from fruits, vegetables, and beverages) [21]. The 1200 mg total dose aligns with the upper range of polyphenol doses demonstrating immunomodulatory efficacy without adverse effects in previous athletic population studies, where quercetin (500—1000 mg), EGCG (270—400 mg), resveratrol (150—500 mg), and curcumin (200—500 mg) have been individually investigated [16, 26, 27]. The formulation was divided into two 600 mg doses administered at 7:00 AM and 7:00 PM with meals to optimize bioavailability and maintain therapeutic plasma concentrations.

As shown in Table 2, the formulation comprised four polyphenolic compounds with documented immunomodulatory properties in prior literature. Each compound was selected based on established evidence of anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, with formulation characteristics optimized to enhance bioavailability. Manufacturing followed Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards with HPLC verification confirming > 95% purity. The curcumin component included 5% piperine to enhance bioavailability 20-fold through glucuronidation inhibition. Participants consumed supplements with ≥ 10 g dietary fat while avoiding calcium-rich foods, iron supplements, and polyphenol-rich beverages within two hours of administration.

Compliance monitoring employed electronic medication event monitoring systems (eMEMS), bi-weekly pill counts, and LC–MS/MS quantification of urinary metabolites (quercetin-3’-glucuronide, EGCG-3’-sulfate, resveratrol-3-O-glucuronide, curcumin glucuronide) at weeks 4, 8, and 12. Participants maintaining < 80% compliance received adherence counseling; < 70% over consecutive assessments constituted protocol violation. The matched placebo contained microcrystalline cellulose with identical appearance and packaging. Quality assurance included third-party testing for contaminants and WADA-prohibited substances, with controlled storage at 20—25 °C maintaining > 98% compound stability throughout the intervention period.

Sample collection time points

Biological specimens were collected at four critical time points throughout the 12-week intervention period to capture the dynamic immunometabolic responses to high-intensity training and polyphenol supplementation, as detailed in Table 3. Sample collection was strategically timed to capture accumulated training effects within each phase rather than acute exercise responses. All samples were collected at the end of each training phase: T0 (baseline, prior to intervention), T1 (end of week 4, after preparatory phase), T2 (end of week 8, after peak competition phase), and T3 (end of week 12, after recovery phase). To isolate chronic training adaptations from acute exercise perturbations, samples were collected 36—48 h after the last training session of each phase, following an overnight fast (≥ 10 h). All procedures were performed between 6:00—8:00 AM to minimize circadian variation. Athletes remained in a seated position for 10 min before venipuncture to standardize hemodynamic conditions.

Biological sample collection schedule and analytical applications

As shown in Table 3, the comprehensive sampling strategy enabled multi-dimensional assessment of immune function across systemic (blood), mucosal (saliva), and gut-associated (feces) compartments. Blood samples were processed within 2 h of collection, with EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood used for immediate flow cytometric analysis of lymphocyte subsets (CD3⁺CD4⁺, CD3⁺CD8⁺, CD19⁺, CD16⁺CD56⁺, CD4⁺CD25⁺CD127^low Tregs) and remaining samples centrifuged at 1500 × g for peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) isolation using density gradient separation. Serum was obtained through centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min and aliquoted to avoid freeze–thaw cycles.

Saliva collection employed passive drool technique using Salimetrics SalivaBio collection devices, with samples centrifuged at 3000 × g to remove particulates before storage. First-morning urine samples were collected in preservative-free containers, with specific gravity measured to normalize biomarker concentrations. Fecal samples were collected using OMNIgene•GUT kits (DNA Genotek) containing stabilization buffer to preserve microbial DNA and metabolites at ambient temperature during transport. All biological specimens were aliquoted into cryovials, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 30 min of processing, and stored at -80 °C until batch analysis to minimize analytical variation. Sample tracking employed barcode labeling with duplicate backup storage to ensure specimen integrity throughout the analytical pipeline.

Detection indicators and methods

Comprehensive immunological, metabolic, and molecular profiling was performed using state-of-the-art analytical platforms to characterize the multidimensional effects of polyphenol intervention on exercise-induced immunosuppression, as outlined in Table S1. All assays were conducted in duplicate with inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation maintained below 10% through rigorous quality control protocols including reference standards, internal controls, and batch randomization to minimize technical variation.

As shown in Table S1, the multi-platform analytical approach enabled comprehensive characterization of immune function across cellular, molecular, and functional dimensions. Flow cytometric immunophenotyping employed standardized panels validated according to Human Immunophenotyping Consortium guidelines, with automated compensation matrices and hierarchical gating strategies to ensure reproducibility. Cytokine quantification utilized high-sensitivity multiplex platforms capable of detecting sub-picogram concentrations, critical for measuring low-abundance regulatory cytokines in trained athletes.

Metabolomic profiling integrated targeted quantification of immunologically relevant metabolites with untargeted discovery approaches, employing both hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) and reversed-phase (RP) separation to maximize metabolite coverage. Transcriptomic analysis required RNA integrity numbers (RIN) > 7.0, with library preparation using poly-A selection and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) to minimize PCR amplification bias. Proteomic workflows incorporated tandem mass tag (TMT) labeling for precise quantification across multiple time points, with phosphoproteome enrichment revealing dynamic signaling cascades underlying immune cell activation states.

Microbiome analysis combined amplicon sequencing for taxonomic profiling with shotgun metagenomics for functional characterization, utilizing the latest SILVA database (v138.1) for taxonomic assignment and HUMAnN3 pipeline for metabolic pathway reconstruction. All omics data underwent rigorous quality control including removal of low-quality reads (Phred score < 30), normalization for sequencing depth, and batch effect correction using ComBat-seq algorithms. Data integration employed mixOmics framework for multi-omics factor analysis, enabling identification of correlated molecular signatures across analytical platforms.

Systems biology integration analysis

Multi-omics data integration was performed using advanced computational frameworks to elucidate the complex molecular networks underlying polyphenol-mediated immunomodulation during high-intensity training, as detailed in Table S2. The analytical pipeline employed hierarchical integration strategies combining supervised and unsupervised methods to identify convergent biological pathways and predictive biomarker signatures while accounting for the inherent heterogeneity and dimensionality of multi-platform datasets.

As shown in Table S2, the systems biology framework integrated multiple analytical layers from data preprocessing through biological interpretation. Initial data harmonization employed platform-specific normalization strategies selected based on data distribution characteristics and established benchmarking studies. For proteomics data, variance stabilizing normalization (VSN) was applied as it effectively addresses the mean–variance relationship inherent in mass spectrometry intensity data and has demonstrated superior performance in comparative benchmark studies [15]. For RNA-seq count data, trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) normalization was employed to account for compositional biases and library size differences [33].

Batch effect correction utilized ComBat-seq rather than the original ComBat algorithm, as ComBat-seq was specifically designed for sequencing count data and preserves the integer nature of counts required for downstream negative binomial-based differential expression analysis [45]. ComBat-seq was selected over alternative methods (e.g., RUVseq, svaseq) based on its superior performance in preserving biological signal while removing technical variation in benchmarking studies involving multi-site exercise omics datasets [25].

For multi-omics integration, MOFA2 (Multi-Omics Factor Analysis version 2) was selected for several methodological reasons: (1) MOFA2 employs a Bayesian group factor analysis framework that naturally handles missing values across omics layers without requiring imputation; (2) it identifies latent factors that explain coordinated variation across platforms, enabling biological interpretation of shared molecular signatures; (3) MOFA2 has demonstrated superior performance compared to alternative integration methods (e.g., iCluster, JIVE) in benchmarks involving matched multi-omics datasets with similar sample sizes [1]. The algorithm was configured with default hyperparameters (K = 15 initial factors, automatic relevance determination for factor pruning, convergence threshold = 0.01). Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) constructed scale-free networks identifying hub genes within functionally related modules, with module-trait correlations revealing immunologically relevant gene clusters.

Machine learning approaches employed elastic net regularization (α = 0.5) for biomarker selection, balancing LASSO sparsity with Ridge regression stability. Random Forest models (ntree = 500, mtry = √p) achieved area under the curve (AUC) > 0.85 for predicting individual intervention responses, with variable importance measures identifying key discriminatory features. Temporal dynamics were captured through pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle3, revealing branching points in immune cell differentiation influenced by polyphenol exposure. Causal inference employed PC-stable algorithm with conditional independence tests (α = 0.01) to construct directed acyclic graphs representing putative regulatory relationships.

Pathway-level integration utilized Signaling Pathway Impact Analysis (SPIA) combining over-representation with topology-based evidence to identify perturbed immunological pathways. Cross-omics correlation networks constructed using Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent cOmponents (DIABLO) revealed multi-level molecular signatures associated with training adaptation. All analytical procedures incorporated rigorous validation through tenfold cross-validation, permutation testing (n = 10,000), and bootstrap confidence intervals, ensuring robust and reproducible identification of polyphenol-responsive immunometabolic networks.

Rigorous quality control was implemented for each omics layer prior to integration analysis, with detailed metrics presented in Table S3.

Transcriptomics QC: RNA quality was assessed using Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100, with samples requiring RIN ≥ 7.0 for library preparation. Sequencing quality was evaluated using FastQC (v0.11.9), with criteria including ≥ 20 million raw reads per sample, per-base quality scores ≥ 30 across read length, and adapter content < 1%. Reads were aligned to human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR (v2.7.9a) with default parameters, requiring ≥ 85% mapping rate. PCR duplicates were marked using Picard MarkDuplicates, with samples exceeding 30% duplication rate flagged for potential library complexity issues. Gene-level counts were generated using featureCounts (v2.0.1) with GENCODE v38 annotation. Lowly expressed genes (CPM < 1 in fewer than 20% of samples) were filtered, retaining 14,827 genes for downstream analysis.

Proteomics QC: Raw mass spectrometry files were processed using MaxQuant (v2.0.3.0) with match-between-runs enabled. Protein identification required ≥ 2 unique peptides at 1% FDR. Proteins with > 30% missing values across all samples were excluded. Remaining missing values were imputed using k-nearest neighbors (k = 10) after confirming missing-at-random assumption through Little’s MCAR test. Batch effects between analytical runs were assessed using principal component analysis and corrected using ComBat. Technical replicate correlation (Pearson r > 0.95) confirmed analytical reproducibility.

Metabolomics QC: Pooled quality control (QC) samples were prepared by combining equal aliquots from all study samples and injected every 10 samples throughout analytical runs. Features with CV > 30% in QC samples were excluded. Metabolite annotation was performed using accurate mass matching (± 5 ppm) and MS/MS spectral matching against HMDB (v5.0), METLIN, and in-house standards library. Annotation confidence levels followed Metabolomics Standards Initiative guidelines: Level 1 (authenticated standards, n = 156), Level 2 (spectral library match, n = 256).

Microbiome QC: For 16S rRNA gene sequencing, raw reads were processed using DADA2 pipeline (v1.22) with standard filtering parameters (maxEE = 2, truncQ = 2). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were assigned taxonomy using SILVA database (v138.1) with naive Bayesian classifier. Samples were rarefied to 30,000 reads for alpha diversity calculations, with Good’s coverage > 99% confirming adequate sampling depth. For shotgun metagenomics, quality filtering and host read removal were performed using KneadData (v0.10.0). Functional profiling employed HUMAnN3 (v3.6) with UniRef90 database and MetaCyc pathway definitions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.1) with significance set at α = 0.05. Baseline characteristics were compared using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical data. Primary outcome analysis employed linear mixed-effects models (LMM) with restricted maximum likelihood estimation, including fixed effects for treatment, time, treatment × time interaction, and random intercepts for participants to account for repeated measures correlation. Model assumptions were verified through residual diagnostics and Shapiro–Wilk normality tests.

Secondary outcomes were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with exchangeable correlation structure for non-normally distributed data. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d for between-group comparisons and reported with 95% confidence intervals. Missing data (< 5%) were handled through multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE, m = 20 imputations) under missing-at-random assumption. Sensitivity analyses included per-protocol analysis (≥ 80% compliance) and tipping-point analysis for missing data assumptions. All analyses followed intention-to-treat principles with pre-specified statistical analysis plan registered prior to database lock.

Results

Baseline characteristics and compliance

Participant flow and compliance

Of the 80 randomized participants, 76 (95%) completed the 12-week intervention (Fig. 1). Four participants withdrew: two from the high-polyphenol group (one due to relocation, one due to injury unrelated to intervention) and two from the placebo group (one due to illness unrelated to intervention, one withdrew consent). No adverse events attributable to the intervention were reported.

CONSORT flow diagram of participant enrollment, allocation, follow-up, and analysis

Compliance was excellent in both groups. Mean supplement adherence, assessed by electronic monitoring (eMEMS), was 94.2 ± 4.8% in the high-polyphenol group and 93.8 ± 5.1% in the placebo group (p = 0.72). Urinary polyphenol metabolite concentrations confirmed biological compliance, with quercetin-3’-glucuronide, EGCG-3’-sulfate, resveratrol-3-O-glucuronide, and curcumin glucuronide detected in > 95% of intervention group samples at weeks 4, 8, and 12, and < 5% of placebo group samples (cross-contamination screening). Two participants in the high-polyphenol group required adherence counseling (compliance 75—80%) but remained above the protocol violation threshold.

All 80 randomized participants were included in the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis. For the four participants who withdrew, available data up to the point of withdrawal were included in mixed-effects models, which appropriately handle incomplete data under the missing-at-random assumption. Per-protocol analysis (n = 72, excluding 4 withdrawals and 4 participants with compliance < 80%) yielded consistent results (Supplementary Table S1).

Cellular immune parameters differed significantly between groups throughout the 12-week training period, with the largest between-group differences observed at week 8. Humoral immunity and inflammatory markers

Humoral immune parameters and inflammatory markers showed significant between-group differences throughout the 12-week training period (Table 5, Fig. 3). As detailed in Table 5 and illustrated in Fig. 3, the intervention group maintained mucosal immunity and demonstrated enhanced anti-inflammatory capacity compared to progressive inflammatory dysregulation observed in placebo-treated athletes.

Temporal dynamics of inflammatory balance and immune markers during high-intensity training. (A) Pro-inflammatory/anti-inflammatory cytokine ratio calculated as (IL-6 + TNF-α + IL-1β)/(IL-10 + IL-4 + TGF-β), with shaded zones indicating anti-inflammatory (green), balanced (yellow), and pro-inflammatory (red) states. (B-E) Individual cytokine and immunoglobulin responses showing IL-6, TNF-α, IL-10, and salivary IgA concentrations. Data presented as mean ± 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance: **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 (linear mixed-effects models with Benjamini–Hochberg correction). The humoral immune response demonstrated marked preservation with polyphenol supplementation, as evidenced by maintained immunoglobulin production during peak training stress (Table 5). Salivary IgA, a critical marker of mucosal immunity and infection resistance, declined only 6.5% from baseline in the high-polyphenol group compared to 29.3% in placebo (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.42), representing the largest effect size observed among immunoglobulin parameters. Similarly, serum IgA showed minimal reduction (-4.2%) in the intervention group versus substantial suppression (-21.7%) in controls at week 8.

The pro-inflammatory/anti-inflammatory cytokine ratio diverged significantly between groups (Fig. 3A). The pro-inflammatory/anti-inflammatory ratio, a composite measure of systemic inflammation, diverged significantly between groups from week 4 onwards. While the high-polyphenol group shifted toward an anti-inflammatory state (ratio decreasing from 1.82 to 1.48), the placebo group demonstrated progressive inflammatory dysregulation (ratio increasing from 1.79 to 2.89, p < 0.001). This 95% difference in inflammatory trajectory was driven by opposing changes in both pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators.

Pro-inflammatory cytokines showed attenuated responses in the intervention group, with IL-6 increasing only 21.7% versus 75.0% in placebo, and TNF-α rising 12.2% versus 47.5% respectively at peak training. Conversely, anti-inflammatory cytokines were enhanced with polyphenol supplementation, particularly IL-10 which increased 24.4% in the intervention group while decreasing 10.0% in controls (between-group difference: 3.0 pg/mL, p < 0.001). The IL-1 receptor antagonist showed similar divergent patterns, increasing with intervention while declining in placebo, suggesting enhanced endogenous anti-inflammatory capacity.

C-reactive protein, a clinical marker of systemic inflammation, remained below 1.5 mg/L in the high-polyphenol group throughout training, while exceeding this threshold in placebo athletes at week 8 (2.1 ± 0.7 mg/L, p < 0.001).

Metabolic reprogramming characteristics

Energy metabolism conversion

Metabolic profiles differed significantly between groups, with distinct patterns in oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic markers. As illustrated in Fig. 4, comprehensive metabolomic profiling revealed concurrent shifts in energy metabolism pathways, with the intervention group maintaining metabolic flexibility despite increased training demands.

The glycolytic pathway showed differential regulation between groups, with placebo athletes demonstrating 2.3-fold increases in lactate accumulation and elevated phosphofructokinase activity at week 8, indicative of glycolytic predominance. Conversely, the high-polyphenol group maintained balanced glucose utilization with only 1.4-fold lactate elevation (p < 0.001), suggesting preserved aerobic metabolism. Key glycolytic intermediates including glucose-6-phosphate, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, and pyruvate showed attenuated accumulation in the intervention group, indicating improved metabolic flux rather than pathway bottlenecks.

Mitochondrial oxidative capacity was markedly enhanced with polyphenol supplementation, evidenced by increased citrate synthase activity (28% vs. 5% in placebo, p < 0.001) and elevated TCA cycle intermediates including α-ketoglutarate, succinate, and malate. The ATP/ADP ratio remained stable in the intervention group (3.8 ± 0.4) while declining significantly in placebo (2.9 ± 0.5, p < 0.001), indicating superior energy production efficiency. Molecular mechanism analysis

Multi-omics integration identified correlated molecular signatures associated with polyphenol-mediated effects, encompassing 1,847 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 342 differential proteins, and 287 metabolites. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the Circos plot visualizes cross-platform correlations among transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic features, with correlation analyses revealing potential relationships between these molecular layers. However, it should be noted that these correlational patterns do not establish causal or mechanistically coordinated relationships; whether these associations reflect direct regulatory connections or shared responses to intervention requires further experimental validation. Transcriptomic analysis identified NF-κB signaling as the most significantly enriched pathway (Table S4), with 42 DEGs showing coordinated suppression (enrichment score = 5.24, FDR < 0.001). Conversely, AMPK signaling components showed enhanced expression, with PRKAA1 (encoding AMPKα1) upregulated 2.8-fold and downstream targets including PPARGC1A (PGC-1α, 3.2-fold) and SIRT1 (2.6-fold) demonstrating coordinated activation. These molecular changes, observed across multiple omics layers, are consistent with an integrated response favoring anti-inflammatory, pro-metabolic, and antioxidant programs. While the correlational nature of multi-omics integration does not permit definitive conclusions regarding causality or direct regulatory coordination, the convergence of these pathways on established immunometabolic nodes suggests biologically plausible mechanisms for polyphenol-mediated effects on immune homeostasis.

This enhanced butyrate production correlated strongly with increased Treg cells (r = 0.72, p < 0.001) and reduced inflammatory markers.

Akkermansia muciniphila, a keystone species for metabolic health, increased 2.14-fold with intervention, showing significant correlations with improved CD4 + /CD8 + ratio (r = 0.68) and reduced TNF-α levels (r = -0.54). Conversely, potentially pathogenic bacteria including Escherichia and Clostridium difficile decreased by 57% and 67% respectively, with corresponding reductions in endotoxin levels and pro-inflammatory signaling.

Discussion

The present study provides compelling evidence that high-polyphenol dietary intervention effectively counteracts exercise-induced immunosuppression in adolescent athletes through multi-level modulation of cellular immunity, inflammatory signaling, metabolic reprogramming, and gut microbiome composition. Our findings demonstrate the efficacy of polyphenol supplementation in the physiological context of adolescent athletes. While previous studies have established immunoprotective effects of polyphenols in adult populations [42], direct comparison between age groups was beyond the scope of the present study; therefore, whether the observed mechanisms are age-specific remains to be determined through future comparative investigations. The preservation of CD4 + /CD8 + ratios above clinical thresholds throughout intensive training represents a clinically meaningful outcome, as longitudinal studies have established this parameter as a robust predictor of infection susceptibility and overtraining syndrome in young athletes [25].

The magnitude of immunoprotection observed exceeds that reported in previous nutritional intervention studies employing single polyphenolic compounds. While this may suggest potential additive or synergistic effects of the multi-polyphenol formulation, our study design did not include single-compound control groups; therefore, we cannot definitively attribute the enhanced efficacy to synergistic interactions versus the combined independent effects of individual compounds. While Nieman et al. demonstrated modest immune benefits with quercetin monotherapy, our combined approach yielded effect sizes ranging from 0.75 to 2.31 across multiple immune parameters. Based on established literature, the individual polyphenols in our formulation are known to target distinct molecular pathways: quercetin inhibits NF-κB signaling, EGCG modulates T-cell differentiation, resveratrol activates sirtuins, and curcumin suppresses COX-2-mediated inflammation [18]. These complementary mechanisms provide a plausible rationale for the observed effects, though direct demonstration of synergy would require factorial study designs comparing combined versus individual polyphenol supplementation. The temporal dynamics of immune modulation, with maximal effects at week 8 coinciding with peak training loads, suggests adaptive responses requiring sustained polyphenol exposure rather than acute supplementation strategies commonly employed in sports nutrition [20].

Our transcriptomic findings align with recent advances in exercise immunology demonstrating that metabolic reprogramming underlies immune cell fate decisions during physiological stress [3]. The concurrent upregulation of AMPK signaling components and downstream metabolic targets, including PGC-1α and SIRT1, indicates a fundamental shift toward oxidative metabolism supporting anti-inflammatory immune phenotypes. This metabolic rewiring is particularly relevant for adolescent athletes, as Timmons et al. [38] identified distinct metabolic-immune coupling patterns in youth compared to adults during exercise stress. The preservation of mitochondrial function evidenced by maintained ATP/ADP ratios and enhanced citrate synthase activity contrasts sharply with the metabolic dysfunction typically observed during overreaching states [28].

The profound modulation of the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway deserves particular attention given its central role in exercise-induced immunosuppression. The 48% reduction in kynurenine levels with polyphenol intervention represents a novel finding with important implications for athletic performance and mental health. Elevated kynurenine/tryptophan ratios have been associated with central fatigue, depression, and impaired immune function in athletes [4]. Our observation that polyphenols attenuate kynurenine accumulation while preserving serotonin levels suggests protection against both peripheral and central manifestations of overtraining. This finding corroborates recent work by Contrepois et al. demonstrating that multi-omic responses to endurance exercise are modifiable through targeted nutritional interventions.

The gut microbiome emerged as a critical mediator of polyphenol immunoprotection, with effect sizes for microbiome modulation exceeding those for direct immune parameters. The twofold increase in butyrate-producing bacteria and corresponding SCFA elevation provides mechanistic insight into the observed enhancement of regulatory T-cell populations. Recent studies have established that butyrate directly induces Treg differentiation through histone deacetylase inhibition and metabolic reprogramming [30]. Our findings extend this paradigm to the exercise context, demonstrating that polyphenol-induced microbiome shifts can counteract training-associated dysbiosis. The increased abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila, now recognized as a keystone species for metabolic and immune health [22], suggests benefits beyond immediate immunoprotection, potentially including enhanced insulin sensitivity and reduced systemic inflammation.

The integrated multi-omics analysis revealed complexity in polyphenol-associated molecular changes, with 1,847 differentially expressed genes showing correlated patterns across analytical platforms. These correlations converged on three key regulatory nodes—NF-κB, AMPK-mTOR, and Nrf2—providing a plausible framework for understanding polyphenol effects. However, we acknowledge that correlational analyses cannot establish causal network behavior; the observed associations may reflect either direct regulatory relationships or parallel responses to intervention, and mechanistic validation through targeted experimental approaches would be required to distinguish these possibilities. Similar systems-level responses have been reported in cancer immunotherapy [23], suggesting fundamental principles governing nutritional immunomodulation. The identification of hub genes within co-expression modules offers potential biomarkers for monitoring intervention efficacy and predicting individual responses, addressing a critical need for precision nutrition approaches in sports medicine [10].

Comparison with existing literature reveals both consistencies and novel insights. Our findings corroborate the exercise immunology framework proposed by Walsh [7] regarding nutritional countermeasures for athletes, while extending it through systems biology approaches. The observed Treg/Th17 ratio modulation aligns with recent discoveries in tumor immunology, suggesting shared mechanisms between exercise-induced and cancer-associated immune dysfunction. However, our effect sizes substantially exceed those reported in previous polyphenol studies, likely reflecting optimal dosing, synergistic formulations, and the enhanced polyphenol bioavailability achieved through piperine co-administration [40].

The clinical implications of these findings primarily relate to immune resilience during intensive training periods. The preserved immune function during peak training loads may theoretically support training continuity by reducing the risk of illness-related interruptions, which is particularly relevant for adolescent athletes preparing for major competitions [17]. However, our study did not directly assess training volume tolerance, illness incidence during the intervention period, or actual training days lost; therefore, the translational implications for training prescription require validation through studies incorporating these functional outcomes. The observed metabolic benefits, including enhanced oxidative capacity and reduced lactate accumulation, may translate to improved endurance performance, though this requires direct investigation in performance trials.

Several mechanistic insights warrant further investigation. The opposing trajectories of IL-10 and inflammatory cytokines suggest polyphenol-induced reprogramming of macrophage polarization, shifting from M1 pro-inflammatory to M2 anti-inflammatory phenotypes [14]. This interpretation is supported by the proteomic identification of altered macrophage markers and the metabolomic signature of enhanced oxidative metabolism characteristic of M2 macrophages. The reduction in cortisol and oxidative stress markers suggests attenuation of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activation, which may theoretically support recovery processes (Kelly & O’neill, 2015). However, our study did not include direct assessments of recovery outcomes such as performance restoration, subjective recovery ratings, or return-to-baseline kinetics; this represents an important area for future investigation.

The study’s strengths include its randomized controlled design, comprehensive multi-omics profiling, and focus on the understudied adolescent athlete population. The 12-week intervention period captured complete training macrocycles, enhancing ecological validity. The systems biology approach revealed mechanistic insights unattainable through reductionist methods, validating recent calls for integrative approaches in exercise science [11]. The excellent compliance rates (> 90%) and biological verification through urinary metabolites strengthen confidence in the findings.

However, several limitations merit consideration. First, and most importantly, our study did not include functional or clinical outcome assessments such as upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) incidence, illness symptom duration, missed training days, or performance metrics. While we demonstrated favorable changes in immune biomarkers, the clinical significance of these improvements—specifically whether they translate to reduced illness burden and improved training availability—remains to be established. Future studies should incorporate prospective illness surveillance alongside immunological assessments to determine the functional relevance of polyphenol-mediated immune modulation.

Second, although our study collected comprehensive baseline data including sex, immune parameters, metabolic phenotypes, and microbiome composition that could theoretically enable subgroup analyses to explore individual response heterogeneity, such stratified analyses were not conducted in the present study. The sample size (n = 40 per group), while adequate for detecting overall treatment effects on the primary endpoint, provides limited statistical power for meaningful subgroup analyses (e.g., n≈20 per sex within each treatment arm). Exploratory subgroup analyses with such sample sizes carry substantial risk of both Type I and Type II errors and may generate unreliable effect estimates [6]. Future studies with larger sample sizes specifically powered for subgroup comparisons, or individual participant data meta-analyses pooling multiple trials, are needed to identify predictors of treatment response.

Third, while our multi-omics analyses identified consistent molecular changes across transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic platforms, the conclusions regarding specific pathway modulation rely primarily on correlative data. Direct validation of key mechanistic findings through targeted molecular assays was not performed. Specifically, the proposed suppression of NF-κB signaling and activation of AMPK-mTOR pathways were inferred from differential gene expression and protein abundance data, but were not independently confirmed by Western blotting to quantify protein expression levels and phosphorylation states (e.g., phospho-NF-κB p65, phospho-AMPK), or by RT-qPCR to validate differential expression of top-ranked genes. Although the convergence of findings across independent omics platforms provides a degree of internal cross-validation, these targeted assays would strengthen causal inference for the proposed mechanisms. Future studies should incorporate such molecular validation approaches to establish a more robust mechanistic foundation. Fourth, the study focused exclusively on endurance-trained athletes from three sports disciplines (swimming, track and field, basketball), limiting generalizability to power or team sport athletes with different immunological demands.

Fifth, the predominantly Chinese cohort may not represent global populations with different genetic backgrounds and dietary patterns. Sixth, while we identified correlational patterns through multi-omics integration, causality cannot be definitively established; the observed associations may reflect direct regulatory relationships or parallel responses to intervention. Finally, the intervention combined four polyphenols, preventing attribution of effects to specific compounds, though this reflects real-world supplementation practices.

Additionally, the study design did not include post-intervention follow-up to assess the sustainability of immunoprotective effects after discontinuing polyphenol supplementation. Whether the observed benefits persist, diminish gradually, or reverse rapidly upon cessation remains unknown. Longitudinal assessment of infection incidence during and after the intervention period was not conducted, limiting our ability to evaluate the clinical durability of these findings.

The optimal dosing strategy remains to be determined, as dose–response relationships were not evaluated. Higher doses might yield greater benefits, though gastrointestinal tolerance and potential pro-oxidant effects at supraphysiological concentrations must be considered [24]. The timing of supplementation relative to training sessions may influence efficacy, as suggested by chronobiology research demonstrating circadian variations in polyphenol metabolism [8]. Individual variability in polyphenol metabolism, influenced by genetic polymorphisms and baseline microbiome composition, likely contributes to response heterogeneity (Kelly & O’neill, 2015). Although our comprehensive baseline dataset included microbiome profiling and metabolic phenotyping that could theoretically enable subgroup analyses to identify predictors of response variability, such analyses were not conducted in the present study and represent an important direction for secondary analyses and future research.

Future research should address several key questions. Longer-term studies incorporating post-intervention follow-up are essential to evaluate whether immunoprotective benefits persist after discontinuing supplementation, and to determine optimal supplementation duration and cycling strategies. Longitudinal assessment of infection incidence during extended follow-up periods would strengthen the clinical relevance of these findings. Investigating the potential of polyphenol interventions to mitigate overtraining syndrome—characterized by persistent performance decrements, mood disturbances, and prolonged immune dysfunction—could expand the practical applications for adolescent athlete health management. The minimum effective dose and optimal polyphenol combinations require systematic investigation through factorial designs. Sex differences in polyphenol metabolism and immune responses warrant dedicated studies, as our mixed-sex cohort lacked power for subgroup analyses [19]. Integration with other recovery modalities, including sleep optimization and stress management, may yield synergistic benefits. The development of personalized supplementation protocols based on genetic, metabolic, and microbiome profiling represents the future of precision sports nutrition [32].

In conclusion, this study establishes high-polyphenol dietary intervention as an effective strategy for maintaining immune competence in adolescent athletes undergoing intensive training. The multi-layered mechanisms encompassing direct immunomodulation, metabolic reprogramming, and microbiome optimization provide a robust biological foundation for implementation in sports medicine practice. These findings have immediate translational potential for reducing illness incidence, optimizing training adaptation, and safeguarding the health of young athletes during critical developmental and competitive periods.

Conclusions

This comprehensive investigation demonstrates that high-polyphenol dietary intervention (1200 mg/day) effectively prevents exercise-induced immunosuppression in adolescent athletes during 12-week high-intensity training periods. The intervention preserved CD4 + /CD8 + T-cell ratios above clinical thresholds, enhanced regulatory T-cell function, and maintained mucosal immunity when placebo-treated athletes experienced progressive immune dysfunction. Multi-omics integration revealed convergent mechanisms involving NF-κB suppression, AMPK-mTOR activation, and Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses, coordinating 1,847 differentially expressed genes, 342 proteins, and 287 metabolites. Notably, polyphenol-induced gut microbiome remodeling doubled butyrate-producing bacteria and SCFA production, establishing the gut-immune axis as a critical intervention target. These findings provide mechanistic evidence supporting polyphenol supplementation as a practical, safe strategy for maintaining immune resilience in young athletes. The systems biology framework developed here enables personalized intervention strategies based on individual immunometabolic profiles, advancing precision nutrition approaches in sports medicine. Future implementation could significantly reduce training interruptions due to illness while optimizing adaptation in adolescent athletic populations.
