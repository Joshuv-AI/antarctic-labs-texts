Safety and Effectiveness of Direct Oral Anticoagulants for the Treatment of Gastrointestinal Cancer-Associated Venous Thromboembolism
Authors: Fauzia Ullah, Juhee Song, Cristhiam M Rojas Hernandez, Michael H Kroll, Carmelita P Escalante, Katy M Toale
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with gastrointestinal cancer (GICA) are at high risk for venous thromboembolism (VTE). Data from randomized clinical trials in cancer-associated VTE suggest that direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) conferred similar or superior efficacy but a heterogeneous safety profile in patients with GICA. We compared the safety and effectiveness of DOACs in patients with GICA and VTE at MD Anderson Cancer Center.Materials and Methods This was a retrospective chart review of patients with GICA and VTE receiving treatment with DOACs for a minimum of 6 months. Primary outcomes were the proportion of patients experiencing major bleeding (MB), clinically relevant non-major bleeding (CRNMB), and recurrent VTE. Secondary outcomes were time to bleeding and recurrent VTE.ResultsA cohort of 433 patients with GICA who were prescribed apixaban (n = 300), or rivaroxaban (n = 133) were included. MB occurred in 3.7% (95% confidence interval [CI] 2.1-5.9), CRNMB in 5.3% (95% CI 3.4-7.9), and recurrent VTE in 7.4% (95% CI 5.1-10.3). The cumulative incidence rates of CRNMB and recurrent VTE were not significantly different when comparing apixaban to rivaroxaban.ConclusionApixaban and rivaroxaban had a similar risk of recurrent VTE and bleeding and could be considered as anticoagulant options in selected patients with GICA and VTE.

Implications for Practice

It is well known that gastrointestinal cancer (GICA) is associated with a higher incidence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) compared to other solid tumors. Low-molecular weight heparin was the treatment of choice for VTE in patients with cancer, until major trials showed that direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) conferred similar or superior efficacy, but a heterogeneous safety profile in patients with GICA. This study, which to date presents the largest sample of GICA patients receiving DOACs for VTE, supports the favorable profile of oral apixaban and rivaroxaban; however, more information is needed in patients with upper GICA.

Introduction

Patients with cancer are at increased risk for venous thromboembolism (VTE), even in the absence of traditional VTE risk factors (1). Patients with cancer account for 20% of cases of VTE and approximately one in every 7 deaths in hospitalized cancer patients. Furthermore, patients with VTE and cancer have an elevated risk of bleeding while on anticoagulant treatment and this is a frequent cause of early anticoagulation withdrawal (1-4). The standard long-term therapy for VTE was vitamin K antagonists (VKA), and it was associated with a higher rate of major bleeding in cancer patients (5). Several randomized clinical trials and meta-analyses derived from those trials have shown superior efficacy of low-molecular weight heparin (LMWH) over VKA for the management of cancer-associated VTE (CA-VTE); therefore, LMWH has until recently been widely recommended as the treatment of choice for CA-VTE (6-9).

Several recent randomized clinical trials of CA-VTE have tested the efficacy and safety of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) such as apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban versus LMWH (10-15). Based on the results of those studies, DOACs are recommended as treatment option by different expert consensus statements and clinical guidelines with the caveat that both the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) (16) and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) (17) warn about a higher risk of bleeding in patients with gastric or gastroesophageal cancers treated with DOACs. A meta-analysis of the SELECT-D and Hokusai-VTE cancer trials (18) confirmed an increase in clinically important bleeding events with DOACs in patients with upper gastrointestinal cancer. However, in a subgroup analysis of the Caravaggio trial (19), major gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding in patients with GI cancer (GICA) was low and similar in both apixaban and LMWH groups.

Patients with GICA are underrepresented in the major trials examining treatment of CA-VTE with DOACs. Despite concerns that DOACs pose a significant bleeding risk in GICA patients (20-22), many patients with GICA are treated with apixaban or rivaroxaban in clinical practice (23, 24). We therefore aimed to retrospectively evaluate the safety and effectiveness of DOACs in patients with GICA and CA-VTE treated at MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC).

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We conducted a single-center retrospective chart review of patients with histologically proven GICA and symptomatic or incidental VTE receiving treatment with DOACs at MDACC from March 2016 to September 2021. An electronic database query of all patients with GICA and VTE was initially performed, and then each patient’s electronic record was reviewed for inclusion criteria. GICA subgroup and tumor status were extracted from the tumor board registry. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools available at MDACC (25). Prior to study initiation, we obtained Institutional Review Board approval with a waiver of informed consent and personal health information authorization.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

All patients who had histologically proven GICA and symptomatic or incidental VTE treated with a DOAC (apixaban or rivaroxaban) for at least 6 months at MDACC were identified. Prescription adherence and persistence data were confirmed by reviewing prescription refills and provider notes. GICAs included pancreatic (adenocarcinoma), upper GI (gastric, gastroesophageal or junctional-squamous and adenocarcinoma), colorectal (including anal, appendiceal, and cecal), small bowel (adenocarcinoma), and hepatobiliary (including hepatocellular) cancers. Treatment for cancer at the time of DOAC initiation, tumor status, baseline renal function (creatinine clearance) and platelet count when available were assessed. Acute symptomatic or incidental deep vein thrombosis (DVT, both upper and lower extremity) was diagnosed by venous duplex ultrasound and pulmonary embolism (PE) was confirmed by computed tomography with intravenous contrast. Standard VTE dosing schedules were followed according to FDA labels: Rivaroxaban 15 mg twice daily for the first 3 weeks followed by 20 mg daily and apixaban 10 mg twice daily for the first 7 days followed by 5 mg twice daily. Patients were excluded if DOAC was prescribed for any other reason not related to GICA-VTE and in patients with other malignancies not related to a GI site.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The primary safety outcomes were proportions of patients experiencing MB and CRNMB during the 6-month treatment period. MB was defined by one or more of the following: Hemoglobin drop of ≥ 2 g/dL in 24 hours, transfusion of ≥ 2 units of packed red blood cells, bleeding in a critical site, or bleeding contributing to death (26). CRNMB was any bleeding that is not MB or as defined by one or more of the following: the need for medical intervention, unscheduled contact with a physician, interruption or discontinuation of DOAC therapy, or impairment of activities of daily life. The primary efficacy outcome was recurrent symptomatic or incidental DVT or PE. The secondary safety outcomes were time to onset of first bleeding (MB, CRNMB and total bleeding), and effectiveness outcome was time to recurrent VTE.

Statistical Analyses

Patient demographic and baseline characteristics were summarized for all patients and by DOAC type using descriptive statistics, mean standard deviations (SD), or median interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and count (%) for categorical variables. A two-sample t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparisons of continuous variables. The Chi-square test or Fisher exact test was used for comparisons of categorical variables between apixaban and rivaroxaban groups.

For the primary outcomes, the proportion of patients with MB, CRNMB, total bleeding and recurrent VTE were estimated along with 95% CIs for all patients and by DOAC type. The Chi-square test was used to compare these proportions between apixaban and rivaroxaban groups. For the secondary outcomes, time to first MB, CRNMB, total bleeding, and recurrent VTE were compared between groups using Fine-Gray model and Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence curves were estimated. Patients without an event were censored at the time of last follow-up or death. Death without an event was considered a competing risk event.

Univariate and multivariate Fine-Gray models were fitted to evaluate the effect of DOAC type (apixaban or rivaroxaban) on cumulative incidence of total bleeding, MB, CRNMB, and recurrent VTE. Subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR) and 95% CI were calculated to evaluate the strengths of observed associations. For all analyses, P < .05 was considered statistically significant. SAS Software version 9.4 (SAS institute, Inc., Cary, NC) was used for all data analyses.

RESULTS

Patients

A total of 754 patient charts were reviewed, of which 433 fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Our analysis included patients who received apixaban or rivaroxaban (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics were similar between groups (Table 1). The study cohort comprised a predominantly male and elderly population, as expected for the type of cancers included in the study. Primary tumors of the luminal and non-luminal upper GI tract were approximately two-fifths of the cohort. Nearly two-thirds of patients were on active chemotherapy and 61% of all VTEs were PE. None of the patients included in our analysis were taking medications with significant DOAC drug interaction.

Primary Outcomes

Overall, MB occurred in 16 patients (Table 2); 8 patients who received apixaban and 8 patients who received rivaroxaban (Table 3). MB and CRNMB were not significantly different among patients receiving apixaban or rivaroxaban (Table 3). There was a non-significant increased risk in CRNMB in the rivaroxaban group depending on cancer type, but the sample size was small to make comparisons. DOAC was discontinued due to bleeding in 8.3% (n = 36, 95% CI 5.9-11.1) of all patients, with 7.0% (n = 21) for apixaban, and 11.28% (n = 15) for rivaroxaban. There was no difference in discontinuation rates between apixaban and rivaroxaban (P = .14).

There were equal proportions of patients with resected 20 (51%) and non-resected 19 (49%) cancers among those with bleeding. Secondary Outcomes

The cumulative incidence of total bleeding, MB, CRNMB, and recurrent VTE did not significantly differ between apixaban and rivaroxaban groups (Fig. 2). The median time to MB event was 46 days for rivaroxaban and 59 days for apixaban. The median time to CRNMB event was 46 days for rivaroxaban and 89 days for apixaban. In addition, the median time to recurrent VTE was 38 days for rivaroxaban and 40 days for apixaban. Of the 39 total bleeds, 28 were gastrointestinal, one was intracranial, 3 were retroperitoneal, 4 were genitourinary, and 3 were from the upper airways.

Fine-Gray Models for Secondary Outcomes

Univariate and multivariate Fine-Gray models were performed to assess the effect of DOAC type, body mass index, creatinine clearance, platelet count, cancer stage and type, and ECOG score on the cumulative incidence of total bleeding, MB and recurrent VTE. Overall, DOAC type was not significantly associated with cumulative incidence of total bleeding (sHR 0.60, 95% CI 0.32-1.13; P = .11), MB (sHR 0.43, 95% CI 0.16-1.16; P = .10), or recurrent VTE (sHR 0.73, 95% CI 0.35-1.53; P = .41) when significant variables were adjusted (Table 4). Higher baseline platelet count (>100K) at DOAC initiation was associated with a lower cumulative incidence of total bleeding risk (sHR 0.34, 95% CI 0.15-0.77; P = .01) and MB (sHR 0.19, 95% CI 0.06-0.61; P = .005). Metastatic disease was associated with higher cumulative incidence of total bleeding (sHR 2.27, 95% CI 1.04-4.96; P = .04). There was no statistically significant difference in cumulative incidence of MB between apixaban and rivaroxaban. Patients with ECOG scores 3-4 (sHR 5.60, 95% CI 1.77-17.72; P = .003), symptomatic VTE (sHR 2.70, 95% CI 1.34-5.42; P = .005) and metastatic disease (sHR 2.92, 95% CI 1.19-7.18; P = .02) were associated with a higher cumulative incidence rate of recurrent VTE (Table 4).

Discussion

The results of our study suggest that apixaban and rivaroxaban could be considered as anticoagulant options for the treatment of VTE in patients with GICA. The rates of MB, CRNMB, and recurrent VTE were not statistically significant between the apixaban and rivaroxaban cohorts. The time to MB and CRNMB was longer in the apixaban cohort. The overall incidence of VTE recurrence was 7.4% (6.7% for apixaban and 9.0% for rivaroxaban).

Although recent cancer-specific clinical trials comparing DOAC and LMWH have shown DOAC has noninferior or superior efficacy to dalteparin for CA-VTE; however, there were some conflicting results regarding bleeding risk (10-13). These trials have indicated that patients with esophageal or gastroesophageal cancer may have an increased risk of bleeding on DOACs therapy for VTE. It is important to note that most of the trials included patients with a history of cancer diagnosed within 2 years before enrolment, and the definition of active cancer, including cancer diagnosis and treatment in the previous 6 months was used. This suggests that patients without a currently existing cancer lesion at the beginning or during anticoagulation could also have been enrolled in those trials, which might have influenced bleeding risk.

The SELECT-D trial (10,27) showed rivaroxaban was associated with a relatively low VTE recurrence (4% with rivaroxaban and 11% with dalteparin; HR = 0.43) but higher incidence of CRNMB compared with dalteparin (6-month cumulative rate, 13% versus 4%, HR = 3.76), and similar MB (6% vs 4%, HR = 1.83). An interim safety analysis revealed that 36% of MB in the rivaroxaban group had esophageal or gastroesophageal cancer, compared to 5% with dalteparin leading to subsequent exclusion of these cancer types from enrolment as a precautionary measure (17). The ADAM-VTE trial (11,27) hypothesized that apixaban was associated with a significantly lower MB rate compared to dalteparin, failed to show the superiority of apixaban in term of MB (0% of 145 patients receiving apixaban vs 1.4% of 142 patients receiving dalteparin; P = .138) or CRNMB (6.2% in apixaban vs 6.3% in dalteparin, P = .881). VTE recurrence was lower for apixaban (0.7%) than for dalteparin (6.3%, HR=0.099). The Caravaggio trial (13,19,27) showed that apixaban was noninferior to dalteparin for recurrent CA-VTE (HR = 0.63, P < .001 for noninferiority; P = .09 for superiority) without an increased risk of MB (3.8% in apixaban vs 4.0% in dalteparin; HR = 0.82) and CRNMB was 9.0% in apixaban vs 6.0% in dalteparin (HR = 1.42). In a modified intention-to-treat analysis of the Caravaggio trial by cancer type, recurrent VTE in patients with GICA occurred in 8.5% with apixaban and 9.1% with dalteparin (risk difference −0.0058, 95% CI −0.1062 to 0.0956) (28). In a subgroup analysis of the Caravaggio trial, the incidence of VTE recurrence was higher in patients with metastatic cancer (8.7%, HR = 3.3) (29). These results suggest that apixaban, unlike other DOACs, appears to have a similar risk of bleeding in CA-VTE treatment compared with dalteparin. The use of apixaban still requires caution, and further studies regarding which DOAC is likely to have a lower risk of bleeding are necessary, in particular, in patients with higher risks of bleeding. Furthermore, the Caravaggio and ADAM-VTE trials included fewer patients with upper GI tract cancer compared to the Hokusai VTE cancer (12) and SELECT-D trials. A systematic review and meta-analysis of the SELECT-D, Hokusai VTE, ADAM-VTE, and Caravaggio trials (30) showed that both apixaban (odds ratio [OR] 0.41, 95% credible interval [CrI] 0.16-0.95) and rivaroxaban (OR 0.58, 95% CrI 0.37-0.90) were associated with significantly decreased incidence of VTE recurrence compared with dalteparin. Taken together, these findings suggest that DOAC therapy may have a lower risk of recurrent VTE compared with LMWH for GICA-associated VTE.

Few other retrospective/registry studies have been performed comparing bleeding outcomes in GICA patients’ treatment with DOAC and/or LMWH. Results of those studies showed outcomes similar to the current study. A retrospective review of 281 patients with GICA, CRNMB occurred significantly more often in patients treated with rivaroxaban than with LMWH (24.4% vs 15.3%, respectively; P = .074) (22). The incidence of MB did not differ between groups (5.1% with rivaroxaban and 8.9% with LMWH; P = .296). In a multivariate analysis, rivaroxaban was associated with a higher risk of CRNMB compared with LMWH (HR = 1.904, P = .040). Most patients in the study had pancreatobiliary cancer (47%), followed by stomach cancer (41.6%), which may suggest that these cancer types might be susceptible to bleeding with DOAC. No inter-group difference was observed for rate of VTE recurrence (3.8% with rivaroxaban vs 3.9% with LMWH; P > .999). A similar study by Seo et al (21) analyzed patients with upper GI and hepatopancreatobiliary cancer treated with LMWH (111) or rivaroxaban (78). CRNMB was significant in the rivaroxaban group (29.5% vs 13.5%; HR=2.16) and MB events were numerically higher in the rivaroxaban group (16.7% vs 7.2%; HR=2.29). The recurrent or aggravated VTEs during anticoagulation were not significant between the 2 groups. In another retrospective study, 69 patients on rivaroxaban and 105 on LMWH with upper GI tract (esophageal/gastric) or hepatopancreatobiliary cancer, MB (17.4% vs 7.6%, P = .072), and CRNMB (14.5% vs 6.7%, P = .122) occurred more frequently in the rivaroxaban group (31). Rivaroxaban exhibited similar risk of recurrent/aggravated VTE compared with LMWH (P = .625).

A phase II study assigned 90 patients to DOAC (n=44, apixaban or rivaroxaban) or dalteparin (n=46) with advanced upper GI and hepatopancreatobiliary cancer (32). Higher rates of MB (18.2% vs 4.3%, P = .047) and CRNMB (18.2% vs 8.7%, P = .186) occurred in the DOAC group. The most common site of CRNMB was GI tract. When comparing apixaban to rivaroxaban, apixaban had higher rates of MB (23.1% vs 16.1%). Recurrent CA-VTE occurred in 2.3% and 2.2% of patients given DOAC and dalteparin, respectively (P=1.0). A relatively recent study from the Mayo Clinic prospectively evaluated 1230 patients, including 452 with GICA, reported that apixaban had a higher rate of MB (6%) in GI tract cancer compared to non-GI tract cancer patients and compared to enoxaparin in GI tract cancer patients, whereas rivaroxaban showed no increased risk of MB (4%) in GI tract cancer compared to non-GI tract cancer patients (33). Apixaban was associated with a higher rate of MB in luminal GICA compared with the non-GICA group (15.59 vs 3.26 per 100 person-years; P = .004) but had a lower rate of CRNMB compared with rivaroxaban in patients with GICA (3.83 vs 9.40 per 100 person-years; P = .03). MB rate with rivaroxaban in the GICA/luminal GICA group was similar to that of the non-GICA group (2.04 vs 4.91 per 100 person-years; P = .37). Because this was a prospective observational cohort, there might have been a selection bias to preferentially use apixaban in GICA patients at an increased risk for bleeding owing to previously published reports of bleeding with rivaroxaban in this patient population, later confirmed in the SELECT-D trial. Compared with reported retrospective studies, the present analyses included 300 GICA patients on apixaban and 133 on rivaroxaban. There was a higher number of patients with colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer and upper GICA (esophageal/gastroesophageal/gastric) in both cohorts. MB and CRNMB were lower, including fewer MB in upper GICA. In addition, there was no MB on rivaroxaban for patients with esophageal/gastroesophageal cancers. However, the number of patients included was relatively low to make direct comparisons. In addition, higher incidence of VTE recurrence might be due to differences in the proportion of patients with GICA, metastatic disease, or higher ECOG scores. Overall, it appears that DOACs may be associated with an increased risk of bleeding in patients with upper GICA. While the results of our study are promising, our data is limited by the total number of patients with primary upper luminal GICA and therefore, we would not routinely recommend DOACs for upper GICA at this time. These findings could help in guiding optimal anticoagulation therapy for CA-VTE, particularly in patients at high risk for bleeding because of the cancer site.

Limitations

A major limitation of the current study is its retrospective nature from a single center, in which DOAC was not randomly allocated to patients, and that it was difficult to delineate interruptions in treatment. Patients with carcinoid tumors and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma of the GI tract were excluded from our study and the findings of the study may not apply to these patients. Another limitation is that the study was not powered to detect a significant difference between DOACs, nor comparison with LMWH, owing to a limited available study population, especially upper GI cancer type, given that DOACs are sometimes avoided in patients with upper GICA. Nevertheless, our study included a large sample of patients with high risk GICA types which provides evidence of the observed frequency of bleeding complications with DOAC when used in clinical practice for CAT in GICA patients and suggest that DOAC can be anticoagulant options to consider in those patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, oral apixaban and rivaroxaban could be considered as anticoagulant options for the treatment of VTE in patients with GICA, however, additional multi-center prospective clinical studies are needed to assess the clinical benefit and safety of extended treatment using DOACs in patients with GICA and VTE.
