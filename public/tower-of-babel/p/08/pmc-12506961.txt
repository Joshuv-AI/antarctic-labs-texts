CRISPR/Cas9-germline editing of Biomphalaria glabrata: A breakthrough in genetic modification of snails that transmit schistosomiasis
Authors: Kouhei Oonuma, Reiko Kuroda, Takayuki Uchida, Si-Ming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Human schistosomiasis remains one of the most devastating parasitic diseases worldwide, and the development of genetically modified vector snails has long been a goal in the field. Here, we report the successful creation of genetically modified Biomphalaria glabrata, an important intermediate host, using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing. We targeted the fibrinogen-related protein 3.1 (FREP3.1) gene, confirmed stable germline transmission of the mutated gene, and established two different homozygous FREP3.1-edited lines. Disruption of the FREP 3.1 gene did not alter snail susceptibility to Schistosoma mansoni infection, possibly due to a limited role of FREP3.1 in resistance or to functional redundancy and/or compensatory expression within the highly diverse FREP gene family. Our study demonstrates successful germline editing, effective ex ovo culture of decapsulated embryos, and the generation of viable, genetically modified B. glabrata snails, thereby establishing a foundation for future genetic strategies to control schistosomiasis.

INTRODUCTION

Schistosomiasis continues to affect approximately 240 million people worldwide, primarily in developing countries (1–3). Current treatment relies exclusively on praziquantel (PZQ), a drug that has been used for about four decades (4). However, PZQ is not a cure-all; it is ineffective against juvenile schistosomes and does not prevent reinfection (5, 6). Moreover, no effective vaccine is currently available.

Transmission of schistosomiasis depends on freshwater snails, which serve as obligate intermediate hosts for the schistosome parasites (1–3). In the absence of suitable snails or when snails do not support schistosome development, transmission cannot occur. Therefore, interrupting the parasite’s life cycle at the intramolluscan stage represents a promising strategy for snail-targeted control programs. Achieving this goal requires germline modification technologies to alter genes of interest. Since the first reports of transgenesis in the early 1980s (7, 8), germline modification has advanced to include modern CRISPR-based genome editing (9–13). However, despite over four decades of research, germline modification has not been accomplished in any schistosomiasis vector snails. This challenge largely stems from the snails’ unique reproductive biology: Successful germline modification requires direct access to and manipulation of germline cells—eggs, sperm, or embryos.

Three snail genera, Biomphalaria, Bulinus, and Oncomelania, are responsible for the global transmission of schistosomiasis. Among these, Biomphalaria and Bulinus (Gastropoda: Planorbidae) account for the vast majority of cases. These hermaphroditic planorbid snails produce both male and female gametes within a shared reproductive organ, the ovotestis. Fertilization occurs internally, using either allosperm or autosperm, making in vitro fertilization and direct genetic manipulation of gametes highly challenging. Following fertilization, a single embryo is enclosed within a capsule filled with albumen gland fluid, at least 100 times the volume of the embryo itself. Multiple capsules form an egg mass (also referred to as a cocoon or egg patch), which is deposited in freshwater (Fig. 1). Embryonic development proceeds slowly, and these reproductive traits render techniques such as electroporation of eggs, sperm, or embryos largely impractical.

Microinjection of embryos offers a potential path forward for germline modification in planorbid snails, yet it poses substantial technical challenges. Once decapsulated, the embryos (measuring only ~0.1 mm in diameter) are extremely fragile and highly susceptible to mechanical damage during injection. Furthermore, because fertilization occurs in vivo, microinjection must be performed immediately after egg laying to minimize mosaic editing. This challenge is compounded by the removal of the protective capsule, which deprives the embryos of albumen fluid, an essential source of nutrients and antimicrobial defense. Consequently, past efforts to culture intact decapsulated embryos ex ovo have consistently failed, with survival not exceeding 3 days, well short of the approximately 7 days required for hatching (14–16).

Despite these obstacles, we have successfully produced a germline-modified planorbid snail, Biomphalaria glabrata, an important intermediate host for the human blood fluke Schistosoma mansoni and a well-established model for schistosomiasis research (17–20). This was achieved through the microinjection of Cas9 mRNA along with guide RNA (gRNA) targeting the fibrinogen-related protein 3.1 (FREP3.1) gene, a putative immune gene (21), into decapsulated embryos, followed by ex ovo culture (EOC).

RESULTS

Expression of exogenous EGFP in B. glabrata embryos

To assess the efficiency of microinjecting decapsulated embryos and evaluate the expression of exogenous genes, we first introduced enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) mRNA into B. glabrata embryos before the cleavage stage. Fertilized eggs were removed from their capsules and gently immobilized using a holding pipette (Fig. 2A). EGFP mRNA, mixed with Fast Green FCF dye, was microinjected into embryos at the one-cell (Fig. 2B) or two-cell stage (Fig. 2C), and fluorescence expression was subsequently monitored.

(A) Microinjection into a decapsulated embryo. The green coloration inside the embryo indicates injected Fast Green FCF dye (white arrowhead). The black arrowhead indicates the glass needle. (B) Enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) fluorescence detected at the blastula and trochophore larval stages after injection at the one-cell stage. (C) EGFP mRNA was injected into one blastomere at the two-cell stage, and green fluorescence was monitored hourly postinjection. Representative images show bright field (BF), Fast Green FCF dye, EGFP fluorescence, and merged views at 10, 70, 250, and 370 min postinjection (mpi). Scale bars, 50 μm.

When EGFP mRNA was injected into one blastomere at the two-cell stage, fluorescence was restricted to half of the embryo, corresponding to the lineage of the injected blastomere, confirming specific expression rather than background autofluorescence (Fig. 2C). EGFP fluorescence was detectable as early as 70 min postinjection and increased in intensity over time, remaining visible through the trochophore larval stage (Fig. 2B). These results demonstrate a successful microinjection protocol enabling exogenous gene expression in early-stage B. glabrata embryos, with an average success rate of approximately 50%.

EOC of decapsulated embryos following microinjection

We next evaluated whether viable juvenile snails could be obtained from decapsulated embryos following microinjection. Injected embryos were individually placed and incubated in glass capillaries, using a modified protocol based on methods previously developed for Lymnaea stagnalis by Kuroda et al. (22). B. glabrata embryos successfully developed into juveniles within the capillaries (Fig. 3). Upon reaching the juvenile stage, the snails were transferred to artificial pond water [APW; Biomedical Research Institute (BRI), www.afbr-bri.org] for further growth. These juveniles continued to develop normally into adult snails. The success rate of EOC ranged from 0 to 75%, with an overall average of approximately 20%.

CRISPR/Cas9–mediated knockout of the FREP3.1 gene

Following the successful establishment of microinjection and EOC techniques, we selected FREP3.1 as the target gene to demonstrate CRISPR/Cas9–mediated genome editing in B. glabrata because FREP3.1 belongs to the FREP gene family. Some members of the FREP family have been found to play an important role in host defense against trematode parasites, including S. mansoni (23–27). Although FREP3.1 has been annotated in both our recently assembled iM line and iBS90 genomes (18, 20), its functional role in host-parasite interactions remains unclear, and the complementary DNA (cDNA) sequence had not been reported. We therefore performed 5′ and 3′ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) to determine the complete transcript sequence, including the untranslated regions (UTRs). The full-length FREP3.1 cDNA sequence was submitted to GenBank (accession number: PV271791) (fig. S1).

A gRNA was designed to target the first exon of the FREP3.1 gene (Fig. 4A). Cas9 mRNA and gRNA were co-injected into fertilized BS90 strain eggs, and embryos were cultured ex ovo. Once the injected embryos developed into juveniles (~2 mm in shell length), genomic DNA was extracted from their tentacles, a noninvasive method that allowed the snails to remain alive. A heteroduplex mobility assay (HMA) was performed to identify gene-edited individuals. This is a reliable technique for detecting CRISPR-induced indels by observing a reduced mobility on nondenaturing polyacrylamide gels of DNA heteroduplexes formed between related DNA sequences (28).

After optimizing the experimental conditions, we successfully generated FREP3.1 knockout snails. HMA analysis of seven G0 snails (labeled nos. 1 to 7), developed from seven individually injected embryos, revealed five mutants (i.e., G0 snails nos. 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5) as they showed extra band(s) in addition to the WT band. No mutations were introduced to snails G0 nos. 6 and 7 (Fig. 4B). One mutant G0 snail was self-fertilized to produce F1 progeny for inheritance analysis, and HMA and mixing HMA (mHMA) analyses of six F1 snails derived from the G0 snail were carried out (Fig. 4C). The mHMA, which uses mixed polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products produced from each F1 snail and a WT snail, can reveal whether the mutants include the wt allele or not. HMA and mHMA together indicated that no. 2 is a homozygous mutant (mut1/mut1, mut = mutation) and nos. 1, 3, 4, and 5 are heterozygous mutants that include the wt allele, with different mutations for no. 1 (mut1) and for nos. 3, 4, and 5 (mut2). Snail no. 6, on the other hand, is a heterozygous mutant with two different mutations (mut1/mut2). By self-fertilizing a heterozygous mutant (no. 6 in Fig. 4C), we established two homozygous mutant lines for mutation 1 and mutation 2. HMA and mHMA of the progeny from homozygous mutant lines showed inherited homozygosity (Fig. 4D). Their nucleotide sequences were determined by Sanger sequencing of the target region as shown in the chromatograms (Fig. 5A). The nucleotide sequences of the two homozygous mutants (mut1/mut1, mut2/mut2) were designated as del4/del4 and del10/del10. Analysis of the FREP3.1 cDNA sequences from these mutants confirmed the deletions and showed no evidence of alternative splicing (Fig. 5B).

To further assess gRNA efficiency, we sequenced DNA from embryos that died during EOC. Various mutations were detected, including del7 + ins4, del4, del13, and del23 (fig. S4). Off-target prediction using the CHOPCHOP web tool (29–31) identified three potential off-target sites, all located in intergenic regions, suggesting minimal risk of off-target effects.

The homozygous mutant lines (del4/del4 and del10/del10) were maintained separately and showed no observable differences from wild-type (WT) snails in egg laying, fertilization, embryonic development, or survival. These results confirm that CRISPR/Cas9 can efficiently induce targeted mutations in the B. glabrata genome and that such mutations are stably inherited.

Evaluation of FREP3.1 knockout effects in BS90 snails on schistosome development

To assess whether disruption of FREP3.1 affects susceptibility to S. mansoni infection, we conducted four infection experiments using two homozygous FREP3.1 mutant lines (del4/del4 and del10/del10), WT BS90, and iM line. It is well established that BS90 is completely resistant to S. mansoni, whereas the iM line is fully susceptible (18). Each experiment included 24 juvenile snails per group (shell diameter: 4 to 6 mm). In all four experiments, no cercarial shedding was observed in either of the two mutant lines or in WT BS90 snails. At 60 days postexposure (dpe), 22 to 24 snails per group survived and remained negative for shedding. In contrast, the iM line consistently showed high susceptibility: cercarial shedding rates were 100% (19 of 19), 96% (21 of 22), 100% (18 of 18), and 100% (20 of 20) across the four trials. These results indicate that knockout of FREP3.1 alone is insufficient to alter the resistant phenotype.

DISCUSSION

The Nobel Prize–winning CRISPR technology has revolutionized genome engineering in agriculture, medicine, and life sciences (9, 10, 12, 32–34). Although gene drives represent a distinct application of CRISPR, they have shown great promise in controlling vector-borne diseases (10, 35–38). However, germline CRISPR–mediated gene editing has not yet been applied to any snails that transmit schistosomiasis. Even within the phylum Mollusca, the second-largest in the animal kingdom, germline CRISPR editing remains rare, particularly among bivalves and gastropods (39–41). Most existing studies have been limited to embryonic-stage gene editing, and live gene–edited mollusks have not been reported to date, with the sole exception of the gastropod L. stagnalis (42) at the time of submission. Building on decades of work in molluscan developmental biology (22, 43), Abe and Kuroda (42) applied CRISPR/Cas9 to L. stagnalis to investigate the genetic basis of chirality. The current study extends this capability to B. glabrata, a medically important vector for S. mansoni. This is facilitated by the anatomical and developmental similarities in the gametes and reproductive systems between Lymnaea and planorbid snails (15, 44). This advancement represents a key step toward genetic-based biocontrol strategies targeting schistosome-transmitting snails.

We established a CRISPR/Cas9–based platform in B. glabrata through direct microinjection of Cas9 mRNA and gRNA into decapsulated embryos, followed by EOC. From these efforts, we obtained viable FREP3.1-disrupted snails, bred them to establish stable homozygous lines, and evaluated gene function. Interest in genetically modified B. glabrata has surged, yet only two prior studies have reported CRISPR-related applications in this species. Coelho et al. (45) introduced Cas9 and gRNA constructs into B. glabrata Bge cell lines via electroporation, targeting the allograft inflammatory factor gene (BgAIF) and confirming gene function in vitro (45). More recently, Luviano et al. (46) injected dCas9-SunTag-DNMT3A plasmids and gRNAs into yolk sacs (capsules) at or after the gastrula stage, achieving transient epigenetic modifications (46). Their method, which relies on capsule injection and transfection reagents, differs substantially from our approach in three ways: First, we directly injected Cas9 mRNA and gRNA into decapsulated embryos, while they used capsule injection along with a transfection reagent to introduce genetic material into embryos. Second, their work induced nonheritable DNA methylation, while our method caused permanent, heritable gene disruptions. Third, our injections were performed at the one- to two-cell stage, reducing mosaicism compared to later-stage editing.

To demonstrate functional CRISPR editing, we targeted the FREP3.1 gene in the schistosome-resistant BS90 strain. We hypothesized that FREP3.1 contributes to resistance and that knocking it out would reverse this phenotype. Previous work has largely focused on other FREP family members, in particular FREP3.2 (AY028461) (24–26, 47). Although structurally similar, both containing two immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) domains and a fibrinogen (FBG) domain, FREP3.1 and FREP3.2 appear to belong to different subfamilies, as defined by an 86% nucleotide identity threshold in IgSF regions (48). Our BLASTx search indicated only 84% identity in IgSFs and 79% overall between the two genes (figs. S2 and S3). FREP3.1 is located on chromosome 13 (20) and was annotated in both the iM and iBS90 lines (GenBank accessions: KAK6966023.1 and KAI8747679.1, respectively) through bioinformatic pipelines (18, 20). Future naming conventions may change pending a comprehensive analysis of the FREP gene family in the B. glabrata genome.

Contrary to our hypothesis, the disruption of FREP3.1 in BS90 snails did not reverse resistance to S. mansoni. This result, although unexpected, is likely due to the complexity of the FREP gene family. Genomically, this family comprises various subfamilies with different gene counts (20, 21, 49, 50). Its diversity is further increased by alternative splicing, retrotransposition, and somatic diversification (48, 51). Structurally, FREPs exist in two major forms, one with a single IgSF domain and another with two, capable of forming thousands of multimeric combinations (21, 52). Functionally, FREPs may act independently or interact with other molecules, such as thioester-containing proteins, to mediate immune responses (26, 27). Our previous studies suggest that some FREPs also have functions beyond antiparasitic defense (53). Therefore, the absence of an altered phenotype in FREP3.1 mutants may result from functional redundancy among FREP members or compensatory upregulation of related genes, as genetic compensation and functional redundancy have been observed in many genes, especially in large gene families (54–56). It is also possible that FREP3.1 does not play a central role in resistance, a notion consistent with our earlier thoughts on the functional diversity of the FREP gene family (21, 53).

To our knowledge, this study represents the first successful germline genetic modification in a schistosomiasis vector snail, B. glabrata, with broad implications for both basic research and vector control strategies. Existing gene functional assays in snails rely predominantly on RNA interference (RNAi) (24, 25, 57–59). While RNAi can transiently reduce gene expression, it often fails to eliminate it completely, and phenotypic effects may be subtle or undetectable (60). In contrast, CRISPR-mediated knockout enables stable, heritable, and complete loss-of-function mutations, making it a more robust tool for gene function studies. This work opens the door to gene drive development in schistosome-transmitting snails. Such technology holds transformative potential for reducing or eliminating the transmission of snail-borne diseases, including schistosomiasis (61, 62).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Snails and parasites

The BS90 strain of B. glabrata used for gene editing is pre-iBS90 snails, which have undergone 31 generations of selfing and are collectively referred to as BS90 snails in this study. These snails have been maintained and bred in the Kuroda and Zhang laboratories. The PR1 strain of S. mansoni was used for functional infection assays.

Generation of plasmid DNA and RNA preparation

The pCS2-nCas9n vector (Addgene plasmid no. 47929) (63) and pCS2-EGFP (42) were used in this study. Both plasmids were linearized using the Not I restriction enzyme, after which EGFP and Cas9 mRNAs were synthesized in vitro using the mMessage mMachine SP6 kit (AM1340, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Dual RNA guides (Alt-R CRISPR-Cas9 crRNA and Alt-R CRISPR-Cas9 tracrRNA) were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies Inc. (IDT). The crRNA sequence targeting the FREP3.1 gene (5′-TTGGCTGGTTCAGGTAATTA-3′) was designed by IDT. Potential off-target sites within the B. glabrata genome were predicted using the CHOPCHOP version 3 web tool (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no) (29–31).

Cloning of FREP3.1 cDNA

To obtain the full-length cDNA sequence of the FREP3.1 gene, including its 5′ and 3′ UTRs, we performed 5′ and 3′ RACE. Total RNA was isolated from an adult snail using the NucleoSpin RNA Plus kit (740984, Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the SMARTer RACE 5′/3′ Kit (634858, Takara, Japan) and used for PCR amplification with the following primers: forward primer for 5′ RACE (universal primer mixture provided with the kit), and reverse primer for 5′ RACE (5′-TGTGTCCAGGAATCCAAAGCTATGAGC-3′; and forward primer for 3′ RACE (5′-TCACAGGTGCATGGTGGTACAAATC-3′), and reverse primer for 3′ RACE (universal reverse primer provided with the kit). A second round of PCR was performed using the first-round product as the template, with the following primers: reverse primer for 5′ RACE (5′-TAGAACTAGTGGATCCGAGATTTGATAACATCAAGATGTTG-3′), forward primer for 3′ RACE (5′-TAGAACTAGTGGATCCCTCCTTTGTAGAGATGAAAATAAGAG-3′), and common primer (5′-CGGGCCCCCCCTCGAGCTAATACGACTCACTATAGGGC-3′). The resulting DNA fragments were cloned into the Bam HI/Xho I sites of the pBluescript II SK(+) vector using the In-Fusion HD cloning kit (639648, Clontech Laboratories).

To isolate the FREP3.1 coding sequence spanning the CRISPR target region (containing 4- and 10-nucleotide deletions in exon 1) from WT and two mutant snail lines, total RNA was extracted from each genotype using the NucleoSpin RNA Plus kit. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of RNA using the SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System (18091050; Thermo Fisher Scientific). PCR amplification of FREP3.1 cDNA was carried out with the following primers: forward primer (5′-GCTCTAGAACTAGTGGATCCATGGCGAGATTATTTTCACG-3′) and reverse primer (5′-GTACCGGGCCCCCCCTCGAGATGAGCCTTGACCAAGTATGAG-3′) (vector-derived sequences are underlined). These fragments were cloned into the Bam HI/Xho I sites of pBluescript II SK(+) using the In-Fusion HD kit (Clontech Laboratories).

Microinjection and incubation

Fertilized eggs were removed from their capsules using a tungsten needle and immobilized with a holding pipette (5195000.036, Eppendorf) connected to a micromanipulator (MMO-4, Narishige). Gentle suction stabilized the eggs during injection. To test exogenous EGFP gene expression, EGFP fluorescence was monitored hourly after injection using a fluorescence microscope (BZX800, Keyence Co. Japan). For gene-editing experiments, Cas9 mRNA (75 ng/μl) and cr/tracr RNAs (10 μM) were prepared in nuclease-free water. Approximately 8.2 pl of this RNA mixture, either alone or supplemented with EGFP mRNA (500 ng/μl) and fast green FCF dye, was injected into fertilized eggs using a glass needle with an open diameter of 15 μm. Injected embryos were individually incubated in glass capillaries at 27°C, following the method described (22). Approximately 2.5 μl of natural capsule fluid, collected from 11 egg capsules, was used as the culture medium for each injected embryo. On the basis of previous observations that B. glabrata larvae hatches in 7 to 8 days at 27°C, we adopted this time frame as the criterion for transferring juveniles from the capillaries and rearing them to maturity in APW at 27°C. The APW was prepared using BRI’s recipe: 0.462 μM FeCl3, 0.219 mM CaCl2, 0.101 mM MgSO4, 0.312 mM KH2PO4, and 14.19 μM (NH4)2SO4.

Genotyping and sequencing

When juvenile snails reached approximately 2 mm, genomic DNA was extracted from their tentacles using a modified protocol from Meeker et al. (64). Each tentacle was lysed in 10 μl of 50 mM NaOH at 95°C for 5 min, then cooled and neutralized with 10 μl of 0.2 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0). For the HMA, the following PCR primers were used: forward, 5′-CTCTCTATGCTTTAAGTGGTTCCTTG-3′; and reverse, 5′-CGTAAATATGGACGCAAACATTTC-3′. PCR amplicons were denatured at 95°C for 5 min and cooled at 0.1°C/s before electrophoresis on 8% polyacrylamide gels.

Once the snails grew larger after the HMA test, genomic DNA was extracted from their tentacles. The genomic region surrounding the CRISPR target was amplified from genomic DNA of both WT and snails developed from injected embryos using the forward primer: 5′-CGGGCCCCCCCTCGAGCTCTCTATGCTTTAAGTGGTTCCTTG-3 and reverse primer: 5′-TAGAACTAGTGGATCCGTAAATATGGACGCAAACATTTC-3′ (vector-derived sequences are underlined). The PCR products were cloned into Bam HI/Xho I sites of pBluescript II SK(+) using the In-Fusion HD kit. Inserts were sequenced via Sanger sequencing to determine the genotype.

Schistosome infection

Two FREP3.1 knockout BS90 lines and WT BS90 snails were transferred from Chubu University to the Center for Evolutionary and Theoretical Immunology at the University of New Mexico (UNM) for infection assays. Each experiment included three groups: two mutants, one WT, and one M line, with 24 snails (4 to 5 mm shell diameter) per group. Each snail was individually exposed overnight to 20 miracidia of PR1 S. mansoni. Postexposure, snails were maintained in large aquatic tanks with APW. Cercarial shedding was monitored at 40, 50, and 60 days dpe following established protocols (65). All procedures were conducted under approved institutional animal care and use committee protocols at UNM.
