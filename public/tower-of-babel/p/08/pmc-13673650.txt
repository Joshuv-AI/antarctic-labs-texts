Does a state mandate translate into jail policy? Evidence from a policy surveillance study of opioid use disorder care in Colorado jails
Authors: Paul J Christine, Jarratt D Pytell, Alena Gabrielson, Selena Muñoz-Jones, Betsy Craft, Hayley Rotello, Ingrid A Binswanger, Katherine LeMasters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with opioid use disorder (OUD) are disproportionately represented in US jails, yet access to evidence-based treatment remains limited. In May 2022, Colorado enacted House Bill 22-1326, which mandated that all county jails provide medications of OUD (MOUD). This study sought to describe Colorado jail policies for OUD treatment after the 2022 state mandate and explore differences by county characteristics.MethodsWe conducted a policy surveillance study of all Colorado jail policies, including public-facing and requested policies, for the treatment of OUD between June and October 2024. We selected the OUD Cascade of Care framework a priori to guide data abstraction and identified jail policies for MOUD availability, peer support services, harm reduction services, and linkage to community-based treatment at the time of release.ResultsOf Colorado’s 46 county jails, 28 (61%) had public-facing policies and/or provided information upon request. Of the 28 policies reviewed, OUD screening (n = 20; 71%), MOUD provision (n = 19; 67%), and post-release linkage to care (n = 17; 63%) were most frequently described. Additional services included counseling (n = 16; 59%), peer support services (n = 7; 26%), and harm reduction interventions (n = 6, 22%). Urban jails generally had more policy elements than rural jails.ConclusionsAlthough MOUD is mandated in all Colorado jails, nearly two in five jails lack publicly available policies on OUD treatment and a minority of available policies indicated that all forms of MOUD were provided. Greater transparency may improve accountability and provide opportunities to support jails in implementing model policies to ensure evidence-based OUD treatment is available for all incarcerated individuals.

Introduction

People with opioid use disorder (OUD) are over-represented in the carceral system (Bronson et al., 2020; Raggio et al., 2017). Nearly 10% of emergency medical services calls for opioid overdose result in incarceration and 21% of people who experience a fatal overdose were incarcerated in the prior two years (Victor et al., 2022). Medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) – including methadone, buprenorphine, and extended-release naltrexone – have been shown to reduce overdose deaths and re-incarceration (Cates & Brown, 2023; Lee et al., 2016). Despite the evidence of efficacy, fewer than half of US jails offered MOUD in 2023 (Flanagan Balawajder et al., 2024) .

In May 2022, Colorado passed House Bill 22-1326, The Fentanyl Accountability and Prevention Act (Fentanyl Accountability And Prevention Act, 2022). In addition to increasing criminal penalties for fentanyl possession and providing additional funding for community-based naloxone distribution, the legislation required that jails provide OUD care, including: (1) developing, implementing, and publishing an MOUD policy; (2) offering MOUD services to eligible individuals; and (3) ensuring continuity of care at release, with full implementation mandated by July 1, 2023. While more states consider mandating MOUD availability in jails (Kramer et al., 2025), the implementation of state-mandated MOUD policies in jails and their translation into jail-specific practices remains understudied (Pivovarova et al., 2022), making Colorado’s legislation a valuable case study for examining this process. Importantly, Colorado is a geographically large state with dense urban and remote rural areas, allowing for examination of MOUD policies in urban and rural jails which often have differential access to community MOUD treatment (Morenz et al., 2025) and staff attitudes towards MOUD (Thomas et al., 2024). This study aimed to describe Colorado jail policies and services for OUD treatment one year after the passage of Colorado’s law and to explore differences between urban and rural jails.

Methods

We conducted a policy surveillance study of all Colorado jails to describe the presence and content of policies related to OUD care. Policy surveillance involves the “systematic collection, analysis, and dissemination of information about laws and other policies of importance to health” (Burris et al., 2016). Similar methods have been used to document jail and prison policies across the United States (Maner et al., 2022; Martinez-Kratz et al., 2025). The study protocol and data abstraction tool were previously published online and are briefly describe here (Christine, 2025). We first reviewed each county and jail website for policies, including those related to health care, behavioral health, and intake and release processes. We then contacted all 46 active county jails by email or phone using the publicly available contact information up to three times to confirm information available online (i.e. that visible on jail websites) and inquire about additional publicly available information (e.g. could they share information on their policies). Data collection, including website review and jail outreach and follow up, occurred between June and October 2024 which was one year after the passage of the Fentanyl Accountability and Prevention Act.

We used the OUD cascade of care framework to guide data analysis. The OUD cascade of care is a conceptual framework that describes the sequential steps required for people with OUD to receive effective, continuous, and lifesaving MOUD treatment (Williams et al., 2022), and has previously been used to assess MOUD treatment in jail settings (Ray et al., 2022). We applied the cascade of care framework to describe and categorize jail policies into the following a priori-defined domains: screening for substance use and withdrawal, initiation and continuation of all FDA-approved MOUD (methadone, buprenorphine, and extended-release injectable naltrexone [“naltrexone”]), and care coordination and linkage to post-release care. We noted policies that referenced MOUD without mentioning specific medications, and classified MOUD policies according to whether they addressed initiation of treatment during incarceration, continuation of MOUD that was started prior to incarceration, or both. We also identified policies for providing medications that treat opioid withdrawal symptoms, which is a part of generally accepted medical practice when caring for people with OUD (Cunningham et al., 2020). Finally, we identified other a priori-defined evidence-based interventions that are not explicitly categorized in the OUD cascade of care framework, including counseling, peer support services (e.g. meetings led by non-clinical, behavioral health workers with lived experience of recovery from substance use disorders and criminal legal involvement), and evidence-based harm-reduction interventions (e.g. naloxone distribution at release) (Ellis et al., 2024).

Two investigators (AG, SMJ) independently reviewed all policy documents using the same a priori policy categorizations detailed above. Discrepancies in the tabulation and categorization of policies were resolved by a third investigator (PJC, JDP, KL) supporting intercoder reliability. Descriptive statistics are presented overall and stratified by county geographic classification (urban or rural, as defined by the Colorado Department of Local Affairs, with rural corresponding to counties with no city over 50,000 residents) due to prior evidence that rural jails may have additional barriers to providing MOUD such as lower density of MOUD providers in the community (Colorado Department of Local Affairs, 2026; McNeely et al., 2024). All frequencies and proportions refer to the number of jails with a policy covering the OUD-related topic of interest, rather than the number of policies themselves, given that some jails could have multiple policies covering the topics of interest. We additionally describe the presence of local treatment resources in each county, including whether there was at least one clinician who prescribed buprenorphine for the treatment of OUD in 2023 or at least one opioid treatment program providing methadone in 2024, based on publicly available public health and behavioral health data sources (Colorado Department of Local Affairs, 2026; Colorado Department of Public Health, 2023; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). County level data on naltrexone availability were not available; however, naltrexone is not a controlled substance and may be prescribed by any licensed clinician (Cunningham et al., 2020). The Colorado Multiple Institution Review Board deemed this research exempt.

Results

In 2024 there were 46 active jails in Colorado, which ranged in size from housing to 1,685 incarcerated individuals daily, and are primarily located in rural counties (n = 34, 75%) (Colorado Division of Criminal Justice, 2024). In the corresponding counties, 23 (82%) had a clinician who prescribed buprenorphine and 11 (40%) had an opioid treatment program providing methadone. Notably, only 2 (12%) of 17 rural counties had an opioid treatment program (12%) compared to 9 (80%) of the urban counties.

Jail policy availability

Overall, 28 (61%) jails’ policies were collected, including 11 of the 12 urban jails (92%) and 17 of the 34 rural jails (50%) (Table 1). Among the 28 jail policies collected, 24 (86%) had information publicly available through their county’s jail website, 14 (50%) providing additional information through outreach, and 4 (14%) providing information only through outreach.

OUD care policies

Twenty (71%) policies included a substance use screening process, 12 (44%) specified standardized assessment, and 4 (33%) policies specified treatment for opioid withdrawal: two specified buprenorphine only and two specified both buprenorphine and methadone. The provision of MOUD was included in 19 (67%) jail policies, with 11 (39%) indicating MOUD was initiated, 14 (50%) indicating MOUD was continued, and 11 (39%) indicating MOUD was both initiated and continued. Buprenorphine and methadone were the most common forms of MOUD mentioned in jail policies (n = 15; 54%, for both) followed by naltrexone (n = 13; 46%). Thirteen jails (46%) had policies listing all three MOUD options. For initiation, buprenorphine was the most common (n = 8, 28%) followed by naltrexone (n = 7; 25%) and methadone (n = 3; 10%). Continuation was more prevalent, with 10 jails (36%) continuing both buprenorphine and methadone, and 8 jails (28%) continuing naltrexone. Care coordination of MOUD treatment at release was described in 17 (63%) policies. Jails in urban counties more commonly had policies for screening, providing MOUD, and care coordination of care post-release than rural counties.

Additional support services

More than half of jail policies indicated that individual or group substance use counseling was available (n = 16, 59%), with a higher proportion in urban jails (n = 10, 83%) than rural jails (n = 6, 40%). Harm reduction services were noted in 6 jail policies (22%), with all 6 jail policies including distribution of naloxone at the time of release and 2 of the jail policies including distribution of harm reduction bags with fentanyl test strips and other supplies at the time of release. Peer support services were described in 7 (26%) jail policies.

Discussion

In this policy surveillance study of Colorado jails, we found that despite a state mandate requiring jails to develop and publish policies for MOUD and implement MOUD treatment, 39% of jails lacked publicly available policies addressing MOUD and other OUD treatment services. Among jails with a publicly available policy, only 46% provided all forms of MOUD as mandated in the Fentanyl Accountability and Prevention Act (Fentanyl Accountability And Prevention Act, 2022). Buprenorphine was the most common medication offered, perhaps reflecting greater availability in the community, and in general, continuation of MOUD was more common than initiation, particularly for methadone. Urban jails more commonly included comprehensive services (e.g., counseling, care coordination), which likely reflects their access to resources and local expertise as evidenced by fewer buprenorphine prescribers and opioid treatment programs in rural counties. While harm reduction interventions and peer support services are evidence-based practices, their inclusion in jail policies was rare, with only 22% of jails having a policy regarding naloxone distribution at release, which is consistent with national findings (Maruschak et al., 2023).

A growing number of states are mandating that all jails make MOUD available for individuals with OUD (Longley et al., 2023). How jails operationalize these mandates and how states incentivize and enforce them is an open question. Colorado’s Fentanyl Accountability and Prevention Act in 2022 included no specific funding to help jails with infrastructure or personnel costs. However, the state Behavioral Health Administration’s Jail Based Behavioral Health Services Programs developed model policies to help jails standardize intake, screening, treatment, and post-release care coordination for individuals with OUD, consistent with the OUD cascade of care framework (Williams et al., 2022). Several state and community programs also provided technical assistance programs to aid jails with implementing MOUD policies. Our study suggests that either few jails have adopted these model policies or that many jail MOUD policies were not publicly available, and those that were available rarely incorporated all recommended steps of the OUD cascade of care. Transparency around addiction treatment in jails may help ensure continuity of care by community-based clinicians, promote understanding and advocacy for treatment by loved ones of incarcerated individuals, and aid state and county agencies with technical assistance. It may also help ensure jails are treating incarcerated individuals in line with national clinical standards (Bureau of Justice Assistance, 2023). Furthermore, providing specific funding to help jails with the infrastructure and staff investments needed to support OUD treatment is likely an important determinant of policy adoption and implementation, and may have impacted the ability of Colorado jails to operationalize the state mandate, particularly in rural areas that traditionally have less addiction treatment infrastructure to rely on (McNeely et al., 2024; Morenz et al., 2025).

Limitations

Response bias may have influenced our findings, as jails more compliant with legislative mandates may have been more likely to share information on their websites or after outreach, leading to an overrepresentation of favorable policies. Second, our policy surveillance study considered policies as-written, not whether the policies translated into practices in jail. Complementary methodologies, such as interviews, surveys, and healthcare administrative data (e.g., medication dispensing) would provide greater clarity on jail practices, including gaps in implementation for programs with policies. Third, we did not collect data on protocols for initiation of extended-release naltrexone, which requires an extended opioid-free period prior to initiation to avoid precipitated withdrawal that may complicate its use. Fourth, the majority of policies lacked data on the different methods that jails use to coordinate reentry and post-release continuity of MOUD care, including arranging community appointments, activating insurance (e.g., Medicaid), or providing bridge prescriptions, all of which would benefit from additional study. Fifth, this study did not evaluate prison policies for MOUD, which were not subject to the state mandate under HB 22-1326, but which are similarly important for ensuring guideline-aligned care for incarcerated people with OUD.

Conclusions

Despite a state mandate requiring MOUD in all jails, nearly 2 in 5 Colorado jails lacked identifiable OUD treatment policies and a minority of available policies indicated that all three FDA-approved MOUD options were provided. Supporting jail implementation of comprehensive and transparent MOUD policies may help ensure evidence-based OUD treatment for incarcerated individuals.
