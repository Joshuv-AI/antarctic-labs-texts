4‐Dimethylamino‐1,8‐Naphthalimide as a Fluorescence Signal Amplification for Site Selective and in Situ Probing of the Hydroxyl Radical
Authors: Yelisetty Venkata Suseela, Sabyasachi Mandal, Denis Jacquemin, Thimmaiah Govindaraju, Peter Faller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOverproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) is involved in several diseases. Copper can be an efficient catalysts for ROS generation, as it was suggested for Cu bound to aberrant sites. Determination of rates and type of ROS production in more complex biological media is challenging and often only down‐stream events can be monitored. Here, a selective detection of the hydroxyl radical (HO•), by a demethylation of 4‐dimethylamino‐1,8‐naphthalimide (NMI) fluorescence probe by HO• resulting in ∼25‐fold increase in quantum yield is reported. To investigate potential applications, the NMI was coupled to the Cu‐binding domain of amyloid‐beta (Aβ16). This allowed the in situ fluorescence detection of HO• produced by Aβ16 bound Cu due to the Fenton‐type reaction. In a more complex medium with scavengers present including in cell culture conditions, the HO• produced in the bulk was not detected, in line with the short life‐time of HO• in the presence of other molecules. Thus, this is a proof of concept to show that NMI reaction‐based HO• detection could be a useful and a versatile tool to investigate in situ the role of HO• in a defined location such as Cu bound to Aβ16 in biologically relevant complex systems

Introduction

Metal ions such as copper or iron play a crucial role in the metabolism of ROS, as they are involved in the production and destruction of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Copper ions are involved in keeping O2 •‐ levels under control via SOD1/3, but they can also catalyze the production of ROS. Cu bound to nonnative sites, often referred to as loosely bound or labile, is very prone to catalyze ROS in an uncontrolled manner. This situation occurs in the cases of a general Cu overload, such as in the genetic Wilson disease.[ 1 ] But more local Cu accumulation in nonnative sites could lead to ROS overproduction. This has been suggested for several neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson disease (PD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Oxidative damage was found on neuronal lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, linking oxidative stress to the etiology of these diseases.[ 2 , 3 , 4 ] The over production of ROS such as HO•, O2 •−, and H2O2, is one of the major sources of oxidative stress. Unlike O2 •− and H2O2 that are more stable and more selective in their reactions with biological molecules, the HO• has high oxidative potential (2.8 V) and are fast reacting with little discrimination.[ 5 , 6 ] It has a lifetime of 2 ns in aqueous solution with a radius of diffusion of about 20 Å.[ 7 ] Thus, they react with most compounds adjacent or proximal to it at near diffusion‐controlled rates (rate constant > 109 M−1s−1). This makes direct detection of HO• in a more complex medium almost impossible, and the mostly used indirect methods based on HO• trapping/scavenging approach and subsequent detection of the product by spectroscopy (luminescence, EPR, absorption, etc) or other methods is still a challenge.[ 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 ]

In AD, loosely and/or nonnative bound Cu able to generate ROS has been reported to be elevated in postmortem AD brains and AD models.[ 12 ] In addition, Cu accumulates in one of the hallmarks of AD, the amyloid plaques, where Cu is mainly bound to the major constituent, the peptide amyloid‐β (Aβ). This Aβ bound Cu has been postulated to be part of the loosely bound Cu that can generate ROS. Indeed, test tube experiments under aerobic conditions show that Cu ions bound to Aβ (Cu‐Aβ) are redox active and can catalytically switch between CuI and CuII in the presence of the physiologically relevant reducing agent ascorbate to generate ROS such as HO•, O2 •−, or H2O2 (Scheme 1A)

Aβ peptide is expected to bind with Cu in the extracellular space when released via proteolytic processing.[ 13 ] In cellulo studies show that Cu‐Aβ is cytotoxic in the presence of ascorbate, and it has been suggested that extracellular production of ROS contribute to the cytotoxicity.[ 14 ] Consistent with this, oxidized forms of Aβ peptide are found in AD plaques.[ 15 ]

These observations suggest that Cu bound to Aβ may contribute to the oxidative stress occurring in AD via the production of ROS. However, Cu‐Aβ catalyzed ROS has been only demonstrated in a buffer by test tube experiments. Rarely are examples of direct in situ monitoring of ROS catalyzed by Cu‐Aβ. Most measurements were mainly restricted to the hydroxyl radical measured by the fluorescence dye carboxy‐coumarin‐3‐carboxylate (CCA). However, only phosphate was found to be a suited buffer, as the other classical buffers (HEPES, Tris) intercept the HO• before they react with the CCA.[ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] Moreover, investigation of the effect of compounds (e.g., chelators) solubilized in DMSO, is problematic as DMSO is a well‐known HO• scavenger.[ 6 ] It appears evident that in situ detection is even more challenging with increasing complexity in the medium. Therefore, direct evidence for ROS production by Cu‐Aβ in more complex systems is lacking. This also makes it difficult to determine if a therapeutic molecule such as an antioxidant, prevents the ROS production by binding to Cu‐Aβ or if it just scavenges the ROS before they can be detected. Therefore, it seems of interest to establish new biological tools to probe Cu catalyzed ROS production more directly in situ for different systems including also the case of Cu‐Aβ in the presence of biological relevant ROS scavengers.

Here, we report a proof of concept for a new fluorescence tool to measure more directly and selectively the HO• production by Cu bound to Aβ1‐16 (Scheme 1A & B). This was achieved by covalently grafting the well‐known 4‐dimethylamino‐1,8‐naphthalimide (NMI) fluorophore[ 20 ] close to the Cu‐binding site in Aβ16 which showed fluorescence signal amplification response (Scheme 1B‐1C) due to HO• induced demethylation of NMI.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of NMI Conjugated Aβ16 Peptides and Their Characterization

This proof of concept study focused on Aβ1‐16 as a valuable counterpart of full‐length Aβ peptide for studying Cu coordination and ROS production since copper binds to N‐truncated part of Aβ (first 16 residues).[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] Indeed, multiple studies showed the very similar ROS production behaviour of Aβ1‐16 and full length Aβ.[ 19 , 24 ] In this report, Aβ1‐16 is termed as Aβ16 for simplicity. To conjugate NMI on Aβ16, two different positions were chosen (position 10 and 4 in place of tyrosine (Tyr) and phenyl alanine (Phe) amino acids respectively) that have been reported to be vulnerable to oxidation during Cu‐Aβ16 catalyzed ROS generation and close enough to the Cu‐binding site on Aβ16 (Scheme 1C and S1).[ 25 ] This strategy of placing the fluorophore close to the Cu(II) binding sphere of the Aβ16 permits the tracking of Cu(II) binding and its release. NMI fluorophore conjugation on Aβ16 at position 10 and 4 resulted in two peptides Aβ16NMI10 and Aβ16NMI4 respectively. The peptides were synthesized in two parts (i) synthesis of glutamate conjugated with NMI in the solution phase and (ii) synthesis of AβNMI peptides through standard solid‐phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) procedures on a Rink amide resin.[ 26 , 27 ] Details of peptide synthesis and characterizations are given in the Experimental Section.

Effect of NMI Conjugation on Cu Interaction With Aβ16NMI and Redox Activity of Cu‐Aβ16NMI

The CuII interaction with the Aβ16NMI peptides was studied by monitoring NMI fluorescence and its quenching by CuII (Figure S1). The NMI conjugated to Aβ16NMI10 showed higher initial fluorescence intensity (λex = 450 nm, λem = 550 nm), compared to Aβ16NMI4 indicating the impact of the peptidic environment. Upon sequential CuII titration, NMI fluorescence of the two Aβ16NMI peptides showed a steady decrease until a plateau is reached at 1:1 stoichiometry verifying 1:1 complex formation of Aβ16 peptide with CuII. The reported conditional affinity of Aβ16 for CuII in the literature has reached consensus value of KD ∼ 10−10 M−1 at pH 7.4.[ 28 ] To evaluate the impact of NMI conjugation on the affinity of Aβ16NMI for CuII, competition experiments were performed for CuII binding between Aβ16 (Aβ1‐16 peptide without NMI) and Aβ16NMI10 (Figure S2A). The results suggested almost equal distribution of CuII between Aβ16NMI10 and Aβ16 peptides confirming their identical affinity.

In the presence of the physiologically relevant reducing agent ascorbate and under aerobic conditions Cu‐Aβ16 can catalyze the oxidation of ascorbate and the subsequent production of ROS. Therefore, the impact of NMI conjugation on ascorbate oxidation was studied by following ascorbate (AscH−) consumption by UV–Visible spectroscopy at 265 nm (εAscH− = 14,500 M−1cm−1) (Figure S2B). The rate of AscH− consumption was compared between Cu‐Aβ16NMI, Cu‐Aβ16 and Cu alone (Figure S2B). Cu alone is highly redox active and oxidizes AscH− at a faster rate under the given conditions with initial AscH− oxidation rate of 16 µMs−1. Cu‐Aβ16 showed a slower initial rate of AscH− oxidation of 3.3 µMs−1 in line with literature of ∼3‐5 µMs−1 under similar conditions.[ 29 , 30 ] Cu‐Aβ16NMI peptides showed similar rates (4.5 µMs−1 for Aβ16NMI10 and 3.3 µMs−1 for Aβ16NMI4) indicating that the NMI moiety does not impact the ability of the bound Cu to oxidize ascorbate.

Detection of ROS by NMI Response

The NMI has been reported as a fluorophore that responds to CuII proximity via quenching the fluorescence signal, in addition to its sensitivity to the environment like polarity.[ 31 , 32 , 33 ] In this work, we report the capability of NMI fluorophore to probe hydroxy radical via a demethylation mechanism. NMI fluorescence (λem at 550 nm) response was monitored to probe the ROS production catalyzed by redox active Cu‐Aβ16NMI complex (Figure 1A and Figure S3‐S4). Initial addition of Cu to Aβ16NMI10 showed quenching of NMI as reported in the literature (Figure S1C).[ 31 ] AscH− was next added to trigger the redox cycling of Cu‐Aβ16NMI10 complex. Interestingly, a more than 4‐fold increase in emission intensity was observed during the AscH− consumption and subsequent ROS production catalyzed by Cu‐Aβ16NMI10 as shown in Figure 1A. The observed fluorescence intensity approached a plateau in ∼30 min. This is consistent with the time (∼39 min) taken to complete AscH− oxidation catalyzed by Cu‐Aβ16NMI10 as shown in AscH− consumption assay (Figure S2B). We hypothesized that the produced ROS (HO•, O2 •−, or H2O2) during the catalytic cycle of Cu‐Aβ16NMI10 is the cause for the amplified emission response of NMI present on Aβ16 peptide. If NMI is assumed to react with one or all the ROS species, then the rate of NMI response is expected to depend on the rate of ROS production.

The rate limiting step for ROS production catalyzed by Cu‐Aβ16 has been suggested to be the first electron transfer from CuI to O2 to form O2 •−.[ 34 ] Adding directly H2O2 eliminates this step and increases the rate of AscH− consumption and consumes only 0.5 AscH− for each HO• produced instead of 1.5 AscH− without H2O2 (Scheme 1A). Hence, Cu‐Aβ16NMI/AscH− system was studied with H2O2 added (Figure 1A and Figure S3‐S4). Indeed, an increase in the rate (gradient of curve) of NMI emission response was observed in presence of Cu‐Aβ16NMI10/AscH− system with added H2O2. Moreover, the fluorescence value at the plateau for NMI increased by twice compared to the NMI response in the system without H2O2 as shown in Figure 1A. It is noted that NMI present at two different positions (4 and 10) showed no major difference in NMI response to the ROS production catalyzed by Cu‐Aβ16NMI peptides (Figure S3). NMI response is also dependent on the amount of AscH− and H2O2 added to the Cu‐Aβ16NMI complex (Figure S5A‐C). AscH‐ concentrations down to 0.5 µM (in presence of H2O2) were sufficient to have signal to noise > 2, indicating that HO• concentration down to 1 µM could be detected (Figure S5C). The limit of detection for HO• using the linear range of graph calculated was 2.5 µM (Figure S5D). The higher the ratio of AscH− or AscH−/ H2O2, the higher is the rate of increase in NMI signal as the system is more efficient in producing HO•. These results tend to indicate that the hydroxy radical is mainly responsible for NMI's enhanced emission response.

Selectivity of NMI for HO

To further identify the selectivity of NMI response to ROS produced by Cu‐Aβ16NMI complex, the enzyme catalase was introduced. Catalase consumes the H2O2 generated and convert it to H2O and O2. Hence catalase competes for the precursor of HO•, but does not affect the production of O2 •− (Figure 1B and S6). In presence of catalase enzyme, the NMI response is significantly inhibited, suggesting HO• and not O2 •− responsible for the fluorescence increase. The residual increase (1.3 fold) of fluorescence intensity is ascribed to incomplete consumption of H2O2 by catalase, rather than reaction with O2 •−. H2O2 addition alone as a control showed no change in response of NMI present on Aβ16NMI10. Thus, absence of NMI signal in presence of H2O2 scavenger (catalase enzyme) is rationalized by the unavailability of HO• production in the system and rules out H2O2 and superoxide (O2 •−) as possible sources of NMI response. This indirectly confirms HO• as a main source for NMI emission response. The selectivity of NMI response to HO• was further studied by screening with other oxidizing potential species like O2 •−, HOCl, singlet oxygen (1O2) and peroxynitrite (ONOO−) (Figure 2C and Figure S7). We utilized the simpler precursor, NMI fluorophore conjugated to the carboxylic acid side chain of glutamate (ENMI) for screening and, ENMI showed no NMI response to other species, further nailing down its selectivity to HO•.

Molecular Mechanism

To gain more insights into the mechanism of NMI emission response, we utilized the precursor ENMI to study the effect of HO•. For that we used a classical EDTA‐FeIII / H2O2 / AscH− Fenton‐like system to produce HO•. NMI of ENMI also showed moderate increase (up to 4‐fold) in response to HO• production similar to NMI present on Aβ16NMI (Figure S8). The rate of increase in ENMI fluorescence response (30 min to reach saturation) is also in coherence with the rate of HO• production. NMI response is thus envisaged to chemical modification prompted by HO•. The reaction of ENMI with HO• was monitored using LC‐MS at different time intervals and identified an evolution of a new peak with a mass of [M‐14] Da for ENMI (Figure S9). The new product was purified by HPLC and characterized by NMR and mass spectroscopy. The product was identified as the monomethylated ENMI (ENMIDM), proposing a loss of one methyl group from the 4‐dimethylamino substituent on NMI (Figure 2A). Based on the literature on oxidation of aromatic amines with free radicals, we hypothesize the possible mechanism is via N‐oxidation (one electron oxidation) by electrophilic HO• attack leading to radical cations that can be further oxidized to iminium cation which is subsequently hydrolyzed to monomethyl‐NMI (NMIDM) and formaldehyde (Scheme S2).[ 35 , 36 ] The purified ENMIDM indeed showed 25‐fold higher fluorescence response than ENMI at a same absorbance (A450nm = 0.1), thus functioning as a fluorescent probe for HO• (Figure 2B and Figure S10). It has been reported that the presence of mono‐alkyl compared to di‐alkyl amino substituent on naphthalene ring often contributes to increase in quantum yield of 1,8‐naphthalimides.[ 37 ] We verified this by determining the quantum yields of ENMIDM and ENMI, where NMIDM showed a significant increase in the yield (ϕNMIDM = 0.47) compared to NMI (ϕNMI = 0.018). The diminution of fluorescence in case of NMI is presumed to be due to steric interaction between one of the methyl groups of the 4‐dimethylamino substituent and the hydrogen at the 5‐position (the peri effect).[ 37 ]

To better understand the impact of the dimethyl‐amino substituent on the observed quantum yields, TD‐DFT calculations were performed on NMIDM and NMI. Foreseeing the possibility of the creation of a so‐called twisted intramolecular charge transfer state (TICT) conformer in the excited state, we performed relaxed TD‐DFT scans of NMIDM and NMI along the dihedral angle between the amino groups and the aromatic core of the dye angle (Figure 2D). Two energy minima are observed in the case of NMI. The first, the closest from the Franck‐Condon (FC) point, is observed at dihedral angle of 20–30̊ and the second, significantly lower in energy, at 90̊, the latter corresponding to the TICT conformation. In that conformation, there is a very strong CT between the amino group and the naphthalimide core, as illustrated in Figure 2D and S11.

The TICT structure of NMI is indeed calculated to be more stable and the barrier to reach it from the first minimum is very small, ca. 0.1 eV, which should be easily crossed. In other words, after excitation to the lowest singlet excited‐state, NMI will quickly reach the TICT conformation, which is both dark (negligible oscillator strength) and low in energy, which respectively indicate very low radiative constant and efficient nonradiative routes, i.e., a situation very unfavourable for emission. This is in line with reports that is manifested as decrease in the quantum yield for NMI (ϕNMI = 0.018). In contrast, in NMIDM a planar conformation is maintained in the excited state: the energy is increasing along the twist, indicating the absence of stable TICT conformation. The planar conformation is characterized by a sizeable gap with the ground‐state and a large oscillator strength, both consistent with the experimentally observed large quantum yield (ϕNMI = 0.47) (Figure 3B).

In light of HO• induced molecular conversion of ENMI to ENMIDM with increased quantum yield, the concentration of HO• radicals is reflected in the fluorescence intensity of NMIDM measured. This is also supported by previous results with 2–3 times higher response of NMI in case of higher HO• production by high ratio of AscH− or AscH−/ H2O2 in the system (Figure S5). To evaluate the ability of NMI to capture HO• and its degree of conversion to NMIDM, successive fixed amounts of AscH− were added to the same solution of CuAβ16NMI10 and H2O2, after every saturation plateau reached (Figure S10D). The highest fluorescence was obtained during the initial addition showing maximum HO• produced are captured by NMI and further additions showed lower gradient in the curve. The complete conversion of NMI to NMIDM and to reach a final saturation plateau required three successive additions of AscH− (100 µM) indicating no more NMI left to convert on Aβ16NMI. The conversion of NMI to NMIDM present on Aβ16NMI10 was also confirmed by LC‐MS (Figure S12). This also implied the detection limit of Cu‐Aβ16NMI system depends on minimum amount of AscH‐ needed to generate HO•.

Proximity Effect on HO• Detection by NMI

From above studies, NMI presents a convenient sensor for HO•. Since HO• are highly reactive and short‐lived, the presence of fluorophore in proximity to the site of HO• generation is essential for detection (without disturbing too much the Cu coordination). Moreover, this opens the possibility to selectively detect HO• generated by Cu bound to Aβ, and not from other sources.

Therefore, we sought to determine if NMI present on Aβ16NMI can differentiate the HO• generated by bound Cu from other potential sources of radical generation. To address this, the difference in ability of NMI to capture HO• generated in proximity by Cu bound to Aβ16NMI (Cu‐Aβ16NMI/AscH−) or HO• generated in bulk solution were compared. For bulk HO• generation the Fenton‐like reaction catalyzed by EDTA‐FeIII in presence of H2O2 and AscH− was used (Figure 3A).

As shown in Figure 3A, NMI of Aβ16NMI10 showed comparable increase in fluorescence response to HO• produced by both bound Cu and EDTA‐FeIII in phosphate buffer. Adding a HO• scavenger like DMSO into both systems resulted in a marked difference of NMI response. DMSO had no influence on the high NMI response of Aβ16NMI10 when HO• was produced in proximity. While the signal of Aβ16NMI10 is efficiently quenched by the presence of DMSO in case of bulk production of HO• by EDTA‐FeIII. (Figure 3A).

To further confirm, NMI present on ENMI was studied (Figure 3B). As expected ENMI reacted like Aβ16NMI10 with the bulk HO• produced by the EDTA‐FeIII / H2O2 / AscH− system. In contrast, the Cu / AscH− reaction was more like a bulk production, in line with the absence of a strong Cu‐binding site of ENMI. In both cases DMSO was a strong quencher. Besides, ENMI showed little response when added to CuAβ16 (without NMI) under the same conditions (Figure S13). Thus, NMI alone or when separated from Aβ16 does not show any proximal effect and therefore responds to HO• only in the absence of radical scavengers. DMSO is not a physiological compound but can be present at low concentrations when more hydrophobic compounds dissolved in DMSO are added. In biological systems a lot of compounds are capable of scavenging HO•. Indeed, a simple change from phosphate to Tris buffer shows this point (Figure S14). Tris buffer is a better HO• scavenger than phosphate buffer that resulted in quenching of NMI response only in bulk source of HO• (by Fe‐EDTA system) and not proximal source of HO• (by bound Cu).[ 38 ]

Thus, NMI amplified emission response can be considered as an indication of the binding of redox‐active Cu on Aβ16NMI. This was verified by recording NMI response in the presence of EDTA which has a higher affinity for CuII compared to Aβ16.[ 39 , 40 ] EDTA retrieves Cu from Aβ and makes it redox silent. As expected, NMI of Aβ16NMI10 in the presence of EDTA shows negligible response indicating no bound Cu on Aβ16NMI10 (Figure S15).[ 29 ]

In Cellulo Probing of NMI Response to HO•

To measure NMI response to HO• directly produced by bound Cu to Aβ16NMI and not from the bulk source in a system more relevant for biological studies, the fluorescence was measured in presence of live PC12 cells (Figure 4). Such systems have been used in the past to measure impact of Cu‐Aβ in presence of ascorbate for cellular toxicity. PC12 cells were treated with CuAβ16NMI10 or Cu‐Aβ16NMI10/ H2O2 after adding AscH− to initiate the HO• production. NMI of Aβ16NMI10 peptide showed 6‐fold increase in emission response indicating Cu‐catalysed HO• production from the CuAβ complex. Fluorescence response in presence of PC12 cells is in accordance with the enhanced emission response of NMI observed in solution. This shows that NMI can be used to monitor HO• production in cell culture conditions in a qualitative manner. To see whether the NMI monitors only nearby produced HO• catalyzed by bound Cu, we separated NMI from Cu‐Aβ16. For that CuAβ16 (without NMI) and ENMI were added together under the same conditions as for CuAβ16NMI No increase in fluorescence response was observed, in line with the short lifetime of HO• and its scavenging before reaching NMI as the HO• production is not in proximity to NMI. These results are consistent with the test tube solution studies of the proximal effect (Figure 3). Hence, the Aβ16NMI seems to be selective for HO• generated by bound Cu and does not react with HO• produced in the bulk. Hence such systems could be used to probe the binding of Cu and HO• production in cell culture systems. Moreover, this approach could be also useful for other peptides or proteins to probe nearby production of HO• more generally.

Conclusion

In situ monitoring of HO• generation seems of paramount importance in unveiling its chemical and biological impact. Even selective indirect detection of HO• is never simple given its short lifetime. The major challenges in developing a desired HO• sensing probe include i) ability to capture short‐lived HO• ii) selectivity: to efficiently differentiate HO• from other ROS particularly against strong oxidants such as HOCl iii) to maximize the capture of HO• in the presence of various endogenous HO• scavengers in more complex systems.

Reaction‐based sensing approach is inherently attractive to particularly distinguish transient ROS that are constantly being generated and consumed or transformed in biological environments. There have been several small molecular probes reported based on different reaction patterns with HO• radicals in the literature.[ 41 ] Our results report on a new approach of the classical fluorophore NMI to detect selectively HO• produced nearby. The selectivity is based on a new function of an old fluorophore by a chemical reaction leading to a N‐demethylation of NMI and a more than 20‐fold increase in fluorescence quantum yield. This result is an enhanced response, which is preferred over a turn‐off for biological application, particularly in the environment of Cu that mostly quenches fluorophores. Being a strong oxidant in nature, turn‐off response is much easier for HO• due to its high reactivity and hence potential to degrade organic fluorophores. As a proof of concept to illustrate the usefulness of this new method to detect HO• by NMI, the fluorophore was covalently linked to the model peptide Aβ16. The fluorophore was close enough to Cu‐binding sites to detect HO• without substantially disturbing the Cu‐binding. Conjugation of NMI to Aβ16 enabled proximity‐based sensing, where HO• generated by the nearby Cu‐site via Fenton‐type reaction was selectively detected, and not HO• produced in the bulk. This opens the possibility to use NMI as a fluorescence reporter to probe locally produced HO• in real‐time for different applications, not only metal catalyzed ROS production but also to extract hydroxy radical production and kinetics of other processes.[ 42 , 43 ] Although fluorescence signal amplification is less suited than when coupled to a fluorescence shift or compared to a ratiometric probe, NMI is easily accessible and many derivatives are reported. Moreover, since the mechanism of HO• detection was revealed here, it will help to design such improvements. This methodology could also be applied to other CuII binding proteins such as alpha‐synuclein, Tau, and Prion to unravel the real source of HO• production under biological conditions, a critical step in full understanding of oxidative stress in neurological disorders.

Experimental Section

Chemical Synthesis of peptides

The NMI conjugated Aβ16 (Aβ16NMI) peptides were synthesized in two parts (i) synthesis of fluorophore precursor NGlu in the solution phase and (ii) synthesis of peptide through the solid phase protocols.

Synthesis of NGln (Scheme 2)

To a solution of 4‐bromo‐1,8‐naphthene anhydride (1.0 g, 3.61 mmol) in dimethylformamide (DMF, 10 mL), a catalytic amount of copper sulfate (CuSO4) and dimethylamine (2 mL, 15% solution) were added. The reaction mixture was transferred to a pressure tube and subjected to stirring conditions at 90 °C for 6 hours. Upon completion of the reaction, the excess solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The resulting crude product was subsequently precipitated in cold ethanol (25 mL). The obtained product was purified through column chromatography, utilizing ethyl acetate (EtOAc) and hexane as eluents. Starting with compound 2 (200 mg, 0.83 mmol) was dissolved in isopropanol (IPA, 15 mL). To this solution, Boc‐protected ethylene diamine (172.5 mg, 1.1 mmol) and triethylamine (0.23 mL, 1.66 mmol) were added. The reaction mixture was then refluxed for 12 hours under a nitrogen atmosphere. The progress of the reaction was monitored by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC). After completion of the reaction, the excess solvent was evaporated, and the crude product was diluted with water (20 mL). The residue was subsequently extracted into ethyl acetate (3 × 20 mL). The combined organic phase was washed with water (1 × 25 mL) and brine (1 × 30 mL), followed by drying over anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4). Finally, the organic layer was concentrated, and the resulting product (3) was purified using column chromatography, employing a mobile phase of ethyl acetate and hexane. To a stirred solution of compound 4 (215 mg, 0.51 mmol) in dimethylformamide (DMF, 10 mL) at 0 °C, diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA, 0.17 mL, 1.02 mmol), 2‐(1H‐benzotriazol‐1‐yl)‐1,1,3,3‐tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate (HBTU, 232.1 mg, 0.61 mmol), and 1‐hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt, 83.0 mg, 0.61 mmol) were added. The reaction mixture was stirred under a nitrogen atmosphere for approximately 20 minutes. Subsequently, Fmoc‐Glu(OtBu)‐OH (2.15 mg, 0.51 mmol) was added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 5–6 hours at room temperature. Completion of the reaction was monitored by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC). Following the removal of DMF, the crude mixture was diluted with water (25 mL). The resulting residue was then extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 20 mL). The combined organic layers were washed with water (1 × 25 mL) and brine (1 × 25 mL), dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), and evaporated under a vacuum to afford the crude peptide. Intermediate 5 was purified using column chromatography with a mobile phase of DCM and methanol. Finally, compound 5 (0.3 g, 0.43 mmol) was dissolved in DCM (10 mL), and TFA (2 mL) was added. The reaction mixture was stirred for 3 hours at room temperature. Dichloromethane was then removed under vacuum, and the crude NGln was precipitated with cold diethyl ether, ready for use in the solid‐phase synthesis of peptides.

Synthesis of the fluorescent amino acid (NGln).

Synthesis of Aβ16NMI peptides (Scheme S1)

Peptide synthesis was performed with Syro II automated peptide synthesizer via solid‐phase peptide synthesis using Fmoc‐protected amino acids. Rink amide resin (200 mg) was thoroughly washed with dichloromethane (DCM) (3 × 3 mL) and DMF (3 × 3 mL). The resin was then incubated in DCM (3 mL) for 30 min under shaking conditions. The DCM was subsequently removed, and DMF (5 mL) containing 20% piperidine (Pip) was added. The reaction mixture was vigorously shaken for 30 minutes at room temperature. After deprotection, the resin was washed again with DCM (3 × 3 mL) and DMF (3 × 3 mL). Coupling of each aminoacid (2 equiv.) was performed in presence of DIPEA (0.8 mL, 4 equiv), HBTU (118.0 mg, 4 equiv), and HOBt (42.0 mg, 4 equiv) were added to the reaction mixture. Completion of each amino acid coupling was monitored by Kaiser test. The resin was washed with DMF (3 × 3 mL). The resin was cleaved by the addition of 3 mL cocktail solution (95:2.5:2.5‐TFA:DCM:TIPS) with vigorous shaking for 2 hours. After cleavage, the TFA was evaporated under reduced pressure and then one or two drops of methanol were added, and the peptides were precipitated in cold diethyl ether solution. The crude peptides were purified by RP‐HPLC and purity was verified by analytical HPLC (calculated purity was > 95% for both peptides) and characterized by MALDI.

Chemicals and preparation of stock solutions

All the chemicals were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich or Spectrochem and were used without further purification unless otherwise specified. Monobasic and dibasic phosphate buffers (Aldrich), dissolved in ultrapure water to reach 0.5 M concentration were mixed to obtain a pH of 7.4. 4‐(2‐ hydroxyethyl)piperazine‐1‐ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (Aldrich) dissolved in Milli‐Q water (ρ = 18.2 MΩ⋅cm−1) (milliQ) water to reach 0.5 M concentration and adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH or H2SO4. Ascorbate solutions (10 mM and 50 mM) were freshly prepared few minutes prior to each experimental set by dissolving L(+)‐ascorbic acid sodium salt (Aldrich) in milliQ water. The solution was kept on ice between measurements. Cu(II) was used from CuSO4. 5(H2O) and was purchased from Sigma. Stock solution of Cu(II) 10 mM was prepared in milliQ water (18.2 mΩ). The concentration was verified by absorption spectroscopy at 800 nm (ε = 12 M−1 cm−1).

Aβ16NMI peptide stock concentration

The peptides were weighed and dissolved in milliQ water and were stored at ‐20 °C. The concentration of the peptide was initially estimated using the NMI absorption at 450 nm with an extinction molar coefficient of 10, 500 M−1 cm−1. The concentrations were further verified by NMI quenching in presence of known concentration of CuII based on the stoichiometric Cu(II)‐binding to Aβ16.

Absorbance and fluorescence measurements

Absorbance of NMI fluorophore of a solution containing a Aβ16NMI peptide was recorded in a 100 µL quartz cuvette (Hellma) 1 cm optical path, at a certain concentration (classically 10 µM) in a 50 mM HEPES buffer (50mM, pH 7.4) was measured using an electronic Agilent Cary60 spectrophotometer (UV‐Vis). The fluorescence intensity spectra were recorded using a Horiba Fluoromax plus fluorimeter with an excitation wavelength set at 450 nm with a slit width of 2 nm. Fluorescence and absorbance of the samples were measured at 24 °C.

CuII titration

The fluorescence quenching of NMI present on Aβ16NMI peptides was followed using a plate reader (CLARIOstar (BMG LABTECH)) at an excitation and emission wavelengths of 450 and 550 nm, respectively, in a 96 well microplate. 10 µM of Aβ16NMI peptides were titrated with 1 µM of CuII (from a CuII stock solution of 500 µM) CuII solution each time in HEPES buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) until saturation plateau was reached at 1:1 stoichiometry.

Ascorbate consumption kinetics

The AscH‐ oxidation was followed by monitoring absorbance using plate reader (CLARIOstar (BMG LABTECH)). Experiments were carried out in a UV‐STAR 96‐well microplate (Greiner, Half Area clear). The kinetic experiments were conducted at 25 °C in HEPES buffer (50mM, pH 7.4). At this pH, ascorbate exists predominately in the ascorbate monoanion form and throughout this work we use AscH‐ interchangeably to represent the ascorbate monoanion. 100 µM AscH‐ was recorded for 5 min prior to the addition of 10 µM CuII alone or Cu‐Aβ16 1:1.2 or Cu‐Aβ16NMI 1:1.2 complexes and follow the kinetics for next 2 h. The initial oxidation rates of AscH‐ were measured from the decrease of absorbance at 265 nm, where ascorbic acid shows a maximum absorption (ε = 1.5 × 104 M−1cm−1).[ 43 ] Within the initial 200 s, the absorbance decreases linearly with time. The initial reaction rate (n0) is therefore taken as m/εl (where m is the slope of the linear portion of the curve, ε is the ascorbate absorption coefficient at 265 nm, and l is the optical path length of the cuvette).

Self‐competition between Aβ16NMI10 and Aβ16 peptides

Self‐competition measurements were performed by adding Aβ16NMI10 or Aβ16 to the preformed complex of Cu‐Aβ16 or Cu‐Aβ16NMI10, respectively, in HEPES buffer (50mM, pH 7.4). CuII binding and release from peptide Aβ16NMI10 is essentially monitored by following quenching and retrieval of NMI fluorescence (λem at 550 nm), respectively, in presence of Aβ16 (self‐competitor). To calculate the percentage of Cu distribution, EDTA (1 mM) having high affinity for CuII (log K7.4 = 15.9) was added at the end to withdraw the bound Cu out from Aβ16 or Aβ16NMI10. Upon addition of Cu, Aβ16NMI10 showed up to 80% of instant quenching in buffer (for 100% Cu binding). Adding Aβ16 to the formed Cu‐Aβ16NMI10 complex reached an instant equilibrium where 50% retrieval in NMI fluorescence was observed due to loss of 50% Cu from Aβ16NMI10 to Aβ16. In the reverse titration of Aβ16NMI10 to Cu‐Aβ16 complex, a similar equilibrium point was reached where 48–50% of Cu was distributed between Aβ16NMI10 and Aβ16. The almost equal distribution of CuII confirms the affinity constants are not so different for Aβ16NMI10 and Aβ16 peptides.

NMI response to ROS production

NMI fluorescence response of redox active Cu‐Aβ16NMI complexes was monitored using CLARIOstar (BMG LABTECH) plate reader. The excitation and emission wavelengths were set to 450 and 550 nm, respectively. Experiments were carried out in a UV‐STAR 96‐well microplate (Greiner, black full area). The kinetic experiments were conducted at 25 °C in phosphate buffer (50mM, pH 7.4). Initial fluorescence of Aβ16NMI peptide (12 µM) was recorded for 5 min and then added with CuII (10 µM) to pre‐form the complex. AscH‐ (100 µM) was added to trigger the redox cycling of Cu‐Aβ16NMI complex and NMI fluorescence monitored for next 1 h. The reaction was performed in presence or absence of H2O2 (250 µM) added before AscH‐. For catalase inhibition experiments, the reaction was performed with catalase enzyme (2.5 mg/mL) added to Cu‐Aβ16NMI complex. The above conditions were followed while measuring NMI response in reactions with varying concentrations of AscH‐ (25‐500 µM) or H2O2 (250 and 500 µM). The detection limit was calculated using 3 times the standard deviation of the blank (3.3*(σ/m) where σ is standard deviation and m is slope), from the linear range of graph fitted using NMI fluorescence response against varying concentrations of Ascorbate (0.5‐100 µM) in presence of 200 µM of H2O2.

Proximal sensing of HO• by NMI in presence of scavengers

To differentiate the ability of NMI to capture HO• generated in proximity by Cu bound to Aβ16NMI, HO• generated in bulk solution was compared. The HO• generation was performed in phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) conditions. For bulk HO• generation, Fenton‐like reaction was used by adding EDTA‐FeIII (1:1 stoichiometry, 10 µM) to Aβ16NMI (10 µM) peptide in presence of H2O2 (250 µM), followed by adding ascorbate (100 µM) to trigger the reaction. For proximal generation, Aβ16NMI (12 µM) peptide was pre‐complexed with Cu (10 µM) in 50 mM PB and 100 µM ascorbate was added to trigger the reaction in presence of H2O2 (250 µM). The proximal and bulk HO• generation reactions were performed in Tris.HCl buffer (50 mM pH 7.4) or with 1% DMSO added in PB buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4). ENMI was also subjected to similar reaction conditions as above in presence of Cu or EDTA‐FeIII/H2O2.

Purification of ENMIDM

ENMI (500 µM) was subjected to HO• generation using EDTA‐FeIII / H2O2/Asc system in 50 mM PB, pH 7.4. The conversion of ENMI to ENMIDM was monitored using LC‐MS (LTQ‐XL Thermo) at different time intervals. The crude product was purified using a preparative reverse‐phase high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, VWR LaPrep Σ equipped with a column XBridge C18 BEH 300 Å, 5 µm, 19 × 150 mm, FLOW) using a gradient of Buffer A and Buffer B (9:1, MeCN: Buffer A) and the purity was confirmed using reverse‐phase analytical HPLC (Hitachi Primaide equipped with a column XBridge C18 BEH 300 Å, 5 µm,4,6 × 150 mm, 37 °C). LC‐MS separation was performed using a linear gradient of Buffer A and Buffer B. Purified solution of ENMIDM was lyophilized and characterized by NMR and LC‐MS.

Screening of ROS species

Emission response of NMI conjugated to ENMI (10 µM) was monitored at 550 nm using plate reader following the production of ROS species generated using following protocols. Various ROS species (HO•, H2O2, 1O2, HOCl, and O2 •−) were prepared according to the following protocols. Hydroxyl radicals were generated in situ using Fe‐EDTA‐Ascorbate redox pair. Singlet oxygen was generated using NaOCl and H2O2. NaOCl was used to generate ClO‐ in situ and its concentration was measured using absorption study (ɛ = 350 M−1cm−1 at 292 nm) at pH 9.0. Stock solutions of ONOO− were prepared immediately prior to use where 3 M NaOH was cooled to 0°C to which 0.7 M H2O2, 0.6 M NaNO2, and 0.6 M HCl were added simultaneously. The freshly prepared ONOO− solution was analyzed spectrophotometrically where ONOO− concentration was estimated through ε = 1,670 ± 50 cm−1 M−1 at 302 nm in 0.1 M NaOH (aq.). Superoxide radicals were generated using xanthine/xanthine oxidoreductase pair. Xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) is a molybdoflavin enzyme that catalyses the terminal two reactions in purine degradation in primates. In this system, the generation of the O2 •− species starts with the oxidation of xanthine to uric acid by the XOR, what provides the electron flux needed for the reduction of molecular oxygen into the superoxide radicals. Xanthine substrate (CAS 69‐89‐6) was dissolved in 1 mL of HEPES buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) with a drop of NaOH 0.1 M. 1/11 v/v XOR (CAS 9002‐17‐9) solution was prepared by diluting 300 µL of the commercial enzyme solution in 3 mL of HEPES buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4). To the solution of ENMI (10 µM) dissolved in PB buffer, 180 µM of Xanthine substrate was added to XOR.

Fluorescence quantum yield (FQY) measurements

The fluorescence quantum yields of ENMIDM and ENMI (λex = 450 nm) were measured in water (1% SDS) using a relative approach as described in literature.[ 44 ] Rhodamine 6G in H2O (1% SDS) was used as a standard (λex = 488 nm, known QY = 95%). Six sample concentrations (optical density or OD ranging from 0.01 to 0.12 per 10mm to minimize the inner‐filter effect) were measured in water to obtain absorbance and fluorescence spectra, providing input for the FQY calculation based on the equation: ϕU=ϕR·mumR·ηu2ηR2 where the subscripts U and R denote unknown and standard, respectively, ϕ is the fluorescence quantum yield, m is the gradient (or slope) obtained from the plot of integrated fluorescence intensity vs absorbance and η is the refractive index of the solvent. The range of adjusted R2 values is from 0.991 to 0.999, substantiating the linear model accuracy for deriving the FQYs.

DFT and TD‐DFT calculations

We have performed the DFT and TD‐DFT calculations with Gaussian 16. Default Gaussian16 thresholds and algorithms were used but for an improved optimization threshold (10−5 au on average residual forces), a stricter self‐consistent field convergence criterion (10−10 a.u.) and the use of the ultrafine DFT integration grid. Firstly, the S0 geometries have been optimized with DFT and the vibrational frequencies have been analytically determined, using the M06‐2X meta‐GGA hybrid exchange‐correlation functional.[ 45 ] These calculations were performed with the 6–311G(d,p) atomic basis set and account for solvent effects through the linear‐response PCM approach considering water as solvent.[ 46 ] Secondly, starting from the optimal ground‐state geometries, we have used TD‐DFT with the same functional and basis set to optimize the S1 geometry and compute the vibrational frequencies. All optimized structures correspond to true minima of the potential energy surface. These optimal structures (close to the FC point) were used as starting point for relaxed TD‐DFT scans, using the same level of theory, in which we constrained the dihedral angle between the core of the dye and the amino group from 0° to 90° by step of 15°. Finally, vertical transition energies were determined on all these structures with TD‐DFT and the same functional, but a larger basis set, namely 6–311 + G(2d,p) and the cLR2 variant of the PCM,[ 47 ] in its nonequilibrium limit, allowing to have accurate energies.
