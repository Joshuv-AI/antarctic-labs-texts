Disrupting the interaction between connexin 43 and calmodulin restores gap junction function and mitigates reperfusion arrhythmias
Authors: Lu Wang, Zhendao Xu, Xiang Huang, Xue Bai, Zijun Wang, Rongfeng Yang, Qi Deng, Hong Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury, the downregulation of Cx43 is a critical factor influencing intercellular electrical coupling and the incidence of reperfusion arrhythmias (RA). Restoring Cx43 protein levels in cardiomyocytes has been proposed as an effective strategy to reduce RA. However, the impact of I/R on the functionality of Cx43-based gap junction (GJ) remains unclear. In this study, we utilized a 40-minute hypoxia model that did not alter Cx43 expression in cardiomyocytes but impaired GJ function. This model enabled the assessment of GJ functionality via fluorescent dye transfer without the confounding factor of reduced GJ quantity. Further investigation revealed that hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) upregulated calmodulin (CaM) expression and enhanced the interaction between CaM and Cx43. SP15, a peptide mimicking the CaM-binding sequence of Cx43, effectively disrupted the CaM-Cx43 interaction, mitigating H/R-induced GJ dysfunction. Additionally, in an ex vivo rat heart I/R model, SP15 improved myocardial electrophysiological parameters and reduced arrhythmia scores by interfering with CaM-Cx43 binding. Introduction

Reperfusion arrhythmia (RA) is an important complication in myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, commonly observed during reperfusion therapy after myocardial infarction1–3. The mechanisms underlying RA have not yet been fully elucidated. Several pathophysiological mechanisms, including alterations in gap junction (GJ)4, calcium overload5, oxidative stress6, and mitochondrial dysfunction7 are considered crucial in RA. The primary gap junction protein in mammalian cardiomyocytes is connexin 43 (Cx43), which plays a key role in ventricular gap junction channels that facilitate electrical coupling in the ventricles8. Our previous studies have demonstrated that I/R leads to the downregulation of Cx43 in cardiomyocytes9 and that increasing Cx43 expression or enhancing its delivery to intercalated discs could reduce the incidence of RA4,10. However, current research on the effects of I/R on GJ functionality is still insufficient. Improving GJ functionality following reperfusion may represent a promising strategy to enhance intercellular communication and reduce the occurrence of RA.

Calmodulin (CaM) is a highly conserved calcium-binding protein that plays a crucial role in regulating various cellular functions11. During myocardial I/R injury, the reduction in oxygen supply disrupts energy metabolism in cardiomyocytes, leading to an increase in calcium influx and subsequent calcium overload12. Elevated cytosolic Ca2+ binds to CaM and activates several CaM-binding proteins including CaM-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII)13. CaMKII could regulate calcium homeostasis in cardiomyocytes by phosphorylating key proteins such as L-type calcium channels or ryanodine receptors, thereby modulating the opening and closing of calcium channels13–15. In addition, previous study has reported that the intracellular loop (CL) domain of Cx43 protein contains a high-affinity binding site for CaM16, and CaM can influence the opening and closing of GJ channels formed by the Cx43 protein through its binding to Cx4317. However, it is still unclear whether CaM affects GJ functionality by increasing its binding to Cx43 during myocardial I/R injury.

This study found that H/R treatment impairs GJ functionality in H9c2 cells through mechanisms beyond the downregulation of the Cx43 protein. To elucidate this mechanism, we synthesized a peptide (SP15) mimicking the amino acid sequence of the CL domain of Cx43 (CaM binding site) to disrupt the interaction between Cx43 and CaM. Our results demonstrate that SP15-mediated disruption of the Cx43-CaM interaction attenuates the GJ dysfunction induced by H/R treatment. Furthermore, in an isolated rat heart IR injury model, SP15 treatment improved myocardial electrophysiological parameters and significantly reduced arrhythmia scores, further highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the Cx43-CaM interaction.

Results

H/R treatment impaired GJ functionality and downregulated Cx43 expression in cardiomyocytes

We first investigated the impact of varying hypoxia durations on GJ functionality between H9c2 cells. The scrape loading/dye transfer assay demonstrated that H/R treatment inhibited LY dye transfer between H9c2 cells in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 1A and B), indicating the impaired GJ functionality after H/R treatment. Given that Cx43 is a key protein forming myocardial GJ, we examined the effect of different hypoxia durations on Cx43 expression in H9c2 cells. Western blot analysis revealed that H/R treatment also led to a time-dependent suppression of Cx43 expression (Fig. 1C and D). Notably, after 40 min of hypoxia, while Cx43 protein levels did not show significant changes, GJ functionality was impaired. This observation suggests that besides downregulating Cx43 expression, H/R treatment might impair GJ functionality through other mechanisms. To further explore this potential mechanism, subsequent experiments employed a 40-minute hypoxia treatment to establish the cardiomyocyte H/R model. ROS level, cell viability, MDA content, SOD activity, and LDH release were measured to evaluate myocardial cell damage induced by H/R treatment. The results indicated that H/R treatment increased ROS and MDA levels, elevated LDH release, and decreased SOD and cell activity (Fig. 1E-I).

H/R treatment enhanced CaM expression and interaction between Cx43 and CaM in cardiomyocytes

To investigate the underlying mechanisms through which H/R treatment impaired GJ functionality, we downloaded RNA-sequencing data under the accession number GSE179286 from the GEO database, including mice myocardial samples from I/R and control group. Compared to the control group, 63 upregulated genes and 256 downregulated genes were identified in the I/R group using the GEO2R online analysis platform (Fig. 2A). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis for molecular function (MF) revealed that these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were significantly enriched in pathways related to protein binding (Fig. 2B), suggesting that I/R treatment may affect protein interactions in myocardial cells. Consequently, we examined the hypothesis that H/R treatment alters Cx43 interactions with other proteins, leading to GJ dysfunction. Given that previous studies have reported high-affinity binding sites for CaM on Cx43, which may influence GJ channel gating17,18, we assessed the impact of H/R treatment on CaM expression and the result demonstrated that H/R treatment increased CaM expression in H9c2 cells (Fig. 2C and D). Moreover, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiment demonstrated an interaction between CaM and Cx43 proteins, and that H/R treatment enhanced this interaction (Figs. 2E and F), supporting the hypothesis that their interaction contributes to the observed GJ dysfunction.

Interfering with the combination of Cx43 and CaM mitigated the impact of H/R on GJ functionality

To further investigate the impact of CaM on GJ functionality, the lentivirus containing shRNA was used to knockdown CaM specifically. Western blot analysis revealed that sh-CaM#1 and sh-CaM#2 significantly reduced CaM expression in H9c2 cells (Fig. 3A and B). The knockdown of CaM attenuated the impairment of GJ functionality induced by H/R treatment (Fig. 3C and D). Additionally, shRNA - CaM#1 was more effective than shRNA - CaM#2 and was used in subsequent animal experiments. We also synthesized a peptide mimic (SP15) based on the amino acid sequence (144–158) where Cx43 interacts with CaM16, with the cell penetrating peptide (CPP) attached to its N-terminus (Fig. 4A)19. SP15 did not affect the expression levels of Cx43 or CaM in H9c2 cells (Fig. 4B and C). Co-IP experiments demonstrated that SP15 reduced the enhanced interaction between Cx43 and CaM induced by H/R (Fig. 4D and E). Moreover, SP15 treatment increased the diffusion distance of LY dye compared to the H/R group (Fig. 4F and G). These results suggest that inhibition of the Cx43-CaM interaction may mitigate the impact of H/R on GJ function.

The effects of CaM knockdown and SP15 on the myocardial electrophysiological parameters and reperfusion arrhythmia

We first established an isolated rat heart IR injury model (Fig. 5A). TTC staining revealed that the myocardial infarct size in the I/R group was significantly larger than that in the control group. (Fig. 5B-C). HE staining demonstrated disorganized myocardial cells, loosened myocardial fibers, and partial myocardial fiber rupture and dissolution in the I/R group (Fig. 5D). Additionally, the I/R group exhibited notable arrhythmias during reperfusion, as shown by the representative arrhythmias in Fig. 5E. These findings confirm the successful establishment of the isolated rat heart IR injury model.

To investigate the effects of disrupting the Cx43-CaM interaction in vivo on RA, we administered AAV containing sh-CaM#1 via the tail vein or introduced SP15 into the myocardial perfusion solution. The results showed that CaM knockdown and SP15 treatment reduced the interaction between Cx43 and CaM (Fig. 6A and B). Immunofluorescence confocal imaging further confirmed that CaM knockdown and SP15 treatment decreased colocalization of Cx43 and CaM in myocardial tissue (Fig. 6C). Moreover, left ventricular activation maps were recorded using a 64-channel multi-electrode array mapping system (Fig. 7A). Both sh-CaM#1 and SP15 mitigated the I/R-induced reduction in conduction velocity, as well as the increase in absolute inhomogeneity and inhomogeneity index (Fig. 7B-D). Additionally, disruption of the Cx43-CaM interaction significantly reduced the arrhythmia score (Fig. 7E), indicating its protective effect against I/R-induced arrhythmias.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that 40-minute hypoxia/reoxygenation impaired GJ function without affecting the expression of Cx43, the primary component of GJ. Moreover, H/R treatment led to an upregulation of CaM expression and enhanced the interaction between CaM and Cx43. Disrupting the Cx43-CaM interaction attenuated H/R-induced GJ dysfunction. Additionally, CaM knockdown and SP15 administration reduced the interaction between Cx43 and CaM in rat myocardium, improving electrophysiological parameters and reducing arrhythmia scores. These results indicate that inhibiting CaM or interfering with the interaction between CaM and Cx43 can promote the opening of gap junctions and restore their function. This leads to improved electrophysiological parameters between cardiomyocytes and a reduction in arrhythmia scores, thereby exerting a cardioprotective effect.

Cx43 is the primary component of GJ in cardiomyocytes, playing a crucial role in the rapid transmission of electrical signals and the coordinated contraction of myocardial cells20,21. Abnormal expression of Cx43 can lead to GJ dysfunction, resulting in conduction disturbances that may contribute to arrhythmias or other cardiac pathologies8,22. Our findings indicate a slight, non-significant increase in Cx43 expression following short-term hypoxia, while prolonged hypoxia led to a marked downregulation of Cx43 expression. Consistent with our results, Lin et al. reported that a 45-minute hypoxic preconditioning upregulated Cx43 expression in astrocytes by reducing its lysosomal degradation23. Wu and colleagues demonstrated that 6-hour hypoxia could downregulate Cx43 expression in cardiomyocytes via matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and the MAPK signaling pathway24. Ischemic preconditioning (IPC) is a cardioprotective strategy, wherein brief, controlled episodes of ischemia enhance the heart’s resistance to subsequent prolonged ischemic events25,26. The above results suggest that one potential cardioprotective mechanism of IPC may involve the upregulation of Cx43, which could mitigate the detrimental effects of ischemia and hypoxia on GJ function.

CaM plays a crucial regulatory role in myocardial I/R injury, contributing to calcium homeostasis, activation of CaMKII, and the modulation of oxidative stress and cardiomyocyte apoptosis27–29. In our previous study, we investigated the effects of ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) on CaMKII activation in myocardial tissue30. The results demonstrated that CaMKII expression was significantly upregulated in the myocardium following I/R injury, which was accompanied by an increase in transmural dispersion of repolarization (TDR)30. These findings suggest that I/R-induced CaMKII activation may increase the risk of ventricular arrhythmias. A previous study demonstrated that a high-fat diet (HFD) induces prolongation of cardiac action potential duration and the occurrence of arrhythmias in mice31. These changes were suppressed by KN93, a specific CaMKII inhibitor, suggesting that CaMKII activation plays a critical role in HFD-induced arrhythmogenesis31. In addition, another study showed that angiotensin II–mediated oxidative stress increased the susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmias in a cardiac hypertrophy rabbit model, which was attenuated by CaMKII inhibition32. However, a recent study indicated that although ROS and CaMKII activation are important contributors to early reperfusion-induced ventricular arrhythmias, genetic ablation of CaMKII oxidation did not protect against early RA in mice33. These conflicting findings suggest that the relationship between CaMKII and arrhythmias is not entirely consistent across studies. Potential reasons for this inconsistency may include differences in experimental animal species, disease models, duration and type of cardiac injury, as well as the methods used to manipulate or measure CaMKII activity. It should be acknowledged that CaMKII activation was not directly evaluated in the present study. This represents a limitation of the current study and a direction for future investigation.

A previous study has identified a high-affinity binding site for CaM on the CL domain of Cx43, suggesting that this interaction may influence the opening and closing of GJ17. To investigate this mechanism, we synthesized a peptide (SP15) mimicking the amino acid sequence of this binding site, aiming to disrupt the interaction between Cx43 and CaM. Our findings demonstrated that SP15 treatment attenuated H/R-induced GJ dysfunction and improved myocardial electrophysiological parameters. Although CPP attached to the N-terminus of mimetic peptide facilitated its intracellular delivery, this approach may be limited by the non-specificity of CPP and their potential cytotoxicity at high concentrations34. Therefore, encapsulating SP15 in liposomes offers a promising alternative, as liposomes can be modified for targeted delivery, minimizing off-target effects and systemic toxicity35–37. Additionally, liposomal encapsulation enhances the stability and half-life of the peptide by protecting it from degradation by serum proteases38,39.

This study provides valuable insights into the relationship between RA and the dysfunction of gap junctions. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the research relied exclusively on in vitro models using isolated perfused rat hearts, which may not fully recapitulate the complex physiological conditions present in vivo or in clinical settings. The absence of in vivo models or clinical trials limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, since only male Sprague Dawley rats were used in our experiment, it remains to be explored whether similar results can be obtained in female Sprague Dawley rats40. Moreover, while the role of CaM interference and SP15 peptide in mitigating RA was explored, the precise molecular mechanisms remain underexplored. Future studies should aim to include a broader range of experimental models and delve deeper into these mechanisms.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that during myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, CaM expression is upregulated. This leads to increased binding between CaM and Cx43, which in turn contributes to the closure of gap junction channels formed by Cx43. This closure impairs electrical coupling between cardiomyocytes, resulting in abnormal electrophysiological parameters and facilitating the development of RA. suggesting that targeting the CaM-Cx43 interaction holds therapeutic potential for mitigating RA. Further studies, particularly those involving alternative peptide delivery systems and in vivo models, are warranted to advance the clinical applicability of this approach.

Method

Cell culture

H9C2 cardiomyocyte cells (RRID: CVCL_0286) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). The cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, C2701-3 L, Beyotime, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (A5256701, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (C0222, Beyotime, China). Mycoplasma contamination testing (4460623, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) confirmed the absence of contamination.

To prepare the hypoxic medium, serum-free medium was placed in a sealed chamber filled with a gas mixture of 95% N2 and 5% CO2 and incubated overnight. Upon reaching 80% confluency, the H9C2 cells were switched from the normoxic medium to the preconditioned hypoxic medium. Cells were then incubated in the sealed hypoxic chamber under the same gas conditions (95% N2, 5% CO2) for the indicated duration. Following hypoxic exposure, the cells were transferred back to normoxic medium for 2 h of reoxygenation.

Scrape loading/dye transfer assay

H9c2 cells (passage numbers 5–12) were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/well and subjected to H/R treatment when they reached approximately 80% confluence. The culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with CaMg-PBS. 1 mL of pre-warmed Lucifer yellow (LY) solution (1 mg/mL, HY-128692, MedChemExpress, China) was added to each well, and the bottom of the plate was scratched with a scalpel. The 6-well plates were then incubated in a cell culture incubator for 5 min. After three washes with CaMg-PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099-3 L, Beyotime, China) for 20 min. Finally, the diffusion distance of LY was observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) to evaluate gap junction functionality between cardiomyocytes.

Western blot assay

Myocardial tissues or H9C2 cells (passage numbers 5–12) were sonicated (on ice, amplitude 30%, pulse on 2 s/off 3 s, total time 30 s) and lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) supplemented with PMSF (ST507, Beyotime, China). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C using a benchtop refrigerated centrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA), and the supernatants were collected. The total protein concentration was quantified using the BCA assay (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a PVDF membrane (88518, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Cx43 (sc-271837, 1:1000, Santa-Cruz, USA), calmodulin (sc-137079, 1:1000, Santa-Cruz, USA) and GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, 1:100000, Proteintech, China). After washed three times with TBST, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (SA00001-1, 1:1000, HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG, Proteintech, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Imaging was performed using an imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Relative protein levels were quantified by calculating the ratio of the target protein band intensity to the GAPDH band intensity.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Intracellular ROS levels in cardiomyocytes were assessed using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (G1706-100T, Servicebio, China). H9c2 cells (passage numbers 5–12) were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/well and subjected to H/R treatment when they reached approximately 80% confluence. After removing the culture medium, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Then, 1 mL of DCFH-DA working solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in a cell culture incubator for 30 min. The DCFH-DA working solution was then removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Finally, fluorescence intensity was observed using a fluorescence microscope to evaluate intracellular ROS levels.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CK04, Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Japan). H9c2 cells (passage numbers 5–12) were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells/well and subjected to H/R treatment when they reached approximately 80% confluence. Then, 10 µL of CCK-8 working solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in a cell culture incubator for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was then recorded using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA).

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

An MDA detection kit (G4300-96T, Servicebio, China) was used to detect cell MDA levels. H9c2 cells (passage numbers 5–12) were homogenized by sonication (on ice, amplitude 30%, pulse on 2 s/off 3 s, total time 30 s) and centrifuged for 15 min at 10,000 g at 4 °C using a benchtop refrigerated centrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA). A 20 µL aliquot of supernatant was mixed with 200 µL MDA detection working solution. The mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 40 min. Following incubation, the sample was immediately subjected to an ice bath for 5 min to stop the reaction. After the ice bath, the sample was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min to collect the supernatant. Next, 200 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a transparent 96-well plate. The absorbance at 532 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA). The MDA content of the supernatant was measured by absorbance at 532 nm and was expressed as µmol/g protein.

SOD activity was measured using a SOD activity kit (G4306-96T, Servicebio, China). H9c2 cells (passage numbers 5–12) were homogenized on ice by sonication (amplitude 30%, pulse on 2 s/off 3 s, total time 30 s) and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C using a benchtop refrigerated centrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA). A 20 µL aliquot of supernatant was mixed with 20 µL of enzyme working solution and 200 µL of SOD detection working solution. The mixture was thoroughly mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Following incubation, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA). SOD activity was calculated according to the formula provided in the kit operation manual.

The release of LDH was determined using the LDH assay kit (G1610-100T, Servicebio, China). Cell medium was collected and centrifuged at 1,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C using a benchtop refrigerated centrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA). An 80 µL aliquot of supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate. LDH detection working solution was added to the wells containing the samples. The plate was incubated in a cell culture incubator for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA). The percentage of LDH release was calculated using the following formula: LDH release rate (%) = (experimental LDH release - spontaneous LDH release)/(maximal LDH release - spontaneous LDH release) × 100%.

GO enrichment analysis

We downloaded the RNA sequencing data from the GEO database under the accession number GSE17928641, which includes datasets from mouse hearts in both control and I/R groups (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Differential expression analysis was performed using the GEO2R online analysis platform42, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified based on the criteria of P < 0.01 and log2|Fold Change| > 1. Subsequently, identified DEGs were subjected to GO enrichment analyses for molecular function (MF) using the DAVID online tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/)43.

Co-IP

After subjecting cells or tissue to hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) or ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) treatments, myocardial cells or tissue were homogenized using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) containing proteinase inhibitors (B14001, Bimake, USA). The lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C using a benchtop refrigerated centrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA). The supernatant was collected, and a small aliquot of the supernatant as the Input sample. The remaining lysate was incubated overnight at 4 °C with either Cx43 antibody (sc-271837, Santa-Cruz, USA) or calmodulin antibody (sc-137079, Santa-Cruz, USA), or an equivalent amount of control IgG. Incubate the mixture with gentle shaking at 4 °C overnight to allow for antigen-antibody binding. The next day, 100 µL of pre-washed Protein A/G agarose beads (PR40025, Proteintech, China) were added and incubated for 4 h at 4 °C. Complexes were washed five times with TBST buffer, resuspended in 5× Sample Buffer (P0015, Beyotime, China), and boiled for 5 min. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 3 min, the supernatant was used for immunoblotting.

RNA interference

Adeno-Associated Viral (AAV) and lentivirus carrying CaM shRNA and scrambled shRNA were synthesized by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). The shRNA targeting sequences were as follows: CaM-shRNA#1, 5′-GCTGAATTCAAGGAA.

GCTTTC-3′; CaM‐shRNA#2, 5′‐GCTTTCTCCCTATTCGATAAA‐3′; scrambled shRNA, 5′‐GGTGTATGGGCTACTATAGAA‐3′.According to the manufacturer’s instructions, H9c2 cells were transfected with lentivirus. Four weeks before the isolated rat heart Langendorff perfusion, the AAV carrying CaM shRNA or scrambled shRNA was administered via tail vein injection.

Heart Ischemia-reperfusion model

The protocol of the experiments was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (approval number: 2403412). We declared that all methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The experiments also comply with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://www.nc3rs.org.uk/arrive-guidelines). Male Sprague Dawley rats (SPF grade, 180–220 g) were procured from the Animal Experiment Center of Guizhou Medical University. Following anticoagulation using intraperitoneal administration of heparin sodium salt (312.5 U/mL, 10 mL/kg, 9041-08-1, MedChemExpress, USA), the rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (2%, 2.5 mL/kg, P3761, Merck, USA). Euthanasia of the rats was performed by cervical dislocation. The heart was subsequently excised and connected to a Langendorff perfusion apparatus for perfusion with Krebs–Henseleit solution (in mmol/L: 120 NaCl, 4.5 KCl, 1.25 CaCl2, 1.2 MgCl2, 1.2 KH2PO4, 20 NaHCO3, 10 glucose, pH 7.4). In the ischemia-reperfusion (IR) group, four weeks before the isolated rat heart Langendorff perfusion, the AAV vector carrying sh-SCR was administered via tail vein injection. After a 30-minute balanced perfusion, the heart was induced to arrest for 40 min using St. Thomas’s solution (in mmol/L: 110 NaCl, 16 KCl, 16 MgCl2, 1.2 CaCl2, 10 NaHCO3; pH 7.8), followed by a 30-minute reperfusion phase. In the sh-CaM#1 group, four weeks before the isolated rat heart Langendorff perfusion, the AAV vector carrying sh-CaM#1 was administered via tail vein injection. All other experimental procedures followed those of the I/R group. In the SP15 group, the synthetic peptide, obtained from ChinaPeptides (Shanghai, China), was added to the Krebs–Henseleit solution at a concentration of 150 µM. The remaining experimental steps were consistent with those of the I/R group. In contrast, the control group underwent continuous perfusion for 120 min without interruption.

Electrophysiological study

Following systemic heparinization and anesthesia, the hearts were mounted on the Langendorff perfusion system using the preceding experimental procedures. The electrode was placed on the epicardial surface of the left ventricular, and electrocardiograms were recorded at the intrinsic heart rate without external pacing, using the BL-420 F Biological Function System (Chengdu Techman Soft Co., Ltd, China). The severity of ventricular arrhythmias was assessed according to the Lambeth Conventions46. Arrhythmias were classified and scored based on their frequency and duration as follows: 0 = no arrhythmia; 1 = occasional premature ventricular contractions (PVCs, < 10 events/5 min); 2 = frequent PVCs (> 10 events/5 min) without ventricular tachycardia (VT); 3 = non-sustained VT (< 15 s); 4 = sustained VT (≥ 15 s); 5 = self-terminating ventricular fibrillation (VF < 30 s); 6 = sustained VF (≥ 30 s or non-terminating). For each animal, the highest score observed during the reperfusion period was recorded. The left ventricular activation map was recorded using a 64-channel multi-electrical array mapping system (EMS64USB-1003, MappingLab Ltd., UK), and the conduction velocity, absolute inhomogeneity, and conduction inhomogeneity index were calculated using EMapRecord 5.0 software (MappingLab Ltd., UK).

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining

Paraffin-embedded myocardial tissue Sect. (5 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene (A530011, Sangon Biotech, China) and rehydrated through a series of graded ethanol (A500737, Sangon Biotech, China) solutions. The sections were stained with hematoxylin (C0105S, Beyotime, China), followed by differentiation with hydrochloric acid in ethanol. Subsequently, eosin (C0105S, Beyotime, China) staining was applied. After staining, the sections were dehydrated in graded ethanol and cleared in xylene. Finally, the sections were dried and mounted on a coverslip using mounting medium.

2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium Chloride (TTC) Staining

The rat hearts were initially stored at −20 °C for 30 min to facilitate slicing. Subsequently, the hearts were transversely sectioned into 1 mm thick slices using a scalpel. The resulting myocardial slices were then immersed in a 2% TTC staining solution (G3005, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following the staining procedure, 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099-3 L, Beyotime, China) was added to fix the tissue sections.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded myocardial tissue Sect. (5 μm thick) were first deparaffinized in xylene (A530011, Sangon Biotech, China) and rehydrated through a series of graded ethanol solutions (A500737, Sangon Biotech, China). Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (P0081, Beyotime, China) through heat-induced epitope retrieval. After cooling, sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (P0096, Beyotime, China) and blocked with 5% normal goat serum (C0265, Beyotime, China) to prevent non-specific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-Cx43 (1:100, 26980-1-AP, Proteintech, China) and mouse anti-calmodulin (1:1000, sc-137079, Santa Cruz, USA). Following incubation, the sections were washed with PBS and incubated with species-specific fluorescent secondary antibodies (A0516, 1:500, Beyotime, China; A0568, 1:500, Beyotime, China) for 1 h. The sections were washed with PBS and counterstained with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime, China) to visualize nuclei. Finally, the stained sections were mounted with antifade mounting medium, and imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan) for analysis and documentation.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test for two-group analyses or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons involving more than two groups. Post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using the Tukey multiple comparison test. A significance level of P < 0.05 was set to determine statistical differences. All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism software.
