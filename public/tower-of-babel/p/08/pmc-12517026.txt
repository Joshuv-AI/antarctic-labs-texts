Exploring the lived experiences of first blood among adolescent girls in Pakistan: a phenomenological qualitative study
Authors: Ruhma Binte Shahzad, Muhammad Mohsin Aftab, Hamda Shahzad, Javeria Saleem, Mehnaz Rashid, Mohsin Arshad, Saira Abbas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe study aims to explore the experiences of adolescent girls with the onset of their first menstruation in the context of Pakistan and to highlight the sociocultural aspects that shape those experiences.DesignThe study employs an exploratory phenomenological approach.SettingThis study has been conducted at a public sector higher education institute, University of the Punjab, Pakistan. Female students enrolled in the first semester of the undergraduate degree programme were included in the study using a predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.ParticipantsData was collected from six 18 years old girls who had their menarche in the last 6 years through face-to-face in-depth interviews using a semi-structured interview guide from June 2024 to August 2024. The interviews were audio-recorded given the written consent of the participants. Transcripts were analysed thematically following Braun and Clarke’s framework.ResultsThematic analysis of six interviews revealed three major themes: (1) Experience of first blood: emotional, social and practical difficulties; (2) The problem of mismanagement of first menstruation and the strategies used; and (3) Restrictions during menstruations. The study found that adolescent girls are provided with little to no prior knowledge regarding menarche and menstruation that causes various complexities and vulnerabilities. The social and cultural expectations and the gendered norms construct the experiences of the adolescent girls regarding their first menstruation, while the idea of womanhood is preserved in the society by restricting, isolating and alienating the adolescent girls and modifying their conduct and behaviour accordingly.ConclusionMenarche is often experienced as distressing, secretive and isolating for adolescent girls in Pakistan. Educational interventions in families and schools are needed to provide accurate, timely information and to support girls in navigating this transition with confidence and dignity.Keywords: QUALITATIVE RESEARCH; Adolescent; Sexual and Gender Minorities; Stress, Psychological; PUBLIC HEALTH; Primary Care

Introduction

Menarche is considered as one of the most significant events in the life of a female. It marks the period of transition from being a girl to developing into a woman.1 During this phase, girls may experience physical, psychological and behavioural changes. When girls reach adolescence, they encounter numerous challenges, including pubertal changes, sexual development, evolving parent-adolescent relationships, self-development and an expanding social network.2 This onset of menarche, marking puberty, can be immensely challenging for adolescent girls, which can make them feel different from, or out of sync with, their unmatured peers.3

Menarche is associated with a vast range of biological changes that encompass physiological, sexual, emotional and cognitive aspects. This can lead to pubertal mood disorders among adolescent girls. Mood disorders in adolescents cannot be attributed solely to hormonal changes. Contributing factors may also include shifting patterns of social interaction, prevailing social and cultural expectations, individual belief systems and evolving self-concept during this transitional stage.4,6 These factors play an important role in shaping adolescents’ experiences of menarche and related mood disturbances. However, these experiences and the response to these changes vary across cultures. It depends on how the social and cultural values, perceptions and practices influence the way individuals cope and react.

Although menarche and menstruation are physiological body functions, the individual and societal attitudes towards them vary substantially across cultures.7 It is often seen as debilitating and contaminating.8 The evidence from developed countries, including the USA and Taiwan, shows that despite adequate facilities and appropriate provision of information, this physiological phenomenon is often considered a shame and embarrassment, specifically within the school context.9,11 However, in the cultural context of Pakistan, female reproductive health and especially menstruation is often treated as a taboo subject, leading to silence and secrecy that undermines girls’ preparedness for menarche.

Recent studies highlight how lack of menstrual education, limited parental guidance and constrained access to menstrual health resources in low- and middle-income countries exacerbate this issue and contribute to the feeling of shame, embarrassment and isolation among girls.12,15 The literature suggests that girls with greater knowledge of menstrual hygiene and safe menstrual practices are less vulnerable to reproductive tract infections and their associated consequences.16 Conversely, inadequate knowledge, coupled with the feelings of shame and embarrassment linked to menstruation, can adversely affect the overall well-being of adolescent girls. This not only compromises menstrual hygiene and increases the susceptibility to health problems17 but also contributes to heightened anxiety, stress, diminished self-esteem and poor mental health, especially within the cultural contexts where menstruation is heavily stigmatised.18,20

Cultural perceptions related to menstruation can significantly impact the reporting of menstrual symptoms and the experience of adolescent girls regarding their first menstrual cycle. Therefore, understanding the experiences of girls during their first menstruation within the specific social and cultural context is very crucial. This exploration helps in identifying the challenges they face, the impact of cultural norms and stigmas and the overall influence of menarche on their physical and psychological well-being. Different cultures have their own adaptive mechanisms for managing the responses. Understanding and improving the support systems around menstruation can significantly impact the psychological well-being of girls, helping them navigate this transition more confidently. Therefore, the present study has been set up with the objective to explore the experiences of adolescent girls regarding the onset of their first menstruation in the sociocultural context of Pakistan—a country where reproductive health of females is highly stigmatised.

The results of the present study can inform policymakers about the specific needs and challenges adolescent girls face, leading to the development of more effective policies and programmes aimed at improving menstrual health and education. Further, by studying and exploring the experiences of adolescent girls during their first menstruation, stakeholders can develop informed strategies to improve menstrual health, education and overall well-being, ultimately contributing to the empowerment and betterment of girls and women in Pakistan.

Materials and methods

Study design

To report the findings of this study, COREQ (Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research) checklist was used. This qualitative study examined the lived experiences of adolescent girls with the onset of their first menstruation in the sociocultural context of Pakistan using an exploratory phenomenological approach. Using this approach, researchers were able to gain insight into the essential meanings of menstrual experiences of participants and, therefore, a deeper understanding of their long-term well-being.

Study setting and participants

The current study has been conducted at the University of the Punjab, Pakistan. Female students enrolled in the first semester of the undergraduate degree programme were selected using a predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria using purposive sampling technique. The University of the Punjab was selected as the research site as it is the largest public sector university, in the largest by population province of the country, with students from different ethnic and social backgrounds enrolled here. Purposive sampling technique was used to select participants of specific age range outlined in inclusion criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria included the age of participants to be 18 years; the episode of menarche in the last 6 years; informed consent and the ability to comprehend and speak English, while the exclusion criteria included the declined consent. In order to reduce the recall bias, only those adolescents were included who had their menarche episode in the last 6 years. 18 years old adolescents were selected so they could provide a valid written consent and could share their experiences and stories in a better way, while English comprehension was included in the eligibility criteria to ensure that participants could understand the study information, provide informed written consent and later review the transcripts during the member checking process.

Sample size

A total of 13 participants were contacted to participate in the study. However, seven of them did not meet the inclusion criteria, that is, either they were under 18 years of age or had experienced their first menstruation more than 6 years prior to data collection. Six participants were finally included. Data saturation was achieved at the fifth interview, as no new codes, concepts or insights emerged from the narratives. An additional sixth interview was conducted to confirm the saturation of data. This decision follows qualitative research guidance that emphasises reaching data saturation as the key criterion for determining sample adequacy, rather than adhering to a predetermined number of participants. The convergence and repetition of themes across participants provided confidence that the sample size was sufficient to capture the depth and variation of experiences relevant to the study aim. None of the participants declined participation, and all of them were approached through face-to-face interactions in a university setting.

Interview guide and data collection process

Face-to-face in-depth semistructured interviews were conducted with the recruited adolescents using a semistructured interview guide. The first draft of the interview guide was developed after extensive literature review17,13 15 18 20 that was refined after pilot testing with two participants. The data of the pilot tested interviews was not included in the final sample and data analysis. Feedback from the pilot interviews highlighted minor issues such as the need to simplify wording in certain questions and to add probing prompts to elicit more detailed narratives. Based on this feedback, the guide was refined to ensure clarity, cultural sensitivity and better alignment with the study objectives before being used in the main data collection. The final interview guide (online supplemental file) had two sections, that is, (1) sociodemographic profile and (2) formal interview questions. In sociodemographic characteristics, current age, age at the time of menarche, mother’s education and the working status of the mother were included. However, for maintaining the anonymity of the participants, only current age, age at the time of menarche and mother working status has been given as indirect identifiers.

The interviews were conducted from June 2024 to August 2024. All the interviews were conducted by two female authors (MR and SA), considering the cultural sensitivity and secrecy attached to menarche and menstruation in Pakistan. Further, two repeat interviews were conducted by the author RBS to validate the data. All the researchers had a previous experience of conducting qualitative interviews and had relevant qualifications, that is, MPhil in social science (SA); MBBS (MR) and MPhil in Public Health (RBS). All the interviews were scheduled and conducted at the time and place convenient for the participants, within the university setting, in the presence of the two interviewers and the participant only.

A systematic approach was followed for the process of data collection. Once the participants were approached, the interviewers introduced themselves and the objectives of the study were communicated to the participants. Then, the interviewers informally shared their personal menarche experiences and stories with the participants. This was done to build a rich relationship and a sense of trustworthiness; to gain a deeper understanding of the details of the story; and to build rapport among the participants. This strategy helped in making the participants comfortable to talk on menarche and menstruation, understanding the complexities and cultural restrictions caused by menstruation from the perspective of the participants and in identifying the specific probing questions to be raised in formal interview sessions.

Following this, a formal consent was taken from the participants and the interview was initiated. Before obtaining informed consent, participants were clearly informed that they would not receive any compensation for their involvement and that their participation was completely voluntary. All the interviews were audio-recorded, given the written consent of the participants, with an average time of 35–45 min. To control the personal bias of the researchers, they kept a diary with themselves and restricted themselves purely to the objectives of the study. Data saturation was achieved by asking probing questions by the interviewers, which also aided in capturing patients’ feelings, emotions and experiences. All six interviews were originally conducted in the local language (Urdu) to allow participants to express themselves freely and comfortably while discussing a culturally sensitive topic. The interviews were then translated into the English language for analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analysed thematically following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework (familiarisation, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining/naming themes and producing the report).21 Themes were derived through both inductive and deductive approaches. First, the collected data went through verbatim transcription in the Urdu language (native language) and then was translated into the English language. Then, the translated manuscripts were returned to the participants for their feedback and to make sure that their stories had been accurately translated, without losing the essence of their emotions. Third, the researcher went through the process of iterative reading. The transcripts were critically read multiple times to build familiarity with the data and to extract the codes from it. The codes were extracted manually, without the use of any software. In the next stage, multiple collective meetings among all the authors were conducted for coding the data. Coding was conducted independently by multiple researchers, followed by meetings to consolidate and refine the codebook. Multiple codes were identified related to the experience of the first episode of menstruation among adolescent girls and the broader sociocultural factors that shape their experiences. The final codebook was improved by the compilation of emergent codes into subthemes and themes. In the fifth step, the initial themes were reviewed, improved and finalised. In the sixth step, the findings and the data were presented to an external colleague to ensure the credibility of the findings, to gain an external perspective and to make sure that no personal potential biases had affected the data analysis and interpretation process. In the last step, member checking was done and the findings were presented to the participants to ensure that the right meaning had been extracted from their quotations. Credibility strategies

To enhance rigour and ensure the trustworthiness of the findings, several strategies were employed. Member checking was conducted by sharing translated transcripts and thematic summaries with participants to confirm the accuracy of interpretations. Peer debriefing was undertaken through discussions with an independent qualitative researcher, which helped validate the coding process and minimise potential researcher bias. In addition, researcher reflexivity was maintained throughout the study by keeping field notes and diaries, allowing the research team to reflect on their positionality and reduce bias during data collection and interpretation.

Patient and public involvement

No patient or public was involved in the present study.

Results

Sociodemographic profile

Six females were included in the study, with all the females 18 years of age. One participant reported to have menarche at the age of 14 years, while three of them reported to have their first menstruation episode at 13 years and two reported to have their first blood at the age of 12 years. The mothers of two of the participants were professionals, while the mother of the rest of the participants was a housewife. The working status of mothers is added to the profile of participants because they are considered as the prime caregivers of the family and play a crucial role in educating their daughters regarding menstruation. Theme 1: experience of first blood – emotional, social and practical difficulties

In the cultural context of Pakistan, the first menstruation for girls can be a very challenging and complex experience, often marked by a mixture of emotional, social and practical difficulties. This milestone, which should be a natural and informative part of growing up, often becomes a source of confusion, fear and shame due to various cultural norms and practices leading to various difficulties. The present theme illustrates such experiences of the participants with their first menstruation with the use of three underlying subthemes, that are, (1) inadequate knowledge and associated distress; (2) shame, embarrassment and discomfort leading to practical difficulties; and (3) enduring menstrual pain.

Inadequate knowledge and associated distress

The rapid physical changes of menarche can be very distressing for girls. These difficulties exaggerate when the girls do not have an adequate level of knowledge regarding the episode of first blood. The majority of the girls (5 out of 6) reported feeling scared when they first menstruated, because they had not been adequately prepared or informed about it. Due to the cultural taboos associated with sexual and reproductive rights and education in Pakistan, families and mothers feel hesitant to talk about menstruation with their daughters, hence leaving them uninformed about one of the most significant events of their lives. It eventually puts them in a state of depression and anxiety during their menarche. Participants shared:

A1: No one had ever talked to me about menstruation. I had no idea what was happening to my body, and it made me feel scared and alone.

A4: When I got my first period, I felt so anxious and depressed. I did not want anyone to know, not even my mother. It felt like something I had to hide.

A6: I was terrified when I saw the blood. I thought I was sick with some kind of cancer or tumor. I was too embarrassed to tell anyone and I hide it for three good days until my mother found it on her own as I stained the bedsheet.

On the contrary to this, one of the participants (A3) shared that she was informed by her mother a few months before she got her first menstruation, due to which she was relatively calm and composed as compared to those who were not or ill-informed. She shared:

A3: My mother told me that all girls menstruate and I will start menstruating soon and it will be on monthly basis. So, when I got my first period, I was very calm and normal and told it to my mother.

However, when the participants were further probed on what kind of information they received regarding menstruation from their mothers, all of the participants reported receiving very little to inadequate information. None of the participants reported receiving reliable information on sexual and reproductive rights, but rather the phrase ‘all girls go through this’ was inoculated at the event. Participants shared:

A4: it was just that my mother said that all girls menstruate in this age, but did not tell why?

A5: my mother told me that it is a part of being a female.

This lack of open dialogue about menstruation made girls feel isolated and unsupported, potentially leading to long-term psychological impacts such as anxiety or low self-confidence. As one participant shared:

A1: my mother told me nothing, she just handed me a piece of cloth and ask to place it in my underpants. I did not know for a long period of time that why it is happening to me and it made me hate myself.

Shame, embarrassment and discomfort leading to practical difficulties

In Pakistan, the first episode of menstruation for girls can often be accompanied by feelings of shame, embarrassment and discomfort. This experience is shaped by several cultural, social and educational factors. Mothers can play a significant part in shaping a positive menarche experience for their girls by explaining the biological aspects of menstruation; helping their daughters understand why it happens; and telling what changes to expect in their bodies. Mothers can dispel common myths and misconceptions about menstruation, providing factual and reliable information. However, menstruation is often considered a taboo subject in Pakistani culture. Many families do not discuss it openly, leading to a sense of secrecy and shame surrounding the topic that eventually is adopted by the next generation. This contributes to causing a sense of embarrassment and shame among adolescent girls. Participants shared that:

A1: I remember I was playing with my cousins (both male and female) at a family event. Suddenly, someone highlighted that I have stained my clothes and my mother took me to washroom angrily. I felt so embarrassed and I thought as if I have done something wrong. I felt as if everyone is looking at me and talking about me. I felt so embarrassed and shameful throughout the event afterwards.

A4: The fear and shame I felt during my first period stayed with me for years. It was hard to shake off those feelings and accept menstruation as a normal part of life.

Further, the sense of shame associated with menstruation in the cultural context of Pakistan causes practical difficulties and discomfort for girls in their daily lives as they remain constantly conscious throughout their menstruation period. Participants reported the feelings of mental fogging and discomfort in daily routine tasks during their initial menstruation episodes due to the sense of shame. Some quotes of the participants are mentioned below to further illustrate the practical difficulties caused by the shame associated with menstruation:

A1: During my first menstruation, I contaminated the cloth I was using instead of pad with my urine while using the toilet. It made me feel so filthy and uncomfortable but I had no idea what to do because my mother told me that I have to use the pad until she tells to.

A2: I did not have any pad when I got my first period. We (me and my mother) were at my aunt’s place. My mother did not want anyone to know about my menarche so she took one handkerchief, folded it thrice and asked me to use it, and it was so uncomfortable to manage.

A5: it was very uncomfortable to walk for me. I felt that as everyone is looking at me and talking about me. So, my mind was all the time occupied with the thought that I had to act normal that drained my energy.

However, this sense of shame is further complicated by the cultural and religious underpinnings and justifications linked with the blood of menstruation, as participants elaborated:

A3: my mother told me that I have to be very cautious about not staining my clothes because I will be sinned by God if any male member sees the stain of my blood. It made me so conscious that I spent all the time in going to washroom and checking on my pads instead of focusing on any other task.

A6: I wanted to talk about it with my friends and my sisters to normalize things and to know why it is happening but my mother told me that good girls do not talk about and discuss menstruation.

Enduring menstrual pain

Along with the emotional and psychological challenges accompanying the first episode of blood among adolescent girls, another challenge that goes along is physical pain. Usually, females in the sociocultural milieu of Pakistan are expected to endure their pains and sufferings and to remain silent about them. Likewise, the participants of the study reflected on similar experiences. All of the participants, though they were previously informed or not, reported experiencing severe physical pain and cramps during their first menstruation. The participants further elaborated that the physical pain was so bad that they were unable to perform their daily tasks normally. Some participants mentioned:

A1: The cramps were unbearable, and I did not know what to do. I just laid in bed, hoping the pain would subside.

A3: The pain was so bad that I had to skip school. I felt embarrassed to tell my teachers the real reason for my absence.

A4: Experiencing cramps at school was a nightmare. I could not concentrate on my studies and was afraid to ask for a break because of the stigma.

A6: I was terrified because I thought something was seriously wrong with me. No one had warned me that the pain could be this bad.

Despite the severe physical pain, participants mentioned that little to no care was provided to them; rather, they were encouraged not to talk about it and to demonstrate a sense of patience because ‘all girls go through the same’ (A1 mentioned). Participants, while sharing their experiences, mentioned:

A2: I tried to endure the pain, but it was so bad that I eventually asked my mother for help. She gave me some traditional home-remedies, but the pain did not completely go away.

A5: It was so bad that I started crying but my mother asked me to bear it with patience because I have to experience it every month like other girls do.

Theme 2: the problem of mismanagement of first menstruation and the strategies used

Some parents, especially mothers, may feel uncomfortable discussing menstruation with their daughters due to their own upbringing and societal norms. This lack of communication can leave girls unprepared for their first menstruation episode, leading to mismanagement and health complexities. The participants of the current study reported having zero to very little prior knowledge regarding menstruation at their menarche. This led to various misconceptions as well as health complications. Based on the experiences of the participants, the present theme has been divided into three subthemes that are (1) lack of mother–daughter communication; (2) relying on oneself and peer group; and (3) infections and the feeling of untidiness.

Lack of mother–daughter communication

The quality of communication between mothers and daughters is crucial. In some families, mothers may prepare their daughters for menstruation, but in the majority, the topic is avoided due to embarrassment or lack of knowledge. Mothers can create an open and supportive environment where daughters feel comfortable asking questions and expressing their feelings about menstruation. By sharing their own experiences and reassuring their daughters that menstruation is a normal part of life, mothers can help reduce any anxiety or embarrassment their daughters might feel. However, the lack of mother–daughter communication regarding menstruation causes a lot of ambiguities in the mind of the adolescent girls, leaving them unprepared, as participants said:

A5: I wish I had known what to expect. My first period came as a complete shock, and I felt so unprepared and confused.

A1: I wish my mother had talked about menstruation with me, so I would not have to rely on other sources for answering my questions.

A2: I wish my mother had talked to me about it openly.

The lack of communication between mother and daughter regarding reproductive health is rooted in the socio-cultural context of Pakistan, where the element of secrecy and shame is associated with female reproductive health. Such factors cause the girls to have a negative self-image. The way menstruation is treated culturally can affect a girl’s self-esteem and body image. Feeling that menstruation is something to be ashamed of can lead to long-term negative perceptions about one’s body. Such feelings were illustrated by participants as:

A1: I felt so isolated and unsupported during my first period. It took a long time for me to feel comfortable in my own body again.

A3: In our culture, menstruating girls are not supposed to talk about it. This made me feel like I was doing something wrong just by having my period.

A4: The pain was bad enough, but what made it worse was that I felt I could not talk about it. I had to pretend everything was fine.

A5: The lack of open discussion about menstruation made me internalize a lot of negative feelings about my body. It affected my self-esteem and confidence.

Relying on oneself and on peer groups

Without proper education, girls may rely on inaccurate information from peers or cultural myths, exacerbating their discomfort and misunderstandings. Similarly, participants of the present study revealed that in order to overcome the negative feelings caused by keeping the menstruation a secret, adolescent girls relied on different sources to answer their ambiguities and questions. Participants shared that due to the misconceptions caused by no to little information regarding menstruation, they relied on different sources of information that ranged from ‘helping oneself through internet’ (A5) to ‘talking to friends’ (A1). However, all of these sources of information did not provide them with sufficient and reliable information that worsened their experience of menstruation. Participants highlighted their wish to have reliable and open discussion with their mothers before their first episode of menarche so that they could have a better experience. The insights from the stories of the participants are:

A1: I discussed it with my neighbor friend who was elder than me in age. She gave me a lot information about menstruation but with it, she also provided me a lot of unnecessary information on sexual intercourse and showed some videos, which, at this point in time, I think was not needed.

A2: I discussed it with my friends, because I wanted to know why this was happening but the information was incomplete and sometimes scary. I wish my mother would have talked to me about this openly.

A4: I talked about it with one of my friends and she did not have her menarche at that time. She was also in shock when I told her and she asked that whether I have done something wrong with a boy (a sexual relation). It made me hate myself even more.

A5: I searched on internet that why there is blood on my underpants and it scared me further because it showed varying results including that of menstruation, piles, gastrointestinal bleeding and even pregnancy.

A6: I googled that why I was bleeding and it showed that it could be a sign of cancer that scared me to death. I cannot forget those days as it was a horrifying experience for me.

Infections and the feeling of untidiness

In some cases, girls might not have access to menstrual products like pads or tampons. They may resort to using makeshift materials, which can be uncomfortable and unhygienic. Limited access to clean and private sanitation facilities can make managing menstruation difficult, leading to discomfort and health issues. Participants revealed various difficulties while managing their first menstruation and reported experiencing difficult walking, staining clothes and even the signs of infections including smell, itching and rashes, due to the lack of hygiene management of their first menstruation. Participants shared:

A1: The cloth I was using got contaminated with my urine and I kept it for the whole day. It started causing severe itching and I got rashes. I felt like crying but I was not told what to do and eventually I got severe infection that I was unable to walk for two days.

A3: I got rashes under my legs and found it very difficult to walk but I was forced by my mother to act normal and not create a drama.

A4: I did not know how to walk and sit properly with the pads on. It was constantly being displaced and I stained my clothes.

A5: No one told me how to manage it properly. I used the same pad for the whole day. It started to stink so badly and I just wanted to scratch myself.

Further, the participants also highlighted the inadequate hygiene facilities at schools and public places that made managing menstruation even more difficult and embarrassing, as one of the participants shared:

A2: The school’s toilets were not equipped for managing periods. I felt dirty and uncomfortable the whole day as I contaminated my trouser and was afraid someone would find out.

The sense of feeling filthy and untidy remained constant among all the participants; however, one of the participants elaborated in detail how the menstruation made her feel disgusted and how the cultural restriction of not taking a bath further exaggerated the feeling of disgust. The participant shared:

A6: I did not tell anyone what was happening to me for three days. I remember there was blood constantly coming out. My clothes became dirty and I started to smell very bad because I was not using anything. I felt disgusted of myself. However, when my mother found out, she told me to use a pad and instructed me not to take a bath until I stop menstruating that further made me feel revolted because how can I stay without taking a bath in summers for days and that too in condition when I am feeling disgusting of myself that something is coming out of my body?

Theme 3: restrictions during menstruations

In Pakistan, cultural, social and religious restrictions during menstruation significantly shape the experiences of girls and women. These restrictions are often rooted in longstanding traditions, societal norms and religious interpretations. Cultural and religious beliefs may impose restrictions on menstruating girls, such as prohibitions on entering religious places, or participating in certain social activities. Likewise, based on the experiences of the participants, the current theme has been divided into two subthemes, that is, (1) restrictions on daily activities and the sense of alienation; and (2) adopting the role of womanhood.

Restrictions on daily activities and the sense of alienation

Menarche is considered one of the most significant events in a girl’s life. As the onset of the menstruation cycle, different sudden social, cultural and religious restrictions are imposed on adolescent girls, which are specific from culture to culture. Pakistan, a country that is culturally a rich one, imposes a variety of restrictions on the menstruating girls. Participants shared a sudden shift in their lives with the onset of the menstruation cycle. Participants highlighted various restrictions imposed on them as soon as they experienced their first blood that ranged from religious to extra-curricular activities. These restrictions do not only disrupt their daily routines but also significantly contribute to creating a sense of isolation and stress among them. As revealed in previous themes, the event of menstruation and the sudden physiological changes in the body already contribute to causing anxiety among adolescent girls. However, the cultural restrictions imposed, including that of not performing religious tasks, playing, eating certain things and not bathing, worsen their experience of transition into the next phase of life, damage their self-esteem and create a sense of alienation among them. Participants shared:

A1: During my period, I was told not to participate in certain activities like religious ones. It made me feel excluded and different from everyone else.

A2: The restrictions of not eating certain things having warm nature (yang foods) like chocolates, dates, eggs, coffee and tea and not doing certain things like fasting, praying and not playing makes me feel like something is wrong with me during my period. It is hard to maintain self-esteem when you're constantly reminded of these limitations.

A3: I love enjoying the rain but my mother told me not to do so while I am menstruating. I cannot expect nature to go along my menstrual cycle. Even though I want to continue with my daily routine, the restrictions and taboos make it difficult to live normally during my period.

A4: I feel so isolated because I cannot go to certain events just because I am menstruating.

A5: I used to participate very actively in sports activities including gymnastics and running in my school days. But after menstruation, I had to miss out on sports and physical activities because it is considered inappropriate for girls to be active at that time.

A6: In our family, we are not allowed to take bath during the days of menstruation that makes me hate my period and myself.

Further, the participants elaborated on how the religious restrictions imposed on them made them feel disconnected from their faith and spirituality and made them question their own worth. Participants said:

A4: During menstruation, I am not allowed to pray or touch the Quran (the Holy Book of Muslims). This makes me feel disconnected from my faith and spirituality during those days.

A5: The cultural and religious restrictions create a sense of shame and isolation. It is as if menstruation defines my worth and abilities for those days.

Adopting the role of womanhood

In Pakistan, menarche, or the first menstrual cycle, marks a significant transition in a girl’s life, often signalling her entry into the phase of womanhood. This transition is accompanied by a host of cultural, social and familial expectations and responsibilities that shape her identity and role within the community. Where different restrictions are imposed on adolescent girls following their menarche, it also comes along with accompanying different sets of rules and expectations levied on them. Participants shared their experiences and highlighted how they experienced a sudden shift in the expectations of society and their families, as soon as they entered their puberty. Along with the state of confusion girls experience during menarche due to the physiological changes, the society demands the adolescent girls to turn into a modest woman overnight and behave in a certain way. The expected behavioural shifts include acting composed and restraining from talking to and playing with male family members, even of their same age group. These shifts in the expectations cause a role strain among the girls and cause a sense of isolation among them. Such feelings occur because girls are forced to detach themselves or to change the intensity of certain activities and relations. Participants used the following quotes to describe their experiences and underpinning emotions:

A1: I have grown up playing with my siblings and cousins. But after my menstruation, everyone expected me to act differently. I got restrictions like not to play and talk with my male cousins though I consider them as my brothers as we have grown up together. It made me feel so isolated and excluded.

A3: My grandmother told me to act composed. I have only one brother and no sister. We used to play a lot with each other. But when I got my periods, my grandmother told me to maintain a distance with my brother and stop playing with him because I am a grown-up girl now.

A5: Since I have got my menstruation, there is one phrase I heard from my mother and my aunts that you are a grown-up girl now and act like one. It feels like one day you are a kid because you are not menstruating and the other day you get up and suddenly you became an adult woman as everyone expect you to act like one. (Added angrily in a loud tone), I do not even know that what is going on in my body, let me understand it first and then expect me to act maturely.

In addition to the expectations of behaving modestly, adolescent girls are also expected to dress in a modest way following their menarche. This means wearing loose-fitting clothes and ensuring that their bodies are covered. The shift towards more conservative clothing is meant to signal their transition into womanhood and to protect their modesty. Further, some girls are encouraged to wear dark-coloured clothing during their periods to avoid visible stains. This is a practical measure to prevent embarrassment but also reinforces the idea that menstruation should be hidden. The participants shared their stories and revealed how they were reinforced to wear certain types of clothes and to cover their bodies. This idea highlights the notations of how female bodies are viewed in society and the cultural underpinning of hiding the bodies to protect the modesty. Participants mentioned:

A2: After my first period, my mother started making me wear loose, dark-colored clothes, specifically in days of menstruation. It was a big change, and I felt like I had to hide my body.

A3: As I was the only sister to my brother, I liked wearing jeans and shirts like him. But as soon as I get my periods, my mother reinforced me to wear shalwar kameez (traditional Pakistani dress) and take dupatta (staller) over my chest (pause and a sigh). This did not only make me uncomfortable for a certain period of time but also made me feel as I have to hide myself.

A4: I am expected to dress more modestly now that I have started menstruating. It remained a constant reminder for me.

A6: During menstruation, my mother always tells me to wear dark colors and avoid light ones, so that no one knows that I am menstruating, in case I stain my clothes.

Discussion

The present study focuses on exploring the experiences of adolescent girls with the onset of their first menstruation in the sociocultural context of Pakistan. The study found that adolescent girls usually enter menarche unprepared, which creates social, emotional and practical difficulties for them. This mirrors regional patterns documented in South Asia, where preparatory guidance is often absent, and stigma remains pervasive.14 15 22 The challenges girls face during menarche include the sense of fear, shame, embarrassment, stress, depression and physical pains and cramps that further lead to practical difficulties in their daily lives and social interactions. These findings echo those reported in previous studies that highlight the negative and distressing experiences of menarche and menstruation.10 23 Conversely, the positive aspects of menarche, including the celebrations of entering womanhood through the rituals of giving gifts,23 remained at odds. The present study does not find any positive experience of adolescent girls associated with menarche. However, the findings illustrate how the topic of menarche is kept a secret and the social and cultural beliefs and attitudes expect them to enter womanhood uncelebrated.

The findings further elaborate that despite the inadequate knowledge about the physiological functioning of their bodies, adolescent girls are expected to stay composed and overcome their psychological and emotional difficulties. In Pakistan, gender stereotyping and gender role formation are sociocultural and relational constructs, which are developed and inculcated in the institutional networks, social interactions and social relationships, especially in family.24 Women are often encouraged to portray and practise silence, patience and forgiveness,25 26 while wisdom is seen as the key to overpowering and dominance.27 These cultural underpinnings of gendered norms are the ones that restrict females to have complete rights over their sexual and reproductive rights and access to adequate information.

In addition to this, this study highlights that entering puberty unprepared causes a lot of mismanagement for the girls at the time of their menarche. The lack of mother–daughter communication regarding reproductive health and specifically menstruation, engulfed in the cultural taboo, creates the element of secrecy surrounding menstruation. Further, the present study elaborates that due to the secrecy and lack of communication surrounding menarche and menstruation, the adolescent girls do not only encounter their menarche unexpectedly, unprepared and unhygienically, but it also makes them vulnerable to different unauthentic sources of information causing further vulnerabilities and emotional and practical difficulties. Notably, in contexts where school-based education or community programmes are present, girls report better emotional and practical preparedness—an opportunity missing in the Pakistani context.

The findings also shed light on the cultural restrictions and the expectations that come along with the menarche for the girls. Different social, cultural and religious underpinnings expect the adolescent girls following their menarche to act in a certain way and to restrain from certain behaviours. Echoing the findings of the previous studies, the present study found that females are restricted from taking baths23 and visiting holy and religious places during their menstruation.23 28 They are restrained from performing various spiritual as well as physical activities that create a sense of isolation, alienation and disconnectedness among them. Echoing findings of the previous studies that highlight the norm of food restrictions during menstruation,28 29 the present study found that adolescent girls are also restrained from eating certain types of food and drinks during their menstruation. These foods specifically include those having ‘warming nature’ (yang foods). However, these restrictions vary across cultures, demonstrating how culturally informed practices shape the experiences of adolescent girls regarding their menarche.

The findings of this study underscore how menarche is not only a biological milestone but also a culturally constructed transition into womanhood, one that carries significant social expectations. In the Pakistani context, the onset of menstruation often triggers reinforced expectations around modesty, conduct and restricted social interactions, reflecting broader cultural narratives about female bodies and morality. Similar observations have been reported in other South Asian studies, where girls are instructed to modify their dress, limit their interactions with men and abstain from religious practices following menarche.30 These cultural prescriptions illustrate how menarche becomes a critical point at which gender norms are more strictly enforced, thereby shaping girls’ identities and everyday lives.

Collectively, the findings summarises that the social and cultural expectations and the gendered norms construct the experiences of the adolescent girls regarding their first menstruation and preserve the idea of womanhood in the society by restricting, isolating and alienating the adolescent girls and modifying their conduct and behaviour. The findings of this study highlight the urgent need for policy-level interventions that address both educational and familial gaps in menstrual preparedness. At the educational level, integrating comprehensive menstrual health education into school curricula could provide adolescent girls with accurate, timely and practical knowledge before the onset of menarche. Such initiatives should also involve teacher training to ensure that menstruation is addressed openly and without stigma in classroom settings. At the familial level, targeted programmes for mothers and caregivers could strengthen maternal–daughter communication, enabling them to provide emotional support, accurate guidance and reassurance during this transition. Community awareness campaigns may also play an important role in challenging prevailing taboos, reducing secrecy and creating an enabling environment where menstruation can be discussed as a natural and healthy process. Collectively, these measures would help adolescent girls navigate menarche with greater confidence and dignity, while reducing the cultural isolation, restriction and alienation that currently shape their experiences.

To the best of our knowledge, there are no prior studies in Pakistan that explore the experience of adolescent girls regarding their first menstrual cycle in the local context. The limitation of the present study lies in its small sample size. Although data saturation was reached with five participants and was confirmed with a sixth, the small and homogenous sample limits the transferability of findings. As participation was limited to adolescents enrolled in higher education, the study may not capture the experiences of out-of-school or less-educated girls, or those from rural and diverse cultural backgrounds. Nevertheless, the homogeneity of the sample, coupled with the depth and richness of the narratives, provided validity within a phenomenological design. Furthermore, credibility strategies such as member checking and peer debriefing enhanced the trustworthiness of the findings. Further, the present study has been conducted in the sociocultural context of Pakistan, where the cultural values play a significant role in shaping the experiences and perceptions of the people, which might reduce the transferability of the findings to another cultural context. Mix-method studies with more diverse samples, including rural, lower socioeconomic and multilingual populations, as well as larger samples capturing the experiences of adolescent girls and their caregivers across different cultural contexts, are needed to deepen understanding of this topic.

Conclusion

The present study concludes that menarche is a very challenging experience for adolescent girls in the social and cultural context of Pakistan that creates social, emotional and practical difficulties for them. The lack of knowledge prior to the event even worsens their experience. However, adolescent girls are expected to conform to social and cultural constructed ideals of womanhood, which creates restrictions, isolation and feelings of alienation, along with psychological and emotional difficulties. Educational interventions in families and schools are needed to provide accurate, timely information and to support girls in navigating this transition with confidence and dignity. Families should promote open mother–daughter communication and provide emotional reassurance, schools should integrate menstrual health education and train teachers to discuss menstruation sensitively and policymakers should implement awareness campaigns and supportive policies to normalise menstruation. Such initiatives will empower the adolescent girls and will help them to make informed decisions and experience the transition into womanhood in a graceful manner.
