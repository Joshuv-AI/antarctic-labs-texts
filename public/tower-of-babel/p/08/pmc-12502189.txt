Analyzing the relationship between psychometric indices of item analysis with attainment of course learning outcomes: cross-sectional study in integrated outcome-based dental curriculum courses
Authors: Kiran Kumar Ganji, Nilakantan Ananthakrishnan, Shivasakthy Manivasakan, Mohammed Katib Alruwaili, Meshal Aber Alonazi, Hmoud Ali Algarni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAssessment plays a crucial role in evaluating student learning and achieving educational goals. This study investigates the relationship between various psychometric properties of assessment items: Discrimination Index, Difficulty Index, KR-20, and KR-21 and the percentage of attainment of Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs) in an integrated, outcome-based dental undergraduate program.MethodsA quantitative, correlational research design was employed at the College of Dentistry, Jouf University, Saudi Arabia, from January to July 2024. Data were collected from three distinct undergraduate courses in the Bachelor of Dental & Oral Surgery program. A total of 425 assessment items were analyzed, ensuring representation across different courses. Psychometric indices were computed using item analysis tool of Blackboard Learning Management System, and CLO attainment was determined based on student performance in mid-block and final block assessments. Pearson correlation analysis examined relationships between CLO attainment and psychometric indices, while multiple linear regression identified significant predictors.ResultsThe mean CLO attainment was 71.40% (SD = 7.19). A strong positive correlation was found between CLO attainment and the Difficulty Index (r = 0.906, p < 0.01), indicating that as the Difficulty Index increases, CLO attainment improves. CLO attainment also showed a moderate positive correlation with KR-21 (r = 0.701, p < 0.05). Multiple regression analysis identified the Difficulty Index as the most significant predictor of CLO attainment, while other indices, including the Discrimination Index and reliability estimates (KR-20, KR-21), did not show significant predictive power.ConclusionThis study underscores the pivotal role of the Difficulty Index in predicting CLO attainment in dental education. While reliability indices and Discrimination Index contribute to test evaluation, the Difficulty Index is the strongest determinant of student success. These findings provide valuable insights for optimizing assessment strategies to enhance student learning outcomes.Keywords: Item analysis, Psychometric properties, Difficulty index, Course learning outcomes, Dental education, Assessment reliability

Background

Medical education relies on effective knowledge measurement using multiple-choice questions (MCQs), which are valuable tools for factual recall and higher-order thinking skills [1]. Regular evaluation of assessment strategies is crucial for curriculum development [2]. To successfully evaluate the cognitive skills of medical students, it is important to routinely analyze items and update question banks with recent breakthroughs [3, 4]. MCQs provide teachers with feedback on the effectiveness of their teaching methods. Designing an MCQ for a multidisciplinary and integrated curriculum is complex and time-consuming. Curriculum development is a crucial assessment strategy that involves evaluating MCQ items for student knowledge effectiveness, reviewing poorly discriminating items, and regularly analyzing data for question quality and course question bank development [5–7].

Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs) are articulated statements that delineate the competencies a successful student or learner is anticipated to acquire upon completing a course/module [8]. The attainment of CLOs refers to the achievement of the expected level of performance in learning outcomes. Learning outcomes can be expressed absolutely or relatively. An important practical consideration for the indices of learning outcomes is that they are not divorced from the context of learning outcomes but are attached to the course assessment items in question. Meanwhile, as there are many course assessment items on which learning outcomes are assessed, the measures of the learning outcomes, Discrimination Index, and Difficulty Index of the learning outcomes should be handled separately for each pair of learning outcomes and course assessment items [9].

Recent evidence offers fresh perspectives on how item-writing flaws in MCQs affect student performance and test reliability in the context of medical education [10]. Creating a balanced MCQ satisfies students’ concerns about an acceptable average score and teachers’ concerns about an adequate score distribution [11]. Item analysis can help improve the quality and effectiveness of MCQs by assessing their Difficulty Index, Discrimination Index, and distractor efficiency. Item analysis evaluates the assessment quality by incorporating moderately difficult, discriminating, and functionally distracting items to enhance test standards, improve overall scores, and accurately discriminate students [6]. The relationship between difficulty and discrimination indices may not always be reciprocal, with questions with high p-values often discriminating poorly and those with low p-values being good discriminators [12]. A negative correlation exists between the Discrimination and Difficulty Indices, suggesting that low performers were more likely to answer correctly, and the Discrimination Index decreased consistently with difficulty [13]. Zainudin et al. explored the use of DI measurements to determine the test item difficulty and CLO achievement in students [14]. Reliability estimates such as Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20) and Kuder–Richardson Formula 21 (KR-21) are measures of internal consistency reliability for tests with dichotomous items; KR-20 provides a more accurate estimate by accounting for actual item difficulty and variance, while KR-21 is a simplified version assuming equal item difficulty; both are significant in evaluating how consistently a test measures the intended construct, with higher values indicating more reliable assessments.

Understanding the relationship between the Discrimination Index, Difficulty Index, KR-20, KR-21 and percentage of CLO attainment is crucial for evaluating the effectiveness of an assessment tool. Rigorous and unbiased statistical packages for approximating these three indices have not yet been widely popularized in institutional quality control practices. Statistically robust and efficient tools can be of substantial assistance in ensuring the validity of assessment processes and the reliability of assessment tools. Discriminatory power and item difficulty are key characteristics at the question level that ensure the reliability of a question bank. Based on this, how a particular learning outcome was responded to by a cohort of students on a specific measure of their performance is depicted in the literature in terms of a learning outcome’s Discrimination and Difficulty Indices. The percentage of attainment of the CLO is the credibility at the scheme level to ensure the fairness of assessments. To date, no study has been conducted to correlate item analysis findings with CLO attainment. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between various psychometric properties of assessment items (Discrimination Index, Difficulty Index, KR-20, and KR-21) and the percentage of CLO attainment in courses of integrated outcome-based dental undergraduate programs.

Methods

This study was conducted at the College of Dentistry, Jouf University, Saudi Arabia, from January 2024 to July 2024, in an undergraduate educational setting. The inclusion criteria included three distinct courses to represent diverse academic disciplines of a single cohort (academic year 2023–24) encompassing a minimum of three CLOs and that the assessment items were adequately mapped to multiple CLOs (Table 1). The exclusion criteria included assessment items not mapped to any CLO, duplicated across courses or assessments, used for practice or non-graded purposes, with incomplete or missing performance data, or assessed group- or project-based outcomes. This study adopted a quantitative correlational research design to examine the relationships between the psychometric properties of assessment items and CLO attainment. The assessment results of undergraduates enrolled in the selected courses of the Bachelor of Oral and Dental Surgery program during the academic semester (2023–24) were obtained. The sample size estimation was performed using the G Power computing tool with effect size (0.3), α (95% confidence level), and power of 0.80. A sample size of 425 assessment items was determined based on the need to conduct robust statistical analyses, specifically correlational and regression analyses, within the context of a psychometric evaluation. A stratified random sampling technique was employed to ensure representation across courses and student performance levels. The population was stratified based on the three selected courses; within each course, the selected assessment items were randomly sampled to achieve a proportional representation. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Local Committee for Bioethics (wide ref: 6-09-45), Jouf University, Sakaka, Saudi Arabia, prior to data collection.

The attainment of each CLO was calculated by aggregating student performance across all assessment items mapped to the respective CLO. The percentage of students who met or exceeded the predefined achievement threshold (70%) was determined for each CLO. This analysis provided a quantitative measure of the extent to which students achieved their intended learning outcomes, serving as a benchmark for evaluating the effectiveness of the curriculum and assessment strategies.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 23. The Pearson Correlation Coefficient was employed to examine the relationships between CLO attainment and psychometric indices, such as the Discrimination Index, Difficulty Index, KR-20, and KR-21 reliability estimates. Multiple Linear Regression Analysis was conducted to identify the most significant predictors among the psychometric indices (Discrimination Index, Difficulty Index, KR-20, and KR-21) and assess their combined effects on CLO attainment.

Discussion

CLOs are concise statements that outline the intended understanding of learners upon finishing a course, as well as their ability to apply acquired knowledge, skills, and professional ethics in real-world clinical settings and patient care [9]. The findings provide significant insights into the relationship between psychometric indices and CLO attainment in undergraduate dental education. Specifically, this study explored the influence of the Discrimination Index, Difficulty Index, KR-20, and KR-21 on the percentage of CLO attainment, with a focus on understanding which indices are the most predictive of student success. In the current study, a negative association was found between the Discrimination and Difficulty Indices, indicating that the Difficulty Index was indirectly proportional to the Discrimination Index. The Discrimination Index decreased as the assessment items became simpler, making it less effective in differentiating between good and poor students. Maximum discrimination was observed with a Difficulty Index between 40 and 74%, according to Si Mui Sim et al. [16]. In the current study, the Discrimination Index ranged from 0.24 to 0.46 of the assessment items, with difficulty scores between 50% and 86%. To calculate the Discrimination Index, our study followed the methodology of [17], which selects 27% of the upper and lower performers. The only drawback of this test is that it cannot be applied to small sample sizes. However, because the sample size in our study was 425 across different groups, the observed results accurately reflected the discriminative potential of the test items. There appears to be a consistent level of test difficulty and discrimination indices maintained throughout all assessment test items relevant to the respective CLO. This observation is because the test items were subjected to a series of vettings before being chosen for the tests. The quality of the test items could be further improved by the actions of the item writer in reviewing distractions based on the calculated Discrimination and Difficulty Index values. A few factors were prevalent in poor discrimination, including imprecise wording, gray areas of opinion, incorrect keys, and areas of contention [18].

This study also aimed to evaluate whether the Discrimination Index significantly influenced CLO attainment. The Pearson correlation between the Discrimination Index and CLO attainment was found to be negative (r = −0.450) and not statistically significant, indicating that items with higher discrimination power did not positively predict higher CLO attainment. This result contrasts with previous research, which highlights the importance of the Discrimination Index in differentiating high-performing from low-performing students. Traditionally, a higher Discrimination Index suggests an item is more effective in distinguishing students with varying levels of knowledge or ability, which theoretically improves learning-outcome predictions [19]. However, in this case, the negative, nonsignificant correlation suggests that the test items, although effective in differentiating performance, may not align well with CLO attainment. This discrepancy warrants further investigation, particularly in the context of assessment design, where poorly constructed or misaligned items may diminish the role of discrimination in achieving the CLO [20].

A significant, strong positive correlation was found between the Difficulty Index and CLO attainment, indicating that CLO attainment improves as the items become more challenging. This suggests that students who performed well on more difficult items tended to demonstrate higher attainment of CLOs. This finding aligns with the existing literature, which often posits that appropriately challenging items can enhance student performance by fostering higher-order thinking and engagement [21]. In line with Kane’s reliability theory, this suggests that items with moderate difficulty contribute positively to both student learning and CLO assessment accuracy [22]. Thus, ensuring an optimal balance of item difficulty remains crucial for fostering effective assessment systems that support students’ overall learning outcomes.

The KR-20 values for the assessment items across the courses showed moderate to high reliability, with all values exceeding the threshold of 0.50, indicating that the tests were reliable according to the recommendations of Kane et al. [22]. The relationship between KR-20 and CLO attainment was found to be weak and negative (r = −0.238) and not statistically significant, suggesting that internal consistency, as measured by KR-20, does not significantly influence CLO attainment. This contradicts research suggesting that tests with high KR-20 values should have consistent item performance and, thus, be more reliable measures of student learning [23]. However, the study showed that other factors, such as item alignment with CLOs or content coverage, might play a more substantial role in determining CLO attainment. In contrast, the KR-21 Index was moderately and positively correlated with CLO attainment (r = 0.701, p < 0.05), indicating that higher KR-21 values were associated with better CLO attainment. This supports the idea that the KR-21, which assumes that all items have the same difficulty, can be a valuable indicator of test reliability when assessing learning outcomes. These results suggest that tests with higher KR-21 values tend to have more reliable measures of CLO attainment, which is consistent with previous research linking higher KR-21 values with better assessment accuracy and test quality [24].

Backward regression analysis identified the Difficulty Index as the sole significant predictor of CLO attainment, with the final model showing an R² of 0.820, indicating that 82% of the variance in CLO attainment could be explained by the Difficulty Index. This finding emphasizes the central role of item difficulty in determining students’ success in achieving CLOs, further corroborating the strong correlation found earlier. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of using items with moderate difficulty levels to promote learning [25], and the current findings support this perspective. While the Discrimination Index, KR-20, and KR-21 did not emerge as significant predictors when the Difficulty Index was included, they still played a supporting role in shaping test reliability and effectiveness, albeit not to the same extent.

The findings revealed that the Difficulty Index was the most significant predictor of CLO attainment. The strong positive correlation (r = 0.906, p < 0.01) between the Difficulty Index and CLO attainment suggests that items with appropriate levels of difficulty are more likely to contribute to CLO achievement. This finding aligns with previous research that emphasizes the importance of item difficulty in assessing student understanding and ensuring that assessments are appropriately challenging to differentiate between varying levels of student performance [20]. This is a useful way for a medical institute to provide feedback to relevant course coordinators regarding the quality control of different tests, thereby affecting CLO performance. If the Difficulty Index is very low, suggesting that the question is challenging, it can indicate that the test item is poorly taught or challenging for students to understand. This also implies that the subject being examined is unsuitable for students at that level [26].

Contrary to expectations, the Discrimination Index and reliability estimates (KR-20 and KR-21) were not significant predictors of CLO attainment in the presence of the Difficulty Index. While the Discrimination Index showed a negative correlation with CLO attainment (r = −0.450), this relationship was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Although unexpected, the observed negative correlation between the Discrimination Index and CLO attainment highlights important considerations in the assessment design. High-discrimination items often target higher-order cognitive skills that may be more difficult for students to master, particularly in the early stages of learning, leading to lower CLO attainment despite strong item performance. This suggests that assessments must be carefully balanced in terms of difficulty and discrimination to effectively differentiate performance and support broader learning outcomes. Future studies should investigate how item alignment with CLO cognitive level influences this relationship.

Similarly, KR-20 and KR-21 did not significantly predict CLO attainment, with KR-20 showing a weak negative correlation (r = −0.238, p > 0.05). These findings suggest that while these indices are valuable for evaluating the quality and consistency of individual test items, they may not directly influence overall CLO attainment when accounting for the Difficulty Index. The lack of a significant positive relationship between the Discrimination Index and CLO attainment could be attributed to the nature of the assessments used in this study. The Discrimination Index is particularly useful for distinguishing between high and low performers for individual items. However, in the context of CLO attainment, a broader measure of student learning, the Difficulty Index may be more directly relevant [23].

The assessment of MCQs in educational settings, particularly medical education, hinges on the evaluation of their Difficulty Index, Discrimination Index, and CLO attainment. These indices serve as critical metrics to ensure the quality and effectiveness of assessments, thereby directly influencing student learning and performance. The Difficulty Index (p-value) quantifies how challenging a question is, with lower values indicating greater difficulty. This metric is essential for item analysis because it helps educators identify questions that may be too easy or too difficult, which can skew assessment results and misrepresent student understanding [27, 28]. A thorough evaluation of each item’s difficulty is crucial for maintaining the quality of assessments, which is a fundamental aspect of curriculum development [27]. Similarly, over successive academic years, there was a significant improvement in the Difficulty Index of the MCQs, indicating a progressive enhancement in the quality of the assessment items [28, 29]. The Discrimination Index assesses how well a question differentiates between high and low performers. A high Discrimination Index indicates that the question effectively distinguishes between students who have mastered the material and those who have not [30, 31]. Recent reports highlighted that both the difficulty and discrimination indices are vital for evaluating the standard of MCQs, with abnormal values suggesting poor quality [30]. Furthermore, Maulina and Novirianthy reported that item analysis, which includes the Discrimination Index, is instrumental in evaluating student performance and ensuring the validity of assessments [31]. The relationship between the difficulty and discrimination indices is also significant; a well-constructed MCQ typically exhibits a balance between these two indices, contributing to the overall effectiveness of the assessment [32]. The attainment of CLO is closely tied to the quality of the MCQs because these assessments are designed to measure whether students have achieved their intended educational objectives. Effective MCQs not only assess recall but also higher-order cognitive skills such as the application and synthesis of knowledge [33, 34]. Ingale et al. argued that properly constructed MCQs can provide a reliable measure of student learning outcomes, as they encompass a range of cognitive processes [33]. Additionally, the analysis of distractor efficiency, which evaluates how well incorrect options challenge students, further enhances the validity of the assessment [35]. The Difficulty Index and Discrimination Index are crucial components of item analysis that significantly impact the quality of MCQs and the attainment of CLO. Continuous evaluation and refinement of these indices are essential to ensure that assessments accurately reflect students’ learning and competency in medical education.

These findings have important implications for educators and test developers in undergraduate dental education. The strong predictive value of the Difficulty Index suggests that greater emphasis should be placed on ensuring that the assessment items are appropriately challenging. Educators should consider conducting item analysis regularly to identify items that are either too easy or too difficult and adjust them accordingly to improve alignment with CLOs. Moreover, while reliability estimates such as the KR-20 and KR-21 are essential for ensuring the consistency of assessments, this study indicates that they may not always be the best predictors of CLO attainment. Test developers should use these indices in conjunction with other measures, such as the Difficulty Index, to create well-balanced assessments that effectively measure student learning outcomes. Although this study provides empirical insights based on traditional psychometric indices, the complexity of learning outcomes, particularly in outcome-based education, warrants a broader perspective. Alternative assessment tools such as rubrics, performance-based evaluations, and reflective practices could offer richer and more nuanced data on student learning. Incorporating qualitative approaches in future research, such as student and faculty interviews or analysis of narrative feedback, could help uncover the contextual factors that influence CLO attainment beyond item-level performance.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The sample size was limited to the assessment items of three courses covering ten CLOs, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Additionally, this study focused exclusively on a specific set of psychometric indices; other factors that could influence CLO attainment, such as instructional methods and student characteristics, were not considered. This study did not compare the relationship of psychometric indices in CLOs attained to the target benchmark levels with those of non-attained CLOs. Future research should explore the impact of other variables on CLO attainment and consider longitudinal studies to assess how these relationships evolve over time. A notable limitation of this study is the contextual homogeneity of course content and student demographics. Variability in cognitive complexity, instructional strategies, and assessment types across courses may have influenced the psychometric behavior of the items. Moreover, the demographic uniformity of the student population in a single institution limits the broader applicability of the results. Future studies incorporating diverse educational and demographic contexts are warranted to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study highlighted the critical role of the Difficulty Index in predicting CLO attainment in undergraduate dental education. These findings suggest that while other psychometric indices, such as the Discrimination Index and reliability estimates, are valuable for evaluating individual test items, the Difficulty Index is the most significant predictor of overall CLO attainment. These insights can inform the development of more effective assessments better aligned with educational goals and ultimately contribute to improved student learning outcomes.
