Combined transcriptomics and metabolomics reveals the molecular mechanism by which rice responds to Ustilaginoidea virens infection
Authors: Rongtao Fu, Huan Li, Xi Luo, Jian Wang, Cheng Chen, Daihua Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice false smut caused by Ustilaginoidea virens is a major fungal disease of rice in rice-growing regions throughout the world. However, the key genes and key metabolites related to U. virens resistance in rice remain unclear. Here, we used transcriptomics and metabolomics to determine the gene expression and metabolite accumulation changes in rice at 5, 7, and 9 d after inoculation with U. virens. By comparing the transcriptomes of IR27 (resistant cultivar) and 9311 (susceptible cultivar) spikelets, variable transcriptional responses under control and infection conditions were revealed. In total, 11,235 and 13,453 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in IR27 and 9311, respectively. The results of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes and co‑expression analyses showed that the DEGs involved in flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and plant hormone signal transduction responded to disease resistance. Several WRKY transcription factors were also differentially regulated in the resistant and susceptible cultivars. The metabolome analysis identified 343 and 303 differentially accumulated metabolites in IR27 and 9311, respectively, including salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, gibberellin, d-pantothenic acid, and p-coumaric acid. Many of these are primarily involved in plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. Furthermore, the combined transcriptome and metabolome analysis revealed that plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis were significantly enriched in resistant rice varieties. Therefore, these results provide valuable information on the molecular mechanisms by which rice defends against U. virens infection, and they will facilitate the development of disease‑resistant rice cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09460-3.Keywords: Ustilaginoidea virens, Rice false smut, Disease resistance, Transcriptome, Metabolome

Introduction

Rice false smut (RFS) caused by Ustilaginoidea virens is one of three major fungal diseases affecting rice in rice-growing regions throughout the world, following rice blight and sheath blight. Infected grains form dark green or black rice balls, reducing rice yield and severely affecting its quality [1]. The fungus produces toxins that are harmful to humans and animals, posing a threat to human and animal health as well as environmental safety [2]. Therefore, the occurrence of this disease is a limiting factor affecting a high and stable yield and high-quality breeding of rice.

Effector proteins or related metabolic substances from plant pathogenic fungi are perceived by their host plants during their interaction [3]. To defend against pathogen invasion, host plants have evolved a complex two-layer innate immune response mechanism: pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP)-triggered immunity (PTI) [4] and effector-triggered immunity (ETI) [5]. Plants secrete enzymes, such as chitinases, β-1,3-glucanases, and superoxide enzymes, to resist the invasion of pathogenic fungi [6]. The secondary metabolites, such as flavonoid, salicylic acid (SA), lignin, and phytoalexins, produced by plants can effectively prevent invasion by pathogenic fungi. SA is a key hormone for plant disease resistance. It can enhance plant defense against pathogen invasion by activating the expression of pathogenesis-related proteins, which results in systemic acquired resistance [7].

Omics approaches, which determine changes in DNA, transcripts, proteins, or metabolites, enable the determination of the molecular mechanisms underlying plant–pathogen interactions. Thus far, the main omics methods include genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics [8]. The multi-omics approach integrates information at different levels, such as the genetic, transcriptional, protein, and metabolic levels, to construct a gene regulatory network. This enables a deeper understanding of the regulatory and causal relationships among molecules, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the gene functions and interaction networks of plants under different stress conditions [9]. Transcriptomics and metabolomics analyses are two relatively mature and widely applied technologies, featuring simple operation procedures and profound analytical capabilities. The comprehensive analysis of the transcriptome and metabolome has been used to evaluate the molecular and physiological conditions of host plants in response to pathogenic fungal infection stress, such as the response of highland barley to powdery mildew [10] and tea plants to blister blight [11]. In our previous study, we used multi-omics analysis to determine the pathogenic mechanism by which U. virens affects indica and japonica rice [12].

At present, there are few reports on the identification of key genes and metabolites involved in the defense response of rice against RFS that combine transcriptomics and metabolomics. In the present study, we employed combined transcriptomics and metabolomics analysis to determine the gene expression and metabolite accumulation in susceptible and resistant rice panicles, analyzing the expression patterns of resistance-related genes and the changes in metabolite accumulation in rice in response to pathogen infection. These results provide a theoretical basis for further elucidating the molecular mechanism of disease resistance in rice and for breeding disease-resistant varieties.

Materials and methods

Fungal cultivation and plant materials

Ustilaginoidea virens strains GY900, MZ1, and PXD25 were preserved at the Plant Protection Institute of Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Chengdu, China). The strains were cultured on potato sucrose agar medium, and hyphae disks were placed in potato sucrose broth (PSB). The PSB cultures were incubated at 28 °C on a shaker at 140 rpm for 12 d. Hyphae and conidia were collected for inoculation.

Resistant rice cultivar ‘IR77298-14-1-2::IRGC117374-1’ (IR27) and susceptible cultivar ‘9311’ were used in this study. The disease resistance of IR27 and 9311 was evaluated from 2021 to 2023. Rice plants were grown under controlled greenhouse conditions at the experimental base of the Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Chengdu, China). During the growth period, conditions were managed according to conventional cultivation management. Inoculation was performed as described by Fu et al. [13] with minor modifications. At the seventh or eighth stage of panicle development, a conidial suspension with a concentration of 3 × 106 mL− 1 (GY900, MZ1, and PXD25 strains) was injected into each rice panicle. Control plants were injected with PSB in all experiments. After inoculation, all rice plants were maintained at 25/30°C (night/day), covered with a sunshade net, and sprayed with water for 10 min every 2 h to maintain a relative humidity > 85% for 4 d. Samples of treatment (resistant, R, and susceptible, S) and control panicles (CK) were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for later use. Three time points at which mycelial symptoms appeared (5, 7, and 9 d) were chosen for sampling. The three samples collected at the infection site from the resistant rice cultivar ‘IR27’ at the three time points were designated R1, R2, and R3, and those from the susceptible cultivar ‘9311’ were designated S1, S2, and S3.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from experiment samples using TRIzol (Aidlab Biotechnologies, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. There were three biological replicates per sample group. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were determined using 1% agarose gel and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). High-quality RNA was sent to Novogene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for library construction and sequencing.

To obtain high-quality clean reads, raw reads were filtered to remove adaptor sequences, duplicate sequences, reads containing more than 10% of unknown sequences (N), and low-quality sequences [14]. The clean reads were mapped onto the reference genome sequence of Oryza sativa L. ssp. indica using HISAT2 to obtain the sample genes [15]. The gene expression level was calculated using HTSeq software (v 0.5.4) and normalized using the fragments per kilobase per million fragments method [16]. DEseq2 was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the rice cultivars before and after inoculation in the R and S groups. Genes with a |log2(foldchange)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value (padj) ≤ 0.05 were considered significant DEGs [17]. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using OmicShare tools, a free online platform for data analysis (www.omicshare.com/tools). Significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways in DEGs, relative to the Nipponbare reference genome (MSU7 background), were defined by a hypergeometric test with padj ≤ 0.05 as the threshold.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation

To validate the DEGs, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted on 10 DEGs, with ubiquitin serving as the internal reference. The primer sets used for qRT-PCR were designed based on individual gene sequences using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool (Supplementary Table S7). The same RNA samples were used for RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) and qRT-PCR. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (TransScript, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed using Takara SYBR Green (Takara, Dalian, China) on a one-step real-time PCR system (Analytik Jena AG, Germany). The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method with triplicate independent samples [18].

Untargeted metabolome detection and analysis

To identify differences in metabolites in resistant and susceptible rice cultivars before and after U. virens infection, 24 samples (3 from each of the 8 groups) were chosen for metabolomics. Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) Q Exactive mass spectrometry (UHPLCQE-MS) was used for untargeted metabolomic profiling. Sample preparation and measurements were performed following previously reported methods with minor modifications [12]. Briefly, the samples were ground to powder in liquid nitrogen. Then, 100 mg of the powder was weighed and dissolved in 2.0 mL of methanol/acetonitrile/water solution (2/2/1, v/v/v), vortexed, ultrasonicated at a low temperature for 30 min, and left to stand at − 20 °C for 12 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 25 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a microporous organic filter membrane (0.22 μm) for analysis using UHPLC. The sample was separated using a Vanquish LC UHPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and then analyzed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) using mass spectrometry on a Q Exactive series mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA).

After mass spectrum analysis of metabolites in samples was performed via triple quadrupole mass spectrometry, the mass spectrum peaks of all substances were integrated, and those of the same metabolites in different samples were calibrated. The data were analyzed qualitatively and quantitatively. Pearson’s correlation coefficients between quality control samples were calculated based on the relative quantitative values of metabolites [19]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the statistical function ‘prcomp’ in R software (http://www.r-project.org/accessed on November 9, 2025). Metabolites between different groups were distinguished by orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified using the following thresholds: variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.0 and p-value < 0.05 [20]. The KEGG database was used to annotate the functions of DAMs, and the metabolic pathways involving DAMs were identified.

Combined transcriptomics and metabolomics

Based on the data from two major omics analyses, the common KEGG pathways of DEGs and DAMs were analyzed. Correlation analysis between the detected genes and metabolites was performed using the ‘cor’ function in R to calculate the Pearson’s correlation coefficients of genes and metabolites. The DEGs and DAMs with a Pearson’s correlation coefficient > 0.8 and a p-value < 0.05 in each group were screened as network maps to show the correlation between metabolites and genes.

Statistical assessment

All data from the experiment were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three replicates for each treatment. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The least significant difference (LSD) test was used for post hoc comparisons among treated groups when ANOVA results were significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Symptoms of false smut in resistant and susceptible cultivars

To confirm RFS resistance or susceptibility in IR27 and 9311, disease symptoms were observed on panicles inoculated with different U. virens strains from 2021 to 2023. The number of infected grains per inoculated spikelet was counted in cultivars IR27 and 9311 (Table 1 and Figure S1). More false smut balls were produced on 9311 panicles than on IR27 panicles for each of three isolates over the 3 years. The average number of false smut balls per panicle on 9311 (38.3 ± 2.30) was significantly more than that on IR27 (1.6 ± 0.51) after PXD25 inoculation. The number of false smut balls produced on both cultivars inoculated with GY900 and MZ1 was less than that formed on the panicles after inoculation with PXD25. The virulence of strains GY900 and MZ1 to both rice cultivars was much less than that of PXD25. Therefore, the PXD25 isolate was chosen for inoculation in further expression profiling analyses.

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis in resistant and susceptible cultivars in response to U. virens infection

Transcriptomes were used to identify DEGs in the samples and determine the molecular response of resistant and susceptible cultivars to PXD25 inoculation. Changes in the gene expression of cultivars IR27 and 9311 at 5, 7, and 9 days after inoculation (dai) with PXD25 were analyzed using RNA-Seq data. A total of 112.8 million clean reads and 158.79 Gb clean bases were generated from 24 samples (susceptible cultivar 9311 and resistant cultivar IR27 at 5, 7, and 9 dai and 6 mock-inoculated controls) (Supplementary Table S1). Compared with the Nipponbare reference genome (MSU7), the mapping efficiency for each sample was > 83%. The error rate, Q20, Q30, and GC content values were < 0.01%, 96.23–98.9%, 90.4–96.78%, and 55.19–56.97%, respectively, indicating that the data were of sufficient quality for further investigation.

To identify the genes involved in RFS resistance, DEGs were identified in IR27 (R) and 9311 (S) at 5, 7, and 9 dai with U. virens compared with mock-inoculated samples (Table 2). At 5 dai, 5995 DEGs (3673 upregulated, 1857 downregulated) were identified in R, compared with 5768 DEGs (2886 upregulated, 2882 downregulated) in S. The number of DEGs in R was highest at 7 dai (7377 DEGs; 3901 upregulated, 3476 downregulated) and 9 dai with PXD25 (6351 DEGs; 4079 upregulated, 2272 downregulated) and exceeded the number of DEGs in S at the same time points (Table 2). The data suggest that differential gene expression was induced to a greater extent in the susceptible cultivar than in the resistant cultivar after U. virens infection.

Metabolic pathways identified after inoculation with U. virens

Pathway-based analysis was conducted using KEGG to investigate the biological functions and interactions of differentially regulated transcripts specific to R and S after U. virens PXD25 inoculation. To determine the differences between the two rice cultivars, KEGG categories with p.adjust < 0.01 were identified to classify upregulated genes into different biological processes (Supplementary Table S2). At 5 dai, the highly represented pathways in R compared with those in S were phosphatidylinositol signaling system (p.adjust = 7.61E − 07), flavonoid biosynthesis (p.adjust = 8.18E − 05), phenylalanine metabolism (p.adjust = 1.05E − 07), diterpenoid biosynthesis (p.adjust = 0.00017), ABC transporters (p.adjust = 0.0045), and phosphatidylinositol signaling system (p.adjust = 0.0053). At 7 dai, the primary pathways enriched in R were phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (p.adjust = 1.26E − 11), phosphatidylinositol signaling system (p. adjust = 2.55E − 05), glutathione metabolism (4.90 E − 05), and plant–pathogen interaction (p.adjust = 6.59E − 05). At 9 dai, the main pathways predominantly enriched in R were diterpenoid biosynthesis (p.adjust = 0.0017), flavonoid biosynthesis (p. adjust = 2.26E − 04), and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (p.adjust = 3.91E − 12). In S, ABC transporters (p.adjust = 0.00248) and plant–pathogen interaction (p.adjust = 1.11E − 04) pathways were significantly enriched. Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and the flavonoid biosynthesis in the R-specific enriched metabolic pathways were significantly enriched across all three inoculation time points. Therefore, we speculate that phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and the flavonoid biosynthesis are closely related to disease resistance in IR27.

Analysis of disease resistance-related DEGs

Differential expression of WRKY transcription factors

WRKY transcription factors comprise one of the largest transcription factor families in plants, and they are widely involved in plant responses to biotic and abiotic stressors. They mainly regulate the expression of target genes and participate in plant responses by specifically binding to the cis-acting element (W-box) in the promoter of target genes [21]. We identified 15 WRKY genes that were differentially expressed in IR27 and 9311 after inoculation with U. virens (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table S3). Among them, the OsWRKY51, OsWRKY55, and OsWRKY56 genes were induced at all three time points in IR27 and significantly inhibited at 5 d in 9311, indicating that these WRKY proteins function as key regulatory factors in the resistance of rice to infection by the RFS fungal pathogen, U. virens.

Analysis of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis-related DEGs

Analysis of DEGs in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway of IR27 and 9311 cultivars revealed that phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) (LOC_Os02g41680 and LOC_Os04g43800), 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL) (LOC_Os02g08100 and LOC_Os08g34790), peroxidase (LOC_Os01g22249, LOC_Os03g22020, LOC_Os05g06970, LOC_Os05g42000, LOC_Os06g16350, LOC_Os06g33080, and LOC_Os09g15500), and phenylalanine/tyrosine ammonia-lyase (PTAL) genes (LOC_Os02g41630 and LOC_Os04g43760) were significantly induced at three time points after inoculation with PXD25 in IR27, but the expression levels of these genes were lower in 9311 than in IR27 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table S4). We speculate that PAL, 4CL, PTAL, and peroxidase genes in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway are involved in the rice disease resistance process.

Analysis of flavonoid biosynthesis-related DEGs

The flavonoid synthesis pathway is closely related to disease resistance in plants. When plants are infected by pathogens, the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway is activated, generating substances with antibacterial activity to prevent further pathogen spread [22]. In this study, we compared the expression of genes in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway in IR27 and 9311 after inoculation with PXD25. The expression levels of 14 genes differed between IR27 and 9311 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table S5). In IR27, 10 genes were significantly upregulated. Only CHS (LOC_Os11g32650), F3H (LOC_Os04g56700), CYP73A (LOC_Os02g26810), and CYP93G1 (LOC_Os04g01140) were significantly upregulated in IR27 and significantly inhibited in 9311 at three time points after inoculation with PXD25. Therefore, we speculate that the CHS, F3H, CYP73A, and CYP93G1 genes in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway play an important role in the resistance of rice to U. virens infection.

Metabolomic analysis

Using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS), untargeted metabolome analysis was carried out to identify the metabolite changes in resistant and susceptible rice cultivars after pathogen infection. First, the variations in metabolites were determined using PCA and PLS-DA. There were differences between the R/S treatments and CK, indicating the stability and replicability of the detection method (Fig. 3a). In addition, PLS-DA score permutations were performed, and evident differences were observed for R1 vs. RCK (R2Y = 1, Q2 = 0.6), R2 vs. RCK (R2Y = 1, Q2 = 0.9), R3 vs. RCK (R2Y = 1, Q2 = 0.91), S1 vs. SCK (R2Y = 1, Q2 = 0.00), S2 vs. SCK (R2Y = 1, Q2 = 0.89), and S3 vs. SCK (R2Y = 1, Q2 = 0.89), demonstrating the suitability of the constructed model. These results indicate that the metabolite profiles of the resistant and susceptible cultivars changed after pathogen infection (Fig. 3b–d, f–h). The DAMs were further screened considering the changes in metabolites in resistant and susceptible rice cultivars after inoculation (Fig. 4a). The DAMs among treatments were illustrated using Venn diagrams (Fig. 4b, c). In the resistant rice cultivar, 26 substances, including lipids and lipid-like molecules (gibberellin, GA), organic acids and their derivatives (pantetheine, d-pantothenic acid, and l-aspartic acid), and benzenoids (SA), were identified. Compared to CK, 83, 209, and 249 DAMs were identified in R1, R2, and R3, respectively. In the susceptible rice cultivar, these groups identified 12 substances, including lipids and lipid-like molecules (jasmonic acid, JA) and organic acids and their derivatives (d-pantothenic acid). Compared with CK, 30, 208, and 225 DAMs were found in S1, S2, and S3, respectively. The results showed that changes in DAMs occurred more frequently in disease-resistant rice than in susceptible rice.

To expand our understanding of the functions of metabolites, we conducted KEGG enrichment analysis on the DAMs. Most of the DAMs were significantly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis in R vs. RCK (Fig. 5a). In S vs. SCK, DAMs were mainly significantly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, pyruvate metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, and pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (Fig. 5b). The top 20 DAMs identified when comparing the resistant and susceptible cultivar before and after inoculation are shown in the order of |log2FC| in Fig. 5c and Supplementary Table S6. The most significant DAM was GA A4, with a log2FC of 7.30 in R1 vs. RCK, 7.41 in R2 vs. RCK, and 9.15 in R3 vs. RCK. According to the human metabolome database (HMDB), GA A4 belongs to the diterpene class. In addition, the significantly accumulated DAMs also included benzene compounds and lipid classes. These results suggest that rice accumulates many secondary metabolites after infection with U. virens, and these substances may be essential for rice to resist pathogen infection.

Response of resistant and susceptible rice cultivars to pathogen infection

Comprehensive analysis of these significant enrichment results showed that numerous DEGs and DAMs were related to plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis. Thus, we conducted in-depth analysis of these pathways (Figs. 6 and 7). In the plant hormone signal transduction pathway, 15 enzymes were annotated, including 9 upregulated and 6 downregulated enzymes (Supplementary Table S7). Specifically, DEGs encoding GID1 in GA A4 were significantly upregulated in R groups. DEGs related to abscisic acid (ABA) synthesis encoding PYL were only upregulated in R groups. DEGs encoding NPR1, TGA, and PR1 in SA were upregulated in R groups (Fig. 6). In addition, we found that the accumulation of salicylic acid was significantly increased in R and was consistent with the expression patterns of the NPR1, TGA, and PR1 genes. Therefore, these results clearly indicated that the defense response system in the R group was activated.

The secondary metabolic pathways related to disease resistance in plants include the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, which together form the core mechanism by which plants resist external stress. Among the annotated DEGs and DAMs in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, DEGs encoding PTAL, 4CL, PAD, and EGS1 were significantly upregulated in the R group. The changes in compounds, including p-coumaric acid, 4-hydroxystyren, and chavicol, were also significantly upregulated (Fig. 7a). As shown in Fig. 7b, five enzymes and two compounds were involved in flavonoid biosynthesis, and the DEGs encoding CHS and chalcone isomerase were significantly upregulated in the R groups compared to the S groups. In addition, the accumulation of naringenin chalcone and naringenin significantly increased after U. virens infection in R, and the accumulation of naringenin chalcone agreed with the results for the CHS gene. The results indicate that these genes and compounds are closely related to U. virens resistance in rice.

To verify the dependability of the RNA-Seq data, we randomly chose 10 DEGs to confirm the sequencing results. Similar to the results of RNA-Seq, the 10 DEGs evaluated using qRT-PCR differed significantly under U. virens infection (Supplementary Table S8).

Correlation analysis

Combined transcriptomics and metabolomics was conducted to determine the functions of DEGs and DAMs in resistant and susceptible rice cultivars after inoculation with U. virens. Many DEGs and DAMs were enriched in the same KEGG pathways, including alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, purine metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, lysine biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. 8a and Supplementary Figure S2). However, only plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis were significantly enriched across the three time points in the disease-resistant rice varieties. Based on these findings, these metabolite changes may be regulated by their respective genes either directly or indirectly, and they may be closely related to how resistant rice responds to U. virens infection.

Based on the Pearson’s correlation coefficient, a correlation network diagram of DEGs and DAMs was constructed to explore the gene regulatory network of resistant rice under U. virens infection. Correlation analysis revealed that PTAL, CAD, and 4CL were highly positively related to p-coumaric acid, and PTAL and 4CL were highly positively related to chavicol in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. Furthermore, CHS was highly positively correlated with naringenin and naringenin chalcone in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. CYP93G1 in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway was positively related to 4CL and CAD in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 8b). These findings indicate that these genes are indispensable for enhancing disease resistance in rice and promoting the accumulation of disease resistance substances.

Discussion

With the large-scale promotion of high-yield and hybrid rice, excessive chemical fertilizer application, and global climate change, RFS has become one of the major diseases in rice-growing regions. Screening rice germplasm resources for resistance to RFS has indicated that some cultivars have relatively stable resistance to RFS, but no disease-related resistance genes have yet been reported [23]. However, the molecular mechanism underlying the durable resistance of rice to RFS is still little understood at present. Due to the development of omics technologies, multi-omics analysis has been widely applied to reveal the complex biological mechanisms by which plant hosts respond to pathogen infection. Transcriptomics and metabolomics, which are relatively mature, in-depth, simple techniques, have been applied in research on the interaction between host plants and pathogenic fungi [24, 25]. Therefore, this study employed transcriptomics and metabolomics to identify changes at the transcriptional and metabolic levels in resistance and susceptible rice cultivars after inoculation with U. virens.

Comparative transcriptomics was used to identify DEGs between RFS-resistant cultivar IR27 and RFS-susceptible cultivar 9311 in response to U. virens at 5, 7, and 9 dai. The expression levels of 11,235 and 13,453 genes were significantly changed in IR27 and 9311, respectively. After inoculation with U. virens, differential gene expression was induced to a greater extent in susceptible cultivar 9311 than in resistant cultivar IR27 at all time points. There were more upregulated genes in 9311 spikelets than in IR27 spikelets. The differential regulation of gene expression among cultivars in this study exhibited a pattern similar to that previously reported for the response of rice plants to inoculation with Rhizoctonia solani [26]. These results indicate that resistant cultivar IR27 rapidly initiates a defense response when infected by U. virens and effectively resists diseases without significantly upregulating gene expression.

Recently, studies have shown that WRKY transcription factors either act as transcriptional activators or repressors, playing a crucial role in the response of rice to pathogen infection [27, 28]. The present study revealed that 15 WRKY transcription factors were differentially regulated in both the resistant and susceptible cultivars after U. virens infection. OsWRKY51, OsWRKY55, and OsWRKY56 were highly upregulated at all three time points in IR27 and suppressed at 5 dai in 9311. Transgenic rice plants overexpressing OsWRKY51 and OsWRKY55 have shown enhanced resistance to blast disease and Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) infection [29, 30]. Therefore, these findings indicate that some WRKY transcription factors, such as OsWRKY51, OsWRKY55, and OsWRKY56, play important roles in the regulation of the rice transcriptome during U. virens infection.

Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and flavonoid biosynthesis are core pathways in plant secondary metabolism, and they play crucial roles in plant disease resistance. Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis is the upstream pathway, providing precursors for disease resistance compounds, such as flavonoids, and the two work together to enhance the plant defense response [31]. In this study, combining transcriptomics and metabolomics data, we determined that DEGs and DAMs were significantly enriched in the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. DEGs encoding PAL, 4CL, PTAL, CHS, and CYP93G1 were significantly upregulated. Genes in the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid pathways (such as PAL and CHS) in Agriophyllum squarrosum are significantly upregulated at high-altitude ecological levels, enhancing stress resistance [32]. p-Coumaric acid, chavicol, naringenin chalcone, and naringenin extensively accumulated under U. virens infection. Studies have shown that metabolites in the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways directly inhibit pathogen growth, acting as signaling molecules, participating in systemic acquired resistance, and coordinating local and systemic defense [33]. We found that the trend between CHS and naringenin was consistent, and correlation analysis showed that CHS was strongly positively correlated with naringenin. Similarly, the trend between PTAL and p-coumaric acid was consistent, and PTAL was strongly positively correlated with p-coumaric acid. These results support previous research and indicate that genes in the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, especially PAL and CHS genes and naringenin and p-coumaric acid, are involved in the resistance response of rice to U. virens infection.

Plant hormone signal transduction plays a crucial regulatory role in plant disease resistance. Plants sense pathogen invasion and activate a series of hormone-mediated signaling pathways (such as ABA, SA, JA, GA, and auxin signaling pathways) to coordinate the defense response [34]. Previous studies have shown that SA accumulation directly inhibits the growth of pathogenic fungi and triggers the expression of downstream defense genes [35]. In this pathway, when SA is combined with the transcriptional activator NPR1, its activity is enhanced, improving the disease resistance of plants [36]. Based our findings, SA accumulation significantly increased, and DEGs encoding NPR1 were significantly upregulated in resistant rice cultivars. In addition, the expression of DEGs encoding TGA and PR1 was upregulated in R. This indicates that SA is crucial for the resistance of disease-resistant rice to U. virens infection. Under biotic and abiotic stress, JA, GA, ABA, and auxin play significant roles in regulating plants’ resistance responses [37]. In the present study, DEGs encoding COI1, JAZ, GID1, PYR, AUX, and ARF showed a decrease and an increase under U. virens infection in resistant rice cultivars. Therefore, we speculate that these hormones play a significant role in the resistance of rice.

Many studies have shown that combined transcriptomics and metabolomics reveals the complexity of biological systems and their interaction networks [36, 38]. Currently, integrated transcriptome and metabolome analyses are widely applied to study pathogen infection mechanisms, including the mechanisms by which soybeans resist infection by Sclerotinia sclerotiorum [24] and wheat responds to infection by Fusarium graminearum [39]. In this study, to obtain an in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms involved in the responses of resistant and susceptible rice cultivars to infection by the pathogen U. virens, combined transcriptomics and metabolomics analysis was performed. Three metabolic pathways (plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis) were significantly co-enriched in the combined analysis in resistant rice cultivars after U. virens infection. Therefore, we speculate that these three metabolic pathways are the key pathways by which rice resists pathogen infection, consistent with previous studies [26, 40]. Network correlation analysis revealed that the DEGs regulating metabolites in the same metabolic pathway differed, further confirming that the molecular mechanisms by which resistant and susceptible rice cultivars resist U. virens infection are distinct. Network correlation analysis revealed a strong correlation between the metabolites in these metabolic pathways and their regulatory genes, especially those of flavonoids and phenylpropanoids, which showed positive or negative regulation.

Conclusion

In our study, transcriptomics and metabolomics were combined to investigate the molecular pathways underlying the response of rice to U. virens infection. DEGs involved in flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and plant hormone signal transduction, especially WRKY51, WRKY55, PAL, 4CL, PTAL, CHS, NPR1, and TGA, may respond to U. virens infection. Lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic acids and their derivatives, and benzenoids, including GA, pantetheine, SA, and naringenin chalcone, accumulated in resistant rice cultivars after U. virens infection, protecting rice plants from the adverse effects of infection and enhancing the plant’s disease resistance. This hypothesis needs to be further verified. This research provides a theoretical basis for exploring the tolerance of rice to pathogen infection and the accumulation of disease resistance substances. This will contribute to further studies on the more complex regulatory mechanisms by which rice responds to pathogen infection.
