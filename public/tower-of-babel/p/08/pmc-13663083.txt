Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses identify OsCGT as essential for C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis in OsDET1-mutant rice seeds
Authors: Backki Kim, Hee-Jin Choi, Sangrae Shim, Seong-Gyu Jang, Dongryung Lee, Endang M Septiningsih, Michael J Thomson, Seung-Hyun Kim, Hee-Jong Koh, Ill-Min Chung, Soon-Wook Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundC-glycosyl flavones rarely accumulate at high levels in rice seeds, and the genetic basis underlying their biosynthesis and regulation remains poorly understood. The rice yel-sdj mutant, carrying a mutation in DE-ETIOLATED 1 (OsDET1), shows elevated accumulation of C-glycosyl flavones in the embryo and pericarp, providing a useful system for dissecting the molecular mechanisms controlling these metabolites. Here, we performed comparative transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, together with genome-editing-based functional validation, to identify key genes involved in C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis in rice seeds.ResultsTranscriptomic profiling of developing seeds revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between wild-type and yel-sdj were significantly enriched in secondary metabolite and flavonoid biosynthetic pathways. Several genes associated with C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis, including the rice C-glycosyltransferase (OsCGT), were upregulated in developing yel-sdj grains. To validate its function, we generated OsCGT knockout lines in the yel-sdj background. Loss of OsCGT markedly reduced the accumulation of C-glycosyl flavones, particularly isoorientin, in the embryo and restored embryo coloration to a wild-type-like phenotype. In contrast, despite the significant reduction in C-glycosyl flavone accumulation, pericarp pigmentation was not obviously altered, and embryo lethality was not rescued.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that OsCGT is essential for C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis in yel-sdj seeds, particularly for isoorientin accumulation in the embryo, and indicate that embryo lethality is a fundamental consequence of the OsDET1 mutation rather than OsCGT-dependent flavone accumulation. This study expands current understanding of flavonoid regulation in rice seeds and identifies OsCGT as a potential target for metabolic engineering to improve the nutraceutical and functional value of rice grains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09551-1.Keywords: OsDET1, OsCGT, C-glycosyl flavones, Isoorientin, CRISPR/Cas9, Transcriptome

Background

Flavonoids are widespread secondary metabolites with diverse functions in plants. From a biological perspective, they contribute to the pigmentation of flowers and fruits, thereby facilitating pollinator attraction [1] and seed dispersal [2], and also participate in plant defense against biotic and abiotic stresses, including pathogen infection [3], UV radiation [4], drought [5], and heavy metal toxicity [6]. From an agricultural and consumer perspective, the color and flavonoid composition of flowers, fruits, vegetables, and grains are important quality traits associated with visual appearance, taste, aroma, and market value [7, 8]. Therefore, understanding the biosynthesis and regulation of flavonoids is important for elucidating plant development and improving crop quality.

Glycosylation of flavonoids plays a crucial role in modifying their solubility and stability, contributing to the bioavailability of secondary metabolites in plants [9]. In plant tissues, flavonoids predominantly exist in glycosylated forms, mainly as O-glycosides and C-glycosides. O-glycosides are formed by attaching sugar moieties to hydroxyl groups on the flavonoid aglycone, whereas in C-glycosides, sugars are directly attached to the flavonoid backbone through carbon–carbon (C-C) bonds [10]. Compared with O-glycosyl flavonoids, C-glycosyl flavonoids contain C-glycosidic linkages that are generally less susceptible to acid hydrolysis and enzymatic deglycosylation, contributing to their relatively high stability under digestive and processing conditions [11, 12]. These properties have led to increasing interest in C-glycosyl flavones as stable dietary flavonoids and plant specialized metabolites.

Consistent with their stability and bioactivity, C-glycosylated flavones are a class of naturally occurring compounds that are widespread in nature. Notably, major cereal crops such as maize (Zea mays), wheat (Triticum aestivum), and rice (Oryza sativa) predominantly produce C-glycosyl flavones [13–15]. In addition, substantial levels of C-glycosyl flavones have been reported in other crops, including buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum) [16], pearl millet (Pennisetum americanum) [17], and flax (Linum usitatissimum) [18]. Among these, the most frequently detected C-glycosides are monoglucosylated flavones, such as vitexin, isovitexin, orientin, and isoorientin. These compounds are synthesized through the action of C-glycosyltransferases (CGTs), which catalyze the transfer of sugar residues to specific flavone substrates, such as apigenin and luteolin. Various CGTs have been identified in several crops, including rice (OsCGT) [13], maize (UGT708A6 and ZmCGT1) [19, 20], buckwheat (FeCGTa and FeCGTb) [16], and soybean (UGT708D1) [21]. Although CGTs have been characterized in various plant species, their functional roles in rice are poorly understood. In particular, the mechanisms by which CGTs influence flavonoid composition in rice seeds remain unclear. Previous metabolite profiling studies have reported low levels of C-glycosyl flavones in most cultivated rice varieties, which may partly explain why the biosynthetic pathway for C-glycosyl flavones has received limited attention in rice.

In our previous study, we identified the yellowish-pericarp embryo lethal (yel) mutant and demonstrated that the corresponding gene, OsDET1, regulates flavonoid accumulation in the embryo and pericarp, as well as embryo development [22]. In the present study, we performed CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of an OsCGT gene to investigate its role in C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis using the yel-sdj mutant, which has high C-glycosyl flavone levels. Specifically, we aimed to clarify the contribution of OsCGT to C-glycosyl flavone accumulation in developing yel-sdj seeds using integrated transcriptomic and targeted metabolomic analyses. Overall, this study provides a foundation for the future breeding of rice varieties enriched with health-promoting flavonoids.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The yel-sdj mutant was derived from the japonica rice cultivar Sindongjin through gamma ray (γ-ray) irradiation. Sindongjin, yel-sdj segregating heterozygous plants, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated OsCGT knockout transgenic lines, F1 plants from crosses between OsCGT knockout lines and yel-sdj segregating heterozygous plants, and the resulting F2 plants were cultivated. All plant materials were grown under conventional field management practices in the Living Modified Organism experimental field (LML 22 − 1) at Pusan National University (Miryang, Korea). Harvested rice grains were air-dried to reduce the moisture content to approximately 13% and stored in an environmentally controlled room at 11 °C for three months. After the storage period, the grains were dehusked and a quality control check was performed to exclude any grains that were physically damaged or visibly discolored by disease. Thereafter the whole grain was then dissected into embryo and endosperm tissues, and the boundary between the two was excised.

Seed germination test

Standard germination test was conducted to evaluate the germination ability of wild-type, yel-sdj, and double mutant seeds. Dehulled seeds were placed on filter paper moistened with sterile distilled water in Petri dishes and incubated at 30 °C. Germination was scored daily, and the final germination rate was calculated at 7 days after incubation. Each experiment included three biological replicates, with 20 seeds per replicate.

Extraction and sample preparation

Total flavonoids and targeted phenolic compounds were extracted from the rice samples using ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) method [23]. Briefly, whole grain rice powder (1 g) was extracted with 10 mL of acetonitrile (ACN) and 2 mL of 0.1 N HCl using a JAC-5020 ultrasonic cleaner ABS (U1tech, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) at 40 kHz for 20 min. The extraction was repeated three times for wild-type (WT) seeds and thirteen times for yel-sdj mutant seeds. All extracts then were concentrated and reconstituted in 5 mL of 80% methanol (MeOH). Thereafter, the final extract was filtered through a 0.22 μm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) syringe filter (CHOICE 13 mm; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Additionally, phenolic compounds were extracted from each embryo (30 mg) and endosperm (100 mg) tissues using the UAE method with slight modifications. This tissue-specific extraction was repeated five times for yel-sdj and oscgt osdet1 double mutant samples, and three times for the WT samples, respectively.

Total Flavonoid Content (TFC)

Briefly, the total flavonoid content of WT and mutant samples was measured using a colorimetric method [23]. Each extract (125 µL) was mixed with 375 µL of MeOH, 25 µL of aluminum nitrate [Al(NO₃)₃], 25 µL of potassium acetate (CH₃COOK), and 700 µL of deionized water (DIW) in a 1.6 mL cuvette. After reacting the mixture for 40 min, the absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. DIW was used as a blank, and the total flavonoid content was expressed as quercetin equivalent per gram of dry weight (µg QUE/g DW).

Targeted phenolics analysis

Briefly, we quantified 56 targeted phenolic compounds and two aromatic amino acids in whole rice grains using a previously reported method based on LC-(-)ESI-MS/MS with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Phenolics were identified by comparing the retention time and mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of the molecular and product ions with those of each authentic phenolic standard [23]. Tissue-specific phenolic profiling of WT and mutant samples was performed using a modified version of the same method to improve detection sensitivity [24]. Notably, the modified method employed an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC; Nexera X3, Shimadzu, Japan) system coupled with a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (LC-MS 8050, Shimadzu). Targeted phenolics were separated using an analytical column (Shim-pack GIST, C18, 2.1 × 100 mm, 2 μm; Shimadzu). The tissue-specific analysis included additional flavonoids, particularly C-glycosyl flavonoids such as isoorientin 2″-O-glucoside, isovitexin 2″-O-glucoside, isoscoparin and isoscoparin 2″-O-glucoside.

Transcriptome sequencing for RNA-Seq

Total RNA was extracted from 7-day-old seeds of Sindongjin (wild type) and the yel-sdj mutants using Takara MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including DNase I treatment to eliminate contaminating genomic DNA. Three biological replicates were used for each genotype, and each replicate was pooled from 10 caryopses. cDNA libraries were prepared for 150 bp paired-end sequencing using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, CA, USA). Briefly, mRNA was purified from 1 µg of total RNA using oligo (dT) magnetic beads. Thereafter, the purified mRNA was fragmented, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers. Second-strand synthesis was performed to generate double-stranded cDNA. After sequential end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation, the cDNA libraries were amplified by PCR for 15 cycles according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, CA, USA), and quantification was performed using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Kapa Biosystems, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster amplification of denatured templates, sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA) in paired-end (2 × 150 bp) mode.

Transcriptome analysis

Raw paired-end sequencing reads were assessed for quality using FastQC (v0.11.9) (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) [25] with Phred + 33 quality-score encoding. Prior to the main alignment, the mean and standard deviation of the inner distance between mate pairs were estimated using a preliminary reference transcriptome-based mapping approach and incorporated into the subsequent paired-end genome alignment. Thereafter, the paired-end RNA-seq reads were aligned to the rice reference genome (IRGSP-1.0 pseudomolecule/MSU7 assembly) [26] using TopHat2 (v2.1.1) [27], with Bowtie2 (v2.3.4.1) [28] serving as its internal alignment engine. This alignment incorporated the reference gene annotations and the mate inner distance distribution to maximize transcriptome mapping accuracy under default parameters. Read counts for each gene’s coding sequence (CDS) were quantified using HTSeq-count (v0.12.4) [29] in ‘union’ mode. Differential expression analysis between the triplicate wild-type and yel-sdj samples was performed using the DESeq2 package (v1.32.0) in R (v4.1.0) [30] and genes with an adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| > 2 were considered differentially expressed. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using the web-based Comprehensive Annotation of Rice Multi-Omics (CARMO) platform (http://bioinfo.sibs.ac.cn/carmo/) [31], with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) [32] pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the TBtools software [33], with a significance threshold of q-value < 0.05. Transcription factor (TF)-encoding DEGs were identified and classified into their respective TF families by querying the DEG list against the Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlnTFDB; http://plntfdb.bio.uni-potsdam.de/v3.0/) [34].

Curation of flavonoid biosynthetic genes

The genes encoding enzymes involved in the flavonoid and anthocyanin biosynthetic pathways in rice were identified through a combination of literature curation and database searches. Known and functionally characterized genes were compiled from public rice annotation databases, including funRiceGenes (https://funricegenes.github.io/) [35] and Oryzabase (https://shigen.nig.ac.jp/rice/oryzabase/) [36]. The gathered genes and their corresponding functional annotations were then verified against the literature to select key biosynthetic genes for subsequent expression analysis.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from developing seeds of the Dongjin (wild type), OsCGT CRISPR/Cas9 knockout mutant, and double mutant using the Takara MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each of the three biological replicates, ten whole caryopses or endosperm parts collected from developing seeds were pooled for RNA extraction. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed to generate first-strand cDNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). RT-qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio™ 1 Real-Time PCR Instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) using FastStart™ Universal SYBR® Green Master (Rox) (Roche, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene ACTIN, and relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) method [37]. Primers used for RT-qPCR analysis are listed in Table S1.

Vector constructs and rice transformation

A CRISPR/Cas9 vector targeting OsCGT was constructed as previously described [38]. Briefly, two guide RNAs (gRNAs) were designed using CRISPR RGEN Tools (http://www.rgenome.net/cas-designer/) [39] and CRISPRdirect (https://crispr.dbcls.jp/) [40]. Each gRNA was individually cloned into the gRNA expression vectors, pYPQ131C (Addgene plasmid #69284) and pYPQ132C (Addgene plasmid #69285). Thereafter, the two gRNA expression cassettes were assembled into the Golden Gate recipient vector pYPQ142 (Addgene plasmid #69294). For the Cas9 expression, the entry vector, pYPQ165 (Addgene plasmid #109327), driven by an egg cell-specific promoter, was used. The final CRISPR/Cas9 binary vector was constructed via a Gateway assembly LR reaction using the Cas9 entry vector (pYPQ165), the assembled gRNA cassette construct (pYPQ142), and the Gateway-compatible binary backbone (pMDC99). Finally, the construct was introduced into the calli of the japonica cultivar Dongjin, via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation using the LBA4404 strain, as described previously [41], with slight modifications. Primers used for vector construction, genotyping, and sequencing of target regions are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

For targeted phenolic metabolite profiling and total flavonoid content (TFC) analysis, results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates, and differences between groups were assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For targeted compound analysis of double-mutant seeds, embryos and endosperms from each genotype were pooled separately, and each pooled sample was divided into two aliquots and independently extracted. The results were presented as the mean ± SD of two extraction-level technical replicates. Differences between the wild type and double mutants were evaluated separately for embryos and endosperms using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Because these duplicate extractions represent technical replicates rather than independent biological replicates, the resulting statistical comparisons should be interpreted with caution and are intended to provide exploratory rather than definitive evidence of genotype-dependent differences. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Analysis System (SAS version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Total flavonoid content and phenolic compound profiling

In this study, the yel-sdj mutant was generated from Sindongjin via gamma-ray-mediated mutagenesis. Notably, the yel-sdj grain exhibited a black embryo and a yellowish or mixed yellow-purple pericarp (Fig. 1A). Total flavonoid content was significantly higher (p < 0.001) in yel-sdj grains (2807.9 ± 242.86 µg QUE/g DW) than in WT grains by approximately 90-fold (Fig. 1B). To better understand the metabolic consequences of the OsDET1 mutation, metabolic profiling was conducted on targeted phenolic compounds belonging to the hydroxybenzoic acids, hydroxycinnamic acids, flavones, and flavonol groups in Sindongjin (WT) and yel-sdj grains. Among the 13 phenolic compounds analyzed, there were significant differences in the levels of 10 compounds (except for p-hydroxybenzoic acid, caffeic acid, and ferulic acid) between WT and yel-sdj (Table 1). Although flavones were the most abundant phenolic group in yel-sdj, they were not detected in WT. Isoorientin was the predominant flavone in yel-sdj, accounting for approximately 89% of the total phenolic content (Table 1). In contrast, anthocyanins were either not detected or remained below the quantification limit in both WT and yel-sdj grains (data not shown).

RNA-seq and DEGs identification of yel-sdj

To investigate differences in gene expression associated with color changes in the embryo and pericarp of yel-sdj during seed development, RNA-seq was performed on 7-day-old developing seeds of Sindongjin (WT) and yel-sdj. From each sample, an average of 86 million paired-end reads were obtained, accounting for 96.6% of the total raw reads. Of these, an average of 81.6% of the paired reads were successfully mapped to the Nipponbare reference genome. A range of 26,640–28,069 genes was expressed in the WT and yel-sdj samples (Table S2). Notably, 1,194 DEGs, including 683 upregulated and 511 downregulated genes, were identified between the WT and yel-sdj samples based on the following thresholds: | log2FC | > 2 and adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05 (Fig. 1C, D).

GO term and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses

GO enrichment analysis was performed to explore the functional significance of DEGs. Importantly, the DEGs were significantly enriched in 20 biological process (BP) terms, 7 cellular component (CC) terms, and 21 molecular function (MF) terms (FDR < 0.05; Table S3). Figure 1E shows the top 20 enriched GO terms for the DEGs. Notably, the DEGs were primarily enriched in “metabolic process,” “response to abiotic stimulus,” and “oxidation-reduction process” in the BP category; in “cytoplasmic membrane-bounded vesicle,” “cell,” and “cell wall” in the CC category; and in “catalytic activity,” “oxidoreductase activity,” and “sequence-specific DNA binding transcription factor activity” in the MF category (Fig. 1E; Table S3). To further investigate the functional pathways potentially involved in seed coloration, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed. A total of 1,194 DEGs were mapped to 155 KEGG pathways (Table S4). Specifically, the DEGs were significantly enriched in nine pathways, including “diterpenoid biosynthesis,” “metabolism,” “biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites,” “metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides,” “cytochrome P450,” “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis,” “flavonoid biosynthesis,” “transcription factors,” and “starch and sucrose metabolism.” In particular, the “metabolism” pathway contained the highest number of DEGs (Fig. 1F; Table S4). Collectively, these findings suggest that genes enriched in these pathways may play pivotal roles in flavonoid biosynthesis and accumulation within the embryo and pericarp of yel-sdj seeds.

Identification of differentially expressed TF genes

TFs are key regulators of secondary metabolite biosynthesis in plants. To investigate the effect of the OsDET1 mutation on TF gene expression, we analyzed changes in the transcript levels of TF-encoding genes. A total of 106 differentially expressed TF genes (57 upregulated and 49 downregulated) representing 30 TF families were identified (Table S5). Among these, AP2-EREBP and bHLH were the most abundant families, followed by HB, MYB, and NAC. Specifically, genes encoding MYB, MYB-related, bZIP and bHLH TFs were predominantly upregulated in developing yel-sdj seeds. In particular, Ra/OSB1, OsC1, and OsP1, which are well-established flavonoid regulatory genes, were significantly upregulated, suggesting their involvement in seed pigmentation during seed development. In contrast, TF genes belonging to the ABI3/VP1, C2H2, GRF, and OFP families were mainly downregulated in developing yel-sdj seeds (Table S5).

Expression profiling of flavonoid biosynthetic genes in yel-sdj seeds

To explore the genes responsible for the pigmentation of yel-sdj seeds, we analyzed the expression of flavonoid biosynthetic genes. First, we screened annotated genes encoding enzymes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis, including the anthocyanin branch. Among the screened genes, expression changes were visualized in the heatmap only for those showing significant differential expression between WT and yel-sdj seeds based on padj < 0.05. As shown in Fig. 2, the pathway involved in C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis was markedly activated in developing yel-sdj seeds. In the phenylpropanoid pathway, the expression of genes such as PAL (LOC_Os04g43800), C4H (LOC_Os01g60450), and 4CL (LOC_Os02g46970) was significantly upregulated in yel-sdj seeds. In addition, genes encoding CHS (LOC_Os11g32650) and CHI (LOC_Os11g02440 and LOC_Os12g02370), which are essential for naringenin biosynthesis, were upregulated in yel-sdj grains. Similarly, genes associated with flavone modification and C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis, including F3’H (LOC_Os10g17260), F2H (LOC_Os06g01250), and CGT (LOC_Os06g18010), were significantly upregulated in yel-sdj seeds. Notably, OsCGT, which catalyzes C-glycosylation to produce C-glycosyl flavones, such as isoorientin and vitexin, was markedly upregulated in developing yel-sdj seeds. In contrast, key genes in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway, including DFR, ANS, and UFGT, showed no significant differences between WT and yel-sdj seeds (Fig. 2). Collectively, these results suggest that the OsDET1 mutation is associated with preferential activation of the C-glycosyl flavone biosynthetic pathway, including the upregulation of key structural genes, such as OsCGT, and subsequent accumulation of C-glycosyl flavones, particularly isoorientin in developing yel-sdj seeds.

Validation of OsCGT function via knockout and double mutants

To further investigate the role of OsCGT in the elevated accumulation of C-glycosyl flavones in yel-sdj mutant seeds and explore whether this accumulation is associated with embryonic lethality, we generated OsCGT knockout (KO) plants using the CRISPR/Cas9 system and developed double mutants with yel-sdj through crossing. Among the T1 transgenic lines, the oscgt#9 − 5 KO line, which carried a 283-bp deletion between the two target sites, was crossed with a heterozygous plant segregating yel-sdj seeds to produce double mutants (Fig. 3A). F1 plants carrying both the OsDET1 (yel-sdj) and OsCGT mutations were selected, and F2 progenies from self-pollinated F1 plants were phenotypically analyzed. Notably, three distinct seed phenotypes were observed in the F2 progenies: normal-type, yel-type, and embryo color-restored phenotypes (Fig. 3B and C). Although the embryo color-restored seeds recovered embryo pigmentation, their pericarp color remained similar to that of the yel-sdj seeds (Fig. 3B and C). Genotyping of the F₂ seeds revealed that all embryo color-restored seeds were homozygous double mutants for osdet1 and oscgt, indicating that the disruption of OsCGT can restore embryo pigmentation but does not influence pericarp coloration.

Quantification of targeted flavones and assessment of germination ability

To determine the role of OsCGT in flavonoid biosynthesis, 11 targeted compounds, including flavanones and flavones, were quantified in the embryos and endosperms of WT, yel-sdj, and double mutant seeds. Notably, the C-glycosyl flavone contents, such as isoorientin, isoscoparin, and isovitexin were significantly lower in the embryo and endosperm of the double mutant than in those of the yel-sdj mutant (Table 2). Similarly, the levels of the two flavanones, eriodictyol and naringenin, were significantly decreased in the embryo of the double mutant. In contrast, the levels of flavones, such as luteolin, apigenin, and tricin, were higher in the endosperm of the double mutant than in that of the yel-sdj. Notably, luteolin was the predominant compound in the endosperm of the double mutant, and apigenin levels were significantly elevated in the embryo and endosperm of the double mutant compared to the yel-sdj mutant seeds. Overall, the total content of the targeted compounds in the embryo and endosperm of the double mutant was reduced by approximately 99 and 82%, respectively, compared with those of the yel-sdj (Table 2). These results suggest that the knockout of OsCGT in the double mutant seeds partially restores embryo pigmentation by reducing C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis, indicating that OsCGT plays a crucial role in the production of C-glycosyl flavones in rice seeds. Moreover, we performed a germination test to assess whether reduced flavone content could rescue embryo lethality. However, while WT seeds germinated normally, double mutant seeds failed to germinate under standard germination conditions, similar to yel-sdj seeds (Fig. 3D), indicating that reduced flavone content did not rescue the embryo-lethal phenotype. Collectively, these findings suggest that embryo lethality in the yel-sdj is primarily due to embryonic developmental defects caused by the OsDET1 mutation rather than flavonoid accumulation or toxicity in the embryo.

Discussion

In flavonoid biosynthesis, the formation of C-glycosyl flavones is mediated by C-glycosyltransferases. In rice, OsCGT has been identified as a glycosyltransferase that produces flavone-6-C-glucosides using 2-hydroxyflavanone as substrates. These substrates are derived from flavanones by flavanones 2-hydroxylase, as shown by recombinant protein expression and in vitro enzyme assays [13, 43]. Given that C-glycosyl flavones rarely accumulate to high levels in the seeds of natural rice germplasm, assessing the in vivo contribution of OsCGT to seeds has remained challenging. In this study, we performed metabolite and transcriptome analyses of the yel-sdj mutant, which accumulates high levels of C-glycosyl flavones in rice seeds due to a mutation in OsDET1. Expression profiling of flavonoid biosynthetic genes revealed activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway, with a pronounced upregulation of the C-glycosyl flavone biosynthetic branch in developing yel-sdj seeds. Notably, OsCGT expression was strongly induced in the yel-sdj during seed development. These transcriptional changes are consistent with our targeted metabolite profiling, which showed a marked accumulation of C-glycosyl flavones, particularly isoorientin, in yel-sdj grains (Table 1; Fig. 2). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the yel-sdj mutant serves as a valuable in vivo platform for the functional validation of OsCGT, revealing that OsCGT plays a critical role in C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis during rice seed development.

Our study revealed that the levels of C-glycosyl flavones and their derivatives were markedly reduced in both the embryo and endosperm of the oscgt osdet1 double mutant (Table 2). Interestingly, however, despite the genetic knockout of OsCGT, low but detectable levels of isoorientin and isoscoparin remained in the double mutant embryo (Table 2). Notably, the relative content of isoscoparin in the double mutant embryo actually increased compared to other targeted metabolites. This residual accumulation, as well as the unexpected increase in the proportion of isoscoparin, suggests that the blockage of the primary pathway might have activated alternative biosynthetic routes, where other enzymes utilize alternative intermediates to synthesize these metabolites [44]. Furthermore, this phenomenon is likely attributed to functional redundancy within the massive UDP-glycosyltransferase (UGT) gene family in rice [45]. Although OsCGT serves as the predominant enzyme for C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis in the embryo, other homologous OsCGT-like genes or related UGTs may exhibit minor cross-activity, partially compensating for the loss of OsCGT. Accordingly, we examined the expression of OsCGT-like genes, orthologs to OsCGT, to assess whether functional redundancy could compensate for the loss of OsCGT at the transcription level. None of the OsCGT-like genes, including OsCGT-like-3 (Os06g0289200/LOC_Os06g18140), which has been reported to be specifically expressed in the endosperm [42], showed significant expression changes between yel-sdj and the double mutant in either whole grains or endosperms (Fig. 4). Overall, these results suggest that while the primary OsCGT-like candidates analyzed here may not be the main contributors, it remains possible that other unidentified members of the extensive UGT family or less conserved OsCGT homologs, which were not targeted in this study, could facilitate the residual C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis observed in the yel-sdj (osdet1) and the double mutant (oscgt osdet1).

In addition, the pericarp color of the double mutant remained similar to that in yel-sdj upon visual inspection, whereas the embryo color was restored to that of the WT seeds (Fig. 3B and C). Our targeted metabolite analysis further showed that OsCGT knockout significantly reduced the accumulation of most downstream metabolites, such as C-glycosyl flavones, in both the embryo and endosperm of the double mutant seeds compared with that of the yel-sdj. Conversely, the levels of targeted flavanones and non-glycosylated flavones (except for eriodictyol), which are synthesized upstream of OsCGT, were significantly increased in the endosperm of the double mutant (Table 2). Although several targeted flavanones and non-glycosylated flavones were significantly increased in the endosperm of the double mutant compared with that of yel-sdj, these changes are unlikely to fully account for the persistent yellowish pericarp color. Therefore, these findings suggest the potential involvement of untargeted metabolites or alternative biosynthetic pathways that contribute to the yellowish pericarp phenotype in both yel-sdj and the double mutant. Supporting this possibility, flavone metabolism in rice seeds has been shown to be highly compartmentalized. In mature Nipponbare seeds, schaftoside and its two isomers were reported as the only flavonoids accumulated in the pericarp/testa tissue, whereas a set of flavones was detected predominantly in the embryo [46]. In addition, quercetin-3-O-glucoside and cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, although known to occur in multiple vegetative and reproductive tissues, were detected in pericarp samples but not in the corresponding embryo samples when developing seeds of purple-pericarp rice were analyzed at the tissue level [47]. Therefore, pericarp-enriched C-glycosyl flavones or other flavonoid pigments not included in our targeted metabolites may contribute to the persistent yellowish pericarp coloration observed in yel-sdj and double mutants. However, tissue-specific flavonoid biosynthesis in rice seeds remains poorly understood. Further studies of OsCGT-regulated metabolites in rice, particularly those focusing on tissue-specific compounds in the embryo and pericarp, are necessary.

One of the striking aspects of the yel-sdj mutant is its loss of germinative capacity. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the embryo viability or germination ability in yel-sdj seeds might be compromised by excessive flavonoid accumulation. However, although C-glycosyl flavone levels, particularly in the embryos, were significantly reduced in the double mutant seeds, the double mutant still failed to germinate (Fig. 3D), indicating that the embryo lethality was independent of flavonoid accumulation. A similar phenomenon has been reported in Arabidopsis [48]. To examine the role of anthocyanin accumulation in the seedling lethality of fusca mutants, double mutants were generated by combining fusca alleles that accumulate high levels of anthocyanins in cotyledons and seedlings and exhibit seedling lethality with mutations in flavonoid biosynthesis genes (TT and TTG). Although the double mutants lacked anthocyanins, the seedlings remained non-viable under both light and dark conditions, indicating that lethality was independent of flavonoid accumulation. Consistent with this finding, our analysis of differentially expressed TFs revealed the predominant downregulation of several TF families with well-established roles in plant development, including ABI3/VP1, AP2-EREBP, and Homeobox (HB) (Table S5). In particular, key developmental regulators, such as LEAFY COTYLEDON (LEC) [49, 50], BABY BOOM (BBM) [51], GROWTH-REGULATING FACTOR (GRF) [52, 53], and WUSCHEL-related homeobox (WOX) genes [54], including OsLEC1, OsLEC2, OsBBM3, OsGRF10, OsWOX6, and OsWOX9C, were significantly downregulated in yel-sdj (Table S5). The essential roles of several regulators in rice embryogenesis have been directly validated. For example, knockout of OsLEC1/OsNF-YB7 causes abnormal embryo development and embryonic lethality [55]. Similarly, the simultaneous knockout of OsBBM1, OsBBM2, and OsBBM3 induces embryo arrest and abortion, indicating that these genes function as key embryogenic triggers required to initiate the zygotic developmental program and maintain embryo identity [56]. In addition, WOX proteins, which form a plant-specific subclade within the HB TF family, play critical roles during early embryogenesis; the oswox9a oswox9c double mutant exhibits germination failure due to early abnormal embryo development and arrest [57, 58]. Taken together, these results support the conclusion that embryo developmental defects and secondary metabolite accumulation are independently regulated in the yel-sdj mutant. Therefore, the embryo abnormalities observed in this study are more likely due to a fundamental developmental defect caused by the loss of OsDET1 function rather than flavonoid overaccumulation.

Conclusions

This study advances current understanding of C-glycosyl flavone biosynthesis in rice seeds by integrating comparative transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses with genome-editing-based functional validation in the yel-sdj mutant background. Our findings identify OsCGT as a key determinant of C-glycosyl flavone accumulation, particularly in the embryo, and further demonstrate that the embryo lethality associated with yel-sdj is a fundamental developmental consequence of the OsDET1 mutation rather than a secondary effect of flavonoid overaccumulation. The persistence of yellowish pericarp pigmentation following OsCGT disruption in the yel-sdj mutant background additionally suggests that flavonoid metabolism in rice seeds is regulated in a tissue-specific manner and involves metabolic components beyond OsCGT-dependent C-glycosyl flavones. Collectively, this work provides a useful framework for dissecting seed-specific flavonoid regulation in rice and highlights OsCGT as a promising target for metabolic engineering aimed at improving the nutritional and functional quality of rice grains.
