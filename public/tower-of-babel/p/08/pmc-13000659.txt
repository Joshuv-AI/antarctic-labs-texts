Red Mud-Based MIL-53(Fe) for the Efficient Adsorption of Methylene Blue: Preparation, Adsorption Performance, and Mechanism
Authors: Shengqian Wu, Xiaocheng Wang, Yun Ma, Lin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work reports the use of red mud (RM) (a widely available industrial byproduct) as the primary raw material for synthesizing cost-effective MIL-53­(Fe) (denoted as RM-MIL-53­(Fe)), which was subsequently applied for the high-efficiency removal of methylene blue (MB) from aqueous environments. The optimum preparation conditions of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) were determined by analyzing the crystal structure and adsorption performance, as follows: pH 1.85, mass ratio of RM to terephthalic acid disodium salt (Na2BDC) 1:0.5, stirring speed 450 rpm, and temperature 80 °C. Characterization results verified the successful construction of a metal–organic framework possessing the typical MIL-53­(Fe) topology, which exhibited a notably high specific surface area of 176.5 mg·g–1. Fitting the equilibrium adsorption data to the Langmuir model yielded a high correlation coefficient, indicating that MB uptake occurred via monolayer coverage on a uniform surface. The calculated saturation capacity reached 167.8 mg·g–1. Thermodynamic parameters demonstrated that the MB adsorption process on RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was thermodynamically spontaneous, where chemisorption acted as the dominant mechanism, and electrostatic interaction served as the auxiliary pathway. Kinetic data fitting results exhibited a superior correlation with the pseudo-second-order kinetic model, further confirming the predominance of chemisorption in the adsorption process. Furthermore, intraparticle diffusion and liquid film diffusion were identified as the colimiting steps governing the adsorption rate. The adsorption efficacy of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was strongly dependent on the sorbent dosage, solution pH, and coexisting ionic species. Mechanistic analysis revealed that MB retention on RM-MIL-53­(Fe) proceeded via a combination of hydrogen bonds, π–π stacking interactions, electrostatic forces, and intrapore filling.

Introduction

The accelerated expansion of the textile, printing, dyeing, and papermaking sectors has led to the widespread use of synthetic dyes, positioning them as a significant source of global aquatic pollution. , It has been reported that over 700,000 tons of synthetic dyes are discharged annually into aquatic environments worldwide. These dye molecules, characterized by complex aromatic structures and high chemical stability, result in reduced water transparency, diminished dissolved oxygen levels, and severe ecological risks to aquatic organisms and human health. Thus, developing efficient, cost-effective, and eco-friendly treatment technologies for dye-contaminated wastewater has become a research hotspot in the field of environmental science and engineering.

Current remediation strategies for dye-contaminated wastewater include chemical oxidation, biodegradation, membrane separation, and adsorption processes. − Among these techniques, adsorption is recognized as one of the most promising methods due to its advantages of simple operation, controllable cost, and low requirements for infrastructure. Conventional adsorbents such as activated carbon and zeolites, however, face limitations in practical applications owing to their restricted adsorption capacities, low regeneration efficiencies, and potential secondary pollution risks. Recent advancements have focused on designing novel adsorbents, including biomass-derived carbons, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), and nanocomposites, which demonstrate enhanced adsorption selectivity and cyclic stability through structural modifications or functionalization. MOFs, in particular, have garnered significant attention as porous crystalline materials with ultrahigh surface areas, tunable pore architectures, and versatile chemical functionalities, showing exceptional potential in gas adsorption, catalysis, and environmental remediation. , Despite their remarkable dye adsorption performance, widespread industrial application of MOFs remains constrained by their reliance on costly metal precursors and organic ligands during synthesis.

Concurrently, the valorization of industrial solid waste has gained prominence as a sustainable strategy for resource conservation, environmental mitigation, and circular economy development. Red mud (RM), a highly alkaline byproduct generated during alumina production (1.0–2.5 tons per ton of alumina), represents a critical environmental challenge due to its massive stockpiling in marine or terrestrial repositories, which poses risks of ecosystem degradation and land occupation. − Recent efforts have been made in RM reutilization. In a study by Li and co-workers, red mud was converted into a Fe2O3/Zn–Al layered double hydroxide and evaluated as a photo-Fenton catalyst. The as-synthesized composite exhibited pronounced photocatalytic activity, achieving 85.3% degradation of tetracycline in an aqueous solution. Fang et al. prepared a complex of humic acid and RM and used it for the adsorption of methylene blue (MB). The as-prepared composite exhibited favorable MB adsorption performance, achieving a removal efficiency of 90%. Notably, the abundant metal oxides (e.g., Fe2O3, Al2O3, and TiO2) in RM share compositional similarities with the metal nodes of MOFs, suggesting a promising pathway for synthesizing MOF-based adsorbents using RM as a low-cost metal source. However, research on RM-based MOFs for environmental applications remains scarce, highlighting both a scientific gap and an opportunity for sustainable material innovation. This approach could simultaneously address two pressing issues: reducing hazardous waste accumulation and developing economical adsorbents for water purification.

In this work, low-cost RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was prepared by using RM as the raw material. The structural characteristics of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) were investigated by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET), field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. The adsorption performance and mechanism of MB on RM-MIL-53­(Fe) were further explored using batch adsorption experiments and characterization techniques. The influence of various experimental factors such as adsorbent dosage, solution pH, and coexisting ions were also investigated. In summary, this study is dedicated to developing a low-cost, MOF-based adsorbent with excellent adsorption capacity, which can realize the efficient sequestration of methylene blue from aqueous systems.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Red mud (RM) was supplied by Apple Aluminum Co., Ltd. (Guangxi, China). Terephthalic acid disodium salt (Na2BDC), hydrochloric acid (HCl), anhydrous ethanol, N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF), sodium chloride (NaCl), calcium chloride (CaCl2), and methylene blue (MB) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Deionized water was produced by XIUILAB UPSY-40L ultrapure water system (Shanghai, China), and the resistivity value was 18.25 MΩ·cm–1 at 25 °C.

Materials Preparation

Precise amount (6 g) of RM was washed 3 times with the mixed solution of anhydrous ethanol and deionized water (V anhydrous ethanol:V deionized water = 7:3) to obtain the pretreated RM. Afterward, the pretreated RM was added to 50 mL of HCl solution (3 mol·L–1), followed by mechanical stirring for 12 h, and the pH of the resulting filtrate was adjusted to different values (1.75, 1.80, 1.85, 1.90, 1.95) after filtration. Furthermore, a different amount of Na2BDC (m RM:m Na2BDC = 1:0.3, 1:0.5, 1:0.625,1:0.8, 1:1) was added to the above filtrate. The mixed solution was continuously stirred at different speeds (300, 350, 400, 450, 500 rpm) and different temperatures (70, 80, 90, 100, 120 °C) for 2 h, followed by mechanical stirring for an additional 12 h. Finally, the mixed solution was centrifuged and the precipitate was washed 3 times with deionized water, DMF, and anhydrous ethanol, respectively. Then, the precipitate was placed in an oven to dry to constant weight, and named as RM-MIL-53­(Fe).

Material Characterizations

A Rigaku SmartLab 3 kW diffractometer (Japan) employing Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5405 Å) was used to collect PXRD data for RM-MIL-53­(Fe) across a 2θ interval of 5–45°, at a step width of 0.02° and a scan speed of 10°·min–1.

FT-IR characterization of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) before and after MB adsorption was performed on a PerkinElmer instrument under ambient conditions. Scans were recorded from 4000 to 400 cm–1 with 4 cm–1 spectral resolution.

Specific surface area and porosity characteristics were evaluated through N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K by employing a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 M analyzer. Prior to analysis, the samples were degassed at 150 °C for 12 h.

Using a Hitachi SU8010 FE-SEM instrument (Japan) with an accelerating voltage of 5 kV, the surface morphology of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was observed. The samples were precoated with a 5 nm gold layer via sputtering to ensure sufficient conductivity during imaging.

XPS was carried out on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250 XI spectrometer to probe the elemental composition and chemical states of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) both prior to and following MB adsorption. High-resolution spectra were recorded by employing a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV) equipped with charge neutralization.

Adsorption Thermodynamics

Thermodynamic studies were carried out by shaking 50 mg of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) with 50 mL of MB solution (100 mg·L–1) at 150 rpm in a thermostatic shaker. The system was maintained at 30, 40, or 50 °C for 4 h, with the initial pH of the MB solution left unadjusted (∼6.5–7.0). The suspension was then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was analyzed for c e using UV–vis spectrophotometry. To investigate the adsorption kinetics, 50 mg of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was added to 100 mL of MB solution at initial concentrations of 10, 20, and 30 mg·L–1. At predetermined time intervals (5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 60, and 90 min), the residual MB concentration was measured using a UV–vis spectrophotometer at 664 nm. Computational Details

We performed all DFT-based simulations for methylene blue (MB) using the Materials Studio 2020 suite (BIOVIA, Dassault Systèmes). The molecular framework was first built and subjected to preliminary optimization in the Visualizer module, followed by rigorous geometry optimization and electronic property evaluation with the Dmol3 module. The ground-state geometry was optimized at the GGA/PBE level by employing a DNP basis set, with convergence criteria fixed at 1.0 × 10–5 Ha (energy), 0.002 Ha/Å (force), and 0.005 Å (displacement). The SCF procedure relied on the direct mixing scheme, requiring a tolerance of 1.0 × 10–6 Ha and allowing up to 1000 cycles. Solvation was accounted for by the COSMO model, using a dielectric constant of 78.54 to emulate water.

After successful optimization, a single-point energy calculation was performed at the same theoretical level to extract the HOMO/LUMO energies and spatial distributions. The electrostatic potential (ESP) map was then constructed from the resulting electron density.

To pinpoint reactive sites, we computed condensed Fukui indices for both nucleophilic and electrophilic attacks via the finite difference approximation. Hirshfeld population analysis was applied to the neutral, cationic, and anionic charge states of MB.

Surface Charge Characterization

The surface charge characteristics of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) as a function of pH were assessed by measuring its point of zero charge (pHpzc) via the pH drift method. In brief, 50 mg portions of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) were introduced into 50 mL aliquots of 0.01 M NaCl solution, each preadjusted to an initial pH (pHi) ranging from 2.0 to 10.0 with 0.1 M HCl or NaOH. These suspensions were equilibrated under constant shaking (150 rpm, 25 °C) for 24 h. The final pH (pHf) of each solution was then measured, and the pHpzc was taken as the pH value at which no net change occurred (i.e., ΔpH = 0).

Results and Discussion

Effect of Preparation Condition on Crystal Structure and Removal Rate

Effect of pH

To evaluate the impact of pH, MOFs were prepared at different pH (1.75, 1.80, 1.85, 1.90, 1.95) under the following conditions: RM-to-Na2BDC mass ratio (m RM:m Na2BDC) 1:0.625, stirring speed 400 rpm, and temperature 90 °C.

As evidenced by the XRD pattern in Figure A, the synthesized MOFs exhibit characteristic Bragg reflections at 2θ = 8.9, 15.0, and 21.3°. The observed reflections at 8.9, 15.0, and 24.3° 2θ are assignable to the (110), (200), and (211) facets of the MIL-53­(Fe) phase (JCPDS 00-052-1509), consistent with simulated patterns and prior literature. , This indicates that RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was successfully synthesized. The solution pH constitutes a critical governing parameter in the hydrothermal synthesis of RM-MIL-53­(Fe), exerting deterministic control over the hydrolysis kinetics of Fe3+ precursors (Fe­(H2O)6 3+ ⇌ Fe­(OH) x (3–x)+ + xH+), regulating the deprotonation equilibria of terephthalic acid ligands (H2BDC ⇌ HBDC– ⇌ BDC2–), and collectively dictating the crystalline phase selectivity, crystal habit development, and framework stability through modulation of nucleation and growth kinetics. − Increasing the pH initially enhanced the (200) diffraction peak intensity, which reached its apex at 1.85, and then progressively declined, as depicted in Figure A. The reason for this is that a pH substantially lower than 1.85 results in a very low concentration of the fully deprotonated BDC2– ligand (pK a2 of H2BDC ≈ 5.51), which is necessary for coordination, thereby limiting framework formation. Conversely, a pH higher than the optimum (1.85) increases the rate of Fe3+ hydrolysis, promoting the formation of iron hydroxo species (e.g., Fe­(OH)2+) that compete with the organic linker for metal coordination. This can lead to the incorporation of oxyhydroxide impurities or amorphous phases, destabilizing the pure MIL-53­(Fe) framework and reducing its crystallinity and, consequently, its adsorption capacity.

Effects of pH (A, a), m RM:m Na2BDC (B, b), stirring speed (C, c), and preparation temperature (D, d) on crystal structure and removal rate of MB.

Figure a shows the adsorption removal rate of MB by RM-MIL-53­(Fe) prepared at different pH. As presented in Figure a, a relatively low MB removal rate was observed when the solution pH was below 1.85. This unsatisfactory performance can be primarily attributed to the leaching of Fe3+ centers, decomposition of the organic ligand, and destabilization of the MOF architecture under highly acidic conditions, which severely reduced the number of accessible adsorption sites. In addition, the excessive H+ concentration facilitated protonation of the surface functional groups on RM-MIL-53­(Fe), creating a positively charged surface that electrostatically repelled the cationic MB dye and, thus, further impeded adsorption. However, when the pH was higher than 1.85, the ligand may undergo hydrolysis or induce Fe­(OH)3 precipitation, thereby destabilizing the MOFs framework and reducing its adsorption capacity.

Effect of m RM:m Na2BDC

To evaluate the impact of m RM:m Na2BDC, RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was prepared at different m RM:m Na2BDC (1:0.3, 1:0.5, 1:0.625, 1:0.8, 1:1) under the following conditions: pH 1.85, stirring speed 400 rpm, and temperature 90 °C.

Figure B indicates the XRD patterns of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) prepared at different m RM:m Na2BDC. All synthesized MOFs exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to the typical crystalline structure of MIL-53­(Fe). The maximum peak intensity of the (200) plane of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was observed at a m RM:m Na2BDC ratio of 1:0.5. Deviations from this mass ratio resulted in a significant weakening of the peak intensity. A similar phenomenon was also observed by Burtch, which was attributed to two primary factors: (1) excessive RM content induced incomplete coordination between its constituent metal ions and ligands, resulting in amorphous byproduct formation and consequent reduction in RM-MIL-53­(Fe) crystallinity; (2) insufficient ligand availability inhibited the full incorporation of Fe3+ into the framework assembly. Residual Fe3+ persisted in free states or hydroxylated forms, thereby impeding the crystal nucleation and growth of RM-MIL-53­(Fe). When the ligand is in excess, it cannot sufficiently coordinate with Fe3+ to form a low-dimensional structure (e.g., layered or chained Fe-BDCs), which in turn leads to a weakening of the intensity of the characteristic peaks of the RM-MIL-53­(Fe) or the appearance of the unknown phase.

Figure b illustrates the MB removal efficiency of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) synthesized under various m RM:m Na2BDC. The removal rate rose progressively as the ratio increased, peaked at 1:0.5, and thereafter dropped sharply upon a further ratio elevation. Under the appropriate ratios, RM-MIL-53­(Fe) formed a highly crystalline single phase, regular one-dimensional rhombic pores, and abundant binding sites, which in turn facilitated the adsorption of MB. High or low m RM:m Na2BDC could lead to incomplete or clogged pore structure of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) as well as reduction of adsorption sites, which ultimately leads to a lower MB removal rate.

Effect of Stirring Speed

To evaluate the impact of stirring speed, RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was prepared at different stirring speeds (300, 350, 400, 450, 500 rpm) under the following conditions: pH 1.85, m RM:m Na2BDC 1:0.5, and temperature 90 °C.

Figure C presents the XRD profiles of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) synthesized at different stirring speeds. As the agitation rate rose from 300 to 450 rpm, the (200) diffraction peak intensity progressively intensified, but declined upon further speed increase. This behavior is ascribed to the fact that a moderate stirring speed facilitates mass transfer, accelerates reactant diffusion, shortens nucleation duration, and promotes the formation of well-defined, homogeneous crystals. Conversely, excessively high stirring speeds may generate shear forces that disrupt crystal growth or induce amorphous byproducts. Figure c displays the removal rate of MB by RM-MIL-53­(Fe) prepared at different stirring speeds. As shown in Figure c, the RM-MIL-53­(Fe) prepared at 450 rpm showed the strongest adsorption capacity for MB. This is mainly due to the fact that the crystal structure of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was most integrated at a stirring speed of 450 rpm, which has been confirmed by the results of the XRD analysis above. Typically, MOFs with higher structural integrity tend to have highly ordered microporous/mesoporous structures, higher specific surface area and pore volume, which in turn provide more adsorption sites.

Effect of Preparation Temperature

To evaluate the impact of temperature, RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was prepared at different temperatures (70, 80, 90, 100, 120 °C)­under the following conditions: pH 1.85, m RM:m Na2BDC 1:0.5, and stirring speed 450 r·min–1.

Figure D presents the XRD patterns of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) prepared at different temperatures. According to Figure D, when the preparation temperature was 70 °C, the RM-MIL-53­(Fe) showed the relatively weak peak intensity of the (200) plane. The possible reason for this could be that when MOFs were prepared at relatively low temperatures, the reaction rate slows down, the nucleation time was prolonged, and small crystals or amorphous products may eventually be produced. The peak intensity at the (200) plane gradually increased with further increase in preparation temperature. Appropriately increased temperatures would result in a significant acceleration of the reaction rate, intensive nucleation, and the production of small-sized nanocrystals. However, further increase in temperature accelerated the hydrolysis of Fe3+, leading to the generation of iron oxide impurities (e.g., FeOOH or Fe3O4), which in turn reduced the peak intensity of the (200) plane.

Figure d illustrates the MB removal efficiency of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) synthesized at various temperatures. The optimal adsorption capacity was attained at 80 °C, which is attributed to the well-preserved crystalline framework and abundant binding sites of the material, as corroborated by the XRD analysis. However, further elevation of the synthesis temperature promoted the hydrolysis of Fe3+ ions, resulting in the formation of iron oxide impurities such as FeOOH or Fe3O4. Additionally, excessively high temperatures may induce the transformation of the MOF structure into a closed-pore configuration, thereby reducing the availability of accessible adsorption sites.

Based on the above crystal structure analysis and the adsorption performance of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) for MB, the optimum prepared conditions of the RM-MIL-53­(Fe) are as follows: pH 1.85, m RM:m Na2BDC 1:0.5, stirring speed 450 rpm, and temperature 80 °C.

Structural Characterization

The molecular structure and functional groups of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) were interrogated by FT-IR spectroscopy, with the resulting spectrum displayed in Figure a. A broad absorption centered at 3408 cm–1 originated from the O–H stretching vibration of surface-adsorbed water molecules. The distinct bands observed at 1676, 1514, 1407, 1020, and 756 cm–1 are characteristic of carboxylate vibrational modes and accord well with previously reported data. , Specifically, the doublet at 1543 and 1396 cm–1 corresponds to the asymmetric (νas(COO–)) and symmetric (νs(COO–)) stretching vibrations of the carboxylate groups, respectively, providing clear evidence for the successful incorporation of the dicarboxylate linker into the framework. Additionally, the signal at 750 cm–1 was ascribed to the C–H bending vibration of the aromatic ring.

FT-IR spectra (a), N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms (b), SEM image (c), and XPS spectroscopy of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) (d–g).

The porous properties of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) were evaluated by N2 physisorption measurements. As displayed in Figure b, the sample exhibited a Type IV isotherm with an H2­(b)-type hysteresis loop, indicative of a hierarchically ordered mesoporous architecture featuring a trimodal pore size distribution. Analysis by the BJH method yielded a total pore volume of 0.33 cm3·g–1, an average pore diameter of 9.53 nm, and a geometric standard deviation of 0.28 nm, confirming the highly uniform mesoporosity of the material. Compared with raw red mud (6.35 m2·g–1), RM-MIL-53­(Fe) possessed a markedly enhanced Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area of 176.5 m2·g–1 (Table ). This 27.8-fold increase in the specific surface area, together with the expanded pore network, significantly improved the accessibility of active sites and the molecular accommodation capacity, thereby boosting the overall adsorption performance. The morphology of the as-synthesized product was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Figure c reveals well-defined, monodisperse hexagonal bipyramidal crystals, which are in good agreement with the crystallographic characteristics documented for MIL-53­(Fe) in earlier reports.

High-resolution XPS measurements were employed to probe the elemental speciation and bonding configurations of RM-MIL-53­(Fe), enabling the quantitative assessment of its surface chemical states. The survey spectrum (Figure d) exhibits distinct core-level signals attributable to C 1s, O 1s, and Fe 2p, confirming the presence of the expected framework constituents on the material surface. The deconvoluted C 1s spectrum (Figure e) displays three components at 284.8 eV (C–C/C  C), 285.4 eV (C–O–C), and 288.8 eV (O–CO), corresponding to aliphatic/olefinic carbon, ether linkages, and carboxylate groups, respectively. In the O 1s region (Figure f), two dominant peaks appear at 532.2 eV (terminal oxygen, OC–O–) and 533.8 eV (bridging oxygen, C–O–), characteristic of the asymmetric charge distribution associated with bidentate carboxylate coordination of the BDC linker. Batch Adsorption Experiments

Adsorption Isotherm

The adsorption equilibrium of MB onto RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was investigated through isotherm studies, with the results depicted in Figure a. The uptake capacity increased steeply at low MB concentrations (0–15 mg·L–1) and gradually approached a plateau at higher concentrations, indicative of monolayer saturation. To gain mechanistic insight, the equilibrium data were fitted to the Langmuir (Figure b) and Freundlich (Figure c) models; the derived parameters are compiled in Table . The Langmuir isotherm yielded an excellent correlation (R 2 ≈ 1), substantially outperforming the Freundlich model. This preference is further supported by the Freundlich exponent 1/n = 0.149 (<1), which confirms conformity with the Langmuir assumptions. The theoretical maximum capacity (q max) calculated from the Langmuir model was 167.8 mg·g–1, in close agreement with the experimental saturation value observed in Figure a, thereby validating the model reliability. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MB adsorption proceeds via monolayer coverage on the energetically uniform surface of RM-MIL-53­(Fe). Adsorption Thermodynamics

The adsorption thermodynamic diagram and fitted parameters are shown in Figure d and Table , respectively. The consistently negative ΔG values across all temperatures confirmed the thermodynamic spontaneity of MB adsorption on RM-MIL-53­(Fe). Generally, physisorption processes exhibit standard ΔG ranging from 0 to −20 kJ·mol–1, whereas chemisorption is characterized by ΔG values between −80 and −400 kJ·mol–1. As shown in Table , the ΔG value were −237.7, −306.9, and −331.4 at the temperature of 298, 308, and 318 K, indicating chemisorption processes govern MB uptake by RM-MIL-53­(Fe). Meanwhile, electrostatic attraction-dominated adsorption mechanisms exhibit characteristic thermodynamic signatures: marginally positive enthalpy (ΔH ≈ 0 or ΔH > 0) coupled with positive entropy change (ΔS > 0). As evidenced by Table , the positive ΔH and ΔS values confirmed electrostatic attraction as a secondary adsorption pathway, alongside chemisorption. Generally, under neutral conditions, the surface charge of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) approaches its isoelectric point (pH 4–6), resulting in diminished electrostatic attraction. Despite the material exhibiting a modest negative surface charge, cationic MB+ can undergo adsorption, facilitated by weak electrostatic interactions. Consequently, the adsorption mechanism of MB on RM-MIL-53­(Fe) is predominantly governed by chemisorption, with electrostatic effects playing a supplementary role. Also, the positive ΔH indicated a strong interaction between the MB and RM-MIL-53­(Fe), suggesting that the adsorption of MB was an endothermic reaction.

Effect of Adsorbent Dosage, Solution pH

The analysis examined the influence of the adsorbent dosage on adsorption performance. As demonstrated in Figure a, adsorbent mass exhibited a pronounced inverse correlation with equilibrium adsorption capacity. This reduction arises primarily from (i) saturation of available binding sites per unit mass at elevated dosages and (ii) particle aggregation and precipitation at higher solid–liquid ratios, which induce active site occlusion and mass transfer limitations.

Effect of adsorbent dosage (a), solution pH (b), Na+ (c), and Ca2+ (d) on the adsorption capacity of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) for MB.

Solution pH plays a critical role in modulating the adsorption performance of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) toward MB, as it governs both the surface charge characteristics of the adsorbent and the speciation of the dye molecule. The pH-dependent adsorption behavior is illustrated in Figure b, wherein the MB uptake capacity increases progressively with rising pH. When the solution pH is below the point of zero charge (pHpzc ≈ 4.8, Table S1), the adsorbent surface acquires a net positive charge, giving rise to strong electrostatic repulsion against the cationic MB+ species and consequently suppressing the adsorption efficiency. Moreover, under mildly acidic conditions (e.g., pH = 3), although the MOF framework remains structurally intact, protonation of the surface carboxylate groups may partially block the binding sites responsible for hydrogen bonding or coordination interactions, further impairing the adsorption capacity. In contrast, under alkaline conditions (pH > pK a ≈ 4.52, Table S2), deprotonation of the surface functional groups renders RM-MIL-53­(Fe) negatively charged, while MB continues to exist predominantly in its cationic form. This synergistic combination of opposite charges markedly enhances the electrostatic attraction between the adsorbent and adsorbate, thereby leading to a substantial improvement in adsorption performance.

Effect of Electrolyte Species

The influence of coexisting Na+ and Ca2+ ions on the adsorption of MB onto RM-MIL-53­(Fe) was evaluated, with the results depicted in Figure c,d. In the case of Na+, the MB uptake capacity first increased and then declined with a rising ion concentration. This nonmonotonic trend arises from two opposing effects: at low concentrations, Na+ ions screen the electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged adsorbent surface and cationic MB+ species via charge shielding, thereby facilitating adsorption; at high concentrations, however, competitive occupancy of active sites, suppression of diffusional transport by increased ionic strength, and partial pore blockage collectively impair the adsorption performance. In contrast, the adsorption capacity increased monotonically with increasing Ca2+ concentration over the entire tested range. This enhancement is attributed to the more effective charge shielding afforded by divalent Ca2+ ions, which mitigates electrostatic repulsion more efficiently than does monovalent Na+. Additionally, the elevated ionic strength may reduce the solubility of MB through a salting-out effect, thereby augmenting the thermodynamic driving force for adsorption.

Adsorption Mechanism

To elucidate the adsorption mechanism of MB onto RM-MIL-53­(Fe), FT-IR and XPS spectra of the adsorbent after MB uptake were scrutinized. As displayed in Figure a, several new absorption bands emerge in the FT-IR spectrum following adsorption. The peak at 1351 cm–1 is ascribed to the stretching vibration of the Ar–N group, while the shift of the aromatic ring skeletal vibration from 751 to 756 cm–1 represents characteristic fingerprints of MB, closely matching its standard spectrum reported in the literature. These observations provide direct spectroscopic confirmation of the successful MB immobilization on the adsorbent surface. The upward shift of the O–H stretching band from 3408 to 3415 cm–1 suggests the involvement of hydrogen bonding between RM-MIL-53­(Fe) and MB. This inference is corroborated by XPS analysis (Figure b), wherein the C–O component in the high-resolution C 1s spectrum shifts from 286.5 to 286.2 eV, further evidencing hydrogen bond formation. Moreover, the displacement of the benzene ring vibration band from 751 to 756 cm–1 in the FT-IR spectrum attests to π–π stacking interactions between the aromatic moieties of the MOF framework and those of the MB molecules. Electrostatic attraction is identified as an additional contributing mechanism, as evidenced by the progressive enhancement of adsorption capacity with increasing solution pH. Finally, pore filling is also considered a plausible adsorption pathway, given that the average pore diameter of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) (9.53 nm) substantially exceeds the molecular dimensions of MB (1.7 nm × 0.76 nm × 0.325 nm).

To probe the interaction sites between RM-MIL-53­(Fe) and MB, the electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution of MB was mapped (Figure a). The ESP map reveals that π-electron density is extensively delocalized over the entire molecular surface of MB. Regions of positive electrostatic potential are generally favorable for nucleophilic attack, while negative ESP zones are susceptible to electrophilic assault. The ESP map of MB displays positive potentials concentrated on the aromatic ring and negative potentials centered around oxygen atoms, suggesting that MB can serve as an electron donor through its oxygen moieties and is also vulnerable to nucleophilic attack. Accordingly, the HOMO of the RM-MIL-53­(Fe)/MB system resides mainly on the MB moiety, whereas the LUMO is largely delocalized over the π-orbitals of the RM-MIL-53­(Fe) framework. Conclusions

The synthesis route of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) has been successfully developed by using RM as the starting material. The optimum synthesis conditions are as follows: pH 1.85, m RM:m Na2BDC 1:0.5, stirring speed 450 rpm, and temperature 80 °C. RM-MIL-53­(Fe) prepared under this condition has a more complete crystal structure and a larger specific surface area (176.5 m2·g–1). The adsorption performance of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) is competitive with those of advanced materials. Its q max (167.8 mg/g) surpasses most reported red-mud-based adsorbents (Table ). Furthermore, equilibrium is achieved within 20 min, indicating kinetics comparable to those of high-performance adsorbents. The most significant advantage, however, is economic. Utilizing red mud, a waste product with disposal costs, as the sole iron source eliminates the major expense associated with MOF synthesis: the metal precursor. The MB uptake on RM-MIL-53­(Fe) occurs via spontaneous chemisorption-dominated processes in the form of homogeneous monolayer coverage facilitated by electrostatic interactions. The adsorption capacity of RM-MIL-53­(Fe) for MB was significantly affected by the adsorbent dosage, pH of the solution, and electrolyte species. The possible adsorption mechanism includes hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, electrostatic interaction, and pore filling. This study provides a reference for high value utilization of RM and preparation of low-cost MOFs with excellent adsorption performance, thereby enhancing its potential application in wastewater treatment.
