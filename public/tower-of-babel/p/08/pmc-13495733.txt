A Single-Enzyme Electrochemical Biosensor for Lactose Detection in Human Breastmilk and Dairy Products
Authors: Raquel Sánchez-Salcedo, Donna T. Geddes, Nicolas H. Voelcker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lactose, a disaccharide found predominantly in human breast milk and dairy products, plays a vital role in human health and nutrition. While lactose is an essential energy source particularly for infant growth, it is also a biomarker of low milk production in breastfeeding women. Furthermore, lactose intolerance affects a substantial portion of the global population, impacting the quality of life. Therefore, the ability to detect and quantify lactose is important as a biomarker for both milk production and nutritional content. Here, we report on the development of a single-enzyme electrochemical biosensor for lactose employing lactose oxidase (LOx), which converts lactose to lactobionic acid and hydrogen peroxide. The sensor showed high specificity, achieving a limit of detection of 1.4 μM. The analytical performance of the sensor was verified in human breast milk as well as bovine milk. This approach allows for a rapid, reliable, and potentially portable platform for lactose detection, making it ideal for both clinical applications and routine controls in the food industry.

Introduction

Lactose, formed by a β-1,4-glycosidic bond between monosaccharides glucose and galactose, constitutes the major disaccharide in milk and plays an important role in human nutrition and health. Lactose accounts for 7% of human breast milk (HBM), whereas it constitutes 4.8% in bovine milk. The level of lactose is important for optimal infant development and growth but also for defining the nutritional value and functional properties of dairy products consumed by the general population. Lactose serves as a key energy source (approximately 40% of total energy HBM) while contributing to the osmotic balance of the milk and acting as a natural prebiotic due to intestinal fermentation processes. Moreover, it has been described as facilitating the absorption of vitamin D and minerals such as calcium and magnesium. −

These multifaceted biological roles underline the importance of sensitive and reliable analytical methods for lactose quantification across various matrices and milk sources. In the case of the dairy industry, the detection of lactose content is necessary for quality assurance, detection of milk adulteration, or/and verification of dairy products labeled as lactose-free or low lactose aimed at individuals who suffer from lactose intolerance. This is a common dietary concern arising from a deficiency in the production of enzyme lactase, which impairs the digestion of lactose in the intestine, causing bad absorption and leading to symptoms such as bloating, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and nausea. − Additionally, lactose levels can serve as an indicator of milk authenticity, and their reliable determination is vital for regulatory compliance and consumer protection.

While lactose analysis is essential for dairy products, the significance of lactose analysis extends to HBM. Beyond infant growth, recent studies have highlighted the bioactive role of lactose in promoting gut health and immune function. These findings fuel an increased focus on lactose quantification. Moreover, dysregulation or changes in lactose levels in HBM have been associated with delayed initiation of lactation and low milk production. − Perceived or actual low milk supply is the main reason for the introduction of infant formula or the cessation before six months postnatal, the period of time recommended for exclusive breastfeeding by the WHO. , Early weaning increases the risk of both acute diseases such as respiratory and gastrointestinal infection, which are major causes of child mortality as well as chronic disease later in life. Thus, lactose emerges as an important biomarker for monitoring not only energy content but also lactation performance, necessitating a precise and accessible analytical tool for its monitoring.

Current analytical methods for lactose present notable limitations. Traditional analytical methods include high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), near-infrared spectroscopy (NIR), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, or enzymatic methods, each with advantages and drawbacks. While HPLC and NMR are highly accurate, their cost and reliance on specialized equipment and highly qualified professionals limit their widespread use. , NIR is a rapid and noninvasive technique although still requires calibration and validation against a reference method such as HPLC and requires relatively large sample volumes (3 mL). Meanwhile, enzymatic methods such as ELISA, although they are routinely used for quality control, are time-consuming, require laboratory facilities and suffer from reduced accuracy due to some interferences such as casein micelles, cytokines and chemokines, fat globules, or proteins present in the sample particularly at low lactose concentration. − Therefore, to assess these challenges, it is critical to explore new approaches that overcome the limitations of current measurement techniques with respect to cost, speed of analysis, portability, and miniaturizability. The development of a biosensor is a promising alternative and would provide valuable insights into milk composition, enhancing food safety and supporting personalized nutritional interventions for both infants and adults.

Electrochemical biosensors align well with the concept of portability, low cost, and reduced analysis time while maintaining high sensitivity and selectivity. Previous efforts have explored these enzymatic electrochemical sensors for lactose detection, often incorporating complex multienzyme reactions and nanomaterials to enhance sensitivity. For instance, Halpin et al. described an enzymatic sensor for lactose in dairy products using platinum and glassy carbon electrodes modified with chitosan, β-galactosidase (β-gal), and glucose oxidase (GOx) and a cross-linker built using a layer-by-layer (LbL) approach. Further, de Brito and co-workers developed an amperometric sensor in which the enzyme is embedded in a carbon nanotubes matrix. Although these strategies achieved sufficient sensitivity, they introduced design complexities, susceptibility to interferences, and increased manufacturing cost. The requirement for the incorporation of nanomaterials or polymers limits their practical deployment, especially for large-scale or disposable applications.

In contrast to lactose measurement employing a double or triple enzyme reaction along with complex modifications, this study reports on an approach employing a single enzyme, lactose oxidase (LOx), to catalyze the conversion of lactose to lactobionic acid and hydrogen peroxide, which can be easily detected by amperometry, allowing the indirect detection of lactose. Unlike multienzyme cascade approaches, which depend on sequential catalytic steps and are therefore governed by the slowest enzymatic reaction and potentially affected by intermediate species, a single-enzyme strategy reduces to a minimum the cumulative kinetic limitations. This approach enhances stability and reliability, which is crucial in complex matrices. The enzyme used in this study has previously only been described and employed as an antimicrobial to inhibit the growth of Listeria monocytogenes in inoculated UHT skim milk.

Hence, we report here the first enzymatic sensor employing LOx-modified gold disc electrodes for the analysis of lactose in both HBM and bovine milk with high sensitivity and selectivity. This alternative method provides a simpler and more direct process, and the results obtained demonstrate an excellent correlation with conventional HPLC detection, showcasing the potential of this biosensor to enable lactose analysis in both clinical and commercial applications. This approach promises to enhance health monitoring, supporting more personalized and accurate nutritional interventions, and potentially improve quality control of dairy product.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Reagents

LactoYield, commonly named lactose oxidase (LOx), was provided by the Novozymes Group Entity (Novozymes Australia). Disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), sodium dihydrogen phosphate monohydrate (NaH2PO4), potassium chloride (KCl), sodium chloride (NaCl), glutaraldehyde, phosphate buffer saline 10x concentrate pH 7.4 (10xPBS), and cysteamine were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. Ultrapure water (resistivity: 18.2 MΩ·cm) was obtained from a Milli-Q System and used to prepare all aqueous solutions unless specifically noted otherwise. Human milk oligosaccharides (lacto-N-tetraose) were synthesized in Dr. Keith Stubbs’ research group at the University of Western Australia. HBM samples were collected from lactating women at the University of Western Australia for milk composition analysis. This study was approved by the University of Western Australia Human Research Ethics Committee (2019/RA/4/20/6134). Informed written consent was obtained from all of the participants. Regular full cream milk, skim milk, lactose-free milk, and soy milk were acquired in local supermarkets.

Enzyme Assay Design

Standard gold disc electrodes (CH instruments Inc., USA; diameter: 2 mm) were mechanically polished with a 0.05 μm alumina slurry using a soft pad. Afterward, the gold surface was electrochemically conditioned in 0.5 M H2SO4 by applying 10 potential cycles from 0 to 1.6 V vs. a reference electrode Ag/AgCl. Then, the electrodes were washed thoroughly with Milli-Q water and dried with nitrogen before the functionalization. The electrodes were covered with 100 mM cysteamine for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Subsequently, a 45 min incubation step was performed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PBS buffer. Afterward, the immobilization of 0.5 U/mL of LOx enzyme was carried out overnight at 4 °C. Volumes of 6 μL were used for the coverage of the electrode surface via drop-casting; after each step, the electrode was washed with the buffer used in the next step and dried under N2 gas.

Electrochemical Measurements

All electrochemical measurements were performed using the CHI 650E electrochemical analyzer (CH Instruments, USA) controlled by Chi 650e software. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements for characterization and amperometry for detection were carried out in a conventional three-electrode cell with a gold disc electrode as the working electrode (WE), a Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode, and a platinum wire as the counter electrode. CV and EIS measurements were performed in 5 mM Fe­(CN)6 –3/–4 in 0.1 M PBS; for the EIS measurements, a frequency range of 0.1 Hz–10 kHz was applied and for the CV, the voltage was cycled from −0.3 to 0.7 V. Lactose detection was performed amperometrically by applying a constant potential of 0.7 V to measure the enzymatically generated H2O2. , Increasing volumes of lactose standard solutions were sequentially added to the electrochemical cell (V cel = 5 mL) under continuous stirring to construct calibration curves. Similarly, breast milk samples, dairy products, and potential interfering species for selectivity assays were introduced into the three-electrode cell under identical experimental conditions.

Extraction and Calculation of the Signal for the Standard Calibration Curve

All electrochemical measurements were carried out by means of amperometry, using sequential additions of lactose standards into the electrochemical cell. For each addition step, the analytical response was defined as the incremental current change (ΔI, A), calculated as the difference between the steady-state current recorded after the addition and the baseline current measured immediately before the addition. Accordingly, the calibration data were constructed using ΔI values for each concentration step rather than the absolute current intensity I(A) observed at individual time points in Figure a. To reduce the impact of interelectrode variability and day-to-day experimental fluctuations, the incremental signals were normalized within each individual experiment. Specifically, the ΔI value obtained at the highest lactose concentration in each experiment was defined as 100%, and all other ΔI responses from that same experiment were expressed as a percentage of the maximum value. Thus, the data presented in Figure b correspond to normalized ΔI(A) and do not represent ratios of absolute I(A).

Treatment of Human Breast Milk Samples

HBM samples were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 5 min, and the up-fat layer was carefully removed. The samples were further diluted 1:10 in ultrapure H2O prior to analysis.

Selectivity and Control Studies for the Lactose Sensor

The selectivity of the developed lactose sensor was assessed against human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs), glucose, and lactate, natural interferences found in HBM, with the latter occurring at much lower concentration. Further negative control tests were conducted analyzing commercial samples of lactose-free bovine milk in which the lactose has been digested adding lactase enzyme during the manufacturing process and vegan soy milk, which does not contain lactose.

Storage and Stability Studies

Stability studies were performed monitoring the response of the sensor in the presence of 100 μM lactose over time up to 2 weeks. Two different storage approaches were assessed. First, for wet conditions, the electrodes were kept in 1xPBS in the fridge at 4 °C. Second, for dry conditions, following a procedure previously described with some modifications, the electrodes were incubated in a solution of 2.5% BSA and 1% of glucose in 1xPBS for 30 min, dried under a N2 flow, and kept in a sealed container at 4 °C. Prior to the measurements, the electrodes were rehydrated in buffer for 10 min.

Ultrahigh Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) Lactose Analysis

HBM samples were also analyzed by UHPLC employing a Shimadzu UHPLC system with a refractive index detector RID-10A and an oven temperature set to 85 °C and a mobile phase flow of 0.7 mL/min. Water was used as the mobile phase previously sonicated for 10 min. The lactose standards were prepared at a concentration of 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 mM in water. The volume injected for both standards and samples was 10 μL and the total run time was 15 min, the average retention time of lactose being 8.86 min. The lactose concentration of the samples was quantified by interpolating the response peak areas in the external calibration curve obtained. Further details and the results of the analysis by UHPLC can be found in the Supporting Information (Table S2).

Results and Discussion

This work reports the development of a single-enzyme electrochemical sensor for the detection of lactose in milk from different sources. The capability to accurately quantify lactose in these matrices is highly valuable for quality control, nutritional monitoring, and clinical assessment of lactation performance. The sensor was designed as a POC platform, focusing on portability and ease of use, thus making it suitable for low resource settings or at-home testing without the need of expertise or further equipment.

Sensor Fabrication

The surface modification of the electrode was electrochemically characterized by CV and EIS recording the signal in 5 mM K4[Fe­(CN)6]/K3[Fe­(CN)6] solution in 0.1 M PBS. The modification protocol followed the procedure described in Section , briefly, electrode functionalization with 100 mM cysteamine and cross-linking with 2.5% glutaraldehyde followed by immobilization of 0.5 U/mL LOx.

The sensor was characterized by means of EIS, and the resulting Nyquist plots (shown in Figure S1) were fitted to a Randles equivalent circuit (R(C[RW])) obtaining the charge transfer resistance (R ct(Ω)) for each stage. The bare gold electrode exhibited an R ct of 0.385 ± 0.002 kΩ. Upon modification with cysteamine, the Rct slightly decreased to 0.12 ± 0.01 kΩ due to the amine groups being highly protonated at the pH of 7.5, leading to electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged redox probe ([Fe­(CN)6]−3/–4). Following the application of glutaraldehyde, the Rct increased to 0.77 ± 0.02 kΩ, consistent with the known behavior of increasing interfacial resistance to outer-sphere redox probes. The immobilization of LOx subsequently led to a sharp increase in R ct to 7.7 ± 0.2 kΩ, confirming the successful formation of an enzyme layer with inherently insulating characteristics, which impedes direct electron transfer between the redox mediator and the electrode surface. These electrochemical signatures validated each modification step and the efficient anchoring of the enzyme. The electrode functionalization steps were further characterized by CV. The voltammograms were recorded (Figure S2) after each modification step. The bare electrode exhibited well-defined redox peaks typical of [Fe­(CN)6]−3/–4 electron transfer, whereas after enzyme functionalization, a marked decrease in peak intensity was observed, consistent with increased charge-transfer resistance confirming the successive deposition of organic layers on the gold electrode surface.

Furthermore, to support the electrochemical evidence, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was performed on gold-sputtered glass substrates after each surface modification step (Figure S3). The modification followed the same protocol as described in Section , and the gold-side substrate was placed in direct contact with the ATR crystal for spectra acquisition. The spectra for the modification of the surface with cysteamine revealed characteristics peaks due to stretching vibrations at 955 cm–1 (C–O stretch), 1380 cm–1 (C–C stretch), 1434 cm–1 (C–H stretch), 1504 cm–1 (N–H bend), 1569 cm–1 (CN stretch), 2928–2990 cm–1 (CH2 stretch), and 3406 cm–1 (N–H stretch). After incubation with glutaraldehyde, a distinct carbonyl stretching band at 1720 cm–1 appeared, confirming successful cross-linking, accompanied by additional bands at 1116 and 1158 cm–1 (C–OH hydroxyl and C–O epoxy stretch, respectively). Upon LOx immobilization, the characteristic bands indicative of enzyme attachment at 1656 cm–1 (carbonyl amide I) and 1530 cm–1 (amide II) were observed. Additionally, the band at 1080 cm–1 can be attributed to the C–O–C bonds of glycosylated residues, consistent with the glycoprotein nature of LOx.

Evaluating Assay Performance in Buffer

The analytical performance of the lactose sensor was first evaluated under controlled conditions using amperometry measurements applying 0.7 V in 0.1 M PBS. The sensor showed a dynamic range up to 0.35 mM lactose, as shown in Figure a. Incremental additions of lactose resulted in proportionally increasing current responses, confirming the sensor’s ability to record the enzymatic oxidation product of lactose. The calibration curve obtained by amperometry in 0.1 M PBS is shown in Figure b. The signalcalculated as the difference between the current measured after the addition and the baseline current before the additionwas normalized by expressing the percentage of the maximum ΔI (A) to account for interday variations (more information about calculation of the signal in Section ). The resulting calibration curves obtained on successive days largely overlapped, demonstrating the robustness of the normalization procedure. Accordingly, Figure b presents the averaged calibration curve derived from all measurements. The calibration curve exhibited a linear range that fits to the equation I current(%) = 310.6 ± 4.2 [lactose]­(mM) + 2.9 ± 0.6 (R 2 = 0.997) with a limit of detection (LOD) of 1.4 × 10–3 mM. The LOD was calculated as 3S b/m, where S b represents the deviation of the blank and m represents the sensitivity. Once the analytical range was stablished, we further assessed the robustness and reusability of the sensor. The reusability was investigated over 15 consecutive measurements cycles. The current response was retained at 95% of the initial signal for the first 9 uses and remained above 80% after 15 uses (data plotted in Figure a), demonstrating the robustness of the immobilized enzyme layer.

Selectivity and Storage Stability of the Sensor

After verifying a positive and reproducible response of the developed enzymatic sensor, selectivity experiments were carried out by challenging the lactose sensor with potential interferents commonly found in HBM, including 1 mM of glucose and lactate, and HMOs, each at 100 μM concentration. The responses to these compounds were significantly lower than the specific response to lactose, which is noteworthy given that they are naturally present at lower concentrations (Figure ). This confirms the high specificity and minimal cross-reactivity of the LOx platform.

Evaluating Assay Performance in Complex Samples

After its analytical performance was validated in a simple buffer, the biosensor was evaluated in HBM samples. The amperometric measurements were conducted under the same conditions as in PBS. The analysis of a batch of 13 real sample was achieved with minimum treatment unlike other methods described in the literature where the samples needed to undergo a more complex pretreatment regimen.

The lactose concentrations measured with the developed sensor correlated strongly with the values obtained by the standard method UHPLC, validating its accuracy and reliability (detailed data from the analysis of the samples by UHPLC are reported in the Supporting Information Table S3). Additionally, the results were coherent with the data provided externally obtained by enzymatic spectroscopy assay using the commercial kit of Megazyme© K-LOLAC. For further evaluation of the operational robustness of the sensor for deployment in a real-world scenario, the biosensor was exposed to open-air environments without temperature or humidity control, simulating scenarios encountered during breastfeeding or milk collection. After 90 min of exposure, the amperometric response to subsequent HBM additions showed no statistically significant difference compared with initial measurements (Figure a), confirming its remarkable environmental stability. Moreover, with a view to integrating the biosensor into breast pumps, the electrochemical response of the biosensor for continuous monitoring was assessed. The signal was recorded for different fluctuations in the concentration either diluting the buffer in the cell or adding known concentrations of lactose. This dynamic amperometric assay mimics inline sensing under flow conditions simulating realistic variations in concentration, matrix composition, and flow that could occur in an integrated setup such as a breastmilk pump line or a flowmeter-controlled channel. The current recorded correlated well with each dilution and addition, and stable steady-state plateaus after each perturbation were observed, indicating that the biosensor is suited for continuous monitoring applications (Figure b).

Whereas most of the reported biosensors rely on multienzyme cascades or complex nanostructures interfaces, the sensor presented in this work achieved a comparable or lower LOD and exhibits a broad linear range spanning physiologically relevant lactose level while maintaining a simple single-enzyme configuration. Furthermore, it is the only sensor validated directly in HBM demonstrating a robust performance in a complex biological matrix and underscoring its suitability for lactation monitoring.

Conclusions

An electrochemical single-enzyme sensor for the detection of lactose was designed and developed providing a robust, selective, and sensitive platform for lactose detection showing high sensitivity and reproducibility. Efficient enzyme immobilization was verified by spectroscopy and electrochemical methods, and the analytical performance was evaluated achieving a LOD of 1.4 μM and a good correlation with standard methods. The sensor’s stability, reusability, and strong performance in both human and bovine milk highlight its viability for practical deployment in portable or online lactose monitoring systems, representing a significant advance toward accessible, real-time milk composition analysis.
