Identification of mitochondrial oxidative stress-related genes for estimating prognosis, immune infiltration landscape and drug candidates in pancreatic adenocarcinoma
Authors: Ruiguang Ma, Xuechun Liu, Ruining Gong, Yueping Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) represents a highly lethal form of cancer. Mitochondrial stress responses play a critical role in cancer development and therapeutic responses.MethodsTranscriptomic and clinical survival data for PAAD and normal pancreatic tissues were sourced from TCGA, GEO, and GTEx databases. Random survival forest and Cox regression analyses were used to identify MOS prognostic genes and construct a prognostic risk model. Subsequently, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calibration curve analysis, and decision curve analysis were performed to evaluate the model’s performance in both the training and validation sets. Finally, enrichment analysis, protein-protein interaction network construction, unsupervised cluster analysis, tumor immune microenvironment assessment, and chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity analyses were conducted. Finally, we also performed RT-qPCR for validation.ResultsFive MOS-related genes were identified to construct the risk signature: ABCG1, CENPT, GMNN, NOG, and TOP2A. The predictive performance of the risk signature was validated in both the training set (TCGA) and the test sets (GSE62452 and GSE78229), with areas under the ROC curve for 3-year survival being 0.77, 0.74, and 0.75, respectively. Mechanistically, functional enrichment analysis revealed that pathways associated with cancer cell proliferation and mitochondrial stress responses were upregulated in the high-risk population. Furthermore, specific small molecules may hold therapeutic potential for treating PAAD patients. RT-qPCR results validated the changes in relative RNA expression levels.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that mitochondrial oxidative stress is correlated with the prognosis of PAAD and may serve as a novel biomarker and therapeutic target for clinical diagnostics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04948-8.Keywords: Pancreatic adenocarcinoma, Mitochondrial oxidative stress, Prognosis, Biomarker

Introduction

Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) is an exceptionally aggressive malignancy characterized by rapid progression, significant invasiveness, and a high propensity for metastasis. The 5-year survival rate for this condition is merely 11% [1]. The sole potentially curative treatment for pancreatic cancer is surgical resection, with the surgical resection rate ranging from 15% to 20% overall [2]. During surgical exploration, distant metastases are observed in up to 26% of patients [3]. Traditional chemotherapy and radiotherapy for pancreatic cancer have shown improvement, and palliative treatments and adjuvant therapies have been developed and implemented in clinical practice [4]. Nonetheless, these advancements have not yet led to a significant improvement in the 5-year survival rate among patients with pancreatic cancer. Consequently, exploring novel prognostic markers and therapeutic targets in PAAD holds substantial clinical significance.

Mitochondria play essential roles in balancing cell viability and function. Mitochondria are involved in energy metabolism, amino acid and fatty acid synthesis, regulation of cell death, aging, disease, and oxidative phosphorylation [5]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), generated as by-products of oxidative phosphorylation and cytokines, are considered important factors in the pathogenesis of pancreatic cancer [6]. Mitochondrial ROS production could shift the cellular redox environment to a more oxidized state, which may contribute to tumorigenesis [7]. Excessive mitochondrial oxidative stress can induce cell death [8]. Cancer cells are subjected to harsh environmental fluctuations, including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and metabolic and oxidative stress [9]. Cancer cells rely on an enhanced mitochondrial stress response to survive in hostile microenvironments to support tumor growth, metastasis, drug resistance, and stemness [10, 11]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms of mitochondrial homeostasis holds excellent promise as a potentially relevant anticancer strategy [9].

Therefore, this study aims to establish a signature of mitochondrial oxidative stress-related genes (MOS) associated with PAAD to identify the characteristics of molecular subtypes. Furthermore, this study seeks to explore the differences in microenvironment infiltration characteristics, mutation profiles, immunotherapy response evaluations, and drug sensitivity analyses among MOS risk subgroups. This research also provides new insights into mitochondrial stress response therapies for patients with pancreatic cancer.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing of PAAD gene expression

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data and clinical information from 179 patients with PAAD and 169 normal pancreatic tissues were retrieved from the TCGA-TARGET-GTEx-transcript cohort, employing the UCSC TOIL RNA-Seq recomputed [12]. Patients were included based on the following criteria: (1) availability of complete survival data and TNM stage information; (2) a follow-up duration exceeding one month. As a result, 169 PAAD samples and 167 normal pancreatic samples were obtained from the UCSC Xena platform (http://xena.ucsc.edu). GSE62452 and GSE78229 were also downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) to validate the prognostic models. The characteristics of the patients are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Somatic data for PAAD patients were obtained from the TCGA database. The Transcripts Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads (TPM) values were converted to a log2 (TPM + 1) scale. Ethical approval was not necessary as this study was a retrospective analysis utilizing publicly available data.

Identification of MOS-related differential expressed genes

Subsequently, differential gene expression (DEGs) analysis was performed using the “limma” package [13], based on the TCGA-TARGET-GTEx-transcript cohort dataset. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (log2FC) | > 2 were considered significantly differentially expressed between tumor and adjacent normal samples. A total of 10,129 MOS-related genes (MOSs) were obtained from the GeneCards database. The intersection of DEGs and MOSs was defined as MOSDEGs.

Establishment and assessment of prognostic MOSDEGs based-signatures

The TCGA-PAAD dataset was utilized as the training set, while two GEO cohorts (GSE62452 and GSE78229) served as validation sets. First, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to identify prognostic-related MOSDEGs using the “survival” R package. A relatively lenient significance threshold of P < 0.2 was applied to avoid prematurely excluding potentially important variables [14]. Subsequently, the selected genes were further analyzed using the random survival forest algorithm implemented in the “randomForestSRC” R package. The model was constructed with the following parameters: ntree = 100 (number of trees), nsplit = 1 (number of random splits to accelerate computation), and method = “vh” (variable hunting) for gene selection. The random seed was set to 30 to ensure reproducibility. Out-of-bag prediction was used to evaluate model performance. Genes identified by the random survival forest analysis were then included in multivariate Cox regression analysis to construct the final prognostic model. Variables with P < 0.05 were retained in the final model. The MOS score for each PAAD patient was calculated as a linear combination of the selected genes weighted by their multivariate Cox regression coefficients: MOS Score = . Here, represents the regression coefficient, while denotes the level of gene expression. Patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median MOS score, as the risk scores did not follow a normal distribution (confirmed by Shapiro–Wilk test, P < 0.05). Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis with the log-rank test was performed using the “survival” and “survminer” R packages to compare overall survival (OS). Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were conducted using the “timeROC” R package to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the MOS score for 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival probabilities. The same formula and evaluation methods were applied to the validation cohorts.

Establishment and evaluation of the nomogram model

Based on the clinical features of PAAD and MOS scores, we employed the Cox regression algorithm to calculate the hazard ratios (HR) and p-values. The prognostic model used the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to evaluate performance for 1-, 2-, and 3-year intervals. The models’ clinical utility and net benefit were assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA). Furthermore, a nomogram was constructed using the “survival” and “regplot” R packages to evaluate the overall survival probabilities for 1-, 2-, and 3-year intervals in patients with PAAD.

Functional annotation analysis of DEGs between subgroups

Using the “limma” R package, DEGs were identified between risk subgroups (high- vs. low-risk groups) based on the thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. Subsequently, heatmaps and volcano plots were employed to illustrate these findings. We conducted enrichment analysis using the “clusterProfiler” R package to elucidate the functional annotation of MOS score-related DEGs. In the Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation analyses, results with p-values and q-values less than 0.01 were considered significantly enriched. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed using p-values and q-values less than 0.05 thresholds. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was also employed to identify the distinct potential biological processes between the subgroups. The predefined gene set “h.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols” was sourced from the MSigDB database, and only pathways with an FDR of less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

PPI network construction and immune infiltration analysis

The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network for the intersection of DEGs between risk subgroups was constructed using the STRING database (v12.0). A confidence threshold of ≥ 0.900 was applied as the screening criterion. Cytoscape software (v3.10.3) was then employed to visualize and construct the PPI network. The maximal clique centrality algorithm in the cytoHubba plugin was used to identify the top 10 hub genes in the network.

The IOBR package was utilized to perform the ESTIMATE algorithm to analyze immune cell infiltration in the immune microenvironment [15]. Marker gene labels for 28 tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte subsets were retrieved from the TISIDB database as the reference gene set for the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm. The impact of MOS signature genes on the abundance of immune cell subtypes was assessed. Additionally, gene expression levels of key immune checkpoint genes (ICGs) and MOS signature genes were evaluated. Furthermore, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between immune cell types.

Somatic mutation burden characteristics and drug sensitivity analysis

Somatic variant data for PAAD were retrieved from the TCGA database in mutation annotation format. Waterfall plots were utilized to display the top 20 genes with the highest mutation frequencies across MOS score subgroups, and tumor mutation burden (TMB) scores were calculated for each sample using “maftools” scripts. Drug sensitivity scores for different subgroups were predicted using the “oncoPredict” R package based on the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer database data.

Unsupervised cluster analysis

We identified MOSDEGs associated with prognosis through univariate Cox regression analysis, applying a threshold of P < 0.05. Genes meeting this significance level were defined as “MOS-Cluster Genes” and subsequently used to conduct unsupervised consensus clustering analysis using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package [16]. To ensure the stability and robustness of the clustering results, the algorithm was run with 1,000 repetitions (reps = 1000) and 80% item resampling (pItem = 0.80). The optimal number of clusters (K = 3) was determined based on the cumulative distribution function curve. Differences among the subgroups were visualized using a t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) plot. To compare survival times between subtypes, KM survival analysis was conducted using the “survival” and “survminer” R packages.

To explore the functional characteristics of the three molecular subtypes, GSEA was performed using the gseGO function in the “clusterProfiler” R package. The analysis was based on all genes ranked by log2FC, and gene sets from the GO database were tested. Significantly enriched terms were defined by |Normalized Enrichment Score| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. Patients in Clusters 1 and 2 were further divided into high-risk and low-risk subgroups based on the median MOS score. Immune cell infiltration abundances were quantified using ssGSEA for 28 immune cell types. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare enrichment scores between high-risk and low-risk groups within each cluster, with P-values adjusted by the FDR method for multiple testing. In addition, the ESTIMATE algorithm was applied to calculate stromal and immune scores to evaluate the tumor microenvironment.

Cell Culture, quantitative real-time PCR, and HPA dataset verification

Human pancreatic cancer cell lines (Panc-1, AsPC-1, and MIA PaCa-2) and normal pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (HPNE) were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Panc-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells were maintained in DMEM (PM150210, Pricella, China), while AsPC-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (PM150110, Pricella, China), both supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Pricella, China) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

Total RNA was isolated from samples using RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme Biotech, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with 1 µg total RNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR v2.0 (Accurate Biology, China), which includes genomic DNA removal steps. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was carried out in triplicate technical replicates on a Roche LightCycler® 480 II system using SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, China). Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH as the endogenous control and calculated via the 2 − ΔΔCT method [17]. All primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

To validate the protein expression levels of the five MOS-related genes, immunohistochemical images were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Representative immunohistochemical images for each gene in pancreatic cancer and normal pancreatic tissues were downloaded. Additionally, online analysis of protein expression levels between tumor and normal samples was performed using the HPA database interface to compare the expression patterns of ABCG1, CENPT, GMNN, NOG, and TOP2A.

By analyzing the expression profiles of 793 MOSDEG signatures in conjunction with the prognostic survival data of PAAD, we thoroughly investigated the role of the MOS gene signature in PAAD prognosis. We screened MOSDEG genes at P < 0.2 based on univariate Cox regression analysis. Subsequently, we performed a random forest analysis combined with a multifactorial Cox analysis. Through this approach, five MOSDEGs were ultimately identified as independent prognostic factors for PAAD (P < 0.05). Among them, ABCG1 (HR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.45–0.95, P = 0.028) and CENPT (HR = 0.54, 95% CI 0.37–0.78, P = 0.001) were identified as protective factors (HR < 1), indicating that higher expression of these genes was associated with better prognosis. In contrast, GMNN (HR = 1.69, 95% CI 1.04–2.74, P = 0.035), NOG (HR = 1.48, 95% CI 1.02–2.15, P = 0.038), and TOP2A (HR = 1.68, 95% CI 1.00–2.81, P = 0.048) were identified as risk factors (HR > 1), suggesting that higher expression of these genes correlated with poorer survival outcomes. Construction of PPI networks and functional annotation for risk subgroups related DEGs

To identify genes differentially expressed between the high-risk and low-risk groups, differential gene expression analysis was performed using screening criteria of FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. Compared with the low-risk group, a total of 227 genes were up-regulated, whereas 473 genes were down-regulated in the high-risk group. A volcano plot (Fig. 3A) and a heatmap (Fig. 3B) illustrate the distribution of DEGs. Interactions among the DEGs related to the risk subgroups were extracted by querying the STRING database with a confidence cutoff of 0.900 (Fig. 3C). Figure 3C presents the top 10 hub genes exhibiting high degrees of connectivity within our PPI network. The TOP2A gene serves as a central node in this network, underscoring the biological coherence of the MOS-related risk model.

GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses were performed to assess the functions of the DEGs. The results of the GO enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were significantly enriched in the mitotic cell cycle (Fig. 3D), including terms such as “mitotic nuclear division” and “chromosome, centromeric region”. Additionally, the KEGG analysis demonstrated that pathways related to cell growth and death and signaling molecules and interactions were also significantly enriched (Fig. 3E). GSEA was employed for further pathway enrichment analysis to investigate the MOS signature’s underlying mechanisms. The results demonstrated significant enrichment of 35 gene sets, comprising three down-regulated and 32 up-regulated gene sets. GSEA indicated that the p53 pathway, TNF-α/NF-κB signaling pathway, unfolded protein response, and apoptosis pathway were significantly up-regulated in the high-risk group (Fig. 3F). These pathways have been closely linked to mitochondrial stress response.

Evaluation of immune feature for risk subgroups

To investigate the immune infiltration status of adenocarcinoma across different risk subgroups, we evaluated the infiltration scores of 28 tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte types using the ssGSEA algorithm. The abundance of central memory CD8 T cells, activated CD4 T cells, central memory CD4 T cells, gamma delta T cells, memory B cells, CD56 bright natural killer cells, and immature dendritic cells increased in high-risk group patients. In contrast, other immune cells, including activated B cells, eosinophils, and mast cells, were enriched in patients in the low-risk group (Fig. 4A, Supplement Table 2). The results from the ESTIMATE analysis indicated no significant difference between the risk groups (Supplement Fig. 1D). Correlation analysis of immune cells suggests a strong association among those with increased relative abundance in the high-risk group (Fig. 4B). We also examined the correlation between MOS signature genes and immune cells, revealing that the expression of most signature genes exhibited a negative correlation with immune cells (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, we evaluated the potential association between the MOS prognostic signature and ICGs, which revealed that the expression of signature genes negatively correlated with most ICGs (Supplement Fig. 1E).

Mutation burden landscape and drug sensitivity

We presented the mutation frequency and types of the top 20 significantly mutated genes in each risk subgroup (Fig. 5A, B). TP53 and CDKN2A were identified as the most frequently mutated genes within the subgroups. The TMB score for each tumor sample was calculated, and all PAAD samples were categorized into High-TMB and Low-TMB groups based on the median TMB score. There was a difference in TMB scores between the High-TMB and Low-TMB groups within the risk subgroups (Supplement Fig. 1F). Differences in survival probabilities were observed between the high TMB and low-risk groups, the high TMB and high-risk groups, the low TMB and low-risk groups, and the low TMB and high-risk groups (P = 0.0045, Supplement Fig. 1G).

Additionally, we investigated the sensitivity of specific small molecule compounds to compare drug sensitivity between patients in the high-risk and low-risk groups. The results indicated that in the low MOS score subgroup, the drug sensitivity of Afatinib_1032, AZD3759_1915, Dasatinib_1079, Lapatinib_1558, and Sapitinib_1549 was significantly higher than that in the high MOS score subgroup (Fig. 5C). The MOS signature may provide insights into potential chemotherapeutic agents, offering hypotheses for personalized treatment strategies that warrant further experimental investigation.

Consensus clustering analysis

To further explore the biological relevance of MOS-related genes, we performed unsupervised consensus clustering analysis based on MOS-Cluster Genes. This analysis stratified patients into three distinct molecular subtypes: Cluster 1 (n = 98), Cluster 2 (n = 60), and Cluster 3 (n = 11), with the optimal number of clusters determined as K = 3 (Fig. 6A-D). Furthermore, the KM curve illustrated that Cluster 3 had a higher overall survival rate than Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (Fig. 6E, χ² = 20.37, P < 0.001). Consistent with this prognostic advantage, all 11 patients in Cluster 3 were classified as low-risk based on the MOS score. In contrast, Cluster 1 consisted of 74 high-risk and 24 low-risk patients, while Cluster 2 comprised 11 high-risk and 49 low-risk patients. Despite the limited sample size of Cluster 3 (n = 11), the significant survival difference and consistent MOS score stratification suggest a distinct molecular subtype.

GSEA-GO enrichment analysis of the three molecular subtypes showed that immune-related pathways were significantly enriched in Clusters 1 and 2 (Fig. 6F). Based on the MOS score, patients in Clusters 1 and 2 were further divided into high- and low-risk subgroups for immune infiltration analysis. The ssGSEA revealed relatively higher abundances of certain T-cell subsets in the high-risk groups of both clusters (Supplement Fig. 2, Supplement Table 3, and 4). However, no significant differences were observed using the ESTIMATE algorithm (Supplement Fig. 3).

Discussion

Pancreatic cancer is a highly lethal tumor. This dismal situation persists for several reasons, one is the lack of effective risk assessment models, posing significant obstacles to developing individualized treatment strategies for patients. Recent extensive studies have revealed the complex role of mitochondrial oxidative stress in cancer development. Therefore, we investigated the role of MOS genes in predicting the prognosis of patients with PAAD. In the current study, a clinical prognostic gene signature was developed using MOSDEGs. This gene signature exhibited good precision in stratifying patients diagnosed with PAAD. Concurrently, two risk subtypes, namely high-risk and low-risk, were identified based on 5 MOS-related signature genes (ABCG1, CENPT, GMNN, NOG, and TOP2A). The differences in immune cell profiles, drug sensitivity, and somatic mutations between the identified risk subgroups were assessed in detail.

These five prognostic MOS genes have been reported to be associated with the progression and prognosis of cancer patients to some extent. ABCG1 is a well-characterized membrane transporter recognized as a key regulator of cellular cholesterol and lipid homeostasis [20]. Control of cholesterol levels is critical for cellular differentiation and growth [21]. Excess lipid accumulation in cells can cause lipotoxicity, leading to cellular damage and apoptosis [22]. Previous studies have shown that the expression of the ABCG1 gene is upregulated in numerous types of malignancies, including pancreatic cancer [18], cholangiocarcinoma [23], metastatic colon cancer [24], glioma [25], and ovarian cancer [26]. ABCG1 exhibits diverse functions in tumors. Xu et al. have demonstrated that ABCG1 plays a key role in the Circ_ASPH/miR-581 signaling pathway and may promote cholangiocarcinoma growth and metastasis [23]. Numerous studies have revealed that significant upregulation of ABCG1 is associated with resistance of cancer cells to chemotherapy drugs [27–29]. Besides tumor cells, the absence of ABCG1 inhibits tumor growth by modulating macrophage function within the tumor [30]. In our risk signature, we also screened ABCG1 as one of the risk factors for predicting the prognosis of PAAD. ABCG1 may serve as a potential novel diagnostic and therapeutic target for PAAD.

TOP2A is encoded by the TOP2A gene, which regulates DNA transcription, replication, and repair during the mitotic phase of the cell cycle [31]. Therefore, dysregulation of TOP2A may result in tumor cells acquiring malignant phenotypes, which enhances cell proliferation and promotes EMT [32]. Pei et al. have demonstrated that TOP2A acts as a midstream molecule in pancreatic cancer induced by miR-139 to promote β-catenin signaling and EMT [19]. Our study identified TOP2A as the core gene and ten hub genes, including CDK1 and CCNB1, through PPI analysis and the maximal clique centrality algorithm. These genes play a crucial role in cell cycle regulation, chromosome segregation, cell division, and the maintenance of genomic stability. Wang et al. have revealed the intricate network of interactions and regulatory mechanisms among TOP2A, CHK1, CDK1, and Cyclin B1 (the coding protein of CCNB1) in hepatocellular carcinoma [33]. These proteins function collaboratively to modulate the cell cycle, particularly emphasizing their pivotal role in facilitating the transition from the G2 phase to the M phase [33, 34]. TOP2A, thus, might serve as an important target for PAAD treatments [35].

Compared with ABCG1 and TOP2A, CENPT, GMNN, and NOG have been less reported on promoting PAAD development. The CENPT gene plays an important role in the cell cycle, an additional factor required for centromere assembly [36]. GMNN-encoded proteins are expressed in the S and G2 phases of the cell cycle and inhibit DNA replication by binding to the DNA replication factor CDT1 [37]. Zhao et al. found that upregulated GMNN may promote malignant progression in adrenocortical carcinoma by regulating the cell cycle [38]. An intriguing genetic alteration we observed is the downregulation of GMNN expression in patients with PAAD, which may result in an imbalance in the regulation of the DNA replication process. This imbalance may subsequently lead to abnormalities in the cell cycle. This abnormal cell proliferation may be a contributing factor to clinical chemotherapy resistance. NOG encodes proteins that regulate the development of bones and other tissues under physiological conditions. Elevated NOG expression is correlated with breast cancer bone metastasis, which affects the skeletal microenvironment and leads to osteoclast degeneration, collectively promoting breast cancer bone metastasis [39]. The role of CENPT, GMNN, and NOG in PAAD progression needs to be elucidated further.

Subsequently, differential gene expression, PPI, and enrichment analyses were conducted for patients in risk subgroups. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses primarily focused on “cell growth and death”, “signaling”, and “metabolism”. Furthermore, to assess the reliability of the results, we conducted a GSEA analysis on DEGs. The findings are consistent with the previously mentioned results. We hypothesize that the high-risk group exhibits elevated levels of oxidative and hypoxic stress, which may lead to cell death. This is evidenced by the upregulation of the apoptosis, hypoxia, glycolysis, and DNA repair pathways observed in our study. Therefore, malignant cells undergo several alterations to adapt to harsh environments and evade cell death.

We speculate that the high-risk group exhibits elevated levels of mitochondrial stress response. Mechanistically, the p53 pathway and the TNF-α/NF-κB signaling transduction pathway are significantly upregulated, which has been shown to activate the mitochondrial stress response to alleviate endogenous stress [10, 40]. The upregulation of the unfolded protein response and apoptosis pathways further exacerbates this effect. Emerging evidence [41] indicates that upregulation of the mitochondrial calcium uniporter is associated with enhanced cancer stem cells stemness and self-renewal capacity of pancreatic cancer stem cells [42]. Cancer stem cells are a major cause of treatment failure and tumor recurrence in clinical oncology [43, 44]. Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation is frequently upregulated in cancer stem cells, supporting stemness maintenance and promoting therapeutic resistance. Mitochondrial calcium uniporter mediates mitochondrial calcium influx, consequently activating endoplasmic reticulum stress and unfolded protein response-related pathways [41]. Upon exposure to ROS, cancer cells initially activate glycolysis to meet energy demands. As ROS levels increase, G6PD activity is suppressed, redirecting metabolic flux from glycolysis to the pentose phosphate pathway [45]. Inhibition of G6PD induces endoplasmic reticulum stress, leading to dysregulation of autophagy [46]. This autophagic response subsequently drives cancer stem cells toward endothelial differentiation [47, 48]. Collectively, these interconnected mechanisms may contribute to the poorer overall survival observed in the PAAD high-risk group.

Mitochondria-derived ROS function as key second messengers in immune regulation [49], while either excessive or insufficient mitochondrial ROS can lead to chronic inflammation, autoimmunity, and cancer [50]. In line with this, the MOSs-risk subgroups revealed the immune infiltration microenvironment and tumor mutation burden associated with varying outcomes, enhancing the model’s interpretability and applicability. The ssGSEA analysis indicates a relatively high abundance of activated CD4 T cells, gamma delta T cells, and CD56 bright NK cells within the high-risk group. The secretion of IFN-γ by these cells plays a crucial role in activating lymphocytes to enhance anti-tumor effects [51]. In our study, the IFN-γ response pathway was found to be upregulated in the high-risk group. In the current investigation, we observed a higher frequency of TP53 and CDKN2A mutations in patients within the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group, which are known drivers of pancreatic cancer [52]. To further explore the biological significance of MOS-related genes, we performed consensus clustering analysis based on MOSDEGs, which classified PAAD patients into three distinct molecular subtypes. Subsequent GSEA‑GO enrichment analysis revealed that Clusters 1 and 2 were significantly enriched in immune-related pathways, further supporting the association between mitochondrial oxidative stress and immune modulation. However, due to the limited sample size of Cluster 3 (n = 11), these findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant validation in larger cohorts. Mitochondrial oxidative stress generates ROS that may function as signaling molecules to promote immune cell recruitment, while simultaneously inducing DNA damage that leads to immune cell dysfunction. Through these complex mechanisms, mitochondrial ROS contribute to the initiation and progression of pancreatic cancer. Additionally, we predicted sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents. However, this sensitivity analysis was based on computational predictions and should be interpreted as exploratory hypotheses requiring experimental confirmation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the MOS score showed significant prognostic value, its short-term predictive accuracy was limited, indicating that it is more suitable for risk stratification than precise individual survival prediction. Future studies with additional predictors are needed to improve model performance. Second, all analyses were based on public databases without validation in independent prospective cohorts, which may introduce potential bias and limit clinical applicability. Third, the molecular mechanisms linking the MOS signature to pancreatic cancer progression remain to be fully elucidated, warranting further in vitro and in vivo experimental validation.

Conclusion

In summary, our study provides a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis that may serve as a foundation for future research on mitochondrial oxidative stress-related prognostic signatures in pancreatic cancer.
