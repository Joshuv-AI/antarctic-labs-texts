Association Between Alcohol Drinking Patterns and Multiple Metabolic Syndrome Components in Non‐Obese and Obese Individuals
Authors: Ayako Kikuchi, Hisashi Yoshimoto, Takafumi Monma, Sakiko Ozawa, Momoko Tsuchida, Fumi Takeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis cross‐sectional study aimed to investigate the relationship between drinking patterns (combination of the frequency and amount of alcohol consumption) and the possession of multiple metabolic syndrome (MetS) components in non‐obese and obese individuals.MethodsData derived from medical examinations, specific health checkup questionnaires, and health insurance claims of 36 006 individuals aged 40–64 years from five health insurance associations affiliated with Japanese companies were analyzed. Modified Poisson regression analysis was conducted with multiple MetS components as the objective variable, drinking patterns as the explanatory variable, and sex, age, weight gain ≥ 10 kg since the age of 20 years, and lifestyle habits except for drinking patterns as adjustment variables.ResultsDrinking patterns associated with a high risk of possessing multiple MetS components were “everyday drinking” ≥ 20 g/day and “sometimes drinking” ≥ 40 g/day in non‐obese individuals and “everyday drinking” ≥ 60 g/day in obese individuals. Across these risky drinking patterns, the prevalence ratios for the possession of multiple MetS components were higher in non‐obese individuals than in obese individuals. Conversely, drinking patterns associated with a lower risk of possessing multiple MetS components were “everyday drinking < 20 g/day” in non‐obese individuals and “sometimes drinking < 20 g/day” and “everyday drinking < 20 g/day” in obese individuals.ConclusionNon‐obese individuals, even when drinking less frequently and in smaller amounts, showed a higher risk of possessing multiple MetS components than obese individuals, underscoring the importance of supporting reduced alcohol consumption among non‐obese individuals.Keywords: amount of alcohol consumption, frequency of drinking, metabolic syndrome, non‐obese, obese

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS), a complex of abdominal obesity, obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and lipid abnormalities, is a risk factor for mortality and cardiovascular disease (CVD) development [1, 2, 3]. In Japan, the diagnostic criteria for MetS include abdominal obesity as an essential component and two or more MetS components, such as hypertension, hyperglycemia, and lipid abnormalities [4]. Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare (MHLW) has mandated the implementation of specific health checkups and guidance for individuals aged 40–74 years in communities and companies to prevent CVD and diabetes [5]. These specific health checkups involve medical examinations focusing on MetS and self‐administered questionnaires on lifestyle habits, including smoking, exercise, diet, drinking, and sleep. Subsequently, specific health guidance is provided to improve the lifestyle habits of obese individuals (abdominal circumference ≥ 85 cm for men and ≥ 90 cm for women or body mass index [BMI] ≥ 25 kg/m2) with multiple MetS components. However, non‐obese individuals with multiple MetS components do not receive health guidance [5].

Previous studies have reported higher mortality rates and CVD incidence rates in both obese and non‐obese individuals with multiple MetS components [6, 7, 8], highlighting the importance of not having multiple MetS components even in the absence of obesity. Few studies have examined lifestyle habits associated with multiple MetS components in non‐obese individuals. We conducted a large survey comparing the lifestyle habits of non‐obese and obese Japanese individuals and found the following [9]. Among 11 lifestyle habits, including smoking, exercise, diet, drinking, and sleep, the amount of alcohol consumption per day exhibited the highest odds ratios (ORs) for possession of multiple MetS components. Higher daily alcohol consumption may increase the risk of possessing multiple MetS components. Conversely, the frequency of alcohol consumption did not show a linear relationship. In particular, “sometimes drinking” in non‐obese individuals and both “sometimes drinking” and “everyday drinking” in obese individuals had lower ORs for possessing multiple MetS components. Given that the relationship between the presence or absence of multiple MetS components differs depending on the frequency and amount of alcohol consumption, clarifying the relationship between drinking patterns (combination of the frequency and amount of alcohol consumption) and MetS components is necessary.

However, although there have been multiple studies on alcohol consumption and MetS, there are few studies on how drinking patterns (combination of frequency and daily alcohol consumption) affect the risk of multiple MetS components, or how these patterns differ between obese and non‐obese individuals. A cross‐sectional study in Japan reported that drinking patterns of a small amount per day (0.1 and < 10 g/day) for 1–2 days per week had a lower ORs for MetS than non‐drinking among women [10]. Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate the relationship between drinking patterns (combination of the frequency and amount of alcohol consumption per day) and multiple MetS components in non‐obese and obese individuals using data derived from specific health checkups in Japan. Based on previous findings [9, 10], we hypothesized that (1) drinking patterns characterized by more frequent drinking and a higher amount of alcohol consumption per day are associated with a high risk of possessing multiple MetS components, (2) drinking patterns that pose a high risk of possessing multiple MetS components differ between obese and non‐obese individuals, and (3) “sometimes drinking” with a lower amount of alcohol consumption per day presents a lower risk of possessing multiple MetS components than non‐drinking. The findings of this study, based on real‐world data, provide empirical evidence regarding specific drinking patterns associated with a high risk of MetS components in non‐obese individuals, offering valuable insights for the formulation of concrete measures to improve drinking habits.

Methods

Materials

This cross‐sectional study analyzed data from specific health checkups (medical examinations and self‐administered questionnaires) and health insurance claims in 2015. This study included 36 006 individuals aged 40–64 years who were enrolled in five health insurance associations affiliated with Japanese manufacturing companies and who had no history of stroke, heart disease, chronic kidney disease, or renal failure. Individuals with serious illness who had stopped drinking alcohol for health reasons were excluded because they might have been included in the study population. Medical history was confirmed by asking standard specific health checkup questions for stroke (cerebral hemorrhage, cerebral infarction, etc.), heart disease (angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, etc.), and chronic kidney disease or renal failure, including treatment history (dialysis therapy).

Anonymous data were provided by the health insurance association, which approved their use for this study. The Research Ethics Committee of the Institute of Health and Sport Sciences of the University of Tsukuba, Japan, approved this study (approval number: Tai 29‐132).

Measures

Medical examinations included sex, age, BMI, abdominal circumference (around the navel), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, fasting blood glucose (requiring a fasting period of at least 10 h), and lipid levels (triglyceride and high‐density lipoprotein [HDL] cholesterol levels). All physical assessments, blood pressure measurements, and blood tests were uniformly performed using standardized techniques across registered health checkup institutions. In Japan, the MHLW defines and mandates inspection methods and ensures quality control for all assessments under the ‘standard program of the specific health checkup and guidance (2013 edition) [11]. Therefore, the accuracy of tests is guaranteed, even when conducted by different health checkup institutions.

Obesity was determined based on waist circumference or BMI. Waist circumference was defined as ≥ 85 cm for men and ≥ 90 cm for women according to the diagnostic criteria for MetS [5]. BMI was defined based on the standard program of the specific health checkup and guidance (2013 edition) [11]. Following the diagnostic criteria for MetS [5], hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg or receiving medications for hypertension, hyperglycemia was defined as fasting blood glucose level ≥ 110 mg/dL or receiving medications for diabetes, and lipid abnormalities were defined as triglyceride level ≥ 150 mg/dL or HDL cholesterol level < 40 mg/dL or receiving medications for dyslipidemia.

Self‐administered questionnaires established in the ‘standard program of the specific health checkups and guidance (2013 edition) [11] covered various health and lifestyle aspects. Data on the following items were collected: medication use (for hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia), weight gain ≥ 10 kg since the age of 20 years, and lifestyle habits, including the frequency of drinking, amount of alcohol consumption per day, current smoking, regular exercise (continually engaging in sweat‐inducing exercise for ≥ 30 min, two or more times weekly, for one or more years), physical activity (walking or engaging in physical activities with an equivalent intensity for one or more hours per day), walking speed (faster than individuals of the same age and sex), eating late‐evening meals (eating meals at ≤ 2 h before bedtime, three or more times per week), eating snacks after dinner (eating evening snacks other than the three regular meals, three or more times per week), skipping breakfast (skipping breakfast three or more times per week), eating speed (faster than others), and sleeping well and enough.

The frequency of drinking (“How often do you drink alcohol?”) was rated on a three‐point scale as follows: “everyday,” “sometimes,” or “hardly—ever/never.” The amount of alcohol consumption per day (“How much alcohol consumption per day on drinking days?”) was rated on a four‐point scale as follows: “< 180 mL/day” (pure alcohol consumption per day; < 20 g/day), “180–360 mL/day” (pure alcohol consumption per day; 20–< 40 g/day), “360–540 mL/day” (pure alcohol consumption per day; 40–< 60 g/day), or “≥ 540 mL/day” (pure ethanol consumption per day; ≥ 60 g/day). With respect to other lifestyle habits, poor lifestyle habits were defined as “yes” for current smoking, “no” for regular exercise, “no” for physical activity, “slow” for walking speed, “yes” for eating late‐evening meals, “yes” for eating snacks after dinner, “yes” for skipping breakfast, “faster” for eating speed, and “no” for sleeping well and enough.

Data Analysis

The following nine drinking patterns were created based on the combination of “frequency of drinking” and “amount of alcohol consumption per day”: (1) hardly ever/never, (2) sometimes < 20 g/day, (3) sometimes 20–< 40 g/day, (4) sometimes 40–< 60 g/day, sometimes ≥ 60 g/day, (6) everyday < 20 g/day, everyday 20–< 40 g/day, (8) everyday 40–< 60 g/day, and (9) everyday ≥ 60 g/day.

Basic statistics were summarized for all drinking pattern variables. The relationship between drinking patterns and the participants' attributes and obesity status was examined using the chi‐square test and residual analysis. Additionally, Modified Poisson regression analysis with robust (sandwich) variance estimation was conducted with multiple MetS components (MetS components ≤ 1: 0; MetS components ≥ 2: 1) as the objective variable, drinking patterns as the explanatory variable, and sex, age, weight gain ≥ 10 kg since the age of 20 years, and lifestyle habits except for drinking patterns as adjustment variables. Prevalence ratios (PR), which are analogous to risk ratios when using cross‐sectional data, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to examine the association with the possession of multiple MetS components in non‐obese and obese individuals after adjusting for variables. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis was conducted for each sex owing to differences in sex distribution.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 24.0J (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.4.3 (rqlm package), with the statistical significance level set at 5%.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between drinking patterns (combination of the frequency and amount of alcohol consumption per day) and multiple MetS components in both non‐obese and obese individuals using data derived from specific health checkups. Of the study participants, 61.2% and 38.8% were non‐obese and obese individuals, respectively. This distribution was similar to that observed in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (non‐obese: 57.8%, obese: 42.2%) [12] and the Large‐scale Workplace Health Survey (non‐obese: 59.8%, obese: 40.2%) [13], which adopted the same definitions as this study.

First, the results of Modified Poisson regression analysis adjusted for sex, age, weight gain ≥ 10 kg since the age of 20 years, and lifestyle habits except for drinking patterns revealed that the drinking patterns associated with the highest risk of possessing multiple MetS components were common to both non‐obese and obese individuals. Everyday drinking ≥ 60 g/day was identified as the drinking pattern with the highest risk. Sensitivity analysis also showed that everyday drinking ≥ 60 g/day was associated with a higher risk in both non‐obese and obese men and in non‐obese women. Therefore, our findings support the first hypothesis that drinking patterns characterized by more frequent drinking and higher amounts of alcohol consumption per day are associated with a higher risk of possessing multiple MetS components.

Second, the drinking patterns associated with a high risk of possessing multiple MetS components were “everyday drinking ≥ 20 g/day” and “sometimes drinking ≥ 40 g/day” in non‐obese individuals, whereas only “everyday drinking ≥ 60 g/day” in obese individuals, furthermore the magnitude of risk of posing multiple MetS components was greater for non‐obese individuals than for obese individuals. On sensitivity analysis, drinking patterns of “sometimes ≥ 20 g/day” also showed an association with a high risk of possessing multiple MetS components in non‐obese men, indicating that even infrequent, low amount drinking poses a risk of posing multiple MetS components in non‐obese men. Therefore, our findings support the second hypothesis that drinking patterns posing a high risk of multiple MetS components differ between obese and non‐obese individuals.

The study findings are consistent with previous findings. For example, drinking had a higher risk of diabetes development [14, 15, 16], and reduced Homeostatic Model Assessment of Beta Cell Function [HOMA‐B] values and abnormal fasting plasma glucose [FPG] levels [17], and hypertension [18, 19] in non‐obese, whereas a relatively low risk in obese individuals. In addition, regular alcohol consumption in obese individuals led to increased HDL‐cholesterol levels, decreased low‐density lipoprotein [LDL]‐cholesterol levels, and improved insulin resistance [20]. Based on the above, it is possible that drinking habits appear to be strongly associated with possessing multiple MetS components in non‐obese individuals, especially in men, even infrequent, low amount drinking poses a risk, whereas obesity itself and lifestyle factors other than drinking habits primarily associated with possessing multiple MetS components in obese individuals. Therefore, maintaining appropriate drinking habits is critically important for non‐obese individuals, and it is considered necessary to reconsider drinking criteria in light of obesity status.

Third, the drinking patterns of everyday drinking < 20 g/day in non‐obese individuals and < 20 g/day in obese individuals were associated with a lower risk of possessing multiple MetS components than non‐drinking. Sensitivity analysis showed that among non‐obese individuals, the “everyday drinking < 20 g/day” drinking pattern was associated with a lower risk in both men and women. Among obese individuals, drinking patterns of < 20 g/day in men and sometimes drinking of 20 g– < 40 g/day in women were associated with a lower risk of possessing multiple MetS components. These findings for obese individuals are consistent with those of a previous study [10] and support the third hypothesis that “sometimes drinking” with a lower amount of alcohol consumption per day presents a lower risk of possessing multiple MetS components than non‐drinking. These results suggest that limiting alcohol consumption to everyday drinking < 20 g/day in non‐obese individuals and sometimes drinking < 20 g/day in obese individuals may be effective in preventing the possession of multiple MetS components.

The present study demonstrated the following points. First, the risk of possessing multiple MetS components increases in non‐obese individuals even at lower levels of alcohol consumption than in the obese group, highlighting the importance of proper drinking habits for non‐obese individuals and the need to reconsider drinking criteria that account for obese status. Second, both increased frequency and increased amount elevated the risk of possessing multiple MetS components, and reducing either the frequency or amount was confirmed to lower the risk. In alcohol reduction support, improving drinking habits is often difficult because alcohol is addictive. The findings of this study suggest that reducing consumption to levels below current habits may decrease the risk of possessing multiple MetS components and that setting incremental goals is likely effective. Standard health checkup and guidance programs require that opportunities for health examinations and guidance be used to actively provide information and health guidance focusing on the risks of alcohol consumption [5]. Based on the “alcohol consumption levels that increase the risk of lifestyle‐related diseases” defined in Health Japan 21 (the second term) (daily pure alcohol consumption of ≥ 40 g in men and ≥ 20 g in women) [21], active support for reducing alcohol consumption should be implemented. Nevertheless, because specific health guidance is implemented for obese individuals, support for non‐obese individuals is limited to the provision of information, with little guidance being offered. In the present study, the proportion of individuals exhibiting high‐risk drinking patterns was higher in the non‐obese group (sometimes ≥ 40 g and everyday ≥ 20 g: 35.3%) than in the obese group (everyday ≥ 60 g: 2.5%). Japan has a non‐obesity rate of 73.3% in the general population aged ≥ 15 years, making it a country with a high proportion of non‐obese individuals globally [22]. Therefore, drinking habits intervention targeting non‐obese individuals may be important for preventing MetS.

This study had some limitations. First, as this was a cross‐sectional study, the causal relationship between drinking patterns and multiple MetS components could not be investigated. Furthermore, longitudinal studies are needed to establish causality regarding whether the threshold for alcohol consumption in health guidance for non‐obese individuals should be set lower than for obese individuals. As this study is cross‐sectional in design, it could not investigate causality. Future longitudinal studies should be conducted to examine causality. Second, generalizing the results of this study to the entire Japanese population is difficult because the study participants belonged to company health insurance societies. Third, data on the frequency and amount of alcohol consumption per day were collected using self‐reported questionnaires in specific health checkups; thus, a reporting bias cannot be ruled out. Further verification with a wider range of objectives and survey data is required. Fourth, information on drinking habits was based on a specific health checkup questionnaire, which only included the frequency and amount of alcohol consumption. Details such as genotypes of alcohol‐metabolizing enzymes involved, types of alcoholic beverages, energy intake from diet, and content of alcohol consumed during drinking were not examined. These unadjusted factors should be verified in future studies. Fifth, this study used the 2015 specific health checkup questionnaire, which did not distinguish ex‐drinkers from never drinkers. Some ex‐drinkers might have stopped drinking because of their deteriorating health, potentially introducing bias into the results. Although this survey excluded individuals with a history of cerebrovascular disease, CVD, or renal failure, some individuals taking medications possibly abstained from drinking alcohol. Future studies should distinguish ex‐drinkers from never drinkers for analysis. Lastly, in the analysis of female drinkers, the number of obese individuals and heavy drinkers was insufficient for a thorough analysis. Future analyses require a larger sample size.

Despite these limitations, the present study has strength in that, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the association between drinking patterns (combination of the frequency and amount of alcohol consumption per day) and the possession of multiple MetS components in a large population of non‐obese and obese individuals. This study demonstrated that non‐obese individuals, even when drinking less frequently and in smaller amounts, had a higher risk of possessing multiple MetS components than obese individuals, underscoring the importance of supporting reduced alcohol consumption among non‐obese individuals. Furthermore, it identified drinking patterns suitable for MetS prevention based on obesity status and suggested that setting stepwise alcohol reduction goals may be effective. This study provided more detailed empirical evidence based on real‐world data to facilitate the formulation of more specific guidelines for personalized improvement of drinking habits.

Conclusions

This study investigated the relationship between drinking patterns and multiple MetS components in non‐obese and obese individuals. The drinking patterns associated with a high risk of possessing multiple MetS components were everyday drinking ≥ 20 g/day and sometimes drinking ≥ 40 g/day in non‐obese individuals and everyday drinking ≥ 60 g/day in obese individuals. The PRs for these risky drinking patterns were higher in non‐obese individuals than in obese individuals. Conversely, the drinking pattern associated with a lower risk of possessing multiple MetS components was everyday drinking < 20 g/day in both non‐obese and obese individuals. Obese individuals with “sometimes drinking < 20 g/day” pattern additionally exhibited a lower risk. These findings suggest that non‐obese individuals had a higher risk of possessing multiple MetS components than obese individuals even at lower alcohol consumption levels, highlighting the importance of support for reducing alcohol intake among non‐obese individuals to prevent MetS.
