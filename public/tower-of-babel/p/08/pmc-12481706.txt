Efficacy of Repeated High-Dose Versus Intermediate-Dose Intravenous Dexamethasone in Reducing Pain After Bilateral Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Burin Sutthapakti, Wiboon Wanitcharoenporn, Katawut Kumplean, Kritsada Sukha, Artit Laoruengthana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal dose of intravenous (IV) dexamethasone for bilateral one-stage total knee arthroplasty (BTKA) remains controversial. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of administering repeated high-dose IV dexamethasone compared to repeated intermediate-dose dexamethasone for BTKA.MethodsIn this randomized, triple-blinded, clinical trial of 46 patients who underwent BTKA were randomly divided into 2 groups. The high-dose group received 16 mg of IV dexamethasone before surgical incision and a repeated dose at 24 hours postoperatively. The intermediate-dose group received 8 mg of dexamethasone at the same time points. The outcomes measured included visual analog scale (VAS) for pain intensity during motion and at rest, morphine consumption (MC), knee flexion angle, knee strength, knee circumference, timed up and go, frequency of postoperative nausea and vomiting, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, blood glucose levels, and complications.ResultsNo significant differences were found between groups regarding VAS, except the high-dose group reporting lower VAS during motion only at 24 hours postoperatively (mean difference: −0.9, P < .001). The high-dose group had lower overall MC during the hospital stay (mean difference: −2.22 mg, P = .030) and smaller knee circumference at 24 and 72 hours (P < .05). However, the difference in VAS and MC did not reach the minimal clinically important difference (1.8 points and 10 mg, respectively). No significant differences were found between groups regarding functional recovery outcomes and biomarkers.ConclusionsRepeated high-dose IV dexamethasone provides comparable clinical outcomes to the intermediate-dose IV dexamethasone. Therefore, intermediate-dose IV dexamethasone may be sufficient for controlling pain and inflammatory response in BTKA setting.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) serves as an effective intervention to enhance both the health span and overall quality of life in patients who had end-stage knee osteoarthritis [1]. Recently, bilateral one-stage TKA (BTKA) has gained popularity due to its potential benefits, such as faster overall recovery following rehabilitation and a reduced need for repeated hospitalizations and anesthesia [[2], [3], [4]]. Recent study has reported that BTKA can meet the expectation of patient who aged >70 with more comorbidities, without higher risk of postoperative complications or readmissions than the younger patient [5]. Nonetheless, postoperative pain is a major concern, especially in elderly patients who had multiple comorbidities undergoing BTKA, as they may have opioids-related side effects such as pruritus, nausea, and vomiting [6,7].

Glucocorticoids have shown substantial benefits in managing postoperative inflammation following TKA. By modulating the innate and adaptive immune responses to surgical injury, they help prevent postoperative systemic inflammatory dysregulation, a condition associated with increased risk of infection, organ dysfunction, and delayed recovery. Acting through both genomic and nongenomic pathways, glucocorticoids suppress excessive proinflammatory cytokine activity while promoting resolution and tissue repair [8]. Clinical studies and meta-analyses support their role in reducing postoperative pain, nausea, fatigue, and complications such as respiratory failure and acute kidney injury, with an acceptable safety profile, particularly in low-risk patients [9,10]. Previous study demonstrated that intravenous (IV) dexamethasone could also benefit patients undergoing BTKA, who are more likely to experience heightened inflammatory responses [11]. Additionally, the use of moderate- to high-dose regimens, and repeated dexamethasone dosing, may enhance recovery outcomes; however, further research is needed to optimize dosing and identify patients who are most likely to benefit [[12], [13], [14]].

According to a meta-analysis comparing low, intermediate, and high doses of dexamethasone, both intermediate (0.11-0.2 mg/kg) and high doses (>0.2 mg/kg) significantly reduced postoperative pain and morphine consumption (MC) after surgical procedures when compared to low doses (<0.11 mg/kg) [15]. Additionally, the use of repeated dexamethasone dosing has been shown to reduce pain scores and opioids consumption better than a single-dose regimen [12,13]. However, the optimal repeated dose remains unclear, particularly for patients undergoing BTKA [16]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the use of repeated high-dose regimens may further reduce pain and enhance recovery outcomes following BTKA. This study aimed to compare postoperative pain, functional recovery, and inflammatory biomarkers between the repeated high-dose and intermediate-dose regimen, in patients who are undergoing BTKA.

Material and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This prospective, randomized, triple-blinded, clinical trial was conducted at a single tertiary hospital from July 2023 to June 2024. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board and the trial was registered with the registration number: TCTR20250104002. Patients scheduled for primary BTKA for osteoarthritis, aged between 55 and 85 years, and weighing between 50 and 80 kg were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria were use of corticosteroids or immunosuppressants within the past 3 months; use of strong opioids within the past 7 days; suspected or confirmed infections; a history of allergy to study medications; and significant comorbidities such as uncontrolled diabetes, active gastric ulcers, severe heart disease, or liver/renal failure. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The required sample size was determined using a formula for comparing 2 independent means. Based on data from a prior study [17], a sample size of 20 patients per group was required to detect a clinically significant difference in the primary outcome with 90% power and a 2-sided alpha level of 0.05. To account for a potential 15% dropout rate, the target enrollment was adjusted to a minimum of 23 patients per group.

A total of 52 patients were screened for eligibility, and 46 participants were subsequently enrolled and randomly assigned to 1 of 2 groups (Fig. 1). Block randomization was performed by a research assistant not involved in patient care. Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened by the anesthetist just before the procedure. Intervention

All patients received a standardized preoperative medication protocol consisting of 1 g of paracetamol and 25 mg of amitriptyline, administered at bedtime on the night before surgery. Anesthesia was performed using spinal anesthesia combined with an adductor canal block (15 ml of 0.3% bupivacaine). All participants were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status 1-3.

All procedures were performed by 1 of 2 senior arthroplasty surgeons following a standardized technique. A prophylactic antibiotic was administered preoperatively, and a tourniquet was inflated to 300 mmHg. A minimally invasive medial parapatellar approach was used with conventional instruments. Bone cuts were made to achieve mechanical alignment, and the patella was not resurfaced. All patients received a cemented, fixed-bearing, posterior-stabilized prosthesis from 1 of 3 systems (Zimmer BIOMET NEXGEN LPS; Smith & Nephew Anthem; Johnson & Johnson ATTUNE). Before wound closure, a periarticular injection containing 100 mg bupivacaine, 1 mg epinephrine, and normal saline to a total volume of 40 ml was administered into the posterior capsule and surrounding soft tissues.

The postoperative multimodal analgesia regimen was identical for both groups and included additional intravenous morphine 2 mg as needed every 2 hours for breakthrough pain, oral meloxicam (7.5 mg once daily), tramadol hydrochloride (50 mg every 8 hours), acetaminophen (500 mg every 8 hours), and amitriptyline (25 mg at bedtime). Catheters were removed on the morning of postoperative day (POD) 1. The rehabilitation protocol included full weight-bearing as tolerated starting on the first POD. Standard deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis, consisting of ankle-pumping exercises and oral aspirin (162 mg once daily), was prescribed for 30 days.

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome was pain intensity during motion by using the visual analog scale (VAS) at 8, 16, 24, 48, and 72 hours postoperatively. The secondary outcomes included VAS at rest at 8, 16, 24, 48, and 72 hours postoperatively, MC, knee circumference (measured 10 cm above the superior pole of the patella), knee flexion (active range of motion was measured in degrees using a goniometer), knee strength (assessed by the ability to perform an active straight leg raise against gravity), timed up and go (TUG), the incidence (yes/no) of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and serum blood glucose levels (BGLs) which were recorded at 24, 48, and 72 hours postoperatively. The complications and infection rates were tracked at 2 weeks, 1 month, and 3 months after surgery. This study was triple-blinded: the patients, the surgical team, and the postoperative outcome assessors (nurses) were all blinded to the group assignments.

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient data. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. To assess the success of randomization, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the groups using independent t-tests for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For the primary outcome, postoperative VAS scores during motion and at rest were compared between the high-dose and intermediate-dose dexamethasone groups using a linear mixed-effects model.

Secondary outcomes including MC, knee flexion, knee circumference, hs-CRP levels, TUG test, and BGLs were compared between groups using a linear mixed-effects model. Knee strength (defined as the ability of patients to independently extend their leg), and PONV were analyzed using a generalized estimating equations model with a binomial family and an identity link function. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX), with statistical significance set at a P < .05.

Inflammatory biomarkers and other related outcomes

Concerning inflammatory biomarkers, no significant differences were observed between the groups for hs-CRP levels; however, the high-dose group demonstrated a significant reduction in knee circumference (an indicator of swelling) at both 24 hours postoperatively (mean difference: −0.63 cm; 95% CI: −1.00 to −0.26, P = .001) and 72 hours postoperatively (mean difference: −1.04 cm; 95% CI: −1.99 to −0.10, P = .031). The BGL and the frequency of PONV showed no difference between groups (Table 3). The incidence of transient hyperglycemia (blood glucose >137 mg/dL) on POD 1 was 39.1% (9 of 23) in the high-dose dexamethasone group and 34.7% (8 of 23) in the intermediate-dose group (P = .76). Moreover, no instances of surgical site infection, periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), or wound dehiscence were observed during the 3-month follow-up period.

Discussion

Currently, dexamethasone is widely employed for controlling pain after TKA, and various studies have evaluated the efficacy of different dosing regimens. Previous clinical trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated that repeated doses of IV dexamethasone appear to further reduce pain, opioid consumption, and PONV after TKA [12,13,17]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether increased doses of dexamethasone provide better dynamic pain control, reduce inflammation, and enhance recovery for patients undergoing more extensive procedures like BTKA [18,19]. For unilateral TKA, a meta-analysis exploring the effect of systemic corticosteroids at varying doses revealed that higher-dose corticosteroids (15-20 mg) were associated with further pain reduction on POD 0 and decreased PONV on PODs 1-2 [18]. Our study in BTKA found that high-dose dexamethasone resulted in a 0.9-point lower VAS during motion at 24 hours postoperatively (P < .001), while there was no difference in VAS scores during motion or at rest at other time points. The high-dose group also required 2.2 mg less MC during the hospital stay (P = .03), but no significant difference in the incidence of PONV was observed. However, the mean differences in VAS during motion and in MC did not exceed the minimal clinically important difference of 1.8 points and 10 mg, respectively [20].

Hence, our findings may illustrate the different effects of dexamethasone dosage in specific patient groups. Additionally, Nielsen et al. [19] compared preoperative high-dose vs intermediate-dose IV dexamethasone in 157 patients with low preoperative pain catastrophizing and no opioid use who were undergoing unilateral TKA. They reported no difference between the groups in the incidence of subjects experiencing a VAS >3 during ambulation at 24 hours, nor were there differences in overall average pain scores, opioid and antiemetic consumption, Quality of Recovery-15 scores, length of stay, or complications. In contrast, they found that high-dose dexamethasone could benefit a high-pain–responder population by reducing moderate-to-severe pain during ambulation at 24 hours post-TKA and improving Quality of Recovery-15 scores [21].

In terms of functional recovery, a randomized controlled trial comparing unilateral TKA subjects who received a placebo, 8 mg of IV dexamethasone, or 16 mg of IV dexamethasone, Chan et al. [22] demonstrated that the high-dose dexamethasone group consumed significantly fewer opioids, had better quadriceps strength during the first 3 days post-TKA, and had a longer walking distance on POD 1 compared to the placebo group. They also found a significant dose-dependent trend between the dexamethasone dose and both pain scores and functional recovery. In our cohort, a notable finding was the significant reduction in knee circumference, indicating less swelling, in the high-dose dexamethasone group at both 24 and 72 hours postoperatively. This reduction in periarticular edema may contribute to improved patient comfort and potentially facilitate earlier mobility during the early recovery phase [23]. Holm et al. [24] indicated that increased postoperative knee swelling is significantly correlated with decreased knee-extension strength, and reduction of knee-extension strength and knee flexion are associated with inferior fast-speed walking performance. Despite this impact of postoperative knee swelling, our study did not find significant differences in other measured functional recovery parameters—including knee flexion, knee strength, and the TUG test—between the high-dose and intermediate-dose dexamethasone groups at any time point during the first 72 hours after BTKA. Furthermore, systemic inflammatory markers, such as the level of hs-CRP, did not differ between the groups. Another study revealed, in accordance with our findings, that functional recovery was comparable among patients who received different doses of IV dexamethasone [20]. Additionally, a recent meta-analysis recommended that administering 8-10 mg of IV dexamethasone perioperatively should be considered adequate for analgesia [13].

Indeed, systemic high-dose corticosteroids may raise concerns about postoperative hyperglycemia, which can be related to subsequent wound complications and PJI. Maintaining a BGL below 137 mg/dL on POD 1 indicates better glycemic control and may help reduce the risk of PJI [25]. A recent study reported that dexamethasone administration is a predictive factor for hyperglycemia, defined as a BGL >137 mg/dL, on POD 1 after TKA, with a higher incidence of hyperglycemia in the dexamethasone group (55% vs 38%; P < .0001) [26]. In the present study, there were no significant differences in mean BGL or the incidence of transient hyperglycemia between the high-dose and intermediate-dose groups. Subsequently, the incidences of wound complications and PJI did not differ between the groups. Supporting our findings, a systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that the perioperative administration of corticosteroids in total joint arthroplasty does not increase the risk of postoperative infections in patients with limited comorbidities, regardless of the dose or frequency of administration [27].

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Our study focused solely on 2 fixed dosing regimens administered perioperatively and at 24 hours, without exploring alternative strategies such as divided or repeated dosing beyond this timeframe. Second, although dexamethasone may have no prolonged effects on pain and patient satisfaction beyond POD 3 after TKA [28], our 3-month follow-up to assess the effects of corticosteroid dosages on the risks of wound complications and PJI may be relatively too short period. Additionally, our sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome of VAS during motion at 24 hours, and thus our population is likely underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences in some secondary outcomes, such as complications. Further research with larger cohort and longer follow-up may be warranted to assess functional outcomes and complications.

Conclusions

Repeated high-dose IV dexamethasone provides comparable clinical outcomes to the intermediate-dose IV dexamethasone in terms of pain scores, MC, functional recovery and change of inflammatory biomarker. Therefore, intermediate-dose IV dexamethasone may be sufficient for controlling pain and inflammatory response in BTKA setting.
