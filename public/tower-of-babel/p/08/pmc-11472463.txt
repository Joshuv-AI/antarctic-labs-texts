Understanding stigma and attitudes towards hepatitis B among university students in Australia of Chinese and Vietnamese background
Authors: Loren Brener, Robyn Horwitz, Elena Cama, Hoang Minh Khoi Vu, Defeng Jin, Kwok On Eric WU, Jake Rance, Timothy Broady, Carla Treloar, Limin Mao, Sylvester Okeke, Joanne Bryant
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHepatitis B is a significant public health concern and a leading cause of liver cancer across the world. In Australia, hepatitis B is largely endemic in migrant communities, particularly amongst the Chinese and Vietnamese communities. Negative attitudes towards hepatitis B can be a major barrier to hepatitis B testing and linkage to care. This mixed-methods research explores the attitudes and beliefs, including stigma, about hepatitis B among students of Vietnamese and Chinese background in Australia. Students were chosen as participants as there is little research examining hepatitis B among university students in Australia and they provide a point of entry to communities with high prevalence of hepatitis B, that otherwise may be hard to access.MethodsOnline surveys were distributed in Chinese, Vietnamese, and English via social media and completed by 112 students of Chinese and 95 students of Vietnamese backgrounds. In-depth interviews were also conducted with 13 Vietnamese and 10 Chinese participants to further explore the survey results.ResultsSurvey findings suggest that students have varied attitudes towards people living with hepatitis B. Around half of the participants reported they would behave negatively towards other people with hepatitis B and that they would expect to experience stigma or discrimination if they had hepatitis B. While over 70% in both samples reported that people who have hepatitis B should not be isolated by family and friends, 47.6% of the Chinese sample and 28.3% of the Vietnamese sample reported they would avoid close contact with someone with hepatitis B. The qualitative data expands on the quantitative data. Four key themes were identified: (1) Caution not discrimination (2) Hepatitis B as a sign of immoral behaviour (3) Discriminatory behaviour based on perceived effects of hepatitis B and (4) Shifting attitudes.ConclusionThis research provides insights that could inform culturally sensitive health promotion programs to address negative attitudes towards hepatitis B among the broader Chinese and Vietnamese communities living in Australia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20226-0.

Introduction

Hepatitis B (HBV) is a significant public health concern and a leading cause of liver cancer across the world [1]. It is estimated that globally approximately 254 million people were living with chronic HBV infection in 2022, with 1.2 million new infection each year [2]. HBV-related diseases resulted in the deaths of 1.1 million people in 2022 [2]. While HBV cannot be cured, treatment can prevent morbidity and mortality [1]. While there are antiviral therapies that effectively manage HBV and decrease the risk of liver failure and liver cancer, only an estimated 10% (29 million) of those chronically infected with HBV are diagnosed, and only 5% of those eligible for treatment actually receive it [3, 4]. HBV is transmitted through blood and other body fluids of infected individuals [1]. It is an infectious disease that can be transmitted through either vertical (mother to child during birth and delivery) or horizontal transmission (blood transfusion, dental services, unprotected sexual contact, substance abuse, sharing of razors, toothbrushes and non-sterile tattooing etc.) (4–5). HBV is vaccine-preventable and all babies should receive the HBV vaccine. In addition, there are effective antiviral medicines to prevent vertical transmission [1, 2].

There is an estimated 205,549 people living with HBV in Australia in 2022 [6]. Even though Australia has supported universal infant vaccination since 2000, Australia has not been able to meet its national target of eliminating HBV. Australia has committed to targets set by the World Health Organization to eliminate hepatitis B as a public health threat by 2030 [7]. HBV is largely endemic in migrant communities in Australia, as approximately 70% of Australians living with HBV were born overseas [8] particularly in Northeast and Southeast Asia [6, 8] and researchers have reported that the most common languages spoken by those living with HBV in Australia are Chinese and Vietnamese [9].

Vietnam is ranked 9th worldwide for the disease burden from HBV infections [3]. Recent evidence suggests that most people diagnosed with HBV in Australia are from Vietnam [8], where liver cancer is known to be the most common type of cancer (14.5%) [10]. There are approximately 257,997 Vietnamese-born people living in Australia [11]. The highest number of HBV infections worldwide is in China, where 87 million people are reported to be living with chronic HBV, accounting for about one-third of global chronic HBV burden [12]. There are approximately 549,618 Chinese-born people in Australia [13]. Thus, Chinese-born people living in Australia are overrepresented among people living with HBV, comprising 2.2% of the total population but over 27% of the group living with HBV [14]. Thus, there is significant cultural and linguistic diversity among people living with or at high risk of HBV in Australia. As a result, people from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds are recognised as a priority population in the national strategy for HBV [15].

It is important to understand how migrant communities in Australia think about HBV because attitudes may influence access to and engagement in testing and care [16]. While effective monitoring, prevention, and treatment strategies are available, stigma and lack of knowledge are recognised as significant barriers to their effectiveness [17–19]. Stigma is a complex construct that involves social processes of exclusion of people for possessing a characteristic that is viewed negatively by a broader social group [20–22]. Stigma is entrenched in social norms and creates feelings of inferiority and shame, with stigmatised populations often avoiding situations in which they fear being the target of discriminatory behaviour [23, 24]. As with other infectious diseases, stigma towards people living with HBV largely stems from fears of transmission [25–27] which may be driven by lack of awareness and knowledge about HBV [28, 29]. Given that people living with HBV may experience avoidance behaviours as discriminatory, this result reflects how fear of transmission of HBV is one of the key drivers of stigma surrounding the virus [25, 30]. For instance, current literature suggests that fear of infection, especially fear of infecting close family, negative assumptions and stereotyping around transmission and the sources of infection, as well as stigma and misinformation have all been linked with negative attitudes towards people living with HBV [31, 32]. Examples of these negative attitudes include a general unwillingness to engage in close physical contact, such as kissing or hugging, with people living with HBV [33, 34], and feelings that people with HBV should not be allowed to work in restaurants [32].

Stigma is often associated with feeling of shame, a painful emotion, often associated with an array of poor health and a belief that a person will be perceived negatively by others and could lead to rejection or exclusion [16, 35–37]. People living with chronic HBV, particularly migrants from Vietnamese or Chinese backgrounds, often report feeling either embarrassed, ashamed, or that their HBV had brought shame to their families [33, 38].

Literature on the intersection between stigma and racism has highlighted negative community perceptions, attitudes, and behaviours towards people of migrant backgrounds based on their race, language, and cultural dissimilarities [39–41]. Extensive research illustrates that stigma acts as a barrier to healthcare and may result in unequal access to health services with stigmatised individuals experiencing judgemental or hostile interactions in health services [16, 42–44]. Racism reported in healthcare services towards migrant populations often takes the form of reduced cultural competency among healthcare providers [45–49], as seen by misuse or overuse of translators [50], healthcare workers who are insensitive to the culture and race of clients [45], and negative attitudes from healthcare workers based on culture or race [49]. Stigma has damaging impacts on the health outcomes and well-being of people living with HBV [51, 52].

Structural systems, including legislation and policy, can further perpetuate and reinforce stigma and discrimination towards marginalised affected populations [53, 54]. Therefore, it is important to look at the effects of historical structural discrimination against people living with HBV. For example, in China, regulations were introduced by the government between 1987 and 1996 that imposed restrictions on all people living with viral hepatitis in employment and education [55]. Despite steps being taken after 2005, to remove structural discrimination against people living with HBV, stigma and discrimination remains a real concern in China and for Chinese migrants [33, 34].

Many students studying within Australia are international and come from countries with high prevalence of HBV. Research focusing on HBV knowledge and testing among these students is limited, despite the vulnerability of tertiary institution students to high-risk practices such as unprotected, casual sexual activity [30, 56–58]. Migrant communities may be hard to access, due to barriers such as language and communication, racism and discrimination, migrant status and cultural difference [59–62]. Further, they often experience higher levels of loneliness, social isolation, financial difficulties and stigma [63] and due to the stigmatised nature of HBV, they may be less likely to seek health advice as they may not want people in the same community to know about their illness. This research sampled tertiary education students from Vietnamese and Chinese communities to explore the values, attitudes, and beliefs, including stigma among these groups of students towards HBV. It is intended that the results of this study will inform the design of health promotion messages and programs to address negative attitudes towards HBV among students and potentially for the broader community of people of Chinese and Vietnamese backgrounds living in Australia.

Materials and methods

Sample and Procedure

In addition to providing data regarding their own perspectives, students were chosen as the sample as they provide a point of entry to communities that otherwise may be hard to access. They understand their broader community and can act as a bridge between their community and the new home country environment. Additionally, a significant proportion of the international students will remain in Australia following their studies.

The research adopted a mixed-methods design, consisting of surveys to explore participants’ attitudes and understandings of hepatitis B, followed by in-depth interviews to explore these attitudes in more detail. This study is part of a broader program of work that monitors experiences of stigma among people affected by blood-borne viruses and sexually transmissible infections [64].

Researchers of Chinese and Vietnamese background distributed advertisements for the survey through Australian wide social media sites for Chinese and Vietnamese university students such as community-based Facebook groups. To be eligible, participants were required to be over 18 years, identify as Vietnamese or Chinese, and be a current student at an Australian educational institution. Prospective participants were directed to a survey website where they could find further information and complete the survey in English, simplified Chinese, or Vietnamese. The surveys took approximately 15–20 min to complete. Each participant received an AUD $15 gift voucher to compensate them for their time.

Interviews were conducted with a subset of students who participated in the online survey component of the research. On completion of the survey, participants were invited to indicate their interest in taking part in an interview. A member of the research team contacted the interested participants by email, provided them with a participant information statement specific to the interview and contact details of the researcher. Those still interested in going ahead with the interview were directed to contact the researcher so that a suitable time for the interview could be arranged. Despite initial interest from 50 survey participants, after 23 participants were recruited and interviewed, no further participants indicated a willingness to be interviewed. After initial analysis of the interview data it was determined that this data provided information relevant to the answer the research questions of the study, and no further recruitment was undertaken. Interviews were conducted by phone or using online platforms such as Teams and Zoom. Interviews were conducted In English by a research team member of the same cultural background as participants (either Vietnamese or Chinese background) and lasted approximately 20–30 min. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. The data was also read and coded by researchers of the same background as participants to ensure the interpretation of the data accurately reflected cultural nuances. Participants were compensated with an AUD $30 gift voucher for their time. This research has ethics approval from the UNSW Human Research Ethics Committee (HC210722).

Quantitative measures

Prior to implementation, the survey was pre-tested with members of the Vietnamese and Chinese communities who were support workers working for multicultural and viral hepatitis community organisations that assisted with data collection. After pre-testing, slight wording changes were made to remove ambiguities and better reflect the language of the participants.

Stigma items

Two items were included in the survey that assessed the potential experience and expression of stigma or discrimination in relation to hepatitis B: “If you had HBV, do you think you would experience stigma or discrimination (e.g., avoidance, pity, blame, shame, rejection, verbal abuse, bullying) in relation to your HBV?” [64] and “Would you behave negatively towards other people because of their hepatitis B?” [65]. The response options for these questions were on a 5-point scale ranging from never (1) to always (5).

Attitude items

Attitudes towards hepatitis B were assessed using nine items adapted from previous research [66] that measured attitudes towards people with HBV. Jin et al., conducted a scoping review of both English and Chinese language attitude measures of stigma towards people living with HBV [66]. From this review, a new measure consisting of key items adapted from those outlined in the scoping review and particularly designed for participants living in Australia was developed and validated [67]. We used a modified version of this validated measure in the present study. For example, “I would not have a romantic relationship with someone who has HBV.” The response option for these questions were on a 5-point scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5).

The attitude and stigma items together detect (1) labelling people living with HBV, (2) stereotyping, and (3) discriminatory behaviour towards people living with HBV. These items were included as significant gaps in knowledge around transmission of the virus as well as misconceptions about ways of preventing transmission and spreading the virus, has been linked with negative attitudes and behaviour towards people living with HBV [66, 68].

Characteristics of the sample

Demographic questions included in the survey, were age, gender, number of years living in Australia, education, financial stability, living arrangements, language spoken at home, language proficiency, HBV status and knowing someone with HBV. Questions around language were worded slightly different for the two samples, based on feedback and pretesting from the Chinese and Vietnamese community.

Qualitative methods

Interviews, guided by a semi-structured interview guide (attached as Appendix), extended on findings from the quantitative data. The questions explored attitudes, beliefs, experiences and cultural values about health generally, specifically about HBV transmission, and impacts of stigma, and whether differences existed in attitudes towards HBV in Australia compared to their home countries.

Data analyses

Quantitative data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 28. Descriptive statistics were used to describe demographic and attitudinal characteristics of the sample. The qualitative data generated through interviews was thematically analysed. Data analysis for interviews involved both inductive and deductive coding and occurred in several iterative stages [69]. First, the team developed a broad coding template based on the research questions and review of the literature. Next, the Vietnamese, Cantonese and Mandarin-speaking researchers used this template to read and code the transcripts from their interviewees and develop preliminary themes and discussion points for the team to consider. A preliminary analysis document was then drafted, and a team meeting was held to discuss themes and check on interpretation with respect to cultural meanings.

Results from the qualitative sample

Sample characteristics

Twenty-three students were interviewed; ten were of Chinese background (including two from Hong Kong) and thirteen were of Vietnamese background. They had lived in Australia for between 2 months and 12 years, and their ages ranged from 19 to 29 years. The names provided along the quotes are all pseudonyms and not the actual names of the participants.

Hepatitis B related stigma and discrimination

Results from the quantitative arm were varied but overall suggested that negative attitudes and stigma are driven by misinformation and lack of knowledge around transmission routes based on findings from attitude items that reflect concerns and behaviours around the way the virus can be spread. Findings were similar among both the Chinese and Vietnamese participants and thus are presented together below except for their understanding of HBV vaccination. Vietnamese participants talked about vaccination differently to the Chinese participants because in Vietnam there is a national vaccination initiative which meant that many more of those participants were vaccinated. Participants from mainland China and Hong Kong rarely mentioned vaccines, while by comparison Vietnamese participants mentioned them much more often. 2) Hepatitis B as a sign of immoral behaviour

A second theme was that attitudes towards hepatitis B were perceived, to be shaped by beliefs around transmission of the virus as related to behaviours which are understood as bad or immoral such as injecting drug use, unprotected sex, sex with multiple partners, and sex work [58]. The stereotyping of people who may have HBV as drug users or those that engage in at risk sexual practices leads to negative attitudes and stigma and potentially discrimination. It further highlights how misinformation around transmission routes and lack of knowledge about transmission can ultimately lead to conceptions that a person living with HBV is someone who engages in practices which are perceived to be immoral.

My parents are quite traditional. If anyone has this disease [hepatitis B], they will think that person is [sexually] promiscuous. I mean in China, this disease is related to sexual life easily, like HIV. The person who has indulgent sexual life will get the disease (Jane, China).

Discussion

Findings across the Chinese and Vietnamese samples highlight varied feelings towards people living with HBV among both samples. For example, 71.25% of all participants reported that people who have HBV should not be isolated by family and friends, while 37.95% responded that they would behave negatively towards other people because of their HBV, including avoiding close contact with them. Negative attitudes and stigma appear to be underpinned by knowledge deficits about HBV transmission routes and fear of HBV infection such as fears about close contact, sharing eating utensils, and the necessity of screening job applicants to prevent transmission [70]. This finding supports previous research where stigma related to HBV appears to be mostly driven by knowledge deficits and fear of HBV infection, especially around sharing food [67, 71]. Hence, identifying these varied attitudes suggests a possible link between misunderstandings of HBV transmission among students of Chinese and Vietnamese background and negative attitudes and discriminatory behaviour.

The interview data builds on the findings from the survey and further explored attitudes that Chinese and Vietnamese students hold towards HBV, how they understand stigma and discrimination, and why they perceive it occurs (i.e., as a means of protection rather than a dislike of the group). While this is not perceived as discriminatory by the participant themselves, it represents a form of stigma by the desire to be distanced from those who have HBV. The qualitative data reflects how fears of transmission of HBV along with lack of knowledge and misinformation may be contributing to stigma surrounding the virus. Stigma was expressed by participants being interviewed as being related to a perceived lack of productivity where the person is perceived as less valuable in the workforce by virtue of having HBV. As a result, they are not valued as highly or seen as productive as those without HBV. The qualitative results support existing literature that show negative attitudes held by participants towards people living with HBV, as well as contradictory explanations around stigma, can partly be explained by lack of knowledge and misinformation about HBV [38, 68, 72, 73].

Thus, these findings suggest that in order to reduce stigma around HBV as well as avoidance behaviour and discriminatory attitudes (such as not wanting to share a room with a person living with HBV) it may be necessary to increase knowledge of the virus and routes of transmission, for example to address the misconception that HBV can be spread through the air or contact [70].

Based on the findings, it is evident that there is a need for targeted HBV intervention and education. In terms of education, the gaps highlighted in the data indicate that it is important to focus on increasing knowledge about hepatitis B transmission and prevention, which may have flow on effects in reducing negative perceptions and assumptions about those living with the virus. This is especially true with respect to knowledge about transmission through food, eating utensils and sharing space, as there may be is a tendency to isolate and exclude others if they have hepatitis B. Sharing a meal is known to be culturally important among these groups and provides a platform for meaningful interaction among family, friends and the community [74, 75]. Hence, if people get excluded or treated differently at family or social gatherings this could be really difficult for them, leading to feelings of shame and loss of connection and can result in concealing their HBV status. Any intervention program must also explain the inconsistencies in understandings and responses to HBV and highlight that distancing oneself through fear of transmission represents a form of discrimination [33, 34, 72]. In addition, any intervention must work to address the negative attitudes that people still hold towards potential transmission routes for blood borne viruses, including both hepatitis B and C, such as injecting drug use and sex work.

The findings indicate specific lack of awareness among participants about what people with HBV may perceive as discriminatory or stigmatizing. The notion of behaviour being deemed acceptable when it is intended to ensure self-protection, yet in essence may be perceived as discriminatory by people living with HBV needs to be addressed. While intervention aimed at improving knowledge about transmission and prevention will reduce negative perceptions about HBV, strategies that specifically aim to change this perception must seek more detailed information about how stigma and discrimination is understood and operates in Vietnamese and Chinese community settings, as these seem to have different meanings for some. This is necessary before any meaningful intervention about HBV stigma can proceed.

Interventions to address negative attitudes could work alongside education to increase knowledge and reduce misunderstandings. Contact with members of the affected community is an important mechanism for increasing positive attitudes. This has been demonstrated in the use of innovative training strategies that involve social contact and testimonies from persons with lived experience, contributing to a reported decrease in stigmatising attitudes amongst healthcare workers [76–82]. Research has shown that attitudes among a sample of people from the general public towards people living with HBV also improved after exposure to a contact-based video [83].

However, it is important to recognise that for these Chinese and Vietnamese communities, and indeed any migrant community, there may also be specific structural factors (such as previously discussed Chinese policy of mandatory HBV testing for job applicants) that may contribute to the development of negative attitudes. This is evidenced in the present study by high number of the sample, nearly 65% of the students of Chinese background, who agreed that testing for HBV is necessary for job applications, indicating that students perceived mandatory testing in the workplace to be acceptable. The responses to this item show how structural stigma can shape the public’s perceptions of HBV and the importance of addressing structural stigma alongside personal and interpersonal stigma in any HBV stigma reduction intervention program.

This study has some limitations which must be mentioned. The sample is self-selected and there is likely a bias in terms of those who volunteer to participate in online survey research versus those that do not. For example, those who choose to participate in a survey on HBV may have more knowledge or more exposure to HBV. In addition, whenever a survey is used it is open to participants’ interpretation of the items and thus findings may reflect these personal interpretations. While many participants indicated that they would be willing to participate in an interview after they had completed the survey, in the end it was difficult to recruit interview participants, particularly those of Chinese background, and those who did agree to an interview may be a group with more awareness of HBV. Additionally, by targeting students we may miss out on the perceptions of older people who may have different views and awareness of HBV Students may also systematically differ from the wider Chinese and Vietnamese communities in many ways and as such another limitation with using students is one of generalizability. Students simply may not represent the views and attitudes of the general Chinese and Vietnamese communities in Australia or elsewhere.

Conclusion

Progress in response to the HBV epidemic in Australia is challenging partly because of the diversity of communities affected by HBV, involving different culturally-based understandings of health and HBV virus [79–87]. This mixed methods study has provided some valuable insights into the main drivers of negative attitudes, stigma (fear of contagion fuelled by misinformation and lack of knowledge) and discriminatory attitudes that students from two large migrant communities hold towards HBV. Students could provide a point of entry to communities that otherwise may be hard to access as they can serve as key informants and provide valuable information regarding strategies to further develop hepatitis B prevention, testing and treatment programs tailored for specific migrant communities.
