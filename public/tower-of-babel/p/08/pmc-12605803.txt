Efficacy of erdafitinib before or after enfortumab vedotin in FGFR3-altered advanced urothelial cancer: analysis of the UNITE collaborative study
Authors: Cindy Y Jiang, Hyunsoo Hwang, Ilana Y Epstein, Dimitra Rafailia Bakaloudi, Rafee Talukder, Amy K Taylor, Amanda Nizam, Tanya Jindal, Michael J Glover, Ali Raza Khaki, Pedro C Barata, Charles B Nguyen, Eugene Oh, Nancy B Davis, Hannah Mabey, Christopher J Hoimes, Sean T Evans, Bashar Abuqayas, Emily Lemke, Irene Tsung, Wei Qiao, Deepak Kilari, Yousef Zakharia, Mehmet A Bilen, Matthew I Milowsky, Sumit A Shah, Shilpa Gupta, Hamid Emamekhoo, Joaquim Bellmunt, Ajjai S Alva, Petros Grivas, Pavlos Msaouel, Vadim S Koshkin, Matthew T Campbell, Omar Alhalabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundErdafitinib is approved for locally advanced/metastatic urothelial cancer (LA/mUC). As enfortumab vedotin (EV) plus pembrolizumab enters frontline management, outcomes with erdafitinib pre- and post-EV are clinically relevant but not specifically evaluated in clinical trials.MethodsUNITE is a multi-institutional retrospective study of patients with LA/mUC treated with novel targeted agents. All patients with FGFR3 alterations treated with EV only, erdafitinib then EV (Erda->EV), and EV then erdafitinib (EV->Erda) were included. Sequential treatment with EV and Erda was not required. Primary endpoints were observed response rates (ORR) and progression-free survival (PFS); secondary endpoint was overall survival (OS).ResultsWe identified 83 patients with FGFR3 alterations and separated them into three cohorts: EV only (n = 44), Erda->EV (n = 24), and EV->Erda (n = 15). Most (72%) received ≥2 lines of therapy before erdafitinib (checkpoint inhibitor [87%], platinum-based chemotherapy [64%]). Median PFS with erdafitinib for EV-naïve cohort was 7.5 months and in EV-treated cohort 4.0 months (HR 0.78; 95% CI 0.35-1.7). ORR with erdafitinib for EV-naïve was 33% and EV-treated 31% (OR 1.1; 95% CI 0.29-4.1). Median PFS with EV for patients who were erdafitinib-naïve was 6 months and in erdafitinib-treated 5.3 months (HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.34-1.09). ORR with EV was 54% in erdafitinib-naïve cohort and 32% in erdafitinib-treated cohort (OR 2.5; 95% CI 0.87-6.3).ConclusionIn patients with FGFR3-altered LA/mUC, erdafitinib is active pre- and post-EV. Outcomes with erdafitinib were consistent with clinical trial data generated prior to broader frontline use of EV. Findings are hypothesis-generating and given small sample size should be interpreted with caution.

Non-trial outcomes of erdafitinib in FGFR3-altered locally advanced/metastatic urothelial cancer are consistent with reported clinical trial data. Erdafitinib therapy is effective in the pre- and post- EV setting.

Introduction

Historically, standard of care for untreated locally advanced/metastatic urothelial carcinoma (LA/mUC) has been platinum-based chemotherapy followed by switch maintenance avelumab in patients with no progression, or for platinum-ineligible patients, treatment with immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) directed at programmed death receptor 1 or its ligand (PD-1 or PDL-1).1–3 In subsequent lines, options have included antibody drug conjugates (ADC), such as enfortumab vedotin (EV), sacituzumab govitecan (SG) (of note, the phase III TROPiCs-04 trial showed no significant improvement in overall survival with SG vs taxane or vinflunine,4 which led to the withdrawal of its FDA approval in the United States, however SG remains a “salvage” treatment option on the NCCN guidelines and its status is less clear in the European Union), and trastuzumab deruxtecan for tumors with HER2 IHC3 (based on gastric cancer scoring algorithm), or targeted therapy with erdafitinib, a fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) inhibitor.5–10 The current available data for the optimal sequencing of these therapies for patients with FGFR3-altered LA/mUC are relatively limited. Additionally, EV in combination with pembrolizumab was approved and is the preferred frontline regimen in LA/mUC and has significantly shifted the treatment landscape, which further highlights the need to explore the activity of erdafitinib after EV.11,12

FGFR3 alterations occur in approximately 20% of patients with LA/mUC and is more frequent in upper tract urothelial carcinoma.9,13,14 Erdafitinib is a potent oral selective pan-FGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor.15 Erdafitinib originally received accelerated FDA approval in April 2019 for LA/mUC with FGFR3 or FGFR2 susceptible alterations following progression on platinum-based chemotherapy based on single arm trial data from the BLC2001 phase II trial showing 40% objective response rate, 5.5 months median progression-free survival (PFS), and 11.3 months median overall survival (OS).9 Subsequently, in January 2024, the FDA granted a full approval for erdafitinib based on the randomized phase III trial (BLC3001, THOR cohort 1) showing longer OS (HR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.47-0.88), PFS (HR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.44-0.78), and higher objective response rate 45.6% (RR = 3.94, 95% CI 2.37-6.57) vs salvage taxane or vinfunine chemotherapy.16 Notably, this full FDA approval was given for tumors harboring FGFR3 alterations only. The BLC3001 trial included patients who had received 1-2 prior lines of treatment, including progression after ICI, but it was conducted prior to the era of EV and pembrolizumab frontline use. Hence, the activity of erdafitinib post-EV or activity of EV post-erdafitinib has been the subject of investigation. It has been suggested that Nectin-4 (the target of EV) expression and perhaps EV activity may be higher in luminal subtypes of UC,17 which is also the subtype where FGFR3 alterations are enriched.18,19 Therefore, it is possible that both agents may target similar molecular subtypes. Interestingly, a pre-clinical study was conducted with findings suggesting that FGFR inhibition may increase Nectin-4 expression, the target of EV.20

Given the significant efficacy and regulatory approval for erdafitinib in FGFR3-altered LA/mUC, we aimed to determine the outcomes of erdafitinib before and after EV. Herein, we present our analysis of the Urothelial Cancer Network to Investigate Therapeutic Experiences (UNITE) study database. We focused specifically on the efficacy of erdafitinib and EV and analyzed three different cohorts: patients with FGFR3 alterations who received (1) EV only, (2) EV followed by erdafitinib, and (3) erdafitinib followed by EV. We hypothesized that patients treated with EV followed by erdafitinib and those treated with erdafitinib followed by EV would experience comparable clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Patients

UNITE is a multi-institutional retrospective study including 17 US academic sites focused on the collection of retrospective data of patients with LA/mUC who receive novel therapeutic agents. Patient eligibility criteria for this analysis specifically, included histologically confirmed urothelial carcinoma (histology subtypes with any percentage allowed), having LA/mUC, presence of FGFR3 alteration based on tumor next generation sequencing (NGS), receiving at least one dose of EV monotherapy (on clinical trial or as part of standard of care), and availability of clinical, pathologic, and imaging data in the electronic medical record. Exclusion criteria included receipt of EV-based combinations, FGFR2 alterations, and patients with FGFR3 loss with lack of activating mutation or fusion. We classified FGFR3 alterations as canonical (standard or well-established FGFR3 mutation or fusion) based on the alterations included in the THOR cohort 1 trial.16 See Table S1 for FGFR3 alterations classified as “canonical.”

The data cutoff date for this initial analysis was August 18, 2023. We grouped patients into three different cohorts: those who received EV only, erdafitinib then EV (Erda->EV), and EV then erdafitinib (EV->Erda). Sequential therapy with EV and erdafitinib immediately after each other was not required. A CONSORT flow diagram is provided in Figure S1. Additional analysis was completed by combining all patients who had received erdafitinib to assess for “standard of care treatment” outcomes of erdafitinib, and similar analysis was performed with all patients who received EV.

To be eligible for response assessment, patients had to have at least one scan completed after starting EV or erdafitinib treatment or clear evidence of clinical progression per the treating physician’s judgement. The observed response rate (ORR) was defined as a complete or partial response based on the investigator’s judgement after reviewing the Electronic Medical Record (EMR). Utilizing the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1) was highly encouraged, however there was no centralized scan review and no formal measurements were undertaken. This study was approved by the institutional review board at each participating institution. All patient data were de-identified with no protected health information included. Data were maintained on a secure database via REDCap (research electronic data capture) hosted on the University of Michigan server.21,22

Molecular testing

Molecular testing was completed based on treating physician’s discretion and included both commercial and institutional NGS platforms. The commercial NGS platforms utilized include Foundation Medicine (n = 22; of note, three of these patients also had Guardant 360 ctDNA testing and one patient also had Strata Oncology testing), Guardant 360 (n = 2), Caris (n = 9; of note, two of these patients also had Guardant 360 ctDNA testing), Strata Oncology (n = 1), Ashion (n = 1), and Tempus (n = 12). Institutional platforms that were used include UCSF500 at University of California San Francisco (n = 5; of note, one of these patients also had Caris testing), MD Anderson Mutation Analysis Precision Panel (n = 29), University of Washington Oncoplex (n = 1), PanSolid Tumor at Cleveland Clinic (n = 1), and OncoPanel at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (n = 14). There was one patient with an unknown NGS platform used. The available NGS data were obtained on tumor tissue samples or circulating tumor DNA that were collected prior to EV treatment.

Statistical analysis

For survival analysis of patients who received erdafitinib, we designated patients as EV-naïve or EV-treated. The EV-naïve group received erdafitinib without prior exposure to EV therapy (erda->EV). The EV-treated group had previously received EV before treatment with erdafitinib (EV->erda). Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from erdafitinib start to time of progression or death. If patients were alive without progression, they were censored at the date of last follow up. Overall survival (OS) with erdafitinib was defined as the time from erdafitinib start to time of death or censored at time of last follow up. We also assessed OS from time of metastatic diagnosis to time of death, with patients being censored at time of last follow up. Additionally, we separately analyzed OS and PFS for all patients who had received erdafitinib at any time. A similar analysis was done for patients who received EV by distinguishing patients as erdafitinib-naïve or erdafitinib-treated. The erdafitinib-naïve group had not received erdafitinib prior to EV (EV->Erda and EV only). The erdafitinib-treated group had received prior erdafitinib before exposure to EV therapy (Erda->EV).

Patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage, while continuous variables were described using median, minimum, and maximum values. To compare two groups, Fisher’s exact test was employed for categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for continuous variables. The primary endpoints were PFS and ORR; we also analyzed OS. OS and PFS were assessed with the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method. The association between survival outcomes and patient characteristics was evaluated using Cox regression models. The median follow-up time was estimated using the reverse KM method. ORR was described with summary statistics and compared between groups using Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.1, with statistical significance set at a P-value of 0.05 and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA).

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

At time of data query in August 2023, the UNITE database included 633 patients from 16 US sites with 487 of them having tumor NGS data available, of which 98 (20%) were identified as having FGFR2/3 alterations. We excluded patients who had received EV combination treatment (n = 7), FGFR2 alterations (n = 4), and patients with FGFR3 4p16.3 loss (n = 4). This led to a total of 83 patients with FGFR3 “activating” alterations included. Canonical FGFR3 alterations were present in 76% of patients with the most common FGFR3 alterations being S249C/R248C (47%) followed by FGFR3-TACC3 fusion (17%) and then Y373C/Y375C/G370C (15%). Figure 1 shows the breakdown of FGFR3 alteration based on sequence of treatment received. In total, there were 34 patients with canonical FGFR3 alterations (S249C, Y373C/Y375C, R248C, and FGFR3-TACC3 fusion) and 5 patients with non-canonical alterations (A248C, A391E, V555L/T807L, FGFR expression, and JAKMIP1-FGFR3 fusion) for patients who received erdafitinib. When assessing the entire study population, the median follow-up time was 46 months from metastatic diagnosis to the time of last follow up. At time of data cutoff, the median duration of treatment on EV was 4.6 months, while the median duration of treatment on erdafitinib was 5.5 months. The median age was 73 years (range 46-93) at the time of EV start. Most patients were male (75%) and White (89%). Most patients had primary tumor in the bladder (67%) and the most common histology was pure urothelial (76%). Most patients at EV start had BMI 19-30 (72%), ECOG ≤ 1 (82%), and visceral metastases, excluding bone metastases (62%). Most patients did not have documented/known diabetes mellitus (78%) or baseline neuropathy (68%). Most patients (80%) received ≥ two lines of therapy prior to EV in the LA/mUC setting. Prior to receiving EV, most patients had received prior checkpoint inhibitor (94%) and prior platinum-based chemotherapy (64%). Before receiving erdafitinib, 72% of patients received ≥ two lines of therapy with checkpoint inhibitor (87%) being the most common followed by platinum-based chemotherapy (54%).

Outcomes with erdafitinib

We found that patients who were EV-naïve prior to erdafitinib treatment (erda->EV) had median PFS 7.5 months with erdafitinib and patients with prior EV (EV->erda) had median PFS 4.0 months with erdafitinib (HR 0.78; 95% CI 0.35-1.7) (Figure 2A). The median OS from time of metastatic diagnosis for all patients treated with erdafitinib was 44.8 months (95% CI 39.0-50.4). The median OS after erdafitinib start in EV-naïve patients was 22.5 months and in EV-treated patients 10.5 months (HR 0.34; 95% CI 0.13-0.89) (Figure S2A). For all patients treated with erdafitinib (N = 39), median PFS was 6.8 months (95% CI 4.5-7.5) and median OS after erdafitinib 18 months (95% CI 14.7-21.3) (Figure 3A and B).

Of the evaluable patients (n = 37), ORR to erdafitinib for all patients was 32% (95% CI 20-49) and all were partial responses (PR) (n = 12) (Table 2). When assessing only patients who were EV-naïve, ORR to erdafitinib was 33%, while patients treated with erdafitinib post-EV had ORR 31% (OR 1.1; 95% CI 0.29-4.1) (Table 2). The reason for erdafitinib discontinuation in the Erda->EV cohort, was mainly progression (79%, n = 19), while the rest experienced intolerance (21%, n = 5). In the EV->Erda cohort, only one patient remains on erdafitinib, and the remainder were taken off erdafitinib due to either progression (67%, n = 10) or intolerance (27%, n = 4).

Outcomes with EV

We found that the median PFS with EV for erdafitinib-naïve patients was 6 months and in erdafitinib-treated patients was 5.3 months (HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.34-1.09) (Figure 2B). The median OS after EV start for erdafitinib-naïve patients was 14.8 months and for patients with prior erdafitinib 11.8 months (HR 0.95; 95% CI 0.52-1.7) (Figure S2B). We also combined all the patients (n = 83) who had received EV and noted median OS after EV start was 13.3 months (95% CI 10.7-15.9) and median PFS was 6 months (95% CI 4.6-7.4) (Figure 3C and D). The median OS from time of metastatic diagnosis for all patients who received EV was 34 months (95% CI 28.3-37.9).

There were 41 evaluable patients in the EV-only group for ORR and 22 evaluable in Erda->EV group. In the EV->erda group, all patients were evaluable (n = 15). When assessing ORR with EV for patients that were erdafitinib-naïve (EV only and EV->erda), ORR was 54%, while the erdafitinib-treated (erda->EV) group had ORR 32% (OR 2.5; 95% CI 0.87-6.3) with EV (Table 3).

At the time of analysis, more than half of the EV only (52%) and Erda->EV patients (63%) were deceased, while most of the EV->Erda cohort (80%) were deceased. In the Erda->EV cohort, only 17% (n = 4) remained on EV, while in the EV only cohort 23% (n = 10) were still on EV treatment. The reason for EV discontinuation in the Erda->EV cohort was mainly progression (58%, n = 14) followed by intolerance (13%, n = 3), death (8%, n = 2), and surgery (4%, n = 1). In the EV->Erda cohort, the majority were taken off EV due to progression (93%, n = 14) and only one patient experienced intolerance. While in the EV only cohort, 45% experienced progression (n = 20) as the reason for EV discontinuation, 15% intolerance (n = 6), and 18% for other reasons (eg, death, physician decision, and second primary malignancy).

Discussion

Our retrospective analysis of a multi-institutional cohort of patients with FGFR3-altered LA/mUC who received novel therapies suggests that erdafitinib is an effective treatment option in both the pre- and post-EV setting. We also found that patients had benefit with EV in both the pre- and post-erdafitinib setting. The results did not contradict the hypothesis that treatment with alternate sequences of these two drugs may yield similar outcomes. Our survival analysis across treatment groups is exploratory and hypothesis generating and may be subject to guarantee (immortal)-time bias. Given the small and imbalanced cohort sizes, statistical comparisons should be interpreted with caution. A larger cohort of patients with adequate sample size of all groups and longer follow up, would be needed to provide more precise results regarding the association between each treatment sequence and outcomes. It is imperative that we continue to develop a better understanding of these agents to minimize adverse events and therapy burden, facilitate therapy continuation when possible, comprehend resistance mechanisms, and inform optimal therapeutic combinations and sequences. Additionally, we found that the outcomes with erdafitinib had slight differences relative to the clinical trial results9,16,23 with ORR 32%, median PFS 7 months and median OS 18 months, which can be explained at least partially by the inherent differences in our retrospective study and prospective clinical trials.

Rouvinov et al. conducted a retrospective study and assessed 25 patients with mUC who received erdafitinib and analyzed clinical benefit in the standard of care setting.24 They found that the clinical benefit of erdafitinib was consistent with the BLC3001 clinical trial population. However, their data did not include patients treated with EV and therefore did not assess its potential impact on outcomes. We were unable to find any published study assessing the potential impact of erdafitinib exposure on outcomes to EV, or vice versa.

Interestingly, we noted that while most patients with FGFR3 alterations who received erdafitinib had canonical mutations (87%), there were fewer patients in the group that received EV only (66%) with canonical mutations (OR = 0.28; 95% CI 0.11, 0.90). It is possible that the type of FGFR3 alteration may have influenced providers in the standard of care setting regarding the decision of whether to proceed with erdafitinib treatment. Due to the limited number of patients with non-canonical alterations in our study, subgroup comparisons of outcomes between patients with canonical versus non-canonical alterations was not feasible. As non-canonical mutations are relatively uncommon, their true impact on treatment response to FGFR inhibitors such as erdafitinib remains unclear.25

Prior molecular profiling studies have shown that patients with FGFR-altered mUC achieved a 67% disease control rate when treated with FGFR inhibitors, and that FGFR3 alterations were mutually exclusive of TP53 alterations.26 Furthermore, it was found that patients with TP53 alterations tended to experience worse survival outcomes in the post-platinum setting.26 Biomarkers have also been explored in the setting of EV monotherapy and CDKN2A and CDKN2B alterations were linked to shorter progression free survival.27 Further investigation is needed to clarify the impact of these molecular alterations and how they relate to treatment sequencing and outcomes.

THOR cohort 2 phase III trial included patients who were PD(L)1 treatment-naïve with FGFR3-altered LA/mUC who had received prior chemotherapy.28 Patients received either erdafitinib or pembrolizumab as second line treatment and results showed no statistically significant difference in OS or PFS between these therapy options, with higher ORR with erdafitinib. The phase II Norse trial evaluated patients with mUC in the frontline setting and randomized them to erdafitinib plus cetrelimab (PD1 inhibitor) vs erdafitinib alone and showed higher ORR with the combination.29 An ongoing clinical trial (NCT04963153) is assessing the combination of EV and erdafitinib after platinum-based chemotherapy and anti-PD(L)1 in patients with mUC harboring FGFR2/3 alterations and recent preliminary report on the 9 enrolled patients showed ORR 100% with most being PRs (n = 8) and median PFS 7.5 months (95% CI 5.5-not reached).30,31 Alternative FGFR inhibitors are currently being explored as well.32

The treatment landscape for LA/mUC is rapidly evolving and there have been significant changes affecting frontline treatment options. Recently, EV plus pembrolizumab as well as the combination of gemcitabine, cisplatin, and nivolumab were approved for the frontline treatment of LA/mUC.33,34 In our study, many of the patients had received prior platinum-based chemotherapy and/or checkpoint inhibitor and despite this their tumor had response with EV or erdafitinib. These are encouraging findings suggesting that erdafitinib may be effective after receiving frontline EV plus pembrolizumab, while more data are needed for this particular patient population. Moving forward, it will be important to define the optimal sequencing of therapies for FGFR3-altered LA/mUC in the context of an evolving treatment landscape.

Despite this being a large multi-institutional collaborative effort, inherent limitations of our study include its retrospective nature, lack of randomization, and relatively modest sample size (with limited power), which could lead to potential selection, and confounding biases. Furthermore, we should consider the guarantee (immortal)-time bias impacting our survival analysis, particularly in the absence of a landmark analysis to account for it. Another limitation is that response assessments were investigator-assessed at each site without central imaging review. Although RECIST v1.1 confirmation was highly encouraged, it was not mandated, which may have introduced variability in ORR reporting. Additionally, the NGS platform/assays and tumor biospecimens (tumor tissue vs ctDNA) used varied between institutions and the FGFR3 alterations were collected based on EMR review without information on the variant/mutation allele fraction in cases of FGFR3 mutation. Moreover, the frequency of restaging scans may have varied, and this may have impacted ORR and PFS analyses. However, despite these limitations, the hypothesis-generating results provide useful insights into the treatment of a common subgroup of patients with LA/mUC in a real-world setting.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our retrospective analysis of the UNITE study showed that erdafitinib is effective before or after treatment with EV. Outcomes with erdafitinib for the entire cohort were relatively consistent with its activity that was observed in clinical trials. As the treatment of LA/mUC continues to evolve rapidly, determining the best sequence of treatments will continue to be of upmost importance. Future studies should focus on identifying prognostic and predictive biomarkers, resistance mechanisms, and evaluate new therapy options and combinations to overcome resistance and further improve outcomes in this patient population.
