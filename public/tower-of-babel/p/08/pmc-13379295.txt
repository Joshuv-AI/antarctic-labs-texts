A La0.9Sr0.1MnO3 perovskite modified glassy carbon electrode for non-enzymatic electrochemical sugar detection
Authors: Nguyen T. Quynh, D. Thu Le, P. Vuong Nguyen, T. T. Huyen Tran, K. Tuan Nguyen, Tran Thanh Tung, Hoang V. Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, La0.9Sr0.1MnO3 perovskite (Pe) material was successfully synthesized and subsequently integrated with chitosan (CS) and graphene oxide (GO) to form a nanocomposite, which was used to modify a glassy carbon electrode for application as a non-enzymatic electrochemical sensor for total sugar detection. The morphology and crystalline structure of the Pe material were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and infrared (IR) spectroscopy. The modified Pe/CS/GO/GCE electrode exhibited high sensitivity to various kinds of sugar, including glucose, sucrose, and lactose, while demonstrating excellent selectivity against common interfering substances present in canned soft drinks, such as citric acid, niacinamide, and sodium citrate. Notably, the activity of Pe/CS/GO/GCE toward glucose was studied using cyclic voltammetry (CV), showing a linear response over a concentration range from 5 to 14 mM, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.381 mM. The electrode was successfully applied to sugar determination in 5% intravenous glucose solution and in real food and beverage samples.

Introduction

The excessive consumption of dietary sugars is a growing global health concern. A primary factor impacting public health is diet, where excessive sugar consumption from beverages, fruits, and food products has led to significant consequences. Uncontrolled intake of dietary sugars can result in conditions such as diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular diseases, and dental problems.1–4 Electrochemical sensors have emerged as a promising analytical technique for the determination of glucose and overall sugar content, attracting significant research interest. Sugar sensors in general and glucose sensors in particular play an important role in the rapid diagnosis of the level of sugar/glucose in the blood to control and detect diabetes. Moreover, this technique offers advantages such as ease of fabrication, high accuracy, and suitability for home-use devices. Enzymatic biosensors have a number of benefits, such as quick analysis and the removal of complicated sample separation processes, allowing for effective and sensitive detection.5–9 However, the operational stability, long-term storage, and extensive practical application of natural enzymes are limited by their need for low-temperature storage and their high susceptibility to environmental denaturation.10–13 Recently, some nanomaterials, especially those known as nanozymes, which can mimic enzymatic activity to catalyse the conversion of sugar molecules, have been explored as alternatives to natural enzymes in the development of non-enzymatic sensors for determining sugar or glucose levels in soft drinks, fruits, and food products, which is essential for food quality and safety monitoring. This advancement can not only address the product quality control requirements of the food industry but can also play a crucial role in safeguarding public health, enabling consumers to make informed choices and prevent diseases associated with excessive sugar intake. The growing interest in graphene oxide (GO) for sensing applications arises from its large specific surface area and the abundance of functional groups on its surface. Although graphene-based materials have been widely investigated for gas sensing and biosensing, comparatively less attention has been given to pristine GO due to its insulating nature, which limits the efficient extraction of electrical signals. Chemically and thermally reduced GO can be more efficiently utilised for sensing applications due to its improved electrical conductivity, while it retains characteristic functional groups suitable for anchoring molecules and nanoparticles for developing chemical sensors.14–19 However, the excessive reduction of GO can decrease the density of oxygen-containing functional groups, which may limit active sites for chemical interactions and reduce sensing selectivity, necessitating careful material design and hybridisation with functional materials. Perovskite structures are highly versatile, offering a broad range of electrical, magnetic, optical, and mechanical properties over a wide temperature range.20–24 Therefore, perovskite materials are considered essential materials for numerous modern technologies, such as high-frequency communications and several other integrated technologies.25–28 To improve material dispersion, interfacial stability, and adhesion on the electrode surface, a suitable polymer binder is required. Chitosan (CS) is a naturally derived, biodegradable polymer with adsorption capacity, excellent film-forming ability, and high biocompatibility.29 Owing to these properties, CS can be used as an effective immobilizing agent for electroactive materials on electrode surfaces, exhibiting good adhesion and structural stability. Based on these considerations, this work proposes a simple composite of Pe, graphene oxide (GO), and chitosan to functionalise a glassy carbon electrode surface for the development of an electrochemical sensor for total sugar determination.

Experimental

Chemicals

La0.9Sr0.1MnO3 perovskite (Pe) was synthesized with modifications from a previous protocol,30 and subsequently the sample was finally calcined at 600 °C for 4 hours. Graphene oxide (GO) was prepared in our laboratory as described in our previous study.14d-(+)-Glucose (C6H12O6 ≥ 99.5 wt%), sucrose (C12H22O11, 100 wt%), α-lactose monohydrate (C12H22O11·H2O ≥ 99.5 wt%), chitosan (CS, 10 mg mL−1), citric acid monohydrate (C6H8O7·H2O ≥ 99.5 wt%), sodium citrate (C6H5Na3O7·2H2O), sodium hydroxide pellets (NaOH, analytical grade > 98 wt%) and niacinamide (C6H6N2O) were supplied by Sigma Aldrich. The intravenous sugar solution (5%) was purchased in a local pharmacy.

Modification of the GCE electrode

Firstly, Pe (2.5 mg mL−1), CS (10 mg mL−1) and GO (10 mg mL−1) were mixed thoroughly using ultrasound and a vortex mixer at ratios of 5 : 1 : 5 and 1 : 1 : 8, respectively, to obtain nanocomposite suspensions. Secondly, the GCE electrode was polished with alumina slurry, rinsed thoroughly with distilled water, and then dried at RT. Subsequently, 3 µL of the mixed Pe/CS/GO solution was drop-cast onto the GCE surface and allowed to dry for 30 minutes at RT.

Characterization of materials

The morphology of the Pe material was analyzed using a Nova NanoSEM 450 microscope (FEI Company), and X-ray diffraction spectra were obtained using PAN Empyrean equipment (λ = 0.154 nm) with a scanning step size of 0.03°/s. The electrochemical behaviour of the GCE and the modified GCE was investigated using DY2200 Series apparatus connected to a three-electrode system, including a Pt plate as the counter electrode, a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) in 3 M KCl solution as the reference electrode, and modified glassy carbon as the working electrode. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was used for investigating the electrochemical behaviour in a solution of 0.1 N NaOH in the voltage range from +0.4 V to +1.3 V (vs. SCE) with a scan rate of 50 mV s−1. For establishing the calibration curve corresponding to glucose detection, CVs of Pe/CS/GO/GCE were measured in 0.1 N NaOH medium with different glucose concentrations.

Results and discussion

Characterization of the synthesized La0.9Sr0.1MnO3 perovskite (Pe) material

The XRD pattern of perovskite sintered at 600 °C for 4 h (Fig. 1A) shows diffraction peaks at 2 theta values ≈ 23°, 32°, 40°, 46.5°, 52.6°, 58° and 68°, which match the (100), (110), (111), (200), (210), (211) and (220) planes of La0.9Sr0.1MnO3, respectively (JCPDS card number 53-0058).20,23,25,28 Small diffraction peaks corresponding to La2O3 were detected in the sample; however, the perovskite remains the principal crystalline phase. The characteristic diffraction peak of the perovskite material is the (110) peak at 2θ = ∼32°, which was used to estimate the crystallite size (D) by Scherrer's equation (eqn (1)):1where β is the full width at half maximum of the Bragg peak, λ is the wavelength of CuKα1 (λ = 1.5406 Å), and θ is the diffraction angle of the most intense peak.20 For the synthesized La0.9Sr0.1MnO3 perovskite, the average crystallite size (D) was found to be 10.67 nm. Fig. 1B shows the FTIR spectrum in the region of 500–4500 cm−1 of the Pe, in which the absorption band at around 610 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of the metal–oxygen bond in the perovskite structure, related to internal motion that changes the bond length of Mn–O–Mn within the MnO6 octahedra. The two peaks at 852 and 1454 cm−1 are attributed to SrCO3 and the peaks at around 2880 cm−1 correspond to C–H stretching vibrations. The peak at 1515 cm−1 is due to the bending mode of adsorbed water molecules, and the peak at around 3299 cm−1 is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations of the same species.24 A strong absorption band appearing in the vicinity of 1380 cm−1, specifically at 1362 cm−1, can be assigned to stretching vibrations of the COO− group.31 This indicates a crystalline powder consisting entirely of the Pe structure.22,26

Electrochemical behavior and glucose oxidation on the Pe/CS/GO/GCE electrode

The electrochemical behavior of the GCE and Pe/CS/GO-modified GCE was investigated using the cyclic voltammogram (CV) technique in 1 M KCl solution containing 3 mM K4[Fe(CN)6] (Fig. 2A). The theoretical peak-to-peak separation (ΔEp) is 59 mV, while for the bare (Fig. 2A(a)) and modified (Fig. 2A(b)) electrodes, the values were 86 and 60 mV, respectively. The value for the modified electrode negligibly diverges from theory, indicating that the transfer of electrons in the redox reaction is reversible. In addition, both the cathodic and anodic peak currents of the modified electrode are much higher than those of the unmodified electrode. This indicates that the material possesses good electrical conductivity, which enhances its electrochemical reactivity. The electroactive surface area was estimated using the Randles–Sevcik equation (eqn (2)):322where Ip is current intensity of the peak (A), with Ip = 3.43 × 10−5 A for the modified GCE and 2.97 × 10−5 A for the GCE; n is the number of electrons transferred in the redox reaction (n = 1); D is the diffusion coefficient (D = 6.66 × 10−6 cm2 s−1); C is the bulk concentration of the electroactive species (C = 3 × 10−6 mol cm−3); v is the scan rate (v = 0.05 V s−1); and A is the electroactive surface area (cm2). A was calculated to be 6.38 mm2 for the bare electrode and 7.38 mm2 for the modified electrode. As shown in Fig. 2B, upon increasing the scan rate, the response redox current intensity in the CVs increased. The linear relationships between the current density (Ipa and Ipc) and both the scan rate (v) (Fig. 2C) and the square root of the scan rate (v1/2) (Fig. 2D) show that the current densities (Ipa and Ipc) have a higher correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.998) with v1/2 than that of Ipa and Ipcvs. scan rate (v) (with R2 = 0.957), implying that the electrochemical process on the modified GCE is diffusion controlled.33

Electrochemical detection of sugars

Fig. 3A shows the CVs of a bare GCE (curves a and c) and Pe/CS/GO/GCE (curves b and d) recorded in a 0.1 N NaOH without (curves a and b) and with (curves c and d) 5 mM glucose. The obtained results indicate that without glucose, both the bare and modified electrode exhibit no oxidation (Fig. 3A, curves a and b). Meanwhile, upon adding 5.0 mM glucose, there is the presence of an oxidation peak at around 1 V, with anodic peak currents (Ipa) of 266 µA for the former and 380 µA for Pe/CS/GO/GCE. These results are consistent with previous published studies, in which glucose oxidation peaks were observed in the potential range of 0.4–1.2 V, depending on the type of reference electrode used.16–18,34 The enhanced current response of the modified electrode can be attributed to the improved electrical conductivity and electron transfer capability provided by the perovskite material. As mentioned before, chitosan plays a role as a binder, facilitating the pre-concentration of analytes; meanwhile, graphene oxide with many functional groups such as carboxyl, carbonyl, and hydroxyl groups significantly enhances the CV by introducing electrochemically active sites, enabling fast Faraday reactions and providing a high specific surface area. Perovskite materials are also known for their intrinsic electrical conductivity, high surface area and ability to promote the electron transfer rates of electrochemical reactions.15 Therefore, the synergistic combination of GO and Pe is expected to form an excellent material for electrochemical reactions in general and for detecting glucose in particular.

To further elucidate this synergistic effect, CV measurements were performed using electrodes modified with individual components, as shown in Fig. 3B. Construction of a calibration curve and determination of the limit of detection (LOD)

The data for the construction of a glucose calibration curve based on various concentrations of glucose from 5 to 14 mM is shown in Fig. 3C. The results indicate that increasing the glucose concentration leads to a corresponding increase in the oxidation peak current intensity. This behaviour can be attributed to a greater number of glucose molecules being available for oxidation at higher concentrations, which enhances the rate of electrochemical reactions and consequently increases the measured current. The calibration curve obtained using the Pe/CS/GO/GCE electrode (Fig. 3D) exhibits a linear response within the investigated concentration range and can be described by the following linear regression equation (eqn (5)):5Ipa = 40.3697 × Cglucose(mM) + 152.3879 (R2 = 0.9955)

The average signal of blank samples containing only 0.1 N NaOH was measured to be 161.00 µA with a standard deviation of 2.26. According to the calibration curve presented in Fig. 3D, the limit of detection (LOD) of the Pe/CS/GO/GCE sensor is 0.381 mM (S/N > 3).

Detection of sugars

For practical applications, the detection of carbohydrates in real samples such as canned soft drinks, milk, and fruits requires sensing materials with high sensitivity and good selectivity. In these real samples, the total sugar content is usually indicated on the ingredients label. Therefore, other carbohydrates such as sucrose and lactose are additionally used in this experiment to investigate the detection abilities of the sensor. Solutions of 0.1 N NaOH containing various substances, i.e., 10 mM glucose, 10 mM sucrose, and 10 mM lactose, were sequentially examined. The results (Fig. 4A) showed that these kinds of sugar show oxidation signals that appear at very close anodic potentials (with Epa = 1 V, 1.065 V and 1.1 V, respectively) with slightly different sensitivity. To verify the aforementioned information, a study was conducted in a mixture of 3.33 mM glucose + 3.33 mM sucrose and 3.33 mM lactose in 0.1 N NaOH solution (Fig. 4B), with the results depicting that there is the presence of only one oxidation peak at potential of 1.0 V. This behavior can be explained by the direct electro-oxidation of the hydroxyl groups present in all three sugars. However, due to the larger molecular sizes of sucrose and lactose compared to glucose, steric hindrance effects inhibit their diffusion and adsorption onto the catalyst surface, resulting in shifted potentials and varying sensitivities. The reaction mechanism can be explained by the following assumptions. Firstly, sucrose and lactose are hydrolysed slowly to glucose and other isomers such as fructose and galactose (eqn (8) and (9)):8C12H22O11(sucrose) + H2O → glucose (C6H12O6) + fructose (C6H12O6)9C12H22O11(lactose) + H2O → glucose (C6H12O6) + galactose (C6H12O6)

Selectivity

Currently, the ingredient lists printed on the labels of soft drinks and beverages often include components other than total sugars, such as acidity regulators (citric acid (330), sodium citrate (331), ascorbic acid (300), etc.), vitamin B3 (niacinamide), caffeine, etc. During sample analysis, the co-existence of these components can potentially interfere with the electrochemical detection of sugars. Therefore, chemical substances commonly found in real-world samples, such as citric acid, niacinamide, and sodium citrate, will be used as interferents to investigate the selectivity of the Pe/CS/GO/GCE sensor. Initially, the experiment was conducted in 0.1 N NaOH solution containing 8 mM citric acid + 8 mM sodium citrate + 8 mM niacinamide, and the results showed no anodic peak (Fig. 4C).

Subsequently, the experiment was performed similarly to the above, with the addition of 8 mM glucose to the mixture. The results (Fig. 4C) revealed a significant anodic peak with a peak current (Ipa) of 476.23 µA, corresponding to 8.02 mM glucose. Thus, it can be concluded that the Pe/CS/GO/GCE-modified electrode exhibits good selectivity, enabling the sensitive detection of glucose as well as other types of sugars.

Repeatability

The obtained measurement results using the Pe/CS/GO/GCE electrode with a 5 : 1 : 5 ratio in 0.1 N NaOH solution at various time intervals were used to investigate the sensor's repeatability. The modified electrode was used for five cycles in 0.1 N NaOH solution. The results are displayed in Fig. 4D showing negligible deviation, with only about 4.8% error. Hence, it can be concluded that the methodology has high precision.

Application of the Pe/CS/GO/GCE electrode in real samples

(i) For 5% glucose intravenous solution

5% glucose intravenous solution is one of the World Health Organization's (WHO) essential medicines. In this solution, every 100 mL contains 5 g of glucose, equivalent to 50 g L−1 (Fig. SI.1A). For testing the application of the developed method, the 5% glucose intravenous solution was diluted 28-fold, giving a glucose concentration of around 10 mM. Fig. 5A shows CVs of the Pe/CS/GO/GCE electrode recorded in 0.1 N NaOH solution containing 28-fold-diluted 5% glucose intravenous solution (curve a) and 10 mM glucose (curve b). As shown in Fig. 5A, these two CVs have an anodic peak current Ipa = 561 µA at Ep = 1 V (vs. SCE), implying that the glucose concentration is 10 mM. This indicates that the modified electrode provides high reliability for glucose detection in real samples with negligible error.

(ii) Ice peach flavored drink

According to the information on the bottle label, the total carbohydrate (or sugar) content in 100 mL is 7.5 g (Fig. 5A, inset). In fact, these carbohydrates (sugars) can be glucose, sucrose, or other kinds of commercial carbohydrate-based sweeteners. Assuming that this sugar is glucose, a 0.24 mL aliquot of the ice peach drink is taken to obtain 10 mM glucose solution, and this amount of drink is added to 0.1 N NaOH solution and the CV is measured under the same experimental conditions. The obtained results show that the CV of this drink nearly overlaps with the 10 mM standard sample with no shift in the highest anodic peak at Ep = 1 V (vs. SCE) (Fig. 5B). The error is only 1.7%, which means the content on the label is reliable. Once more, the modified electrode reveals high accuracy when detecting the sugar content as declared by the manufacturer.

(iii) Raisin and banana samples

We conducted further experiments to test the sugar content in two samples: raisins and bananas. The samples were prepared by crushing and diluting, followed by sonication and centrifugation. Subsequently, 100 µL of the raisin sample and 200 µL of the banana sample were measured individually. Comparing with the glucose calibration curve of the modified electrode, the CV results obtained for the raisin sample gave a concentration of 12.1 mM (Fig. 5C), equivalent to approximately 61.105 g of sugar per 100 g of raisins, and for the banana sample it was 7.8 mM (Fig. 5D), equivalent to approximately 19.46 g of sugar per 100 g of bananas. Conclusions

In this study, a perovskite-based nanocomposite electrode was successfully fabricated for the non-enzymatic electrochemical determination of sugars. The developed Pe/CS/GO/GCE electrode exhibited enhanced electrochemical responses compared with a bare GCE, enabling the detection of glucose and other sugars. The improved performance is attributed to a synergistic interaction between perovskite, graphene oxide, and chitosan, which facilitates efficient electron transfer and stable analyte adsorption. The sensor demonstrated good analytical performance, with a low limit of detection of 0.381 mM, high repeatability, and excellent stability. Accurate glucose quantification was confirmed through close agreement with measurements from 5% intravenous glucose solution. Furthermore, the electrode was successfully applied to total sugar determination in real samples, including banana and raisin extracts, highlighting its practical applicability. Compared with enzymatic sensors, this non-enzymatic platform offers improved robustness and cost effectiveness, making it promising for food and beverage analysis.
