Structures of survival: Syndemic pathways to care among people with HIV involved with the criminal legal system
Authors: Felicia O Casanova, G’Vanni Puchi, Esmeralda Ramirez, Ahzin Bahraini, Zinzi D Bailey, Kathryn M Nowotny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Criminal legal system (CLS) involvement intersects syndemically with HIV, mental illness, substance use, and exposure to violence. Mass incarceration remains both a cause and consequence of racialized health inequities in the U.S. The post-release period is particularly vulnerable for people living with HIV (PLWH) who experience CLS involvement, underscoring the need for care models attuned to their structural and psychosocial contexts. This study examines how syndemic pathways and cumulative life experiences shape HIV care trajectories among people with HIV involved with the CLS.Methods:Guided by a life-course and syndemic framework, we conducted 21 adapted Life History Calendar (A–LHC) interviews in Miami, Florida, an epicenter of both incarceration and HIV. Participants co-constructed visual timelines and in-depth narratives were analyzed using rapid qualitative analysis to identify trajectories, transitions, and turning points in HIV care.Results:Findings reveal that early trauma, housing instability, substance use, and incarceration cluster and accumulate across the life course, contributing to disrupted HIV care. Participants navigated care within contexts of poverty, institutional mistrust, and unstable housing, yet many demonstrated resilience through faith, recovery, and social support. CLS involvement simultaneously disrupted and occasionally facilitated re-engagement in care.Conclusion:This study highlights developmental and structural pathways that shape HIV care continuity among people with HIV involved with the CLS. The A–LHC method offers a feasible tool for capturing temporal and syndemic complexity, informing trauma-informed, life-course–based, and system-integrated approaches to care.Keywords: HIV syndemics, Criminal legal system, Life course perspective, Life history calendar, Care engagement and reengagement

Background

Extensive research has established that mass incarceration is a powerful social-structural determinant of health with devastating public health consequences (Bailey et al., 2015;Brinkley-Rubinstein, 2013; Brinkley-Rubenstein and Cloud, 2020; LeMasters et al., 2022; Nowotny and Kuptsevych-Timmer, 2018). It harms the health and well-being of individuals (Garcia-Grossman et al., 2023; Applegate et al., 2024), destabilizes families (Lee et al., 2014;Sundaresh et al., 2021; Turney, 2017; Turney, 2021; Wildeman et al., 2019), and negatively impacts entire communities (Daza et al., 2020; Reinhart, 2021; Schnittker et al., 2015). For individuals, incarceration functions not only as an interruption in medical care but as a structural turning point that reorganizes health trajectories across the life course of people with HIV (PWH) (Iroh et al., 2015; Porter et al., 2023; Phillips et al., 2025).

People involved in the criminal legal system (CLS) therefore remain a high-priority population for ending the HIV epidemic in the U.S. The transition from incarceration to community reentry represents one of the most precarious periods for health, when overlapping forms of instability—homelessness, substance use, stigma, and trauma—cluster and interact (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024; Shavit et al., 2017; Mallik-Kane and Visher, 2008; Mohammad et al., 2023; Waldrop-Valverde and Valverde, 2005). These overlapping burdens exemplify what Singer and colleagues (2017) describe as a syndemic, a clustering of diseases and social forces that mutually reinforce one another through structural inequality. Yet, studies of HIV and incarceration often examine discrete episodes of confinement or release, rather than the developmental accumulation of disadvantage that precedes and follows them. Understanding HIV care trajectories among CLS-involved people requires attention to both structure and time—how institutional harms and social vulnerabilities accumulate, intersect, and transform across the life course.

A syndemic perspective highlights the interaction of social and biological processes in producing clustered epidemics (Glynn et al., 2019; Singer et al., 2017; Bulled et al., 2022; Quinn et al., 2018). A life-course perspective, in turn, emphasizes timing, transitions, and turning points that shape health trajectories over time (Elder, 1995; Halfon and Hochstein, 2002; Reisner et al., 2023). Integrating these frameworks enables examination of how syndemic conditions emerge and accumulate across developmental stages and how incarceration and reentry operate as pivotal transitions that can redirect or compound existing trajectories. This approach extends syndemic theory by embedding it in a temporal framework that reveals when and how different forms of instability converge or diverge and identifies sensitive periods where intervention may alter long-term outcomes. Tsai (2018) called for moving beyond additive “sum-score” models of syndemics toward frameworks that capture serial causation and structural interaction, while scholars such as Sangaramoorthy and Benton (2022) and Kline (2022) emphasize embedding syndemics within analyses of power to avoid pathologizing marginalized populations.

Extensive research has documented barriers to HIV care among incarcerated and formerly incarcerated populations (Ammon et al., 2018; Binswanger et al., 2011). Clinical and public health studies have described discontinuity of care, poor viral suppression, and elevated mortality following release (Rich et al., 2016; Costa et al., 2018; Porter et al., 2023). Qualitative studies have deepened this picture, illustrating how stigma, fractured social relationships, and fragmented reentry systems disrupt continuity (Kemnitz et al., 2017; Rozanova et al., 2015; Rowell-Cunsolo et al., 2020; Blue et al., 2022). Life-course studies among women and transgender people with HIV similarly show how early violence, social exclusion, and economic precarity shape adult health through “chains of risk” and cumulative trauma (Grammatikopoulou et al., 2021;Lacombe-Duncan and Olawale, 2020; Reisner et al., 2023; Tenkorang and Owusu, 2025). Together, these perspectives point toward a structural-syndemic and life-course synthesis that situates incarceration not as an isolated event but as one link in a longer sequence of structural vulnerability and resilience.

The current study uses an adapted life history calendar method to elicit detailed temporal narratives of health, trauma, and care among people with HIV in Miami, Florida. By situating participants’ experiences within both syndemic and life-course frameworks, we highlight how violence, homelessness, substance use, and CLS involvement accumulate from childhood through adulthood, shaping HIV care engagement and disengagement.

Methods

Anchored in a life-course perspective (Halfon and Hochstein, 2002), which emphasizes how early experiences and social contexts shape later health trajectories, the study conceptualized participants’ narratives in terms of trajectories, transitions, and turning points across the lifespan. We used an adapted life history calendar (LHC) approach (Aralis et al., 2018; Greenberg and Rosenheck, 2008; Harris & Parisi, 2008; Miller et al., 2014) to conduct life history interviews with people in Miami, Florida. The LHC is a well-established quantitative data collection method for constructing trajectories for domains of interest (Adriansen, 2012; Atkinson, 2002). Likewise, life history interviews are a well-established qualitative method for uncovering participants’ interpretations of their experiences over a long period (Harris & Parisi, 2008; Nelson, 2017). The novel integration of these approaches uniquely applies an adapted LHC, which serves as a visual tool to enhance data quality and validity and facilitate researcher-participant rapport. Data collection occurred from June 2021 to February 2022 and was approved by the institutional review board. Twenty-four people were interviewed; however, this analysis is based on 21 complete and fully transcribed interviews since audio recordings were damaged. Two audio files were corrupted, and one interview was partially completed. Information from these three interviews provided contextual data through field notes.

Setting

This study was conducted in Miami–Dade County, Florida—a jurisdiction with among the highest incarceration and HIV rates in the United States. Florida’s incarceration rate (833 per 100,000) exceeds the national average (698 per 100,000); although Black residents comprise 16% of the state’s population, they represent 46% of those incarcerated. The state ranks third nationally in new HIV diagnoses, and seven of the 48 priority jurisdictions identified under the federal Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) framework, still guiding place-based HIV prevention and care efforts, are located within Florida, including Miami–Dade County. Miami continues to report the nation’s highest rate of new HIV diagnoses, and despite significant progress, local reports highlight low viral suppression, treatment enrollment, and PrEP uptake. HIV prevalence among incarcerated persons in Florida prisons (2.8–3.0%) is over seven times that of the U.S. population (Maruschak, 2023; Spaulding et al., 2009), underscoring Miami’s importance for understanding how CLS involvement shapes HIV care trajectories. Although all participants were recruited in Miami–Dade County, several described periods of relocation within and outside Florida due to incarceration transfers, housing instability, or reentry transitions. These mobility patterns reflected both the consequences of life disruption and factors influencing continuity of HIV care.

Participant recruitment

The inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 18 or older, (2) English-speaking, (3) self-report diagnosis with HIV, (4) have experienced incarceration (prison/jail) and/or community supervision (probation/parole) in the past five years, and (5) able to provide informed consent. Given the conditions of the COVID-19 pandemic on human subjects activities, participants were recruited using multiple methods, including a consent-to-contact patient registry funded by the National Institute of Mental Health, outreach to community providers, and snowball sampling. The consent-to-contact patient registry maintained by the Center for HIV and Research in Mental Health (CHARM) is a survey database of people with HIV who have received care at an HIV clinic within a public safety net hospital. The criteria for being included in the database consisted of providing permission to be contacted for future studies. The survey questions included lifetime incarceration and past-year incarceration. For our sampling frame, we extracted individuals who reported experiencing incarceration. All participants met the inclusion criterion of CLS contact within the past five years; each had experienced at least one period of incarceration, and several also described concurrent or subsequent community supervision (e.g., probation or parole). Residential history and mobility were documented during screening and interviews to contextualize participants’ care and incarceration trajectories. Interviews (35–90 min) were conducted in-person (n = 7) or via video chat (n = 14) following COVID-19 safety protocols. Participants gave verbal consent using the University of MiamiHuman Subjects Research Office-approved study protocol and received $75 compensation.

Data collection

The adapted Life History Calendar (A-LHC) differs from traditional LHCs by combining a flexible visual timeline with in-depth, semi-structured interviewing rather than standardized event inventories. Interviews were conducted by trained qualitative researchers using a shared interview guide and calendar template. Interviewers received training in trauma-informed interviewing and life-course elicitation techniques prior to data collection. The calendar was used not only to support recall but also to facilitate temporal comparison across domains (e.g., housing, substance use, incarceration, and HIV care), which directly informed the analytic process by highlighting sequencing, clustering, and turning points across participants’ life courses.

The LHC method was pioneered for large-scale quantitative life-course studies. It was designed to collect retrospective longitudinal data on domains of interest (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2014). This method uses a pre-printed calendar matrix with time cues (e.g., months, years) organized horizontally and domain cues (e.g., education, drug use) organized vertically. The LHC is designed to uncover behavior patterns to reveal continuity and change over the life course (Harris and Parisi, 2007). The A-LHC was less structured: the interviewer started with a blank sheet of paper or whiteboard screen, and the interviewer and participant co-constructed the time cues and domain cues, with participants allowed to generate meaningful domains and concepts of time (see Fig. 1). The interviewer’s role was to outline the parameters of the interview and the domains of interest. However, participants directed the interview, including the order of topics and periods so that they could build towards emotionally laden topics when ready. The A-LHC was used as a visual tool to enhance data quality and validity and to facilitate researcher-participant rapport (Aralis et al., 2018).

Life history interviews focused on co-constructing timelines of life events leading to, during, and after HIV diagnosis that have overlapped and influenced the likelihood of HIV exposure, HIV testing, and HIV from the participanťs perspective. The current study examines the domains of HIV care, violence, homelessness, substance use, and CLS involvement. Fig. 1 illustrates the co-construction of life course stages and intersecting experiences before, during, and after HIV diagnosis. Guided by a life-course framework (Halfon and Hochstein, 2002), we used the A-LHC not only as a data collection tool but also as an analytic device to trace developmental and temporal sequences in participants’ lives. The A-LHC facilitated mapping participants’ experiences across distinct life stages (e.g., childhood, adolescence, emerging adulthood, and adulthood), allowing the identification of key turning points that shaped HIV care trajectories. This integration of temporal structure and participant-driven reflection provided insights into cumulative exposures, sensitive periods, and inflection points for engagement or disengagement in care that would not have emerged through standard semi-structured interviews alone. Accordingly, our analysis and presentation of findings are organized around these life-course stages to illustrate how developmental timing and turning points shape trajectories of HIV care engagement.

Data analysis

Transcripts were imported into a secure cloud-based document management program and were analyzed using Hamilton (2023) and Hamilton and Finley (2019) rapid qualitative analysis (RQA) method. Verification through consensus meetings at each stage ensured trust-worthiness and qualitative rigor (Guba and Lincoln, 1981; Harkness, et al., 2022). The RQA process involves four iterative stages: (1) interview-summary creation; (2) matrix construction; (3) theme identification; and (4) cross-case verification and saturation check (Hamilton, 2023; Vindrola-Padros and Johnson, 2020). First, we developed an “interview summary” template following guidelines established by Harkness and colleagues (2022). The interview summary served as a tool for team members to condense the interview data into qualitative trajectories across essential domains. This phase was non-interpretive, primarily focusing on capturing key statements and time-order of events. A structured process was followed to complete and review interview summaries for each participant. An analyst created the initial interview summary; a second team member audited it for accuracy and consensus. The lead author conducted a final quality control review.

Following this, we adhered to Hamilton and Finely guidelines by transferring key statements to a matrix summary (2019). This matrix summary allowed analysts to collectively review participant responses within each conceptual domain rather than individually (Hamilton and Finley, 2019). Team members independently identified emergent themes. They thoroughly reviewed all data within each domain (within the matrix summary), defined themes, and extracted illustrative quotations in three stages. The lead author then reviewed and assessed the consistency of the themes identified by the analysts. The calendars facilitated identification of timing, sequencing, and clustering across cases; for example, substance use frequently followed early trauma but preceded housing instability and incarceration, while re-engagement in HIV care often coincided with later-life transitions such as entry into recovery programs or access to stable housing. Any discrepancies were discussed as a team until a consensus was reached. Thematic coding continued until no new categories or relationships emerged, a point of thematic saturation (Guest et al., 2006). We achieved thematic saturation during our fourth analytic stage, confirming adequacy of sample, sufficient depth and diversity of perspectives for the study aims. Trajectories were identified through iterative cross-case comparison of individual life history calendars and summary matrices, with attention to shared patterns in the timing, sequencing, and transitions of HIV care engagement, substance use, housing instability, and CLS involvement. Analytic team meetings were used to compare interpretations and reach consensus on higher-level trajectory patterns. Our team collaboratively reviewed and discussed the final themes to confirm the accuracy of the findings, reported below.

The sample size (n = 21) was appropriate for the study’s focused qualitative objectives and the specificity of the inclusion criteria. All participants met criteria for recent CLS involvement and were living with HIV, a population that is difficult to access and often underrepresented in qualitative research. Recruitment was also affected by COVID-19 restrictions, which required the use of both virtual and in-person modalities. While the findings are not statistically generalizable, they offer transferable insights relevant to similar justice-impacted populations living with HIV.

Findings

Participant characteristics and overview

A total of 21 participants were included in the study (Table 1). The average age was 50.1 years (SD = 10.9; range = 29–66). Eleven participants (52%) identified as Black or African American, nine (43%) as White, and one (5%) as biracial. Six participants (29%) identified as Hispanic or Latino (including Puerto Rican, Mexican, Cuban, and Portuguese), while the remaining 15 (71%) identified as non-Hispanic. Twelve participants (57%) identified as male and nine (43%) as female. In terms of sexual orientation, 18 participants (86%) identified as heterosexual or straight, one (5%) as gay, one (5%) as bisexual, and one (5%) did not disclose.

Most participants completed high school (15, 71%), with an additional two (10%) reporting some college, one (5%) an associate’s degree, one (5%) a bachelor’s degree, and two (10%) having completed only middle school. All participants had CLS involvement within the past five years. Fourteen participants (67%) had been detained in county jail, three (14%) in state prison, and four (19%) in federal prison. Eight participants (38%) reported additional community supervision, including state (n = 5, 24%) or federal probation (n = 1, 5%).

Twelve participants (57%) initiated HIV treatment upon diagnosis, while nine (43%) delayed care. Only two participants (10%) had maintained continuous adherence since diagnosis, while nineteen (90%) reported at least one period of nonadherence.

Beyond these demographic characteristics, participants’ narratives revealed patterned trajectories of adversity and resilience across the life course. Fig. 2 synthesizes these temporal patterns, mapping the overlap of violence, homelessness, substance use, and CLS involvement clustered and intensified during key developmental periods, from childhood through late adulthood.

Early exposure to violence and family instability established enduring vulnerabilities, often accompanied by early mobility, including movement through foster care, running away, or relocation across regions in adolescence or early adulthood. In some cases, early displacement intersected with exploitative or controlling relationships, including coercion into sex work, further shaping exposure to risk. Substance use commonly emerged during adolescence or early adulthood as a coping response to trauma and intensified alongside housing instability. Homelessness and CLS involvement peaked in young and middle adulthood, frequently disrupting HIV care. For some participants, incarceration marked a temporary turning point, either reinforcing disengagement or creating brief windows for treatment and reflection. By late adulthood, renewed care engagement often followed access to stable housing, recovery programs, or faith-based networks.

Viewed across the life course, these patterns highlight that syndemic factors were developmentally sequenced and mutually reinforcing, shaping HIV care trajectories through cumulative structural and psychosocial stressors rather than discrete barrier or events. Findings are organized chronologically to trace participants’ life-course trajectories, from early exposures to violence and instability, through cycles of substance use and incarceration, to later turning points of recovery and re-engagement in HIV care. For example, the calendars revealed that substance use frequently followed early trauma but preceded incarceration, while re-engagement in HIV care often coincided with later-life transitions such as recovery program entry or access to stable housing.

Childhood and early trauma: foundations of vulnerability and social

Support Participants’ earliest experiences revealed how violence, neglect, and instability established the foundations for later health vulnerabilities. Many participants in the study dealt with deeply traumatic experiences throughout their life. These events compounded, often never giving them the opportunity to heal from their traumatic experiences. Participants grew up in foster care, abusive households, or households with caregivers with substance use disorders. These experiences often werenť mutually exclusive and impacted their lives deeply. Willow is a 45-year-old woman who was diagnosed at the age of 21 and who grew up in a home where neglect and abuse by her parents led her to surviving in the streets. She described her childhood experiences: “My mom thought she was disciplining us, but it went way beyond normal because we got beaten with water hoses, rebars, 2 × 4’s … my mom stopped feeding me. Whatever was in the house, if I ate it, once iťs done, iťs done.” These early traumas shaped their sense of self and survival strategies. As a result, participants faced increased vulnerability to HIV and significant barriers to consistent care. Rachel shared her story:

The first time I was molested, I was four or five. The babysitter’s husband touched me and I was too scared to tell my mother, because I thought it was my fault. Then it was one of my mother’s boyfriends who was brushing my hair and reaching around me … rubbing me on my chest. I told her [Mother]. She goes, “He didnť touch you like that, you’re confused.” I went to school with a black eye. I had these lines on my cheek from her rings. I had welts … black, bleeding welts all over my legs from her hitting me with the hanger. I just ran away … [My mother] was a heavy drinker. So, I just kept running away from home looking for happiness in all the wrong places … I got put in foster care. I kept running away. I would stay a little bit and as soon as I got really close to the people, I would run away. I felt like I didn’t deserve to be happy. I felt like it was a temporary home. Even though I was happy for that moment, it wasn’t real. So I just kept running away, until I met this guy, he was a pimp. I got with him and he had 15 women. I started working for him, with his girls. They took me down and showed me how to work the streets.

Themes of neglect and abandonment were common, including assuming significant responsibilities at a young age, caring for themselves or their siblings. Nelson described his experience:

My mom put me out when I was 12. I learned how to sell drugs. From 12 to 16, I learned a lot on my own. Like I had to learn fast. I was the only kid outside at one o’clock in the morning roaming the streets. I was scared too. Then I was getting taken advantage of by the crack heads and doing drugs. It made me feel like I was worthless for one thing. I never expected that out of my mom. I never thought in a million years she would do me like that.

Exposure to death from a young age was also common. Participants lost parents, uncles, grandparents, and siblings to illness, overdose, violence, or suicide. People who felt abandoned by their family often ended up losing custody of their children and noted it as a significant stressor in their lives. Attempts to rekindle relationships later in their life failed more than they succeeded. Rachel shared, “My kids got taken from me. They terminated my parental rights because I was still getting high. I donť know if I would’ve been a good parent to them. I dream about them. I still dream of them being little, because I havenť seen them.”

Across the adulthood stage, participants experienced many violent incidents ranging from physical and sexual assault from associates, law enforcement, romantic partners, and strangers. Participants recall violent encounters as common and frequent experiences. Cole said:

When I sold drugs, I met a lot of violence. I didn’t do it all the time, but I got in the shoot-offs before, been shot at. I’ve been robbed. When I was selling drugs it’s like, when I saw what was happening to those boys over there … somebody got shot. It made me more aggressive and trying to get more money to get out of the situation. I’m already selling it so I got to keep doing what I got to do. Made me go get another gun, stuff like that. They try me, I’m going to have to shoot them. But I had to make my money. Once you’re caught up in that lifestyle, you’re in it.

Ted, a 39-year-old man, experienced multiple episodes of depression after losing his father at the age of 13. He described his experiences with cocaine, which he attributed to his involvement with the CLS and increased sexual promiscuity leading to his HIV diagnosis. He said, “It gets real violent sometimes behind arguing about drugs and stuff like that. I’ve had individuals punch me in my face behind things I’ve said. I’m not a violent person, I’ve had violence perpetrated against me.” Periods of incarceration were also noted as violent times. According to Ted:

I’m telling you the county jail it’s horrible, they totally misrepresent how they accommodate us. When you walk through the door, they let you know that you’re not going to get your basic needs met. They like, ‘We donť want no shit out of nobody, we donť want to have to lock you down like the animals you are.’ And … I’m like an animal?

As seen in Fig. 2, more than half of participants were diagnosed with HIV by the time they reached early adulthood, which means that during this life stage they were dealing with a high degree of violence while attempting to understand their diagnosis and how to initiate or manage their care. Social support was absent for most of their lives. But, recent support provided from their care team, faith-based organization, or a close friend was instrumental to their HIV care trajectories. Jasmin, a 29-year-old woman, entered the foster care system at the age of 12 after experiencing physical and sexual abuse from family members. Throughout her life, she experienced repeated incarceration, primarily for drug-related charges and prostitution occurring while homelessness. She had five children and lost custody of them. Jasmin said:

I realized that I love myself and I didn’t want to die. I wanted to be able to be here for my kids, some form of a way. I didn’t want to just give up on them completely because I do love my children. They’d never seen me down and out. They’ve never seen me use drugs so that built my strength up. That helped me a lot. And like I said, I have a psychiatrist. I have a psych-doctor. I have a therapist. I have all the things I need. I take medication. I have an open case of disability going on right now. We’ll see what happens … I have people to talk to and I love myself today. I donť want to go down that road again … I got married. My husband supports me … I have a lot of people that are in recovery that I met through the meetings. I have a network … And I’m undetectable. I’ve come a long way.”

Jasmin shared that she initially ran away from her diagnosis but later credits starting and continuing her care to her time after being released from jail and completing a drug detox program. She has insurance coverage and consistently adheres to her HIV medication and therapy. Jasmine has been able to reconnect with some of her children, who now serve as a source of resilience and strength in her life.

Notably, religion served as a vital source of support for some participants, helping them navigate difficult life experiences and cope with their HIV diagnosis. For some, belonging to a church provided a renewed source of hope, transforming their outlook on living with HIV, inspiring changes in their daily lives, and enabling them to positively impact their communities. For example, Selena a 57-year-old woman grew up experiencing familial and intimate partner violence as well as periods of being unhoused. She found strength in her faith, using her life story as a testimony to mentor young girls. Her lived experience with HIV became an opportunity for education and awareness within her church, which was a source of pride.

Many participants experienced severe childhood trauma, including abuse, neglect, and early exposure to violence—that shaped their sense of self and led to heightened vulnerability to HIV and difficulties accessing care. Despite these hardships, some found strength through stable housing, recovery, and social networks, sustaining adherence or re-engaging with care and envisioning new paths. These instances of resilience and positive deviance highlight modifiable protective factors. As participants entered emerging adulthood, trauma compounded with economic precarity, driving cycles of substance use and early system entrenchment.

Emerging adulthood: substance use and system entrenchment

Many participants had a long history of substance use. It was often noted as the assumed source of HIV transmission: participants identified likely mechanisms associated with syringe use or heightened sexually “promiscuous” behavior while high. For example, Ted said, “I can only speculate that it [contracting HIV] was during the phase in my life when I was wrapped up in cocaine. I was really promiscuous and I was not using protection. 85% of the time I was not using protection.” Participanťs engagement in substance use varied across the life course, with many initiating use during adulthood. A minority of participants described being introduced to substances during childhood, as young as 9 years old.

HIV also led to substance use with some participants using after their diagnosis and others severely increasing their use. Adam said:

I started doing crack heavy after my diagnosis. I didnť smoke weed, I didnť do any of that. It could have been worse though, I could have been on heroin, or gotten killed or something, but it was just crack cocaine. Somebody introduced me to it and said, ‘This’ll help you come out of your state of mind.’ So I tried it and got hooked. I started doing things that I wasn’t supposed to do, staying away from my family and going fighting people for no reason. During that time, I was having unprotected sex with the virus in me.

Ted similarly described his experience: “I found myself spiraling really, really quickly. At the start of using cocaine, thaťs when my jail visits started getting real frequent.”

For many participants, drug use led to experiences of incarceration. Fred is a 55-year-old man who experienced frequent incarceration throughout his life, primarily for drug-related charges, with his longest sentence lasting seven years. Fred attributed his drug use to negative peer influences in his community while growing up. At age 39, while serving time in prison, he was diagnosed with HIV. Although he initially received treatment, he often discontinued care when he felt physically healthy: “I was incarcerated and I had got sick. I was in prison … I had went to a doctor and they told me. No, I had pneumonia. I had caught pneumonia, and I had a list of things I had. I started losing weight. I couldnť eat, coughing. So, I went to the doctor in prison again. They told me I had HIV.” Participants also described substance use as a “coping mechanism” to deal with traumatic life experiences, including their HIV diagnosis. Johan explained:

Getting high. My whole world right then, all I was doing all the time was centered around drugs, period. It wasn’t centered around nothing else but using crack cocaine. I wasn’t homeless or anything. Just crack overruled, it ran my life. Every day I woke up, I was more concerned about getting high, than I was about eating, or my hygiene, or anything else. I was more concerned about getting high. If AIDS had to be part of my life, then it didn’t matter.

Some people found support for their recovery during drug treatment or in the bonds formed while being homeless, as Henry states:

I do have a couple of people that I do call. One, my very good friend that I met out here when I was homeless. He looked out for me when I was on the street, clothing, food, necessities, whatever I needed. Also, my NA sponsor, which I talk to frequently, I go to him, I call him all the time.

Substance use was a common and deeply intertwined aspect of participants’ lives, often cited as both a likely cause of HIV transmission and a coping mechanism following diagnosis. Many began using drugs in adulthood, though some started in childhood, with substance use frequently escalating after learning of their HIV status. For several participants, drug use led to incarceration, disrupted care, and further isolation, while also serving as a way to numb the pain of trauma and stigma. By midlife, participants encountered key inflection points: periods of incarceration, recovery, and reconnection, that shaped HIV care trajectories.

Midlife turning points: homelessness, incarceration, and recovery

Housing instability emerged as a common theme woven through the life narratives of participants, often connected to challenges with HIV care, safety, CLS-involvement, and substance use, persistent in adulthood. Among the 21 individuals included in our study, 18 recounted experiences of being unhoused. Within this subgroup, the temporal relationship between being unhoused and HIV diagnosis varied: seven participants received their HIV diagnoses while experiencing homelessness, six were homeless before receiving their diagnosis, and five became homeless after their diagnosis. Participants identified the root causes of homelessness as escaping from abusive environments, escaping households entrenched with substance use, sudden loss of income during adulthood, familial neglect in their childhood stages, lack of viable housing options, and forcible eviction by family.

The precarious nature of homelessness posed significant barriers to accessing HIV care, a challenge articulated by participants describing agonizing decisions between prioritizing their health or surviving. Nelson has been homeless since the age of 12. The daily ordeal of striving to earn a living, compounded by the challenges associated with being unhoused, led to a 10-year hiatus from HIV care: “Just an everyday struggle, trying to make sure I could make a dollar. I was not even worried about my health. It was kind of rough, sleeping on the street and I just didnť want to deal with the medication. I didnť even go pick it up.” Participants described harrowing narratives of significant violence while homeless, often at the hands of people they knew or law enforcement agents. Sexual assault emerged as a frequent experience among female participants. Sheila recounted her experience of sexual assault while living on the streets and the subsequent mental toll: “Some guy raped me. I had bruises on my neck and everything. They [ED] checked me. I didnť have any infections or anything. I used to see the guy walking around all the time. It used to affect me. It was messed up. There wasnť too much I could do because I was homeless and skinny and people took advantage of you.” Adam described being overwhelmed with sadness, leading him to withdraw from his support network and ultimately sever ties with his spouse:

My life was wonderful and peaceful before my diagnosis. I was communicating with my family more because I got married at age 21. It [HIV diagnosis] took a toll on my life where I got depressed. I started feeling worthless, so I started living on the streets from it. I took my own self into homelessness, and I isolated myself from people a lot. People were concerned. I’d lay down, wake up, and find that I’d have tears coming from my eyes because it affected me that bad … When I was living on the railroad track, it was bad for me. That’s why I try not to befriend people, because they die so fast, when they’ve got HIV or AIDS. When people are telling me they have HIV, I’m considering it as AIDS. To me, it was AIDS. But it was just HIV. And then after I saw my friend and how he died. The rescue squad came, picked him up … I guess they gave him oxygen and whatever else that they did … The next morning, I woke up and he was dead. I had seen him deteriorate as he was dying. He was walking slowly, dragging, and wasn’t eating.

Similar to Shelia, Adam’s narrative demonstrates the interrelatedness of mental health, HIV, and homelessness.

Living conditions while homeless varied, ranging from sleeping on sidewalks and in bushes to makeshift shelters like cardboard boxes. Only a small number of participants reported occasional access to shelters, halfway houses, or community programs for accommodations. Peter is a 29-year-old man who was homeless at the time of the interview. Peter recounted the challenges, including stigma and mistreatment, faced by unhoused individuals, as well as the perceived necessity of substance use for survival:

It’s still like not fucking easy. Especially since people treat you like you’re kind of crazy sometimes. Finding sleeping places is pretty annoying. Other than that the streets are whatever. I don’t feel like you can be homeless and not have a drug problem … I wouldn’t be able to deal with being on the street if I wasn’t fucking high all the time. That shit was fucked up.

While many participants expressed a perceived necessity for substance use while unhoused, Cole stood out among the narratives, for his decision to cease his substance use:

I used to pray at night. I used to be on the streets. I was homeless when I quit [using drugs]. I was sleeping out there. I was going to a labor pool. I ended up selling [drugs] again, instead of smoking it because I ended up like a lot of people that was going to labor pools. You got people that’s functional, that can go to work. They use. Then you’ve got some that’s not going to work, ain’t going to bathe … They’re not functional crack heads, they’re just people that try to keep smoking.

The findings from Cole’s interview resonated with the harsh realities encountered by many of the interviewees who were unhoused. Multiple participants shared experiences of feeling compelled to engage in prostitution as a means of survival, often relying on substance use to cope. Experiences of violence and the threat of death were pervasive themes.

Despite the widespread distrust and lack of support experienced while unhoused, some individuals were able to form meaningful social bonds with others in their community. Mark, for example, felt that although the unhoused community he belonged to was tough, it was also a family: “I had actually accumulated a homeless family … I was accepted into this little community and was part of the family, but … it came at a heavy toll, but it was just something I felt like I had to do to survive.” Jasmin recounted her experience of meeting a companion on the street that led to a lasting marriage: “Most of the time I was by myself. Then I met a guy, Gino, so we started to hangout a lot together. He’d be in and out of jail and I was in and out of jail. But we stayed together. We ended up getting married. We’re still together now.”

Housing instability was a dominant theme, with most participants experiencing homelessness at some point in their lives, often linked to trauma, substance use, and barriers to HIV care. The harsh realities of being unhoused—including violence, sexual assault, and mental health struggles, frequently disrupted treatment and deepened isolation. Yet, some found strength through relationships formed on the streets, moments of recovery, and access to support networks that helped them begin rebuilding their lives. Across these cumulative exposures, participants’ engagement with HIV care reflected both disruption and resilience.

Trajectories of HIV care: engagement, disruption, and reconnection

Across the ife course, sequencing from the A-LHC revealed continuity and rupture in care trajectories, distinguishing cumulative versus acute barriers. Diagnosis occurred through community, correctional, and healthcare settings, including routine or unrelated medical encounters. Participants described varied experiences linking to HIV treatment after diagnosis, with some reporting immediate care engagement and others experiencing long delays. Participants who were diagnosed after receiving HIV testing through syringe services programs (SSP) and public safety net hospitals were likely to be immediately transitioned into care. Peter, a 29-year-old man who was diagnosed with HIV at the age of 27, describes his care experience and attributes his initiation into and continuity of care to a local SSP: “I took my medication ever since [my diagnosis with HIV], so I never had a period of not being [virally] suppressed or anything like that … But I feel like thaťs only because of the fact that the [SSP] helped me get into care easily.”

For several participants, repeated HIV testing while incarcerated reflected ongoing health surveillance prior to diagnosis rather than disengagement after care initiation. This distinction underscores how correctional settings often served as intermittent points of testing but not sustained treatment, blurring the line between prevention and continuity of care. However, not all participants had immediate support. While almost half of the participants received care immediately after diagnosis, many described long gaps to initiating HIV care, ranging from 2 to 10 years. Some people described barriers to accessing HIV care due to difficulties navigating the complex health system while homeless. Rachel, a 56-year-old woman who was diagnosed with HIV when she was 49, shares her experience while homeless:

Every time I went to jail, twice a year sometimes because I would always go to jail for drugs or prostitution, I would always get tested [for HIV] and it was always negative. In the summer of 2014, I kept telling my friend who’s [HIV] positive, I said, ‘Something is wrong with me. I’m not feeling right.’ She goes, ‘Girl, go to the hospital.’ I did … I didn’t seek care right away because I didnť know how.

Daniel, a 49-year-old man who was diagnosed with HIV when he was 44, shared his experience attempting to get care:

“Mentally, physically. Any money I ever got, I would spend it on drugs. I wasnť receiving any medicine at the time. Nobody to help me. I was homeless on the streets, sleeping on the sidewalk, sleeping in the bushes … I went to [local] Hospital. Didnť really help me at all. I found a place called [syringe program] and got some medicine there … It seemed like it took forever … at least two years … but I was able to get the medicine through them.”

Coverage of care also acted as a barrier for receiving and maintaining HIV care. Under Medicaid, participants reported difficulties maintaining care due to physicians being out of network. Consequently, when their insurance changed, their primary physician also did. Participants with private insurance were often out of care for long periods of time due to the loss of insurance at varied periods of time. Adam, who was diagnosed at age 40 and was 68 years old when interviewed discussed his challenges with changing insurance:

“I had gotten my meds for free all this time but when I transferred myself to [a public safety net hospital], all of a sudden, I’m paying these copays. I’m working with Medicaid and Medicare trying to find out how come I pay a copay for HIV meds. One time, it was 15 dollars, another time 25 dollars. They started me out free and all of the sudden I’m paying for it? So I’m paying to stay alive, that can take a toll on you.”

Stigma played a large role in a participanťs ability to engage in care and acknowledge their status. Many participants did not feel comfortable telling anyone about their HIV status, especially family and friends. Often, the stigma surrounding HIV completely hindered individuals’ ability to build new interpersonal connections. Some participants described negative coping behaviors, like substance use and social isolation, upon learning of their diagnosis. A 52-year-old participant named Mark, who was diagnosed at the age of 22, talked about his shock and how he coped after being diagnosed:

“It was a real bad stigma back then and I didnť want that stigma. So I never really told anybody about it. It shocked me. All I can remember is just going to get some money and I went on a binge. I was introduced to crack cocaine, and I went on a binge. I was drinking. I had stopped eating. I had stopped communicating and hanging out with friends, people that I normally hung out with.”

Misinformation also caused many participants to deny their status. Kendrick is a 62-year-old man who couldnť recall the exact age of his diagnosis that occurred during young adulthood, between ages 24 to 27. He described being diagnosed with HIV:

“When it first came out [HIV]. That’s when I believed that since I’m young and I was strong that I wasn’t going to get that stuff. I didn’t even pay it any mind. So, I kept getting all kinds of venereal diseases from unprotected sex and stuff. I was in denial for 3 or 4 years … I went to [a local nonprofit], this is an HIV place. They fed you, they had counseling, so I said, ‘oh.’ So, then I started learning about [HIV]. They was talking about, ‘You donť have to die from this. You can live with this.’ When I heard that I felt that I could live again because I’m getting information from the street, and they were saying, ‘You know so-and-so? He was taking that [HIV medication] and man he died from that medication.’ So you mean to tell me you can live with HIV? You donť have to die from HIV? There were people there who had HIV that were talking about life more abundantly.”

All participants were worried about being perceived negatively in their communities. However, HIV education helped participants change their outlook on life. Some individuals began to view life as precious and short. For others, HIV was the catalyst to change their life around, which most participants described as getting away from “street life” or a particular “lifestyle.” Cole, a 54-year-old man, who was diagnosed at 30 expressed his outlook on his diagnosis: “HIV made me more aware of things, a lot of things. Life’s short, you know? And I know that other people got worse situations than me. HIV isnť the worst disease in this world no more.”

CLS-involvement was a prominent theme in participants’ lives. Participants were less than five years removed from their last CLS experience at the time they were interviewed. Most participants had their first experience with the CLS during adolescence or early adulthood. On average, participants were arrested ten times, ranging from two to approximately sixty times, many losing count of the number of arrests. The most common CLS experience was jail detainment. Incarceration episodes ranged from being in county jail overnight to more than fifteen years in prison. Narratives illustrate a cyclical relationship of instability, drug use, and incarceration. Criminalized behaviors were discussed in relation to drug use, which included violence, drug selling, theft, sex work, and engagement in “street activity.” Ted expressed the connection between the crimes he committed to his drug use: “Definitely, like committing crimes that I normally probably wouldnť have committed if I wasnť like on it [high] or whatever like that, or was in need of it [experiencing withdrawal], have me stealing stuff from my family.” Like Ted, several participants discussed how drug use made it easier to engage in behaviors that they wouldnť have done sober.

While incarcerated, participants had varied experience with receiving HIV care. Some participants reported they always received treatment while incarcerated, while others reported never receiving treatment while incarcerated. One 52-year-old participant became homeless at age 19 and engaged in selling sex to men to “survive.” He was diagnosed with HIV at age 22 and “went on a bender for months, doing crack cocaine, drinking, not eating. Not taking care of myself. I didnť tell nobody.” He reported that the only time he was on medication was when he was “forced into treatment” in prison, never receiving medication while in jail. It was not until his early 40s that he decided he needed to “start taking care of himself” and went to the doctor for HIV treatment.

On the other hand, another participant was diagnosed with HIV in 2001 and said “I was kind of like psyching myself out like I really donť have this. I feel great.” She didnť start treatment until she was hospitalized for pneumonia in 2010. She has been to different county jails about 20 times, all for minor crimes (e.g., driving without a license, prostitution, trespassing). She said, “They are cruel to people in jail. Once we go in there, we have no rights. You donť get treated fairly.” The last time she was in jail was 2018 for burglary of an unoccupied building when her and her husband were caught sleeping in an abandoned building. She was locked up for 9 months and says she never received medication even though she was tested at the jail. In fact, she says that she has never received HIV meds while in jail: “They said there’s nothing we could do because they donť have my medicine. They say iťs too expensive, and all the medicines they give in county are donated like if you have old medication you could take it to a donation, and they thaťs what they give you. And they didnť have mines period but it would have been old medicine anyway.” Her perception of medications in jail as “old” and “donated” demonstrates her deep mistrust of jail-based medical services. Participants noted other barriers to receiving HIV medication while incarcerated. According to Gia:

“It wasn’t up to them to give you the medication, but they’d take their time coming to get you to go down there to have the request to see the nurse. Then you’re sitting there in the cell and you need to see the nurse because you want to get your medication. So you’re going days without it until you’re able to get there.

These institutions often instilled a sense of hopelessness, depression, and fear. Ted shared his experience:

It just feels like you are in a place of hopelessness. You really have no sense of when you’re going to be able to go home or whatever like that because your faith and your destiny is in somebody’s hands that’s going to decide whether you get to go home or not. It’s very nerve-racking, I think it really took a toll on my diagnosis when I was in there because I had lost about 35 pounds when I was in jail.

Incarceration experiences were interconnected with the disadvantages, traumas, and complex barriers that our participants faced from childhood to adulthood and impacted them across the HIV care continuum.

Discussion

Participants’ experiences with HIV care were shaped by deeply interwoven challenges, including trauma, housing instability, substance use, and involvement with the CLS. While some were linked to care immediately through harm reduction programs or hospitals, many faced long delays due to stigma, misinformation, and lack of support, barriers that were exacerbated by homelessness and incarceration. Substance use was perceived as both a cause and consequence of HIV, often tied to trauma and survival, while frequent CLS involvement disrupted care and deepened mistrust in medical systems. Despite these overlapping hardships, some individuals found strength through recovery, social bonds, and support networks, allowing them to re-engage with care and envision new paths forward. At the same time, several participants demonstrated resilience, re-engaging or sustaining HIV care through faith, recovery, and social support networks, highlighting how adaptation and persistence can emerge even within cumulative syndemic adversity.

A life-course lens reveals how early adversity sets trajectories of risk, while later turning points, incarceration, treatment, or stable housing, offer opportunities for re-engagement and resilience. This developmental framing moves beyond describing discrete barriers to illustrate how early trauma and CLS involvement accumulate and transform across the life course, shaping both vulnerability and capacity for recovery. Mobility, often resulting from incarceration transfers, eviction, or reentry transitions, further compounded instability and interrupted continuity of HIV care.

These findings complement recent studies documenting how incarceration, stigma, and housing instability disrupt HIV treatment and continuity of care (Blue et al., 2022; Porter et al., 2023; Mohammad et al., 2023). By situating these dynamics within a life-course framework, our study extends this work to show how cumulative disadvantage and structural environments shape re-engagement in care over time. Our study underscores the critical importance of addressing syndemic pathways in HIV care interventions for CLS-impacted populations. The intertwining of substance use, mental illness, and exposure to violence not only exacerbates HIV transmission risks but also impedes engagement with care and adherence to antiretroviral therapy (Springer et al., 2004;Stephenson et al., 2005). Moreover, CLS involvement further compounds these challenges, creating barriers to accessing healthcare, stable housing, and social support networks. These syndemic factors did not merely co-occur; substance use intensified housing instability, which increased exposure to CLS involvement and further disrupted care, producing compounded effects across time. These syndemic pathways also reflect the enduring impacts of structural racism, as participants’ life trajectories unfolded within systems that disproportionately criminalize and marginalize Black and Latinx communities. Participants’ life-course trajectories unfolded within racialized systems of policing, housing exclusion, and healthcare access in Miami, where Black and Latinx communities experience disproportionate surveillance, incarceration, and barriers to stable HIV care.

Although participants’ CLS experiences differed in length and intensity, all had been incarcerated within the past five years, and several had also experienced community supervision. This overlap limited our ability to compare trajectories or experiences by correctional type. However, future research with larger samples could explore how differences in incarceration type and supervision status shape HIV care trajectories and engagement.

Future directions for addressing individual-level syndemics among CLS-impacted individuals with HIV in South Florida must prioritize comprehensive, multidisciplinary approaches. Integrating mental health services within HIV care settings can help mitigate the adverse effects of syndemic factors on treatment adherence and health outcomes (Chakrabarti et al., 2023). Culturally sensitive and trauma-informed care models are essential for addressing the needs of CLS-involved populations and fostering engagement.

Interventions targeting syndemic pathways should adopt a life course perspective, addressing sensitive periods and social determinants of health. Investing in community-based resources, including housing assistance, substance use treatment programs, and violence prevention services, is crucial for promoting holistic health and well-being among justice-impacted individuals living with HIV.

The findings from this study advance areas of understanding in public health, sociology, criminology, and qualitative methods. By contextualizing the contributing factors to health behaviors and deeply exploring the increased likelihood of exposure throughout the life course, our study contributes to justice and deviant behavior research. From a sociological and public health perspective, this study provides an interdisciplinary framework to understand how healthcare disparities intersect with elements of the social world. The study advances our understanding of cumulative and intersectional disadvantages and how care trajectories are shaped by individual and structural factors at various stages of life rather than specific moments. Overall, the methods of this paper progress our understanding of how qualitative oral life history methodology can contribute to a deeper understanding of narrative inquiry as well as complex topics like HIV with fluctuating trajectories. The methodology also provides insights into its capacity to be adapted to virtual modalities in times, like during COVID-19 social distancing, when in-person research is not attainable.

In conclusion, our study underscores the urgent need for tailored interventions addressing individual-level syndemics and enhancing mental health services within HIV care settings for CLS-impacted populations in South Florida. By highlighting both sustained and disrupted care trajectories, we show how resilience and structural constraint co-exist within participants’ life courses. Addressing the interplay of syndemic factors and structural barriers can improve health outcomes, reduce inequities, and advance well-being among people affected by HIV and CLS involvement.
