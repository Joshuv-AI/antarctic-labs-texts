Population aging and changes in mortality from four major noncommunicable diseases in Suzhou, China: a population-based study from 2000 to 2020
Authors: Chunyan Huang, Menglin Bai, Yujie Hua, Huanhuan Zhao, Junpeng Cui, Zibo Gao, Lamei Shen, Qing Zhang, Hao Wu, Haitao Wang, Yan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulation aging is accelerating worldwide and poses long-term health challenges for four major chronic noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), including cancer, cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic respiratory disease (CRD), and diabetes.AimsTo analyze trends in mortality of these four NCDs in Suzhou, and examine the impact of population aging on mortality rates.MethodsData on the household-registered population of Suzhou and deaths from major NCDs from 2000 to 2020 were collected. The age structure of the population, crude mortality rate (CMR), age-standardized mortality rate by the Segi’s world standard population (ASMRW), years of life lost (YLL), and premature mortality of the four NCDs were calculated. Joinpoint regression was used to analyze temporal trends. A difference decomposition method was employed to estimate the contributions of population aging and non-demographic factors to changes in mortality rates.ResultsFrom 2000 to 2020, the degree of population aging in Suzhou continued to increase. The CMR for cancer, CVD, and diabetes showed an upward trend, the CMR for CRD declined significantly. The ASMRW and mortality burden for cancer, CVD, and CRD showed a downward trend, however, the mortality burden of diabetes continued to rise. Population aging is a key factor driving the increase in deaths from cancer and CVD, while the decline in deaths from CRD is more influenced by non-demographic factors; the rise in diabetes mortality is driven by the combined effects of population aging and non-demographic factors.ConclusionPopulation aging in Suzhou intensified significantly, contributing substantially to mortality from major NCDs. Targeted strategies for healthy aging and chronic disease prevention are urgently needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28499-3.

Introduction

With the upward shift in the global population’s age structure, the issue of population aging has emerged as a worldwide challenge. The World Health Organization (WHO) projects that the global population aged 60 and above is expected to rise from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion in 2030, reaching 2.1 billion by 2050 [1].

At the same time, the focus of both the disease spectrum and the cause-of-death spectrum among residents is gradually shifting toward chronic noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) [2]. The four major NCDs, including cancer, cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic respiratory diseases (CRD), and diabetes, have become the primary sources of the global disease burden and mortality burden, posing a serious threat to global health and socioeconomic development. The WHO reports that in 2021, at least 35 million people worldwide died from these four NCDs, accounting for 75% of all noncommunicable deaths [3]. In China, 82.5% of all deaths among the population are attributed to the major NCDs [4].

The burden of NCDs is closely linked to population age structure [5]. Against the backdrop of China’s deepening population aging, the elderly population continues to expand, and the risk of age-related diseases continues to accumulate. As a result, the number of deaths from major NCDs, as well as mortality rates, continues to rise [6, 7]. Therefore, changes in population age structure are not only a demographic issue but also a key factor influencing the evolution of the disease burden and public health governance. A systematic analysis of trends in mortality associated with the four major NCDs in the context of population aging is of great significance for accurately understanding the patterns of health transition among residents and evaluating the effectiveness of chronic disease prevention and control [8].

Suzhou, a highly urbanized and economically developed city in the Yangtze River Delta, has experienced rapid socioeconomic transformation since the early 2000s, driven by industrial upgrading and foreign direct investment, reshaping its demographics, living environment, and healthcare system. Its advanced population aging and long-term, high-quality mortality registry data make it a suitable sentinel site for studying the impact of aging on the NCDs mortality burden.

Based on this, this study aims to analyze trends in mortality rates and the burden of mortality from the four major NCDs among residents of Suzhou from 2000 to 2020, and further explore the impact of population aging on mortality rates, thereby providing a basis for chronic disease prevention and control, the implementation of healthy aging strategies, and the reduction of mortality risks associated with major NCDs.

Methods

Data sources

Death data in this study were sourced from the death registration system of the Suzhou Center of Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), while the household-registered population data were from the Suzhou Public Security Bureau. Deaths due to cancer, CVD, CRD, and diabetes were retrieved from 2000 to 2020. The corresponding codes in the 10th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), were C00-C97, I00-I99, J30-J98, and E10-E14, respectively.

Quality control

Death certificates were completed by trained and certified physicians and were entered into a death registration information system. The coding of causes of death strictly adhered to the ICD-10 classification system. All records underwent multi-level quality control, including item-by-item review by dedicated personnel at the district/county level, followed by regular cleaning and collation conducted by municipal and provincial CDC. To ensure completeness of reporting, mortality data were regularly linked and cross-validated with multiple administrative sources, including public security, civil affairs, and maternal and child health registration systems. Furthermore, a population-wide underreporting survey of deaths among residents has been carried out at least once every three years, and the underreporting rates were consistently below 5%.

Statistical analysis

Based on Suzhou’s population data, we described changes in the age structure of the population in terms of both the size of the elderly population and the degree of aging. Indicators of the age structure include the number of people aged 60 and above, aged 65 and above, and their proportion of the overall population, respectively, and the proportion of people aged 80 and above within the population aged 60 and above.

The analysis of mortality data primarily focuses on mortality rates and the burden of mortality for four NCDs. For each NCDs, we calculated the crude mortality rate (CMR) and age-standardized mortality rate by the Segi’s world standard (ASMRW), per 100 000 persons, stratified by sex. The Poisson approximation and the Mantel-Haenszel method were used to compare CMR and ASMRW between the sexes. Years of life lost (YLL) and rate of Years of life lost (YLLr) were calculated using the WHO template [9]. In accordance with the WHO definition, premature mortality was calculated using the life table method, specifically the probability of death from any of the four major NCDs among individuals aged 30–70 years [10].

The Joinpoint Regression Program V.5.4.0.0 (downloaded from National Cancer Institute, https://www.cancer.gov) was used to examine the temporal trends of the population age structure, CMR, ASMRW, YLLr, and premature mortality. Changes in these indicators were statistically analyzed using annual percentage change (APC), average annual percentage change (AAPC), and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

We used difference decomposition to quantify the contributions of population aging and non-demographic factors to changes in mortality rates between 2000 and 2020 [11]. The population aging reflects the part of mortality change attributable to changes in population age structure, whereas the non-demographic factors represent residual changes in age-specific mortality rates after excluding the effect of population aging. In this study, CDR1 and CDR2 denote the CMR observed in 2000 and 2020, respectively, and diff refers to the absolute change in CMR between the two time points. For each age group i, Ci represents the population proportion and Mi represents the age-specific mortality rate.

Results

Aging population

From 2000 to 2020, the age structure of the population showed a significant aging trend. The proportion of residents aged 60 and above increased from 16.24% in 2000 to 25.51% in 2020, with an AAPC of 2.46% (95% CI: 1.85% to 3.07%, p < 0.05). Among males and females, the corresponding proportions rose from 15.15% to 24.94% and from 17.31% to 26.04%, respectively (male AAPC = 2.66%, 95% CI: 2.03% to 3.29%; female AAPC = 2.14%, 95% CI: 1.51% to 2.78%, all p < 0.05). A more pronounced increase was observed among residents aged 65 and above, whose proportion increased from 11.60% to 19.17% overall (AAPC = 2.68%, 95%CI: 2.21% to 3.15%, p < 0.05), with consistent increases in both sexes (all p < 0.05). The proportion of Suzhou residents aged 80 and above among those aged 60 and above also increased during the study period, reaching 15.72% in 2020 compared with 11.34% in 2000 (AAPC = 1.70%, 95% CI: 0.89% to 2.52%, p < 0.05) (Table 1 and Online Supplementary Table S1).

Deaths due to the four major NCDs

The number of cancer deaths increased from 10 524 in 2000 to 14 754 in 2020, with the CMR increasing from 182.24/105 to 201.15/105, while the ASMRW decreased to 80.47/105. Concurrently, the YLL increased from 300 626.58 years in 2000 to 320 789.57 years in 2020, with the corresponding YLLr decreasing from 5 205.95/105 to 4 373.60/105, and the premature mortality also falling from 9.16% to 5.03%.

CVD accounted for 11 609 deaths in 2000, increasing to 17 662 in 2020; the CMR rose from 201.03/105 to 240.80/105, while the ASMRW decreased to 74.89/105; YLL rose from 227 572.59 years to 243 596.95 years, the YLLr decreased from 3 940.87/105 to 3 321.16/105, and the premature mortality fell from 4.76% to 1.87%.

The number of deaths due to CRD decreased from 6 437 in 2000 to 3 399 in 2020; CMR and ASMRW decreased from 111.47/105 to 46.34/105, and from 63.80/105 to 13.57/105; YLL decreased from 109 042.48 years to 41 892.67 years, the YLLr fell from 1 888.29/105 to 571.16/105, and the premature mortality dropped from 1.86% to 0.20%.

Diabetes accounted for 587 deaths in 2000, increasing to 1 639 in 2020. Both CMR and ASMRW remained at low levels, while YLL rose from 14 189.35 years to 27 843.47 years, with the corresponding YLLr increasing to 379.61/105, and the premature mortality slightly decreasing to 0.30%.

In general, among deaths from the four NCDs in 2000 and 2020, CVD accounted for the highest number of deaths, followed by cancer, CRD and diabetes. While the mortality burden of diabetes increased, the age-standardized mortality rates and mortality burdens of cancer, CVD, and CRD declined to varying degrees (Table 2).

Trends in mortality rates from four major NCDs

Between 2000 and 2020, the CMR for cancer among male residents of Suzhou showed an upward trend, with an AAPC of 0.55% (95% CI: 0.19% to 0.91%, p < 0.05); the AAPC for females was 0.27% (95% CI: -0.16% to 0.69%, p = 0.22). In contrast, the ASMRW for males decreased significantly, with an AAPC of -1.90% (95% CI: -2.87% to -0.92%, p < 0.05); for females, the AAPC was − 2.12% (95% CI: -2.34% to -1.89%, p < 0.05). Regarding CVD, CMR increased significantly in both males and females (male AAPC = 0.59%, female AAPC = 0.47%; all p < 0.05), however, the ASMRW decreased significantly (male AAPC=-2.31%, female AAPC=-2.66%; all p < 0.05). For CRD, both CMR and ASMRW decreased significantly among males and females in Suzhou, with the ASMRW for females decreasing particularly rapidly (AAPC=-8.67%, 95% CI: -9.49% to -7.85%, p < 0.05). Among deaths in diabetes, both CMR and ASMRW increased in both males and females, and it was the only disease in the four NCDs which ASMRW also showed an upward trend. However, the increase in ASMRW was statistically significant only among males (AAPC = 2.56%, 95% CI: 0.32% to 4.85%, p < 0.05); no significant changes were observed among females (AAPC = 0.17%, 95% CI: -1.67% to 2.05%, p = 0.86) (Fig. 1, and Online Supplementary Table S2, 3).

The CMRs for cancer and CRD were higher in males, nearly double that of females, while the CMRs for CVD and diabetes were higher in females (all p < 0.05). After age standardization, males had higher ASMRWs for cancer, CVD and CRD (all p < 0.05), whereas no significant gender differences were observed for diabetes (p > 0.05).

Trends in mortality burden from four major NCDs

During the study period, the YLLr declined significantly for cancer, CVD and CRD. The AAPCs were − 0.94% for cancer (95% CI: -1.17% to -0.71%, p < 0.05), -1.23% for CVD (95% CI: -1.58% to -0.89%, p < 0.05), and − 5.75% for CRD (95% CI: -7.31% to -4.16%, p < 0.05). However, the YLLr for diabetes showed a significant increasing trend (AAPC = 2.22%, 95% CI: 1.17% to 3.28%, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A and Online Supplementary Table S4).

A similar pattern was observed for premature mortality. Cancer (AAPC=-3.10%, 95% CI: -3.32% to -2.89%, p < 0.05), CVD (AAPC=-4.70%, 95% CI: -5.58% to -3.81%, p < 0.05), and CRD demonstrated significant downward trends in premature mortality rates (AAPC=-10.26%, 95% CI: -11.17% to -9.34%, p < 0.05). However, the decline in premature mortality for diabetes was not statistically significant (AAPC=-1.00%, 95% CI: -2.69% to 0.72%, p = 0.25) (Fig. 2B and Online Supplementary Table S5).

Quantitative impact of population aging and non-demographic factors on mortality rates

The rise in mortality rates from cancer and CVD was primarily attributed to population aging, with contribution rates of 402.58% and 333.20%, respectively. Results by sex showed that population aging has exerted a more pronounced impact on the rise in cancer mortality among females, with a contribution rate of 962.56%; for CVD, population aging also contributed significantly to the rise in female mortality rates, with a contribution rate of 338.15%. The decline in mortality rates from CRD was primarily attributed to non-demographic factors, with a contribution rate of 188.17%, while demographic factors partially offset the downward trend caused by non-demographic factors, and this offsetting effect was more pronounced among males. The rise in diabetes mortality was driven by both population aging and non-demographic factors, with population aging playing a larger role, accounting for 61.82% of the increase, and differences between genders were minimal (Table 3).

Discussion

Against the backdrop of rapid population aging, this study comprehensively analyzed trends in mortality rates and the burden of mortality for four major NCDs in Suzhou from 2000 to 2020, and further examined the contribution of population aging to mortality rates. The study yielded three main findings: First, the process of population aging in Suzhou accelerated significantly during the study period. Second, there has been some improvement in the four major NCDs over the past 20 years, though with variations among different diseases: cancer, CVD and CRD all showed some improvement, with CRD showing the greatest improvement, while no significant improvement was observed for diabetes. Third, the difference decomposition indicated that population aging had varying degrees of positive effects on these four major NCDs mortality, with the most significant impact observed on cancer and CVD.

Population aging refers to the process by which the age structure of a population shifts toward older age groups, typically manifested by an increase in the number of elderly people and a continued rise in the proportion of the elderly population [12, 13]. Since 2000, when the proportion of the population aged 65 and above reached 7% and China officially entered an aging society, the degree of population aging in China has continued to deepen [14]. Results from the Seventh National Population Census in 2020 showed that in 2020, 18.73% of China’s population was aged 60 and above, 13.52% was aged 65 and above, and those aged 80 and above accounted for 13.56% of the population aged 60 and above [15]. Between 2000 and 2020, the proportion of the population aged 60 and above in Suzhou increased at an AAPC of 2.46% to reach 25.51%, while the proportion of the population aged 65 and above increased at an AAPC of 2.68% to reach 19.17%. At the same time, the elderly population itself is aging; the proportion of people aged 80 and above within the population aged 60 and above increased by 15.72% at an AAPC of 1.70%. This indicates that the aging level of Suzhou’s population has reached the threshold of an aging society and is steadily intensifying over time.

NCDs are the leading cause of death and disability worldwide, and mortality from NCDs has risen in tandem with increasing life expectancy [8]. During the study period, we found that cancer and CVD were the two leading causes of death from NCDs in Suzhou, ranking in the top two among Suzhou residents. The CMR of cancer and CVD for both sexes showed an upward trend, with the increase being greater among males than among females. However, because the elderly population was larger among females, the decline in ASMRW of cancer and CVD was greater among females than among males after adjusting for age structure. This suggests that changes in CMR is influenced by the age structure of the population, a phenomenon consistent with national trends [16, 17]. In addition, consistent with previous findings in China, the greater increase observed among males suggests that males may bear a heavier mortality burden from cancer and CVD than females, particularly due to the higher prevalence and cumulative exposure of smoking and alcohol consumption among males [18, 19]. Further analysis through the difference decomposition of mortality rates revealed that population aging contributed as much as 402.58% and 333.20% to the mortality rates of cancer and CVD, respectively. Research from Shandong Province and Shanghai, both of which share borders with Jiangsu Province, have likewise found that the rise in mortality rates for cancer and CVD is primarily driven by population aging [20, 21]. At the same time, both the YLLr and premature mortality for cancer and CVD showed a downward trend, consistent with findings from studies in Nanjing and nationwide [22–24]. These findings indicate that advances in medical technology, improvements in early diagnosis and treatment, increased health awareness, and better management of major risk factors in Suzhou in recent years possibly have helped control the risk factors for cancer and CVD to some extent.

In contrast, the decline in mortality from CRD was most pronounced in Suzhou, with both CMR and ASMRW showing a downward trend between 2000 and 2020. The rates of decline were higher among females than among males, consistent with a study from Spain, whose trend analysis showed that CMR and ASMRW for COPD in Spain declined between 1996 and 2017, with the rate of decline among females being 1.24 times that of males [25]. This sex disparity is likely attributable to the substantially lower long-term smoking prevalence among females compared to males, resulting in considerably lower cumulative tobacco exposure [26]. At the same time, the results of the difference decomposition indicate that population aging has a negative contribution to the decline in mortality from CRD, acting as a restraining factor on the decline in mortality; whereas non-demographic factors have promoted the decline in mortality. Trends in the burden of mortality indicate that the decline in YLLr and premature mortality rates for CRD ranked first among the four major NCDs. This improvement coincided with concurrent public health efforts in Suzhou, including strengthened tobacco control, improved air quality, and enhanced healthcare delivery [27, 28].

Although diabetes accounted for the fewest deaths among the four major NCDs, it showed the least favorable changes in mortality rates and mortality burden. During the study period, both the CMR and ASMRW for diabetes increased in males and females, consistent with national trends [29]. However, the increase was statistically significant only among males, suggesting that the rising diabetes mortality burden in Suzhou was more evident in males, possibly reflecting sex differences in metabolic risk profiles and diabetes management [30]. The difference decomposition revealed that the increase in diabetes mortality resulted from the combined effects of population aging and non-demographic factors, indicating that risk factors for diabetes have not improved and may even have increased. Similarly, YLLr of diabetes showed an upward trend; although premature mortality exhibited a downward trend, the results were not statistically significant. This pattern may reflect the persistent and interacting effects of metabolic risk factors, including obesity, central adiposity, physical inactivity, unhealthy dietary patterns, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, together with challenges in diabetes awareness, treatment, and control [31, 32]. Recent national trend studies from South Korea also reported increasing type 2 diabetes prevalence across central adiposity groups, a rising prevalence of combined hypertension and diabetes particularly among individuals with short sleep duration, and a persistent increase in dyslipidemia prevalence despite improvements in awareness and treatment [33–35].

This study utilized death and population data from 2000 to 2020 to present mortality rates and temporal trends for four major NCDs. A difference decomposition method was further employed to isolate the impact of population aging on mortality changes. The findings provide empirical support for the assessment of disease burden. It should be acknowledged that this study has several limitations. First, diabetes may be under‑ascertained as the underlying cause of death, as it is often recorded as a contributing condition or subsumed under CVD causes in multimorbid patients, which may affect the recorded diabetes mortality burden. Second, due to the lack of data on individual-level risk factors, medical costs, and other related variables, it cannot establish a relationship between these factors and mortality outcomes. Third, the study was based on Suzhou’s household‑registered population, and findings may not be fully generalizable to non‑registered migrants, who may differ in age structure, socioeconomic status, and healthcare‑seeking behavior. Future studies incorporating individual‑level longitudinal data and broader socioeconomic and behavioral indicators are warranted.

In summary, this study indicates that although Suzhou has made progress in preventing and controlling deaths caused by four major NCDs between 2000 and 2020, NCDs remain a major challenge in the field of public health. Population aging is the primary driver of rising mortality rates for these four NCDs, but non-demographic factors have significantly mitigated its impact. The findings suggest that the current challenges in prevention and control lie not only in managing the risk factors associated with NCDs mortality but also in managing the health of elderly patients against the backdrop of an aging population and complex co-morbidities, thereby reducing the growing disease burden.
