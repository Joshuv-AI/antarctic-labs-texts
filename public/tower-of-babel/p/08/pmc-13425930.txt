scZGA: a novel model based on ZINB distribution and graph attention for scRNA-seq data clustering
Authors: Yansheng Kan, Yuling Liu, Jiacheng Pan, Ruochen Wang, Chen-Yu Zhang, Zhen Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying different cell types is a prerequisite step in the analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, with clustering being a common technique utilized for this purpose. However, high dropout rates inherent in scRNA-seq data and complex intercellular relationships become main challenges in scRNA-seq data analysis.ResultsTo address these issues, we proposed a novel model based on zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) distribution and graph attention network for scRNA-seq data clustering (scZGA). scZGA consists of three key modules. The first module captures the global probabilistic structure using a ZINB model. The second module constructs the graph with Pearson’s correlation coefficient, and employs a graph autoencoder with residual connection to learn important neighbor relationships while preserving topological structure information simultaneously. The final module conducts deep clustering through a self-optimizing embedding algorithm.ConclusionsWith these improvements, clustering results show that scZGA consistently achieves higher scores across six scRNA-seq datasets by using evaluation metrics such as normalized mutual information and adjusted rand index.Keywords: Clustering, scRNA-seq, ZINB autoencoder, Graph neural network

Background

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a revolutionary high-throughput technology capable of simultaneously measuring the expression levels of thousands of genes at the resolution of individual cells. Unlike traditional bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq), scRNA-seq unveils the inherent heterogeneity within cell populations, providing an unprecedented perspective for discovering novel cell subtypes, understanding differentiation trajectories, and exploring functional diversity. Consequently, clustering analysis of scRNA-seq data, which entails grouping cells into biologically meaningful subpopulations based on transcriptomic similarity, has become a pivotal task in downstream analyses within this field.

Accurate clustering of cells in scRNA-seq datasets remains a significant challenge. Due to low mRNA capture efficiency, scRNA-seq has a high dropout rate resulting in high sparsity of the gene expression matrix. To address this issue, earlier researchers proposed several dimensionality reduction methods, such as principal component analysis (PCA) [1], T-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (tSNE) [2], and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) [3]. PCA employs linear transformations to retain essential information and denoising. In particular, Spearman subsampling-clustering-classification (SSCC) [4] utilized PCA to project subsampled cells into feature space. t-SNE is a non-linear method designed to preserve pairwise similarities between data points when projecting data to a lower-dimensional space. Similar to tSNE, UMAP also preserves similarities but provides a better balance between local and global structures.

All these methods have increased information density and alleviated the problem of data sparsity to some extent. Nonetheless, not all zeros in the expression matrix are ‘true’ zeros, which means these genes are expressed but were not captured by the scRNA-seq. To impute these missing values, scRMD [5] employs the robust matrix decomposition method by minimizing objective function including both low-rank and sparsity penalty term. scImpute [6] infers dropout events with Gamma-Normal mixture model and applies Lasso-penalized linear regression for imputation. CIDR [7] estimates true gene expression levels using a mixture model of expressed and unexpressed gene states.

With increasing sequencing data, true distribution of single-cell data is gradually being revealed. This provides more prior knowledge about the distribution and makes regularized autoencoders a popular tool for imputing scRNA-seq data. VASC [8] fits single-cell data to normal distribution using a variational autoencoder. scGNN [9] uses left-truncated mixture Gaussian to model single-cell data. In recent years, the autoencoder model based on zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) distribution has gained increasing acceptance [10–12]. Methods such as scDeepCluster [13], scVI [14], scTPC [15] encode scRNA-seq data with ZINB model and achieved promising results.

To better characterize the relationship between global cell populations and graph representation, the emerging graph neural network (GNN) techniques are increasingly utilized. Seurat [16] builds a k-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph of cells. scGNN employs graph convolutional network (GCN) to learn cell representations using graph structural information. Similarly, scGAC [17] designed a graph attentional autoencoder to propagate information across cells with different weights.

Despite substantial advances in clustering cell types from scRNA-seq data, numerous issues continue to persist without resolution. These issues include effective construction of cell relationship graph, over-smoothing problem of graph convolutional network, and separation between embedding-learning and clustering modules. The first issue arises mainly because KNN graphs utilize Euclidean distance, which only measures linear similarity among cells and fails to capture underlying relationships. Secondly, as the graph convolution is essentially a form of Laplacian smoothing, repeatedly applying Laplacian smoothing may mix features of vertices from different clusters and make them indistinguishable. Finally, the drawback of sequential multistep clustering approaches is that the learned embedding may not be the optimal fit for subsequent clustering task.

To address these issues, we propose a novel model called single-cell clustering based on ZINB model and graph attention network (scZGA). The scZGA model comprises three components: distribution fitting, representation learning, and cell clustering. Instead of using Euclidean distance, scZGA incorporates Pearson’s correlation coefficient into KNN method to effectively measure similarity among cells. To avoid the over-smoothing issue inherent in GCNs, graph attention networks are employed to learn the weights of edges, and residual connections are utilized to preserve the original features of the data. Conventional joint deep clustering approaches are adapted to GNNs to learn clustering-friendly representations.

The performance of scZGA was evaluated against five competing methods. These methods are scDeepCluster [13], SSCC [4], Seurat [16], scGNN [9] and scGAC [17]. Among these methods, scDeepCluster employs a deep clustering model. Seurat utilizes graph structure. Both scGNN and scGAC apply graph neural networks.

Boldface values indicate that scZGA outperforms all other methods evaluated on the given dataset. For methods that can specify or adjust the number of clusters, CA was calculated (Fig. 2). As shown in Table 2, scZGA achieves the best NMI scores on five out of the six heterogeneous datasets. The higher NMI scores suggest that the clustering results of scZGA share more mutual information with the underlying biological categories. Table 3; Fig. 2 demonstrate that scZGA achieves the highest ARI and CA scores across all datasets. The strong ARI scores further indicate a high agreement between the pairwise sample groupings produced by scZGA and the true labels, significantly surpassing the level expected by chance. Concurrently, obtaining the best CA reflects the method’s capability to correctly match individual samples to their true classes.

Parameter sensitivity analysis

As a method based on graph attention networks, performance of scZGA is influenced by the number of attention heads. Accordingly, different parameter values were employed to examine their effects on final clustering performance.

scZGA uses multi-head attention mechanism to stabilize the learning process. To investigate the impact of multi-head attention, the number of attention heads was set from 1 to 5, and the resulting NMI scores were observed. As shown in Fig. 3a, four out of six datasets achieved the best NMI scores with 5 attention heads, except for the Zeisel and Human3 datasets. Therefore, attention heads were set to 5 in the following experiments.

Model performance in terms of NMI score under different numbers of attention heads and GCN layers. It is evident that the number of attention heads has a greater impact on model performance, whereas the number of GCN layers has a smaller impact

Furthermore, to evaluate the performance of scZGA in preventing over-smoothing, the number of GCN layers were set to 1, 2, 3, and 4 for experimental comparison. As shown in Fig. 3b, as GCN layers increased, NMI values remained stable.

Ablation study

In scZGA, ZINB autoencoder, graph autoencoder, and deep clustering model are combined. First, ZINB autoencoder captures global probabilistic structure of data. Second, Graph autoencoder is an autoencoder with graph attention network to learn important neighbor relationships while preserving topological structure information, which enables flexible information sharing between adjacent cells in the graph. Finally, deep clustering model conducts clustering within latent space.

To evaluate contributions of various components in our method, three different experiments were designed: complete scZGA model, scZGA without ZINB autoencoder (No_ZINB), and scZGA without deep clustering (No_DC).

It is worth noting that the deep clustering model relies on the graph autoencoder, so it is unreasonable to conduct an experiment for scZGA without the graph autoencoder. For No_ZINB, the matrix was reduced to 32 dimensions using PCA.

As shown in Fig. 4, scZGA outperforms both No_ZINB and No_DC with higher NMI scores across all datasets. Additionally, from comparison between No_ZINB and No_DC, it is evident that ZINB autoencoder makes a more significant contribution to clustering in entire model.

Discussion

In conclusion, we proposed scZGA, an attention-based deep learning model. scZGA consists of two autoencoder models and a deep clustering model. In autoencoder models, ZINB autoencoder effectively handles scRNA-seq data with excessive zeros and figures out its low dimensional embedding. By performing graph reconstruction, the graph autoencoder preserves cell-cell topological structure and retains original data information, while the deep clustering model employs k-means for initial cluster center acquisition, followed by embedding self-optimization. Unlike prior methods that typically lack either a zero-inflated probabilistic model (e.g., scGNN, scGAC) or graph attention mechanisms (e.g., scDeepCluster), scZGA bridges this gap by integrating a ZINB autoencoder with graph attention and deep clustering into a unified framework, enabling synergistic learning of both gene expression characteristics and cellular topology.

Some limitations can be found in scZGA. First is the instability of k-means algorithm. Due to the randomness of initial cluster center selection, clustering performance may experience a certain extent of variability. This dependence on initialization can lead to inconsistent results across runs, which limits the robustness of the overall pipeline. In addition, k-means requires specifying the number of clusters. Although different results may be correct given the concept of cell subpopulations, it is challenging to match the cell classification resolution of the ground truth. Consequently, multiple experimental trials were performed to identify the most suitable k value, ultimately documenting the optimal outcome achieved. Future work will explore more stable clustering strategies, such as deterministic initialization or ensemble approaches, and the integration of data-driven methods for automatic cluster number estimation. Second, scZGA exhibits moderate runtime efficiency. Being an integration of three models, the run time of scZGA significantly increases with the number of cells. This scalability limitation may hinder application to atlas-scale datasets, and thus optimizing the model architecture, e.g., via graph sampling or knowledge distillation, will be a priority for future studies. Third, the performance evaluation in this study is primarily focused on comparisons with established baseline models or closely related algorithmic approaches. Therefore, large-scale benchmarking against state-of-the-art models is considered a direction for future work. Beyond benchmarking, future extensions of scZGA could involve the incorporation of multi-omics data and improved model interpretability to enhance its biological utility.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed a novel scRNA-seq clustering model called scZGA. This model innovatively combines ZINB and graph attention network. scZGA effectively overcomes challenges of high dropout rate and complex intercellular relationships inherent in single-cell data. ScZGA integrates three core modules: (1) ZINB module: capturing the global probability structure of data; (2) Graph Autoencoder Module: Using Pearson correlation coefficient to construct cell relationship graph, and learning key neighborhood relationships through graph autoencoder with residual connections while preserving topological structure information; (3) Deep clustering module: using self-optimizing embedding algorithm to achieve final clustering. Experimental evaluation shows that scZGA has significantly better clustering performance than baseline methods on six single-cell datasets, and its advantages are consistently reflected in key evaluation indicators such as NMI and ARI. Therefore, scZGA provides a powerful and reliable tool for single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis.
