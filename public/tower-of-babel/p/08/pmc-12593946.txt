The effect of educational program delivered Whatsapp based on the theory of planned behavior on dietary behaviors of women with prediabetes: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Nasibeh Zerangian, MohammadAli Kianfard, Fatemeh Mardanpour, Mohammad Vahedian Shahroodi, Mehrsadat Mahdizadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and objectiveAn unhealthy diet is one of the most important behavioral factors associated with the risk of progression of prediabetes to diabetes. The present study aimed to determine the effect of an educational program on WhatsApp based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) on the dietary behaviors of women with prediabetes.MethodsA quasi-experimental study with a control group was conducted on 72 women with prediabetes referring to health centers in Mashhad, Iran. The samples were randomly assigned to two experimental and control groups. Participants in the experimental group received an 8-session educational program based on TPB via WhatsApp. Data were collected using a demographic information questionnaire and a questionnaire based on TPB constructs before, immediately after the intervention and at 3-month follow-up. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 25 software, with a significance level of p < 0.05.FindingsThe mean scores of attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, intention, and dietary behavior in the experimental group significantly differed from the control group from baseline to follow-up. (P < 0.001). However, these changes were not significant in the control group.ConclusionThe results suggest that TPB-based nutrition education via WhatsApp can promote dietary behaviors in women with prediabetes. TPB is a suitable theoretical framework for healthy behaviors and can be used to design educational interventions via social media platforms to empower patients with prediabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04121-3.Keywords: Dietary behaviors, Nutrition, Pre-diabetes, Health education, Theory of planned behavior

Introduction

Pre-diabetes is a significant global health issue as individuals with prediabetes are at a high risk of progressing to diabetes, as well as developing kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and even mortality [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines prediabetes as an intermediate hyperglycemic state using two specific parameters. Impaired Fasting Glucose (IFG) is defined as fasting plasma glucose between 6.1 and 6.9 mmol/L (110 to 125 mg/dL), and Impaired Glucose Tolerance (IGT) is defined as plasma glucose 2 h after consuming 75 g of oral glucose or a combination of these two, ranging from 7.8 to 11.1 mmol/L (140 to 200 mg/dL) [2].

Pre-diabetes represents a window of opportunity during which modifiable risk factors, such as overweight and obesity, high-calorie diet, and insufficient physical activity, can be controlled to prevent or delay the development of type 2 diabetes [3]. The progressive nature of type 2 diabetes and its chronic complications is complex. Dealing with this requires lifestyle modification interventions for prevention and control, which have gained significant importance. Intervention in the pre-diabetes stage and the adoption of effective preventive strategies are the most efficient measures in diabetes management [4].

In a systematic review and meta-analysis, concluded that lifestyle interventions, including a healthy diet and physical activity, could reduce the risk of progression to type 2 diabetes in high-risk women [5]. Another meta-analysis showed that lifestyle interventions could reduce the risk of developing type 2 diabetes by up to 25% and decrease HbA1c levels by up to 0.15% [6].

The most effective training programs are based on behavior-change theories. Therefore, choosing the appropriate educational model is the first step in designing an educational program, and the effectiveness of health education depends on the ability to use these theories [7]. The theory of planned behavior (TPB) is one of behavioral change. This theory considers people’s intentions to be the most important determinant of behavior, which is influenced by the three components of attitude, mental norms, and perceived behavioral control. Behavioral intention or intention predicts the performance of a desired behavior. Attitude refers to a person’s positive or negative evaluation of the results of a particular behavior. Subjective norms represent the perceived pressure from important people in life regarding the implementation or non-implementation of a certain behavior. Perceived behavioral control reflects a person’s belief and confidence in their ability to perform a specific behavior [8].

A systematic review of clinical trial interventions based on theories showed it was shown that interventions using theories were effective in improving knowledge, self-efficacy and HbA1c levels in diabetic patients [9]. Another study showed that self-care programs and education through social networks can increase knowledge and self-care skills in diabetic patients [10]. In recent years, mobile-based interaction has changed the dynamics of education. Social media is a powerful catalyst for education [11]. Studies have shown that delivering an educational intervention via mobile phone can have a moderate to large impact on improving self-care performance and disease outcomes in people with type 2 diabetes [10, 12–14]. However, a review suggests that the effectiveness of using mobile-based technologies to motivate behavior change remains unclear. Further research using Android-based technology to change lifestyle behavior and provide information to people with diabetes, particularly through live chat or educational videos packaged in mobile apps, is needed [15]. Furthermore, research evaluating the use of social media to improve diabetes management in the Middle East is scarce [16]. Considering the insufficient findings about the effectiveness of educational programs based on theory [17] and the use of WhatsApp in changing nutritional behavior, the present study aimed to determine the effect of the educational intervention based on TPB through WhatsApp on dietary behaviors in women with prediabetes.

Method

Study design and participants

A semi-experimental study was conducted with a control group in Mashhad, Iran. This study adheres to checklist for quasi-experimental study of JBI critical appraisal tools [21][18].The eligibility criteria were as followed: (1) informed consent, (2) Female sex, (3) diagnosis of prediabetes with blood glucose levels between 100 and 125 and recorded in the electronic health record, (4) age range of 30 to 59 years, (5) Iranian nationality, (6) Ability to use WhatsApp. The exclusion criteria included; (1) unwillingness to continue participating in the study and (2) absence from more than one session of online educational classes.

The sample size was determined by using a conventional formula for repeated measures designs (comparing two groups in three measurements), applying rule-of-thumb values for the required parameters. was used to calculate the sample size, with an effect size of 0.5, correlation 0.5, variance 1 and a test power of 0.8 at a significance level of 0.05, the sample size was 32 participants in each group. Considering the intervention nature of the study and a potential dropout rate of by approximately 10%, the final sample size was established at 36 individuals in each group.

We also used G*Power software to calculate sample size to improve accuracy. A power analysis (Statistical test: ANOVA: Repeated measures, within–between interaction; Type of power analysis: Sensitivity: Compute required effect size—given α, power, and sample size) using the G*Power software (version 3) specified that 36 individuals in each group, and a total of 72 individuals, were required to detect an average effect size (f = 0.151), considering power = 0.80, α = 0.05, number of groups = 2, number of measurements = 3, correlation between measurements = 0.5, and nonsphericity correction ε = 1.

In our study, since the questionnaires were collected from the individuals one by one by the relevant researcher, by phone (or in person), for this reason, there was no drop in sample size in the immediate after-intervention and follow-up phases.

Randomization and recruitment

Multistage random sampling was used to collect samples. Four comprehensive health service centers were selected as clusters as described in a previous study [19]. These comprehensive health service centers were selected so that they had an acceptable distance from each other to minimize sample contact and had the highest prevalence of prediabetes. In addition, they were randomly selected to ensure homogeneity in the cultural, economic, and social contexts. Using the lottery method, two comprehensive health service centers were assigned to the experimental group and two comprehensive health service centers were assigned to the control group. Each participant was assigned a unique code based on a list of names registered in the electronic health information system. Eighteen women with prediabetes were selected from each comprehensive health service center to participate in the study according to the eligibility criteria. A total of 36 participants with prediabetes were randomly assigned to the experimental group and 36 participants to the control group. All participants in the intervention and control groups completed questionnaires before the intervention and at the follow-up stage. A flowchart of the sampling process is shown in Fig. 1.

Intervention

The intervention was conducted based on the template, as depicted in the intervention description (Table 1). TPB was employed as a guide to provide a conceptual framework for understanding the determinants of behavior and designing effective interventions. This framework allows program designers and policymakers to identify essential elements guiding and enhancing behavior in intervention designs. TPB, which includes structures such as attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention, has a very important effect on eating behavior. The educational intervention consisted of eight sessions in four weeks, each lasting 30 min. The meetings were conducted through the WhatsApp platform.

Data collection tools

The questionnaire consisted of two sections: (A) demographic Information: age, height, weight (the BMI variable is created from the height and weight variables), number of children, marital status, individual’s education, spouse’s education, spouse’s occupation, family income, history of illness (having hypertension and blood lipids and not having), and family history of diabetes. (B) TPB constructs-based questions: attitude construct (6 questions); Example: “Because diabetes is not present in my family, I will not get diabetes.” subjective norms construct (2 questions); Example: “my doctor encourages me to follow a healthy diet, including daily consumption of fruits and vegetables, dairy products, and avoiding fatty and sweet foods. Perceived behavioral control structure (2 questions); example: “I have a high degree of control over the foods I eat, even in difficult situations”. Behavioral intention structure (3 questions); example: “I intend to use less unhealthy foods such as fast food, soft drinks, cakes, and chocolates. Responses to these questions were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “completely agree (5)” to “completely disagree (1).” The behavior construct (3 questions); as an example, I consume healthy foods (including dairy, proteins, fruits and vegetables, etc.) daily according to the Food Guide. A 5-point Likert scale from “always (5)” to “never (1)” was used for evaluation (see S 2). To determine the face validity of the questionnaire, both qualitative and quantitative methods were employed. Qualitatively, the questionnaire was provided to 10 participants to discuss issues related to comprehension difficulty, simplicity, clarity of items, and the possibility of misinterpretations. Necessary revisions were made based on their feedback. Face validity was further confirmed quantitatively by asking another 10 eligible individuals to rate the importance of each item for measuring the intended construct based on their experiences, assigning scores to each item. Items with scores equal to or greater than 1.5 were considered suitable.

For content validity determination, the questionnaire was distributed among 2 nutrition specialists and 8 health education specialists. The Content Validity Index (CVI) was found to be above 0.79, and the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) exceeded 0.62, meeting the acceptance criteria. To examine the structural validity, a confirmatory factor analysis was conducted on 196 individuals using AMOS software, and the questionnaire was scrutinized. In the confirmatory factor analysis, the goodness of fit indices was acceptable (RMSEA = 0.052, GFI = 0.922, TLI = 0.931, CMIN/DF = 1.524). The reliability of the instrument was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.74. For the reproducibility of the questionnaire, intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated, which was 0.92 for the questionnaire.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee for Human Research at Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, under the code IR.MUMS.REC.1399.324. Informed verbal consent was obtained from the study participants via telephone, during which the research objectives were clearly articulated. Additionally, participants were assured of the confidentiality of their information throughout the study.

Statistical analyses

After collecting research data, the questionnaires were encoded for descriptive data analysis. Subsequently, they were entered into the statistical software SPSS version 25. Following a meticulous review and validation of data entry accuracy, descriptive statistics including the mean and standard deviation for quantitative variables and frequency and percentage for qualitative variables were determined.

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to assess variable distributions in the experimental and control groups. Because the distribution of all quantitative variables in the study was normal, parametric tests were used in this study. To ascertain homogeneity in the two groups, for quantitative variables, independent samples t-test, and for qualitative variables chi-square test were employed. For pre-, immediate, and follow-up comparisons of variables, repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted. Also, the effect size value (Partial Eta Squared) is reported for comparisons. The significance level for all tests was set at 0.05.

The majority were married women (86.1%), 40.3% had elementary education, 73.6% had an income below the poverty line, and 47.2% had disease. Discussion

The present study aimed to determine the impact of a planned educational intervention based on the TPB on promoting dietary behaviors among women with prediabetes. According to the findings of the study, the educational intervention resulted in a significant increase in the favorable attitude of individuals with prediabetes in the intervention group. It seems that participants gained a positive attitude towards the importance of healthy eating in controlling their disease by becoming familiar with preventive measures and becoming sensitive to their positive and negative consequences. These outcomes align with the findings of previous studies [20, 21]. In the study by MohammadniaMotlagh, after the in-person educational intervention, the mean scores of attitude and perceived control regarding physical activity and dietary performance increased significantly in the intervention group. In addition, fasting blood glucose levels in the intervention group improved significantly compared to the control group [21]. These findings indicate that our educational program implemented via WhatsApp, similar to the effects of in-person educational sessions, was able to have a significant impact on creating positive attitudes towards healthy eating in the management of prediabetes.

Individual attitudes play a substantial role in shaping behavior and adherence to treatment plans, affecting patient performance. Studies have demonstrated that a positive attitude toward dietary regimen and physical activity in diabetes management can lead to increased treatment adherence and better outcome [22]. Culture is an important determinant of health that influences the formation of beliefs and attitudes in the interaction of service providers with patients [23]. A study found that healthcare providers should tailor interventions to cultural factors and health beliefs [24]). Also, studies have shown that cultural barriers have been a limiting factor in achieving optimal diabetes management among patients [25]. Iran is a country with different ethnicities and religions that has created a special cultural diversity [26]. In Iranian culture, the preference for traditional foods containing red meat and saturated fats, the mother’s role in planning family meals that is often aligned with the tastes of her husband and children, and the father’s role in grocery shopping can strongly influence dietary behaviors. In this study, to promote positive attitudes and reduce cultural barriers, a family member participated in an educational group on WhatsApp to facilitate the adoption of healthy eating behaviors in women with prediabetes.

The results of the present study showed that the mean scores of the subjective norm construct increased statistically significantly after the educational intervention in the experimental group. This finding is consistent with previous research that emphasized the effectiveness of educational programs based on behavioral theories such as TPB in promoting self-care behaviors and health literacy in people with type 2 diabetes [20, 27]. Additionally, subjective norms contribute to dietary behaviors in diabetic men [28] Some educational interventions have involved not only patients but also their close relatives in the educational program [29, 30]. Family member participation and the receipt of information have led to an increase in subjective norms. Family involvement in educational interventions for diabetic patients can have a positive impact on diabetes management. Research shows that family support has been effective in improving medication adherence and blood sugar control in adults with diabetes [31] and diabetes management education [32]. In addition, the involvement of family members in the daily care of diabetic patients, such as providing reminders, assistance, and support, can be beneficial in enhancing outcome [33, 34].

The results of the present study showed a significant increase in the mean scores of the perceived behavioral control construct in the experimental group after the educational intervention via WhatsApp. The increase in perceived behavioral control scores indicates that the program was able to increase participants’ confidence in their ability to control conditions that affect effectively manage their prediabetes condition. Similar to these findings, in a study has indicated that educational interventions based on the locus of control construct in attribution theory can have a positive effect on self-care behaviors of type 2 diabetes patients [31]. The results of the present study showed that the mean scores of the behavioral intention construct in the experimental group increased significantly after the educational intervention. The significant improvement in behavioral intention scores indicates that the WhatsApp intervention successfully had a positive effect on the participants’ motivation to adopt healthier eating behaviors. This finding is consistent with several previous studies [27, 29, 35].

The results of the present study showed a significant increase in the mean scores of the behavioral construct in the experimental group after the educational intervention. This increase in behavioral construct scores indicates that the educational program had a significant impact on the actual behaviors of prediabetics in the experimental group. Several studies have shown the effectiveness of educational interventions based on the theory of planned behavior in promoting various behaviors related to diabetes management [27, 36, 37]. These results indicate that theory-based educational intervention via WhatsApp can have a positive impact on the management of type 2 diabetes and its consequences, similar to face-to-face educational interventions.

Limitations

This study had limitations, including the self-reported nature of data collection, which may not accurately reflect the actual performance of study participants. Due to economic constraints and restrictions on the use of laboratory services by health insurance companies for determining HbA1c and FBS, as well as holding meetings on WhatsApp and completing most of the questionnaires over the phone, it was not possible to collect data to determine BMI and other objective findings related to the primary outcome. Another limitation was that the cultural and socioeconomic status of the families, a factor influencing the management of prediabetes, was not under the control of the researcher. To overcome this limitation, women with prediabetes were suggested to choose affordable and economical food items and use these food items as an alternative to maintain a healthy diet. Also, a family member was involved on WhatsApp to reduce cultural barriers and facilitate the use of educational content. In addition, most of the questionnaires were completed via the phone, a method that was sometimes difficult for participants to complete. In Iran, mobile phones and social media are available, but the quality and stability of the Internet are subject to regional and time constraints that can affect the delivery of messages and motivation for continuous and sustained use of educational content and limit the generalizability of the findings to other countries with high Internet access. Also, this study was conducted only on women with prediabetes. Therefore, these results cannot be generalized to men with prediabetes. It is suggested that future studies should determine objective measures such as H bA1c and FBS or BMI to strengthen the primary outcome. Also, it is suggested to conduct a cross-cultural study in different social and cultural conditions to validate the generalizability of online educational interventions.

Conclusion

The results show that nutrition education based on the theory of planned behavior, delivered through social messaging platforms such as WhatsApp, can have a positive role in improving dietary behaviors among women with prediabetes. Therefore, it can be claimed that the application of this theory can change the attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and intention of women with prediabetes and lead to the promotion of healthy dietary behaviors with the aim of preventing diabetes. It is suggested that health planners and decision-makers use this method to design and implement community-based educational programs to empower patients with prediabetes.
