SIRT6 activator MDL-800 alleviates periodontal inflammation by inhibiting AIM2 pathway
Authors: Peilei Shi, Rui Ma, Xiaobing Lan, Li Feng, Kun Xue, Runnan Dai, Xudong Xie, Chunmei Xu, Tao Pang, Jun Wang, Jian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriodontitis is an infectious and inflammatory disease. The inability to control clinical inflammation significantly contributes to unsuccessful periodontal treatment. As a key regulator of epigenetics, Sirtuin 6 is one of the NAD+-dependent histone deacetylase family members and has recently gained considerable interest in the context of inflammation. While MDL-800 has been demonstrated as an activator of SIRT6, its function in treating periodontitis remains unknown. In this study, animal experiments revealed that MDL-800 reduced alveolar bone resorption in ligature-induced periodontitis, particularly via reducing inflammation and inhibiting osteoclast production in a SIRT6-dependent manner. Furthermore, human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) were exposed to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to mimic periodontal inflammation in vitro. We noted that MDL-800 remarkably reduced expressions of pro-inflammatory cytokines in LPS-induced HGFs, which was neutralized by inhibiting SIRT6. Besides, MDL-800 could also reduce oxidative stress damage under inflammatory conditions. The suppression of AIM2 inflammasome activation was associated with the significant anti-inflammatory properties of MDL-800, as demonstrated by RNA sequencing and validation experiments. In conclusion, these findings suggest that MDL-800 was effective in reducing periodontal inflammation, partly by inhibiting AIM2 pathway in a SIRT6-dependent manner, demonstrating significant promise for enhancing therapeutic outcomes in periodontitis.KEY WORDS: SIRT6, MDL-800, Periodontitis, AIM2 inflammasome, Inflammation, Human gingival fibroblast, Epigenetic, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Periodontitis is a long-lasting inflammatory disease influenced by multiple factors and manifested as an imbalance between microbial biofilms and host immune responses, which eventually causes the breakdown of the teeth's supporting structure1. Periodontitis is recognized as a possible hazardous factor for several systemic diseases, including cardiovascular disease2, adverse pregnancy outcomes3, and rheumatoid arthritis4. The first step in treating periodontitis is scaling and root planning, but it may not always work, particularly in populations that are highly susceptible5. Therefore, it's crucial to prioritize the advancement of innovative anti-inflammatory drugs for the treatment of periodontitis.

An individual's susceptibility to periodontal diseases is collectively affected by various factors, including environmental, genetic, and epigenetic influences. Epigenetics involves studying chemical modifications that affect gene expression while leaving the DNA sequence unchanged, encompassing DNA methylation, non-coding RNA interference and histone modifications. It serves as a pivotal bridge connecting genetic and environmental factors6. Earlier research has demonstrated that periodontal pathogens increase the expression of histone acetyltransferase and induce acetylation of histone 37,8, ultimately contributing to the exacerbation of periodontitis.

As an essential epigenetic regulator, sirtuins (SIRTs) represent a conserved NAD+-dependent histone deacetylase family. They execute crucial roles in the modulation of many pathways, such as gene expression, DNA repair, cellular metabolism, and mitochondrial biology9. In mammals, there are seven sirtuins (SIRT1–7), each with unique functional profiles, substrate affinities, and subcellular regions10. SIRT6 is a deacetylase located in the nucleus. Studies have concluded that it inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion in multiple inflammatory disorders including osteoarthritis and neuroinflammation. These cytokines encompass interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α11, 12, 13. A recent study has highlighted that overexpression of SIRT6 reduces the inflammatory response triggered by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway14. We previously discovered MDL-800, which is a SIRT6 activator with high potency and selectivity15. It has been reported that MDL-800 activates SIRT6 via a unique surface allosteric site, which distinguishes itself structurally among members of the sirtuin family, thereby facilitating highly specific ligand interactions16. However, the effectiveness of this small-molecule SIRT6 activator in treating periodontitis remains uncertain at present.

Currently, there is a deficiency of clear and comprehensive evaluation of epigenetic alterations in periodontitis. This investigation aims to assess the protective role of MDL-800 (Fig. 1A), the activator of SIRT6, in an experimental periodontitis mouse model in vivo, and to explore further molecular mechanisms that govern the anti-inflammatory properties of MDL-800 by using human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) in vitro. We have elucidated that MDL-800 exerts important therapeutic effects on periodontitis via inhibiting AIM2 pathway in a SIRT6-dependent manner. Our research findings have confirmed the histone modifications in periodontitis and highlighted histone deacetylase SIRT6 as a viable therapeutic target for periodontitis.

Materials and methods

Establishment of an experimental periodontitis model in mice

To analyse the anti-inflammatory properties of MDL-800, we established a ligature-induced murine periodontitis model. The Research Ethics Board of West China School of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-D-2022-207) approved this animal study, performed in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

8-Week-old male C57BL/6J mice were reared with 12 h light/dark cycles. Following a week of adjusted feeding, ligature placement initiated experimental periodontitis. In general, a 5-0 suture was inserted into the right mandibular first molar (M1) sulcus, designating the right side as ligated side and the left side as non-ligated side. The whole operation was conducted under general anesthesia (intraperitoneal injection of 20 μL/g avertin). After that, mice were randomly assigned to designed groups (n = 8). Starting from the day of ligature, one group received intragastric administration of MDL-800 at dosage of 60 mg/kg, the drug being suspended in 300 μL normal saline solution. Another group was administered a same volume of normal saline solution. According to ligature or not, M1 of all mice were allocated into four experiment groups: saline, MDL-800, Ligature + Saline, and Ligature + MDL-800. Intragastric administration was given every second day over a four-week period, following which all mice were sacrificed.

To further verify the key role of activating SIRT6 in the anti-inflammatory effects of MDL-800, we used in vivo siRNA to silence Sirt6 in periodontal tissues. Sequences of Sirt6 and NC gene in vivo siRNAs were GAAGCUCCCAAUGCAAUAAAU and UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU, respectively. The Sirt6 siRNA was injected on the buccal and lingual sides of M1 at a dosage of 0.05 nmol/g body weight 3 days before ligature. Eventually, M1 of all mice were divided into five experiment groups (n = 5 in each group): (1) Control group; (2) Ligature group, establishing experimental periodontitis model; (3) Ligature + MDL-800 group, establishing experimental periodontitis model and receiving MDL-800; (4) si-Sirt6 + Ligature group, establishing experimental periodontitis model and injecting SIRT6 siRNA into gingiva around M1; (5) si-Sirt6 + Ligature + MDL-800 group, establishing experimental periodontitis model, injecting Sirt6 siRNA and receiving MDL-800. Administration and frequency of MDL-800 were the same as in the above experiments. Sirt6 siRNA injection was given every 3 days for two weeks, and then all the mice were sacrificed.

To validate the anti-inflammation function of SIRT6, we used SIRT6 inhibitor (compound 11e, 30 mg/kg)17 for mice with experimental periodontitis. According to the methods above, M1 of all mice was also allocated into four distinct experimental sets (n = 6 in each group): Saline, 11e, Ligature + Saline, and Ligature + 11e. Administration and frequency of drugs and harvest time are the same as in the above SIRT6 activator experiments.

Micro-CT analysis

Upon the mice's euthanasia, samples from the bilateral mandibular molar area were harvested. Micro-CT (SCANCO Medical, Switzerland) was utilized for examining samples (10 μm, 70 kV, 200 μA). 3D reconstruction and trabecular bone parameters were executed using SCANCO Medical Evaluation software as previously reported18,19.

Histological and IHC analyses

Fixed mandibles were decalcified in 10% EDTA, immersed in paraffin and then sliced into 5-μm thickness for use. H&E and Masson's trichrome staining were conducted as previously described20. Immunostaining was performed as previously reported21 with primary antibodies as follows: anti-IL-1β rabbit polyclonal antibody (ab205924, Abcam; 1:200) and anti-AIM2 rabbit polyclonal antibody (PB9683, Boster; 1:200).

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase staining

Following the operating instructions provided by the manufacturer, we used a kit (Sigma–Aldrich, Germany) to carry out TRAP staining. Osteoclasts that are positive for TRAP are those monocyte or multinucleated cells that are located on the bone surface, have wine red staining in the cytoplasm, and are relatively large in size.

Cell culture

This study has been deemed ethical by the Ethics Board of West China School of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-D-2022-207). In alignment with the Declaration of Helsinki's specific requirements, all individuals involved in this study have executed informed consent forms. HGFs were collected from clinically healthy gingival connective tissue explants from patients who underwent crown lengthening. Briefly, the tissues were rinsed repeatedly using PBS with 10% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) and then digested with 3 mg/mL collagenase type 1 (Biofroxx, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. These tissues were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco), under culture conditions set at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 95% air and 5% CO2. HGFs from the 3rd to 6th generations were adopted for subsequent procedures. Escherichia coli LPS (O55:B5, InvivoGen) was used to stimulate inflammation in HGFs.

Cell proliferation assay

The HGFs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. HGFs were exposed to MDL-800 (5–50 μmol/L)) for 24, 48, and 72 h. Cell proliferation ability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo Laboratories, Japan), and the operational procedure adhered precisely to the manufacturer's specifications.

Cell transfection

To thoroughly investigate the effect of diminished SIRT6 expression on inflammatory cytokine levels, we used Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) to transfect SIRT6 gene siRNA (20 nmol/L) and NC into HGFs cells, and the transfection time was set to 48 h. Sequences of SIRT6 and NC gene si-RNAs were GAAUGUGCCAAGUGUAAGATT and UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT, respectively.

For the genomic knockout of SIRT6, a CRISPR/Cas9-based ribonucleoprotein (RNP) approach was employed using the 4D-Nucleofector™ X unit (Lonza, Switzerland). Briefly, HGFs (1 × 105 cells per reaction) were resuspended in 20 μL of P3 Primary Cell Nucleofector™ Solution and mixed with the pre-assembled RNP complex, which consisted of 1.5 pmol/μL sgRNA and 0.15 pmol/μL Cas9 protein (final concentrations). The sgRNA sequences for SIRT6 were 5′-GCTCCACGGGAACATGTTTG-3′ and 5′-GTAAGACGTGAGTGCCACCT-3′. The cell-RNP suspension was transferred to 20 μL Nucleocuvette™ strips, and a dual-electroporation protocol consisting of the FF-120 and CM-137 programs was applied sequentially to maximize knockout efficiency. For NC group, cells were subjected to the same electroporation procedure without the addition of RNP complexes. Following electroporation, cells were immediately recovered in pre-warmed culture medium and incubated for 48 h before subsequent analysis.

RT-qPCR and RNA-seq

We extracted total RNA with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and then reverse transcribed RNA using RT reagent kit (TaKaRa Bio, Japan) to obtain stable cDNA. The mRNA expression level was evaluated using SYBR Green mix (Bio-Rad, USA) in the LightCycler96® instrument (Roche, Germany). We used GAPDH as housekeeping gene and the 2−ΔΔCt method to calculate and obtain the results. Primer sequences of targeted gene are listed (Supporting information Table S1).

HGFs were also collected for RNA-seq after LPS/MDL-800 treatment to clarify the possible processes that contribute to significant anti-inflammatory effects of MDL-800 under LPS-induced conditions. We ascertained the integrity of RNA utilising RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit with Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, USA), and only high-quality RNA samples were kept to construct the sequence library on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The high-quality data we obtained from double-end sequencing were compared to the human reference genome (hg38) using HISAT2 v2.0.5 software. The DESeq2 R package was used to carry out differential expression analysis. Our criterion for DEGs is that genes with a P value < 0.05 identified by DESeq2 and |log2FoldChange| > 1. The clusterProfiler R package was applied to conduct KEGG pathway analysis. In order to further study the interactions between DEGs, STRING database was adopted to build a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network.

ROS assay

Intracellular ROS were measured by using a fluorescent probe (Beyotime, China). First, LPS (1 μg/mL)-induced HGFs were treated for 24 h with or without MDL-800 (25 μmol/L). HGFs were cultured at 37 °C for 1 h with DCFH-DA. Signals emitted by green fluorescence were detected under a fluorescence microscope.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) analysis

HGFs were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Subsequently, cell membrane permeation was performed using a PBS solution with 0.3% Triton X-100. For blocking, HGFs were treated in PBS with 10% goat serum for 30 min, then an additional 1 h incubation at ambient temperature with AlexaFluor-488 (1:200). HGFs were incubated with 0.5 μg/mL DAPI for 5 min before being mounted on microscope slides. The cells were then imaged with an Olympus microscope.

Inflammasome activation

In a six-well plate, each well was seeded with 5 × 105 HGFs. To activate the AIM2 inflammasome, LPS (1 μg/mL) was first applied to stimulate HGFs for 2 h, followed by transfection with poly (dA:dT) (Invivogen, USA; 1.5 μg/mL) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The transfection time was set to 6 h.

Western blot analysis

We extracted total cellular proteins of HGFs using a protein extract kit (PE001, Signalway Antibody, USA). Protein separation was carried out by electrophoresis on 10% SDS-PAGE polyacrylamide gels (Epizyme Biomedical Technology, China) according to the molecular weights of the proteins. Then, a wet transfer apparatus (Bio-Rad, USA) was used to electrically transfer the protein to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After soaked in 5%skimmed milk for 1 h, the membranes were probed with primary antibodies against SIRT6, H3K9Ac, H3, β-actin, p-p65, AIM2, IL-1β, and cleaved IL-1β (All from CST, UK,1:1000) at 4 °C overnight. Then the membrane and the peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Signalway Antibody, USA) were incubated for 2 h before final antigen testing.

ELISA analysis

We used an ELISA Kit (Multi Sciences, China) to measure the density of IL-1β in cell supernatant according to the instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and processed with SPSS 21.0 software. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to evaluate the normality of the data distribution. Student's t-test was used for pairwise comparisons between two groups. For the 2 × 2 factorial design, we employed two-way ANOVA to assess the main effects of each factor and their interaction. When the interaction or main effects were significant, post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey's HSD test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

MDL-800 treatment reduces periodontal tissue destruction in mice with experimental periodontitis

First, we conducted an in vivo investigation to explore the therapeutic potential of MDL-800 using ligature-induced experimental periodontitis mice, as depicted in the schematic diagram (Fig. 1B). We then performed micro-CT analysis to evaluate alveolar bone architecture. According to the reconstructed images, we observed severe bone loss in Ligature + Saline group mice, indicating the successful setup of an experimental periodontitis model in mice (Fig. 1C, middle-right panel). We noticed that continuous administration of MDL-800 for four weeks markedly improved ligature-mediated alveolar bone loss. Next, quantitative results further confirmed that MDL-800 prevented alveolar bone loss in mice with experimental periodontitis (Fig. 1D). In addition, MDL-800 treatment contributed to increased bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and reduced trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) in experimental periodontitis mice, suggesting that MDL-800 could markedly improve alveolar bone architecture (Fig. 1E).

Next, H&E staining revealed a widened PDL space with more inflammatory cells infiltrated in experimental periodontitis mice, which had been partially rescued after MDL-800 administration (Fig. 2A). According to Masson's trichrome staining, MDL-800 treatment contributed to more dense collagen fibers in the PDL of experimental periodontitis mice (Fig. 2B). TRAP staining exhibited more TRAP-positive osteoclasts in experimental periodontitis mice, whereas TRAP-positive osteoclasts decreased in response to MDL-800 treatment (Fig. 2C). Additionally, the immunohistochemical (IHC) staining results revealed a reduction of IL-1β expression in periodontal tissues of the Ligature + MDL-800 group, compared with Ligature + Saline group (Fig. 2D). Collectively, the above in vivo data demonstrated that MDL-800 administration attenuated periodontal tissue destruction in the setting of periodontitis.

Red arrows indicate the TRAP-positive multinucleated cells. AB, alveolar bone; PDL, periodontal ligament; M1, mandibular first molar.

MDL-800 reduces inflammatory cytokine production and ROS levels in LPS-stimulated HGFs

LPS serves as a key virulence determinant in Gram-negative bacteria, eliciting host immune reactions and contributing significantly to the onset of periodontitis22. Given that HGFs are the major cell type within gingival tissues and actively release a range of cytokines in response to pathogenic stimuli23, we used HGFs exposed to E. coli LPS to create a periodontal inflammatory environment in vitro. First, CCK-8 assay was applied to evaluate the impact of MDL-800 at various dilution levels ranging from 5 to 50 μmol/L on the proliferative ability of HGFs. We noted that the proliferation of HGFs remained unaffected by MDL-800 administration at concentrations up to 25 μmol/L (Fig. 3A). Thus, we examined MDL-800's anti-inflammatory effects on HGFs at doses ranging from 5 to 25 μmol/L. The RT-qPCR data showed that pre-administration of MDL-800 lowered levels of pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL1B, IL6, CXCL8, IL32, TNF and CCL2, in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3B). Additionally, we assessed the oxidative stress level in LPS-induced HGFs, which contributed to the establishment and progression of periodontitis24. The findings demonstrated that MDL-800 markedly decreased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and enabled nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (NRF2) to migrate to the nucleus (Fig. 3C and D), thus resulting in reducing oxidative damage and inflammation. Moreover, MDL-800 failed to restore the oxidative stress in SIRT6-silencing conditions (Supporting information Fig. S1). Altogether, we found that pre-treatment of HGFs with MDL-800 exerted an anti-inflammatory effect in an LPS-induced inflammatory microenvironment.

SIRT6 is critically involved in mediating the anti-inflammatory effects induced by MDL-800

Next, we examined how silencing SIRT6 affects the anti-inflammatory properties of MDL-800 in LPS-stimulated HGFs. We noted the expression level of SIRT6 markedly increased after MDL-800 administration in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the reduction of acetylated histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9Ac) confirmed the activation of SIRT6 in response to MDL-800 administration (Fig. 4A). Following this, SIRT6 was successfully silenced in HGFs with SIRT6 siRNA (Supporting information Fig. S1A and S1B). To further validate these findings, a CRISPR/Cas9-based RNP (ribonucleoprotein) approach was employed to achieve genomic knockout of SIRT6 in HGFs (Fig. 4B). RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that both the silencing and knockout of SIRT6 markedly attenuated the suppressive impact of MDL-800 on pro-inflammatory messengers in HGFs, including IL1B, IL6, CXCL8, IL32, TNF and CCL2 (Fig. 4C, Fig. S1C and S1D). These results collectively indicate that SIRT6 is essential for the anti-inflammatory effects of MDL-800 in HGFs.

Subsequently, to validate the key role of SIRT6 in periodontal tissue in vivo, we used Sirt6 siRNA to reduce SIRT6 expression (Fig. 4D and F). H&E staining and quantitative analysis of CEJ-ABC distance showed that Sirt6 silencing resulted in more severe bone resorption (Fig. 4E and G). IHC staining also revealed that IL-1β was upregulated after Sirt6 silencing (Fig. 4E), consistent with the mRNA expression levels of IL1B in gingival tissues (Fig. 4H). All these alterations could not be restored by MDL-800 treatment, which verified the key role of activating SIRT6 in the anti-inflammatory effects of MDL-800 in vivo.

Moreover, we conducted animal experiments using a potent and highly selective SIRT6 inhibitor (compound 11e)17. Micro-CT analysis and H&E staining showed that compared with Ligature + Saline group, Ligature + compound 11e group exhibited more severe periodontal destruction (Supporting information Fig. S2). Collectively, we reasonably propose that SIRT6 plays an essential role in MDL-800-mediated anti-inflammatory effects.

RNA-sequencing reveals critical transcriptome changes upon MDL-800 treatment in LPS-stimulated HGFs

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was undertaken to explore alterations in the gene expression characteristics of LPS-stimulated HGFs with and without MDL-800 treatment. Volcano plots first revealed 593 downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 49 upregulated DEGs with the stimulation of LPS (Fig. 5A, left panel). Then, a total of 37 DEGs were found to be downregulated, while 132 DEGs were upregulated in the group treated with both LPS and MDL-800 compared to the group treated with LPS alone (Fig. 5A, right panel). Subsequently, we performed KEGG functional enrichment analysis and noted that several inflammation-associated cell signaling pathways such as NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway and IL-17 signaling pathway were significantly upregulated after LPS treatment (Fig. 5B), while MDL-800 treatment remarkably inhibited these inflammatory responses (Fig. 5C). The heatmap further revealed that inflammation-associated genes were downregulated after MDL-800 treatment (Fig. 5D).

MDL-800 treatment inhibits AIM2 inflammasome activation

RNA-seq analysis further showed that significantly enriched genes in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway including AIM2, GBP1/GBP2/GBP4/GBP5, IL6, CCL2 and RIPK3, were upregulated in response to LPS stimulation but downregulated after MDL-800 treatment (Fig. 6A). Additionally, the AIM2 inflammasome has been observed to act as a central regulator of the occurrence and progression of inflammatory diseases, including periodontitis25. AIM2 and GBPs have been recognized as key factors in initiating the activation of AIM2 inflammasome, which is also intimately connected to IL-6, CCL2 and RIPK3 (Fig. 6B). AIM2 is an intracellular recognition sensor that triggers inflammasomes, and inflammasome activation is a strongly inflammatory process commonly prompted by pathogen infections26. Moreover, GBPs would directly facilitate the AIM2 inflammasome complex assembly27. The RNA-seq results were confirmed by RT-qPCR; however, there was no noticeable change in RIPK3 expression (Fig. 6C and Supporting information Fig. S3). Thus, we investigated whether its protective effects against periodontitis are associated with the inhibition of AIM2 inflammasome activation. Subsequently, we used LPS as the first signal and poly (dA:dT) (mimicking microbial DNA) as the second signal to stimulate AIM2 activation according to previous studies28. Subsequently, we conducted Western blot and ELISA analyses to evaluate protein levels of AIM2 and IL-1β (Fig. 6D). Our findings indicated the expression levels of AIM2 inflammasome components were remarkably downregulated after MDL-800 treatment relative to the LPS + poly (dA: dT) group (Fig. 6E). Moreover, SIRT6 silencing in HGFs resulted in an increase in AIM2 protein levels under inflammatory conditions (Fig. S1B). In vivo periodontitis experiments also showed that AIM2 was downregulated in Ligature + MDL-800 group while upregulated in Ligature + 11e group, relative to Ligature + Saline group (Supporting information Figs. S4 and S5). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that MDL-800 treatment could modulate AIM2 inflammasome activation in a SIRT6-dependent manner, contributing to ameliorating periodontal inflammation.

Discussion

Periodontitis is a disease of infection and inflammation resulting from an imbalance between oral microbiota and host immune defenses, and it is associated with various systemic conditions. Chronic and progressive periodontitis involves persistent inflammation, leading to host-driven degradation of periodontal soft and hard tissues29. Previous studies show that epigenetic modifications deposited on histones, such as histone acetylation, play a significant role in inflammation30.

MDL-800 is a first-in-class, highly selective allosteric activator of SIRT6, increasing its deacetylase activity by up to 22-fold via binding to a unique allosteric site. This selectivity distinguishes it from other SIRT6 modulators, such as S6 (an inhibitor) and other activators like MDL-801 and MDL-811, which may have different potency or target profiles16. Herein, we demonstrate for the first time that MDL-800 significantly reduced inflammatory reaction and alveolar bone resorption in experimental periodontitis. MDL-800 remarkably downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in HGFs by inhibiting AIM2 pathway in a SIRT6-dependent manner. These results emphasize that SIRT6 activation holds great promise in treating periodontitis.

First, the ligature-induced experimental periodontitis resulted in obvious periodontal destruction, including a decline in alveolar bone mass and impairment of the trabecular bone structure, which was consistent with previous studies31. However, these pathological effects were attenuated by MDL-800 treatment. Moreover, the results of histological staining and IHC staining revealed that MDL-800 could also reduce the damage to periodontal ligament fibers, formation of osteoclasts and expression of inflammatory factor IL-1β. SIRT6, an NAD+-dependent histone deacetylase, has gained significant attention for its role in transcriptional regulation related to longevity and inflammation32,33. A recent investigation demonstrated that a deficiency of SIRT6 in macrophages resulted in persistent inflammation and exacerbated periodontitis within a high-glucose environment, characterized by the accumulation of apoptotic neutrophils and an increased formation of neutrophil extracellular traps34, which was in line with our findings that pharmacologically activating SIRT6 could be a promising treatment for periodontal diseases. Additionally, MDL-800 has been recently applied in treating other inflammation-associated diseases including tubulointerstitial inflammation and fibrosis35, ischemic stroke13, heart failure with diabetes36 and cutaneous wound healing37, via activating SIRT6. A limitation of our work is that the use of systemic delivery precludes a conclusive distinction between local and systemic mechanisms of action. Consequently, evaluating the efficacy of localized MDL-800 administration in a clinically relevant large animal model represents an essential next step for translational development.

HGFs, the most predominant resident cells in the periodontium, could express plenty of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and IL-838, when exposed to virulence factors from periodontal infections, leading to periodontal tissue degradation. Additionally, unlike monocytes and macrophages that are recognised for developing LPS tolerance (a mechanism that curbs excessive or prolonged responses), HGFs could maintain inflammatory cytokine generation due to a lack of LPS tolerance, which implies that HGFs play crucial roles in the advancement of periodontal diseases23. Therefore, we next examined the effects of MDL-800 treatment on HGFs in the LPS-induced inflammatory environment. Specific histone acetylation patterns, including increased H3K9 acetylation levels in cells and tissues from periodontitis, have been reported7. Our findings, agreeing with previous research13, showed that MDL-800 could effectively reduce the expression level of H3K9Ac in HGFs in a time-dependent manner. qPCR findings further suggested that MDL-800 significantly reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine production in LPS-stimulated HGFs. Besides, in periodontal tissues, upregulation of ROS may be involved in promoting periodontal inflammation, matrix decomposition and bone absorption through the development of oxidative stress24. NRF2 is a crucial transcription factor that can reduce cell damage caused by ROS and maintain redox homeostasis39. In this study, we noted that MDL-800 could significantly reduce ROS levels in LPS-induced HGFs and activate NRF2 by promoting NRF2 to transfer to the nucleus. Moreover, SIRT6 knockdown abolished the potent anti-inflammation effects of MDL-800 in LPS-HGFs. Similar to our results, a recent report found that up-regulation of SIRT6 enhanced the viability and osteogenic differentiation of LPS-induced stem cells with suppression of inflammatory response14. These findings indicate that MDL-800 has therapeutic potential for periodontal regeneration under inflammatory conditions.

Inflammasomes are cytoplasmic multiprotein complexes facilitating the actuation of caspase-1, promoting the maturation and subsequent secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β in response to pathogenic stimuli40. Over 10 different inflammasomes have been well characterized in the pathogenesis of periodontitis with strong literature support26. AIM2, a cytosolic dsDNA sensor, was the first non-NLR family member recognized for its role in facilitating inflammasome assembly with subsequent release of inflammatory cytokines to initiate immune responses41,42. It has been well documented that numerous inflammatory diseases exhibit increased expression of AIM243,44, indicating a fundamental role for the DNA sensor AIM2 in regulating inflammatory responses. Recent research evidenced that AIM2 was expressed at an elevated level in gingival tissues from periodontitis25, suggesting that AIM2 is a potential risk gene for periodontitis45. In the present study, RNA-seq results revealed that AIM2 was significantly upregulated upon LPS stimulation but downregulated after MDL-800 treatment. In addition, the reduced expression of AIM2 and IL-1β at the protein level further confirmed that MDL-800 could inhibit AIM2 activation under inflammatory conditions. Results in this study also showed that SIRT6 silencing in HGFs resulted in an increase in AIM2 protein levels. In vivo results of experiment periodontitis further revealed a decreased expression of AIM2 in periodontal tissues after MDL-800 treatment. Although we have validated the essential role of SIRT6 using both siRNA-mediated interference and CRISPR/Cas9-based RNP knockout models, certain molecular nuances warrant further exploration. It remains to be fully elucidated whether SIRT6 activation leads to AIM2 downregulation through direct deacetylation of the AIM2 protein itself or via the modulation of an intermediary transcriptional regulator. Therefore, identifying the precise deacetylation targets of SIRT6 and their specific impact on the AIM2 signaling axis remains a key objective for future investigation to unequivocally map the downstream therapeutic mechanisms of MDL-800 in periodontitis.

Currently, there exists a clinical demand for methods to suppress inflammation, aimed at enhancing the long-term prognosis of teeth affected by periodontitis. The reversibility of epigenetic mechanisms renders them highly appealing targets for new treatment models within inflammatory diseases6.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that pharmacological activation of SIRT6 by MDL-800 effectively attenuates periodontal inflammation and associated alveolar bone resorption. Mechanistically, we identify the AIM2 inflammasome as a critical downstream effector of SIRT6-mediated anti-inflammatory actions, wherein MDL-800 suppresses AIM2 pathway activation in a SIRT6-dependent manner, both in ligature-induced murine periodontitis and in LPS-stimulated human gingival fibroblasts. Collectively, our findings on SIRT6 activator provide a novel approach for treating periodontitis.
