Phosphorus starvation-induced alternative splicing landscape of rice confirms the regulatory function of Pup1 QTL
Authors: Ratna Prabha, Karishma Seem, Amrender Kumar, K K Vinod, Trilochan Mohapatra, Suresh Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow phosphorus (P) use efficiency of rice, in spite of sufficient P present in soil (mainly as insoluble complex), and excessive use of phosphatic fertilizers by farmers may cause several issues including higher cost of cultivation and environmental pollution. Alternative splicing of pre-mRNA is one of the strategies for transcriptome/proteome diversity that help plants to optimize acquisition and mobilization of P. Comparative analysis of rice [Pusa-44 and its Near-Isogenic Line (NIL)-23] grown hydroponically under contrasting conditions (16 ppm and 0 ppm) until vegetative stage deciphered stress-induced alternative splicing regulated through Pup1 QTL. The modulation in alternative splicing in root and shoot of rice under stress can be attributed to the regulatory function of Pup1. In addition to a considerable increase in the number of genes showing up-regulated expression, ∼20 genes demonstrated differential expression as well as alternative splicing (with a decrease in root but increase in shoot) in NIL-23under stress. Increase in alternative splicing of transcripts for ‘iron-regulated bHLH transcription factor’, ‘regulation of P homeostasis’ in roots while ‘serine hydroxymethyltransferase’ and ‘iron-regulated bHLH transcription factor’ in shoot, particularly those associated with P homeostasis/P-starvation stress-responsive signaling pathway, were recorded in the case of NIL-23 under stress. Genes/transcripts, particularly those associated with biological process in roots, showing reversed (increased vs. decreased) level of splicing in NIL-23 (compared to that in its parent) on P-starvation confirmed regulatory role of the QTL. Better understanding of splicing event/gene/transcript and its regulation might provide novel strategies to modulate gene expression for nutrient homeostasis and enhancing stress resilience in crop plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07379-9.Keywords: Oryza sativa, Alternative splicing, Gene expression, P-starvation, Abiotic stress

Introduction

Demand for rice (Oryza sativa L.) is continuously increasing with the increasing global population, as it is one the most important cereals for global food security providing food to half of the global population [1–3]. According to a recent report by FAO, the global production of rice in 2024 was 543 million tons, while its global demand was about 597 million tons in 2025 [4]. Ensuring adequate/sustainable rice production to meet the demand of continuously increasing human population has become a challenge for most rice-growing/consuming countries. Various abiotic stresses cause adverse effects on growth and development of rice, resulting in a decline in overall production [2, 5–7]. Understanding the complex regulatory mechanism for tolerance to abiotic stresses is necessary to develop climate-resilient rice varieties [2, 8, 9].

Phosphorus (P) is one of the vital components of nucleic acids, cell membranes and enzymes crucial for various physio-biochemical processes like photosynthesis, carbohydrate/energy metabolism, redox homeostasis, and signaling. Limited availability of inorganic phosphate (Pi) presents crucial restrictions on crop productivity. Despite the abundance of total P in soil, it is one of the major limiting nutrients for plants owing to its less solubility, higher adsorption, and formation of insoluble complexes with cations of Aluminum, Calcium, Iron, etc. [10–12]. To deal with low P availability, plants deploy various adaptive mechanisms for P acquisition, mobilization, and utilization to minimize the adverse effects of low P stress on growth and productivity [12]. Because of a prevailing myth that higher usage of fertilizers increases crop yield, excessive phosphatic fertilizer might be utilized in crops, which not only causes environmental pollution (eutrophication) but also increases the cost of cultivation [8, 13].

Reducing the growth of shoot (but increased growth of roots) is one of the common adaptive (physiological) strategies of plants to cope with nutrient deficiency to let the photosynthates translocate to roots for prolific growth [12]. Other important strategies for tolerance to low P stress are biochemical and molecular adaptations, including reprograming/fine-tuning of gene expression through alternative splicing of pre-mRNAs, epitranscriptomic and epigenetic modulations etc. [14–16]. Alteration in the composition of membrane lipids is another vital strategy deployed by plants to improve P use efficiency (PUE) for maintaining cellular P homeostasis [17–19]. While some of the rice genotypes can perform better under limited P availability, others are sensitive to P deficiency. Importance of root system architecture in low P tolerance has been explored to a great extent and Kasalath (an aus landrace of rice) has been identified as P-deficiency tolerant rice genotype because of possessing a major quantitative trait locus (QTL) for phosphorus uptake 1 (Pup1). Previous efforts on identification of Pup1 QTL-based mechanisms for better PUE utilizing near-isogenic lines (NILs) revealed its major role in root development resulting in about 3-fold higher P uptake [20, 21]. Transcriptome analysis under P-starvation stress has been carried out in different plant species including Arabidopsis, rice, wheat, barley, maize, etc. [3, 22–28]. However, majority of the studies focused on identification of differentially expressed genes associated with transporters, phosphatases, transcription processes, carbon metabolism/photosynthesis, lipid metabolism, cell wall remodeling, etc. Many of the studies [23, 24, 26–31] were carried out with imposition of a short-term P-starvation/deficiency stress for investigating the effects, which is not the case in a field unless phosphatic fertilizers are not applied in soil. Comparatively longer stress imposition enables morpho-physiological studies on the changes in plants.

Alternative splicing (production of different mRNA transcripts from the same gene on differential processing of the introns and exons in a pre-mRNA) is a widespread and important regulatory mechanism in multicellular organisms, including plants and animals. This significantly expands functional diversity of the gene, contributing to the complexity of regulatory mechanisms, and functions of the encoded proteins [32–34]. It is a highly regulated process that needs both cis- as well as trans-acting elements. The cis-regulatory elements are present in the exonic/intronic region to which the trans-acting elements (transcription factor, microRNA, and other regulatory RNA/protein) bind and act as highly conserved mechanism in eukaryotes [2, 34, 35]. Alternative splicing has been involved in functional evolution of eukaryotic genomes, as it increases proteome diversity, functional capability, and adaptability of the organisms [2, 35–37]. Changes in relative transcript abundance and differential transcript usage (DTU) are important phenomena in differential gene expression. A gene may get spliced differently under different environmental conditions and relative abundance of a transcript might vary irrespective of any change in the overall expression of the gene. DTU indicates variation in the abundance/distribution of transcript of a gene under different conditions. DTU may lead to different functional consequences like switching from a protein-coding transcript to a non-coding transcript or even switching between the transcripts having diverse functions [38]. Such disproportionate usage of alternatively spliced transcripts under stress has been demonstrated in Arabidopsis thaliana [39].

Alternative splicing play important role in fine-tuning the expression of gene under stress [2, 37, 40] through differential joining/skipping of exons/introns [41] and generating different mRNAs/proteins from a single gene. Such splicing events can be classified into five different types: alternative 3′ splice sites (A3SS), alternative 5′ splice sites (A5SS), skipped exon (SE), retained intron (RI), and mutually exclusive exons (MX) [42]. While RI has been identified as the most common alternative splicing event in plants, SE is most commonly reported in animals [2, 35, 43]. This distinction in alternative splicing between animal and plant is mainly due to architectural differences like the presence of much shorter introns (that facilitate more efficient/rapid splicing) and the intron-defined splicing process in plants [2, 41]. RI-mediated generation of alternatively spliced transcripts of a gene in response to stress was reported earlier in Arabidopsis [2, 43].

Analyses of RNA-seq data to identify alternative splicing events in rice under abiotic stresses including drought [44, 45], salinity [46, 47], chilling stress [48], light stress [49, 50], hypoxia [51], metallic stress [52, 53], nutrient/mineral stress [41], and biotic stresses [2, 54–57] have been reported earlier. Studies identified alternative splicing to fine-tune expression of stress-associated genes in roots and shoot of rice [2, 58]. Spliceosome-related proteins (Lsm8 and U1A) were reported to be up-regulated, while splicing factors like SPF and SR were down-regulated in Oryza rufipogon under low P stress [2, 59]. Increased expression of RNA splicing factor and translation elongation factor indicated that alternative splicing is a stress-induced post-transcriptional regulatory process adopted by the wild rice under stress [2]. Despite the prevalence of alternative splicing events and their role in stress responses, the regulatory mechanisms and function of the splice variants are meagerly understood in plants [60].

In the present study, comparative analyses of RNA-seq data of a pair of contrasting rice [Pusa-44 (P-deficiency sensitive) and its Near-Isogenic Line (NIL)−23 (P-deficiency tolerant)] genotypes grown hydroponically under control and P-starvation until vegetative stage of growth were performed to identify various alternative splicing events. The study aimed at deciphering P starvation-induced changes in alternative splicing to unravel regulatory function of Pup1 QTL in fine-tuning gene expression in rice.

Results

Effects of P‑starvation on morphology and growth of plant

Significant morphological changes (in height and biomass production) were recorded in growth of the plants due to P-starvation (Fig. 1). P-starvation caused stunted growth, reduced tillering, shorter/erect leaves of plants. Growth of the NIL-23 plants (harboring Pup1 QTL) was comparatively better than that observed for Pusa-44 plants under P-starved as well as sufficient conditions. Visual observations on growth of roots indicated significant reduction in the number and biomass of roots in both the rice genotypes due to P-starvation (Fig. 2A−D). Moreover, the number of root tips, root forks, projected root area, and root surface area were significantly higher in the case of NIL-23 compared to that recorded for Pusa-44 under P-starvation (Fig. 2E−H).

Differential expression of genes under P-starvation

Analyses of 16 datasets for RNA-seq libraries (mapped on the rice reference genome) for two tissues from two rice genotypes grown under P-starvation stress (0 ppm Pi) over control (16 ppm Pi) indicated the number of trimmed reads to vary from 8.5 to 13.1 million. The mapping efficiency of data ranged from 65.7 to 95.2% (comparatively lesser reads and mapping efficiency for roots, particularly of Pusa-44 under stress) (Supplementary Table S1). Comparative expression analysis resulted in the identification of 4719 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) containing 2,393 up-regulated and 2,326 down-regulated in roots of Pusa-44 (stress-sensitive genotype) grown under stress. Whereas, in roots of NIL-23 (stress-tolerant genotype) grown under P-starvation stress showed 4,614 DEGs with more (3,260) up-regulated and lesser (1,354) down-regulated genes. In contrast, shoot of Pusa-44 showed only 2,983 DEGs with 1,991 up-regulated and 992 down-regulated genes under stress; whereas, 8,513 DEGs with more number of up-regulated (5,626) as well as more down-regulated (2,887) genes were recorded in shoot of NIL-23 under P-starvation (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Identification of DET, DTU and DAS genes

Analysis of the datasets using 3D RNA-seq tool, resulted in identification of the differentially expressed transcript (DET), differential transcript usage (DTU), and differential alternative splicing (DAS) of genes under different P regimes in the rice genotypes. This indicated only less than 30 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that were alternatively spliced also. In Pusa-44, a total of 2,481 DE and 451 DAS genes were recorded, while it was 3128 DE and 390 DAS in the case of NIL-23 under stress (Supplementary Table S2). Interestingly, the number of genes showing DE + DAS and DAS only in shoot were more than that recorded in roots of NIL-23; whereas the numbers were lesser in shoot than that in roots of Pusa-44. Similarly, only some (less than 170) of the transcripts showed differential expression as well as differential transcript usage. In the case of Pusa-44, 1834 DETs were identified in roots while 978 DETs in the shoot with more DET + DTU (163) in roots (compared to only 90 DET + DTU in shoot). On the contrary, the number of DET + DTU was more (134) in shoot of NIL-23 (compared to only 90 DET + DTU in shoot of Pusa-44) under stress (Supplementary Table S2). The DEGs, DAS genes, DETs, and DTU observed under P-starvation were further divided into up- and down-regulated groups (Fig. 3).

Out of the 263 DAS genes, 141 were up-regulated and 129 were down-regulated in roots of Pusa-44, while out of the 136 DAS genes in NIL-23 roots, 73 were up-regulated and 63 were down-regulated. In contrast, 90 DAS genes were up-regulated and 83 were down-regulated in shoot of Pusa-44, compared to 99 up-regulated and 143 down-regulated DAS genes in shoot of NIL-23 (Fig. 3A). Thus, a greater number of transcripts showed differential transcript usage (in comparison to differential alternative splicing) in the rice genotypes under P-starvation stress (Supplementary Fig. 2). A similar trend for DTU was observed in NIL-23, particularly for the shoot. Among the 475 DTU in shoot of NIL-23, 224 transcripts showed up-regulated expression while 251 transcripts showed down-regulated expression (Fig. 3B), which were > 60 units higher from those recorded in the shoot of Pusa-44. Considerably higher numbers (> 200 down- regulated and > 500 units up-regulated) of DETs, particularly in shoot, was recorded in NIL-23, compared to those observed in the case of Pusa-44 (Fig. 3).

When the genes showing DE as well as DAS were analyzed, it was recorded that 23 genes in roots of NIL-23, while 28 genes were recorded in shoot of NIL-23, compared to 30 genes in roots and only 15 genes in shoot of Pusa-44 (Supplementary Tables S2). Thus, 45 genes showed both DE and DAS in Pusa-44 (Fig. 4A), which was more (51 genes showing both DE and DAS) in the case of NIL-23 (Fig. 4B). Therefore, alternative splicing of differentially expressed genes in NIL-23 (harboring Pup1 QTL) was more compared to that recorded in the case of Pusa-44 under P-starvation (Fig. 5). These indicate important role of Pup1 QTL in resilience to P-starvation stress, particularly through DAS/DTU.

Among the DE + DAS genes, Os01g0106900, Os02g0120800, Os02g0725200, Os03g0250200, Os03g0379300, Os03g0738400, Os04g0488600 and Os10g0567400 showed up-regulated expression in shoot of NIL-23, while several others showed down-regulated expression (Supplementary Table S3). The up-regulated Os10g0567400 gene (associated with oxidation-reduction process) was transcribed in three different transcripts (i) Os10t0567400- (regulation of photosynthesis, chlorophyll degradation and ROS scavenging), (ii) Os10t0567400-02 (isoform of chlorophyllide a oxygenase), and (iii) Os10t0567400-03 (another isoform of chlorophyllide a oxygenase), which perform important role in photosynthetic process. Moreover the up-regulated expression of DE + DAS genes namely Os03g0379300 (iron regulated bHLH TF) and Os03g0738400 (serine hydroxymethyltransferase 1) in NIL-23 favor stress tolerance to the plants. Among the down-regulated genes in shoot of NIL-23 (Supplementary Table S3), for example SPX domain protein 1 (Os06g0603600) and RAC/ROP-type GTPase 6 (Os02g0120800) associated with negative regulation for phosphate homeostasis and abiotic stress tolerance, favor its better performance under stress. Furthermore, the transcripts of Os03g0379300 (basic Helix-loop-helix protein 63) were up-regulated and alternatively spliced in shoot as well as roots of NIL-23 (compared to that recorded in Pusa-44) under stress (Supplementary Table S3) indicating the regulatory function of Pup1 QTL through differential expression/splicing of genes in stress resilience.

Of the DE + DAS genes, > 70% were down-regulated in roots of NIL-23 (Supplementary Table S3). Among these, four genes Os02g0120800 (RAC/ROP-Type GTPase 6), Os03g0379300 (bHLH transcription factor), Os04g0517100, and Os12g0265100 were commonly differentially expressed/spliced in shoot as well as root of NIL-23. Comparison of expression/splicing of genes/transcripts in Pusa-44 and NIL-23 in response to P-starvation indicated that only a few (6) genes/transcripts were commonly differentially expressed and alternatively spliced in both Pusa-44 and NIL-23 (Supplementary Table S3). These indicate that NIL-23 (harboring Pup1 QTL) responds differently at gene/transcript level for expression/splicing (compared to the responses of Pusa-44) under P-starvation due to introgression of the QTL.

Differential alternative splicing events in rice under stress

On an average, > 8,800 alternative splicing events could be identified for each sample/rice genotype. While the highest number (> 11,000) of alternative splicing events was recorded in Pusa-44 shoot under P-starvation, the lowest number (∼6,000) was recorded in roots of Pusa-44 under stress (Supplementary Table S4). Among the splicing events, Alternative 3’ Splice Sites was observed to be the most abundant, particularly in shoot of Pusa-44 grown under P-starvation. Moreover, among the splicing events, Mutually Exclusive Exons (MX) splicing was recorded to be the least, particularly in NIL-23 shoot under stress (Supplementary Table S4). Comparison of alternative splicing events in roots of the rice genotypes indicated increase in RI, alternative exon first (AF), alternative splice site 3′ (A3′) and 5′ (A5′), but decrease in skipping exon (SE) in NIL-23 (stress tolerant genotype), compared to that observed in Pusa-44 (stress sensitive genotype) under P-starvation (Fig. 6). But in case of shoot, a considerable increase in RI, but decrease in SE, A3′, A5′ events was recorded in NIL-23 (compared to that recorded in Pusa-44) under stress (Fig. 7). Thus, a decrease in alternative splicing events was recorded under stress in the rice genotypes, particularly in the shoot of NIL-23 (Supplementary Table S4).

Gene ontology analysis of transcripts showing alternative splicing

Considering a significant change (> 35%) in the number of transcripts showing DAS, gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis was performed to categorize them into biological process, molecular function and cellular component. Considerable change (reduction) in the number of transcripts showing AE, AS, and MX events (while an increase in the transcripts showing RI and SE events) was recorded in roots of NIL-23 under stress (Fig. 8). The number of transcripts (associated with different GO terms) showing alternative splicing increased by > 5 times, particularly for RI and SE events, in NIL-23 on P-starvation compared to that observed under control (Supplementary Fig. S3). The changes in number of SE events in transcripts for different GO terms under P-starvation was > 15 times higher in roots of NIL-23, compared to that recorded in the case of Pusa-44 under stress. A considerable reduction in the number of events for all five types of splicing was recorded in NIL-23 roots under control condition (compared to that recorded in Pusa-44 roots) for two GO terms (catalytic activity and binding) of molecular Function category. However, 5 and 16 times increase in the number of RI and SE splicing events, respectively, was recorded in NIL-23 roots under stress, which was opposite to the trend recorded in roots of Pusa-44 (Supplementary Fig. S4). A significant increase (∼20 times) in the number of splicing events for the transcripts associated with different GO terms was recorded in roots of NIL-23 under stress (compared to that under control), which was in opposite trend (particularly for SE events) to that recorded in roots of Pusa-44 under stress (Supplementary Fig. S5).

A significant (∼2 times) decrease in the number of genes showing alternative splicing through different events (except for ∼10 times reduction in SE events) was recorded in shoot of NIL-23 on P-starvation (Fig. 9). Biological regulation, regulation of biological process, cellular process, localization, metabolic process, and response to stimulus were the most enriched GO terms (biological process), of which alternative splicing of transcripts associated with metabolic processes and cellular processes were more abundant in shoot of the rice genotypes. A decrease in the number of alternative splicing of the genes associated with different GO terms was recorded in shoot of the rice genotypes, which was comparatively more in the case of NIL-23 under stress. Detailed GO enrichment analysis at level 3 for ‘biological process’ in shoot indicated ∼1.5 time reduction in the number of splicing events (except for the SE event showing ∼12 times reduction) for most of the (twelve) GO terms in NIL-23 under stress (Supplementary Fig. S6). Moreover, the genes/transcripts associated with ‘molecular function’ showing alternative splicing demonstrated ∼0.3 to 10 times decrease in the number of splicing for most of the events in shoot of rice genotypes (comparatively more in NIL-23) under P-starvation. However, splicing of the transcripts for the GO term transporter activity through SE event was recorded exclusively in shoot of NIL-23 under stress (Supplementary Fig. S7). Detailed GO enrichment analysis using BLAST2GO at level 3 for ‘molecular function’ in shoot indicated reduction in the number of splicing events, with a drastic reduction in SE, for most (nine) of the GO terms which was matching with that recorded for ‘biological process’ in NIL-23 under stress (Supplementary Fig. S8). While ∼14% lesser splicing events was recorded in shoot of NIL-23 compared to that in Pusa-44 shoot, ∼40% reduction was recorded in shoot of NIL-23 under stress compared to that under control.

Comparative analysis of GO terms associated with alternate splicing

Comparison for the GO terms belonging to ‘biological process’ in the rice genotypes for shoot and root tissues indicated that most of the GO terms present in shoot (Supplementary Fig. S6) were also present in roots (Supplementary Fig. S3). However, four GO terms (i.e., catabolic process, cell communication, transmembrane transport, and signal transduction) were additionally present in roots. None of these showed alternative splicing in NIL-23 under stress. Similarly, comparative analysis for the GO terms belonging to ‘molecular function’ in the rice genotypes indicated that the GO term catalytic activity and binding were present in both shoot (Supplementary Fig. S7) and root tissues, but the term structural molecule activity showed splicing exclusively in roots of NIL-23 with a decrease in MX events under stress (Supplementary Fig. S4), whereas transporter activity showed splicing exclusively in shoot of NIL-23 under stress (Supplementary Fig. S4, S7). Therefore, further comparative analyses of transcripts/splicing events for the GO terms associated with ‘biological process’ and ‘molecular function’ in roots and shoot of rice genotypes (grown at 0 ppm and 16 ppm Pi) were performed.

GO analysis of differentially expressed and/or alternatively spliced genes

While the number of genes differentially expressed only in roots under P-starvation stress was ∼50 units higher for Pusa-44 (compared to that recorded for NIL-23), it was considerably higher (∼700 units) in the case of NIL-23 shoot (Fig. 10A). On the contrary, the genes showing only differential alternative splicing was ∼100 units lesser in roots of NIL-23 (compared to that in Pusa-44) under P-starvation, it was ∼60 units higher in the shoot of NIL-23 (Fig. 10B). Thus, a significant difference was recorded in DAS only genes in NIL-23 (with reduction in roots but increase in shoot) compared to that recorded in Pusa-44 under P-starvation stress. A similar trend was recorded for DE + DAS genes (with 7 units lesser) in roots of NIL-23, while considerably higher (13 units) DE + DAS genes in the shoot of NIL-23, compared to that recorded in the case of Pusa-44 (Fig. 10C). Thus, significant difference in DE + DAS genes was recorded in NIL-23 (with a significant reduction in roots but increase in shoot) compared to that in Pusa-44 under stress.

Genes for ‘biological process’ showing alternative splicing in root

Gene ontology of the transcripts associated with GO terms of ‘biological process’ and showing alternative splicing were analyzed in roots of the rice genotypes. Out of 69 GO terms showing alternative splice site (AS) events in the rice genotypes, 56 terms were common in both NIL-23 and Pusa-44 under control as well as stress. While four GO terms (organelle organization, cellular macromolecule biosynthetic process, DNA metabolic process and Proteolysis) were detected exclusively under control, three terms (viz. cellular protein metabolic process, cellular protein modification process and transcription-DNA-templated) were recorded under stress as well as control in NIL-23 (Fig. 11). Similarly, in the case of alternative exons (AE), nine GO terms (viz. defense response, cellular amide metabolic process, organonitrogen compound biosynthetic process, peptide metabolic process, DNA metabolic process, cellular macromolecule biosynthetic process, peptide biosynthetic process, translation and amide biosynthetic process) were exclusively present in NIL-23 under control, four GO terms (viz. cellular protein metabolic process, regulation of transcription: DNA-templated’, cellular protein modification process, transcription: DNA-templated) were recorded at both control as well as stress in roots of NIL-23. With respect to the mutually exclusive exon (MX) splicing event, one of the GO terms (viz. transcription: DNA-templated) showed exclusive presence under stress (0 ppm Pi) while 25 GO terms were exclusively present under control (16 ppm Pi) in NIL-23. However, for SE and RI events, eight GO terms (viz. transmembrane transport, small molecule metabolic process, cellular protein metabolic process, organelle organization, proteolysis, cellular protein modification process, regulation of transcription: DNA-templated, transcription: DNA-templated) and four terms (viz. cellular protein metabolic process, cellular protein modification process, regulation of transcription: DNA-templated, transcription: DNA-templated), respectively, were exclusively present under control (16 ppm Pi) in roots of NIL-23 (Fig. 11).

Genes for ‘molecular function’ showing alternative splicing in root

GO analysis of the genes/transcripts (showing AS, AE, MX, RI, and SE splicing events) associated with ‘molecular function’ in roots of the rice genotypes indicated three GO terms (viz. catalytic activity on nucleic acid, transition metal ion binding, and zinc ion binding) exhibiting AS event exclusively in roots of NIL-23 under control, whereas the GO term protein binding showed AS event exclusively in roots of NIL-23 under stress (Supplementary Fig. S9). Similarly, in the case of AE, the term protein binding was recorded exclusively in NIL-23 under stress (Supplementary Fig. S8). For MX event, four GO terms (viz. structural molecule activity, catalytic activity on nucleic acid, structural constituent of ribosome, and enzyme binding) were recorded exclusively in roots of NIL-23 under control; whereas, MX splicing of transcripts for the term protein binding was recorded under control as well as stress. Similarly for SE event, one GO term (protein binding) was recorded in NIL-23 roots under control as well as stress; whereas it was also recorded for the gene associated with the GO term catalytic activity on nucleic acid exclusively in roots of NIL-23 under control (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Genes for ‘biological process’ showing alternative splicing in shoot

GO analysis of the genes/transcripts showing alternative splicing and associated with ‘biological process’ in shoot of the rice genotypes indicated their important role in stress management. All the events (AS, AE, MX, RI, and SE) were involved in alternative splicing of transcripts for at least one GO term exclusively represented in NIL-23 shoot under stress (Fig. 12). Transcripts for the GO term small molecule metabolic process showed AS, AE, RI as well as MX events exclusively in shoot of NIL-23 under stress. The transcripts for one GO term showed SE splicing exclusively in NIL-23 shoot under control condition, while another GO term showed SE splicing exclusively in the shoot under stress (Fig. 12).

GO analysis of the genes/transcripts associated with molecular Function indicated 31−33 terms to have differential alternative splicing through AS, AE, MX, RI, and SE events in shoot of the rice genotypes (Supplementary Fig. S10). However, two GO terms (transporter activity and catalytic activity on nucleic acid) showed splicing through SE event exclusively in NIL-23 shoot under P-starvation stress (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Discussion

Phosphorus is a well-known component of several biomolecules and crucial for numerous physio-biochemical processes in living organisms. Plants have evolved with several regulatory systems to control growth/development in responses to nutrient deficiencies [41]. Deficiency of mineral nutrient affects crop productivity as well as quality of the produce [5, 61]. Our comparative analyses demonstrated reduction in root and shoot growth/biomass of the rice genotypes on P-starvation. Moreover, comparatively better growth of NIL-23 (harboring Pup1 QTL) under P-starved (as well as sufficient condition) can be attributed to the introgressed QTL (Fig. 1). Pusa-44 and NIL-23 (being the near isogenic line for Pup1 QTL) grown under similar experimental set-up and analyzed for comparative changes under P-starvation reiterated the role of QTL in P uptake. It has been demonstrated earlier that Pusa-44 requires higher (∼2 times) input of P (in hydroponic culture) to attain a similar growth/development, compared to that required by NIL-23 [61]. Better growth of NIL-23 plant at a given P input (particularly under P-starvation stress) indicated better P use efficiency of the genotype (Pusa-44) on introgression of Pup1 QTL. Favorable changes in root system architectural parameters also confirmed better adaptability of NIL-23, which could be evidenced by better survival of NIL-23 under P-starvation, compared to that recorded for Pusa-44 (lacking Pup1 QTL) (Fig. 2). These confirmed efficacy of the QTL in P uptake/utilization efficiency, which corroborate with the earlier reports [3, 8]. Since the stress-tolerant genotype (NIL-23) used in the present study has been a near-isogenic line for Pup1 QTL (introgressed in the recurrent parent Pusa-44), the observed differences in P-use efficiency can be attributed to the QTL. In view of the presence of only a few protein-coding genes on the QTL [13, 62], it was hypothesized that the QTL might have important roles in regulatory functions [63]. Therefore, to decipher regulatory function of Pup1, particularly through alternative splicing, transcriptomic data for the contrasting rice genotypes [grown under most divergent (P-sufficient and -starved) conditions] were analyzed. As reported earlier [13], the plants failed to thrive beyond 60 days of hydroponic culture under P-starvation, plant tissue samples were collected at the age of 45 days.

Transcriptomic analysis has been extensively used to investigate the responses of rice to P-starvation/deficiency [3, 8, 13, 62–65]. Differential expression of the genes, we observed in this study, has been consistent with those reported earlier [3, 13, 41, 63]. Comparatively lesser reads/poor quality and mapping efficiency of the RNA-seq data for roots in the case of stress-sensitive genotypes under P-starvation stress, align with the necessity of P in synthesis of nucleic acid, transcription process, growth as well as development of plant (particularly at vegetative stage of growth). A considerably higher number of up-regulated genes in shoot as well as root of NIL-23 on P-starvation must be responsible for better performance under stress. Up-regulation of genes like expansins, glycosyl transferases, and xyloglucan galactosyl transferases, etc. (associated with root system architecture) in NIL-23 (stress-tolerant genotype) under stress corroborate with earlier findings [3, 13, 63, 66]. The observed organ-specific alternative splicing, playing important role in regulating/fine-tuning gene expression as well as in functional complexity, corroborate with the earlier reports [52, 67].

Some of the genes/transcripts identified to be differentially expressed under P-starvation were also recorded to show DTU (Supplementary Table S2), which indicate differential responses of NIL-23 at the transcript level, compared to that for Pusa-44, mainly due to the introgression of Pup1 QTL. Moreover, the number of DE + DTU and DTU only genes showed tissue-specific changes (∼2 and ∼1.5 times, respectively) on P-starvation, with an opposite trend between the rice genotypes. Our comparative analyses indicated that DAS as well as DTU play important roles in stress tolerance, which has been crucial for in depth understanding of the regulatory functions of the QTL under stress. Similar observations on the involvement of DAS and DTU were reported in rice under N-starvation stress [68]. Genome-wide analysis of DTU in Arabidopsis suggested that genes showing no DTU are often associated with housekeeping functions, whereas the genes showing DTU are more often associated with regulatory functions [69]. Different genes/GO terms related to metabolic processes showed dynamic transcript usage under P-starvation in NIL-23. The transcriptional dynamics observed in genotype, tissue, and condition-specific manner in rice (Supplementary Table S2) reiterates regulatory function of the QTL and corroborate with the earlier findings under nutrient deficiency stress [68].

The higher number of differentially expressed genes than those alternatively spliced genes observed in this study under P-starvation has been reported earlier on nutrient deficiency in rice [41]. Our comparative analysis indicated a decrease in alternative splicing events under stress (0 ppm Pi) in comparison to that recorded under control (16 ppm Pi), particularly in tolerant genotype (NIL-23). The importance of alternative splicing events in stress tolerance could be deciphered on comparative analysis. This indicated an overall increase in RI and AS events, but decrease in MX and SE events in roots (Fig. 6) as well as in shoot (Fig. 7) of NIL-23 under P-starvation, which corroborate with the earlier reports in Arabidopsis, Nicotiana tabacum, wheat, tea, and rice [6, 41, 70–72]. While a decrease in total alternative splicing events was recorded in shoot, an increase was recorded in roots of NIL-23 under stress. Moreover, the number of splicing events was recorded to be opposite in shoot and root of Pusa-44 compared to that recorded in the case of NIL-23 (Supplementary Table S4). NIL-23 being a near-isogenic line for Pup1 in Pusa-44 background, our finding on changes (opposite trend) in alternative splicing in these two genotypes under stress reiterate the earlier report on regulatory function of the QTL [13, 63]. While many of the studies on alternative splicing in rice were carried out at seedling/early stage of plant growth on imposition of stress for a shorter period using single genotype of plant [41, 47, 53], this study utilized root as well as shoot from the plants grown under most contrasting (P-sufficient and P-starved) conditions until vegetative stage of plant growth (> 30 days of stress).

Earlier studies under nutrient starvation/deficiency aimed at understanding transcriptional regulation to decipher molecular responses of plants with respect to transcription factors and signal transduction [73–75]. Now transcriptomic data are being used for the identification of broad range of alternative splicing events in plants, wherein 40−60% of the intron-possessing genes exhibit at least one type of splicing event [41, 67, 73–76]. Alternative splicing has been reported earlier to play important role in regulating P uptake and mobilization in rice under phosphorus/multiple nutrient starvation stress [41]. Our investigation focused on the regulatory function of Pup1 QTL utilizing near-isogenic line of rice. The observed difference in the number of genes demonstrating DE and/or DAS, indicated important role of the QTL in responses of plants to P-starvation. The findings corroborate with earlier reports [68, 77] on smaller overlap between DE and DAS genes under nutrient deficiency. Such overlap for a small number of genes emphasizes the need of diversity in regulatory function under nutrient deficiency [41]. These signify various regulatory mechanisms operative at the transcription and the mRNA splicing levels, supporting the fact that environmental cues modulate gene expression through various mechanisms [34].

While RI splicing has been proposed to be common in plants, SE has been reported among animals [39, 41, 78] possibly because of the facts that plants possess smaller introns and intron-defined splicing mechanism [41, 67]. However, our observations on significant (5−25%) increase in RI splicing event under P-starvation in roots as well as shoot of NIL-23 corroborate with the earlier reports in plants under stress [73, 79, 80]. These findings well corroborate with a study by Dong et al. [41], wherein RI splicing was reported to be highly enriched event in rice under P-starvation. Moreover, RI splicing has been reported to be one of the common events in plant, particularly in response to abiotic stresses [2, 43]. While a significant decrease in overall SE events was recorded in NIL-23 (Figs. 6 and 7), increased splicing through SE was recorded for the genes/transcripts associated with biological process (particularly cellular process, metabolic process, and response to stimulus) in roots of NIL-23 under stress (Fig. 8). This confirms the stress-induced regulatory function of Pup1 QTL through alternative splicing.

Our comparative analyses demonstrated that alternative splicing of genes helps in fine-tuning the expression of gene, particularly those associated with metabolic processes under nutrient deficiency. The GO terms associated with enzymes (hydrolases, transferases, catalases, and other catalysts acting on protein/nucleic acids) involved in metabolite pathways as well as the term ‘response to stimulus’ were significantly enriched with DAS genes in NIL-23 on P-starvation. The finding corroborates with report on differential splicing of genes associated with metabolism in rice under nitrogen deficiency [68]. Phosphorylation of several splicing (regulatory) factors and spliceosomal proteins has been reported to play crucial roles in pre-mRNA splicing [81]. Therefore, P-starvation might alter phosphorylation status, and thus function, of splicing regulators/proteins [60].

Among the GO terms that showed differential expression/splicing in roots and shoot of NIL-23 under P-starvation, the most abundant terms were cellular process, metabolic process, biological regulation, regulation of biological process, response to stimulus, localization, etc. (Figures 8 and 9). However, the term localization was the least abundant across the datasets (Fig. 9). Our findings on enrichment of the GO terms ‘membrane transport’ and ‘signaling’ in NIL-23 are justified by the fact that membranes and transmembrane transport control the movement or concentration of solutes across the cellular compartments affecting complex interactions among the lipids, proteins, carbohydrates, and signaling molecules [82]. Similarly, signaling involves transmission of information within a biological system, which initiates with active signal molecule and ends with triggered cellular response, generally to regulate transcription and/or downstream cellular/metabolic processes. Among the alternative splicing events, MX was recorded in the least number of transcripts in roots, while SE was recorded to be the least (showing drastic reduction) in shoot of NIL-23 under stress. Several transcripts associated with metabolic processes (including cellular metabolic process, primary metabolic process, nitrogenous compound metabolic process, and organic compound metabolic process) showed opposite trend of alternative splicing (particularly for RI and SE events) between Pusa-44 and NIL-23 on P-starvation (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Contrasting/diverse trend of splicing through SE and RI events observed in Pusa-44 and NIL-23 under P-sufficient (16 ppm Pi) and P-starved (0 ppm Pi) conditions reiterates the stress-induced regulatory functions of Pup1. This was true for the GO terms engaging binding activities, wherein RI and SE splicing events were noticeably reduced, particularly in shoot of NIL-23 under P-starvation (Supplementary Fig. S8). The term binding refers to selective/non-covalent (sometimes stoichiometric) interaction between molecules involving one or more specific sites that create diversification under stress. Alternate splicing affects protein diversity by the formation of different transcripts from a single gene, which can serve not only as an energy-saving strategy [12] but also as a necessary strategy to improve P-use efficiency in shoot of NIL-23 under stress.

The events of alternative splicing may change according to the environmental cues to facilitate quicker responses/adjustment in abundance/diversity of stress-responsive proteins [2, 36, 40]. Comparative analysis of alternative splicing events have been reported to help understanding the adaptability of plants under diverse environmental conditions [2, 83], which could be linked with evolutionary process. Our observations on up-regulated expression of the transcripts showing alternative splicing in NIL-23 under P-starvation [e.g., Fe-regulated bHLH transcription factor (TF) and phosphate homeostasis in root, while serine hydroxymethyltransferase and iron-regulated bHLH TF in shoot] producing multiple protein isoforms from a single gene corroborate with the earlier reports on abiotic stress tolerance in rice. Two alternatively spliced transcripts for the gene (Os05g0557700) involved in P homeostasis [negative regulator of Pi-starvation response-signaling pathway (Os05t0557700-01) and a Pi starvation-induced isoform (Os05t0557700-02)], while three transcripts for the gene (Os03g0738400) [involved in photorespiration: regulation of abiotic stress tolerance (Os03t0738400-01), similar to serine hydroxymethyltransferase (Os03t0738400-02 and Os03t0738400-03)] play important roles in environmental/nutrient stress tolerance. Twenty-two serine/threonine genes reported in rice help respond to a variety of environmental stresses. Alternative splicing of serine/threonine transcripts, that regulate splicing of downstream genes, have been reported earlier in plants [84, 85]. Most of the serine/threonine genes were reported to be differentially spliced under mineral deficiency in rice [41]. Differential splicing of pre-mRNAs for Ser/Arg-rich protein was reported to dramatically alter responses of plants to environmental stresses [41, 86–88]. Rice mutants for Ser/Arg-rich splicing factors revealed their crucial regulatory role in P, Zn, and Mn nutrition. Moreover, the uptake and mobilization of P within the aerial parts of rice was reported to be regulated by three Ser/Arg-rich proteins [41].

Genes/transcripts for several transcription factors (including bZIP, ARF, bHLH, etc.), regulatory proteins (like protein phosphatases, NPR1, etc.), signaling molecules (like Pi-starvation response-signaling molecule), etc. showed contrasting trend for DAS in different rice genotypes, tissues and/or growth conditions. This not aligned with the protein expression profile (of the rice genotypes grown under similar conditions) reported earlier by our group [64] but also confirmed P-starvation induced/tissue-specific regulatory function (through alternative splicing) of Pup1 QTL in rice. Comparative analysis of the GO terms for the transcripts showing DAS in root and shoot tissues of the rice genotypes reiterated the regulatory function of Pup1 QTL in modulating tissue-specific gene expression. The GO term ‘cellular and metabolic processes’ was more affected/disturbed under stress, at both transcript and DAS levels, in shoot of Pusa-44.

Previous studies have reported differential expression of genes in response to environmental stresses controlling both transcriptional modulation as well as alternative splicing [34, 36, 68]. Such post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression has been reported to be one of the key adaptive features for abiotic stress tolerance in plants [89, 90]. However, the exact relationship between transcriptional regulation and alternative splicing has been a topic of discussion. One of the areas of interest has been to find that whether transcription regulation and alternative splicing operate individually or together under different stresses. Ongoing research indicates that the relationship between transcription regulation and alternative splicing varies across the species, stressors, and growth stages. The genes showing differential expression and those exhibiting alternative splicing under mineral (Fe, Cu, Mn, P, and/or Zn) deficiency in rice did not show significant overlap [41], while differential expression and alternative splicing of genes exhibited strong correlation in tea under drought stress [72]. In wheat, the relationship (between differential expression and alternative splicing of genes) has been reported to be even more complex under stress [71]. While alternative splicing was reported to be closely related with modulation of gene regulation in wheat after one hour of Heat treatment, it was not detected to be involved in the regulation after 6 h of stress [71].

Alternative splicing has not only been reported to fine-tune the expression of gene at transcript level but also aids in creating diversity in proteome profile under stress. It might regulate the number of stable/unstable transcripts/mRNA either through nonsense-mediated decay, small-noncoding RNAs, or other mechanisms like translation efficiency [60]. Alternative splicing (exclusion//inclusion) of exons was reported to increase functional diversity of proteins [91]. However, functional analysis of individual mRNA isoforms becomes necessary to understand the importance/function of alternative splicing in plant, particularly under stress. Existence of tissue-specific exons has been demonstrated to play critical role in maintaining identity of the tissue and specific function of the protein [91]. Tissue-specific differential splicing of exons/introns has been reported earlier to affect surface-loops, binding regions of the encoded protein, and protein–protein interaction networks [92]. The accumulating data on alternative splicing in plants enable the identification of lineage-specific, conserved splicing events, and the regulatory elements that control changes in splicing events.

Validating the expression of five randomly selected genes (showing DE as well as DAS) each from shoot as well as roots of the rice genotypes by RT-qPCR analysis, indicated trustworthiness of the RNA-seq datasets utilized for analyses in the present study. However, networking with other regulatory mechanisms like epigenomic-, epitranscriptomic-, and transposable element-mediated controls [13, 15, 16, 66] might provide deeper insights on pleotropic regulatory effects of Pup1 QTL in PUE in rice.

Conclusion

As plants are immobile, they need quicker response/adaptation to the changing environmental conditions to survive and thrive well. This necessitates utilizing most of the simple as well as intricate mechanisms to cope with environmental stresses. Plants possess complex regulatory networks enabling them to quickly adjust their gene expression in response to fluctuating environmental conditions including nutrient deficiencies. Though studies on alternative splicing in rice (including mineral deficiencies) have been reported, many of these were carried out utilizing a popular/high-yielding cultivar at an early stage of growth imposing a short period of stress. Utilization of a high-yielding rice cultivar and its near-isogenic line haboring Pup1 QTL grown under most contrasting conditions for > 30 days of stress makes the present study unique and outstanding. Aiming to decipher the P-starvation induced alternative splicing landscape of rice; this study unravels another regulatory function of Pup1 QTL. Several alternatively spliced transcripts (particularly through RI, MX, and SE events) associated with biological process showed opposite trend in roots of Pusa-44 and NIL-23 under P-starvation, reiterating the regulatory function of the QTL. However, functional validation of alternatively spliced transcripts (detected to play important roles in P-starvation tolerance in NIL-23) would be desirable. Understanding the developmental and environmental signals that help regulate alternative splicing (reprogramming gene expression) in response to abiotic/biotic stresses will have important implications in crop improvement. Unraveling differential splicing of transcripts at different developmental stages, in different tissues, on varying P regime in rice would further improve our understanding on regulation of gene expression.

In spite of considerable advances in understanding the alternative splicing process in the recent years, challenges do exist in deploying it for crop improvement. This include but not limited to (1) the identification of alternatively spliced variants in a cost-effective and high-throughput manner in the individuals of breeding populations, (2) determining the biological function of individual splice variants/transcripts, (3) precisely reconstituting the transcript isoforms and quantifying their relative abundance, and (4) identification of genetic alterations like InDels, SNPs, structural/chromosomal variation, epigenetic landscape, etc. associated directly/indirectly with alternatively spliced variants. Unless the technical issues in detection and functional characterization of splice variants are resolved, possible utilization of splice variants in plant improvement programs will have limited utility. Further investigations would be required to decipher the molecular basis and regulatory mechanisms of alternative splicing, particularly in nutrient homeostasis. These might not only provide opportunities for enhancing/fine-tuning gene expression but also genetic improvement of crops to enhance stress tolerance. We envisage that the gathered information on this study would help devising molecular strategies for enhanced PUE and increasing yielding potential of rice in P-deficient soils.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, growth conditions, and stress imposition

A pair of contrasting rice genotypes Pusa-44 and NIL-23 (BC3F6 progeny of Pusa-44 × IR64-Pup1-F harboring Pup1 QTL originally from Kasalath landrace), procured from the Division of Genetics, ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute, New Delhi, were utilized in the present study. Mature seeds of the rice genotype were used to grow plants hydroponically in PusaRicH medium (prepared in RO water) under P-sufficient (16 ppm Pi) and P-starved (0 ppm Pi) conditions. Such extreme/contrasting growth condition was used in the present study for comparative expression analysis of genes and alternative splicing of transcripts induced by P-starvation stress. Root and shoot tissues were collected from the plants at the vegetative/tillering stage (45-day-old plants) for morpho-physiological and molecular analyses.

Morphology of plants under stress

Morphological data on growth of shoot and root under P-starvation stress was collected in three biological replications from 45-day-old plants (at tillering stage). To analyze the effects of stress on root system architecture in contrasting rice genotypes (Pusa-44 and NIL-23), roots from the hydroponically grown plants were analyzed in triplicate using a table-top scanner (La2400, WinRHIZO Pro; Regent Instruments Inc, Montreal, Canada). The roots were cut at the root–shoot junction, washed with tap water, and placed in a scanning tray (30 × 40 cm) filled with water up to 1 cm depth. The roots were scanned at 600 dpi on grayscale. The images were analyzed using WinRHIZO Pro software.

Analysis of RNA-seq data

RNA-seq data generated for root and shoot tissues from a pair of contrasting rice genotypes grown under P-sufficient (16 ppm Pi) and P-starved (0 ppm Pi) conditions were utilized in the present study. The data for 16 transcriptome libraries [prepared in two technical replications by pooling total RNAs from six biological replications in two (3 + 3) groups, sequenced at Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform using PE150 bp chemistry, submitted to SRA database of NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) under BioProject ID PRJNA667189] were utilized for bioinformatics analysis. The raw reads were checked using FastQC and processed for adapter trimming, removal of low-quality reads, etc. for the paired-end data using the Trimmomatic tool [93] at the parameters set for LEADING: 3, TRAILING: 3, SLIDINGWINDOW: 4:15, MINLEN: 36 with Phred33 quality score. Reference-based assembly of the transcriptome data was carried out on Os-Nipponbare-Reference-IRGSP-1.0 from “The Rice Annotation Project Database” [RAP-DB, http://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/ and NCBI database (RefSeq assembly accession: GCF_001433935.1)]. For quantification of transcript abundance, the transcript per million (TPM) value was calculated for each transcript using Salmon v1.4.0 (https://combine-lab.github.io/salmon/) (Supplementary Table S5−S20). Transcript sequences were mapped on IRGSP-1.0 using HiSat aligner [94] with default parameters; quantification and gtf file with updated transcript annotation were performed with the help of StringTie [95].

Identification of differentially expressed genes and transcripts

Differential expression analysis was performed using 3D RNAseq R package (R Shiny-based interactive tool for RNA-seq data analysis, https://3drnaseq.hutton.ac.uk/app_direct/3DRNAseq/) [96]. Differential transcript usage (DTU) analysis was carried out with the help of Limma software package [97] available at 3DRNAseq server. While initial gene length correction was performed using lengthScaledTPM and tximport of R package version 1.10.0, transcripts with low expression were filtered out based on counts per million reads (CPM) ≥1. The trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) approach was used for normalization of gene expression. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed and the normalized read counts were transferred to the Limma R package for 3D expression comparison to identify differentially expressed genes, differentially alternatively spliced (DAS) genes, differentially expressed transcripts (DETs), and DTU.

For the identification of DEGs/DETs, Log2 fold change () of gene and transcript abundance was calculated for the contrasting groups, while significance of expression change was predicted using the t-test. The P-value of multiple testing was adjusted using the BH method [98] to correct the false discovery rate (FDR). A gene/transcript was considered to be significantly differentially expressed in a contrasting group if it had adjusted P-value < 0.01 and 1. For detecting alternative splicing, DTU was calculated by comparing of a transcript with the weighted average of for the rest of the transcripts of the same gene. A transcript was considered to have a significant DTU if it had an adjusted P-value <0.01 and delta-percent spliced (PS) 0.1. A gene was considered to be significantly DAS in a contrasting group if it had an adjusted P-value < 0.01 and any of its transcripts had PS 0.1.

Identification of alternative splicing events

For identification of alternative splicing events, updated gtf files and SUPPA tool were used to perform analysis of alternative splicing events [99]. Five different types of alternative splicing events namely Alternative 5’/3’ Splice Sites (AS), Alternative First/Last Exons (AE), Mutually Exclusive Exons (MX), Retained Intron (RI) and Skipping Exon (SE) were identified in the transcripts. The relative number and corresponding proportion of the events were also calculated.

Gene ontology analysis

GO analysis of transcripts showing alternative splicing events was performed using BLAST2GO package (https://www.blast2go.com/). All the transcripts showing alternative splicing were processed for gene ontology and subjected to categorization into biological process, molecular function, and cellular component. Each category was further analyzed to identify the transcripts associated with particular GO term.

RT‑qPCR validation of differentially expressed and spliced genes

Five randomly selected genes (showing variation between the genotypes/growth conditions in RNA-seq analysis) exhibiting differential expression as well as alternative splicing in shoot and/or root of the contrasting rice genotypes were validated by RT-qPCR following the MIQE guidelines. An independent set of tissue samples was used for validation of gene expression. Detailed procedure for the validation has been described earlier [100, 101].

Statistical analysis

Most of the experiments were carried out with three or more biological/technical replications. The data were statistically analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT), and/or Post-hoc Tukey’s test at the significance level P ≤ 0.05 to compare mean of the treatments. Relative expression of gene in RT-qPCR investigation denotes fold-change in the gene expression under stress (0 ppm Pi) over the control (16 ppm Pi). Error bar indicates standard deviation (± SD).
