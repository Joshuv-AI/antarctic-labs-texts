We appreciate the opportunity to address the technical queries raised by Dr Roy. Please allow us to further clarify the rigorous validation and standardized protocols that underpin our artificial intelligence (AI)–derived biomechanical index.

Regarding the disparity between individual feature areas under the curve and the ensemble model’s performance, in a soft voting ensemble integrating multiple Random Forest and Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifiers, diagnostic power is derived from high-dimensional, nonlinear interactions rather than a linear summation of individual parameters. Our K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) Decision Boundary analysis (Fig. S9) confirms that while single features may have overlapping distributions, their combined spatial orientation provides a robust separation between Normal (N) and Ectasia (E) groups.1 To ensure generalizability, feature selection was performed via Recursive Feature Elimination exclusively on the training subset from Iran. This protocol strictly prevents “data leakage,” ensuring the 10-feature subset was identified without influence from testing or external validation sets. The ultimate proof of the model’s robustness is its performance (area under the curve: 1.0) on an entirely independent dataset from Germany with different geographic and racial demographics than the the training dataset.

Dr Roy suggests that 2-dimensional digital image correlation may be compromised by out-of-plane motion. The proposed preprocessing protocol addressed this through automatic and manual quality control processes. More specifically, as noted in our previously published work,2 we used automatic release and conducted a manual, frame-by-frame analysis by an independent masked examiner to exclude any acquisitions showing artifacts. Furthermore, because our feature extraction is fully automated and does not rely on subjective manual segmentation, the repeatability of the AI-derived index is inherently tied to the high fidelity of the raw digital signal. Finally, the independent external validation demonstrates that these features remain robust and discriminative across different clinical settings and populations.

Regarding the clinical definition, Dr Roy suggests it is “improbable” that all Very Asymmetric Ectasia with Normal Topography (VAE-NT) eyes in this study were affected by disease. This argument presents a fundamental clinical contradiction. Our study strictly included VAE-NT eyes defined as the topographically normal eyes of patients with manifest, frank keratoconus in the fellow eye. According to the Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases,3 “true unilateral keratoconus does not exist.” Therefore, any eye in a VAE-NT cohort is, by definition, an eye with subclinical biomechanical susceptibility. The fact that traditional indices like Tomographic Biomechanical Index (TBI) or Corvis Biomechanical Index (CBI) identified some of these eyes as “nonectatic” is not a sign of their “reasonableness” but rather a documented limitation of their sensitivity. Our model’s ability to identify these cases as ectatic aligns with the clinical reality that these eyes are pathologically altered at a molecular and structural level before topography can detect them.

Dr Roy notes that while Stiffness Parameter at first applanation (SP-A1) differs significantly between groups, our study merges VAE-NT and Mild Keratoconus (MKC) together. This is a demonstration of the model’s robustness. While single-point metrics like SP-A1 may show a statistical “gradient,” the AI-derived index identifies the overarching biomechanical signature of ectatic susceptibility. The overlap of Kmax and central corneal thickness between groups (Table 1 in the original article) demonstrates why traditional morphology-based screening may fail. Contrary to the concern that an AI-derived index may label patients as “ineligible” for surgery, it actually provides the surgeon with the precision to distinguish a “thin but stable” cornea from a “thin and ectatic” one. By correctly identifying biomechanical weakness in a “normal-looking” thin cornea, our model prevents postrefractive ectasia—a distinction that the “reasonable” results of TBI and CBI missed in this cohort. In conclusion, our study derived an AI-backed index to bridge the gap between structural tomography and focal biomechanics, representing a verifiable step forward in early ectasia screening.
