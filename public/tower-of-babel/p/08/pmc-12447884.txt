Unnoticed early pregnancy during myomectomy with favorable outcome: Case report
Authors: Ronak Mulji, Lusajo Mwagobele, Farhat Seif, Zainab Fidaali, Munawar Kaguta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUterine fibroids are a common cause of infertility, and myomectomy remains a key surgical intervention to improve reproductive outcomes. However, the occurrence of an undetected pregnancy during myomectomy is rare and poses significant clinical risks.Case PresentationWe report a 34-year-old woman, nulliparous with a history of secondary infertility and three first-trimester miscarriages. She had multiple fibroids and was managed for polycystic ovarian syndrome and hyperprolactinemia. Scheduled for abdominal myomectomy, the surgery proceeded uneventfully, with six fibroids removed and no breach of the endometrial cavity. Five weeks postoperatively, she presented with symptoms suggestive of early pregnancy. Ultrasound confirmed a viable intrauterine gestation of 8 weeks and 1 day. She later developed cervical shortening at 11 weeks and underwent cerclage. The pregnancy progressed without further complications, and she delivered a healthy infant via elective caesarean section at 36 weeks and 2 days.DiscussionThis case underscores the importance of thorough preoperative assessment to exclude pregnancy. It also reflects emerging evidence suggesting that conception shortly after myomectomy does not necessarily increase obstetric risk, especially when the uterine cavity remains intact.ConclusionAlthough conception during or soon after myomectomy is generally avoided, favorable outcomes are possible with careful surgical technique and close antenatal monitoring. Individualized counseling may be more appropriate than rigid timing guidelines for conception post-myomectomy.Keywords: Myomectomy, Pregnancy testing, Early pregnancy, Fibroids

Introduction

Uterine fibroids are the most common benign tumors in females. It affects females of reproductive age and is found in up to 25 % of the population. [1] About 1–2 % of pregnant women will undergo a non-obstetric surgical procedure. [2]The presence of fibroids in pregnancy has been linked to a range of adverse outcomes such as preterm labor, miscarriages, malpresentation, labor abnormalities and increased rate of caesarean delivery. Myomectomy can be considered as a treatment option especially if there is distortion of the uterine cavity. [3] Antepartum myomectomy is usually avoided as its considered a high risk for hemorrhage and obstetric complications, however occasionally it may be considered. [4] However multiple studies show a favorable outcome following myomectomy in pregnancy. [4,5] We report a case of a 34-year-old female with secondary infertility who underwent myomectomy during early pregnancy that was undiagnosed. She went on to deliver a healthy baby at term by caesarean section. This report has been prepared in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines. [6]

Patient and observation

Patient Information

We present a 34-year-old patient para 0 + 3 with a chief complaint of difficult conception. She had three previous miscarriages, all in the first trimester and no history of surgical treatment for the abortions. She had a history of multiple fibroids, intramural and subserosal with largest measuring 5.3 cm by 4.3 cm. She was also a known patient with polycystic ovarian syndrome and hyperprolactinemia on medication. She had a history of ovarian drilling and ovulation induction with clomiphene, and follicle stimulating hormone injectables. She was scheduled for myomectomy in May but due to patient related factors she was admitted for myomectomy in July. She underwent surgery without any complications.

Clinical findings

On examination she was alert, afebrile, oriented and not pale. Per abdomen she had non distended, normal contour, soft with no palpable mass. Other systemic examinations were essentially normal.

Diagnostic assessment

complete blood count, electrolytes and prolactin levels were within the normal range. Transvaginal ultrasound revealed multiple intramural fibroids measuring 4.3*5.3 cm (Fig. 1a and 1b).

Multiple intramural uterine fibroids noted largest seen posteriorly measuring 4.3 × 5.3 cm.

Therapeutic interventions

Intraoperatively, an enlarged uterus with multiple fibroids was found, largest was 8*6 cm intra mural on the fundus, the rest were multiple small subserosal fibroid of approximately 2.4 cm. There was no fibroid near the ostia, the fallopian tubes and ovaries appeared to be normal. A total of 6 myomas were extracted, there was no endometrial breech. No complications were encountered and surgery took about 110 min. No uterotonic agents were used due to unknown pregnancy status.

She recovered well during her follow up visits one and two weeks after the procedure. However, about 5 weeks after the procedure she presented with nausea, vomiting and fatigue. Upon investigation beta human chorionic gonadotropin hormone (b-hcg) was 123,035 mIU/ml and ultrasound showed single viable intrauterine pregnancy with gestation age of 8 weeks plus one day (fig. 2a and 2b), thus she was therefore about 3 weeks pregnant at the time of her myomectomy.

showing a viable intrauterine pregnancy with fetal pole measurements corresponding to gestation age of 8 weeks and 1 day.

She started an antenatal care, at 11 weeks she presented with an episode of pinkish discharge and a speculum examination revealed a shortened and opened cervix. Based on the physical findings she underwent placement of cervical cerclage. The procedure was uneventful. At 24 weeks received dexamethasone course lung maturation because she was considered at high risk for preterm birth. She continued routine follow up uneventfully and the serial ultrasounds were normal. No other high-risk interventions were required throughout pregnancy. A routine third trimester ultrasound was done and revealed a normal growing fetus at gestation age of 34 weeks and 6 days. (fig. 3a, 3b and 3c).

and 3c: showing a normal growing fetus at gestation age of 34 weeks and 6 days.

She underwent an elective caesarean section at gestational age of 36 weeks and 2 days due to concern of potential uterine rupture as a result of overlapping duration of pregnancy and surgery. She delivered a live baby that scored well and had an uncomplicated surgery.

Follow-up

The postpartum follow-up was unremarkable at 1 week and 6 weeks post-surgery.

Patient perspective

“I am so grateful I am a mother now, I was losing hope and I did not think I could be pregnant without any intervention, I am grateful that I have my baby despite the surgery.”

Discussion

Uterine fibroids are the most common benign tumors of the female reproductive tract, affecting up to 25 % of women of reproductive age. [1] Their prevalence tends to increase with age and is higher among women of African descent [7]. Fibroids can present with a range of symptoms including abnormal uterine bleeding, pelvic pain, pressure symptoms, and—most significantly for reproductive health—infertility and pregnancy loss. In women seeking to conceive, fibroids may interfere with implantation or early pregnancy maintenance, particularly when they are large, intramural, or distort the endometrial cavity. [8] In our case, the patient, a 34-year-old woman with a history of recurrent first-trimester miscarriages and a background of polycystic ovarian syndrome and hyperprolactinemia, was found to have multiple intramural and sub serosal fibroids. Myomectomy was indicated in her case due to the association between her fibroid burden and infertility, in line with current recommendations for surgical management when fibroids are suspected to impair fertility. [8] In this case, if positive pregnancy status was known prior, surgery would have not been indicated.

Myomectomy, though fertility-preserving, is not without surgical risks. Intraoperative complications may include significant blood loss, injury to surrounding organs, and, in rare cases, the need for emergency hysterectomy. Postoperatively, the uterine wall may be weakened, which has implications for future pregnancies. Caesarean delivery is often recommended in subsequent pregnancies due to concerns about uterine rupture during labor. [9] Although uncommon, the occurrence of an unrecognized early pregnancy during myomectomy is a critical scenario. [10] The physiological changes of early pregnancy may not yet be clinically apparent, and unless pregnancy is confirmed preoperatively, it can go undetected. In our patient, conception occurred approximately three weeks prior to surgery but was not identified preoperatively. Remarkably, the surgery was completed without breaching the endometrial cavity, and the pregnancy progressed with a favorable outcome after cervical cerclage was placed for cervical shortening.

The case raises important considerations about timing conception after myomectomy. A recent systematic review by Margueritte and et al. examined the interval between myomectomy and pregnancy and found that the mean time to conception was 17.6 months, with shortest intervals reported as early as one month [9]. The review concluded that there is insufficient evidence to recommend a mandatory delay before attempting conception. Notably, the risk of uterine rupture was low (0.5 %) and not correlated with the interval between surgery and conception. [9] These findings challenge the long-standing practice of advising extended delays post-myomectomy and emphasize that uterine rupture, while serious, is rare and multifactorial. In our patient's case, the lack of cavity entry, the intramural location of fibroids away from the uterine ostia, and a meticulous surgical technique may have contributed to the successful pregnancy outcome despite the unusually short interval between surgery and gestation.

This case adds to the growing body of evidence suggesting that favorable outcomes are possible even when conception occurs shortly after myomectomy, provided key surgical principles are respected. While this does not negate the importance of thorough preoperative assessment—including pregnancy testing—it supports a more individualized approach to post-myomectomy fertility counseling, considering patient age, fertility history, and fibroid characteristics rather than relying solely on time intervals. [11]

The American Society of Anesthesiologists doesn't recommend routine pregnancy testing before surgery, it should be considered if the result will influence the course of management. [11] In surgeries that involve the uterus or procedures that disrupt blood flow to the uterus careful consideration is advised. Currently, there is insufficient evidence to confirm any harmful effects of anesthesia on the fetus, and no anesthetic agents in standard dosages have been shown to cause teratogenic effects. [12]

Routine pregnancy testing also raises ethical issues as the patients have the right to decide whether to undergo the testing. Informed consent should be obtained and patients should be counselled about risks, benefits and alternatives including possibility of false results. [12]

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare but successful outcome of an undetected early pregnancy during abdominal myomectomy. Despite the inherent risks, including potential pregnancy loss and uterine complications, the integrity of the endometrial cavity was preserved, and the patient benefited from prompt and appropriate antenatal care. Her outcome supports growing evidence that conception soon after myomectomy may not necessarily result in poor obstetric outcomes, provided key surgical principles are upheld. While pregnancy should ideally be ruled out before such procedures, this case reinforces the need for individualized management and shows that favorable results are achievable even under atypical circumstances.
