Brief Clinical Vignette

A 57-year-old Caucasian man with no significant past medical history presented for evaluation of a first-lifetime episode of palpitations. His medical history was notable only for a mild COVID-19 infection 2 years prior. Cardiac auscultation revealed an irregular rhythm with varying intensity of the first heart sound, but no audible murmurs. There were no signs of peripheral congestion or edema. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a structurally normal heart with preserved systolic and diastolic function and no valvular pathology. A standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) was obtained (Figure 1A). Discussion

Careful analysis of the ECG reveals sinus P waves at a rate of 50 beats/min. The ventricular rhythm is periodically irregular and exhibits varying QRS morphologies throughout the recording. QRS complexes with a right bundle branch block (RBBB) morphology (130 milliseconds) are observed. Intermittently, significantly broader complexes appear (up to 160 milliseconds), displaying varying degrees of a distinctive leftward axis shift (left anterior fascicular block pattern). Careful analysis reveals up to 6 distinct QRS morphologies across the precordium (eg, in lead V4), representing a fluctuating continuum of progressive aberrancy. This variation is driven by a baseline RBBB combined with a relatively long and variable refractory period of the left anterior fascicle. This variability in QRS duration and axis creates a chaotic appearance, mimicking a complex polymorphic arrhythmia. The presence of consistent P waves excludes atrial fibrillation. The fixed coupling of the QRS complexes to the preceding P-wave argues against ventricular extrasystoles. Finally, although junctional extrasystoles can mimic this pattern, the strict linkage to sinus P waves makes this unlikely, as junctional ectopy typically manifests with variable coupling or atrioventricular dissociation.

The definitive diagnosis was established during the electrophysiology study, although the procedure was technically challenging. Furthermore, the clinical arrhythmia subsided spontaneously during the study. Atrial pacing maneuvers confirmed dual atrioventricular nodal physiology. Retrograde conduction was excluded. Representative recordings (Figure 1B) confirmed that a single atrial impulse conducted simultaneously via the fast pathway and the slow pathway. The administration of isoproterenol, as well as atrial burst pacing, suppressed the paired ventricular complexes. This response made junctional extrasystolic beats very unlikely, as these arrhythmias typically intensify with adrenergic stimulation.

The slow pathway was targeted by applying radiofrequency energy at the base of the triangle of Koch, between the tricuspid annulus and the superior border of the coronary sinus ostium. The ablation was deemed high risk due to the confirmed absence of retrograde conduction, which precluded standard monitoring of ventriculoatrial conduction integrity during junctional rhythm. To mitigate this risk, radiofrequency energy was delivered via a 4-mm nonirrigated catheter using a pulsed application method (short, intermittent energy delivery). A total of 6 applications were delivered with a cumulative duration of 2 minutes and 42 seconds. The double ventricular response terminated during energy application, followed by typical slow junctional beats exhibiting an isolated RBBB morphology without left anterior fascicular block, confirming successful and safe modification of the substrate. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient remained completely asymptomatic with no recurrence of the arrhythmia.
