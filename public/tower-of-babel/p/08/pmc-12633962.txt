Determinants of tobacco product sales near educational institutions in Telangana and Meghalaya
Authors: Ravita Yadav, Sonu Goel, Rajeev Kumar, Rana J. Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Tobacco use among Indian youth remains a significant public health concern, with a high prevalence of early initiation driven by accessibility, peer pressure, and targeted marketing. Despite legislative measures like the Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act (COTPA), enforcement of the ban on tobacco sales within 100 yards of educational institutions remains inadequate. This study examines the factors influencing tobacco sales near schools in Telangana and Meghalaya, highlighting the urgent need for stronger regulations and monitoring to reduce youth exposure to tobacco products.Methodology:A cross-sectional study was conducted in seven districts of Meghalaya and Telangana, surveying 234 educational institutions selected through convenient sampling. Tobacco vendors within 100 yards of institutions were mapped and observed for compliance with Section 6 (b) of COTPA. Data on institution type, signage, and points of sale were collected through unannounced observations. Statistical analysis using descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests was performed in STATA 15.0 to identify factors influencing compliance.Results:Among 234 institutions surveyed, 25% had tobacco sales within 100 yards, with higher prevalence in urban areas (30.6%) than rural (20.9%), and government institutions (31.1%) compared to private (21.1%). Middle and secondary schools had higher sales proximity (61.5% and 36.7%) than senior secondary (10.3%) or above (17.7%). Tobacco-free signage reduced sales prevalence (33.3% vs. 66.6%), and institutions with fewer than five PoS accounted for 80% of violations.Conclusion:This study highlights significant violations of COTPA Section 6 (b), with a deliberate clustering of tobacco retailers near government, middle, and secondary schools, particularly in urban areas. Weak enforcement, lack of tobacco-free signage, and targeted marketing exacerbate youth exposure to tobacco products, fostering early initiation and lifelong addiction. Strengthened regulatory enforcement, stricter penalties, improved monitoring, and youth-focused awareness campaigns are essential to curb tobacco sales near educational institutions and protect vulnerable populations.

Introduction

Tobacco-related diseases contribute to approximately 1.35 million deaths annually in India, with an overall tobacco use prevalence of 28.6% among adults (GATS-2) and 8.5% among Indian youth aged 13–15 years.[1] Alarmingly, early initiation of tobacco use is highly prevalent among youth, with many future victims of tobacco-related illnesses being today’s children.[2] The studies indicate that the prevalence of tobacco use among school-going adolescents ranges from 5.9% to 49%[3], with high rates of initiation and experimentation during adolescence[45] driven by factors like peer pressure, accessibility, and targeted marketing.[456] This vulnerable age group is increasingly exposed to changing lifestyles and behaviors that negatively impact their health.[7] Furthermore, adolescents and young adults, particularly in colleges, are frequently targeted by aggressive marketing strategies of the tobacco industry, exacerbating the problem.[8]

The Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act (COTPA) is the key legislation governing tobacco control in India.[9] Formulated in the year 2005, this law aims to reduce tobacco consumption across the country while protecting vulnerable groups, particularly the youth. Under Section 6 (b) of the Act, the sale of tobacco products is banned within a 100-yard radius of educational institutions. Additionally, the National Tobacco Control Programme (NTCP) has issued guidelines for Tobacco Free Educational Institutions (ToFEI), mandating the display of “Tobacco Free Area” signage and the designation of Tobacco Monitors to enforce tobacco-free policies.[10] Enforcing Section 6 (b) of COTPA remains a significant challenge, despite multiple directives from Indian High Courts over the years, urging the ban of tobacco sales near school premises.[11] The widespread and unregulated availability of tobacco products in India, mainly through small neighbourhood stores, kiosks, and pan stalls near school premises, has intensified the accessibility and affordability of tobacco products among youths.[12] Further, the tobacco industry makes substantial investments in advertising in stores, aiming at convincing youth to consume tobacco.[13] Given that tobacco experimentation and addiction often begin in adolescence, primary care physicians (PCPs) play a critical role in identifying at-risk individuals and providing early intervention. PCPs can integrate tobacco use screening into routine adolescent health check-ups, offer brief counseling sessions, and educate families about the dangers of early tobacco exposure. Additionally, they can advocate for stronger enforcement of tobacco control policies within their communities and collaborate with educational institutions to promote anti-tobacco awareness programs.

Addressing the availability of tobacco within proximity to educational institutions is crucial for reducing exposure to tobacco products, particularly for school-aged youth.[1415] This study provides insights that can assist PCPs in recognizing tobacco-related behavioral patterns in adolescents, equipping them with strategies to counsel at-risk youth, and reinforcing their role as key stakeholders in community-based tobacco control initiatives.

This study aims to examine the determinants of tobacco product sales within 100 yards of educational institutions in two states of India.

Methodology

This cross-sectional study, conducted in seven districts of Meghalaya and Telangana states, utilises a convenient sampling technique to map points of sale (PoS) around educational institutions. A total of 234 educational institutions were selected from the seven districts, based on the formula for sample size estimation. The “educational institution” included both public and private sector institutions. The field investigators visited district headquarters along with rural blocks and observed each of the sample units (educational institutions) unobstructively one by one during peak hours as per the prefixed schedule. Each district was treated as a distinct stratum, with one primary survey unit (PSU) selected in urban areas and two PSUs in rural areas per district.

Measures

The primary independent variable in this study was the presence of a point of sale for tobacco products within 100 yards of the educational institution’s boundary. A point of sale was defined as any retail outlet, kiosk, or establishment selling or displaying tobacco products. The dependent variables included the area of the institution (urban or rural), type of institution (private or government), grade level (up to grade 5, grade 8, grade 10, grade 12, or above grade 12), display of tobacco-free signage (Yes/No) with specifications as outlined in COTPA, signage for smoking or chewing tobacco (Yes/No), and the number of points of sale (0, less than 5, or greater than 5) within the vicinity of the educational institution. Descriptive statistics and cross-tabulation were used to summarize the data, and bivariate analysis using Chi-square tests was applied to examine the relationships between independent and dependent variables. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA 15.0.

Ethical approval for the research proposal and data collection tools was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Review Board of PGIMER, Chandigarh. Unannounced observations of tobacco retail outlets was done in December 2022.

Discussion

The study observed that a quarter of educational institutions reported the presence of PoS of tobacco products within 100 yards of their premises, thus violating Section 6 (b) of COTPA. The findings were consistent with earlier studies done by Pimple et al.,[13] and Rajesh et al.,[14], which reported similar violations with 222 (100%) tobacco retail outlets and 48 (100%) shops located within a 100-yard radius of the schools, respectively. Few studies, viz. Pradhan et al.,[15] and Ganganahalli et al.,[16] at 69 and 64%, respectively, reported higher violations of Section 6 (b). The variation in the level of violations could be attributed to differences in the enforcement mechanisms across regions. Further, it was observed that higher violations of Section 6 (b) is directly proportional to tobacco use prevalence in the state, which has not been consistently documented in previous studies.

In Meghalaya and Telangana, there are notably more tobacco points of sale (PoS) near educational institutions in urban areas than in rural regions. The density of commercial businesses, including tobacco sellers, is often more prominent in metropolitan and urban areas, as they intend to make more profits in those locations. The studies have also found that urban areas have 32% more tobacco retailers than non-urban areas.[17] Further, urban schools usually serve larger populations, making them prime locations for retailers looking to maximize their sales, which includes tobacco products.[18] Additionally, urban areas provide greater foot traffic and business opportunities for tobacco sellers. The issue is further exacerbated by weaker enforcement of tobacco control laws in cities, which hampers effective monitoring of tobacco vendors.[19]

The study reveals that private institutions have fewer tobacco points of sale (PoS) nearby as compared to government institutions. This may be attributed to private schools maintaining stricter internal surveillance systems and prioritizing their reputation to attract students. A survey conducted across four Indian states found that urban schools, which often include private institutions, reported higher compliance with tobacco control laws, suggesting that enhanced monitoring and a focus on institutional reputation contribute to reduced violations.[20] Further, parents of students in private institutions are often more proactive and may pressurize school administration to ensure a safe and tobacco-free environment around the premises.[1921] In contrast, government schools, especially those in rural or remote areas, often face limited oversight, creating opportunities for tobacco vendors to set up PoS near the educational institutions. The weaker enforcement of tobacco control laws in these regions further exacerbates the problem, as highlighted by earlier studies on regulatory disparities in rural and urban settings.[22]

In urban areas, the high density of tobacco retailers around middle and secondary schools, with 73.3% located 100 meters or less from the nearest school, suggests a deliberate pattern where the tobacco industry may target young individuals, introducing them to tobacco at an early age, potentially leading to lifelong addiction. By ensuring easy access to tobacco products near schools, the industry might be fostering a future customer base, as documented in earlier studies. Nyirakamana et al.,[23] in their study, in Rwanda, have highlighted how weak enforcement of age-related sales restrictions allowed minors to access tobacco products easily near schools. This lack of robust regulatory action creates an environment conducive to underage tobacco consumption and undermines the protective intent of such laws. Anee et al.[24] further underscore the role of aggressive tobacco industry tactics in exacerbating this issue. Their study revealed targeted marketing and brand promotions around educational institutions aimed specifically at influencing youth.

The current study shows that 20% of the educational institutions have more than five PoS within 100 yards of their premises, which may be a proxy of higher consumption of tobacco products in these locations. Previous studies have shown that greater access to nearby tobacco retail outlets significantly increases susceptibility among adolescents in urban Indian settings.[25] As more PoS are established in a given area, the likelihood of individuals encountering tobacco products increases, which drives demand. This, in turn, makes it more profitable for additional retailers to open new PoS, often clustering in these areas, driving a larger customer base— a trend also supported by economic analyses of tobacco sales and taxation dynamics in India.[24] A similar clustering effect was observed in a study in Scotland by Shortt et al.[25]

The existing literature supports the findings of our study that retailers selling tobacco products avoid displaying T-free signage because it directly conflicts with their sales interests. Displaying such signage might discourage customers from purchasing tobacco products, which can reduce their revenue.[262728] Studies have also shown that in areas where tobacco PoS are prevalent, compliance with anti-tobacco laws, such as the COTPA regulations in India, is often lower. This includes failing to display required signage like T-free or no-smoking signs,[2129] as also demonstrated in the current study.

Primary care physicians (PCPs) play a crucial role in addressing the impact of such tobacco availability near educational institutions. Given that adolescents are at high risk of initiation, PCPs can incorporate routine tobacco use screening and intervention strategies in their practice. Counseling young patients and their families on the dangers of early tobacco exposure, providing behavioral interventions, and reinforcing cessation techniques can help curb the influence of nearby PoS. Furthermore, PCPs can advocate for stricter enforcement of tobacco-free zones around schools by collaborating with policymakers, educational institutions, and law enforcement agencies. Strengthening their role in tobacco control can bridge the gap between public health policies and real-world implementation, ultimately reducing youth tobacco initiation rates.

This study had a couple of limitations. First, being a cross-sectional survey, the dependent and independent variables were collected simultaneously, making it difficult to establish a clear cause-and-effect relationship. It remains to be seen whether the presence of educational institutions directly influenced the high density of tobacco retailers or if these retailers were already present before the schools were established. Additionally, external factors, such as population density, may have contributed to the clustering of educational institutions’ locations and cigarette shops. Second, the study employed convenient sampling, which may limit the generalizability of the findings, as the sample may need to be more representative of all schools and communities. This method could introduce potential biases, influencing the overall accuracy of the results.

Conclusion

The findings highlight a deliberate clustering of tobacco retailers near government, middle and secondary schools, especially in urban areas, which poses a considerable risk to youth, exposing them to tobacco products at an early age. This fuels a self-reinforcing cycle of higher tobacco consumption and establishment of PoS. The lack of compliance with tobacco-free signage due to weak enforcement further exacerbates this issue. The study underscores the need for a targeted approach towards stronger enforcement mechanisms and stricter penalties for COTPA Section 6 (b) violations, better monitoring, and stricter adherence to tobacco control laws. Further, the resources should be directed to tailored youth awareness campaigns to prevent tobacco sales near these institutions.
