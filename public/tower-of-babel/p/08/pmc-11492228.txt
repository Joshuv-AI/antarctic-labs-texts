Q Fever-Related Glomerulonephritis Unveiled by Metagenomic Next-Generation Sequencing
Authors: Yang Li, Yueping Shen, Hongyu Yang, Min Li, Lei Jiang, Wei Ma, Fude Zhou, Tao Su
Published: 2024

Introduction

Q fever, a zoonosis caused by the intracellular bacterium Coxiella burnetii, is a significant infectious disease that affects both humans and animals.1 It is characterized by a wide array of clinical presentations, which can range from asymptomatic to severe and life-threatening conditions. One of the most serious complications of Q fever is the development of glomerulonephritis, which can progress to chronic kidney disease if left untreated.2 The epidemiology of Q fever in China, as in many other regions, has been obscured by a lack of comprehensive data, leading to frequent misdiagnosis and under reporting.2,3 This is partly due to the nonspecific nature of its symptoms, which can mimic other febrile illnesses or autoimmune diseases, and the slow-growing nature of Coxiella burnetii, which can delay detection in standard blood cultures. A literature review shows that there are only 21 biopsy-proven cases of Q fever–related glomerulonephritis (Qf-GN) reported over the past 6 decades, and no case series or cohort studies are available to date (Supplementary Table S1).

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has revolutionized pathogen detection by extracting and amplifying their DNA or RNA (further elaborated in the Supplementary Methods). Unlike traditional diagnostic methods, mNGS is a high-throughput sequencing method allowing for the unbiased and comprehensive analysis of all genetic material in a clinical sample. This includes the detection of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and host cells, broadening the scope of analysis beyond conventional approaches.4 This powerful technology can identify a broad range of pathogens in a single test within a few hours, even those present at low levels or those that are difficult to culture. The rapid turnaround time of mNGS makes it particularly useful for the diagnosis of acute infections, including Q fever, where timely diagnosis and targeted treatment are crucial to improving patient outcomes.

In this current study, we aimed to describe the clinical and pathological characteristics of 7 patients diagnosed with Qf-GN. Our findings highlight the educational value and the transformative potential of mNGS in diagnosing and managing Q fever.

Results

The study included 7 patients, comprising 1 female and 6 males, with an age range from 42 to 83 years (median age: 58 years) and male predominance. The source of the patients, Qf-GN diagnostic criteria, and mNGS testing methods are included in the Supplementary Material. All patients resided in rural areas, and only 2 of them (2 out of 7) had previously been involved in sheep farming. Among the 5 patients (#1, #2, #3, #5, and #7), fever was a common symptom, lasting from 1 month to 3 years, along with constitutional symptoms such as weight loss, decreased appetite, and severe fatigue. Extrarenal manifestations included endocarditis, pancytopenia, splenomegaly, and hepatitis. All the patients had a history of preexisting valvular heart conditions or had undergone artificial aortic graft replacement (Table 1). They presented with mild to moderate valvular regurgitation, predominantly tricuspid, followed by aortic and mitral. Notably, nearly half of the cases showed exclusive thickening and calcification in the aortic valve (Supplementary Figure S1). One patient exhibited Janeway lesions (Supplementary Figure S2). Positron emission tomography/computed tomography scans of 3 patients revealed high standardized uptake value lesions in 1 patient’s artificial aortic graft and in the ascending aorta of another patient. Standard blood cultures for aerobic and anaerobic bacteria were negative for all patients. The mNGS testing was conducted on peripheral blood samples from all 7 patients, urine samples from 3 patients, kidney biopsy samples from 2 patients, and a liver biopsy from 1 patient. The detection rate for Coxiella burnetii in blood, urine, and liver samples was 100% (Supplementary Figure S3). All patients presented with nephrotic syndrome and renal dysfunction, among which 3 experienced acute kidney disease or worsening of chronic kidney disease. This was characterized by a systemic inflammatory response, including sustained elevation of C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate in some cases (Table 1). Three patients required hemodialysis. The duration of kidney disease varied from 1 month to 2 years before the diagnosis of Qf-GN. Admission laboratory tests indicated serum creatinine levels ranging from 1.55 to 5.24 mg/dl, with significant proteinuria, hematuria, and sterile leukocyturia, but no glycosuria. Renal tubular acidosis was diagnosed based on reduced urinary NH4+ and titratable acid levels, indicating tubular damage.

We noticed that membranous proliferative glomerulonephritis, was the most common pathological pattern (Supplementary Table S2). Other patterns were also observed, including crescentic glomerulonephritis, focal proliferative glomerulonephritis with crescents formation (2.0%–8.0% of glomeruli), and endocapillary proliferative glomerulonephritis concurrently with tubulointerstitial nephritis (Figure 1). Histological red blood cell casts were noted in 2 cases. Doxycycline or minocycline, and hydroxychloroquine were administrated to all patients. Two patients received short-term steroid therapy, and one of them underwent a course of methylprednisolone pulse therapy. During the follow-up period, 1 patient progressed to chronic kidney disease stage 5 non-dialysis, whereas the renal function of the other 5 patients achieved partial recovery, with the estimated glomerular filtration rate maintained between 28.4 and 52.3 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (Table 1).

Discussion

Q fever caused by Coxiella burnetii presents with a spectrum of clinical manifestations that are often nonspecific, which frequently leads to its underdiagnosis, particularly in regions where the disease is not highly suspected or well-recognized.5 Glomerulonephritis is one of the severe complications associated with Q fever, and though its pathogenesis is not fully understood, Qf-GN is hypothesized to result from exacerbated immune-mediated inflammatory response to the pathogen. In our case series, renal pathology revealed not only immune complex–mediated membranous proliferative glomerulonephritis, which has been observed in other reports, but also a very diverse range of patterns involving glomeruli and tubulointerstitial region. Because Coxiella burnetii was detected in the blood of all Qf-GN, the presence of preexisting valvular heart disease or a history of artificial aortic graft replacement, might be considered potential risk factors for the development of Q fever-related infective endocarditis and nephritis.

The pathogenesis of Qf-GN is complex. The findings of elevated standardized uptake value in positron emission tomography/computed tomography image could provide evidence of active Q-fever inflammatory lesion. However, Coxiella burnetii could not be detected from the kidneys in some of our cases. The immune response in Q fever is critical for controlling the disease and is accompanied by systemic inflammation.1 Elevated IFN-γ levels in patients with Q fever activate macrophages to combat Coxiella burnetii,4 with both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells participating in the immune inflammation.6 However, chronic infection can impair immune effectiveness, leading to persistent bacterial replication.7 The adaptive response to Coxiella burnetii leads to antibody production, though the mechanisms are not fully elucidated8,9 (Figure S3). Serological findings such as cryoglobulinemia, positive PR3-ANCA antibody, decreased serum complement 3, elevated rheumatoid factor, serum IgA and IgG, along with the occurrence of monoclonal protein, provide compelling evidence. This suggests that the pathogenesis of Qf-GN involves an exaggerated immune-mediated inflammatory response to Coxiella burnetii rather than a direct invasion of renal tubular epithelial cells.S1,S2

Among the various detective methods, serological antibody testing by complement fixation is commonly employed, but is known for its limited accuracy and efficacy.S3 mNGS has become an invaluable diagnostic tool for identification of infectious agents, particularly those typically elusive due to culturing difficulties or low prevalence. It provides a holistic and unbiased strategy for detecting pathogens, capable of uncovering an extensive spectrum of microorganisms within a single test. This technology was pivotal in our Qf-GN cases, providing a rapid and definitive diagnosis of Q fever by detecting Coxiella burnetii DNA in blood and/or urine samples, thereby advantageous in guiding targeted antibiotic therapy.

Conclusion

Qf-GN poses a diagnostic challenge because of its nonspecific symptoms and its capacity to mimic autoimmune diseases. mNGS has been a crucial tool in our case series, facilitating the prompt and precise identification of Coxiella burnetii. As our understanding of Q fever's broader public health impact grows, the significance of advanced diagnostics such as mNGS is becoming more evident.
