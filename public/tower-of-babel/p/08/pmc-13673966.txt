Aortic-pulmonary conduit technique for tension-free coronary reimplantation in anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery repair
Authors: Eijiro Yamashita, Shivani Patel, Allison B Davila, Elisabeth Martin, David S Winlaw, Michael C Mongé
Published: 2026

LCAPA, Anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery; PA, pulmonary artery.

Central Message

In anatomically unfavorable ALCAPA, pulmonary commissural takedown enables wide autologous pulmonary arterial flap harvest for tension-free native tissue coronary reimplantation.

Anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA) is optimally treated by restoration of a dual-coronary system. Direct reimplantation of the anomalous left coronary artery (LCA) into the aorta provides excellent long-term outcomes.1 However, direct reimplantation may be technically challenging when the LCA arises from a posterior or nonfacing sinus of the pulmonary artery (PA) remote from the aorta. We describe a coronary elongation technique, highlighting pulmonary commissural takedown to facilitate wide PA flap harvest for tension-free LCA reimplantation in 3 infants. Institutional review board approval was not required, and informed consent was waived.

Operative Technique

Preoperative anatomy is shown in Figure 1, A. The branch PAs were snared immediately after commencing cardiopulmonary bypass to prevent coronary runoff. Antegrade cardioplegia was delivered into the aortic root, with an additional half-dose into the main PA to protect the anomalous coronary circulation. The main PA was transected. After we identified the coronary orifice, a generous coronary button was harvested together with a longitudinal anterior PA wall flap (Figure 1, B). When the coronary origin was adjacent to a pulmonary commissure, the commissure was partially taken down and the leaflet temporarily unhinged from the annulus to facilitate adequate PA flap harvest. A medially based flap was fashioned from the leftward aspect of the ascending aorta above the sinotubular junction. The aortic flap was first anastomosed to the coronary button (Figure 1, C). The aortic and PA flaps were then sutured together longitudinally with running 7-0 polypropylene sutures, creating a tubular conduit that elongated the coronary artery. In this configuration, the aortic flap formed the posterior wall of the conduit, and the PA flap formed the anterior wall (Figure 1, D). The aortic defect after flap harvest was closed using a PA flap and the PA defect was reconstructed using decellularized homograft (MatrACELL; LifeNet Health) patch. When a pulmonary valve commissure had been unhinged, it was reconstructed with interrupted 7-0 horizontal mattress sutures, ensuring appropriate leaflet coaptation. After repair, antegrade coronary flow was confirmed by intraoperative echocardiography. No mitral valve intervention was performed. Left atrial pressure was monitored during weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass.

Discussion

Restoration of a dual-coronary system by direct aortic implantation remains the standard treatment for ALCAPA. Long-term outcomes after direct reimplantation demonstrate excellent survival and recovery of ventricular function.1 In anatomically unfavorable cases, particularly when the LCA arises from a posterior or nonfacing pulmonary sinus, insufficient coronary length may cause tension at the anastomosis. Jonas2 emphasized the importance of a tension- and torsion-free coronary transfer and described a similar elongation technique using aortic and PA flaps. Amanullah and colleagues3 also reported a similar autogenous arterial conduit using posterior flap designs. In contrast, our anteriorly based aortic and PA flaps allow more straightforward donor-site reconstruction. Although the Takeuchi procedure remains an alternative strategy for anatomically challenging ALCAPA by constructing an intrapulmonary tunnel through an aortopulmonary window, late complications such as baffle obstruction have been described, and the procedure is now less commonly used.4 The present technique provides controlled elongation using combined autologous aortic and PA flaps without pericardial or synthetic interposition. Limitations include long-term remodeling of the autologous conduit.
