Cough biomarkers for diagnosis and monitoring of respiratory disease: a systematic review
Authors: Jinming Xu, Thayabaran Kathiresan, Alveena Siddiqui, Stuart B. Mazzone, Adam Vogel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cough is a common and physiologically informative component of respiratory morbidity, but its potential for diagnosing and monitoring disease is not thoroughly investigated. This systematic review synthesised the literature on algorithmic and statistical models analysing cough acoustics for diagnosing or monitoring respiratory conditions. Following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines, five databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and CENTRAL) were systematically searched for studies published from January 2010 to June 2025. Eligible studies performed quantitative acoustic feature analysis of human coughs using statistical, machine learning or deep learning models and reported diagnostic or prognostic performance. 89 studies from 34 countries were assessed, covering cough detection (n=31), disease classification (n=55) and disease severity prediction (n=3), reflecting potential applications in disease monitoring. Deep learning approaches, especially convolutional and recurrent networks, were predominant (n=56) and tended to achieve the higher accuracies, although machine learning ensemble methods and logistic regression also demonstrated strong performance, particularly with well-engineered features. Across different diseases, sensitivities and specificities were often reported to be ≥90%, notably for tuberculosis, asthma and COVID-19. However, methodological weaknesses were common, with only 11.2% of studies introducing external validation, 71.1–87.6% demonstrating high risk of bias (according to PROBAST-AI) and most based on small, homogeneous or crowdsourced cohorts with limited generalisability. These limitations contribute to inflated internal performance and uncertainty about real-world applicability. Cough acoustic biomarkers hold promise as an adjunctive tool for screening and longitudinal monitoring in low-resource environments. Nevertheless, widespread implementation will require large, multicentre validation, standardised calibration, bias control and incorporation into privacy-preserving workflows.

Introduction

Respiratory diseases are one of the leading causes of global hospitalisation and death [1, 2]. Chronic respiratory conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, affect hundreds of millions of patients globally [3]. COPD alone impacts near to 200 million individuals and causes over 3 million deaths yearly, making it the third leading cause of death [4]. Asthma, the most prevalent chronic disease among children, affects more than 350 million people worldwide [5]. Infectious respiratory diseases, including pneumonia and tuberculosis (TB), cause an estimated 2.4 million and 1.4 million yearly deaths, respectively [4]. The COVID-19 pandemic has revealed the critical vulnerabilities of current respiratory healthcare infrastructure, especially for the limited diagnostic capacity and surveillance resources [6]. Standard assessments (e.g. imaging, spirometry, sputum examination) may be unavailable in primary care settings, hindering timely diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring [7–9]. Therefore, noninvasive, scalable and affordable strategies are needed in this area.

Cough, a common cause of respiratory morbidity, can be readily recorded in both clinical and home settings using wearable and mobile technologies [10]. The acoustic signals of cough could reflect airway physiology and pathophysiological changes [11], enabling a novel and objective measure of disease [12]. Historically, physicians have diagnosed illness based on qualitative features of cough (e.g. “wet” versus “dry”) [13]. Advances in digital signal processing offer the possibility for quantitative characterisation of cough acoustics, rendering cough as a candidate digital biomarker for diagnosis and ongoing management [14].

Typically, acoustic analysis of cough involves temporal and spectral parameters, such as event frequency, duration, amplitude and pitch [15–17]. For instance, features describing the distribution of energy across sound frequencies (mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs)), measures of spectral complexity (spectral entropy) and resonance peaks of the vocal tract that shape the sound (formant frequencies) are commonly introduced to capture frequency content and airflow dynamics [18]. Specific spectral features may be linked with COVID-19, pneumonia, and asthma, enabling a machine learning based classifier to identify features of cough unique to these conditions [19]. Quantitative metrics such as cough frequency are found to correlate with clinical outcomes in COVID-19, pulmonary fibrosis and COPD patients [20–22]. Automated analysis of wet and dry coughs has been employed to determine the presence of sputum [23]. Collectively, these findings indicate that cough-based acoustic analysis is a noninvasive and promising approach, which could complement standard clinical evaluations [24, 25].

The clinical assessment of cough often relies on subjective and intermittent measures (e.g. auscultation, symptom scores and patient recall), which could result in variability and bias [13, 26]. The diagnostic accuracy of subjectively assessing cough sounds for physicians can even be as low as 34% [27]. The lack of continuous and standardised cough monitoring remains an unmet need for both diagnosis and disease management.

Research in this field has prospered in recent years, particularly since the COVID-19 pandemic [13]. Most studies have focused on single diseases (e.g. COVID-19) or on cough detection [13]. To date, no systematic review has exclusively synthesised evidence on single-modality cough-based models for diagnosing or prognosticating respiratory diseases.

Here, we synthesise studies that using cough acoustics for diagnosis or prognosis of respiratory diseases. It examines feature extraction strategies, modelling approaches, clinical end-points and performance metrics. Both contemporary algorithmic approaches and traditional statistical approaches were considered when they involved quantitative analysis of cough acoustic features.

Methods

Protocol registration

This review followed a predefined protocol registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration number: CRD420251084670). The review was designed and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [28].

Search strategy

Search terms are provided in supplementary material 1. Systematic literature searches were conducted across five databases, including PubMed (Medline), Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). Preprints, abstracts without full text and grey literature were excluded.

The timeframe for the search was limited to 1 January 2010–4 June 2025. Studies utilising traditional statistical models, such as logistic or linear regression, were also included if they performed quantitative analysis of cough acoustic features.

Screening and eligibility criteria

Following PRISMA 2020 guidelines [29], the study selection was performed in two steps. In the first step, two independent reviewers (J. Xu and A. Siddiqui) assessed the titles and abstracts of studies retrieved through the search strategy for relevance on COVIDENCE platform (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne). In the second step, full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or judgment by a third reviewer (A. Vogel).

Studies were eligible if they 1) reported original, peer-reviewed research involving human participants with diagnosed or suspected respiratory diseases (e.g. asthma, COPD, cystic fibrosis, pneumonia), 2) applied quantitative analysis of cough-based acoustic features for the diagnosis, monitoring or prognosis of respiratory conditions, 3) employed either statistical models (e.g. logistic or linear regression) or algorithmic approaches (e.g. machine learning, deep learning) to process cough audio recordings, 4) reported at least one diagnostic or prognostic performance metric (e.g. sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve (AUC)), and 5) were published in English with full-text availability.

Nonempirical studies (e.g. reviews, editorials, preprints), trial protocols and conference abstracts were excluded. The full eligibility criteria and the patient, intervention, comparison, outcome (PICO) framework can be found in supplementary material 2 [30].

Data extraction

Data were extracted by two independent reviewers (J. Xu and A. Siddiqui) from each study and included publication details (author, year, country), population characteristics (sample size, disease type), recording device, acoustic features, modelling approach, reference standard and performance metrics. Studies were coded as traditional statistical, conventional machine learning, and/or deep learning based on reported models. Multi-label coding was used when a study reported multiple modelling paradigms (nonmutually exclusive).

External validation was defined as evaluation on an independent dataset from a different site or centre. Internal validation referred to evaluations in which the data were collected from the development site, including evaluations across different time periods, devices or patient subgroups. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or judgment by a third reviewer (A. Vogel).

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and risk of each included study was evaluated by PROBAST-AI tool [31]. Four domains (participants, predictors, outcomes and analysis) were considered during the assessment. Two reviewers (J. Xu and A. Siddiqui) independently conducted the assessments and disagreements were resolved through discussion. Applicability was judged alongside the risk of bias. Studies were not excluded based on these assessments.

Results

Study selection

The search strategy yielded 4353 records. After removing 1975 duplicates, 2378 papers were further to be screened, following which 110 articles were retrieved for full-text evaluation. 89 studies met inclusion criteria and were synthesised (figure 1).

The flow chart of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA).

Overview of included studies

A total of 89 studies from 34 countries were included. Most were from Australia (n=15), the USA (n=13), the UK (n=11), China (n=11) and India (n=9) (figure 2). Among single-condition studies, 17 studies assessed COVID-19, nine assessed asthma and 14 assessed other conditions. In contrast, 49 studies focused on multiple respiratory diseases, including obstructive and infectious diseases without specific categories. 17 studies were paediatric-based, 42 were adult-based and 12 included both populations, whereas 18 did not report the age group. For synthesis, papers were classified by primary objective into three domains, namely 1) cough detection, for models designed to identify cough events from audio recordings, 2) disease classification, for models distinguishing patients using cough recordings, 3) and disease severity, for models evaluating current or future disease status. Studies that addressed disease classification or severity were placed in these categories even if they incorporated cough detection modules as intermediate steps.

Studies were further categorised based on methodological approaches used. Deep learning methods were used in 56 studies, while 29 studies employed conventional machine learning algorithms such as support vector machines (SVMs) and random forests (RFs). A smaller subset (n=21) utilised traditional statistical methods. One study did not state the exact type of the modelling approach applied. Data collection also varied, with 52 studies recording audio data in clinic or hospital settings, 27 employing public datasets (e.g. Virufy, Coswara, COUGHVID) and 10 collecting data in nonmedical or community-based settings. Analysis of publication trends revealed that seven articles were published between 2010 and 2014, 18 articles between 2015 and 2019, and 64 articles between 2020 and 2025, corresponding to a surge of interest in this field in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Cough detection studies

31 studies evaluated models for automatic cough detection, including datasets ranging from several hundred to over 650 000 cough events (table 1). Most studies enrolled relatively small cohort (10–30 participants), typically comprising healthy control participants and patients with pneumonia, COPD, asthma or interstitial lung disease. Spontaneous cough recordings were used in 21 studies and voluntary recordings in four studies. Cough audio was captured by external microphones (n=11) or smartphones (n=9), of which seven developed purpose-built recording hardware (e.g. customised microphones or wearable devices). Seven studies used public datasets, while the remainder relied on proprietary or hospital-derived recordings. Manual annotation by trained raters was the reference standard in nearly all investigations (n=28), typically with frame- or event-level labels.

Deep learning methods were employed in 22 studies, most frequently convolutional neural networks (CNNs) (n=12). Machine learning based studies (n=6) mainly used SVMs and RFs, whereas investigations employing statistical methods (n=5) often implemented logistic regression (LR) and hidden Markov models (HMMs). Acoustic feature extraction centred on mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) (n=16) and Mel spectrograms (n=7) and temporal derivatives (first- and second-order derivatives of MFCCs; n=3 each) to capture temporal dynamics. Less common features included formant frequencies (n=2) and other spectral features such as zero-crossing rate (ZCR), perceptual linear prediction (PLP) and relative spectral transform-PLP (RASTA-PLP) [62, 63]. Validation methods also varied substantially: cross-validation (leave-one-out or k-fold) was reported for 16 studies, independent train–test sets for 12 and only five studies had external validation on fully independent cohorts.

Overall model performance of cough event detection varied substantially across studies, with reported sensitivities ranging from 32% to 97.6% and specificities from 90.1% to 100% across audio-based systems (e.g. smartphone, microphone and wearable devices). Deep learning models (e.g. CNN- and bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM)-based models) frequently reported high accuracy and specificity (occasionally approaching 100%), while several traditional models (e.g. LR and SVM) also achieved accuracy and specificity above 90%. However, reliance on proprietary data and the absence of external validation in several studies may lead to optimistically biased performance estimates.

Cough-based disease classification studies

55 studies developed models to classify respiratory diseases using cough acoustics, analysing datasets varying from fewer than 50 to more than 5000 cough recordings (table 2). 17 studies exclusively focused on COVID-19, five on TB, five on pneumonia, four on asthma, one each on COPD and pertussis, and three on cough type (wet or dry), while 19 investigated multiple respiratory diseases. Adults represented the population most frequently studied (n=18), with 12 paediatric cohorts, nine of mixed groups and 16 studies lacking age specification. Voluntary coughs (n=18) were the most frequently obtained in comparison to spontaneous (n=8), with 24 not specifying the type of cough. Smartphones (n=30) were the most predominant recording device, followed by external microphones connected to recording systems (n=9) and stethoscope–smartphone hybrid systems (n=2). Physician-confirmed diagnosis (n=30) served as the primary reference standard, while additional reliance on laboratory testing (e.g. PCR) (n=8) and self-report (n=6).

MFCCs were the most frequent of the common acoustic features (n=24), complemented by ΔMFCCs (n=3), mel spectrograms (n=5), formant frequencies (n=6) and ZCR (n=16). CNN was the most implemented model (n=19), followed by recurrent systems such as Bi-LSTM and GRU (gated recurrent unit) (n=7), transformer-based or hybrid architectures (n=4) and ensemble schemes (n=3). Machine learning methods comprised SVM (n=16), and RF (n=7), whereas statistical approaches were dominated by LR (n=12). While rule-based algorithms were rarely implemented (n=1). Internal validation commonly involved K-fold or leave-one-out cross-validation (n=35) and train–test splits (n=25). Only five studies performed external validation, and some of them reported obviously lower performance compared with studies without external validation [76, 82].

Model performance varied across diseases. The reported metrics were derived from studies using different classification frameworks, most commonly binary disease versus healthy tasks, with fewer addressing multiple diseases directly. For COVID-19 (n=17), sensitivities ranged 72.0–97.9%, specificities 62.9–100.0% and AUCs 63.9–97.3%. For TB (n=5), sensitivities 93.0–98.1%, specificities 94.99–96.0% and one external validation reported an AUC of 90.7%. An asthma classifier (n=4) reported sensitivities ranged 80.41–98.19% and specificities 98.3–98.9%. Pneumonia models (n=5) yielded sensitivities that ranged 82.1–94.1%, specificities 71.4–87.5%, and AUCs 79.9–86.4%. Across 15 additional studies that assessed multiple respiratory diseases, larger datasets (>1000 recordings) were associated with more stable performance (>90% sensitivity and specificity), while smaller cohorts showed greater variability. Among studies directly comparing algorithms, deep learning methods (particularly CNN-based and hybrid architectures) generally achieved a better performance than traditional classifiers such as SVMs and RFs.

Cough-based disease severity prediction studies

Three studies investigated disease severity prediction, with sample sizes ranging from 123 to 224 participants (supplementary material 3). Conditions included COVID-19 (n=1) [20], asthma (n=1) [116] and multiple respiratory diseases (n=1) [117]. Two studies enrolled adults, while one focused on children. Smartphones were introduced for audio acquisition in all studies, with voluntary (n=1), spontaneous (n=1) and mixed (n=1) cough recordings. All data were collected exclusively from hospital sources. Clinical outcomes (e.g. intubation or death), physician-confirmed diagnoses or pulmonary function tests served as reference standards.

Features included MFCCs, ZCR, spectral energy, bispectrum, wavelet-based measures and cough rate. LR was used in two studies, while one study compared different classical machine learning algorithms, including support vector regression, gradient boosting and RFs. Deep learning models were not applied. Leave-one-out cross-validation (n=1), five-fold cross-validation (n=1) and combined train–test splits with cross-validation (n=1) were introduced as validation methods. None of the studies performed external validation.

Predictive performance was modest. LR for COVID-19 severity yielded AUCs of 76.1–79.2%, with sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 23.3–32.9%. For paediatric asthma, accuracy reached 80.0% (sensitivity 82.6%, specificity 78.4%). Regression-based prediction of spirometry indices demonstrated R2 values of 0.82 for forced expiratory volume in 1 s and 0.84 for forced vital capacity.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias and applicability was assessed using the PROBAST-AI framework (figure 3 and supplementary material 4). Substantial methodological heterogeneity was observed across studies. Risk of bias indicates methodological limitations may compromise the internal validity. Applicability indicates if a study aligns with the intended clinical setting. During model development, 19.3% of studies were judged high risk in the participants and data source domain, typically due to reliance on crowd-sourced datasets or cohorts comprising only healthy participants. This also raised high applicability concerns in 20.5% of cases. Predictor related risk was consistently low (>98%), reflecting the objectivity and reproducibility of cough acoustic features and binary disease labels. Outcome risk was moderate, with 15.7% of studies rated high due to self-reported or unverified diagnostic labels. The analysis domain was most problematic (e.g. data leakage, poor handling of missing data, inadequate calibration reporting and lack of overfitting control), only 28.9% of studies were rated low risk.

In the evaluation phase, 41.6% of studies were rated high risk for participants and data sources, while predictor and outcome domains remained largely low risk. However, 85.4% of evaluation studies exhibited high risk in analysis because of the inconsistent reporting of performance metrics and lack of pre-specified decision thresholds. Overall, 71.1% of development and 87.6% of evaluation studies demonstrated high risk of bias, while high applicability concerns were less prevalent (25.3% and 43.8%, respectively). These methodological shortcomings collectively limit the generalisability of reported findings and may contribute to inflated performance estimates.

Discussion

This systematic review investigated current evidence for the role of cough acoustics as digital biomarkers for diagnosing and monitoring respiratory diseases. Across 89 studies spanning cough detection, disease classification and severity prediction, 71 studies reported sensitivity, of which 43 (60.6%) were ≥90%; 56 studies reported specificity, of which 39 (69.6%) were ≥90%; 61 studies reported accuracy, of which 41 (67.2%) were ≥90%. This suggests that cough has the potential to be assessed as a quantifiable biomarker that could enhance standard assessments in acute and chronic respiratory practice. However, these metrics were frequently obtained from study designs with substantial risk of bias and should be interpreted cautiously.

Cough is often related to a pathophysiological process in the airways [118], although it can also occur in the absence of any identifiable disease [119]. Its acoustic properties, i.e. frequency, time dynamics and spectrum profile, can represent both structural and functional changes in the respiratory tract [104, 113, 120]. Computer analysis has the potential to mitigate subjectivity and intermittency of conventional assessments such as auscultation or patient history by presenting standardised and quantifiable evaluations and is beginning to support continuous monitoring [121–123].

Physiological basis and potential clinical utility

A typical cough consists of an inspiratory phase, a compressive phase and an expiratory phase [11], and its acoustic signal encodes specific changes in airway mechanics and morphology, including phase timing and burst structure. For instance, reduced airway calibre in asthma may alter the spectral shape of cough sounds (e.g. increased high-frequency energy), consistent with more turbulent airflow through narrowed airways [124]. Mucus hypersecretion may enhance air–mucus interactions, introducing intermittent “wet” components and increasing cough spectro–temporal variability, which may be characterised by spectral complexity and energy-envelope dynamics [125]. Pathological changes in airway structure and wall mechanics may alter airway acoustic impedance and resonant/attenuation characteristics, potentially reshaping the spectral envelope of cough sounds (e.g. MFCCs) [104, 126, 127]. Consequently, continuous digital cough monitoring meets core biomarker criteria; it is objective, scalable and responsive to disease presence and progression, enabling both diagnostic classification [20] and longitudinal tracking [128].

Features and analytic approaches in modelling

Cough acoustics research typically involves feature representation (how the sound is described) and analytic modelling (how the description is linked to diagnosis or prognosis). For feature representations, handcrafted features (e.g. MFCCs and spectral entropy) are selected based on prior knowledge and bring varying levels of interpretability [129]; whereas learned features are derived directly from the waveform or time-frequency representations (e.g. VGGish embeddings extracted from log-mel spectrograms [130]), which can capture complex, high-dimensional patterns but are less directly interpretable. Modelling approaches range from traditional statistical algorithms (e.g. LR or HMMs), which are interpretable and adequate for small datasets [131, 132]; traditional machine learning methods (e.g. random forests or support vector machines), which are flexible but feature-dependent [133, 134]; and deep learning methods (e.g. CNN-/recurrent neural network -based models), which frequently achieve strong performance when data scale and cohort diversity are adequate [135]. Deep learning can learn task-relevant patterns and decision function automatically [136], rather than depending on a predefined feature set. However, deep learning is more computationally demanding and requires careful training and validation strategies to mitigate overfitting in small or homogeneous datasets [137, 138]. Supplementary material 5 provides an intuitive comparison of these approaches and features.

Within the studies included in this systematic review, these analytic approaches demonstrated different performance profiles depending on data scale, cohort diversity and validation strategy. Traditional machine learning models such as RF performed well when paired with proper engineered acoustic features. For example, Mahmood et al. [90] reported an F1 score of about 97% (accuracy 94%) for TB classification using an RF model in their in-house dataset. Deep learning consistently achieved the highest accuracies by automatically extracting salient time–frequency patterns. Convolutional neural network systems were the most widely implemented, with Barata et al. [36] demonstrating ∼99% accuracy for cough detection in a curated cohort. These networks frequently outperformed SVM- or RF-based models in disease classification by improving AUC or sensitivity by several points [114, 139]. Nonetheless, the performance of deep learning mainly depended on sample size and diversity. Models trained on small, homogeneous cohorts risk overfitting and report inflated internal metrics, sometimes approaching perfect accuracy without external validation [70, 75, 97]. Simpler statistical models, such as LR, also demonstrated diagnostic value in cough analysis. A TB screening study combining sequential feature selection with LR achieved an AUC of ∼0.94 (93% sensitivity, 95% specificity) [91], meeting World Health Organization triage standards [140]. Similarly, an LR model for pertussis diagnosis achieved ∼92% sensitivity and 90% specificity [85]. These findings suggest that even deep learning dominates as data scale increases, both tree-based ensembles and linear models still can efficiently capture disease-specific acoustic patterns.

Data sources and dataset quality

Public datasets including COUGHVID and Coswara [141, 142] provide large and diverse samples that contain varied recording conditions and demographics, making it feasible to develop robust models. Crowdsourced datasets often rely on self-reported labels and variable recording quality, which have been shown to contribute to label noise and performance variability, especially when compared to datasets with medically confirmed diagnoses [143, 144]. Many COVID-19 diagnostic models trained on public datasets reported high cross-validation accuracy [70, 75, 78]. However, when assessed in independent cohorts or cross-dataset evaluation that better reflect real-world deployment, their performance was often attenuated, with receiver operating characteristic AUCs typically 0.65–0.85 [82, 74]. By contrast, hospital-derived datasets, which use physician-confirmed diagnoses and controlled acquisition, supported models achieving internal accuracies (>90% in many studies) [67, 68, 90, 93]. However, their small and homogenous cohorts limited generalisability. 11.2% of studies implemented external validation, most of which showed marked reductions in accuracy when tested on independent data. Within disease classification studies, voluntary coughs were more commonly used than spontaneous cough. Physiologically, voluntary cough is a cortically mediated respiratory event, whereas spontaneous cough can be an automated brainstem reflex or an evoked response with varying levels of volitional or cognitive control [145]. Accordingly, volitional and spontaneous cough could cause materially different biomechanics and acoustic patterns [146]. In addition, voluntary coughs are usually recorded in more standardised settings, while spontaneous coughs are often captured in noisier environments. Those distinctions have direct implications for model generalisability and Sharma et al. [147] reported a substantial performance reduction when a model trained on spontaneous coughs was evaluated on voluntary coughs. These findings underscore the need to balance data variety and quality. Combined strategies that pre-train models on large public datasets and then fine-tune them on high-quality clinical samples may offer a plausible path to robust performance; however, this approach remains underexplored in the current literature.

Despite promising results [148], widespread clinical adoption of artificial intelligence-based cough analysis remains constrained by issues such as dataset standardisation [77], limited external validation [13, 23] and concerns about model explainability [149]. Addressing these barriers through rigorous validation and transparent methodologies will be critical for translation into real-world respiratory care.

Comparison with previous studies

The findings of this systematic review are consistent with previous studies suggesting that cough sounds contain diagnostically meaningful attributes. Work by Laguarta et al. [150] reported near-perfect COVID-19 classification using neural networks (sensitivity 98.5%). However, these findings were later moderated by more rigorous evaluations, including those by Coppock et al. [151] and Han et al. [152], which revealed biases related to demographic imbalances and study design issues. Pramono et al. [85] demonstrated the feasibility of automated pertussis detection via MFCC-based LR and similar principles have since been applied to pneumonia, TB and asthma. Porter et al. [153] further validated smartphone-based pneumonia diagnosis with ∼94% AUC in primary care. Notably, most recent studies still rely on cough data alone to improve applicability in remote or low-resource settings where advanced diagnostic tools are limited. Meanwhile, some studies have begun to integrate cough audio with additional data modalities, such as patient-reported outcomes, respiratory sounds or other clinical biomarkers [154, 155], to improve performance and clinical robustness. Despite cough alone representing a promising digital biomarker, these models require stringent validation in diverse populations, including variability in demographics, underlying causes of cough and recording environments, to ensure generalisability.

Many studies exhibited high diagnostic performance, but these metrics are likely optimistic and methodological shortcomings limit the real-world translation. Most studies employed internal validation alone, used small or homogeneous cohorts, and did not report pre-specified thresholds, calibration or handling of missing data. Under these conditions, models may overfit cohort-specific patterns, leading to inflated performance that may not generalise to routine care. For example, small sample sizes and homogeneous participant groups can reduce clinical variability and exaggerate apparent discrimination. The lack of external validation provides limited evidence on performance across sites, devices and populations. Incomplete separation of participants or recordings may introduce data leakage (e.g. coughs from the same subject appearing in multiple splits), allowing models to exploit participant- or recording-specific “shortcuts” and thereby inflating accuracy. In addition, some studies reported performance at the participant level, while others relied on window-, event- or recording-level evaluation, regarding correlated within-subject segments or events as independent samples (pseudo-replication). Together, these issues could encourage models to learn participant- or recording-specific patterns rather than disease-related pathology, leading to inflated metrics.

Although recent studies have begun to explore transformer networks [95, 107] and self-supervised learning [70] and the use of cough monitoring for prognostic purposes (e.g. lung function tracking in chronic diseases) [117], these approaches remain in early stages and have not yet addressed the prevailing methodological limitations. Consistent with this, PROBAST-AI assessments revealed that over 70% of development and evaluation studies were at high risk of bias, largely due to issues such as poor control for overfitting and inconsistent reporting. Collectively, these shortcomings suggest that, despite promising results, the reliability of current models remains uncertain without prospective studies including external validation and standardised methodology.

Clinical implications

Triage and diagnostic workflow

Clinical diagnosis of respiratory conditions requires integration of medical history, imaging, laboratory investigations and physiological tests [156]. In this context, cough-based algorithms could assist triage and telemedicine [20, 157] by providing rapid and noninvasive assessment when access to imaging or laboratory testing is limited [110]. Our review identified models for TB approached the triage performance suggested by World Health Organization [91]. Integrating these tools into telemedicine platforms could enable “screen-out” decisions in low-resource areas [153, 158], while prioritising high-risk patients for confirmatory testing and in-person assessment.

Longitudinal monitoring

Continuous acoustic monitoring can provide a more objective measure of cough frequency and temporal pattern than patient recall [159]. As cough burden can correlate with disease severity in conditions such as TB and COPD (e.g. an increase in cough frequency could serve as an early warning signal) [160]. Similarly, reduction in cough burden may provide a complementary marker of treatment response (e.g. following antibiotic therapy), supporting treatment optimisation. Though our review found that severity prediction models are currently less common, these longitudinal applications remain a valuable translational direction that require pre-defined alerting rules and meaningful change.

Practical implementation

Commercial systems, including Hyfe AI, Strados RESP, and ResAppDx (supplementary material 6), illustrate ongoing translational efforts. These platforms report cough event detection sensitivities near 90% with low false-positive rates [161–163]. In addition, VitaloJAK cough monitoring system is Conformité Européenne (CE)-marked and has US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 510(k) clearance [14]. Nevertheless, broader clinical adoption will depend on demonstrating that these tools complement standard diagnostic pathways and provide measurable improvements in clinical outcomes, workflow efficiency or accessibility [164]. Widespread clinical adoption must address privacy and data security [165], as audio-based monitoring is privacy-sensitive. Emerging approaches such as on-device/edge processing and feature-based encryption [166, 167], may offer practical solutions. In this architecture, raw audio is processed locally on patients’ phones or wearable devices to extract anonymous features and only the nonreconstructable data would be transmitted to clinical centre. Ultimately, successful deployment will require interoperability with electronic health records to support clinical decision-making.

Study limitations

Despite the comprehensive approach, this review still has limitations. First, although the search strategy was refined with a librarian to balance the sensitivity and specificity, some eligible studies may still have been missed due to the heterogeneous terminology across papers. Second, restricting the search to English-language, peer-reviewed literature may have excluded relevant studies from regions with high respiratory disease burdens. In addition, excluding grey literature may have introduced availability and publication bias and underrepresented emerging methods, since digital health evidence was often disseminated via nonpeer-reviewed channels during COVID-19 era. This review aimed to assess cough as a single biomarker for respiratory conditions. Models using multimodal data were not included, so some higher-performing approaches may have been overlooked [168]. The included studies varied widely in populations, reference standards, evaluation units, validation designs and reported metrics, without sufficiently comparable endpoints to support structured quantitative synthesis. Therefore, meta-analysis and other quantitative pooling was not undertaken. Furthermore, publication bias may have favoured studies reporting positive results [169] and the absence of prospective or longitudinal evaluations restricts inferences about real-world effectiveness.

Conclusion

This systematic review shows that cough acoustics analysed with statistical and algorithmic models can achieve high performance for classification and monitoring across respiratory diseases. Cough is physiologically informative and easy to record, and its acoustic signatures exhibit promise as digital biomarkers to complement standard assessments. However, methodological limitations such as small and homogeneous cohorts, lack of external validation, and inconsistent analytic standards restrict generalisability and real-world reliability. Translational potential is greatest for telemedicine and triage in low-resource settings. Future research should prioritise large multicentre validation, longitudinal applications for treatment response and privacy-preserving clinical integration to ensure robust and equitable deployment.

Questions for future research

Current research is predominantly limited to single-label classification due to the lack of multimorbidity datasets and the complexity of overlapping acoustic features. This limitation is salient for co-presentations such as asthma with COVID-19 or COPD with TB, which may reduce disease specificity in cough-based models. Some studies addressed this by grouping heterogeneous conditions under a generic “patient” label (including both single and comorbid conditions) [108]. Meanwhile, cough elicitation context is a major source of domain shift, potentially limiting cross-setting generalisability.
