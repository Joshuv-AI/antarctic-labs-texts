The buffering effect of social support on the link between biologically embedded childhood maltreatment and adult cardiovascular disease
Authors: Man-Kit Lei, Rachael Dean Weaver
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Associations between childhood maltreatment and adult cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk are well-documented. However, previous research has relied on self-rated health and non-representative cross-sectional data, raising questions about the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, less is known about factors that may buffer the biological embedding of early adversity into health outcomes in adulthood.Objective:This study examines whether childhood maltreatment predicts CVD risk in adulthood using objective biomarkers and whether social support buffers this relationship in line with the stress-buffering hypothesis.Methods:We analyzed data from 4137 participants in Waves 1, 3, and 5 of the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health). Childhood maltreatment and social support were measured using validated scales, and 30-year CVD risk was estimated using the Framingham Cardiovascular Risk Score. Fractional outcome models were used to examine the main and moderating effects, and sensitivity analyses using Heckman selection models were conducted to address potential selection bias.Results:Childhood maltreatment was significantly related to elevated adult CVD risk, supporting the biological embedding model. In line with the stress-buffering hypothesis, social support moderated this association, buffering the adverse health effects of early maltreatment.Conclusions:Findings support the stress-buffering hypothesis and highlight the importance of early social support in mitigating the long-term physiological impact of childhood adversity. Integrating the biological embedding model with the stress-buffering hypothesis can improve understanding of resilience and inform strategies to reduce health disparities rooted in early life experiences.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, contributing significantly to long-term disability and escalating healthcare costs (Ahmad et al., 2024). Although lifestyle and genetic factors are well-established contributors, a growing body of evidence reports that childhood experiences can profoundly impact CVD risk across the lifespan (Basu et al., 2017; Doom et al., 2017; Suglia et al., 2018). Among these experiences, childhood maltreatment, including abuse and neglect, represents a potent form of toxic stress that has been linked to a range of adverse health outcomes in adulthood, including elevated risk for cardiovascular conditions (Deer et al., 2025; McEwen and McEwen, 2017). For example, Basu et al. (2017), in a systematic review of 40 studies, found that 91.7 percent reported significant associations between childhood maltreatment and CVD, while over 88 percent and 60 percent linked childhood maltreatment to diabetes and hypertension, respectively.

One of the most widely accepted explanations for how early experiences shape adult health is the biological embedding model (Hertzman, 2012; Miller et al., 2011). This model posits that early-life environments, with a focus on those involving exposure to stress and caregiving adversity, “get under the skin” and become encoded, producing lasting effects on physiological pathways involving the nervous, immune, and endocrine systems (Shonkoff et al., 2009). Through these mechanisms, early environmental exposures have stable, enduring effects on developmental phenotypes relevant to health and bodily systems (Aristizabal et al., 2020; Traniello and Robinson, 2021). This biological embedding acts as a kind of imprint on one’s DNA and brain, recording the suffering and stress one has experienced and setting up the body’s response to future stressors. Embedded exposures shape both the strengths and vulnerabilities of a developing system and allow individuals to adapt to their early environments and prepare for facing similar ones in the future. However, if later-life events differ significantly from those encountered early on, these coping strategies may become mismatched, increasing the risk for maladaptive outcomes and disease (Bateson et al., 2014; Berens et al., 2017). In this way, early experiences like childhood maltreatment and adversity leave a “biological record” that contributes to long-term patterns of health and disease, influencing health trajectories across the life course (McGowan, 2012).

Although the biological embedding model provides a useful framework for understanding how early adversity influences later health outcomes, it does not fully account for the heterogeneity in CVD risk among individuals exposed to childhood maltreatment. Individuals who experience childhood maltreatment are generally at higher risk for CVD; however, a significant number of those who experience abuse and neglect do not show an increased risk. At the most general level, this discrepancy is often attributed to resilience, which is the ability to maintain health despite exposure to serious early stress (Masten, 2001, 2018). One important factor that may help explain this resilience is social support, which is defined as the range of support people receive from their social networks, such as parents, friends, and significant others, to effectively manage stressful life events (Thoits, 2011).

Consistent with the view of social support as a form of resilience, extensive research within the stress-buffering hypothesis suggests that social support can reduce the negative effects of childhood toxic stress on adult health outcomes (Brody et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2017; Cohen and Wills, 1985; John-Henderson et al., 2020; Lagdon et al., 2021; Lei et al., 2020; Panagou and Macbeth, 2024; Su et al., 2022). Supportive relationships within social networks help regulate stress responses, reduce physiological strain, and promote more effective coping strategies, thereby providing protection against adverse physical health outcomes. In this way, social support helps protect long-term health following childhood maltreatment by buffering stress, enhancing emotional well-being, and fostering adaptive behaviors that can prevent or even reverse the biological embedding of social adversity (Entringer and Heim, 2025), ultimately contributing to better physical health throughout the life course. Accordingly, by integrating physiological and social mechanisms, including the protective role of social support, this combined perspective helps explain how childhood maltreatment contributes to later health disparities while accounting for individual differences in resilience. In integrating the biological embedding model with the stress-buffering hypothesis, the present study extends previous work in several ways by more precisely examining the link between childhood maltreatment and adult CVD, as well as how social support buffers this relationship.

First, relatively few studies on childhood maltreatment have assessed objective indicators of physical health. Although self-rated health can be a stronger predictor of mortality than some objective biomarkers (Altman et al., 2016), it has important limitations. Research relying on self-reported health measures may either overestimate or overlook important social factors influencing physical illness (Au and Johnston, 2014; Simons et al., 2021). For instance, Dowd and Todd (2011) reported that failing to account for differences in age, sex, education, and race when analyzing self-rated health can lead to a significant underestimation of health disparities. Moreover, such measures may miss underlying health problems entirely, as they typically fail to detect asymptomatic or pre-disease conditions. Research also suggests that self-assessments are susceptible to various cognitive biases and limitations and thus may be more reflective of individuals’ emotional states or perceptions than their objective physiological condition (Bollen et al., 2021; Widom, 2019). Beyond these concerns about validity and reliability, self-reported health measures provide limited insight into how social experiences contribute to disease.

To address these limitations and better understand how social environments impact health in adulthood, researchers have increasingly turned to objective biomarkers. These biomarkers offer quantifiable and reproducible measures of physiological functioning, helping to clarify the biological pathways through which social stressors affect health (Harris and Schorpp, 2018). In particular, CVD risk biomarkers receive particular attention given the well-established links between stress, inflammation, and cardiovascular disease (Vaccarino and Bremner, 2024). Furthermore, focusing on CVD risk, rather than only diagnosed disease, enables early identification of individuals at heightened risk, providing a critical window for preventive strategies and timely interventions that can reduce the onset or severity of CVD.

In this study, we use the Framingham Cardiovascular Risk Score (Pencina et al., 2009), a widely validated and objective tool developed from the Framingham Heart Study. This score integrates multiple clinical and behavioral risk factors, including age, sex, blood pressure, use of antihypertensive treatment, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and diabetes mellitus, to estimate an individual’s probability of developing CVD within the next 30 years (D’Agostino Sr. et al., 2013). This comprehensive risk score offers a more precise assessment of CVD risk, going beyond subjective measures and improving understanding of the biological embedding of childhood maltreatment and its long-term consequences.

Second, upon close inspection of the literature, the American Heart Association (AHA) recently released a scientific statement highlighting important limitations in current research on the impact of childhood adversity, particularly the predominant use of cross-sectional reports of early life conditions (Suglia et al., 2018). Studies using cross-sectional designs cannot determine whether childhood adversity precedes the development of cardiovascular disease (CVD), limiting their value for prevention research. This approach also raises concerns about recall bias, potentially obscuring the true nature of the relationship (Hardt and Rutter, 2004). To overcome this issue, longitudinal data are important. Following individuals over time allows researchers to establish temporal ordering, capture the dynamic nature of adversity, and thus more reliably assess its effects on later life CVD. Further, childhood maltreatment in this study refers specifically to experiences before sixth grade (approximately age 11), a developmental period preceding puberty and adolescence. This distinction is important, as timing may alter biological embedding processes such as recalibration of the HPA axis.

Finally, previous work has consistently linked early-life adversity to health outcomes (Carson et al., 2022; Kisely et al., 2023; Lei et al., 2020). Building on these findings, analyses of nationally representative data such as the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health) have shown that childhood maltreatment predicts an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease in adulthood (e.g., Gooding et al., 2014; Norman et al., 2012; Parker & La Manna, 2004; Suglia et al., 2014). More recently, researchers have examined potential moderators and found that protective resources, particularly strong social support, can buffer the long-term cardiometabolic consequences of early maltreatment (John-Henderson et al., 2020; Lagdon et al., 2021; Lei et al., 2020; Panagou and Macbeth, 2024). However, studies exploring these moderating effects remain relatively scarce, and even fewer have used objective physical health biomarkers as outcome measures. This gap limits our understanding of both the generalizability of existing findings and the mechanisms through which resilience may mitigate long-term consequences.

To address this, we drew on nationally representative, longitudinal data from the Add Health, which followed participants over a 20-year period. This dataset allows for a robust examination of the association between childhood maltreatment and adult CVD risk, as measured by the Framingham 30-year CVD risk score. It also allows us to examine whether perceived social support can moderate this association, offering insights into potential resilience pathways and informing future intervention strategies. We proposed two hypotheses. First, based on the biological embedding model, we hypothesized that retrospective reports of childhood maltreatment would be associated with increased cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in adulthood, as measured at Wave 5 (ages 33 to 39), even after controlling for sociodemographic and health behavior covariates. Second, in line with the stress-buffering hypothesis, we hypothesized that perceived social support at baseline would moderate this association. Specifically, individuals with higher levels of perceived support were expected to show a weaker association between childhood maltreatment and adult CVD risk, whereas those with lower levels of support were expected to exhibit a stronger link between childhood maltreatment and elevated CVD risk in adulthood.

Method

Participants

Our analyses utilized data from Waves 1, 3, and 5 of the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health), a nationally representative dataset (Harris et al., 2019). The study employed a multistage, stratified, school-based, cluster sampling design to select a probability sample. The first wave of data collection began in 1994 with 20,745 adolescents in grades 7–12. Of these adolescents, 15,197 at Wave 3 (2001–2002, ages 18–26), and 5381 at Wave 5 (2016–2018, ages 33–39) had biomarker data available. The Add Health study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of North Carolina. The secondary analysis was approved by our institution’s IRB (Protocol Title: Add Health Analysis; Study Number: 00000291). Of the 20,745 participants in Wave 1, 16,607 were excluded due to missing data in Waves 1, 3, or 5, missing biomarker data at Wave 5, or missing sample weight variables. The final analytic sample consists of 4137 participants with complete data on all relevant measures. Participant flow through the study is detailed in the flowchart (Fig. 1).

Measures

Cardiovascular risk.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk was calculated using the gender-specific 30-year Framingham risk algorithm developed by Pencina et al. (2009) (https://www.framinghamheartstudy.org/fhs-risk-functions/cardiovascular-disease-30-year-risk/). The Framingham algorithm estimates the 30-year risk of CVD events, including coronary death, myocardial infarction, fatal or non-fatal stroke, transient ischemic attack, intermittent claudication, and congestive heart failure based on risk factors such as systolic blood pressure (SBP), body mass index (BMI), diabetes status, age, sex, current smoking status, and use of antihypertensive medication. SBP was measured after a 5-min seated rest; three readings were taken at 30-s intervals, and the average of the final two measurements was used to determine resting SBP. Among the 4137 participants, the mean SBP was 122.08 mmHg (SD = 14.17), and the mean BMI was 30.18 kg/m2 (SD = 7.24). Diabetes was defined as either the use of anti-diabetic medication or having a hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level greater than 6.4 %, with 5.73 % of participants classified as diabetic under this criterion. Using the Framingham algorithm, the predicted 30-year CVD risk represents the estimated probability of developing CVD within the next 30 years, with values ranging from 0 to 100 percent. At the time of the Wave 5 blood draw, the mean predicted 30-year CVD risk was 13.48 %. As shown in online Supplementary Fig. S2, the density plot shows the distribution of 30-year CVD risk, implying variability among individuals and a peak around 0.10 (10 %).

Childhood Maltreatment.

During Wave 3 (2001–2002), participants were retrospectively asked four items assessing the frequency of specific forms of childhood maltreatment by a parent or other adult caregiver before sixth grade (i.e., before age 11, prior to most participants’ pubertal onset) (see Bayly et al., 2022; Fuemmeler et al., 2009; Kim, 2009; Suglia et al., 2014; Valgardson and Schwartz, 2018). Given the sensitivity of the topic, all items were administered via computer-assisted self-interviewing (CASI). Response options ranged from 1 (one time) to 5 (more than ten times), with an additional option of 6 (this has never happened). Consistent with prior research, the rarity of repeated occurrences led us to dichotomize each item by coding the presence of a maltreatment type as 1 and its absence as 0 and then sum these indicators to construct a cumulative maltreatment index (Brumley et al., 2019). The items included physical abuse (e.g., being slapped, hit, or kicked), sexual abuse (e.g., being touched sexually, forced to touch someone sexually, or forced into sexual activity), physical neglect (e.g., lacking basic needs such as cleanliness, food, or clothing), and supervisory neglect (e.g., being left home alone when adult supervision was necessary). Responses were summed to create a total childhood maltreatment score ranging from 0 to 4.

Social Support.

Social support was measured at baseline as the average of responses to four items assessing how much participants felt that (1 = not at all; 5 = very much): (a) adults care about them, (b) teachers care about them, (c) their parents care about them, and (d) their friends care about them (Shen et al., 2025). Higher scores reflect greater perceived social support. The scores were averaged to create the social support measure (α ≥ .60).

Control variables.

We controlled for several demographic variables. Chronological age was calculated by subtracting the date of birth from the date of biomarker collection at Wave 5. Sex is a binary variable where 1 indicates the respondent is male. We used dummy variables to specify race, including Black, Hispanic, and other (Asian and Pacific Islander, Native American, and other), with White as the reference group. Educational attainment was based on responses to the Wave 5 question, “What is the highest level of education that you have achieved to date?” The original 16 response categories were coded into five levels: 0 = less than 8th grade, 1 = less than high school, 2 = high school graduate or GED, 3 = some education beyond high school (vocational or college), and 4 = college or university graduate.

In addition, we controlled for various early health behaviors that may have impacted the CVD risk outcome calculated at Wave 5. At Wave 1, respondents reported past-week moderate-to-vigorous physical activity through three items on active recreation, sports, and exercise. Responses ranged from none to ≥5 times. Following previous studies (e.g., García-Hermoso et al., 2023), engaging in more than five MVPA sessions per week (coded as 1; otherwise = 0) served as a proxy for meeting physical activity guidelines. Adequate sleep was assessed with a single item at Wave 1: “Do you usually get enough sleep?” A “yes” response was coded as 1, and a “no” response was coded as 0. Healthy diet was also assessed at Wave 1 with two items asking how many times the respondent had eaten fruit and vegetables the previous day. Responses were coded to 0 = none, 1 = once, and 2 = twice or more, and the two items were then averaged to create a composite healthy-diet score (higher values indicate more frequent consumption). Finally, we controlled for an important social determinant of health, neighborhood poverty, defined as the proportion of individuals in the respondent’s block group living below the poverty line at Wave 1.

Analytic strategy

All analyses were performed using Stata 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) and were weighted using the Wave 5 biomarker sample weight (W5BIOWGT). We employed fractional outcome regression models using the Stata “fracreg” command with a logit link function (Baum, 2008), as the dependent variable, Framingham 30-year CVD risk score, is a proportion bounded between 0 and 1. The use of a logit link allows for interpretation of the estimated coefficients in a manner similar to a logit model. Results are reported as odds ratios (eb), which reflect the multiplicative change in the odds of higher CVD risk associated with a one-unit change in the independent variables.

Variables were entered into fractional outcome regression models in the following steps: (a) the main effects model (model 1), which estimated the effect of childhood maltreatment on 30-year CVD risk; (b) the main effects model with control variables (model 2), which tested the effects while adjusting for covariates; (c) the main and control effects model with social support (model 3), which examined the association between social support and the outcome; and (d) the stress-buffering model (model 4), which tested interaction effects by including the interaction between childhood maltreatment and social support. Since the interaction term was significant, we conducted simple slope tests and graphed the interaction using 95 % error bars, plotting regression lines for childhood maltreatment at one standard deviation above and below the mean of social support.

Finally, to address potential selection bias from sample attrition and missing data and to test the robustness of our findings, we reestimated the theoretical models using Heckman’s two-step selection procedure (Heckman, 1979; Bushway et al., 2007). In the first step, the inverse Mills ratio was calculated; in the second step, this value was included in the original statistical model to test whether the theoretical model was influenced by selection bias.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Table 1 presents the bivariate correlations among the focal study variables, health behaviors, and demographic covariates. CVD risk shows significant zero-order associations with childhood maltreatment experiences and perceived social support, and it is also linked to age, educational attainment, gender, race, healthy-diet adherence, and neighborhood poverty. Numerous covariates are correlated with one another, suggesting the value of retaining them as controls in subsequent analyses.

Means and percentages for study variables are displayed in Table 2. Among the 4137 respondents used in our analysis (2519 females and 1618 males), the mean age at Wave 5 was 38.43 years (SD = 1.904), and 48.8 percent had completed four years of college or more. The racial/ethnic composition of the sample was 62.6 % White, 18.1 % Black, 12.1 % Hispanic, and 7.2 % identifying as other. The average neighborhood poverty rate at baseline was 0.107 (SD = 0.124), indicating that, on average, 10.7 % of residents in respondents’ census block groups lived below the poverty line. In addition, 32.7 % of respondents engaged in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, 69.9 % reported getting adequate sleep, and the average healthy-diet score was 1.132 (SD = 0.613) at baseline. The average 30-year CVD risk at Wave 5 was 0.135 (SD = 0.113), suggesting that respondents had an estimated 13.5 % probability of developing CVD over the next 30 years. Frequency analysis showed that 47.4 % of respondents reported no experience of childhood maltreatment, 31.5 % experienced one type of maltreatment, and 21.1 % experienced two or more types. The mean score for social support was 4.268 (SD = 0.525).

Childhood maltreatment and cardiovascular risk

Table 3 presents the results from fractional regression models with a logit link function to examine the effect of childhood maltreatment on Framingham 30-year CVD risk. Model 1 shows that childhood maltreatment was significantly associated with elevated CVD risk. Model 2 added control variables, including age, educational attainment, sex, race, physical activity, healthy diet, adequate sleep, and neighborhood poverty. As hypothesized, the association between childhood maltreatment and CVD risk remained significant and in the expected direction (b = 0.061, p = .010), suggesting that each unit increase in childhood maltreatment was associated with a 6.290 % increase in the odds of elevated CVD risk. In addition, this model showed that age was positively and significantly associated with CVD risk, while higher education attainment and identifying as Hispanic were associated with lower odds of CVD risk.

Main effects and stress-buffering role of social support

Turning to Table 4, Model 3 included social support while controlling for sociodemographic and health behavior covariates, which was significantly and negatively associated with CVD risk, as expected. In addition, the effect of childhood maltreatment remained significant even after accounting for social support. To address the stress-buffering hypothesis of social support, Model 4 included a multiplicative interaction between childhood maltreatment and social support in predicting 30-year CVD risk, while controlling for the full set of covariates. The analysis revealed a significant interaction effect (b = −0.098, p = .022). To further examine this interaction, we graphed the effect in Fig. 1 with 95 % confidence intervals. High and low levels of social support were defined as one standard deviation (1 SD) above and below the sample mean, respectively. In our sample, 11.29 % of respondents scored above 1 SD, and 11 % scored below 1 SD on the social support measure. As shown in Fig. 2, childhood maltreatment was not significantly associated with elevated CVD risk among those with high levels of social support (b = −0.002, p = .635). In contrast, among those with low social support, childhood maltreatment was significantly and positively associated with elevated CVD risk (b = 0.011, p = .003). Moreover, the highest CVD risk levels were observed among individuals who reported experiencing four types of childhood maltreatment and perceived low social support. Conversely, those who reported no history of childhood maltreatment exhibited the lowest CVD risk, regardless of their level of social support.

Sensitivity analyses

To address potential selection bias among respondents who provided biomarker data at Wave 5, we re-estimated the model using Heckman’s (1979) two-step procedure. In the first step, we divided the full sample (N = 20,745) into two groups: those retained for analysis (n = 4137) and those excluded (n = 16,608). To address potential differential attrition, gender, age, race, and baseline (Wave 1) measures of physical activity, healthy diet, adequate sleep, and neighborhood poverty were incorporated as covariates in a probit model predicting group membership. Wave 1 measures were chosen because they were collected before any attrition occurred and represent the most complete baseline information common to all participants. Using maximum likelihood estimation, we obtained parameter estimates for the linear predictor (Xiβ^), which were then used to compute the inverse Mills ratio: λi=ϕ(Xiβ^)/Φ(Xiβ^), where ϕ(.) represents the standard normal density function and Φ(.) represents the standard normal cumulative distribution function. In the second step, λi was included in the outcome model. A statistically significant λi would indicate the presence of selection bias. As shown in Table 5 and Supplementary Fig. S2, the inverse Mills ratio (λi) was not statistically significant, and the pattern of effects mirrored those in Table 4 and Fig. 2, suggesting that attrition did not bias the results.

Then, to examine potential gender and racial differences, we conducted sensitivity analyses. Specifically, we included interaction terms between childhood maltreatment and social support with gender and racial group measures. The online Supplement Table S1 and S2 show that these interaction effects were not significant, suggesting that the moderating role of social support on the relationship between childhood maltreatment and adult cardiovascular risk does not differ significantly across gender or racial groups in our sample.

Although most studies measure childhood maltreatment with dichotomous indicators representing exposure versus non-exposure (Brumley et al., 2019; Parker and La Manna, 2024; Shen et al., 2025), the Add Health survey assesses these experiences using Likert-type response options. Recent work has, therefore, summed item frequencies to produce a continuous maltreatment score (e.g., Deer et al., 2025). To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we repeated all analyses with this frequency-based measure, and Supplementary Table S3 and Fig. S3 present results consistent with those in Table 4, indicating that childhood maltreatment predicts higher 30-year CVD risk among individuals with low levels of social support.

Finally, Supplementary Table S4 presents sensitivity analyses that examine each subtype of social support separately—parent, peer, teacher, and other adult. Only the interaction between childhood maltreatment and teacher support was significant. Interactions involving parent, peer, or other adult support were small and not significant, and all main effects matched those in the primary models. Because the four support items are moderately correlated (r = 0.21–0.43), the composite measure avoids issues related to multiple testing and aligns with prior Add Health research. We therefore retain the composite social-support index as the focal moderator and provide the subtype findings as a robustness check.

Discussion

The current study extends previous research linking early life experiences to long-term health outcomes by explaining an association between childhood maltreatment and elevated CVD risk in adulthood using a comprehensive, precise risk score. Specifically, individuals who experienced higher levels of childhood maltreatment showed a significantly greater 30-year risk for CVD as measured by the Framingham algorithm, even after controlling for sociodemographic and health-related characteristics. Furthermore, we provide evidence that perceived social support, particularly teacher support, during adolescence can buffer this relationship. These results offer further insight into the interplay between early adversity and resilience factors and highlight the long-term importance of social relationships in shaping adult physical health. It is important to note that our maltreatment measures capture experiences prior to adolescence, a sensitive developmental window before puberty when biological recalibration occurs.

This study builds on the biological embedding model, which posits that early life experiences become embedded in biological systems and shape stress regulatory processes that contribute to chronic disease (Miller et al., 2011; Shonkoff et al., 2009). Childhood maltreatment, as an important form of toxic stress, can disrupt the development of the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) axis, immune functioning, and cardiovascular systems, ultimately leading to multisystem dysregulation and heightened vulnerability to chronic illness (Berens et al., 2017; McEwen and McEwen, 2017). Our finding that childhood maltreatment significantly predicted higher CVD risk is consistent with this framework and corroborates prior work documenting the long-term consequences of early adversity (Basu et al., 2017; Suglia et al., 2018). Importantly, this association remained even after controlling for covariates, including sex, race, education, physical activity, and neighborhood-level poverty, suggesting a direct and enduring physiological path of childhood stress exposure.

At the same time, we also show substantial empirical support for the stress-buffering hypothesis, which posits that supportive social relationships can attenuate stress’s physiological and psychological toll (Thoits, 2011). Consistent with this framework, we found that the association between childhood maltreatment and adult CVD risk was significantly weaker for adolescents who reported higher levels of perceived social support, an effect explained mainly by support from teachers who serve as stable, nonfamilial adults present throughout much of adolescents’ daily lives during a formative developmental period. Among individuals with low social support, exposure to childhood maltreatment was associated with a significant increase in predicted adult CVD risk. In contrast, among those with high social support, the relationship between maltreatment and CVD risk was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that social support, especially teacher support, is an important protective factor that interrupts the progression from early stress to physiological wear and tear that promotes long-term CVD.

Several mechanisms may help explain this moderating effect. First, social support may influence health through behavioral pathways by promoting engagement in health-protective behaviors and reducing reliance on maladaptive coping strategies (Lei et al., 2024). Second, and more relevant to the biological embedding framework, social support may regulate stress-responsive physiological systems, including the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system, reducing allostatic load and associated inflammation (Entringer and Heim, 2025). Supportive relationships may create a physiological milieu that protects against accelerated disease processes by dampening stress reactivity and enhancing recovery following stressful events. Third, individuals with higher social support may experience greater emotional regulation and perceived control, which have been linked to reduced biological aging and better cardiovascular outcomes.

These findings also raise important questions about the pathways through which early adversity becomes biologically embedded and how interventions might best disrupt these trajectories. Although the direct effect of childhood maltreatment on CVD risk was significant, the moderating role of social support suggests that resilience factors can meaningfully change the course of biological risk. This pattern is consistent with emerging research emphasizing the importance of early relational environments and the potential for targeted prevention efforts to enhance health outcomes (John-Henderson et al., 2020; Slatcher and Selcuk, 2017). Future research should explore whether specific forms of support (e.g., parental vs. romantic partner) are more effective than others in buffering physiological stress, whether some forms are more influential during specific developmental stages or transitional periods, and whether the quality and stability of support over time play an important role. In particular, examining support within the context of critical periods of neurodevelopment may be especially fruitful, as evidence suggests that the biological embedding of stress may be particularly sensitive to experiences during these windows. Interventions delivered during these sensitive windows may be especially efficient in reducing long-term health risks.

Furthermore, the present study has a few strengths that enhance its contribution to literature. Previous work has often relied on self-rated health or single-item indicators, limiting insights into underlying disease processes (Altman et al., 2016; Au and Johnston, 2014). By using the Framingham 30-year CVD risk score, we were able to provide a more accurate and nuanced assessment of physical health risk. Additionally, many prior studies have been cross-sectional or limited in generalizability. Drawing on longitudinal, nationally representative Add Health data spanning more than two decades, the current study establishes temporal order and enhances confidence in the robustness of the observed associations.

Although our study extended previous research in several important ways, it also has several limitations. First, childhood maltreatment was assessed retrospectively, which introduces the possibility of recall bias (Hardt and Rutter, 2004). While previous studies suggest that retrospective reports can still meaningfully capture early life stress and predict health outcomes in adulthood (Berg et al., 2020; Lei et al., 2020), further research is needed to replicate these findings using prospective measures. Second, while the current study examines social support as a moderator, it relies on self-reported measures, which may also be subject to recall bias or influenced by respondents’ psychological states at the time of assessment. Future research should replicate and extend this work by incorporating objective indicators of social networks. Third, although the Framingham Cardiovascular Risk Score offers an objective and validated measure of cardiovascular risk within the next 30 years, it remains a predictive model rather than a direct measure of clinical outcomes such as myocardial infarction or stroke. As such, this CVD risk score may not fully capture the progression of CVD. Fourth, the social support scale showed relatively low internal consistency; this limitation could diminish the precision of our effect estimates, so future studies should replicate our findings with more comprehensive and reliable support inventories.

Another limitation involves potential additional mechanisms and unmeasured confounding. Our findings revealed the impact of childhood maltreatment on CVD in adulthood, supporting the biological embedding model and suggesting that early adverse environments contribute to physiological wear and tear. However, this study does not rule out the possibility of additional mechanisms that may be at play. For example, a recent study by Beach et al. (2025) revealed that the relationship between early-life experiences and later health outcomes may be mediated through behavioral and immune pathways. Similarly, although we control several demographic and health-related covariates, unmeasured confounders such as genetic predisposition or early access to healthcare services may still influence the observed associations. Accordingly, longitudinal data improves our ability to establish temporal ordering, but they do not by themselves prove causality because residual confounding, selection bias, and other threats to validity can remain. Further research is needed to disentangle these complex mechanisms and to fully understand how early adversity shapes long-term health trajectories. Finally, while Heckman selection models were employed to address potential selection bias due to sample attrition, the possibility of residual bias cannot be fully excluded.

Despite these limitations, the current study makes several important contributions. By integrating the biological embedding model with the stress-buffering hypothesis, we indicate that childhood maltreatment contributes to elevated long-term cardiovascular risk, but that this trajectory is not immutable. Rather, perceived social support during adolescence can significantly buffer the negative health effects of childhood maltreatment. Supportive relationships with parents, friends, teachers, and other significant adults serve an important protective function. It will be important for future studies to explain how social support confers protection and inform targeted and efficacious interventions. In the end, intervention programs that increase social support in adolescence promise to reduce the long-term burden of cardiovascular disease and foster resilience throughout life.
