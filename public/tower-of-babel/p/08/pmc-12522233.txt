Changes in microbiological characteristics of bloodstream infections before and after the COVID-19 pandemic in a tertiary teaching hospital in Iran
Authors: Zahra Jafarpour, Masoud Karimi, Gholamreza Pouladfar, Bahman Pourabbas, Amin Abbasian, Mohebat Vali, Pejman Abbasi, Fatemeh Ghasemi, Mohammad Ali Dehyadegari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has significantly impacted healthcare systems, leading to increased antibiotic misuse and multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections. This study assessed microorganisms and antibiotic-resistance patterns in bloodstream infections at a tertiary teaching hospital in Shiraz, Iran.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed blood culture results from the Professor Alborzi Clinical Microbiology Research Center (2017–2023), comparing two periods: pre-pandemic (Period 1) (2017–2020) and pandemic/post-pandemic (Period 2) (2020–2023).ResultsAmong 5,970 pathogenic strains, Gram-negative bacteria predominated (81.2% Period 1, 77.3% Period 2), maintaining a 1:5 ratio with Gram-positive bacteria. Stenotrophomonas maltophilia was the most common pathogen (23%) but declined significantly from 30 to 11% post-2020. In contrast, Escherichia coli (8–14%), Klebsiella spp. (6–13%), and Enterobacter spp. (6–13%) increased significantly. Staphylococcus aureus (7–12%) and Enterococcus spp. (6–8%) showed non-significant increases, while Pseudomonas spp. (10–6%) and streptococcus viridans (5–2%) declined without statistical significance. Acinetobacter spp. remained stable at 6%.Time-series analysis revealed pandemic-induced shifts: Enterobacter spp. reversed its pre-pandemic rise post-COVID (p < 0.001), while Stenotrophomonas maltophilia exhibited a declining trend in Period 1 and an increasing one in Period 2 (p = 0.016). No significant trend changes were observed for the remaining examined bacterial species.Among Gram-negative fermenters, carbapenems and amikacin remained the most effective antibiotics. While Acinetobacter spp. demonstrated elevated resistance to all tested antibiotics, including carbapenems, other Gram- negative non-fermenter bacteria displayed mixed resistance patterns, with increases, decreases, and stable resistance levels observed when comparing data from two periods. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) rates doubled (24% to 50%, p < 0.0001), with increased resistance to ciprofloxacin. High 54 rates of vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE, 62%) were observed, with a 55 non-significant upward change (67%).ConclusionsThe study highlights shifts in pathogen epidemiology and antibiotic resistance during the pandemic, emphasizing the need for continuous surveillance and targeted antibiotic stewardship programs.

Introduction

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria are associated with high morbidity and mortality among patients, as well as significant costs for healthcare systems worldwide [1]. Despite advances in antimicrobial agents and sepsis management, the increase in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has led to a significant increase in adverse clinical outcomes associated with BSIs [1]. AMR has become a major global threat to public health in the twenty-first century [2]. A systematic analysis revealed that approximately 4.95 million deaths worldwide in 2019 were associated with bacterial AMR, with 1.27 million directly attributed to it [3]. A recent high-profile report estimates that, by 2050, 10 million people could die annually due to AMR unless a global response to this problem is implemented [4, 5].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified a list of priority bacterial pathogens that are of public health importance and urgently require new and effective antibiotic treatments due to AMR [6].

The key pathogens identified in high-income countries include Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterococcus spp [7]. with the SENTRY Antimicrobial Surveillance Program from 2013 to 2016, highlighting Escherichia coli and S. aureus as the most common pathogens in the Asia‒Pacific region, followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and Enterobacter cloacae [8].

The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidance document addresses infections caused by a range of MDR pathogens, including extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (ESBL-E), AmpC β-lactamase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (AmpC-E), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), difficult-to-treat resistant P. aeruginosa (DTR P. aeruginosa), carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia [9]. Regional variations in clinical pathogens can be attributed to demographic traits such as population, geographic location, and socioeconomic status. Additionally, differences in healthcare settings, such as antimicrobial stewardship and infection control programs, lead to increased antibiotic resistance, fostering the emergence and spread of MDR pathogens [10–12].

Most studies indicate that AMR has increased during the COVID-19 pandemic [13, 14]. The overuse of antibiotics in healthcare facilities and communities during the COVID-19 pandemic has significantly contributed to the rise of AMR [15]. To our knowledge, Iran has not reported any reports of antibiotic resistance in patients with BSI during the COVID-19 pandemic. Conducting surveillance programs and analyzing AMR before and during the COVID-19 outbreak is crucial. This is essential for gaining insights into the pandemic’s impact and help developed effective strategies at the local and global levels. This study aimed to evaluate the microbiological characteristics of BSIs before and during the COVID-19 pandemic (2017–2023) in a tertiary teaching hospital in Shiraz, southern Iran, with an emphasis on antibiotic-resistant “priority pathogens” published by the WHO [6].

Materials and methods

Study design

The study collected bloodstream infection (BSI) isolates from March 21, 2017, to March 20, 2023, at Nemazee Teaching Hospital in Shiraz, Iran, which included samples from four universities and eight non-university hospitals; isolates were categorized into two periods: pre-pandemic (Period 1) (March 21, 2017 - March 19, 2020) and pandemic/post-pandemic (Period 2) (March 20, 2020 - March 20, 2023), analyzing equal time frames of 36 months while excluding blood cultures positive for fungi and certain skin flora, with ethical approval obtained from Shiraz University of Medical Science and no need for informed consent, as the study utilized laboratory management data.

Identification and confirmation of the isolated bacteria

The isolated bacteria were identified via biochemical methods, including oxidase reactions, pigmentation assessment, temperature growth tests, cultivation on MacConkey agar, and two miniaturized multisets identification systems (API and Microgen) [16–18]. Focused on nine common bacterial species responsible for BSIs, comprising three Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus spp., Streptococcus viridans.), three fermenting Gram-negative bacteria (E.coli, Klebsiella sp., Enterobacter sp.), and three non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria (Acinetobacter sp., Pseudomonas sp., S. maltophilia).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The disk diffusion method was used to assess susceptibility to 19 antimicrobial agents, including amikacin (30 µg), ampicillin-sulbactam (10/10 µg), aztreonam (30 µg), cefepime (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole (1.25/23.75 µg), colistin (10 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), levofloxacin (5 µg), imipenem (10 µg), meropenem (10 µg), piperacillin-tazobactam (100/10 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), ticarcillin (75 µg), tigecycline (30 µg), and tobramycin (10 µg). All the isolates were tested using cation-adjusted Mueller‒Hinton agar (Merck Co., Germany). The findings were analyzed in accordance with the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [19]. For each calendar year of the study (2017–2023), we applied the CLSI guidelines current at the time of testing. Due to the limited number of cases analyzed for colistin, tigecycline, and linezolid, the antimicrobial resistance results for these antibiotics were not included in the manuscript. Based on the list of antibiotic-resistant “priority pathogens” published by the WHO in 2017, we reported all three pathogens with critical priority, including carbapenem-resistant (CR) - Acinetobacter sp., CR-Pseudomonas sp., and ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae [20]. The presence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) was determined via the use of cefotaxime, cefotaxime-clavulanic acid (30/10 µg), ceftazidime, and ceftazidime–clavulanic acid disks (30/10 µg), following the CLSI guidelines [19]. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) was identified using a cefoxitin disc.

Statistical analysis

The study combined pathogens with intermediate and complete resistance to calculate the overall resistance rate, as both types are treated similarly in clinical practice. The data were analyzed for two periods—Period 1 (March 21, 2017–March 19, 2020) and Period 2 (March 20, 2020–March 20, 2023)—using WHONET 5.6 software. Only the first isolate from multiple samples collected from the same patient was included in the analysis, which presented data as absolute and percentage frequencies. The statistical significance of AMR trends was determined via Z test to compare two proportions, with p values ≤ 0.05 considered significant. We conducted an additional investigation into the incidence trends during the pandemic period compared with those during the pre-pandemic period. We aimed to explore the trends in the incidence of BSIs caused by high-priority pathogens before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. The results were categorized based on seasonality. We then performed a time series analysis via Stata version 12.

Results

Distribution of common pathogens responsible for BSIs

A total of 5,970 bacterial isolates were identified from BSIs between 2017 and 2023, with 62% (n = 3,705) occurring in the Period 1 (2017–2020) and 38% (n = 2,265) Period 2 (2020–2023). The distribution of isolates revealed a greater percentage from noninvasive care units (74% Period 1, 77% Period 2) and a lower rate from intensive care units (17% Period 1, 14% Period 2). The male-to-female ratio remained consistent at 57/43%. Before the pandemic, 1,828 (49%) isolates were from pediatric wards, 1,488 (40%) from adult wards, and 389 (11%) were undetermined; during Period 2, these numbers changed to 542 (24%) from pediatric wards, 1,481 (65%) from adult wards, and 242 (11%) undetermined. Regarding bacterial strains, 674 (18.2%) were Gram-positive, and 3,014 (81.2%) were Gram-negative before the pandemic. During Period 2, 504 (22.2%) were Gram-positive, and 1,752 (77.3%) were Gram-negative, maintaining an approximate ratio of 1:5 between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, with no significant difference observed.

The most prevalent pathogens isolated from BSI were S. maltophilia (Period 1: 30%; Period 2: 11%) and E. coli (Period 1: 8%; Period 2: 14%). Notable variations were found in the frequency of Enterobacter and Klebsiella isolates across the two study phases. Table 1 displays the relative prevalence of the common pathogens in both study periods, highlighting significant changes. The interrupted time series analysis revealed distinct COVID-19 pandemic impacts on pathogen epidemiology. Enterobacter spp. exhibited the most dramatic pattern with a strong Period 1 upward trajectory (β1 = 1.043, 95% CI: 0.722, 1.364, p < 0.001) that reversed Period 2, showing a significant negative trend change (β3=−1.625, 95% CI:−2.422, −0.828, p < 0.001) despite an initial non-significant level increase (β2 = 5.907, p = 0.062) (Fig. 1-c). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing for Gram-negative non-fermentative bacilli (GNNFs)

Acinetobacter sp., Pseudomonas sp., and S. maltophilia were identified as the three most common nonfermenting bacteria, with gentamicin, imipenem, and amikacin being the most effective antibiotics against these isolates. The antimicrobial resistance patterns of these bacteria are presented in Table 4. During the pandemic period, Acinetobacter sp. exhibited a statistically significant increase in resistance to all antibiotics. In contrast, Pseudomonas sp. showed a no significant increase in resistance to carbapenems; but a decrease in resistance rates to several commonly used antibiotics were observed, suggesting a shift in susceptibility patterns. For S. maltophilia, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole remained the most effective antibiotic, with resistance rates slightly increasing from 2% Period 1 to 4% during period 2 (p = 0.072). (Table 4)

For S. aureus, vancomycin and rifampin were the most effective antibiotics. Still, MRSA rates significantly increased from 24% in Period 1 to 50% during the period 2 (p < 0.0001), along with notable increases in resistance to ciprofloxacin, indicating increasing resistance to commonly used antibiotics. Enterococcus sp. demonstrated vancomycin and chloramphenicol as the most effective antibiotics, with VRE rates increasing from 62 to 67% during the pandemic (p = 0.319). The antibiogram analysis of S. viridans revealed significant changes during the pandemic, with increased resistance rates to rifampin and chloramphenicol, whereas resistance to doxycycline and ampicillin decreased.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing for pathogens with the critical priority of the WHO

The study analyzed the prevalence of ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae and CR-Acinetobacter sp. and Pseudomonas sp. Among 1,522 isolates, 64% produced ESBL, with similar rates of 63.4% during and 63.5% before the pandemic. A significant increase in carbapenem resistance was observed in Acinetobacter sp., increasing from 68% before the pandemic to 83% during the pandemic (p = 0.0001). Although there was an increase in CR-Pseudomonas sp., it was not statistically significant, with rates of 29% before the pandemic and 34% during the pandemic (p = 0.277).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the changes in pathogen epidemiology and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) patterns associated with BSIs before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. The findings revealed significant shifts in the distribution of pathogens, their resistance profiles, and the impact of the pandemic on these trends.

The statistically significant rise in non-ICU isolates (absolute 3%, p = 0.0004) may mirror operational changes during the pandemic - particularly increased culturing of febrile respiratory patients in general wards and reduced elective services due to bed reallocation.

It revealed that Gram-negative bacteria were more prevalent than Gram-positive pathogens both before and during the pandemic, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [1, 20]. Historically, S. aureus was the dominant pathogen from 1997 to 2004 according to SENTRY antimicrobial surveillance program [8]. Still, a global trend toward an increasing predominance of Gram-negative isolates in BSI, rather than Gram-positive pathogens, has been reported between 1996 and 2012 in most healthcare systems worldwide [1]. This shift attributes to various healthcare practices, such as the increased use of antibiotics like β-lactams, macrolides, and quinolones for prophylaxis [21] and the establishment of specialized teams for central-line insertion, which helps to decrease line-related infections [22].

The study identified S. maltophilia as the most frequently isolated pathogen (23%), though its prevalence declined significantly from 30% Period 1 to 11% during the Period 2(p ≤ 0.001). This decline may reflect changes in healthcare practices, such as enhanced infection control measures or shifts in antibiotic prescribing patterns during the pandemic. In contrast, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., and Enterobacter spp. showed an upward trend. This rise may be linked to the extensive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics during the pandemic, potentially favoring the selection of these resistant strains [23]. The rise in E. coli and Klebsiella spp. is particularly concerning given their association with multidrug resistance and healthcare-associated infections, which aligns with prior studies [20, 24–26]. While S. aureus and Enterococcus spp. exhibited only marginal, non-significant increases in prevalence, the doubling of MRSA rates (24–50%; p < 0.0001) and rising VRE rates underscore the escalating challenge of resistant Gram-positive infections. These trends highlight the urgent need for effective strategies to combat resistance in these pathogens. This trend aligns with findings of a systematic review [27] and potentially linked to heightened workloads, extensive use of immunomodulatory medications, and changes in the lung microbiota [28, 29]. A moderate increase in Enterococcus spp. infections was observed, which is consistent with systematic reviews indicating higher rates during the pandemic [30]. A slight decrease in trends for Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas spp. was noted; however, other reviews reported higher rates of Acinetobacter infections in ICUs during the pandemic [30].

Antimicrobial resistance trends

The study revealed significant changes in antimicrobial resistance patterns during the pandemic. Among Gram-negative fermenters, E. coli exhibited decreased resistance to amikacin but increased resistance to meropenem, while Klebsiella spp. showed significant resistance increases to tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, and gentamicin. Enterobacter spp. demonstrated increased resistance to most antibiotics, except chloramphenicol, with carbapenem resistance rising notably. Similarly COVID-19 has changed the profile of AMR in Egypt [31].

Among Gram-negative non-fermenters, Acinetobacter spp. exhibited a significant increase in resistance to all antibiotics, while Pseudomonas spp. showed a significant rise in resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole but decreased resistance to other antibiotics. S.maltophilia maintained low resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, though resistance to ceftazidime increased significantly. These trends suggest that the pandemic may have influenced the susceptibility patterns of non-fermentative Gram-negative bacteria, potentially due to changes in antibiotic use or infection control practices.

For Gram-positive bacteria, the significant increase in MRSA rates and the rise in VRE rates highlight the growing burden of resistant Gram-positive infections. The high but stable resistance to ampicillin in Enterococcus spp. (68 and 72% in Period 1 and Period 2, respectively) and the decrease in resistance to doxycycline and ampicillin in S. viridans reflect complex changes in antibiotic resistance patterns that warrant further investigation.

The interrupted time series analysis revealed distinct pandemic impacts on pathogen epidemiology. These findings suggest that the pandemic had varying effects on different pathogens, with some showing significant changes in epidemiology and others remaining stable.

Critical priority pathogens

The study also highlighted the prevalence of critical priority pathogens identified by the WHO. ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae remained stable (63.4% vs. 63.5%). Acinetobacter sp. exhibited increased resistance to all tested antibiotics, particularly carbapenems, mirroring findings from other studies [14, 26].This increase in carbapenem resistance is alarming given the limited treatment options for infections caused by Acinetobacter spp. Although carbapenem resistance in Pseudomonas spp. increased from 29 to 34%, this change was not statistically significant (p = 0.277).

The increasing resistance rates to carbapenems, which remain the most effective treatment for E. coli, signals a worrying trend that necessitates further study. Other studies have observed similar trends [14, 32–34]. This study reported a high prevalence of CR Klebsiella (37–40%), likely due to the extensive use of carbapenem antibiotics in hospitals, as revealed in a worldwide point prevalence survey in 69 countries [35]. There was a notable increase in resistance rates to carbapenems among Enterobacter sp. during the pandemic, suggesting that Enterobacter has adapted to the selective pressure of antibiotics. This trend aligns with similar studies indicating reduced susceptibility in Enterobacteriaceae isolates [30, 36]. This alarming trend underscores the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies against this MDR pathogen [2].

Limitations of this study

The limitations include: [1] The study relies on retrospective data, which may be subject to biases such as incomplete records [2]. This study was conducted in a tertiary hospital in a developing country, the findings may not be generalizable to other regions or healthcare settings [3]. Clinical outcomes (e.g., mortality, treatment success) were not assessed, limiting the interpretation of microbiological trends [4]. The pandemic led to systemic changes (e.g., antibiotic prescribing, infection control measures) that may have confounded observed trends.

Conclusion

Our study reveals notable changes in certain microbiological profiles and antibiotic resistance trends among BSIs during the COVID-19 pandemic. These results emphasize the importance of continuous surveillance to guide empirical antibiotic treatment strategies and enhance infection control protocols in the probable future pandemics.
