Diabetic Myonecrosis: An Under-Recognised Complication of Poorly Controlled Diabetes with a High Morbidity– A Clinico-Radiological Study
Authors: Varun Kumar, Mohammed A. Hussain, Mathews E. Kurian, Neeraja Balan, Benjamin B. Mathew, Madhavi Kandagaddala, Harini S. Arumugam, Kripa E. Cherian, Nitin Kapoor, Hesarghatta S. Asha, Nihal Thomas, Felix Jebasingh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Diabetic myonecrosis is an uncommon and under-recognised complication of diabetes mellitus. We aimed to describe the clinical profile, imaging characteristics, management strategies, and outcomes of patients with diabetic myonecrosis.Methods:This retrospective study included 30 patients diagnosed with diabetic myonecrosis between 2002 and 2024. Diagnosis – based on clinical suspicion, supported by characteristic ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging. Demographic data, diabetes characteristics, biochemical parameters, imaging features, management approaches, recurrence, and mortality were analysed.Results:The mean age was 49.1 ± 11.7 years, with a mean diabetes duration of 12.6 ± 6.9 years and poor glycaemic control (HbA1c 8.83 ± 1.88%). Acute limb pain and swelling were the most common presentations (93.3%), predominantly involving the thigh and calf muscles. MRI revealed characteristic muscle oedema and infarction, frequently extending beyond the quadriceps to involve hamstrings, adductors, and posterior compartments. Diabetic nephropathy and retinopathy were present in 70% of patients. Most patients (83.3%) improved with conservative management. Recurrence occurred in 23.3% of cases, and the overall mortality rate was 33.3%. Recurrence was the strongest predictor of mortality (adjusted OR, 8.20; 95% CI, 1.45–46.40), while advanced kidney disease increased the risk, and higher serum albumin levels were protective.Conclusion:Diabetic myonecrosis should be considered in patients with diabetes who present with acute localised muscle pain; the presence of advanced CKD, significant proteinuria, or hypoalbuminemia identifies those at increased risk of adverse outcomes. MRI is the diagnostic modality of choice. Despite response to conservative therapy, high recurrence and mortality rates highlight the need for early recognition and long-term surveillance.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic myonecrosis, defined as spontaneous infarction of skeletal muscle in individuals with diabetes, is an uncommon but clinically important complication. First described by Angervall and Stener in 1965, it presents with acute, localised limb pain and swelling, most commonly in the thighs.[1] Diabetic Myonecrosis often mimics pyomyositis, cellulitis, myositis or deep vein thrombosis, leading to diagnostic delays and unnecessary interventions.[23]

Despite its characteristic clinical and radiological features, diabetic myonecrosis remains under-recognised, with limited data from India, where diabetes is highly prevalent.[45]

The pathophysiology of myonecrosis remains incompletely understood, but prevailing hypotheses implicate diabetic microangiopathy causing focal muscle ischaemia with subsequent ischaemia–reperfusion injury and inflammation. While quadriceps involvement predominates, hamstrings, adductors, and upper-extremity muscles can also be affected.[6] Bilateral disease, though uncommon, has been reported.[7] MRI demonstrates characteristic muscle oedema and infarction and is the diagnostic modality of choice. Diagnostic muscle biopsies, which were previously common, are now largely avoided due to concerns about poor wound healing and increased risk of infection.[5]

Given these challenges, we aimed to systematically characterise the clinical presentation, imaging patterns, management, and outcomes of diabetic myonecrosis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

Christian Medical College Vellore (IRB Min No: 13437; Date: 23 September 2020). The study systematically analysed the clinical presentation, biochemical and imaging findings, treatment modalities, recurrence and long-term outcomes in patients with diabetic myonecrosis from the electronic medical records.

Participants

Patients having diabetes mellitus and clinical presentation of acute, localised limb muscle pain and swelling with diagnosis of diabetic myonecrosis confirmed by characteristic musculoskeletal findings on ultrasonography or MRI findings (muscle oedema/infarction on T2/STIR and corresponding T1 changes) were included. Patients with alternative diagnoses (pyomyositis, abscess, myositis, deep vein thrombosis, traumatic muscle injury, or other non-diabetic causes) confirmed by clinical evaluation, imaging, microbiology or histopathology were excluded. Thirty patients meeting clinical and radiological criteria were included.

Study procedures

Data were extracted from hospital electronic medical records using a standardised case report form. Data included demographics (age, gender), diabetes details (type, duration, treatment, HbA1c), clinical features (site, laterality, fever), labs (CBC, ESR/CRP, creatinine/eGFR, albumin, electrolytes, CPK, LDH, 24-h urine protein), imaging (USG echotexture/vascularity; MRI T1/T2/STIR sequences for extent/compartments involved, management (conservative, antibiotics, surgery), recurrence, and survival (via telephonic follow-up). MRI was performed in axial and coronal planes using T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and STIR (or T2 fat-suppressed) sequences, with post-contrast T1 fat-suppressed imaging when feasible.[38]

Analgesic management included paracetamol/NSAIDs (when not contraindicated), tramadol, gabapentin/pregabalin. Antibiotic therapy was administered based on clinical suspicion of infection or documented bacterial coinfection. Ancillary measures included physiotherapy, limb elevation, magnesium sulphate dressings, and immobilisation. Structured physiotherapy focusing on quadriceps strengthening and ankle range-of-motion exercises was employed to facilitate functional recovery.

Assessment of outcomes

Primary outcomes were all-cause mortality and recurrence of diabetic myonecrosis. Secondary outcomes included time to death, time to recurrence, need for surgical intervention, and duration of hospital stay. Recurrence: defined as a subsequent clinically and radiologically confirmed episode in the same or contralateral limb after resolution of the index event.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics v29.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), depending on distribution; categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Univariate associations with mortality and recurrence were assessed using t-tests/Mann–Whitney U tests or χ²/Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate testing or judged clinically important (age, diabetes duration, CKD stage, albumin, haemoglobin, CRP, recurrence) were entered into multivariable models.

For mortality, we performed multivariable logistic regression (odds ratios [OR] with 95% confidence intervals). For recurrence, multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated and stratified by recurrence status and CKD stage (≥4 vs. <4). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Ethical aspects

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, Christian Medical College Vellore (IRB Min No: 13437; Date: 23 Sep 2020). The study procedures adhered to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its subsequent revisions.

Patient Consent: Informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the study’s retrospective design.

RESULTS

The mean age at diagnosis was 49.1 ± 11.7 years (range: 30–75), with slight male predominance (56.7%). Mean duration of diabetes was 12.6 ± 6.9 years, and mean age of diabetes onset was 32.5 ± 15 years. Of 30 patients, 28 (93.3%) had type 2 diabetes, one had FCPD, and one had type 1 diabetes (anti-GAD positive, >2000 IU/ml) [Table 1].

Acute limb pain was the most common presentation (93.3%), primarily involving the thigh. Swelling occurred in 85.8% of patients, and fever in 35.3%. One patient had confirmed deep vein thrombosis. Cutaneous ulcers were present in four patients (13.3%), one with a sinus tract; four had prior diabetic foot ulcers.

Diabetic retinopathy was found in 70% (21/30): 1 had mild NPDR, 9 had moderate NPDR, 7 had severe NPDR, and 4 had PDR. CSME was noted in 7 (23.3%). Therefore, sight-threatening retinopathy (PDR or CSME) affected 11 patients (36.7%). Diabetic nephropathy was also present in 21 patients (70%). CKD staging showed: stage 5 (8 patients, 26.7%), stage 4 (1, 3.3%), stage 3 (2, 6.7%), stage 2 (1, 3.3%), stage 1 (9, 30%), and no nephropathy (9, 30%) [Table 2].

Comorbid conditions were prevalent and most likely contributed to the severe disease burden. Hypertension was present in 50% (n = 15), chronic kidney disease in 70% (n = 21), anaemia in 46.7% (n = 14), dyslipidaemia in 26.7% (n = 8), history of prior myonecrosis in 23.3% (n = 7), hypothyroidism in 20% (n = 6), stroke in 6.7% (n = 2), and coronary artery disease in 3.3% (n = 1).

The mean HbA1c was 8.83 ± 1.88%. Anaemia (<13 g/dL in males, <12 g/dL in females) was common (mean haemoglobin 9.47 ± 2.40g/dL), with 43.3% diagnosed as anaemia of chronic disease. Inflammatory markers were elevated, with CRP and ESR levels at 92.8 ± 78.5 mg/L and 70.5 ± 33 mm/hr, respectively. Muscle enzymes were elevated [CPK: median 400 U/L (IQR 200–1200); LDH: 441 ± 190 U/L]. Mean urinary protein excretion was 2,471 ± 1,600 mg/24 h. Hypoalbuminemia (3.15 ± 0.90 g/L) and elevated serum creatinine [median 1.74 mg/dL (IQR 1.05–3.37)] indicated renal impairment. Serum electrolytes suggested metabolic acidosis (HCO3-:19.9 ± 3.2 mEq/L) [Table 1].

Imaging

Ultrasonography of the affected limb suggested diabetic myonecrosis in 17 patients (56.7%). Typical findings included swollen, hypoechoic muscles with heterogeneous echotexture, loss of the normal fibrillary pattern, and minimal or absent intramuscular Doppler flow, consistent with muscle oedema or infarction. These features help distinguish myonecrosis from pyomyositis or abscess (focal fluid collections with peripheral hyperaemia) and from cellulitis (predominant subcutaneous thickening with increased superficial Doppler flow). However, many ultrasonograms were non-specific or normal, highlighting the limited sensitivity of USG, which mainly excluded abscesses and DVT.

MRI provided a definitive diagnosis in all patients. Muscle involvement extended beyond classical quadriceps to include the adductor group, hamstrings, and posterior compartments of the leg (gastrocnemius and soleus), with calf involvement in 8/30 (27%) of cases. Six patients (20%) showed bilateral or multicompartmental disease, including up to six thigh compartments with extension into the leg [Figure 1a and b].

Unusual patterns, such as isolated involvement of the vastus medialis or soleus, and complex combinations (e.g. adductor magnus with biceps femoris), led to diagnostic confusion with cellulitis or pyomyositis [Supplementary Figures 1-3]. A muscle biopsy was performed in 17% (5/30) of patients, wherein diagnoses remained unclear. All biopsies were suggestive of myonecrosis.

Multivariable analysis of mortality

The results indicate that, beyond recurrence, renal dysfunction and hypoalbuminemia are independent predictors of long-term mortality in diabetic myonecrosis (all models adjusted for age, diabetes duration, and follow-up). Recurrence of myonecrosis remained a strong, independent predictor of death (OR ≈ 8.2, 95% CI 1.45–46.4; P = 0.016), consistent with prior findings. Advanced CKD (stage ≥4) and lower serum albumin significantly increased mortality risk (e.g., albumin OR ≈ 0.60 per g/dL, P = 0.018). In contrast, haemoglobin and CRP showed no significant association after adjustment [Table 3].

Survival analysis stratified by recurrence status revealed markedly divergent survival trajectories between the two patient groups [Figure 2]. The “No Recurrence” group (n = 23) demonstrated superior long-term survival, with 78.3% of patients (18/23) surviving, and 5 deaths (21.7%). In contrast, the «With Recurrence» group (n = 7) had significantly worse outcomes: only 28.6% (2/7) survived, with a mortality rate of 71.4% (5/7), most recurrences occurring within 5–6 years of the index episode. The overall mortality rate was 33.3% (10/30 patients) over a median follow-up period of 6 years. Among the seven patients with recurrence, 5 (71.4%) died during follow-up – three due to complications of advanced diabetic kidney disease (uraemic symptoms, fluid overload), one from sepsis-related multi-organ failure, and one from a fatal cerebrovascular accident. Among the 23 patients without recurrence, 5 (21.7%) also died: 2 due to end-stage renal disease-related complications, 1 from a cerebrovascular accident, 1 from sepsis, and 1 from myocardial infarction.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves stratified by recurrence status. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis stratified by recurrence status revealed markedly divergent survival trajectories between the two patient groups

During the follow-up period, seven patients (23.3%) experienced a recurrence of diabetic myonecrosis, while 23 patients (76.7%) remained recurrence-free over a median follow-up of 6.0 years (mean, 6.4 ± 4.7 years; range, 0.083–20.0 years). The Kaplan-Meier recurrence-free survival curve showed that approximately 95% of patients remained recurrence-free at 2 years, declining to approximately 75% at 5 years of follow-up.

Management and outcomes

The median hospital stay was 10 days (interquartile range 6–12.5 days). All patients were managed conservatively, with primary focus on glycaemic optimisation, pain control, and rest. Insulin therapy was administered to 73% of patients during hospitalisation, while the remaining 27% were managed with oral antidiabetic medications.

Surgical intervention was reserved for specific complications and performed in five patients (16.7%). Procedures included incision and drainage with anterior and lateral fasciotomy (n = 1), left below-knee amputation for coexisting pyogenic osteomyelitis (n = 1), muscle biopsy (n = 3), and fasciotomy of the anterior compartment of the left thigh (n = 1). Of these, two patients died during follow-up, while the remaining 3 had partial or complete recovery following surgery and continued medical management. Twenty-five patients (83.3%) responded well to conservative management alone.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective series of 30 patients spanning 22 years represents one of the largest single-centre studies from India on diabetic myonecrosis, providing insights into presentation, diagnosis, and outcomes in a high-diabetes-burden setting.

Our series had a male preponderance (56.7%), type 2 diabetes (86.7%), with a mean diabetes duration of 12.6 years and a mean age at onset of 49.1 years. However, Trujillo-Santos’ systematic review (n = 115 patients) reported female predominance (61.5%) and predominance of type 1 diabetes (59%), with a mean diabetes duration of 14.3 years.[9] However, our findings align more closely with other Indian cohorts: Meher et al.[4] described six patients with predominantly type 2 diabetes (mean age 54.2 years, mean diabetes duration 10.16 years), with equal distribution of males and females and poor glycaemic control (mean HbA1c 9.5%). This demographic shift toward type 2 diabetes and older age likely reflects the increasing burden of type 2 diabetes in India and suggests phenotypic differences between high type 2 diabetes-burden regions and Western populations. Furthermore, our findings were consistent with the literature: acute localised limb pain (93.3%) and swelling (85.2%) were the predominant presenting symptoms, primarily affecting thigh and lower limb muscles. Case series by Meher et al.[4] similarly described, sudden-onset pain and swelling in all six patients with diabetic myonecrosis, reflecting a consistent clinical pattern in our study. The presentation frequently mimicked cellulitis, deep vein thrombosis, or pyomyositis, contributing to diagnostic delays and misclassification, as noted in a systematic review by Horton et al.[13] However, fever occurred in 34.5% of our patients, which is slightly higher than reported, potentially reflecting an institutional referral bias toward severe presentations, with concurrent infections.

Ultrasonography, employed in 56.7% of our patients, demonstrated variable sensitivity (56.7% sensitivity for characteristic findings). While USG helped to exclude abscess and DVT, many scans were non-specific or falsely reassuring, consistent with the literature reporting limited sensitivity of ultrasound for myonecrosis. Our findings reinforce that USG should be considered a screening tool for differential diagnosis, rather than confirmatory for myonecrosis.[310] Over the study period, there was a shift in diagnostic approach, with MRI being increasingly used as the initial imaging modality, due to its superior diagnostic yield compared to ultrasonography.

MRI provided a definitive diagnosis in all 30 patients (100% diagnostic certainty), demonstrating characteristic T2/STIR hyperintensity and T1 hypointensity patterns, as described in our previous series.[14] Importantly, our study revealed significantly more extensive and diverse muscle involvement than typically described in Western literature.[13] While classical reports emphasise isolated or predominant quadriceps involvement (60–65% of cases in Western series), our series demonstrated involvement of posterior compartments (gastrocnemius, soleus), hamstrings, adductors, and vastus groups in 70% of patients. Notably, 20% (6/30) of patients had multicompartmental disease with up to six thigh compartments affected. Additionally, 16.6% (5/30) of patients had bilateral limb involvement at presentation. This broader anatomical distribution may reflect delayed presentation due to diagnostic uncertainty or a characteristic pattern in advanced, prolonged uncontrolled glycaemic status.

Muscle biopsy was performed in only five patients (17%) and was reserved for patients with diagnostic uncertainty or when alternative pathology (e.g., malignancy, myositis) remained in the differential diagnosis. None of our biopsies revealed unexpected findings or altered management decisions. This aligns with the current literature consensus that biopsy provides limited additional value and carries risks of poor wound healing and infection. Our conservative approach to biopsy [17% vs historical rates of 30–50% in older series][413] reflects modern practice, which emphasises non-invasive imaging-based diagnosis. We recommend biopsy only in exceptional circumstances when imaging is inconclusive and diagnostic uncertainty persists despite an MRI.

A critical finding consistent with Indian experience is that diabetic myonecrosis represents a marker of severe systemic microangiopathy rather than an isolated muscular event. Diabetic nephropathy was present in 70% of our cohort (21/30 patients), with 30% in advanced stages (CKD stages 4–5); diabetic retinopathy occurred in 70% (21/30), including 36.7% with sight-threatening disease (PDR or CSME). These rates exceed those typically seen in general diabetes populations.[15] Similarly, Meher et al.[4] reported that all their patients had neuropathy and dyslipidaemia, with nephropathy present in four of six cases and retinopathy in four cases. Our findings reinforce that diabetic myonecrosis should serve as a clinical indicator for comprehensive screening for microvascular complications.

Our multivariable analysis identified recurrence as the strongest independent predictor of mortality (OR 8.20, 95% CI 1.45–46.40; P = 0.016), with recurrent patients experiencing a mortality rate of 71.4% compared to 21.7% in non-recurrent patients. Advanced CKD (stage ≥4) and hypoalbuminemia also independently predicted mortality. Importantly, the majority of deaths among recurrent cases were related to renal failure or infections, reinforcing the notion that recurrence signals underlying systemic deterioration.

Notably, inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) were markedly elevated (mean CRP 92.8 mg/L, ESR 70.5 mm/hr) but did not independently predict mortality after adjustment, suggesting that while inflammation accompanies myonecrosis, systemic factors (renal function, nutritional status) are more prognostic of long-term survival. Lower serum albumin, a marker of malnutrition, systemic illness, and advanced nephropathy, was independently associated with mortality, likely reflecting chronic inflammation, proteinuria, and advanced diabetic kidney disease.

The overall 33.3% mortality (10/30) over a median 6-year follow-up is substantially higher than that of general diabetes populations, reflecting advanced systemic disease.[1116] The high recurrence rate (23.3%) with a median time to recurrence of 5 years indicates that myonecrosis patients require extended surveillance protocols beyond initial hospitalisation. Survival curves diverged within 2–3 years and remained significantly separated at 5-year follow-up (85% vs. 43% survival), emphasising the prognostic significance of early identification of recurrence [Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure 4]. Recurrence occurred predominantly in patients with stages 4–5 CKD, heavier 24hour proteinuria, lower serum albumin and longer diabetes duration, suggesting that recurrence clusters in individuals with severe systemic microangiopathy and chronic systemic illness.

Although primary prevention is unproven, high-risk patients with longer diabetes duration and microvascular disease warrant closer surveillance, aggressive optimisation of glycaemic control, renoprotective therapy, proteinuria management, and prompt reassessment of new limb pain/swelling.

Apart from isolated case reports and small short-term case series, comparable long-term outcome data and analyses linking recurrence to survival have not, to our knowledge, been previously reported.

Consistent with emerging consensus, our study demonstrates that conservative management is effective and preferred for diabetic myonecrosis.[17] All patients were managed conservatively initially, with surgical intervention (16.7%) reserved for specific complications (abscess, compartment syndrome, unmanageable infection). The majority (83.3%) responded well to conservative therapy alone, supporting non-operative approaches as first-line treatment.

Our management protocol emphasised glycaemic optimisation (insulin, 73%; oral agents, 27%), pain control (NSAIDs, paracetamol, pregabalin), physiotherapy, and supportive measures. This approach aligns with the study by Kapur et al.,[18] which demonstrates superior outcomes in non-surgically treated patients. The median hospital stay of 10 days was relatively brief, reflecting early recognition and conservative management, which reduced the length of stay compared to historical series with more aggressive surgical approaches.

Finally, our findings suggest that diabetic myonecrosis is higher in type 2 diabetes-burden populations, which may present with more advanced and diffuse disease patterns. Moreover, 70% had concurrent nephropathy, and 70% had retinopathy, supporting myonecrosis as a clinical red flag for systemic microangiopathy requiring aggressive comorbidity management and multidisciplinary follow-up. These findings support recognising diabetic myonecrosis as a sentinel manifestation prompting comprehensive screening for microvascular complications.

Strengths of this study include its status as one of the largest single-centre diabetic myonecrosis series with comprehensive clinical and imaging data collection, and use of multivariable regression and survival analysis to identify prognostic factors, including recurrence and mortality. Limitations include the retrospective design and incomplete follow-up for some patients. Standardised assessments such as visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for pain were unavailable, and documentation of symptom resolution timing was inconsistent, limiting quantification of symptom intensity and duration.

CONCLUSION

From a practical standpoint, diabetic myonecrosis is a rare but significant complication of long-standing diabetes, often reflecting advanced microvascular disease. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients presenting with sudden-onset, localised limb pain and swelling. While ultrasonography can support the diagnosis and help exclude alternative causes such as abscesses or deep vein thrombosis, early MRI remains essential for confirming myonecrosis and delineating muscle involvement. Conservative management—including rest, analgesia, physiotherapy, and glycaemic optimisation is effective in most cases, while biopsy and surgery should be avoided unless clearly indicated.

The high recurrence and mortality rates observed in our study highlight the need for aggressive optimisation of comorbidities, multidisciplinary management, and structured follow-up. Early recognition enables targeted interventions and may improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
