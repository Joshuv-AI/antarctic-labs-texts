Proliferative myositis of the biceps brachii: A sarcoma mimicker
Authors: Taibi Ouiam, Zaynab Iraqi Houssaini, Rania Bouanane, Salma El Aouadi, Zineb Labbi, Omar El Aoufir, Laila Jroundi, Ola Messaoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProliferative myositis is a rare benign intramuscular inflammatory pseudotumor that may clinically mimic a soft tissue sarcoma due to its rapid growth and painful presentation. We report the case of a 50-year-old man presenting with a rapidly enlarging, firm, and painful mass of the left arm, which had appeared 3 weeks earlier, without any history of trauma or systemic symptoms. Ultrasound demonstrated an oblong intramuscular pseudomass within the biceps brachii muscle, with preserved fibrillar architecture and mild Doppler vascularity. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a lesion involving the biceps brachii and coracobrachialis muscles, appearing hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on STIR sequences, with homogeneous enhancement after gadolinium administration and associated subcutaneous edema. The preservation of muscle fascicles within the lesion resulted in a characteristic fibrillar pattern. A follow-up ultrasound performed 1 month later showed complete resolution. In this clinical and imaging context, proliferative myositis should be considered as a leading diagnostic hypothesis. Although the absence of histopathologic confirmation represents a limitation in our case, the characteristic imaging features combined with complete spontaneous resolution strongly support this diagnosis and make a malignant process such as sarcoma highly unlikely. Recognition of this presentation is essential to guide appropriate management and potentially avoid unnecessary biopsy in selected cases.Keywords: Proliferative myositis, MRI, Ultrasound, Differential diagnosis

Introduction

Proliferative myositis is a rare benign intramuscular pseudosarcomatous lesion that typically presents as a rapidly growing and painful mass, often mimicking soft tissue sarcoma.

Proliferative myositis mainly affects middle-aged adults, most commonly between the fourth and sixth decades of life, with no clear sex predilection. The lesion most frequently involves the muscles of the trunk, shoulder girdle, and upper extremities, although various anatomical locations have been reported in the literature [1,2].

Imaging, particularly ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging, may suggest the diagnosis by demonstrating preservation of muscle fascicles within the lesion, resulting in a characteristic fibrillar pattern. However, distinguishing this entity from malignant tumors may remain challenging in some cases.

We report a case of proliferative myositis of the biceps brachii, emphasizing its imaging features and diagnostic considerations.

Case report

A 50-year-old man with no significant past medical history presented with a rapidly enlarging, firm, and painful mass of the left arm, first noticed 3 weeks earlier. There was no history of trauma, fever, or systemic symptoms (Figs. 1,2).

Longitudinal (A) and axial (B) ultrasound images of the left arm demonstrate an oblong intramuscular pseudomass within the biceps brachii muscle (blue arrow). The lesion shows preserved fibrillar architecture, corresponding to intact muscle fascicles, and mild internal vascularity on color Doppler (red arrow), with associated adjacent soft-tissue edema (green arrow).

Magnetic resonance imaging of the left arm demonstrating an intramuscular lesion within the biceps brachii muscle (arrow). The lesion appears hypointense on T1-weighted images (A), markedly hyperintense on STIR sequences (B), and demonstrates homogeneous enhancement following gadolinium administration (C). Diffusion-weighted imaging shows hyperintensity (D) with corresponding elevated apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values (E), consistent with facilitated diffusion. Associated subcutaneous soft-tissue edema is also present.

On physical examination, a palpable intramuscular mass was noted, with normal overlying skin and no signs of inflammation. No neurological deficit or limitation of range of motion was reported. Laboratory tests showed no evidence of inflammatory syndrome.

Ultrasound examination demonstrated an oblong intramuscular pseudomass within the biceps brachii muscle. The lesion exhibited preserved fibrillar echotexture corresponding to intact muscle fascicles traversing the mass, with associated adjacent soft tissue edematous changes. No calcifications were identified. Color Doppler evaluation demonstrated mild internal vascularity. The preservation of muscular architecture suggested a benign intramuscular process rather than a true neoplastic lesion, although malignancy could not be formally excluded at this stage.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a lesion located along the anteromedial aspect of the left arm, involving the biceps brachii and coracobrachialis muscles. The lesion appeared hypointense on T1-weighted images and markedly hyperintense on STIR sequences. Diffusion-weighted imaging showed hyperintensity without evidence of true restricted diffusion. Following intravenous gadolinium administration, the lesion demonstrated homogeneous enhancement. Normal muscle fascicles were preserved within the lesion, producing a characteristic fibrillar pattern. Associated subcutaneous soft-tissue edema was noted, without evidence of necrosis or destructive changes.

A follow-up ultrasound performed 1 month later demonstrated complete resolution of the lesion.

Given the typical imaging appearance and spontaneous regression, a diagnosis of proliferative myositis was considered highly probable.

Discussion

Proliferative myositis is an uncommon benign inflammatory condition affecting skeletal muscle, considered a self-limited reactive process of uncertain etiology [3]. Histologically, it is characterized by fibroblastic and myofibroblastic proliferation interspersed between preserved muscle fibers, which explains its distinctive imaging appearance [3].

The main diagnostic challenge lies in its differentiation from malignant soft tissue tumors, particularly sarcomas, due to its rapid onset and painful presentation [4]. However, unlike malignant lesions, proliferative myositis typically preserves the underlying muscle architecture, with intact muscle fascicles visible within the lesion. This results in the characteristic fibrillar or “checkerboard” pattern observed on both ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging, which represents a key diagnostic clue [4,5].

On ultrasound, proliferative myositis appears as an ill-defined intramuscular mass with linear echogenic muscle fibers traversing the lesion, corresponding to preserved fascicles [6]. Doppler imaging may demonstrate mild internal vascularity [6]. Magnetic resonance imaging typically shows T1 hypointensity and T2/STIR hyperintensity with homogeneous enhancement after contrast administration, without necrosis or significant architectural distortion [5,7]. Diffusion-weighted imaging may show hyperintensity without true restricted diffusion, likely related to increased water content and vasogenic edema rather than malignancy.

Despite these characteristic findings, imaging alone is not always sufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis, particularly in atypical presentations. Histopathologic confirmation remains the reference standard when malignancy cannot be confidently excluded.

Previously reported cases of proliferative myositis predominantly involved middle-aged adults, with lesions most frequently arising in the shoulder girdle, trunk, and upper extremities [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Patients typically presented with a rapidly enlarging, painful intramuscular mass, clinically raising concern for soft tissue sarcoma [1,8]. Lesion size varied considerably across reports, ranging from small focal nodules to larger infiltrative pseudomasses [8]. In most published cases, histopathologic confirmation was performed because of the suspicious clinical and imaging presentation [1,2]. However, several authors have emphasized that characteristic imaging findings, particularly the preservation of muscle fascicles producing the typical fibrillar or checkerboard pattern, may strongly suggest the diagnosis and potentially support conservative management with short-term imaging follow-up in selected patients [8,9]. Our case is consistent with these previous reports, as the patient presented with a rapidly enlarging painful lesion of the upper extremity showing typical ultrasound and MRI features, followed by complete spontaneous regression on follow-up imaging.

Several entities should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Early-stage myositis ossificans may present as a rapidly growing intramuscular mass without calcifications on initial imaging, but typically evolves toward peripheral ossification on follow-up [10]. Nodular fasciitis is another benign pseudosarcomatous lesion characterized by rapid growth, although it more commonly involves fascia or subcutaneous tissues and lacks preservation of muscle architecture [11]. Infectious myositis should be suspected in the presence of systemic symptoms or biological inflammatory syndrome and may demonstrate abscess formation or rim enhancement on imaging [12]. Intramuscular hematoma may also mimic a soft tissue mass, particularly in the absence of a clear traumatic history, but its signal characteristics vary according to the stage of blood degradation and do not show preserved fibrillar architecture [13]. Finally, soft tissue sarcoma remains the most critical diagnosis to exclude and typically demonstrates aggressive features such as architectural destruction, necrosis, heterogeneous enhancement, and true restricted diffusion [7].

Potential pitfalls include misinterpreting the rapid growth of the lesion as a sign of malignancy, overlooking the preservation of muscle fascicles, or incorrectly interpreting diffusion hyperintensity as restricted diffusion.

In the appropriate clinical and imaging context, proliferative myositis should be considered as a leading diagnostic hypothesis. In our case, although the absence of histopathologic confirmation represents a limitation, the combination of characteristic imaging features and complete spontaneous resolution strongly supports this diagnosis and makes a malignant process such as sarcoma highly unlikely. Short-term imaging follow-up may therefore be a reasonable approach in selected cases to avoid unnecessary invasive procedures.

Conclusion

Proliferative myositis is a benign, self-limited intramuscular pseudotumor that may mimic soft tissue sarcoma. The presence of preserved muscle architecture with a characteristic fibrillar pattern should strongly suggest this diagnosis. In our case, despite the absence of histopathologic confirmation, typical imaging features combined with complete spontaneous resolution strongly support a benign process and make malignancy highly unlikely. Awareness of this entity is essential to guide appropriate management and avoid unnecessary invasive procedures.
