Transforming soil decontamination: the role and prospects of nanotechnological remediation
Authors: Vartika Singh, Hina Upadhyay, Tabarak Malik, Sajad Ali, Richa Mishra, Anand Mohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soil pollution, driven by industrialization and intensive agriculture, poses critical risks and global threats, with over 16% of global soils estimated to be contaminated by heavy metals, organic pollutants, and pesticides. This is impacting ecosystems, food security, and human health through bioaccumulation and carcinogenicity. Conventional remediation methods are often costly, destructive, and insufficient for deep and heterogenous contamination, often generating secondary waste and require long time for treatment. Nanotechnology introduces a paradigm shift, leveraging nanomaterials with high surface area, tunable reactivities, and targeted delivery capabilities, offering rapid, versatile, and potentially sustainable in situ remediation. Nanoremediation mechanisms includes adsorption and immobilization, chemical reduction and degradation, catalytic and photocatalytic degradation, nano-enhanced bioremediation and phytoremediation. Key NM includes nZVI, FeO, doped TiO2, carbon nanotubes, graphene oxide, dendrimers, and nanobubbles, each tailored for specific contaminants and remediation strategies. Advances in green synthesis, surface functionalization, and delivery methods such as colloidal suspensions, electrokinetic injection addresses the challenge of NM scalability, mobility, and specificity in complex soil. Field-scale applications, in situ injection and nano-enhanced PRBs, demonstrate significant reduction in contaminant concentration and remediation times, though scalability and cost-benefit analyses remain ongoing. However, concerns about NM ecotoxicity, fate, and long-term environmental impacts necessitate comprehensive risk assessment, LCA, and robust regulatory frameworks. Nanotechnology offers transformative potential for soil remediation, with demonstrated efficacy in pollutant removal and process efficiency. Future researchers must focus on smart, sustainable NM design, predictive modelling, long-term field studies, and public engagement to ensure safe, effective, and widely accepted deployment of NMs. Despite growing body of the literature on NM-based remediation, majority of the existing studies are confined to aqueous batch system and controlled laboratory setups. Field scale investigations conducted under authentic soil conditions are scarce. This disparity limits direct translation of mechanistic findings in real-world soil decontamination conditions. Consequently, future research studies must prioritize validated soil column studies, pilot-scale trials, and in situ field studies for bridging gap between theoretical potential and practical applications. This review fills critical and long-standing gap by providing actionable, visually guided, and quantitatively supported information in a single data-rich document, satisfying the scope for transformative and interdisciplinary research.

Introduction

Soil pollution from heavy metals, organic pollutants, pesticides, and emerging contaminants is a surfacing global threat, with extensive data indicating widespread contamination and significant risk to ecosystems, food security, water resources, animal and human health. Over 20 million hectares of land are contaminated with heavy metals such as, Zn, Pb, Ni, As, Hg, Cd, Cu, and Cr. The average global pollution load index of agricultural soil is 2.03, indicating moderate pollution because of industrial activities (31.5%), agricultural activities (27.1%), and atmospheric deposition (22.7%), as major contributor of contamination.1–3 In urban and industrial region, exceedance rates for Cd (0.53 mg kg−1), Cu (171.15 mg kg−1), and Ni (125.42 mg kg−1) in soils can reach up to 98–100%, which is above background values.4 POPs like, DDT, endrin and pesticides are widely detected from soil, with bioaccumulation documented in grains, vegetables, fruits, seafood, and even cow milk.5 Emerging contaminants like PFAS have global soil burdens exceeding 7000 metric tons for PFOS, and 1860 metric tons for PFOA where, PFAS concentration is >160 ng kg−1 of soil.6 Soil pollution reduces fertility, biodiversity, and productivity of ecosystem, and nutrient cycling gets impacted due to increase in the emission of greenhouse gases.7,8 About 95% of food come from soil, contamination leads to crop yield loss, food chain bioaccumulation, and global risk to food safety and security.7,9,10 Such pollutants from soil leach into groundwater and rivers, compounding water pollution and reducing safe water access for over 900 million people worldwide. Pollutants in soil, air, and water matrices cause at least 9 million premature deaths annually, with over 60% due to cardiovascular diseases. Chronic exposure of pollutants such as, heavy metal and pesticides are linked to cancer, neurological, renal, and cardiovascular diseases.6,11,12

Conventional soil remediation methods, both ex situ (excavation, soil washing, thermal treatment) and in situ faces significant limitation related to cost, efficiency, environmental impact, and technical feasibility. Ex situ methods are generally more destructive and costly compared to in situ methods, which are often slower and less effective for deep and heterogeneous contamination. Ex situ remediation can cost up to $500 per ton of soil (or $1500 per m3 or $100 per m2) and take up to 15 years of time, depending on site and technique for removal of contamination.13 Similarly, thermal desorption costs €1782 per m3, compared to €228–371 per m3 for bioremediation and soil washing.14 Excavation disrupt ecosystem, increases health risks, and generates large volume of waste, requiring further treatment and disposal.15,16 Soil washing and thermal treatment method produces secondary waste such as contaminated wash water, off-gases, and residue fines, requiring additional management of contaminants.17,18 Offsite ex situ thermal desorption causes highest environmental impact among the common methods due to transport and use of energy.19In situ bioremediation and chemical oxidation are slower than ex situ methods, which require years to achieve regulatory targets.20 Effectiveness of such remediation method is significantly lower in deep contaminated and heterogenous soils due to poor distribution of amendments and preferential flow paths.21,22 Efficacy data of Wei et al. are indicating that in situ chemical oxidation achieves average remediation efficiencies of 65.8% for soil and 82.7% for groundwater but, rebound effects can cause residual contaminants to increase from 19.1% to 57.7% of the original content due to non-aqueous phase liquid, adsorption effect, contaminant aging, vapor migration, and low permeability zones.23 Pump-and-treat systems require decades for operation due to slow contaminant back-diffusion and diminishing returns whereas, bioremediation and mycoremediation is limited by factors such as, oxygen, nutrient availability, temperature, and contaminant bioavailability.20,24 Nanotechnology involves manipulation of materials at nanoscale (1–100 nm), where unique kind of physical and chemical phenomena emerges. In environmental remediation, NMs are engineered to interact with soil and water pollutants, leveraging their small size and high reactivity for contaminant removal and immobilization.25–27 NMs provide more reactive sites per unit mass, enhancing adsorption and catalytic degradation of pollutants.25,27–29 For example, nZVI can immobilize heavy metals (Cd, Ni, Pb) with high efficiency, reducing pollutant bioavailability up to 45.3% in soil and 37.6% in plants.25,30,31 NMs are functionalized for selective targeting of specific contaminants, improving efficacy and selectivity of remediation. Engineered NMs can be designed for in situ applications, allowing direct treatment of contaminated sites without excavation of soil.27,29

Ionic material and NOM are controlled by behaviour of NMs in soil. Across metal, metal-oxide, carbon and nZVI systems, multiple variables govern aggregation, mobility, surface coatings and ultimately contaminant binding and immobilization of pollutants on NM surface. For citrate-coated AgNPs, high ionic strength accelerate aggregation and increases soil adsorption while, humic-acid NOM reduces aggregation and soil adsorption, keeping particle mobile in porewater.32 Zeta potential becomes more negative at lower ionic strength and higher NOM, indicating stronger electrostatic repulsion along with better colloidal stability. For biochar NPs, transport through soil is high at low electrolyte (0.1–1 mM) but fall with the rise of ionic strength, for e.g. Ca2+ suppresses transport more than Na+ by stronger charge screening. NOM (humic acid) generally increases transport, but high NOM and Ca2+ result in cation-bridging, resulting in renewed retention.33 Chemistry of soil solution, ionic strength and dissolved organic matter alters aggregation, aging, and heteroaggregation with minerals and microbes, causing change in fate and toxicity. Surface coatings of polymers, biomolecules, and humic acid are useful in inhibiting agglomeration and maintain small aggregation even under high salinity and variable NOM. Coating of zwitterionic on the surface show strong stability and coating of humic acid promote dissolution and bioavailability of metal-oxide NPs, altering redox and adsorption behaviour. Environmental studies emphasize that pH, competing ions, organic matter and ionic strength must be optimized for real-field nanoremediation performance.33–36

Nanotechnological remediation offers transformative approach to soil decontamination by synergistically integrating NMs with plant and microbes. The process hinges on four interconnected mechanisms: adsorption and immobilization, reduction and degradation, catalytic and photocatalytic oxidation, and nano-enhanced biological processes that create a positive feedback loop with bioremediation. NMs are classified as reactive metals and metal oxides, carbon-based nanostructures, polymer-based structures with delivery vectors ensuring effective application. Deploying in situ, ex situ, and hybrid nano-phytoremediation strategies, coupled with smart nanosensors achieves real-time monitoring and enhances remediation effectiveness. Nanoremediation is promising yet, concerns remain about the long-term fate, ecotoxicity, and potential accumulation of NMs in the environment. Modified and coated NMs can reduce toxicity to soil microbes, but more research is needed for large-scale, sustainable application of such materials. Their release poses ecotoxicological risks, necessitating life-cycle analysis and consideration of economic, regulatory and social factors. Ultimately, NM composition dictates properties, properties define mechanisms, mechanisms comprehend application strategies, and all are tempered by environmental and regulatory realities. This interconnected architecture makes review not only a catalog of methods but decision-support framework for researches, engineers, and policymakers seeking to advance sustainable soil remediation (Fig. 1).

Origin of study and literature review

A systematic literature search was conducted across twelve major scientific databases, Science Direct, MDPI, RSC, ACS, Nature, Wiley, Springer Nature, PubMed, Google Scholar, ASCE Library, Taylor and Francis, and Frontiers. Comprehensive set of Keywords centered on nanoremediation including, adsorption, nano-phytoremediation, immobilization, nZVI, chemical reduction, photocatalytic oxidation, carbon-based nanostructures, dendrimers, hyperbranched polymer were used for literature search. This search initially returned 1275 records, which were then examined for duplicate entries. After removing 209 duplicate records, 1066 unique papers were proceeded for title and abstract screening phase. At this stage, strict inclusion criteria were applied including; studies had to address the subject area and scope of nanotechnological remediation of contaminated soil and relevant matrices, fall mostly within 5–10 year, and be written in English. Applying these criteria led to exclusion of 615 records that were either out of the specific time frame or irrelevant to the topic. Consequently, 451 articles were retrieved for detailed eligibility assessment. During text review a further 147 studies were excluded because of the lack of at least one of the required keywords and are methodologically insufficient. As a result, 304 studies fully satisfied all the predefined eligibility criteria and were included in review paper. The entire work flow from initial database search through, synthesis of finding, and manuscript preparation were completed within 7-month timeframe, ensuring comprehensive and timely review (Fig. 2).

Fundamental mechanisms of nanoremediation

Adsorption and immobilization

Nanoremediation leverages NMs for the removal and immobilization of heavy metals and radionuclides from contaminated soil and water. The primary mechanism involved in remediation are physisorption and chemisorption, which enables high efficiency adsorption, reducing contaminant bioavailability and leachability. Physisorption involves weak van der Waals forces whereas, chemisorption involves formation of strong chemical bonds between contaminant and NM surfaces. In both mechanisms, NM surface, functional group, and solution pH influences the mechanism of physisorption and chemisorption.37–41 Metal oxides (Fe, Zn, Zr oxides) carry surface hydroxyl ( <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> MOH) groups which bind metal(loid)s by inner-sphere complexes, described as surface complexation and ligand exchange.38,42,43 For AsO43− and H2PO4−, adsorption commonly proceeds by ligand exchange of surface –OH with anion, forming strong and pH dependent complexes. Negatively charged surfaces like biochar, clay, and other oxides at high pH exchanges resident cations (Ca2+, Na+) with contaminated metal ions. Cation exchange is key immobilization route in soils and clay-rich systems. At low pH, oxide and biochar become protonated and positively charged, attracting electrostatically anionic species. Metal oxide-loaded biochar exhibits enhanced Cd and Pb adsorption, governed by ion-exchange and surface complexation. Fe/Zr-modified biochar capture anions by electrostatic and inner sphere complexation. At higher pH, suitable counter-ions adsorbed metals form surface precipitate and co-precipitate (metal hydroxides, phosphates, arsenates) on oxide and biochar surfaces. Clay and oxide surfaces nucleate new mineral phases, leading to long-term immobilization due to precipitation and nucleation growth processes.37–41 ZnO nanoparticles exhibit adsorption capacity ranging from 0.3 to 1500 mg g−1 for multiple metals, using electrostatic interaction, complexation, ion-exchange, and precipitation.38 Change in pH cause significant shift in the adsorption capacity of NMs. TiO2 show pH dependent optimal adsorption at pH 3 and 7. At pH 3, Pb(ii) adsorption is 30.8 mg g−1, Cd(ii) adsorption is at 10.02 mg g−1 whereas, at pH 7, Pb(ii) adsorption is 26.20 mg g−1, and Cd(ii) adsorption is 26.75 mg g−1 with 100 mg L−1 of metal concentration in aqueous system.44 From multiple studies it is observed that, higher surface area and tailored functional groups (–SO3H, –COOH, –NH2) enhances adsorption whereas, optimal pH are suitable for removal of Pb (5–8) and Cu (5–9), Cr (2–4), and Hg (3–8).37,39,41,45,46 Majority of the systems follow pseudo-second-order kinetics, indicating dominance of chemisorption, and NMs retain >90% of efficiency after 5–7 cycle of remediation with adsorption capacities of 21.99, 29.65, 31.01 mg g−1 for As(v), Cd(ii) and Pb(ii) in groundwater and industrial wastewater.47,48

For heavy metals, nanomaterials mostly immobilize rather than destroy pollutants but for many organic compounds true degradation is possible. Immobilization alters contaminant mobility and bioavailability without removing the element from soil. Heavy metals are primarily immobilized through adsorption, complexation, reduction to less soluble form, and co-precipitation, decreasing mobility and bioavailability, but leaves the metal in place.49,50 In contrast, degradation refer to bond-breaking and destruction of organic pollutants via oxidation or reductive nanocatalytic processes. Heavy-metal nanoremediation is mainly risk management (stabilization/immobilization), not removal of element. Metals are not biodegradable and cannot be destroyed. It focuses on valence state like, Cr(vi) to Cr(iii) precipitation/complexation, which is stabilization, not elimination.50,51 Many amendments indicate strong initial reduction in available metals, but immobilization can weaken over years. Biochar is often effective for 2–3 and sometimes 5 years but, acid rain, flooding, soil pH, and redox changes can reduce stability of contaminants.52,53 nZVI provide stronger short-term immobilization, but ageing (Fe crystallization) causes more Pb desorption. ZVI may retain metals better over long periods in some soils and Z(zeolite-supported)-nZVI transforms As, Cd, Pb into residual forms, strongly resisting acid-rain leaching, indicating high long-term stability.54 S(Sulfidated)-ZVI maintains low dissolved Pb, Cd, As under oxic-anoxic cycles and unmodified ZVI can also shift residual metals to more labile fraction over time. Nano-silica drives metals from exchangeable/carbonate to Fe–Mn oxide, organic, and residual fractions, implying more stable sequestration. In Pb-contaminated soil, higher dissolved Pb occurs at slightly anoxic Eh, linked to dissolution of Fe/Mn oxides and release of co-precipitated Pb.52,53,55 Soil organic matter act as an electron shuttle, driving reduction of redox-active metals, decreasing mobility and making outcome sensitive to redox and OM composition. Plant roots, earthworms, and soil microbes can weaken biochar-based immobilization over time due to change in pH, redox, and organic ligands. Phosphorus-modified biochar changes microbial community structure and links specific bacterial/fungal phyla to particular metal functions, indicating active microbially mediated metal transformation within soil matrix. Anionic species (As, Cr) immobilize better in acidic soils and cationic metals (Cd, Pb, Zn) in calcareous soils with higher pH and carbonate content, even with the same nZVI (Table 1).52–55

Adsorption capacities and efficiencies of NMs for removal of heavy metals

Chemical reduction and degradation

NM leverages the unique properties of high surface area, reactivities, and tunable surface chemistry which accelerate chemical reduction and degradation for wide range of contaminants. Key mechanism includes, catalytic reduction, photocatalytic degradation, advanced oxidation, along with synergistic sorption-catalysis, where removal efficiencies exceed 90% for targeted contaminant in optimized conditions.71–74 Chemical reduction and degradation with the help of NMs, especially nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVI) and their modifications are fundamental for the remediation of halogenated organic contaminants like TCE and PCE. nZVI remove contaminants by redox reaction at a corroding Fe0 core with evolving surface phases such as oxides, hydroxides, sulfides which control electron transfer. nZVI is rapidly oxidized by water and O2, forming Fe(II/III) oxides/hydroxides (FeO, Fe(OH)2, Fe3O4, FeOOH), which passivate the surface and lower down reductivity. The core–shell structure provides Fe0 as electron donor and oxides of Fe(ii/iii) mediate transfer to contaminants and activate oxidants. Chemical reduction relies on direct electron transfer from nanomaterial to the contaminant, leading to dechlorination and detoxification. nZVI and its sulfidated and bimetallic forms facilitate electron transfer to halogenated organics due to breaking of carbon–halogen bonds. This is particularly effective for compounds like TCE and chlorinated pesticides, resulting in their stepwise reduction into less harmful products such as ethene and ethane.75–78 Sulfidated nZVI (S-nZVI) and bimetallic nZVI (e.g., Fe–Pd) exhibit dramatic increase in TCE reduction rate. Fe–Pd, S-nZVI, Fe–Ni, Fe–Cu, and Fe–Ag cause surface area normalized TCE reduction rates (k′SA) approximately 6800, 190, 130, 20, and 8-fold higher than unmodified nZVI, respectively.76 S-nZVI (Fe/S = 10) achieved TCE removal efficiency of 92.6% in 24 hours, compared to 30.2% for unmodified nZVI.77 Graphene-modified microscale ZVI (mZVI-GO) showed TCE removal reaching up to 99%, versus 30% for nZVI alone.79 Electron use efficiency (εe) for dechlorination of TCE have increased from 2% (nZVI) to ∼100% (Fe–Pd), 69% (Fe–Ni), and 72% (S-nZVI) due to modification of nZVI.76 Sulfidated (s)-ZVI achieved 70% electron use efficiency which is 160-fold higher than nZVI (<0.5%).80 Sulfidation reduces the consumption of Fe(0) due to hydrogen evolution from nZVI (77.0%) compared to S-nZVI (12.8%), allowing more electrons to be used for the reduction of contaminant.75,77–79 S-nZVI exhibit pseudo-first-order kinetic rate constants of 48.5% in degradation of TCE, which is higher than nZVI.77 Sulfidation increases surface area by 40-fold and normalized first-order TCE degradation rate constant.78 nZVI achieved 57% reduction in TCE saturation by gas-induced immobilization and 13% by direct degradation at pore scale.81

Cerium oxide (CeO2) NPs have demonstrated ∼97% degradation for various organic pollutants. Integration of NMs with biocatalysts, polymers, and carbon-based support enhances stability, reusability, and selectivity.74 NMs used in remediation are metal oxides (e.g., TiO2, ZnO, Fe3O4), nZVI, bimetallic NPs, carbon-based NMs, and polymer supported composites. Metal-oxides (CeO2, TiO2, ZnO) have achieved >97% degradation of dyes and pharmaceuticals under visible and UV light with the rate constant of 0.0701 min−1.82–85 Encapsulated transition-metal NPs and hybrid nanocomposites activates persulfate and peroxymonosulfate, causing generation of sulfate radicals for efficient degradation of (97.27%) tetracycline.72,73 Above 90% of removal efficiencies for organic dyes, pharmaceuticals, and pesticides under optimal conditions are frequently reported.73,84 Rate constant for degradation of dyes have ranged from 1.13 × 10−2 mmol g−1 h−1 (AgNPs) to 6.566 × 10−2 min−1 (ZnO).85–87 Many of the NMs retain >85% of activities after 5–11 cycles of adsorption–desorption, exhibiting high scalability.74 Degradation of real pharmaceutical wastewater have achieved 89.79% tetracycline removal, 80.65% TOC reduction, and 78.64% COD reduction in 2 h.73

Catalytic degradation and photocatalytic oxidation

Nanoremediation employs NMs to either chemically reduce organic pollutants or to degrade and mineralize them. Such processes often rely on catalytic and photocatalytic activities to generate reactive radicals. Chemical reduction converts contaminant to a less toxic and less mobile form, not necessarily destroy it. It is typical for metals and some organics such as TCE, by redox-active NPs is typical. It directly transfers electron from NM or NM-activated reductant to contaminant, often called sorption–reduction when coupled with adsorption. Degradation/mineralization of organics result in breaking of organic molecules and ideally mineralizes to CO2, water, and inorganic ions. It is a powerful approach for the removal of complex and POPs. Both chemical catalysis and photocatalysis leverages NMs to generate reactive radicals which mineralizes pollutants.88 During photocatalytic oxidation, light excites semiconductor NMs, creating electron–hole pairs which generate hydroxyl and superoxide radicals, causing non-selectively oxidation of organic pollutants to CO2, water and inorganic salts. NMs such as metal oxide and metal NPs catalyze oxidants (H2O2, persulfate) to radicals like ˙OH and SO4˙− which can break down organics. When nickel ferrite (NiFe2O4) NPs are mechanically stimulated, it can achieve a significant degradation of malachite green dye and convert them in to CO2 and H2O. The efficiency of degradation is enhanced by stirring speed and addition of Al2O3, demonstrating robust pollutant removal and easy recovery of catalyst because of magnetic properties.89 Similarly, Cu–Ag nanocomposites with H2O2 and visible light have achieved complete mineralization of pharmaceuticals like, sulfamethoxazole and norfloxacin, where hydroxyl radicals acted as main active species.90 Semiconductor NMs (TiO2, ZnO, g-C3N4) generates electron–hole pairs under light and produces radicals to degrade pollutants. TiO2 nanocone photoelectrocatalysts achieved 99% degradation of 4-chlorophenol (20 ppm) and over 55% mineralization, which outperform traditional morphologies due to improved charge separation and mass transfer.51 TiO2/g-C3N5 nanocomposites degrade 96% of rhodamine B and 95% of methylene blue in 150 minutes under simulated solar light, maintaining >85% efficiency after 4 cycles of adsorption and desorption.91 CdS/TiO2 nanocomposites exhibited 84% degradation of acid blue dye in 90 minutes compared to 68% for CdS and only 9% for TiO2 alone, highlighting the benefit of heterojunctions for charge separation and visible light responses. Both chemical and photocatalytic nano-catalyst have demonstrated high efficiency in degrading POPs, with the data and result indicating >90% removal in majority of cases.88

During in situ soil photocatalysis, weak light penetration in soil which is explicitly identified as a limitation, and effective treatment would require constant land ploughing to expose photocatalysts to sunlight. Opaque and highly absorbing matrices hinder light reaching catalyst surfaces, reducing radical generation. This effect is clearly visible for pesticide solutions and nanocatalytic suspensions.92,93 Soil solutions with high organic matter cause aggregation of TiO2 and modification in bandgap whereas, acidic conditions hinder adsorption, reducing photocatalytic effectiveness. In more alkaline, high ionic strength soil solutions, organic compounds adsorb to the surface of NPs and block active sites, decreasing activity. Adsorption of soil component changes TiO2 crystallite size, surface charge, and function groups causing alteration in photocatalytic properties.94,95 Photocatalytic activity requires water and oxygen at catalyst surface and photogenerated holes oxidizes water/hydroxide to ˙OH and electrons reduce O2 to superoxide. In soils, variable moisture content and limited diffusion of gases can restrict contact with O2 and H2O, potentially lowering ROS generation compared to well-aerated aqueous systems.92,93 Light excites semiconductor NMs, creating electron–hole pairs. Electron reduces O2 to superoxide (˙O2−), holes oxidize water/OH− to ˙OH and both are strong oxidant which non-selectively attack organics, leading to degradation and often complete mineralization.35,93 TiO2-based systems degrade dyes, antibiotics, PAHs pesticides and CeO2-based composites degrade organics under visible light. NMs (iron oxides, nZVI) activate oxidants like H2O2 and persulfate to form sulfate or hydroxyl radicles, degrading a broad range of organic pollutants. Soil pH, organic matter and competing sorption indicate significant impact on hydroxyl radicle generation and photocatalytic performance in situ.34,35,93 Photocatalytic and catalytic oxidation by NMs not only result in ˙OH and SO4˙− formation, but act as network of interfacial charge-transfer and pathway shift between radical and nonradical oxidation. In SnO2/SnS2 and BiOBr/SnO2 phase composition and p–n junctions, tune band positions drives charge separation and selective formation of ˙OH, 1O2, and O2˙− with controlled oxidation pathway. Through a combination of electron paramagnetic resonance, selective quenching experiments, and theoretical calculations, singlet oxygen (1O2) and superoxide anion radicals (˙O2−) were identified as the primary reactive intermediates, while sulfate radicals (SO4˙−) and hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) played only minor roles.96 Confining Co single atom to mesoporous silica shifts Fenton-like system from 1O2 to electron-transfer pathway, boosting peroxymonosulfate utilization and 4-chlorophenol degradation. DMPO and TEMP-based spin-trapping with kinetic EPR have exhibited time-resolved quantification of 1O2 and radicals, revealing false positive.35,97–99

Light-driven ROS in nanoremediaiton of soils

Light-activated NMs increasingly underpin nanoremediation strategies. ROS is generated under solar and visible light causing degradation of organic contaminants in soil and aqueous systems. This work is mostly in aqueous media but directly relevant to surface and soil porewaters. Chromophoric DOM adsorbed on hematite nanoplatelets act as molecular photosensitizer after solar excitation, electron transfer from DOM to Fe(iii)-oxide, forming surface Fe(ii) which reduces O2 to ˙O2− and ultimately singlet oxygen, driving DOM degradation. Similarly, hematite-oxalate systems produce carboxyl radicals, H2O2, and then ˙OH by Fe(ii)/Fe(iii) cycling, efficiently mineralizing model dyes and soil DOM extracts. These studies indicate molecular-level control by ligand binding, surface facet, and Fe valence cycling.97,98 Facet engineering of FeWO4 nanosheets have indicated that, exposing the {001} facet enhances oxalic-acid activation, H2O2 production, and diffusion of ˙OH into solution, indicating higher degradation of dyes and Cr(vi) reduction than {010} facets under visible light. Nano-enhanced biological processes

Classes of nanomaterials in nanoremediation

Role of reactive metal and metal oxides in nanoremediation

Metallic and metal oxide nanomaterials are used at the forefront of soil nanoremediation due to their high reactivity, versatility, and ability to target a wide range of contaminants. Among them, nZVI are the most widely used, with proven high reduction power and broad contaminant applicability.130,133–136 Advances such as doping of nZVI with Pd, Ni, and Cu have led to bimetallic nanoparticles which have enhanced reactivity and longevity.135–137 Iron oxide NPs such as magnetite, maghemite, and goethite are highly effective in adsorption and magnetic separation. TiO2 NPs are premier photocatalysts under UV light, which further get improved due to doping with nano-metals (N, C, S) to enable visible light activity.133,138–140 Other oxides such as ZnO, MnO2, and Al2O3 are also being employed due to their adsorption and catalytic properties.42,133,138 Evidences strongly support the effectiveness of metallic and metal oxide NMs, especially in nZVI and its bimetallic forms for the remediation of soil. Quantitative data from laboratory, pilot, and field scale studies have exhibited high removal rates and immobilization efficiencies of NMs. 5% of nZVI can reduce exchangeable arsenic up to 70% and mercury up to 90%. nZVI is the most widely used NM for soil remediation, with highly specific surface area and strong reducing power, causing rapid reduction and immobilization of wide range of contaminants, including heavy metals (Cr, As, Pb, Cd) and organic pollutants such as chlorinated solvents and PCBs.130,133,135–137,140 During pot experiment, integration of nZVI@BC (8 g kg−1) achieves 100% immobilization of Cr(vi) in 15 days and 89.5% decrease in Arsenic at a dose of 2%,129 and 80–100% removal of various organic and inorganic pollutants from soil and groundwater matrices.136,141,142 Bimetallic nZVI such as Fe–Pd, Fe–Ni, and Fe–Cu, which are doped with Pd, Ni, and Cu are increasing reactivity and longevity, and Fe–Pd have exhibited 6800-fold increase in TCS reduction rate as compare to unmodified nZVI. Modified nZVI perform better in reducing toxicity from environmental and biological matrices compared to bare unmodified nZVI.76,133,136,137 nZVI is a very common NM for in situ soil and groundwater remediation in Europe and USA, with successful pilot and field-scale applications.134,136,140 nZVI/copper slang have removed 98.47% of metronidazole (MNZ) at pH 5.04 and 6.8 g L−1 of adsorbent along with 35.5 min contact time. Due to nZVI integration, iron content increased by 20%, contributing in 90% removal of MNZ from aqueous media with strong reusability after 6 regeneration cycles.143 ZnO/Sb2MoO6 Z-scheme heterojunction exhibited enhanced reliable photocatalytic activity at pH 9, 1 g L−1 composite and 5 mg L−1 of dye dosage. For RhB, degradation rate constant was 589.3 × 10−4 min−1, demonstrating the way to develop photocatalysts by Z-scheme electron transfer pathway.144 Magnetite (Fe3O4) and maghemite (γ-Fe2O3) are used for the adsorption and magnetic separation of heavy metals (As, Pb, Cd, Ni, Cu).129,145,146 Goethite nanospheres have achieved 82.5% decrease in arsenic concentration at 0.2% dose with a notable immobilization at low concentration.129 nZVI supported by magnetic biochar can reduce As and Cd content by 13.7% and 12.3%, decreasing their bioavailability by 34.1% and 93.2%.147 Magnetic NMs can be recovered easily and reused for their high adsorption capacities while, reducing their environmental risk.133,146 TiO2 is a premium photocatalyst for degrading organic contaminants under UV lights. Doping TiO2 with N, C, and S enable visible light activity, expanding their applicability in natural sunlight. TiO2 have achieved 57.1% reduction of Zn in calcareous soils with the dose of 3% of NM.133,140 ZnO, MnO2, and Al2O3 are used for adsorption and catalytic remediation of heavy metals, organic pollutants, and other emerging pollutants. 3% of SiO2 can reduce Cd by 56.1% in calcareous soil whereas, 1% of Al2O3 can reduce Cd by 38.3% in non-calcareous soils. Hollow mesoporous MnO2 promote soil aggregate formation which improves microbial community structure formation during the process of remediation.42,133 Several classes of nanomaterials act through multiple mechanisms at once in soil. nZVI and iron oxides (Fe0, Fe3O4) provide high surface area for adsorption/immobilization of metals and organics. They also act as strong redox reactivity for reductivity of chlorinated solvents, heavy metals, and other pollutants. Fe3O4 and other oxides generate ˙OH radicals by Fenton or photo-Fenton processes to degrade organics. Metal and metal oxides are widely used in nano-bioremediation and nano-phytoremediation to stimulate and support microbial/plant degradation.25,34,35 TiO2, ZnO, and Mn/Zn oxides adsorb organics and metals on oxide surfaces, undergoing oxidative degradation and redox catalysis. Classical UV/visible-driven photocatalysts can degrade dyes, pesticides, fertilizers, and other organics in soil/water. TiO2, ZnO, Mn/Zn oxides are also used in nano-bioremediation to pre-oxidize contaminants into more biodegradable forms.35,50

nZVI mechanisms in soil nanoremediation

nZVI is widely used for remediation of soil contaminated from heavy metals and organic pollutants due to strong reductant with a large reactive surface. Its performance depends on coupled processes such as Fe0 corrosion, electron transfer to contaminants, surface passivation, and modifications such as sulfidation, bimetallic doping, and support on carriers. nZVI reacts with H2O and O2, producing Fe2+/Fe3+ (oxides/hydroxides) and H2, corrosion drives reduction and causes surface passivation.148,149 H2 from corrosion fuels dehalogenation of microbes, causing significant enhancement in dechlorination of PCBs, TCE, and other organohalides.149 During electron transfer, contaminants accept electrons directly from Fe0 and FeSx shells/secondary Fe minerals, driving reduction of Cr(vi) to Cr(iii), nitrate, nitro-compounds, and chlorinated organics.149,150 nZVI exhibit high reductive dechlorination reactivity for chlorinated solvents and organochlorine pesticides. Process of removal is boosted by coupling with dechlorinating bacteria which used H2 and Fe(iii) as electron acceptors.151,152 Rate of passivation and composition depends on nZVI formulation, solution chemistry, pH, O2, and time. Some mixed-valence shells can adsorb and transform contaminants into their less toxic form.148 Polymer/surfactant coatings, biochar/bentonite supports, and emulsification cause reduction in aggregation, slow passivation, creating combined adsorption–reduction systems. For Cd-contaminated soil, S-nZVI converts >97.6% of exchangeable Cd to less available Fe/Mn-oxides, organic-bound forms and CdO, CdS are formed alongside FeOOH phases.153 S-nZVI can reduce 75 mg g−1 of Cr(vi) in to Cr(iii), by chemisorption and Cr–Fe coprecipitation, causing rise in pH and drop in redox potential during reaction.150 Biochar-supported S-nZVI (S-nZVI/BC) have exhibited a shift in mobile Cd to residual forms (+32.04% residual fraction), cutting DTPA-extractable Cd from 2.14 to 0.42 mg kg−1 and halves leachable Cd.152 Doping of nZVI with noble/transition metals (Pd, Ni, Cu) increases dechlorination rates and electron use efficacy, partly overcoming passivation and aggregation.149,151 Soil condition such as pH, redox potential, organic matter, and cation exchange capacity strongly impacts nZVI performance and metal specification; external amendments often increases pH, EC, and organic C, improving immobilization. nZVI and supported form enhances soil enzyme activities (dehydrogenase, urease, catalase) and microbial richness, supporting biodegradation and long-term stability.150,152,154

Carbon-based nanostructures in nanoremediation

Carbon-based nanomaterials such as, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene oxide (GO), and fullerenes (C60) are forefront of soil and water remediation due to their high surface area, tunable surface chemistry, and strong adsorption capabilities. Adsorption efficiencies indicate distinct adsorption profile in multi-walled CNTs (MWCNTs) and single-walled CNTs (SWCNTs) at 500 mg kg−1 Pb + 250 mg kg−1 Cd, 250 mg kg−1 Pb + 125 mg kg−1 Cd, and 125 mg kg−1 Pb + 62.5 mg kg−1 Cd in ash. For Pb(ii) removal, amine functionalized MWCNTs achieved 92.46% adsorption and for SWCNTs ranged from 6.53% to 79.31%, depending on preparation mechanism and surrounding conditions. For Cd(ii), MWCNTs have reached up to 67.92% and SWCNTs up to 61.32% adsorption.155 Acidified (H2O2 + HNO3 in ratio of 3 : 1) MWCNTs with a surface area of 194 m2 g−1 have removed Pb2+ (93%), Ni2+ (83%), Cu2+ (78%), and Cd2+ (15%) in 6 hours.156 Functionalization of such nanomaterials enhances hydrophobicity, selectivity, and reduces toxicity in environmental matrices.157 Some of the materials indicate good reusability such as, MWCNTs embedded with Fe–Pd–Fe3O4 have maintained 43% of the removal efficiency after 5 cycles of adsorption–desorption.158 Graphene Oxide (GO)-based materials have achieved up to 99% removal efficiency for Pd2+, Cd2+, and Hg2+ due to abundant oxygen-containing groups and higher surface area at pH 4 and high temperature. GO have outperformed CNTs and activated carbon in adsorption capacity.133 GO NPs immobilizes Cu, Pb, and Cd in soils at a dose as low as 0.2–5% but, could also immobilizes As and P, requiring careful application of such NMs for remediation.159 GO@Fe3O2 nanocomposites have reached a maximum adsorption capacity of 151.5 mg g−1 for Ni(ii).160 Metal decorated Fullerenes (C60) such as, Ga and In showed strong adsorption energies for organophosphate pollutants (up to −114 kJ mol−1), indicating high sensitivity and chemisorption strength.161 Cationic C60 derivatives also act as efficient photosensitizer for antimicrobial application, leveraging singlet oxygen generation.162 Pristine C60 at 5 mg kg−1 soil haven't impacted total microbial biomass but reduced fast growing bacterial population by 3–4×, which cause persistent but moderate shift in the microbial communities.163 Carbon nanotubes, graphene, and biochar-based nanocomposites exhibit very strong adsorption/filtration of metals, PAHs, pesticides via π–π and surface interactions. It also supports redox reactions such as electron shuttle or support for catalytic metals. Graphene/TiO2, sulfonated graphene-Ag-TiO2 exhibit enhanced photocatalytic degradation of dyes and organics. Biological agents function as nanocarriers for microbes/enzymes and act as amendment for enhancing plant growth and stress tolerance, forming core tools in nano-bioremediation and nano-phytoremediation.25,27,35,50,129

Polymer-based structure in remediation of contaminated sites

Dendrimers and hyperbranched polymers are advanced NMs which are highly branched and have 3-D architecture, abundant surface, and functional groups, making them powerful agent for soil and water remediation. Their internal cavities and modifiable surfaces enable efficient encapsulation and chelation of heavy metals and other contaminants, offering a high selectivity, capacity, and reusability. Dendrimers (PAMAM), are perfectly branched, monodispersed polymers which have terminal functional groups (amines, carboxylates) and strongly chelated metal ions. Hyperbranched polymers, which are less structurally perfect can be synthesized in a single step and are more cost-effective for long-scale applications.164–166 Removal of contaminants mainly occurred due to surface binding, chelation, and encapsulation within internal cavities for functionalized variation through specific interactions such as sulfur group for Hg2+.167,168 Functional groups like amines, amides, carboxylates, and sulfur enables strong and selective binding of heavy metals like Hg2+, Cu2+, Cr3+, Pb2+, and Cd2+.167,169–172 Many of these systems retain >90% capacities after multiple cycles, supporting cost-effective and sustainable remediation.49,172 Such hyperbranched polymers are favored for industrial use because of simpler and cheaper synthesis compared to perfect dendrimers (Table 3).164,166

Delivery vectors in nanoremediation

Nanobubbles are advanced classes of NMs, mainly used to enhance remediation processes and deliver or activate other agents, rather than acting as classical remediation agent. They have high surface area, long stability, and high gas-transfer efficiencies, improving air sparging, oxidation, and flotation in water, wastewater, and groundwater treatment plans.181–183 During flotation, nanobubbles increases collision/adhesion with fine particles, boosting removal of emulsified oil, amine precipitate, and other pollutants. Microemulsions have ultralow interfacial tension, high solubilization capacity, and droplet size typically <100 nm, allowing them to penetrate pores or detach hydrophobic contaminants. It also serves as nanoreactor for the synthesis of nanoparticle and as a nanoscale reaction media for catalyst-free phytodegradation of dyes.184 Molecular simulation indicate that microemulsions swell the oil phase, reduce polar contaminant fraction, and micelles dissociate at contaminant water interface, making desorption thermodynamically favorable.185 Ozone nanobubbles (ONB) enhances oxidation of organic and inorganic contaminants, while oil-in-water emulsions improve the delivery and reactivity of nZVI for contaminant reduction. Ozone nanobubbles (ONBs) significantly increases ozone solubility up to 1.7× and mass transfer up to 4.7× compared to nanobubbles, exhibiting higher oxidation rates for organic pollutants.182,186–188 The collapse of nanobubbles generate ROS, such as hydroxyl radicals, overcoming low mass transfer and limited reactive sites, reducing energy consumption which drive rapid degradation of contaminants.182,186,187 100% removal of methylene blue (10 mg L−1) and direct blue 71 (DB-71) (30 mg L−1) dyes have been observed in 16 minute under optimal ONB conditions.181–183 In field and lab studies, ONBs have achieved 85–100% removal of benzene and significant reduction in the concentration of toluene and naphthalene in 5 days.189–191 For Ammonia, ONBs have achieved 82.5% removal compared to 44.2% from microbubbles with a kinetic rate constant which is nearly four times higher.188,192 This method is effective for groundwater, sediment, and wastewater remediation which also includes highly saline and industrial effluents.181,183,192 Oil-in-water emulsions encapsulated by nZVI have improved stability, transport, and reactivity in low-permeability soils.193–195 Xanthan-stabilized emulsified nZVI achieved 8 times higher degradation of nitrobenzene than EZVI alone in 2-D tank tests.195 Field application of EZVI have led to >80% reduction of TCE from soil and 57–100% from groundwater in 90 days (Fig. 5).193,194

Synthesis, functionalization, and delivery of nanomaterials

Nanomaterials (NMs) synthesis for environmental application relies mainly on two strategies which is bottom-up and top-down approaches, along with growing emphasis on green synthesis using plant extract, microorganisms, and fungi for sustainability. In bottom-up approach, NMs are constructed from atomic and molecular precursors by chemical (e.g., sol–gel, hydrothermal, chemical vapor deposition) and biological methods (using biopolymers, bacteria, yeast, plant, fungi).196–198 This method offers a precise control over particle size and morphology, which is essential for catalysis and environmental remediation. This method is generally more energy efficient and can be tailored for large-scale and uniform production of NMs.196–201 In top-down approach bulk materials are reduced to nanoscale using physical/mechanical processes like ball milling, lithography, laser ablation, and grinding. Top-down method is mostly suitable for the production of large quantities of NMs, often in broader size distributions and require high energy consumption. Top-down methods are not suitable for the applications requiring precise control over NM characterization.196–201 Green bottom-up synthesis can reduce energy consumption by 30%, can cut cost by 40% and increase production output by 50% compared to conventional methods. Top-down methods are more energy-intensive and may introduce defects and contamination in the process of synthesis.196,198,200 Utilizing renewable biological sources such as plants, microbes, fungi, and agricultural waste as reducing and capping agents help in avoiding toxic chemicals.197,200–203 This process of nanomaterial synthesis can be achieved at 30% lower consumption of energy and 40% cost savings compared to conventional methods.200 This enables production of biocompatible, non-toxic, and eco-friendly NMs, suitable for environmental remediation, water purification, and antimicrobial actions.200,202,203 Plant-based synthesis is particularly scalable and efficient, with plant extracts reducing metal ions faster while producing more stable NMs than microbial method of synthesis (Fig. 6). Over 1814 commercial products use nano-sized materials, with dominance of 435 Ag-based NMs available globally. Asia-pacific leads the market of NM with a share of 34%, followed by North America (31%) and Europe (30%).203 Most of the green-synthesized NMs are dominantly used in water and soil purification, soil conditioning, nanofertilization, pest control, and food quality enhancement.201

Surface functionalization is essential for improving NM stability, targeted specificity, and reduces non-targeted toxicity in environmental matrices and biomedical applications. Surface modification with polymer like CMC and PAA prevent aggregation and increases colloidal stability. CMC/PAA hydrogels have exhibited strong, single-ion affinity and multi-ion absorbability, following Langmuir adsorption model, with high reusability for the removal of heavy metals.204–206 Functional group such as thiols, carboxyls and ligands such as folic acid, biotin increases specificity for contaminants. Delivering NMs into subsurface environment is critical in application such as groundwater, soil matrices remediation, but heterogeneity including clay content, organic matter composition, and variability in pH conditions cause NM filtration, aggregation, and reduction in the mobility. Composition (clay, organic matter) and texture of soil significantly reduce NM mobility. For example, TiO2 NPs showed <5% mass recovery in all tested soils due to strong aggregation, while CuO NPs were more mobile, with retention rate >86% but some transport was observed regardless of soil type.237–240 Clay minerals such as kaolinite can decrease NM mobility up to 51% compared to illite (77% mobility under similar conditions), mainly because of smaller pore throats and more complex flow paths.237,238 Lower pH and higher ionic strength increase aggregation and retention limiting the transport of NMs.241–243 Surface coating such as polyvinylpyrrolidone, citrate, and humic acids can increase transport of NM by blocking retention site and stabilizing suspension. For AgNPs, >73.9% of retention was observed without coatings, but mobility increases with the modifications on surface of NM.244,245 Natural colloids such as bentonite clay can either enhance or reduce NM transport depending on the stability and environmental conditions. For CuO NPs, retention increases from 70.4% to 91.7% with bentonite clay at 10 mM NaCl.240 Shear-thinning gels such as guar gum, xanthan gum improves delivery of NMs. For example, 1.75 g L−1 biopolymer gel enables a 20 g L−1 Fe NPs suspension to reach 73 cm radius of influence in a sand aquifer at <0.4 bar injection pressure, which help in achieving homogeneous distribution.246–248 Polymeric solutions of 1.5–7 g L−1 can stabilize high concentration NM suspension preventing aggregation, and allow for a controlled and low-pressure injection.248

Application strategies and field-scale implementation

In situ injection and permeable reactive barriers (PRBs)

Field-scale implementation of NM-based remediation strategies, such as in situ injection and nano-enhanced PRBs are advancing as a cost-effective and sustainable approach for treating contaminated matrices. Direct injection of NM suspensions, such as iron oxide NPs, into contaminated aquifers has been demonstrated at field scale. For example, at a brownfield site in Spain, 1500 kg of goethite NPs (average diameter 461 ± 266 nm) injected at a low pressure of <0.6 bar through nine wells, achieving a radius of influence 2.5 meter per well and formed a 22 × 3 × 9 m in situ barrier. This led to an immediate and sustained reduction in the concentration of Zn, Pb, and Ni over 189-days of monitoring at pH 4–6 (acidic).249 Similarly, field-scale As remediation using 280 kg of iron oxide NPs reduces groundwater As by 50% with a stable barrier formation without downstream NP migration.250 Nano-enhanced PRBs are constructed as subsurface walls containing reactive NMs which intercept and treat contaminated plumes. Field and pilot studies have indicated that such barriers can immobilize and degrade a wide range of contaminants. It includes heavy metals, chlorinated solvents, nitrates, and POPs, with removal efficiencies often exceeding 90% for targeted pollutants.249–251 The choice of NM (nZVI, iron oxide, graphene nanofibers) and barrier design (continuous trench, funnel-and-gate) are tailored for site-specific hydrogeology and profile of contamination.252–255 Successful field implementations require careful selection of NMs (colloidal stability, reactivity, toxicity), delivery method (low-pressure injection, direct push), and modelling for transportation of contaminant. Longevity of NM gets impacted by aggregation, barrier clogging, and site geochemistry. Ecotoxicity and microbial impacts are poorly understood, especially for in situ soil, groundwater and, concerns include toxicity to microorganisms along with disruption of ecosystem function. Once NMs injected into soil, recovery and separation of NPs is very difficult, contaminants may later desorb from nanoadsorbents, raising long-term risk questions.27In situ treatment in soil and groundwater create challenge of transport through porous media, controlling distribution, long-term fate, and ecosystem safety.249,250 In nanoremediation, nanoparticles act as sorbent and reactive carrier for contaminants. Their aggregation, mobility, and surface chemistry are strongly controlled by ionic strength, and NOM governs how well they can reach, bind, and release pollutants. Increase of ionic strength compresses electric double layer, weakens electrostatic repulsion, promote aggregation, and reduces mobility as observed in AgNPs, nano-SiO2, bichar NPs, nanoplastics, and soil NPs.256 Ca2+ is more effective than Na+ at inducing aggregation and retention due to charge screening at cation-bridging which can move systems from reversible secondary-minimum retention to irreversible primary-minimum deposition.256,257 Soil-specific transport is observed as higher ionic strength strongly inhibit polystyrene NPs and biochar NPs in soil, mobility is greatest at low ionic strength, high pH, and low Fe/Al oxides. Surfactants, chelating agents, or changes in pH/ionic strength can desorb pollutants from nanoparticle surfaces, allowing the pollutants to be extracted in solution. The nanoparticles themselves may be recovered or left in place.256 Adsorbed NOM (humic/fulvic substances) increases negative surface charge and add a steric layer, raising critical coagulation concentration, and stabilizes soil NPs, ferrihydrite NPs, AuNPs, biochar NPs, and nano-SiO2 suspensions.256–260 High-molecular-weight NOM creates thicker adsorbed layer, stronger steric repulsion and low-molecular-weight fraction which can destabilize positively charged NPs via patch-charge attraction.259 In soil, NOM adsorption can override original capping agents such as citrate, PVP, PEG and make particle behavior more similar across coatings, promoting rapid hetero-aggregation with soil colloids. Stable nano-SiO2, biochar NPs, and ferrihydrite NPs sorbs metals and organics, enhancing their movement. NOM-stabilized NPs and MPs, indicate higher breakthrough and can act as mobile contaminant carriers. NOM quality and ionic strength are determined on the basis of NP it coats and thus which particle become key sorbents or remain mobile at low ionic strength and mainly Na+, NOM stabilizes NPs by electrostatic repulsion. Ca2+ and carboxyl-rich NOM can bridge flocculation and aggregation, decreasing mobility and potentially trapping contaminants.32,256–260

Ex situ nanoremediation for field-scale implementation

Ex situ nanoremediation involves treatment of excavated contaminated soil in controlled environment such as, reactors and soil piles, which offer precise process control at higher cost and energy demand. Application of nZVI to contaminated soils has demonstrated significant immobilization of heavy metals. In a study, using 5% nZVI (w/w) in Zn, Pb, and Cd contaminated soil, Pb immobilization reached 20% within 120 days while, Zn and Cd immobilization were 8% and negligible. The maximum impact of remediation was observed within 15 days, but the effectiveness decreased over time, highlighting the need of careful monitoring for long-term outcome. Swift is observed in microbial communities, with an initial increase in metabolically active bacteria without any significant recovery in overall biodiversity.233 Application of graphene oxide nanoparticles (nGOx) in ex situ conditions have effectively immobilized Cu, Pb, and Cd in polluted soils, but mobilizes As and P, which are present in low doses. Dual-effect suggests that nGOx can tailor specific contaminant profile and also requires careful selection based on site-specific mixture of contaminants. Soil pH and electrical conductivity (EC) were slightly affected, supporting the feasibility of ex situ use in controlled environmental conditions.159 Combining NMs with other techniques of remediation can enhance overall effectiveness. nZVI and nGOx have been used together for immobilization of heavy metals while supporting plant-based remediation, leveraging the strength of both approaches in ex situ reactors.25,159 Most of the nanoremediation research remains at laboratory and pilot-scale yet, ex situ applications are also emerging. For example, pilot studies have validated that upscaling of laboratory findings such as immobilization of heavy metals using goethite NPs in field barriers, with in situ adsorption capacities of 23 mg Zn/g and efficient immobilization for 90 days.261Ex situ offer better control and easier recovery such as magnetic separation and membranes but requires excavation/pumping and still faces fouling and regeneration issue. For magnetic or magnetically recoverable nanoparticles (e.g., Fe3O4 or nZVI coated with a magnetic shell), an external magnetic field can be applied to collect the nanoparticles along with the sorbed pollutants. This is particularly feasible for ex situ treatment or for slurry-phase reactors (Table 5).

Nano-phytoremediation combined system

Integrating NMs with hyperaccumulator plant offers a promising strategy for field-scale remediation of contaminated soil and water. TiO2 NPs combined with Brassica juncea increased Cd removal from soil by 55.11% at 500 mg kg−1 TiO2 NP, compared to 32.46% without NPs, with enhanced photosynthetic activity and plant growth.269,270 Sulfur-doped carbon nanodots with Tagetes patula have achieved 53.2% removal of Cd, which is 20.1% higher than plants alone and regulation of Cd migration significantly improved plant physiology.132 Multi walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) at 500 mg kg−1 have improved biomass of Solanum nigrum (27.97%) and increases accumulation of Cd and As by 18.29% and 32.47%.120 Functional carbon nanodots (FCNs) integration with Eichhornia crassipes (water hyacinth) enhances removal of heavy metals and improved plant tolerance, outperforming conventional nanomaterials.271 Molybdenum trioxide NPs (MoO3 NPs) at 4 mg kg−1 have shown Cd accumulation in Solanum nigrum by activating nutrient assimilation and antioxidant pathways.270,272 Biogenic/green-synthesized NPs from plants, fungi, and bacteria combines adsorption, catalysis, and close integration with microbial and plant system enables nano-bioremediation where, NMs pre-transform pollutants and, microbes complete biodegradation.50

Smart nanosensors for monitoring and control

NM-enabled sensors can detect contaminants (pesticides, heavy metals, pathogens) at nanomolar to sub-picomolar levels, allowing field-deployment and real-time monitoring.273–275 DNA-based nanosensors offer direct and sensitive detection of pollutants in water, soil, and crop matrices, with electrochemical, optical, and colorimetric formats, enabling real-time, in situ analysis.276 Graphene-based immunosensors detects Pb ions in water at 0.01 ppb whereas, Au NP sensors detect Hg at 0.005 ppb.273 Machine learning (ML) optimized TiO2 NPs with Ceratophyllum demersum have achieved 99.58% Cd adsorption, reducing residual Cd to 0.0199 mg L−1.117 Nanozyme-based sensors can provide a robust, reusable, and field-ready pollutant detection and remediation along with high stability in variable environmental conditions.275 Most nanosensors have recognition element (aptamer, antibody, enzymes, microbial cell, functional ligand) which binds with target, nanomaterial transducer (metal NPs, carbon NMs, quantum dots, MOFs, nanozymes), and a signal-readout system (electrochemical and optical). Binding or reacting with the contaminant result in change of current, voltage, color, fluorescence, and Raman signal, which is correlated to concentration, often down to nano or picomolar level.277–280 Nanomaterial-modified screen-printed electrodes with anodic stripping voltammetry enables a portable trace-metal analysis in soils. Graphene-based and AuNP-based biosensors detect Pb2+ and Hg2+ in water at 0.01–0.005 ppb, illustrating the achievable sensitivity for environmental matrices.273,278,281 Silica-coated magnetic NPs along with quantum dots can detect Hg2+ at 1 nM and then magnetically remove it, combining sensing and remediating.278 Optical nanoparticle sensors such as Au, Ag, TiO2, QDs, CNTs uses color change, fluorescence and SERS to report pesticide residue where limit of detection are typically far below conventional chromatography methods.280 LSPR-based color change and fluorescence quenching/enhancement allow visual and instrumental quantification of insecticides, herbicides and fungicides directly from soil extracts.280,282 A Cu-nanocluster fluorescent array has distinguished 9 metals and 5 pesticides in 10 minutes, with ∼0.5 nM LOD for metals and ∼7.1 ppb for pesticides, validated in real soils and other complex samples.35 Nanomaterial-based biosensors such as graphene immunosensors and nanozyme-based systems detect pathogens and other biological agents with high specificity, supporting environmental and agroecosystem monitoring. Microbial nanosensors use living cells coupled with nanomaterials for reporting toxicants and soil pollutants at very low concentration in situ. Nanozymes can mimic natural enzymes which can sense and remediate pollutants. Their catalytic activity generates colorimetric and electrochemical signals for heavy metals, pesticides, and degradation of persistent organics (pesticides, herbicides, dyes, antibiotics) (Fig. 7).273,277–282

Environmental implications, risk assessment, and life-cycle analysis

Nanoremediation remove and immobilize diverse contaminants in soil with high efficiency, often in situ and at relatively low cost, using nZVI, metal oxides, carbon nanomaterial and nano-bio approaches. The rapid expansion of nanotechnology has led to the increased release of NMs into environmental matrices, raising concerns about their dual-edge effects of potential benefits and significant ecological risks. High nanoparticle doses often used in lab are impractical and potentially toxic to soil. NMs at high concentration, disrupt soil microbial communities, alter enzyme activities, and affect nutrient cycling, leading to reduced diversity of microbe population.283–286 Invertebrates, such as earthworms and nematodes, show avoidance behavior and biochemical stress at higher NM concentrations though, reproductive effect are less consistent.287–289 Plant may experience inhibition on growth and germination, with certain dicotyledonous species such as lettuce and tomato being particularly sensitive to certain NMs such as TiSiO4, Co3O4 at concentrations above 200 mg kg−1 in soil.287,288 Reported effects also include inhibition of soil microbial enzymes, reduced seed germination, root/shoot growth, and possible organ damage along with genetic defect at high doses.144 Certain NMs can also stimulate plant growth regulators concentration, exhibiting hormone-based response in plant.290 Toxicity mechanisms include oxidative stress, membrane damage, metal ion release, and physical interactions such as aggregation and surface adsorption.283–285,291,292 Key factors which modulate toxicity are, NM composition, size, surface coating, and soil characteristics such as, soil pH, organic matter, and co-contaminants.284–286,292 Smaller NMs with higher reactive surface area are more toxic and soil organic matter can mitigate effects by binding with NMs.285 NMs exhibit long-term persistence due to their stability and resistance to degradation. Once released, NMs undergo dynamic transformations such as oxidation, sulfidation, dissolution, and aggregation, causing significant alteration in mobility and toxicity of NMs in environmental matrices.293–296 In iron oxide NPs, adsorption of solutes, aggregation, and aging markedly change behavior and, toxicity differs between pristine vs. aged particles and between single vs. co-exposed pollutants. Silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) can transform into less toxic silver sulfide in sediments and TiO2 NMs can aggregate, reducing their bioavailability. Overall terrestrial risk of Ag NPs may be low because only a small fraction reaches soil, nano-specific properties diminish rapidly, and bioaccumulate in edible plant part is minor.293,294,296 Environmental factors such as pH, organic matter, and ionic strength significantly influence such processes. It can leach into groundwater, when they remain dispersed and stabilized by coatings, raising concerns about long term contamination of water resources.34,297 Models such as multimedia compartmental models along with spatial river/watershed models are being used for the prediction of NM distribution but still lack consideration on transformation processes such as sulfidation and photoreaction. Redox and sulfidation of metallic nanoparticles (nZVI, metal sulfide) and partial dissolution of metal/metal oxide NPs can detoxify and enhance toxicity, depending on conditions. Aggregation tends to reduce mobility and change interaction with biota. Surface oxidation, interaction with organic matter along with coating changes cause attenuation, and in some cases, prolonged toxicity effects. Polymer or carbon-based coatings significantly improve performance and reduce acute toxicity but add complexity to degradation pathways and long-term fate of NMs.294,297

There is a critical need for standardized protocol for assessment of NM risks, as current framework lacks detailed decision criteria and harmonized testing model. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and projects like MACRAMÉ emphasizes on physicochemical characterization followed by exposure and hazard assessment. The Problem–Complexity–Solution (PCS) Framework addresses NM application by systematic identification of NM with their transformative and exposure roots. Standardized procedures (injection methods, monitoring, regeneration/reuse) are largely missing, making it hard to design reliable in situ and ex situ operations. Existing toxicity tests and monitoring tools are not well suited to track nanoparticle transformation, persistence, and exposure in real environmental matrices.25,27,142 This review stress that current knowledge is dominated by short-term lab studies and field-scale data on long-term fate in heterogenous soil, repeated applications, and end-of-life fate are scarce. At the same time, long-term fate, transformation, and regulation of such materials are only partially understood, so the risk assessment and governance are lagging behind in the technical development.293,294,296 Available studies on LCA of NMs provide only limited, indirect statistics. Bibliometric data of general LAC publications indicate that USA and China are the most productive countries overall, with USA leading in total citations. In the last 3 years of datasets, China produced 10.85% of LCA papers, exceeding USA (9.52%) and Italy (6.99%), with strong contributions from other East Asian, North American and Western European countries.298 A review of 71 LCA studies on nanomaterials from (2001–2020) have indicated that about 95% are conducted in Europe (36) and North America (28), based on first-author affiliations. Economical, regulatory, and social consideration

Nanoremediation offers rapid, efficient pollutant removal with the potential for sustainable land restoration, yet field scale adaptation depends mainly on economic viability, regulatory clarity, and public acceptance. Nanoremediation is generally less costly and more effective than traditional methods such as thermal treatment and pump-and-treat for in situ applications. High surface area for reactivity of NMs allows for lower quantities and faster remediation yet, cost of production remains significant for engineering and coating of NMs.25,26,50,300In situ deployment reduces the need of transport and excavation, lowering labor and transport expenses.25,50,300 Such NMs can remove a wide range of pollutants such as heavy metals, hydrocarbons, and POPs with removal rate often exceeding 90%, leading to a faster restoration of site and restores land value. Most of the NM-based studies are focused on lab and pilot-based experiments. Field-scale demonstration is needed to validate cost-effectiveness and long-term benefits of the NMs.50 The US EPA and EU REACH have begun to address NM-specific risks still, standardized protocol and guidance are lacking. Absence of harmonized global standard along with uncertainties in hazard exposure assessment and slow adaptation of existing chemical regulations are major policy gaps. Public acceptance is still lacking due to nano stigma concerns about unknown risks and environmental fate. Transparent communication, public agreement, demonstration of eco-friendly, biodegradable NMs are essential for building trust and acceptance in common population.301–303 There are no widely accepted global guidelines for production, use, and disposal of NMs in remediation, such regulatory gap slows commercialization and public acceptance (Fig. 9).27,142,300

Future perspective and conclusion

Soil pollution is a pervasive, data-backed global threat, which undermine ecosystem health, food security, water quality, and human well-being. Nanoremediation is economically promising and can outperform conventional remediation methods, but the widespread adaptation requires cost reduction, robust field data, clear regulations, and proactive public engagement for overcoming skepticism to ensure safe and sustainable use. Soil heterogeneity is still a barrier for effective delivery of NM into subsurface, but data indicate that subsurface modification and shear-thinning fluids can enhance distribution and mobility of NPs. Heterojunctions and composite NMs enhances performance, making such approaches highly promising for the purpose of environmental remediation. Batch-to-batch variability and incomplete understanding of biosynthesis mechanism cause limitation on large-scale uniform NM production. Metal-oxide photocatalysts (TiO2, ZnO, Mn/Zn oxides), carbon NMs, and biogenic nano-hybrids are classes which simultaneously contribute in physical adsorption, chemical redox/immobilization, light-driven photocatalysis, and biologically-assisted remediation. In soil systems, integration of 8 g kg−1 of nZVI@BC have achieved 100% immobilization of Cr(vi) after 15 days, and modification of nZVI had ∼6800-fold greater surface area and dramatic increase in TCE reduction rate. Fe–Pd NPs achieved highest reactivity (∼6800-fold increase), whereas Fe–Ag provided only ∼8-fold increase, underscoring that noble coupling profoundly and significantly contribute in influencing catalytic dechlorination efficiency.

Research is needed for the continuous, robust, and scalable mechanisms for green synthesis. Successful field implementation requires very careful selection of NM (colloidal stability, reactivity, type of contamination), delivery method (low-pressure injection, direct push), and modelling of contaminant transport. Longevity can be impacted by NM aggregation, clogging of barrier, and geochemistry of site. Long-term research work and studies have reported sustained performance for months to year, effective for 2–3 and sometimes 5 years yet, acid rain, flooding, and soil pH/redox changes lead to reduce stability of contaminants and need site-specific modelling and monitoring. Co-ordinated global action and integrated monitoring are essential for mitigating these risks and protect future generations from such risks. Conventional remediation techniques are often constrained by high cost ($1500 per m3 or $100 per m2), disrupted environment, slow rate and other technical challenges for complex contaminants. Data-driven research has confirmed that bottom-up, especially green synthesis using plant extract, microbe, and fungi, offers significant environmental and economic advantage over traditional conventional methods. Surface functionalization using polymer, surfactants, and thiol groups cause significant improvement in stability, specificity, and safety of nanomaterial. The key conclusion is that, nanoremediation transition from lab concept to sustainable field-scale solutions is possible only when green synthesis, biological coupling, and regulatory considerations are holistically addressed.
