Noncontact injury and acute knee pain in an adolescent
Authors: Jiebo Chen, Jinzhong Zhao, Caiqi Xu
Published: 2026

A 16-year-old adolescent boy presented to the emergency department after a noncontact injury to his right leg during a basketball game. He reported landing with excessive internal rotation in knee flexion, accompanied by a sudden pop, with pain and instability when moving his knee. On examination, his knee was swollen with lateral tenderness and limited range of motion. Radiographs showed an avulsion fracture of the lateral tibial plateau, identified as a Segond fracture (Figure 1). In follow-up with a sports medicine specialist, the patient had positive anterior drawer, Lachman, and high-grade pivot shift tests. Magnetic resonance imaging (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251840/tab-related-content) confirmed a ruptured anterior cruciate ligament. Given the patient’s age, level of activity, and desire to return to pivoting sports, we performed anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. He underwent postoperative rehabilitation following standard principles to restore motion, strengthen muscles, reduce pain and swelling, and prevent re-injury. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient had started noncontact activities in preparation for returning to play.

Radiographs of the right knee of a 16-year-old boy showing (A) the anteroposterior view and (B) the lateral view. The anteroposterior view shows an avulsion bony fragment at the proximolateral tibia (white circle).

Segond fracture is a small bony avulsion of the proximolateral tibia at the anterolateral ligament or complex insertion.1 Notably, when a young patient with acute knee pain visits a health care provider, but swelling and pain prevent performing specialized physical tests and the injury pattern is not clearly remembered, the detection of Segond fracture on initial plain radiographs is a strong indicator of ACL rupture.2 Segond fracture has concomitant ACL rupture in 75% to 100% of cases. Segond fracture repair is not conventionally performed, as no evidence shows that an unrepaired Segond fracture has any negative effect on clinical outcomes after ACL reconstruction.3 In this case, the patient’s history, specialized physical tests, and imaging findings, including Segond fracture, strongly indicated ACL rupture. Anterior cruciate ligament rupture may be treated with rehabilitation as a primary option, but reconstruction is recommended for young, active patients and for those with persistent instability in their desired activity.4
