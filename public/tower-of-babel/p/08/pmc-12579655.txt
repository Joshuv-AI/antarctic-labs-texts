Fatigue Experience in Oncology; a Targeted Qualitative Literature Review and Novel Patient-Centric Conceptual Model
Authors: Chloe Carmichael, Cecile Gousset, Danielle Burns, Jordan Miller, Sophie Van Tomme, Helen Kitchen, Harriet Makin, Natalie Aldhouse, Paul Cordero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTraditionally, treatment endpoints in oncology have focused on increasing overall survival and progression-free survival. Although fatigue may be a meaningful outcome measure across different cancers, it is assessed less frequently. To explore the impact of fatigue on patients with cancer, confirm the importance of fatigue measurement and inform future measurement strategies in oncology, we aimed to characterise the patient experience of fatigue and the associated impact on how patients feel and function, including aspects of health-related quality of life (HRQoL).MethodsA targeted literature review of published fatigue-related qualitative publications in oncology was conducted in MEDLINE, Embase and PsycINFO, limited to studies published between 2018 and 2023. Concepts were extracted from the qualitative literature and patient quotes or author descriptions/interpretations were subject to secondary analysis using semantic, qualitative, directed content analysis techniques via ATLAS.ti v9. The literature review informed the development of a preliminary patient-centric conceptual model of fatigue in oncology.ResultsOf 1210 identified publications, 26 were selected for data extraction, including 512 patients living with 28 different oncology indications across geographies. Frequently reported fatigue triggers included treatments and cancer itself. Patients used various fatigue descriptors, including low/lack/loss of energy, tiredness and exhaustion, across disease stages. Physiological and cognitive manifestations of fatigue were reported, and fatigue episodes were described as unpredictable and of variable severity, duration and frequency. Cancer-related fatigue had a substantial negative effect on patients’ QoL, including emotional, physical, physiological, social, activities of daily living, sleep and work impacts. Unmet needs included poor support from healthcare professionals (HCPs).ConclusionsThis review demonstrated that fatigue is an impactful symptom across oncology indications and provides the first holistic conceptual model of cancer-related fatigue across disease stages and treatments. Future research should review clinical outcome assessments (COA) that may be fit-for-purpose for measuring fatigue in oncology clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03330-4.

Introduction

Fatigue is one of the most common symptoms experienced by patients with cancer, with an overall prevalence of 49% [1], and is also associated with several cancer treatments [2] in addition to the diagnosed disease. Cancer-related fatigue is defined as a distressing, persistent, subjective sense of physical, emotional and/or cognitive tiredness or exhaustion related to cancer or cancer treatment that is not proportional to recent activity and that interferes with usual functioning [3, 4]. Moreover, cancer-related fatigue is less likely to be relieved by rest than fatigue experienced by healthy individuals [3, 4]. Fatigue at cancer diagnosis [5, 6] and during survivorship [7] is associated with increased mortality. Although guidelines recommend screening for and continued management of cancer-related fatigue from diagnosis through to follow-up [4], fatigue is under-diagnosed and under-treated [3, 4, 8]. Studies report that approximately 60% of patients, 2 years after diagnosis, did not feel well informed about fatigue. Approximately 40% had never been asked about being exhausted by their treating physician [9], while up to 50% of survivors describe unmet needs in relation to discussing cancer-related fatigue, receiving advice or receiving help [10–12].

Fatigue is typically considered to be best measured through patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures, as it is a symptom that only patients can feel and report on. However, a complexity in measuring fatigue is that it is often experienced alongside and moderated by other cancer-related symptoms, including pain, sleep disturbance and depression. For example, there may be a relationship between fatigue and depression [13] and fatigue and physical function [14]. Conceptualising the patient experience, typically through the development of a conceptual model, is a key initial step in identifying PROs to measure fatigue. While indication-specific conceptual models of fatigue provide a focused understanding of the experience of fatigue in particular oncology indications, they do not holistically represent fatigue across oncology indications, throughout the course of the disease, and evaluate how cancer-related fatigue can be associated with health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and functional impacts [15, 16].

The primary aim of this targeted qualitative literature review was to characterise the patient experience of cancer-related fatigue by identifying how patients describe fatigue in the context of oncology, including emotional, cognitive and physical fatigue, whether fatigue varies according to the treatment stage, and the impact of cancer-related fatigue on how patients feel and function.

Data Sources, Search Strategy and Quality Assessment

Relevant articles (full publications or conference abstracts) indexed on MEDLINE®, Embase and PsycINFO were identified using database search strings. Three search strings were developed to account for differences in syntax within each database and combined terms for “cancer” AND “fatigue” AND “qualitative research studies” OR “conceptual models”. Supplementary hand-searching of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) websites, and the reference lists of any included studies identified from database searches, was conducted.

Electronic database searching was conducted on 21 August 2023. A bespoke Microsoft Access database, with all identified records uploaded, was used to manage citation screening. Once duplicates had been identified and removed, studies were initially screened by their titles and abstracts (first pass) by two authors. Potentially relevant studies were obtained in full and reviewed (second pass) by two authors. Any discrepancies in decisions were resolved through discussion with the wider team of authors. The reasons for exclusions at first and second pass (in accordance with the eligibility criteria presented in Table 1) were summarised in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flowchart (Fig. 1). Data Extraction and Analysis

Data extraction and analysis of patient quotes or author descriptions/interpretations was conducted by two authors trained in qualitative research techniques. Extracted data underwent secondary analysis using semantic, qualitative, directed content analysis techniques [18, 19]. The analysis followed an experiential, realist approach, which focused on participants’ individual perspectives and experiences.

Qualitative directed content analysis, consisting of a five-step process, was applied to all included data [20]: immersion in the data, coding, iterative review of codes, defining and refining concepts and domains and reporting. Coding of extracted data was facilitated by the qualitative analysis software, ATLAS.ti version 9. Extracted data were then synthesised to develop a conceptual model to visually depict the patient experience of cancer-related fatigue.

Ethics Compliance

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Findings

Study Selection

The electronic search yielded 1210 articles. Duplicates were removed (n = 368), and records were screened by title and abstract (first pass, n = 842). Records were removed if they did not meet the eligibility criteria (n = 679). The full-text publications of the remaining records were reviewed (second pass, n = 163), resulting in 142 publications which appeared eligible for data extraction (n = 40 excluded, n = 19 identified via supplementary hand-searching from FDA/EMA webpages and references from included studies).

A study selection meeting was held with all authors to select the most relevant publications for development of the conceptual model. Publications were initially ordered on the volume of extractable qualitative data (e.g. pages, paragraphs or sentences of direct quotes or author interpretations). To avoid the selection, and subsequent overrepresentation, of specific cancer indications that have been researched more intensively than others, consideration was given to a range of cancer types across oncology (e.g. solid tumours, blood cancers, etc.) and varied representation of cancer journey stages. Any qualitative publications containing a conceptual model of fatigue in oncology were also recommended for inclusion. The 26 most relevant publications were retained for data extraction (Fig. 1).

Study Characteristics

In total, 26 publications reporting on the experiences of 512 patients with cancer-related fatigue across 28 different oncology indications were included in this review. A wide range of oncology indications were identified, most frequently breast cancer [21–25], lung cancer [22, 26–29] and skin cancer [15, 16, 30, 31], with five studies including a mixed population of cancer indications [21, 22, 27, 32, 33] (Fig. 2). Across the included studies, sample size (range: 7–37 patients) and mean patient age (mean range: 53–76 years) varied; 51% of patients were female (n = 261/512) and 49% were male (n = 251/512). Most studies were conducted in the USA [16, 28, 31, 33–39] or Europe [21, 23, 27, 40] and utilised semi-structured interviews (n = 22) as a method of data collection. A variety of analytic approaches were used, most often thematic analysis (n = 11) [15, 22, 24, 27, 31–33, 36, 39, 41, 42] and content analysis (n = 10) [16, 23, 25, 26, 29, 32, 35, 37, 38, 40]. Fatigue Triggers

The most commonly reported trigger for cancer-related fatigue was treatment (or the absence of certain treatment, e.g. blood transfusions), including venesections, immunotherapy and novel hormone therapy [15, 22, 25–28, 32–34, 37–44]. Cancer itself was explicitly acknowledged as triggering fatigue [15, 22, 26, 32, 33], along with certain cancer symptoms or side effects of treatment, such as pain [41], dyspnea [27], diarrhoea, acid reflux, dry mouth and infections [38].

Studies reported that older age [25, 26, 38, 41, 44] and increased distress [22, 29, 41] or uncertainty [22] could contribute to cancer-related fatigue. Fatigue was also attributed to exertion/activity [24, 25, 32, 39, 41], travel/commuting [41] and the level of rest patients received, with a lack of rest/sleep [22, 44], too much rest [28] or altered body clock [41] triggering fatigue. Patient diet [29, 41], appetite [22, 27, 29] and blood parameters [41] further impacted fatigue, as did ambient temperature (Table S2) [41].

Fatigue Descriptors

As the studies included in this review represented diverse experiences, fatigue descriptors were stratified into temporal stages according to whether the fatigue was experienced while living with cancer or during active treatment, post-treatment or long-term in cancer survivors (up to 2 years after completion of primary treatment). General descriptions of fatigue, such as low/lack/loss of energy, tiredness and exhaustion, were reported across all three temporal stages related to treatment. Cognitive or mental fatigue was described by patients living with cancer or during active treatment who had haematological malignancies [35, 41]; fatigue led to brain fog and difficulty with focus, concentration or paying attention [26, 35, 41] finding words and memory [41], leaving patients prone to errors or feeling spacey and jetlagged [41]. Severe fatigue experiences, such as overwhelming or crippling/crushing fatigue, were most frequently reported in patients living with cancer or during active treatment and post-treatment, with long-term fatigue in cancer survivors described as invisible to others (Table S3).

The onset of fatigue was reported as unpredictable and/or unexpected across all temporal treatment stages [25, 27, 32, 34], and was described as “sudden” [25, 41]. However, in patients who were living with cancer or receiving treatment for their cancer, fatigue would begin after periods of rest or sleep [16, 36, 41, 44] (Table S4).

The frequency and duration of cancer-related fatigue episodes varied across temporal stages. Some patients living with cancer or receiving treatment [22, 31, 33, 39, 41, 43] and post-treatment [25] reported that fatigue occurred persistently across each day, while other patients who were living with cancer or receiving treatment reported that fatigue fluctuated [27, 28, 37, 38, 41, 44]. Similarly, fatigue episodes were also described as intermittent, or as occurring in waves, by patients who were post-treatment [25] and survivors [32] (Table S5).

Fatigue was often described as chronic by patients who were living with cancer [33, 41–43] and survivors [21, 40]. However, other publications reporting on patients living with cancer or receiving treatment, or post-treatment, described more acute durations of fatigue, limited to days [25, 38, 42], weeks [26, 38], hours [25, 41] or months [41] (Table S6).

Fatigue was commonly described as either severe or highly bothersome [21, 24, 25, 33, 34, 39, 41–43], debilitating [34, 43], extreme [21, 25] or uncontrollable [25, 26, 32]. Publications reported that fatigue was not relieved with rest [22, 25, 28] or treatment [22, 25, 40] and worsened/built during the day [21, 26, 29, 41, 44] or varied in severity [16, 21, 25, 31, 32]. However, a number of papers reported that fatigue was tolerable [28, 29, 31, 38, 39, 43, 44] or eventually improved following treatment or for other unspecified reasons [25, 27, 38] (Table S7).

Impacts

Physiological

The most frequently reported physiological consequence of cancer-related fatigue was physical weakness, including when standing and carrying items [16, 21, 22, 25, 26, 31, 33–35, 38–42], and low stamina in relation to physical activities [16, 30, 39–42, 44]. Patients experienced shortness of breath (dyspnoea) [21, 26, 27, 34], lethargy [30, 39], pain [23], dizziness and clumsiness due to their fatigue [41], along with a reduction in, or absence of, appetite [26, 27, 29, 44] (Table S8).

Physical

The most frequently reported physical impact of cancer-related fatigue was a need to rest and/or sleep both post-exertion and to recover after completing activities [16, 21, 24–29, 32–36, 38, 39, 41–44]. Getting out of the house was challenging because of fatigue, with some patients reporting that it was easier to stay at home [24, 29, 30, 36, 39, 41, 44].

Patients reported limited movement or an inability to move at all [16, 25, 26, 32, 35, 39, 41, 43, 44] and described difficulty with transitional movements [22, 24, 25, 33], climbing stairs [21, 33, 36, 39], standing, even briefly [25, 31, 33] and getting out of bed, with some patients essentially bedbound [30, 37–39]. Patients also experienced psychomotor slowing [26, 38, 42]. For some patients, fatigue was so severe that they were unable to talk [25, 26].

Cancer-related fatigue also impacted patients’ ability to engage in physical activity, including unspecified exercise [24, 26, 32, 34, 35, 38, 41, 42], any form of physical activity (described more generally) [16, 27, 28, 33, 35, 37], light/moderate activity [30, 39], sport/vigorous activity [22, 26, 33, 39, 41], walking [16, 21, 26, 33, 34, 39, 42, 44] and cycling [33, 34, 39] (Table S9).

Emotional

Cancer-related fatigue frequently led to apathy, with patients describing a lack of motivation and an inability to enjoy activities that they had previously liked [16, 21, 26–30, 32, 34, 35, 41, 44]. Sadness/depression [24–26, 29, 30, 32, 34, 36, 41, 44], distress [22, 25, 26, 29, 32, 38, 41] and hopelessness [26, 37, 41] were also reported. Fatigue led to a loss of self/autonomy for some patients, who described grieving for their previous self, acknowledging that they had changed because of their fatigue experiences [21, 22, 25, 34, 40, 41]. Patients reported that they felt they were a burden to others [22, 25, 26], helplessness [22, 25, 26, 30], loneliness [24, 25] and, less frequently, feelings of confusion [41], shock, disappointment or suffocation [25].

Patients also experienced anxiety/worry due to the unpredictability of their fatigue, anticipating bouts of fatigue and doubting that their treatment regime was efficacious because of their fatigue experiences [21, 26, 29, 32, 41]. Patients frequently reported uncertainty/self-doubt regarding the authenticity of their fatigue experiences and their ability to cope [23, 25, 29, 30, 41], and feared death, the potential loss of their independence and leaving the house/meeting other people [25, 26, 29], although some expressed determination to overcome fatigue [22, 25, 29]. Anger/frustration was common [16, 22, 25, 26, 32, 35, 41], and patients often felt more irritable regarding their own actions (such as accidentally knocking things over) and, at times, with the continued presence of others [22, 25, 35, 37, 41] (Table S10).

Activities of Daily Living

The most frequently reported effect of fatigue on activities of daily living was difficulty completing household tasks [16, 21, 26, 27, 31–33, 35, 36, 39, 41, 44], eating or cooking [21, 25, 26, 31–34, 39], gardening [30, 41, 44] and shopping/running errands [39]. Patients also struggled to engage in a range of leisure activities/hobbies [28, 30, 31, 36, 38, 39] and had difficulty travelling [26, 30, 38, 41, 44], with some patients reporting that driving was challenging or unsafe [33, 39, 44].

Cancer-related fatigue impacted patients’ ability to engage in self-care [16, 22, 25, 26, 29, 41], and patients felt that they had lost independence because of their fatigue, often relying on others to assist them with daily life [22, 25, 26, 33, 35]. Patients also experienced a lack of privacy because of requiring help [25] and changes in living arrangements [41] (Table S11).

Sleep

Cancer-related fatigue led to daytime sleepiness, as patients would often need to rest [27, 33, 37, 41–43]. Patients had difficulty initiating sleep/falling asleep [16, 37, 44] and experienced worsening sleep quality/efficiency because of fatigue but also noted that poor/shorter sleep exacerbated their fatigue experiences [28, 29, 35] (Table S12).

Social Functioning and Relationships

Cancer-related fatigue led to difficulty participating in social events or a complete inability to attend, resulting in patients reducing the frequency or length of their attendance and their engagement with others in social settings [21, 22, 30, 31, 39–41, 44]. Fatigue impacted patients’ ability to maintain friendships [22, 24, 29, 30, 35, 40, 41], and patients experienced a lack of understanding from others regarding the impact of fatigue on their day-to-day lives, placing undue pressure on them to rest and/or exercise [21, 23, 25, 37, 41].

Cancer-related fatigue impacted the roles or relationships of patients within their families, including a shift from parent/caregiver to requiring care themselves [16, 25, 29, 35, 37, 39, 43, 44]. Patients experienced difficulties with their romantic relationships, including complete relationship breakdowns, or with sexual intimacy [21, 25, 37, 40, 41] (Table S13).

Work and Financial

Fatigue caused some patients to be completely unable to work [22, 35, 36, 39, 41, 44], which might lead to financial stress, alongside medical bills [22, 41]. Others reported a negative impact on their productivity/performance or time working [22, 33, 35, 40, 41], or a need to adapt to a work-from-home schedule to accommodate their fatigue [41]. Consequently, cancer-related fatigue impacted patients’ career/retirement plans, necessitating early retirement in some cases [39, 41] (Table S14).

Coping Strategies

Patients coped with their cancer-related fatigue by managing their energy expenditure or planning activities to maximise their capabilities [16, 21, 22, 24, 25, 27, 28, 32, 38, 40, 41]. Engaging in activities/hobbies [28–30, 41], or physical activity and/or exercise, such as walking, helped some patients cope [21, 23, 27–29, 38, 41, 44], as did passive activities such as massage or stretching [24]. Patients adapted their diet to increase their energy levels [23, 24, 29, 38, 39, 41] and utilised herbal and/or alternative therapies, such as dried mugwort and seeking sunlight/warming therapies [24, 29].

A range of psychological coping strategies were also reported, including psychological therapies [41], attempting to fight off the fatigue [25, 29], acceptance of fatigue as part of daily life [21, 22, 25, 29, 37, 41], waiting to feel better [23, 25, 26, 42] or adopting a positive mindset and retaining hope that their condition would improve while acknowledging that they had navigated previous tough instances of fatigue [22, 25, 29, 30, 40, 41]. Less frequently reported psychological coping strategies included relaxation and being outdoors [29], drawing upon religion/spirituality [22, 29], shifting attention away from their fatigue [29] and their personal fate or ‘ming’ [29]. Patients also turned to their immediate network for both physical and emotional support, including their family or partner, friends, community and HCPs [22, 25, 26, 29, 37, 40, 41] (Table S15).

Unmet Needs

The most frequently reported unmet treatment need regarding cancer-related fatigue was that patients felt there was a lack of, or inappropriate, support from HCPs, including the perception that HCPs were disinterested [21, 23, 25, 41, 44] or did not have adequate time to address their fatigue-related concerns [22, 23, 44]. Some patients requested further information relating to their diagnosis and subsequent treatment [23, 29, 37]. An absence of holistic assessments [23] and a lack of efficacious treatment to treat cancer-related fatigue was also reported [44] (Table S16).

Discussion

Over 100 unique concepts for the patient experience of cancer-related fatigue were identified and synthesised from 26 primary qualitative studies globally (N = 512 patients), including a wide range of oncology indications. These concepts were aggregated into fatigue triggers, descriptors, onset, frequency, severity, duration and impact on HRQoL. While cognitive or mental fatigue was described by patients living with cancer or during active treatment, physiological fatigue was reported across all three temporal treatment stages. Comparison of fatigue descriptors suggested that severe fatigue experiences may be more common while living with cancer or during active treatment and post-treatment. Cancer-related fatigue was triggered by oncology treatments, disease-related factors, demographic factors, daily activities and lifestyle factors, meaning that the onset and trajectory of fatigue were often varied and unpredictable between patients. In different publications, fatigue was described as occurring persistently across each day, or as fluctuating with intermittent fatigue episodes. Fatigue also varied in duration across temporal stages, lasting several years in some patients and hours, days, weeks or months in others. While fatigue was commonly described as severe or highly bothersome and, for some patients, was not relieved with rest or treatment, other patients referred to fatigue as tolerable. However, fatigue considerably affected how patients function in their activities of daily living, alongside their physical and social functioning, emotional and physiological wellbeing, sleep, work and finances, emphasising the importance of assessing fatigue-related outcomes in oncology clinical trials, alongside other symptoms and traditional endpoints.

Coping strategies were largely self-developed by patients and included managing their energy expenditure or planning activities to maximise their capabilities, engaging in exercise or walking, turning to their immediate network for both physical and emotional support and following a good diet. Patients reported substantial unmet needs in cancer-related fatigue treatment; there was inappropriate or a lack of support from HCPs, with HCPs appearing to be disinterested or to have inadequate time to address their fatigue-related concerns, and a lack of holistic assessments, efficacious treatment or information relating to their diagnosis and subsequent treatment.

The findings of this review were used to inform the development of a new holistic conceptual model of fatigue in oncology, incorporating patient coping strategies and unmet treatment needs. In a previously published model focusing on pain and fatigue in soft tissue sarcoma, fatigue was conceptualised across eight concepts within three broad domains ("fatigability”, “fatigue symptoms” and “fatigue impact”) [15]. The current model builds upon the high-level concepts included in this model and broadly aligns with the Assessing Symptoms of Cancer Using Patient-Reported Outcomes (ASCPRO) conceptual model of cancer-related fatigue [45], where cancer-related fatigue was shown to be influenced by cancer and cancer treatments. Fatigue was described as the subjective experience of weariness, tiredness and low energy, and/or the perceived impact on functioning (e.g. physical, cognitive, emotional and social functioning). In the ASCPRO model, a separate domain highlighted the impact that fatigue and other symptoms of cancer has on functioning (social, mental and physical) and overall HRQoL. This further supports the conclusion that fatigue is a multi-faceted experience for patients across different oncology diagnoses and at different stages in their disease journey.

This holistic conceptual model provides a useful foundation for early phase clinical trial decision making. However, industry guidance should be followed when selecting, developing or modifying PRO measures in pivotal clinical trials to ensure the correct measurement approach is selected for the population of interest [46, 47]. Conceptual models can facilitate both the communication of concepts to be assessed and decision making by regulatory reviewers as to whether a proposed COA/PRO fully captures a concept [46]. As treatments are beginning to extend patient survival, and may be less aggressive, HRQoL-related concepts such as fatigue should be considered for measurement to enable patient-centric treatment development. Identifying or developing appropriate PRO measures to better assess these concepts also has important applications for routine clinical practice and may facilitate improved patient and clinician discussions about fatigue [48]. Despite the availability of several PRO measures that assess aspects of fatigue in oncology, further research is required to explore which measures are most appropriate for different clinical research scenarios in different contexts, while considering relevant industry guidelines, such as FDA Patient-Focused Drug Development (PFDD) Guidance [46, 49].

This targeted literature review informed the development of a novel conceptual model for cancer-related fatigue which, to our knowledge, is the first that is informed by the experiences of a large number of patients across a range of disease stages and oncology indications globally, covering both solid and haematological malignancies. The model provides a holistic overview of the patient experience of fatigue in oncology, alongside patient coping strategies and unmet needs, which confirms the importance of fatigue measurement in a clinical trial setting and may guide the selection of appropriate measurement strategies in future oncology clinical trials. As patient experiences of fatigue are influenced by oncology indication and treatment, some aspects of the conceptual model developed in this review may not be relevant to all patients. Therefore, the application of the model should be tailored to each disease and population to ensure relevance. Furthermore, planning the timing of assessments to detect changes in fatigue is difficult when the symptom fluctuates substantially, and frequent assessments during long oncology trials might increase patient burden. Additionally, further research would be necessary to determine whether the experience of fatigue is comparable across all forms of cancer. Although consideration was given to the varied representation of cancer types and stages when selecting the most relevant publications for inclusion, it is acknowledged that the conceptual model does not capture the patient experience of fatigue regarding other cancer types not identified in the literature review. It is unclear whether other cancer types were not identified because of a lack of research more generally or whether fatigue is not a key component of the patient experience in other cancer types.

Conclusion

This review demonstrated that fatigue is an important, impactful symptom for patients with cancer, who commonly experience unmet treatment needs, including poor support from HCPs, and informed the first conceptual model of cancer-related fatigue across oncology indications, treatments and disease stages. Cancer-related fatigue is a key concept for assessment in oncology clinical trials and further research must be conducted to identify and evaluate how currently used PROs and COAs cover the patient fatigue experience.
