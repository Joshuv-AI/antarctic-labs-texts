“Family-as-Root” and “Family-Centeredness” in hospice care: a concept analysis and implications for nursing practice
Authors: Chao Yan, Ai Ji, Yonghong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamilies are widely recognised as central actors in hospice and palliative care, and “family-centered care” has become a dominant practice model. However, the ontological and axiological status of the family in many cultural contexts—particularly East Asian Confucian traditions—extends far beyond the functional roles usually described by family-centeredness. This study conceptualizes and clarifies “Family-as-Root” - a culturally grounded, ontological-axiological orientation to family that is widely recognizable in Chinese contexts but remains under-theorized in international hospice literature - and distinguishes it from, while relating it to, “Family-Centeredness” in hospice care.MethodsWe conducted a concept analysis using Walker and Avant’s eight-step method, treating “Family-as-Root” as an analytical term developed to capture conceptual gaps not fully addressed by existing family-centered paradigms. A systematic literature search of major English- and Chinese-language databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang Data) was undertaken from inception to August 2025. The corpus included theoretical, empirical and review articles addressing families in hospice or palliative care, end-of-life decision-making, and family-related cultural values. Through iterative reading and coding, we identified defining attributes, antecedents and consequences of “Family-as-Root” and “Family-Centeredness”, and constructed model, borderline and contrary cases to illustrate their manifestations in practice.ResultsThe analysis showed that “Family-as-Root” is an ontological–axiological construct grounded in a view of the person as inherently embedded in intergenerational family relationships. It emphasises the family as the primary locus of identity, value transmission, moral obligation and meaning-making in serious illness and dying. By contrast, “Family-Centeredness” is a functional care paradigm that focuses on collaborative decision-making, information sharing, and role negotiation between professionals, patients and family members within existing healthcare structures. In hospice care, these two concepts intersect but are not interchangeable: Family-as-Root shapes how patients and relatives understand suffering, good death and acceptable choices, while Family-Centeredness structures how professionals engage families in care processes. Recognising this distinction highlights expanded nursing roles as interpreters of cultural values, facilitators of intra-family dialogue, and safeguards of patients’ and families’ sense of meaning.Conclusions“Family-as-Root” and “Family-Centeredness” represent two interrelated but conceptually distinct dimensions of family involvement in hospice care—one ontological–axiological, the other functional–interactional. Differentiating these concepts can support the design of culturally sensitive hospice models, guide assessment of family needs and resources, and inform the development of nursing interventions and measurement tools that more adequately reflect diverse family cultures in China and other settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02078-0.

Introduction

In the global field of hospice care, “family-centered care” has become an established gold standard [1]. This paradigm rightly recognizes that the end-of-life journey is a shared experience affecting the entire family system, rather than an isolated individual event [2]. However, when this model, which originated primarily from Western individualistic cultures [3], is applied to a society like China that emphasizes collectivism and relational interdependence, its philosophical foundations and practical logic reveal increasing limitations [4]. The tensions arising from this cross-cultural practice urgently demand a deeper cultural examination and philosophical discernment of the core concept of “family” itself [5].

Within the healthcare context of Chinese communities, the concepts of “Family-as-Root” and “Family-Centeredness” are often used interchangeably in both clinical practice and academic discourse. Yet this conflation is precisely the source of many persistent dilemmas in hospice care [4]. At a conceptual level, “Family-Centeredness” is primarily a sociological and nursing practice paradigm that views the family as the basic unit of care [1–2]. Its core concern is the optimisation of family functioning in relation to healthcare processes, such as facilitating communication, supporting decision-making and mobilising resources [2–3]. In contrast, “Family-as-Root” is an ontological–axiological construct deeply embedded in Chinese philosophical traditions, especially Confucian ethics of filial piety. It regards the family as the ultimate locus of personal identity, moral practice and life meaning, with its inner logic centred on intergenerational responsibility, cultural transmission and relational continuity [4–5]. In this paper, “Family-as-Root” is used as an analytic concept label rather than a universally established clinical term; it names a pattern that is familiar in Chinese practice but insufficiently captured by existing family-centered language in the hospice literature.

In current hospice and palliative care practice, most interventions remain confined to the functional level of family-centred care, focusing on how families participate in information exchange, treatment choices and caregiving tasks [6]. While such interventions are indispensable, they often fail to reach the deeper meaning-making dimension implied by Family-as-Root. As a result, clinicians may struggle to understand and respond to behaviours that are common among Chinese families at the end of life—such as “protective truth concealment” about diagnosis or prognosis [7], or the expression of grief and loyalty through complex ritual practices [8]. When these behaviours are interpreted only as dysfunctional communication or barriers to patient autonomy, the cultural logic that underpins them is easily overlooked. This, in turn, constrains the cultural sensitivity of care and limits the effectiveness of interventions aimed at improving the quality of dying and bereavement support.

Although the concept of Family-as-Root emerges from the specific historical and philosophical background of Chinese societies, its theoretical implications are highly relevant to global hospice care. Cultural logics that resemble Family-as-Root—emphasising strong intergenerational bonds, collective identity and the primacy of family honour—are also evident in Latino familism, African kinship-based communities and many Southern European families [9, 10]. Intergenerational dynamics - including adult children’s advocacy, protective information-filtering, and family-led decision processes - are also described in Western settings, although they are often framed through different ethical and legal vocabularies. Even in highly individualistic Western contexts, when people face serious illness and impending death, the pursuit of “relational integrity” [11] and “meaning in life” [12] often becomes central. A more finely grained conceptual distinction between deep cultural–ontological views of the family and functionally oriented family-centred care can therefore enrich our understanding of how families make sense of suffering, “good death” and morally acceptable choices in diverse settings.

Against this background, a systematic concept analysis of both “Family-as-Root” and “Family-Centeredness” is needed. Academically, such an analysis can clarify their theoretical origins, defining attributes, antecedents and consequences, and delineate their conceptual boundaries. Practically, it can illuminate why a purely functional view of the family may be insufficient in hospice care, particularly in cultural contexts where the family constitutes the primary field of moral identity and existential meaning. Therefore, this study aims to (1) elucidate the defining attributes, theoretical genealogy and practical manifestations of Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness in hospice care; (2) compare their points of convergence and divergence at different logical levels; and (3) derive implications for assessment, intervention design and role development in nurse-led hospice care. By doing so, we seek to provide a more robust theoretical foundation and forward-looking strategic insights for culturally sensitive hospice practice in Chinese communities and beyond.

Research methods

Concept analysis methodology

This study employed the classic eight–step method of concept analysis proposed by Walker and Avant [13]. This approach was chosen because it provides a systematic procedure for clarifying complex and ambiguously used concepts that are central to nursing practice. The eight steps include: (1) selecting the concept; (2) determining the purpose of the analysis; (3) identifying all uses of the concept that can be discovered; (4) determining the defining attributes; (5) constructing model, borderline, related and contrary cases; (6) identifying antecedents and consequences; (7) defining empirical referents; and (8) considering theoretical connections with broader frameworks.

In this study, we applied Walker and Avant’s method in a way that explicitly foregrounds both the empirical literature and the underlying philosophical–cultural context. Empirical research and theoretical papers in hospice and palliative care were used to identify how “Family-as-Root” and “Family-Centeredness” appear and function in practice, while classical and contemporary writings on Chinese family ethics and related cultural traditions were treated as conceptual resources that inform, rather than determine, the analytic process.

Rationale for concept selection

The decision to focus on “Family-as-Root” and “Family-Centeredness” as the paired objects of analysis was based on three main considerations:

Clinical relevance

In hospice care practice, many of the most challenging situations—such as protective truth concealment, family-driven treatment decisions and conflicts between patient preference and collective expectations—arise from the way families understand illness, dying and obligations [14]. These situations are often described using the language of “family-centered care”, yet they frequently reflect much deeper cultural and existential commitments. Clarifying the relationship between Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness therefore has direct implications for assessment, communication and intervention in hospice care.

Theoretical ambiguity

Existing literature in palliative care, family nursing and medical sociology often uses terms such as “family-centered care”, “family involvement”, “familism” and “relational autonomy” in overlapping and sometimes inconsistent ways [15, 16]. The ontological–axiological status of the family (for example, as a primary locus of identity and moral obligation) is seldom distinguished from the functional model of family-centred practice adopted by healthcare systems. This conceptual blurring makes it difficult to develop theory-based instruments and interventions. A rigorous concept analysis can help disentangle these layers.

Cultural urgency

In Chinese communities, as well as in many other collectivist or family-oriented cultures, the family is not only a unit of care but also the root of personal existence, value transmission and lineage continuity [17]. Rapid demographic change, medicalisation of dying and the diffusion of Western bioethical norms have created new tensions within hospice care. There is an urgent need for theoretical work that can make visible the cultural logic of Family-as-Root while remaining dialogical with international discourse on family-centred care.

Data sources and search strategy

To ensure a comprehensive and culturally inclusive evidence base, we conducted a systematic literature search of major English- and Chinese-language databases. The search covered PubMed, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Web of Science for international literature, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and Wanfang Data for Chinese literature. All databases were searched from their inception until August 2025.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (e.g. MeSH) and free-text keywords related to: (1) hospice care, palliative care, end-of-life care; (2) family, family-centred care, family involvement, familism, kinship; and (3) serious illness, advanced disease, terminal illness, dying. Synonyms and variant spellings were included, and Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to construct sensitive yet focused search strings tailored to each database [18]. For example, the PubMed strategy combined terms for family (e.g. “family*”, “familism”, “family-centered care”) with terms for hospice and palliative care and end-of-life contexts.

Reference lists of key articles were also screened to identify additional relevant publications not captured by the database searches (citation chasing). The search and reporting processes were informed by PRISMA guidance as far as appropriate for concept analysis studies, and the overall screening flow is summarised in Fig. 1.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

We applied explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria to select literature that was theoretically and empirically relevant to the clarification of Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness in hospice care.

Inclusion criteria were

The article contained explicit discussion, analysis or operationalisation of families, family roles, family-centred care, familism or closely related constructs in the context of hospice care, palliative care, end-of-life care or serious illness.

The study was theoretical, empirical (qualitative, quantitative or mixed-method), or a review article that contributed conceptual insight into how families are positioned, valued or involved in care.

The publication date was up to August 2025.

The full text was available in English or Chinese.

Exclusion criteria were

Conference abstracts, dissertations, commentaries, news reports and non-scholarly texts without sufficient conceptual or empirical content.

Articles where families were only mentioned tangentially and not treated as a central focus of analysis or intervention.

Literature clearly unrelated to hospice, palliative care, end-of-life situations or serious illness.

Duplicate records across databases.

All records retrieved from the database searches were imported into a reference management programme, and duplicates were removed. Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Potentially relevant articles were retrieved in full text and assessed independently by the same two authors. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third author until consensus was reached.The initial search identified 3,138 records. After removal of duplicates and stepwise screening of titles, abstracts and full texts, 31 articles met the inclusion criteria and were retained for concept analysis. Data extraction and analytic procedure

A structured data extraction form was developed to capture key information from each included article, including: publication year, country or region, study design (theoretical, qualitative, quantitative, mixed-method, review), care setting, characteristics of patients and families, and how family-related constructs were defined, operationalised and discussed. Particular attention was paid to passages that addressed the meaning, value, roles, obligations and decision-making positions of families in hospice and palliative care.

The concept analysis itself proceeded iteratively. First, all included articles were read in full by at least two authors, who independently noted candidate attributes, antecedents and consequences related to Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness. These initial codes were then compared, discussed and grouped into higher-order categories through constant comparison within and across Chinese and international literature. Second, we examined how these categories converged or diverged between the two concepts, and which empirical patterns supported or challenged their differentiation. Third, drawing on both the coded literature and relevant philosophical writings, we refined the defining attributes of each concept and articulated their antecedents and consequences.

Model, borderline, related and contrary cases were subsequently constructed to illustrate how Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness manifest in real-world hospice care scenarios. These cases were based on composite depictions synthesised from published empirical studies and theoretical descriptions, rather than on new primary data, in order to protect confidentiality while preserving conceptual richness. Finally, we identified existing measurement tools and nursing interventions that partially reflect aspects of Family-as-Root or Family-Centeredness, and considered how the clarified concepts might inform future development of culturally sensitive assessment instruments and care models.

Conceptual origins and theoretical positioning

Philosophical origins of “Family-as-Root”

The concept of “Family-as-Root” arises from the deep structure of Chinese philosophical and cultural traditions, in which the family is regarded as the fundamental field of human existence. Classical Confucian texts portray the person not as an isolated individual but as a node within intergenerational family relationships, ordered by filial piety, role obligations and ritual propriety [19, 20]. Within the sequence of “cultivating the self, regulating the family, ordering the state, and bringing peace to all under heaven”, the family is positioned as the core locus where moral character is formed, social norms are internalised, and life meaning is transmitted across generations.

From this perspective, the “root” of the person lies not in autonomous choice but in relational embeddedness and continuity of lineage. Individual joys and sufferings are interpreted through the lens of family honour and shame, and major life events—including illness, dying and bereavement—are seen as moments where the integrity of the family as a moral and spiritual community is tested. This ontological–axiological view emphasises value-rootedness, relational ontology, sacred transcendence (such as ancestral connections and ritualised remembrance) and diachronic continuity as core features of Family-as-Root.

Theoretical genealogy of “Family-Centeredness”

In contrast, the concept of “Family-Centeredness” is rooted in modern social science and nursing theories. Its genealogy can be traced to structural–functional sociology, family systems theory and the development of family nursing and family-centred care models in paediatrics and chronic illness management [15, 21, 22]. Within these traditions, the family is conceptualised as a relatively bounded social unit whose structural characteristics, communicative patterns and problem-solving capacities influence health outcomes.

Family-centred care emerges as a practice paradigm that seeks to recognise families as partners in care, to involve them in decision-making, and to support their functioning as they interact with healthcare systems. Its primary focus is on optimising the structural and functional operations of the family: how information is shared, how roles are negotiated, how resources are mobilised, and how conflicts are resolved in the context of illness and care.

Taken together, Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness occupy different logical levels. Family-as-Root refers to a deep cultural–ontological view of the person–family relationship, emphasising identity, value and meaning; Family-Centeredness refers to a functional, system-level care model that organises how professionals collaborate with families in practice. The subsequent sections build on this distinction to clarify each concept’s defining attributes and practical manifestations. Importantly, analysing Family-Centeredness across the included literature revealed a recurrent conceptual gap: procedural family involvement does not necessarily account for how families construct moral meaning, filial duty, and intergenerational continuity at the end of life. “Family-as-Root” is articulated in this paper as a complementary concept to make this gap analytically visible and clinically addressable.

Defining attributes and conceptual models

Defining attributes of “Family-as-Root”

The analysis identified four interrelated attributes that characterise Family-as-Root:

Value-rootedness

The family constitutes the primary framework within which individuals understand what counts as a good life, a good death and a worthy way of facing illness. Personal achievements and failures are interpreted through the lens of family honour or disgrace [23]. When facing life-limiting illness, patients and relatives frequently articulate concerns such as “not bringing shame to the family”, “repaying parental kindness” or “leaving peace among siblings”, indicating that moral evaluation is rooted in family-related values rather than in individual preference alone.

Relational ontology

The person is understood as fundamentally relational, with self-identity and self-worth grounded in fulfilling family roles (such as being a filial child, responsible parent or supportive spouse) [24]. Decision-making in serious illness often reflects this relational ontology: patients may prioritise the needs and emotions of family members, and family members may assume a collective subject-position when negotiating treatment options, expressing grief or organising care.

Sacred transcendence

Family-as-Root carries a dimension of sacred or transcendent meaning. Through ancestral worship, religious or folk rituals, and symbolic practices around dying and funerals, families seek to maintain continuity between the living and the deceased [25]. Hospice-related decisions—such as where to die, how to handle the body, or which rituals to perform—are often guided by beliefs about maintaining harmony with ancestors and securing spiritual protection for descendants.

Diachronic continuity

A key attribute of Family-as-Root is its orientation toward intergenerational continuity. Individuals understand their lives as embedded within a longer family timeline that extends backwards to ancestors and forwards to future generations [26]. Behaviours during illness and dying, such as bearing suffering, making financial sacrifices or preserving family property, are frequently justified with reference to “future children and grandchildren” or “continuing the family line”.

Together, these attributes construct a conceptual model in which the family is the root of personal existence, moral obligation, spiritual connection and temporal continuity. Hospice care practices that engage with Family-as-Root must therefore address not only immediate clinical needs but also the family’s pursuit of moral integrity, spiritual coherence and lineage continuity.

Defining attributes of “Family-Centeredness”

For Family-Centeredness, four defining attributes were identified from the literature:

Structural functionality

The family is conceptualised as a social system that performs specific functions, such as providing emotional support, coordinating care, making decisions and allocating resources [27]. The focus is on whether these functions operate effectively in the context of illness and care, and on how healthcare teams can support or compensate for functional deficits.

Problem-solving orientation

Family-centred care emphasises the capacity of the family to recognise problems, mobilise internal and external resources and implement coping strategies when facing crises such as symptom exacerbations, hospitalisation or transition to hospice [28]. Interventions are often designed to strengthen communication, enhance coping skills and improve joint decision-making.

Actual interactivity

A core concern of Family-Centeredness is the quality of real-time interactions between families and healthcare professionals, including information exchange, shared deliberation about treatment options, negotiation of roles and responsibilities, and mutual respect in collaborative care [29]. The emphasis is on observable interactional processes rather than on deeper cultural meanings.

Synchronic coordination

Family-centred models typically operate within a synchronic time frame, focusing on coordination among family members and between families and professionals at specific points or phases of care. Multidisciplinary family meetings, care conferences and coordinated discharge planning exemplify this attribute [30].

These attributes collectively depict Family-Centeredness as a functional, process-oriented paradigm that guides how health systems and professionals engage families in care. Representations in hospice care and typical cases

Practical differentiation of conceptual representations

Synthesising the defining attributes with empirical findings from hospice and palliative care studies, we observed that Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness generate systematically different patterns of behaviour and expectations in practice.

In situations of truth telling about diagnosis and prognosis, Family-as-Root often manifests as protective concealment motivated by filial piety and a desire to prevent “psychological harm” or loss of hope for the patient. From a Family-Centeredness perspective, this can be interpreted as a communication barrier or a threat to patient autonomy, whereas from a Family-as-Root perspective it reflects a moral decision grounded in family-based value hierarchies.

In treatment decision-making, Family-as-Root is reflected in collective decision structures, appeals to ancestral expectations and considerations of family reputation and future continuity. Family-Centeredness, in contrast, emphasises structured family meetings, clarification of preferences and shared decision-making techniques that align treatment plans with expressed values and goals of care.

Regarding caregiving and rituals, Family-as-Root is expressed through intensive family involvement in hands-on care, the presence of extended kin networks at the bedside, and elaborate rituals before and after death that affirm intergenerational bonds. Family-centred practice focuses more on supporting caregivers’ burden, facilitating role distribution, coordinating services and providing bereavement support.

Presentation of model cases

Model case for “Family-as-Root”

The first model case illustrates Family-as-Root as the dominant logic in hospice care. It is a composite case developed from published qualitative studies of Chinese families experiencing advanced cancer [30].

Mr. Chen, aged 78, originally from a rural village, had long lived with his adult children in a metropolitan city. After being diagnosed with advanced lung cancer, his children requested that the oncologist avoid explicitly disclosing the terminal nature of the illness to him, arguing that “an old father should not carry such heavy knowledge” and that “if he loses hope, he will give up living”. A family meeting was convened without Mr. Chen to decide on treatment. The children agreed to pursue aggressive chemotherapy despite the limited expected benefit, believing that “if we do not try everything, we will fail our duty and be unable to face our ancestors and relatives in the village”.

Throughout the illness trajectory, the family organised care in a way that reflected strong relational ontology and diachronic continuity. Different children took responsibility for hospital accompaniment, financial payments and ritual preparations. When Mr. Chen became agitated and tried to remove his infusion lines, the children interpreted this not only as a medical issue but also as a sign that “our father’s spirit is struggling”. In the final days, they insisted on transferring him back to his hometown so that he could “die where generations of our family are buried”. Taoist rituals and ancestral offerings were prepared in advance, and grandchildren were taught how to bow and speak to the ancestors during the funeral.

This model case demonstrates how value-rootedness (family honour, filial duty), relational ontology (collective subjectivity), sacred transcendence (ancestral connection) and diachronic continuity (concern for lineage) guide decisions and actions. Professional involvement remains largely confined to symptom management and technical care, whereas the family assumes primary responsibility for meaning-making and moral judgement.

Model case for “Family-Centeredness”

The second model case exemplifies a setting in which Family-Centeredness is explicitly adopted as an organising principle for hospice care. It is a composite scenario based on studies of family meetings and family-centred interventions in palliative care [31].

Mrs. Li, a 65-year-old woman with advanced colorectal cancer, was referred to a hospital-based hospice unit. Upon admission, the team conducted a family assessment, identifying her spouse and two adult children as primary caregivers. A structured family meeting was arranged with Mrs. Li present, during which the palliative care physician explained the prognosis and potential care options. Guided by family-centred principles, the team invited each family member to voice concerns, encouraged Mrs. Li to express her priorities and facilitated negotiation of roles (who would provide home care, who would coordinate with the hospice team, how financial responsibilities would be shared).

During subsequent weeks, regular family meetings were used to review symptom control, adjust the care plan and address emerging conflicts. Nurses provided psychoeducational support to family caregivers, taught skills for managing pain and other symptoms at home, and assessed caregiver burden. Social workers helped connect the family with community resources and facilitated advance care planning discussions. After Mrs. Li’s death, bereavement follow-up focused on supporting the surviving spouse and children as they adapted to new roles and routines.

This case highlights structural functionality (clear division of caregiving roles), problem-solving orientation (jointly addressing clinical and practical issues), actual interactivity (ongoing communication between family and team) and synchronic coordination (multidisciplinary collaboration at key time points). While the family’s deeper cultural values undoubtedly shape their preferences, the primary focus of care is on optimising processes and outcomes within the family–healthcare system interface.Together, the two model cases illustrate that Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness can coexist in hospice care, but they emphasise different dimensions of family involvement. Recognising which logic is predominant in a given situation can help nurses tailor communication and interventions [30, 31].

Antecedents, consequences and impact on nursing practice

Antecedent–consequence chain of “Family-as-Root”

The antecedents of Family-as-Root include: (1) long-standing cultural traditions that emphasise filial piety, ancestor veneration and family honour; (2) social structures that sustain multi-generational households and strong kinship networks; and (3) life-course experiences in which individuals learn to interpret major events through the lens of family obligations and lineage continuity [32–34]. These antecedents shape expectations about how illness and dying should be handled, who should be informed and what counts as an acceptable decision.

The consequences of Family-as-Root in hospice care are ambivalent. On the one hand, it can generate high levels of family involvement, practical support and emotional holding for the patient; strengthen resilience by embedding suffering within a larger narrative of familial meaning; and motivate families to mobilise extensive resources for care [35]. On the other hand, it may lead to marginalisation of the patient’s explicitly stated wishes, concealment of prognostic information, moral pressure to pursue burdensome treatments “for the sake of the family”, and complex grief reactions when families feel they have failed to fulfil perceived obligations [36].

For nursing practice, this antecedent–consequence chain implies that nurses need to be able to recognise Family-as-Root as a cultural logic, neither idealising it nor pathologising it. Assessment should include exploration of how families understand their obligations, fears and hopes in relation to ancestors and future generations, and how these understandings shape decisions about place of care, use of life-sustaining treatments and preferred rituals.

Antecedent–consequence chain of “Family-Centeredness”

The antecedents of Family-Centeredness lie primarily in health policy, organisational culture and professional training. They include: (1) the promotion of patient- and family-centred care as a quality standard; (2) development of multidisciplinary hospice teams and structured family meeting protocols; and (3) training programmes that emphasise communication, shared decision-making and caregiver support [20, 37, 38].

When these antecedents are present, the consequences for hospice care can be observed at multiple levels: families are more systematically included in information exchange and care planning; conflicts among family members and between families and professionals are more likely to be surfaced and negotiated; caregiver burden can be identified and addressed earlier; and continuity of care across settings is improved [39]. However, if Family-Centeredness is implemented in a purely procedural manner—restricted to checklists and standardised meetings—its potential to engage families’ deeper values and narratives may remain underused, and cultural tensions may be exacerbated when professional recommendations clash with family beliefs and practices [40].

Nursing practice within a family-centred framework requires competencies in conducting family assessments, facilitating inclusive discussions, supporting role negotiation and providing psychoeducational interventions. At the same time, nurses need to remain sensitive to the possibility that family-centred procedures may sometimes collide with the moral expectations generated by Family-as-Root, requiring nuanced negotiation.

Multi-level impact on nursing practice

Integrating the two concepts suggests a multi-level impact on nursing practice in hospice care.

At the individual nurse level, the analysis calls for enhanced cultural humility and reflexivity. Nurses are invited to move beyond viewing families solely as care resources or obstacles, and instead to engage with how families’ ontological and axiological commitments shape their responses to illness and dying [41].

At the patient and family level, distinguishing between Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness helps professionals recognise different decision-making logics—whether priority is given to individual autonomy or to family collective will—and to interpret diverse patterns of grief expression and evaluation of care [42–44].

At the service system level, the analysis supports advocacy for hospice models that allow flexible involvement of extended kin, accommodation of culturally significant rituals and integration of community and spiritual resources. Policies and protocols can be reviewed to ensure that they do not inadvertently suppress culturally rooted expressions of care, while also protecting patients from coercion or harm.

Empirical referents

Measurement tools related to “Family-as-Root”

The literature review identified a small but growing number of instruments that resonate with aspects of Family-as-Root, particularly in Chinese contexts. Among them, the Contemporary Chinese Familism Scale is a representative tool that measures beliefs about family obligation, intergenerational responsibility and the priority of family interests over individual interests [45]. Subscales such as “family obligation”, “respect for elders” and “collective orientation” correspond closely to value-rootedness and diachronic continuity.

Other instruments, including scales of filial piety, intergenerational solidarity and family obligation, provide partial coverage of the Family-as-Root construct. However, most existing tools focus on cognitive–attitudinal components and do not fully capture the sacred transcendence (e.g. ancestral connections, ritual significance) or the lived relational ontology that we identified as key attributes. Moreover, few of these instruments have been validated specifically in hospice or end-of-life care settings.

These gaps suggest the need for future measurement development that explicitly incorporates the four defining attributes of Family-as-Root, is sensitive to hospice-specific contexts, and can be used to assess how strongly families orient to this logic when facing serious illness and dying.

Measurement tools related to “Family-Centeredness”

In contrast, there is a richer array of instruments operationalising Family-Centeredness and related constructs. Family Adaptability and Cohesion Evaluation Scales (FACES) and the Family Assessment Device (FAD) are widely used tools that assess family cohesion, adaptability, communication and problem-solving [46, 47]. These instruments reflect the structural functionality and problem-solving orientation attributes identified in our analysis, and have been applied across various illnesses and cultural contexts.

In addition, specific family-centred care measures, such as the Perceptions of Family-Centered Care Scale and related tools in paediatric and adult settings, focus on families’ experiences of respect, information sharing, participation in decision-making and collaboration with health professionals [48]. These instruments align closely with the attributes of actual interactivity and synchronic coordination.

Taken together, the empirical referents show that while Family-Centeredness has been extensively operationalised through validated measurement tools, Family-as-Root remains only partially captured by existing scales. This supports the conclusion that Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness are conceptually distinct and unevenly represented in current empirical work, and underscores the importance of developing culturally attuned instruments and interventions for hospice care.

Discussion

This concept analysis differentiates “Family-as-Root” and “Family-Centeredness” as two interrelated but distinct dimensions of family involvement in hospice care. Family-as-Root captures an ontological–axiological view of the family as the locus of identity, moral obligation and meaning-making, whereas Family-Centeredness represents a functional, interactional paradigm that organises how health professionals collaborate with families. Our findings suggest that conflating these two logics obscures important sources of tension in hospice care and risks limiting culturally sensitive practice to procedural adjustments. In this discussion, we highlight the philosophical contribution of the analysis, its implications for reconstructing culturally sensitive hospice care, its relevance beyond Chinese contexts and the ways in which it invites evolution of the nurse’s role [30, 31].

From function to meaning: deepening the perspective of nursing philosophy

A central contribution of this study is to show that the “ceiling” of many current family-centred approaches lies in their predominant focus on function and process. Family-centred care models have significantly advanced nursing practice by emphasising collaboration, communication and shared decision-making. However, they are usually grounded in a systems–functional understanding of the family as a unit that performs certain roles, rather than as the “root” of persons’ existence and value orientation. When the family is seen primarily as a resource or a context for implementing patient-centred care, the deeper moral and existential meanings that families attach to illness, dying and bereavement may remain under-theorised.

By articulating Family-as-Root as a distinct concept, our analysis deepens the philosophical foundations of nursing by foregrounding relational ontology and value-rootedness. The person is understood as inherently embedded within webs of intergenerational relationships; identity is co-constituted through fulfilling family roles and sustaining lineage continuity. This resonates with relational approaches in nursing philosophy and bioethics, but adds a more explicit account of how family-based values shape what counts as a “good” or “failed” way of dying from the perspectives of patients and relatives. Importantly, our aim is not to valorise Family-as-Root uncritically, but to make explicit the normative framework within which many families interpret suffering and choice [30].

At the same time, distinguishing Family-as-Root from Family-Centeredness does not imply a dichotomy. Rather, the analysis suggests that functional, interactional work with families is always already situated within, and constrained by, deeper ontological–axiological commitments. In practice, family-centred interventions that attend only to communication patterns and decision-making processes may be effective in the short term, but will not fully address tensions that are rooted in concerns about filial duty, family honour or intergenerational continuity. From a nursing philosophy perspective, this calls for models of care that hold together process-oriented family-centred practice with explicit engagement of families’ meaning-making frameworks, rather than treating culture merely as a “modifier” of family function [31].

Reconstructing culturally sensitive care: from “awareness” to “integration”

Cultural sensitivity in hospice care is often conceptualised as a matter of awareness and attitude: professionals are encouraged to respect diverse cultural beliefs, to avoid stereotyping and to adapt communication styles. While such awareness is essential, our findings indicate that it is insufficient when cultural logics such as Family-as-Root fundamentally shape how families understand illness, truth telling, treatment choices and rituals surrounding death. In such contexts, simply “respecting cultural differences” without a conceptual grasp of the underlying logic may lead to superficial accommodation or, conversely, to unrecognised conflict between professional norms and family expectations.

The distinction between Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness provides a more operational theoretical tool for reconstructing culturally sensitive care. At the assessment stage, nurses and teams can systematically explore not only who is involved in decision-making and caregiving, but also how family members define their obligations to each other, how they relate present choices to ancestors and future generations and what they regard as a morally acceptable way of dying. Questions such as “What will allow your family to feel at peace with these decisions in the long run?” or “How do your family’s traditions guide you in this situation?” invite articulation of Family-as-Root concerns rather than restricting the conversation to immediate practical issues. This aligns with relational and family-based understandings of autonomy and decision-making in multicultural contexts [41].

In care planning, recognising Family-as-Root encourages integration of meaning-centred interventions with functional supports. For example, alongside pain and symptom management, teams can facilitate structured family conversations about unresolved obligations, forgiveness and legacy; support families in planning rituals that are important for maintaining continuity with ancestors; and create space for intergenerational storytelling or life review. These practices reframe certain behaviours—such as insistence on going “home” to die or elaborate post-death rituals—not as irrational demands but as expressions of diachronic continuity and sacred transcendence.

Documentation and evaluation of hospice care can likewise move from generic “respect for culture” statements to more precise recording of how Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness were addressed. Did the team succeed in aligning care plans with both the patient’s expressed wishes and the family’s sense of moral obligation? Were conflicts between individual autonomy and family-based values acknowledged and worked through, rather than simply overridden by institutional protocols or family pressure? Addressing these questions requires integrating Family-as-Root into the everyday language and routines of hospice practice, rather than treating it as an optional add-on.

Implications beyond the Chinese context: insights for global hospice care

Although Family-as-Root emerges from Chinese philosophical traditions, the analysis suggests that its underlying logic is not unique to Chinese societies. In many cultural settings, including various Latin American, African and Southern European communities, strong intergenerational bonds, family honour, religious beliefs and kinship-based obligations play central roles in shaping end-of-life decisions and mourning practices [9, 10]. Even in more individualistic Western contexts, qualitative studies of serious illness and bereavement have repeatedly shown that people make sense of their experiences through family narratives, shared identities and enduring relational ties [11, 12].

The conceptual distinction between Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness offers a generalisable framework for understanding these phenomena. Family-as-Root provides a lens for describing cultural configurations in which the family is experienced as the primary moral universe within which choices gain meaning, while Family-Centeredness captures how health systems attempt to formally involve families in care. In some contexts, the two may be largely aligned; in others, formal family-centred procedures may clash with family-based expectations grounded in religion, customary law or clan structures. Making this duality visible can support more nuanced comparative research on families in hospice and palliative care.

For global hospice care, the analysis underscores the importance of moving beyond a simple binary of “individualistic vs collectivistic” cultures. Instead, it invites attention to the specific ways in which families are conceived as roots of identity and obligation, and to how these conceptions intersect with institutional models of family-centred care. Future cross-cultural research could use the attributes of Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness as sensitising concepts when analysing qualitative data, designing comparative case studies or developing measurement tools. Such work would help to refine and test the transferability of the framework and to identify context-specific patterns of convergence and divergence [9].

Innovation in clinical application and evolution of the nurse’s role

Finally, this concept analysis points toward concrete innovations in clinical application and in the evolution of the nurse’s role in hospice care. At the bedside, nurses are often the professionals who spend the most time with patients and families and who witness the complexities of family dynamics around truth telling, decision-making and caregiving. The distinction between Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness equips nurses with a vocabulary to interpret these dynamics in ways that neither romanticise nor pathologise family behaviours. Instead of viewing protective concealment or insistence on certain rituals simply as “non-compliance” or “cultural barriers”, nurses can recognise them as expressions of specific value hierarchies and existential concerns [41, 42].

This recognition expands the nurse’s role from functional coordinator to interpreter of cultural values and facilitator of intra-family dialogue. Nurses can help families articulate their implicit assumptions, bring patient and family narratives into conversation and identify points where Family-as-Root commitments and formal family-centred procedures are in tension. In interdisciplinary meetings, nurses can act as advocates for integrating meaning-focused interventions—such as life review, narrative work or ritual accommodation—into care plans, alongside symptom management and caregiver support [49–51].

At the team and organisational levels, the analysis suggests directions for education and service development. Training programmes for hospice nurses could include structured teaching on concepts such as Family-as-Root, relational ontology and cultural humility, combined with case-based learning that explores how these ideas play out in complex clinical situations [41–44]. Service protocols could be reviewed to ensure that they provide flexible space for involving extended kin, accommodating key rituals and balancing respect for family-based values with safeguards for patient rights and wellbeing. In policy discourse, distinguishing Family-as-Root from Family-Centeredness may help to articulate why hospice models designed in individualistic contexts cannot simply be transplanted without adaptation [39, 40].

While this study is conceptual in nature, it generates a set of hypotheses and practical propositions that can be examined in future empirical research. For example, it would be valuable to explore whether families with a stronger orientation to Family-as-Root exhibit different patterns of decision-making, caregiving and bereavement outcomes, and how tailored nursing interventions might support such families more effectively [29, 30, 45]. These directions are further elaborated in the subsequent sections on study limitations and future research.

Ethical and legal considerations

Within principlism, this tension is often framed as balancing respect for autonomy with beneficence and non-maleficence, while care ethics and relational autonomy emphasise that preferences are frequently formed and sustained within relationships and responsibilities [5, 43]. Our analysis does not argue for replacing patient autonomy with family authority; instead, it clarifies how family moral worlds shape end-of-life meanings and why clinicians need explicit strategies to integrate family involvement without eroding patient protections.

From an ethical perspective, Family-Centeredness is often implemented through procedural safeguards - transparent information sharing, elicitation of goals, and structured family meetings - that aim to support beneficence and reduce harm while respecting autonomy [39, 40]. Family-as-Root highlights why these procedures may be experienced as morally incomplete if they ignore filial duties, family honour, and intergenerational continuity. At the same time, Family-as-Root can create risks of undue pressure or the marginalisation of a competent patient’s explicit wishes [43].

From a legal and regulatory perspective, most jurisdictions distinguish between (a) family involvement that is authorised by the patient (for example, when a patient prefers family-mediated communication or appoints a surrogate) and (b) family decision-making that overrides the patient’s known preferences. The former can be compatible with informed consent when clinicians document the patient’s preferences and decision-making capacity, whereas the latter typically requires additional safeguards, such as ethics consultation, clarification of proxy authority, and recourse to advance directives or other legally recognised mechanisms [14, 15].

For nursing practice, this implies a dual responsibility: (1) to facilitate culturally meaningful family dialogue and honour legitimate family roles, and (2) to safeguard the patient against coercion and prevent avoidable harm. Practical strategies include assessing how the patient wishes information to be handled, making space for family deliberation while keeping the patient’s voice present when desired, documenting agreed roles, and escalating unresolved conflicts through team processes and ethics pathways when necessary [41].

Intergenerational dynamics across cultures

Intergenerational dynamics also shape end-of-life communication and decision-making in Western contexts, even where healthcare systems are strongly oriented toward individual consent. Adult children and spouses often act as advocates, information brokers, and moral witnesses, and families may differ in how they prioritise protection, truth telling, and responsibility at the end of life [21, 33, 39]. Therefore, the key cross-cultural issue is not whether family influence exists, but how it is ethically justified, legally channelled, and clinically negotiated in each setting. This reinforces the value of distinguishing Family-as-Root (a deep meaning and obligation structure) from Family-Centeredness (a care-process paradigm) without reducing cultures to a simple individualistic-versus-collectivistic binary [10].

Study limitations

This concept analysis has several limitations. First, as an inherently interpretive work, it does not provide direct empirical evidence regarding outcomes associated with “Family-as-Root” or “Family-Centeredness”. The attributes, antecedents and consequences identified here reflect analytic judgements based on available literature, and alternative readings are possible. Second, the use of Walker and Avant’s method, while offering procedural clarity, is rooted in an essentially essentialist tradition and may insufficiently capture the historically and contextually evolving nature of family concepts in hospice care. Third, the evidence base was limited to English- and Chinese-language publications indexed in the selected databases; relevant scholarship in other languages, grey literature and community-based knowledge may have been overlooked, particularly with respect to Family-as-Root-like logics outside Chinese contexts. Fourth, the philosophical resources drawn on were not systematically reviewed but purposively selected, and the authors’ positionality as researchers embedded in Chinese cultural and clinical settings inevitably shaped the analysis. Finally, the overview of measurement tools and interventions was illustrative rather than exhaustive and did not include a full psychometric or effectiveness evaluation, restricting the strength of conclusions regarding empirical referents.

Conclusion

This concept analysis delineates “Family-as-Root” and “Family-Centeredness” as two intertwined yet conceptually distinct dimensions of family involvement in hospice care. “Family-as-Root” designates an ontological–axiological configuration in which the family functions as the root of personal identity, moral obligation, spiritual connection and intergenerational continuity, whereas “Family-Centeredness” denotes a functional–interactional paradigm structuring how health professionals engage families in communication, decision-making and care coordination. Clarifying their defining attributes, antecedents and consequences, and illustrating their manifestations through composite cases, demonstrates that conflating these logics obscures key tensions around truth telling, collective decision-making and ritual practice, particularly in Chinese and other family-oriented cultural contexts. At the same time, the analysis suggests that Family-as-Root and Family-Centeredness can be productively integrated within multi-level hospice models that combine robust family-centred processes with explicit engagement of families’ meaning-making frameworks. For nursing, this implies an expanded role in interpreting cultural values, facilitating intra-family dialogue and designing interventions that address both functional needs and existential concerns. Future empirical and cross-cultural research should refine, operationalise and test this dual-framework, and support the development of hospice care that is not only family-centred in procedure but also responsive to the family as a root of meaning in serious illness and dying.
