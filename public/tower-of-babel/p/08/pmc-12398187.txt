Background

Maternal mortality represents one of the most pressing global health challenges, with approximately 260,000 women dying during pregnancy and childbirth in 2023. The burden demonstrates stark global inequities, as 92% of these deaths occurred in low- and lower-middle-income countries, where maternal mortality ratios reach 346 per 100,000 live births compared to only 10 per 100,000 in high-income countries. Despite regional progress, the global maternal mortality ratio remained at 197 per 100,000 live births in 2023, far from the Sustainable Development Goal target of below 70 by 2030. Severe bleeding, infections, hypertensive disorders, delivery complications, and unsafe abortion constitute approximately 75% of all maternal deaths, most of which are preventable with appropriate healthcare interventions [1, 2]. Maternal disorders (MD) constitute the direct obstetric complications underlying these mortality patterns. Among maternal disorders directly linked to pregnancy and childbirth, six disorders—Maternal Hemorrhage (MH), Maternal Sepsis and Other Maternal Infections (MSI), Maternal Hypertensive Disorders (MHD), Maternal Obstructed Labor and Uterine Rupture (MOLUR), Maternal Abortion and Miscarriage (MAM), and Ectopic Pregnancy (EP)—account for the majority of maternal morbidity and mortality globally [3].

While maternal mortality has received substantial research attention, the broader spectrum of maternal disorders contributing to morbidity and long-term disability appears to remain inadequately characterized. Although previous global burden analyses have provided valuable insights into maternal health trends, significant knowledge gaps may persist. Earlier studies have primarily relied on traditional epidemiological approaches and have rarely employed comprehensive quantitative modeling methodologies to simultaneously examine the complex interplay between demographic transitions, health inequalities, and temporal trends. Furthermore, existing analyses appear to have infrequently integrated both absolute and relative inequality measures, potentially limiting our ability to provide nuanced insights into equity patterns across different socioeconomic contexts, particularly within the evolving landscape of global health interventions and socioeconomic development patterns. These methodological limitations suggest that a more comprehensive analytical framework may be needed to better understand the multifaceted nature of maternal health disparities [3–5].

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study provides the most comprehensive and recent estimates of disease burden, incorporating data from over 100,000 sources across 204 countries and territories [6]. Building upon this robust dataset, our study employs advanced statistical methodologies including joinpoint regression, decomposition analysis, and health inequality assessment to provide nuanced insights into maternal disorder trends and disparities. Our study aims to quantify global and regional trends in maternal disorder burden from 1990 to 2021, assess both absolute and relative inequalities across sociodemographic strata, identify key demographic and epidemiological drivers of burden changes, and project future incidence trends through 2044 to inform strategic healthcare planning. These findings may contribute valuable evidence for targeted maternal health interventions and policy development, particularly in addressing persistent health inequalities.

Methods

Data source and GBD modeling framework

We extracted comprehensive data on maternal disorders from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 database (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The GBD 2021 provides systematic estimates of disease burden for 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021, utilizing data from diverse sources including vital registration systems, verbal autopsies, censuses, household surveys, disease-specific registries, and health service contact data [6]. The GBD 2021 modeling framework employs standardized methods for cross-country comparisons using age-standardized rates based on the GBD 2019 reference population structure, derived from countries with sociodemographic indices above 0.75. The analytical pipeline includes data quality assessment, age-sex splitting with crosswalk adjustments, space-time Gaussian process regression for estimation, and uncertainty quantification through 1,000 posterior draws. Age-standardization follows the direct method to enable comparisons across populations with varying age distributions, while 95% uncertainty intervals (2.5th-97.5th percentiles) capture both data and model parameter uncertainty [7, 8].

Study population and outcome measures

Our analysis focused on women aged 15 to 49 years, corresponding to the WHO-defined reproductive age range [9]. We examined six specific maternal disorders as defined by the GBD classification system: maternal hemorrhage, maternal sepsis and other maternal infections, maternal hypertensive disorders, maternal obstructed labor and uterine rupture, maternal abortion and miscarriage, and ectopic pregnancy. Case definitions followed GBD 2021 methodology [3]. Primary outcome measures included DALYs rates and incidence rates per 100,000 women aged 15–49 years.

Sociodemographic classification

Countries and territories were categorized into five Sociodemographic Index (SDI) quintiles based on GBD 2021 SDI values: Low, Low-middle, Middle, High-middle, and High SDI. The SDI is a composite indicator of development status strongly correlated with health outcomes, calculated as the geometric mean of three normalized indices (0–1 scale): total fertility rate under age 25, mean education for those ages 15 and older, and lag distributed income per capita. As a composite measure, an SDI of 0 represents the theoretical minimum level of development relevant to health, while an SDI of 1 represents the theoretical maximum level, providing a more comprehensive socioeconomic indicator than traditional income-based classifications alone [10].

Decomposition analysis

We applied the Das Gupta decomposition method to quantify contributions from aging, population growth, and epidemiological changes to observed DALY changes between 1990 and 2021 [12, 13]. This method effectively isolates demographic factors from disease-specific epidemiological changes, providing insights into the mechanisms driving burden shifts.

Joinpoint regression analysis

Temporal trends were characterized using Joinpoint regression analysis, which identifies statistically significant inflection points in time series data. The method calculates Annual Percent Changes for individual segments and Average Annual Percent Changes (AAPC) for overall trends. Confidence intervals and significance testing were performed for all trend estimates.

Age-Period-Cohort analysis and future projections

We conducted Age-Period-Cohort (APC) modeling to disentangle age-specific effects from period and cohort influences on maternal disorder incidence, utilizing seven five-year age groups ranging from 15 to 19 to 45–49 years and seven five-year periods from 1990 to 2021. This capability allows deeper insights into how different demographic factors uniquely contribute to disease incidence trends [14].

Building upon the APC framework, we employed Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) modeling to project maternal disorder incidence through 2044. The BAPC model was chosen due to its robustness in managing uncertainty and incorporating prior information, thus improving the reliability of forecasts [15]. Model validation included internal validation (1990–2011 to predict 2012–2021) using Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and external validation using NORDPRED modeling.

Statistical software and confidence interval Estimation

Confidence intervals were calculated using method-specific approaches: bootstrap methods with 1,000 iterations for inequality measures (SII and CI) [16], asymptotic standard errors for joinpoint regression [17], maximum likelihood estimation for Age-Period-Cohort analysis [18], and Bayesian credible intervals from MCMC sampling for BAPC projections [19]. Decomposition analysis provided deterministic mathematical decomposition of observed changes without confidence intervals following Das Gupta methodology [12]. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with results presented as point estimates and 95% confidence intervals where applicable.

Results

Overview of global maternal disorder burden

Between 1990 and 2021, the global burden of maternal disorders among women aged 15–49 years declined substantially across all measures. Age-standardized DALY rates decreased by 61.3% from 1,609.33 (95% CI: 1,464.34-1,760.52) to 622.97 (95% CI: 536.47-731.85) per 100,000 women, while absolute DALY numbers decreased by 43.6% from 21.52 million (95% CI: 19.58–23.54 million) to 12.14 million (95% CI: 10.46–14.26 million). Age-standardized incidence rates declined by 38.2% from 9,239.42 (95% CI: 8,018.78-10,579.64) to 5,705.12 (95% CI: 5,036.56-6,404.77) per 100,000 women, whereas absolute incidence numbers showed a more modest decrease of 10.0% from 123.56 million (95% CI: 107.24-141.49 million) to 111.18 million (95% CI: 98.16-124.82 million) cases (Additional file 1).

Geographic variation in maternal disorder burden remains substantial (Fig. 1, Additional file 1). In 2021, the highest age-standardized DALY rates were observed in Chad (6,141.74 per 100,000, 95% CI: 4,030.19-8,773.02), Somalia (5,535.9 per 100,000, 95% CI: 3,548.16-7,940.74), and South Sudan (4,874.03 per 100,000, 95% CI: 2,980.25-7,663.36), while the lowest rates occurred in Singapore (12.66 per 100,000, 95% CI: 9.5-16.37), Iceland (22.14 per 100,000, 95% CI: 15.7-29.47), and Norway (22.02 per 100,000, 95% CI: 14.95–31.08). Low-SDI regions experienced DALY rates 41 times higher than high-SDI regions (2,353.17 vs. 57.37 per 100,000, p < 0.001), demonstrating profound global health inequalities in maternal health outcomes.

Global trends analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis revealed heterogeneous temporal patterns across disorders and regions (Fig. 2, Additional file 2). Globally, all six maternal disorders demonstrated significant declining trends with distinct temporal patterns. Maternal Abortion and Miscarriage showed consistently steep declines across all periods, with the most pronounced reduction during 1999–2003 (APC = −5.89%, 95% CI: −6.83 to −4.94, p < 0.001), followed by sustained decline through 2021 (APC = −4.52%, 95% CI: −4.67 to −4.37, p < 0.001). Maternal Hemorrhage exhibited accelerated decline globally between 2012 and 2015 (APC = −5.60%, 95% CI: −6.69 to −4.49, p < 0.001), consistent with the steepest reduction period. Maternal Sepsis and Other Maternal Infections demonstrated the most pronounced decline during 2006–2015 (APC = −5.11%, 95% CI: −5.26 to −4.96, p < 0.001), with a notable period of increase from 1995 to 2000 (APC = + 1.48%, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.95, p < 0.001).Maternal Hypertensive Disorders showed variable decline rates across periods, with the steepest reduction during 1990–1992 (APC = −3.17%, 95% CI: −4.38 to −1.94, p < 0.001) and recent acceleration from 2017 to 2021 (APC = −2.76%, 95% CI: −3.15 to −2.37, p < 0.001). Maternal Obstructed Labor and Uterine Rupture demonstrated its most rapid decline during 2000–2004 (APC = −4.83%, 95% CI: −5.80 to −3.84, p < 0.001). Discussion

Our study systematically evaluated the changes in the global burden of MD among women aged 15 to 49 years over a 32-year period (1990–2021), assessed SDI-related inequalities, and projected future incidence through 2044. This age group aligns with WHO-defined reproductive years, encompassing most maternal health risks [20]. Our comprehensive analysis reveals significant progress alongside persistent challenges in achieving maternal health equity. The substantial decline in global burden, with DALY rates decreasing by 61.3% over the study period, reflects meaningful advances in maternal healthcare delivery, medical technology, and public health interventions worldwide [21].

However, our inequality analyses reveal a concerning paradox: while absolute disparities between high and low-SDI regions have narrowed, relative inequalities have persisted or increased for most maternal disorders. This pattern suggests that improvements in maternal health have been unevenly distributed, with high-resource settings experiencing proportionally greater benefits than low-resource areas [22–24].

The interpretation of global health progress requires careful consideration of how inequality is measured and conceptualized. Absolute inequality metrics, such as the slope index of inequality, quantify the actual magnitude of health outcome differences between populations with the highest and lowest socioeconomic status. Relative inequality measures, including the concentration index, examine the proportional distribution of health outcomes across the entire socioeconomic spectrum, providing insights into whether health improvements are distributed equitably across all population segments [23, 24]. This methodological distinction is critical for understanding why improvements in absolute health gaps may coincide with persistent or worsening relative disparities—a phenomenon that occurs when health gains are proportionally greater in higher-resource settings.This paradoxical pattern has been documented across multiple domains of maternal health research. Contemporary studies indicate that while absolute health inequalities frequently diminish over time, relative inequalities may remain stable or increase [23]. Several theoretical frameworks, including the inverse equity hypothesis, suggest that health interventions may initially be more accessible to advantaged populations before diffusing to disadvantaged groups, potentially explaining this temporal pattern [25].

Our findings align with this body of evidence and suggest several interpretations. The observed reductions in absolute maternal disorder burden across SDI regions, coupled with persistent relative disparities, may reflect the complex dynamics of global health system development rather than failures in intervention design or implementation. Health innovations and evidence-based interventions often follow predictable diffusion patterns, initially reaching populations with greater healthcare infrastructure and resources before extending to more disadvantaged settings. This temporal sequence may represent a transitional phase in global health equity rather than a permanent state of disparity. However, these patterns warrant systematic monitoring to ensure that future maternal health investments incorporate explicit equity considerations in their design, implementation, and evaluation frameworks.

Our decomposition analyses identified population growth and epidemiological changes as the primary drivers of maternal disorder trends, with distinct patterns emerging across SDI regions. Epidemiological improvements consistently contributed to burden reductions across all six maternal disorders, likely reflecting advances in clinical care delivery, evidence-based prevention strategies, and health system strengthening. These improvements may encompass enhanced prenatal screening programs, expanded access to skilled birth attendance, improved antimicrobial stewardship for sepsis prevention, and strengthened emergency obstetric care capacity [26–29].

However, rapid population growth in low-SDI regions substantially offset these epidemiological gains, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia where demographic transitions remain incomplete. For instance, countries like Nigeria and Pakistan, despite implementing maternal health programs, continue to experience high absolute maternal disorder burdens due to sustained population growth rates exceeding 2% annually. This demographic challenge underscores the interconnected nature of reproductive health, family planning access, and maternal health outcomes [30, 31].

Notably, our analysis revealed minimal contribution from population aging across all maternal disorders, which aligns with the reproductive age focus of our study population (15–49 years). This finding suggests that while population aging represents a growing global health challenge, its impact on maternal health burden remains limited during our study period, though continued monitoring may be warranted as demographic transitions progress globally.

Temporal trend analysis revealed heterogeneous patterns across disorders and regions. The identification of specific inflection points, such as the accelerated decline in maternal hemorrhage burden between 2012 and 2015, likely reflects the impact of targeted global health initiatives during these periods, potentially including the implementation of WHO guidelines for prevention and treatment of postpartum hemorrhage and expanded access to uterotonics [32, 33]. Notably, our analysis suggests that these inflection points may serve as natural experiments demonstrating the effectiveness of coordinated policy interventions, providing valuable insights for future intervention timing and implementation strategies.

The observed shift in peak maternal disorder incidence from younger to older age groups reflects global demographic transitions toward delayed childbearing. Studies from multiple countries demonstrate that delayed childbearing has increased significantly over recent decades, with women in higher-income and more urbanized regions experiencing this transition more prominently [34, 35]. Our findings indicate that this age pattern shift has been most pronounced in middle and high-SDI regions, consistent with research demonstrating associations between delayed childbearing and higher educational attainment, increased labor force participation, and economic development [35, 36].

This demographic shift presents new challenges for healthcare systems, particularly regarding age-related pregnancy complications and the need for specialized obstetric care [34, 37]. However, empirical evidence suggests this pattern reflects broader changes in women’s life course opportunities and reproductive decision-making capacity [38]. Research from multiple settings indicates that delayed childbearing is frequently associated with women’s pursuit of higher education, career development, and economic independence—factors that enhance reproductive autonomy and expand life choices beyond traditional reproductive roles [35, 38]. Studies specifically demonstrate that women with higher education levels and greater economic resources are more likely to delay childbearing and have greater control over fertility timing and family size decisions [37, 38]. This pattern suggests that while delayed childbearing may introduce certain clinical challenges, it often represents an expansion of women’s agency in reproductive decision-making within evolving socioeconomic contexts.

Our BAPC projections through 2044 provide important insights into the future landscape of maternal health burden. The continued declining trends projected across all maternal disorders suggest that current epidemiological patterns, if sustained, may contribute to further reductions in global maternal disorder burden. However, the substantial variation in projected decline rates across different disorders highlights the complex nature of maternal health improvement trajectories. The more modest projected improvements for certain disorders suggest that these may require enhanced attention and potentially different intervention approaches compared to those showing steeper decline trajectories.

These differential patterns may reflect varying intervention effectiveness, accessibility of care for specific disorders, or inherent biological and social determinants that influence disease progression. The variations underscore the importance of disorder-specific monitoring and targeted intervention strategies rather than uniform approaches to maternal health programming.

The validation results strengthen confidence in these projections, with both internal and external validation demonstrating reasonable accuracy compared to established forecasting benchmarks. Our internal validation achieved MAPE values ranging from 8.2 to 15.7%, indicating reliable predictive performance within acceptable forecasting standards. The NORDPRED external validation provides important independent corroboration of trend directions and magnitude estimates, even if specific numeric predictions may vary. This multi-method validation approach addresses concerns about prediction reliability in the context of variable data quality across regions—a methodological consideration that has gained prominence following recent global health forecasting challenges [7].

However, we acknowledge that these projections carry important limitations that warrant careful interpretation. The models assume continuation of current epidemiological trends and may not capture potential disruptions from pandemics, conflicts, or major policy changes. The COVID-19 pandemic exemplified how rapidly global health trajectories can shift, with significant impacts on maternal health service delivery documented globally [39]. Additionally, our aggregate projections may mask important subnational variations where targeted interventions are typically implemented. We emphasize that these projections should be interpreted as baseline scenarios rather than deterministic forecasts, providing strategic guidance for resource planning while acknowledging inherent uncertainty in long-term health predictions.

Our study benefits from the comprehensive GBD 2021 database and the application of multiple complementary analytical approaches—including inequality assessment, decomposition analysis, and joinpoint regression—providing a multifaceted perspective on maternal health trends. The integration of both absolute and relative inequality measures represents a methodological advancement that addresses limitations identified in previous maternal health burden studies. Additionally, our temporal decomposition approach provides novel insights into the relative contributions of demographic versus epidemiological factors, which we believe offers valuable mechanistic understanding beyond traditional trend analysis.

However, several broader methodological limitations warrant acknowledgment. Data quality varies significantly across regions, with potential underreporting and misclassification particularly problematic in low-resource settings where vital registration systems may be incomplete—a challenge that continues to affect global health research despite ongoing improvements in data collection methodologies. The GBD modeling approach necessarily relies on statistical assumptions to address data gaps, which may introduce uncertainty in regional estimates, particularly for countries with limited health information systems [40]. Furthermore, while the SDI-based stratification provides useful global comparisons, we recognize that this composite measure may not fully capture the complexity of socioeconomic determinants affecting maternal health within countries, potentially masking important intranational variations that influence policy effectiveness.

Our findings have several important implications for global maternal health policy and practice. The persistence of relative inequalities despite overall progress suggests that current global health strategies, while beneficial, may require rebalancing to ensure more equitable benefit distribution.

Priority areas for intervention in low-SDI regions should include: strengthening healthcare infrastructure and workforce capacity; improving access to quality prenatal, delivery, and postnatal care; enhancing emergency obstetric services; and addressing broader determinants of maternal health including nutrition, education, and women’s empowerment.

The demographic challenges revealed by our decomposition analyses highlight the need for integrated approaches combining maternal health interventions with broader reproductive health and family planning services. Additionally, the identification of successful periods of burden reduction provides valuable lessons for scaling effective interventions.

Drawing from recent evidence and successful implementation models, several specific strategies warrant prioritization. The UNFPA Maternal and Newborn Health Fund demonstrates the potential effectiveness of comprehensive, catalytic financing approaches in scaling maternal health interventions. Since its inception, this fund has achieved substantial cumulative impacts: supporting 28 million women with safe delivery care, training 350,000 midwives, and establishing quality standards for over 1,500 midwifery schools globally. The fund’s success in redesigning emergency obstetric and newborn care networks across 14 Sub-Saharan countries offers a replicable model for health system strengthening. Particularly noteworthy is the multi-country implementation across Mozambique, Zambia, Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda, and Benin, which reached over 2 million people with sexual and reproductive health information while training 1,333 healthcare providers and establishing mentorship programs reaching 279 providers with quality improvement guidance [41]. Countries seeking to scale maternal health interventions should consider adopting similar integrated financing mechanisms that combine infrastructure development, workforce capacity building, service delivery improvement, and comprehensive support for survivors of maternal complications within unified programmatic frameworks.

Building upon these findings, several research priorities may warrant consideration. Methodologically, enhanced statistical approaches that better integrate multiple inequality measures while accounting for time-varying confounders might strengthen future analyses. Substantively, longitudinal studies examining individual-level maternal health trajectories across diverse socioeconomic contexts could potentially illuminate causal pathways driving observed disparities. Additionally, implementation research evaluating targeted interventions across different health system contexts might inform evidence-based policy development. However, careful evaluation of feasibility and validity would be essential before broader application.

Conclusion

This analysis provides important evidence of both progress and persistent challenges in global maternal health. While substantial reductions in maternal disorder burden demonstrate the effectiveness of coordinated global health efforts, persistent inequalities highlight the need for continued attention to equity in intervention design and implementation. Addressing these disparities will require sustained commitment to strengthening health systems in low-resource settings, alongside continued investment in research and development of effective maternal health interventions. Regular monitoring of both absolute and relative inequalities will be essential to ensure that future progress benefits all women, regardless of their socioeconomic circumstances.

Our projections through 2044, while acknowledging inherent limitations, suggest that continued progress in maternal health is achievable under current trajectories. However, the forecasted persistence of inequalities highlights that additional targeted efforts will be necessary to achieve truly equitable outcomes. The validation of our predictive models provides reasonable confidence in these baseline projections, though ongoing monitoring and model updates will be essential as new data become available.

The complex patterns revealed by our analyses underscore that achieving equitable maternal health outcomes requires nuanced, context-specific approaches rather than one-size-fits-all solutions. Continued collaboration between researchers, policymakers, and healthcare providers will be essential to translate these findings into meaningful improvements in maternal health outcomes globally.
