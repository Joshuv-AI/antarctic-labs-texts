The Christianson syndrome protein, sodium hydrogen exchanger isoform 6, is required for fat accumulation
Authors: Ruby Gupta, Allatah X Mekile, Rachana L Patnayak, Monish Ram Makena, Ray Wesley Bowman, Cory J White, Damaris N Lorenzo, Rajini Rao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractMutations in NHE6 (human gene name SLC9A6) lead to the rare X‐linked disorder Christianson syndrome (CS) characterized by intellectual disability, autism and ataxia. NHE6 is a Na+/H+ exchanger that regulates pH and ionic equilibria across endosomal membranes in a variety of tissues, although its non‐neuronal role is understudied. A common co‐morbidity in CS is low body weight, with body mass index in the 3–5th percentile range. In the present study, we use a Nhe6KO mouse model of CS to demonstrate a role for NHE6 in fat accumulation and glucose homeostasis. On both chow and a high‐fat diet, Nhe6KO mice displayed lower body weight and decreased fat accumulation in adipose tissue. By contrast, glycogen accumulation was strongly increased in liver of Nhe6KO mice on a high‐fat diet. Knockdown of NHE6 (NHE6 KD) in 3T3L1 adipocytes resulted in smaller lipid droplets, pointing to a cell autonomous role in fat accumulation. NHE6 KD adipocytes also exhibit dysregulation of insulin‐responsive uptake of fatty acids, glucose and Fe/transferrin, as well as the cell surface translocation of the corresponding transporters. Furthermore, we observed selective downregulation of key components of the insulin signalling pathway that could be restored using the Na+/H+ exchanger mimetic monensin or the V‐ATPase inhibitor bafilomycin, pointing to pH dysregulation as the underlying defect. These findings establish NHE6 as a novel contributor to energy metabolism with implications for CS patients. Key points NHE6 is an endosomal Na+/H+ exchanger mutated in Christianson syndrome. Nhe6 null mice accumulate less fat and weigh less than control animals.In adipocytes, NHE6 knockdown reduces insulin‐stimulated fatty acid and glucose uptake.NHE6 exerts proteostatic control over multiple components of the insulin signalling pathway. Keywords: endosomes, fat droplets, GLUT4, insulin, insulin receptor, Na+/H+ exchanger

Introduction

Endosomal pH is set by a balance of proton pump (V‐ATPase) and proton leak pathways. Dominant sources of proton ‘leak’ derive from the activity of endosomal Na+/H+ exchangers (eNHE), representing a distinct and evolutionarily ancient subtype of the superfamily of electroneutral Na+/H+ exchangers (Brett et al., 2005; Pedersen & Counillon 2019). In mammals, there are two eNHE isoforms, NHE6 and NHE9, distributed in the membranes of early and recycling endosomes where they modulate pHendo by replacing luminal protons with cations (Brett et al., 2002; Nakamura et al., 2005). We and others have shown that gain or loss of eNHE activity results in alkaline or acidic luminal pH, respectively, with significant and broad impact on brain tumour growth, neurodevelopment and neurodegeneration (Kondapalli et al., 2013, 2014, 2015; Pedersen & Counillon 2019). In humans, Christianson syndrome (CS) is a severe X‐linked neurological disorder resulting from mutations in NHE6 (SLC9A6) characterized by autism, intellectual disability and ataxia (Christianson et al., 1999; Gilfillan et al., 2008).

Despite a broad tissue distribution, the physiological role of NHE6 in non‐neurological systems is understudied. Low body weight (<3 percentile body mass index) is a common comorbidity observed in CS patients that could be caused by defects in glucose and fat metabolism in adipose and other tissues, or impairment of anabolic responses linked to insulin (Christianson et al., 1999; Gilfillan et al., 2008; Kavanaugh et al., 2024). We note that starvation upregulates eNHE expression in all organisms and model systems examined (Prasad & Rao, 2018b), although the functional significance of this observation is not understood. We showed that NHE6 is a target of the transcription factor cAMP‐response element‐binding protein (CREB), a known regulator of cellular responses to low‐nutrient conditions, and that NHE6 expression can be increased by pharmacological and epigenetic modulators targeting the CREB pathway (Prasad & Rao, 2018a, 2018b). These observations suggest that changes in endosomal pH regulation in response to nutritional status may be important for metabolic adaptation. However, this link has not been directly investigated in animal models and the underlying causal mechanisms are unknown.

Obesity and metabolic disorders have emerged as serious public health problems affecting an estimated 40% of the American population (Nianogo & Arah, 2022). Adipose tissue is central to energy metabolism as a hormone‐responsive fat reservoir and regulator of glucose homeostasis (Ishiki & Klip, 2005; Rosen & Spiegelman, 2006). In response to insulin, adipocytes rapidly mobilize storage vesicles that deliver nutrient transporters and receptors to the plasma membrane to clear post prandial glucose from the blood stream, promote fat storage and import Fe3+ for mitochondrial respiration (Bryant et al., 2002; Hilton et al., 2023; Klip et al., 2019). Essential steps in vesicle sorting, control of proteostasis and mitochondrial function occur within the lumen of these vesicles where pH homeostasis plays a critical role (Malikova et al., 2004; Shi & Kandror, 2007). Yet, the factors that regulate luminal ions are undefined and not mechanistically explored. For the first time, we have obtained compelling new data linking NHE6 to lipid and glucose metabolism in adipocytes that could lead to a better understanding and management of insulin resistance and obesity. We use both mouse models and adipocyte cultures to show that knockout or knockdown of NHE6 reduces insulin‐responsive fat accumulation and glucose uptake by selectively altering surface expression of fatty acid and glucose transporters.

Methods

Animals

All animal procedures (protocol #MO24M231) were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine and were carried out in accordance with institutional and national regulations governing the ethical use of animals in research. All protocols adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines for reporting animal research. The authors confirm that this work complies with the ethics checklist of The Journal of Physiology.

Mouse models

Mice with a targeted disruption in Slc9a6 (encodes Nhe6) (Jackson Laboratories Stock# 005843, strain name B6.129P2‐Slc9a65tm1Dgen) were obtained from Steven Walkley (Albert Einstein College of Medicine, Bronx, NY, USA). Breeding and genotyping were performed as previously described (Stromme et al., 2011). Because CS is an X‐linked disorder, male mice were used for metabolic studies to model patients. Histological analyses were performed on both male and female knockout mice, as specified where appropriate. Mice were housed under standard environmental conditions (12:12 h light/dark photocycle) with ad libitum access to water. Depending on the study design, mice were fed either standard rodent chow (#2018; Teklad, Indianapolis, IN, USA) or a high‐fat diet (D12492; 60% kcal from fat; Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, USA). For metabolic tolerance tests, mice were subjected to short‐term fasting periods in accordance with established institutional standard operating procedures. In studies involving indirect calorimetry or home cage monitoring, mice were evaluated under both fed and fasted conditions, including up to 24 h of food deprivation. This fasting paradigm was used to assess physiological responses to negative energy balance and to characterize metabolic adaptations during extended fasting. During fasting, animals were monitored visually and, for calorimetry studies, also via continuous readouts of oxygen consumption (V˙O2), carbon dioxide production (V˙CO2), respiratory exchange ratio (RER), energy expenditure (EE) and locomotor activity. Mice were weighed before fasting, at the end of the fasting period, and again after a 24 h refeeding period. Death was achieved using carbon dioxide inhalation in a compressed gas chamber setup approved by the institutional IACUC, followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary method.

Body composition analysis

Body composition measurements were performed to assess total fat mass, lean mass and total body water content using a quantitative magnetic resonance system (EchoMRI‐100; Echo Medical Systems, Waco, TX, USA).

Indirect calorimetry

Wild‐type (WT) and Slc9a6 knockout (Nhe6KO ) male mice were transferred to the Metabolic Core Facility at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine for IACUC‐approved studies, following Research Animal Resources (RAR) transfer approval. Mice were housed individually in ventilated cages and allowed to acclimate to the facility for a minimum of 3 days prior to the start of data collection. Body weight, food intake, physical activity and whole‐body metabolic parameters were assessed using an open‐flow indirect calorimetry system (Comprehensive Laboratory Animal Monitoring System, CLAMS; Columbus Instruments, Columbus, OH, USA), as described previously (Chen et al., 2025). Data were collected continuously for 6 days. The initial 3 days served as an acclimation period to the metabolic chambers, during which stabilization of body weight, food intake and diurnal metabolic rhythms was confirmed. Analyses were performed using data from all 6 days, which included a 24 h ad libitum fed period, a 24 h fasting period and a subsequent 24 h refeeding period. Rates of oxygen consumption (V˙O2; mL kg−1 h−1) and carbon dioxide production (V˙CO2; mL kg−1 h−1) were recorded every 24 min. RER was calculated as V˙CO2/V˙O2 using CLAMS, version 5.66 (Columbus Instruments) to estimate the relative contribution of carbohydrate (RER ∼1.0) and fat (RER ∼0.7) oxidation, with no correction for protein oxidation. Energy expenditure was computed as EE = V˙O2 × [3.815 + (1.232 × RER)] and normalized to lean body mass as measured by quantitative magnetic resonance (EchoMRI). Ambulatory physical activity was quantified by counting infrared beam breaks within the x‐ and y‐axes of the metabolic chambers.

Glucose tolerance test

Glucose tolerance tests (GTT) were conducted to assess systemic glucose handling. Mice were fasted for 6 h during the light phase of the diurnal cycle, with access to water. Fresh bedding (Alpha‐Dri or Diamond‐Dri; Shepherd Specialty Papers, Milford, NJ, USA) was used to minimize stress during the fasting period. At the end of the fast, baseline (fasted) blood glucose levels were measured. A sterile glucose solution was prepared by reconstituting d‐(+)‐glucose (Sigma‐Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) in 0.9% sterile saline (NaCl, 9 g L−1), followed by filtration through a 0.22 µm syringe filter. Mice were administered an i.p. injection of glucose at a dose of 1 mg g−1 body weight (5 µL g−1 body weight). Blood glucose was measured at baseline and at 15, 30, 60, 120 and 180 min post‐injection using a handheld glucometer (NovaMax Plus; Billerica, MA, USA). A small blood sample was obtained via tail‐tip sampling. The distal 0.5–1.0 mm of the tail was snipped using sterile surgical scissors after cleaning the site with 70% ethanol. At each time point, a drop of blood was gently expressed from the same tail site and applied directly to a glucometer test strip. Re‐sampling did not require repeated snipping because the wound typically remained open across time points, minimizing repeated injury.

Histology and image analysis

Brown adipose tissue (BAT), inguinal (subcutaneous) white adipose tissue (iWAT), gonadal (visceral) white adipose tissue (gWAT) and liver were dissected and fixed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin. Paraffin embedding, tissue sectioning and staining were performed by the Pathology Core Facility at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to assess tissue architecture and adipocyte morphology. Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining was used to visualize glycogen and carbohydrate‐rich structures in hepatocytes. Periodic acid–Schiff with diastase digestion (PASD) staining was used to differentiate glycogen‐specific staining by removing glycogen prior to PAS application. Images were acquired using a Keyence BZ‐X700 All‐in‐One fluorescence microscope (Keyence Corp., Itasca, IL, USA). Adipocyte cross‐sectional area was quantified in gWAT and iWAT, and lipid droplet area was assessed in liver tissue using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) (Schneider et al., 2012). For each tissue section, all adipocytes or hepatocytes within a single representative field of view at 40× magnification were analyzed per mouse.

Cell lines and culture

3T3‐L1 fibroblasts (gifted from the laboratory of Dr G. William Wong, The Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Baltimore, MD, USA), were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 10% bovine calf serum (Sigma‐Aldrich). Cells were always maintained at 30% confluence. Fibroblasts were differentiated into adipocytes using methods adapted from a protocol previously described (Zebisch et al., 2012). 3T3‐L1 fibroblasts containing stable expression of myc‐GLUT4‐mCherry (Lim et al., 2015; Lorenzo & Bennett, 2017) were maintained and differentiated as above. Mature 3T3‐L1 adipocytes were maintained in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma Aldrich) until used for experimentation.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription‐PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. For this, 1 µg of RNA was converted to cDNA using the High‐Capacity RNA to cDNA kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). For qRT‐PCR, 50 ng of cDNA was added to master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and probes Slc9a6 (Mm00555445_m1), Slc2a4 (Mm00436615_m1), Adipoq (Mm00456425_m1), Plin1 (Mm00558672_m1), Plin2 (Mm004757_m1), Slc9a9 (Mm00626012_m1), Gapdh (Mm99999915_g1), Insr (Mm01211875_m1) and Slc2a1 (Mm00441480_m1) as specified.

Lentiviral transduction

NHE6 shRNA sequence (5′‐CCGGCGTCCTAGTGCATGTCTCTGTTCAAGAGACAGAGACATGCACTAGGACTTTTTC‐3′) was designed to target 3′ untranslated region and previously validated (Prasad & Rao, 2015, 2018a). The non‐targeting shRNA sequence used as negative control was 5′‐CAACAAGATGAAGAGCACCAA‐3′ (Sigma‐Aldrich). Lentivirus was collected beginning 48 h post‐transfection and concentrated with Lenti‐X Concentrator (Takara Bio USA, Mountain View, CA, USA) for 3 consecutive days. Cells were transduced with lentivirus in Opti‐MEM Reduced Serum medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 48 h. For shRNA knockdown, cells were selected with puromycin at 10 µg mL−1, based on kill curve data for cell line. Knockdown was confirmed by qRT‐PCR.

Oil Red O staining

Mature adipocytes were washed twice with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated in 0.5% Oil Red O solution in 60% isopropanol (v/v) for 1 h at room temperature. Following two washes in PBS, cells remained in PBS at 4°C until imaged. Images were captured using a Cytation C10 microscope (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). For quantification, images were converted to greyscale and lipid droplets analyzed using ImageJ (NIH).

Immunofluorescence

Cell surface labelling

Mature adipocytes (differentiation day 8) were serum starved for 2 h at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cells were rinsed once in ice cold 1X Dulbecco's phosphate‐buffered saline (Quality Biological, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and fixed in ice cold 2% paraformaldehyde for 5 min at room temperature. Following a 5 min wash in PBS with 10 mm glycine, primary antibodies: anti‐c‐Myc (MA1‐980; Invitrogen) and anti‐HA (#3724; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA USA), were added to unpermeabilized cells at room temperature for 15 min. After subsequent washes in PBS 10 mm glycine, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (A11001; Invitrogen) and anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor 555 (A32727; Invitrogen) for 20 min at room temperature. Following washes in PBS with 10 mm glycine, glass coverslips were mounted to glass slides using Dako mounting medium (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and stored at 4°C until analysis.

Intracellular labelling

Mature adipocytes (differentiation day 8) were serum starved for 2 h. Cells were rinsed once in ice cold 1X Dulbecco's phosphate‐buffered saline (Quality Biological) and fixed in ice cold 2% paraformaldehyde for 5 min at room temperature. Following a 5 min wash in PBS with 10 mm glycine, cells were permeabilized using 0.1% saponin in PBS + 10 mm glycine at room temperature for 10 min. Primary antibody anti‐mCherry (E5D8F; Cell Signaling Technology) was added at room temperature for 15 min with washes in PBS 10 mm glycine to follow. Cells were incubated with secondary antibody anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor 633 (A21070; Invitrogen) for 20 min at room temperature. Following washes in PBS 10 mm glycine, glass coverslips were mounted to glass slides using DAKO mounting medium (Agilent) and stored at 4°C until analysis.

CD36 translocation

CD36 expression plasmid pCAGGS‐CD36‐V5×7‐GFP was obtained as a gift from Professor Takaya Satoh (Osaka Metropolitan University, Osaka, Japan) and introduced into adipocytes using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were then starved in serum free DMEM for 3 h, and then stimulated with basal or 100 nm insulin containing DMEM media for 20 min at 37°C. Post‐treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, and then incubated overnight at 4°C with an antibody against V5 epitope tag (A190‐119 A) purchased from Roche Applied Science (Penzberg, Germany) at a dilution of 1:100 without permeabilizing the cells. Cells were then incubated with anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor 633 (A21070; Invitrogen) to detect the anti‐V5 tag antibody for 1 h at rppm temperature. Cell nuclei were stained blue using 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole, washed with PBS and coverslips were mounted onto glass slides to image using confocal microscope. To estimate the relative translocation of CD36‐V5×7‐GFP to the plasma membrane, the ratio of fluorescence intensities of V5 tag to GFP within a region of interest was determined using ImageJ (NIH).

Microscopy and image quantification

Images were acquired using a LSM 700 laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with 63×/1.4 Oil DIC M27 objective and illumination lasers at 405, 488, 561 and 633 nm. CZI images were loaded into ZEN, version 2.3 (blue edition) (Zeiss) to process 2‐D data. To quantify surface localization, CZI images were loaded into Imaris, version 9.5.1 (Oxford Instruments, Oxford, UK). Plasma membrane renderings using the Surfaces function on 3‐D data were created followed by mean intensity value calculations. For 3‐D reconstructions, the Spots function was used to represent voxels at the cell surface. For co‐localization analysis, the Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to quantify overlap.

Fatty acid uptake assay

Fatty acid uptake was investigated using a fluorescence labelled fatty acid analog BODIPY™ FL C12 (4,4‐difluoro‐5,7‐dimethyl‐4‐bora‐3a,4a‐diaza‐s‐indacene‐3‐dodecanoic acid; Invitrogen). Briefly, adipocytes were incubated in DMEM media free of glucose and serum for 2 h and then stimulated with 100 nm insulin containing DMEM media for 20 min at 37°C. Control cells received no insulin. Following incubation, cells were stained with 2 µm BODIPY™ FL C12 for the specified time. For flow cytometry analysis, cells were washed with PBS and harvested. The cell pellets obtained were washed and resuspended in PBS to be immediately analyzed using a FACSymphony A3 flow cytometer (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). For confocal imaging, post staining the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were washed again with PBS, and coverslips were mounted on glass slides to image using a LSM 700 confocal microscope (Zeiss).

Electron microscopy

Cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 3 mm MgCl2 and 0.1 m sodium cacodylate buffer. After rinses in buffer, cells were post‐fixed for 1 h in 1% osmium tetroxide. Samples were rinsed in water then dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol followed by Embed 812 epoxy resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA). The next day, resin was changed out for fresh resin every 2 h, and then the plate was dried in an oven set at 60°C. After the resin (containing cells) was solidified, it was removed from the plate and trimmed to blocks for sectioning. Ultrathin (60–70 nm) sections were cut using a UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and collected on copper grids, followed by staining with UranyLess (UranyLess, Mauressac, France) and lead citrate. Transmission electron microscopy reagents were purchased from Electron Microscopy Sciences. Transmission electron microscopy imaging was performed using a Talos L120C (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 120 KV and acquired with a Ceta CMOS camera (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Glucose uptake assay

Mature adipocytes (differentiation day 8) were serum starved in DMEM for 2 h. Cells were incubated with 100 nm insulin in KRH buffer (140 mm NaCl, 5 mm KCl, 1.3 mm CaCl2, 1.2 mm KH2PO4, 2.5 mm MgSO4, 5 mm NaHCO3, 25 mm Hepes and 0.1% bovine serum albumin) for 20 min. Next, 2‐deoxy‐d‐[1,2‐3H] glucose (0.1 µCi; 0.2 mm) in KRH buffer containing 100 nm (or 0 nm insulin for no insulin controls) was added to each well for 10 min. After uptake, cells were washed three times in PBS and lysed overnight in NaOH. Radioactivity was measured by liquid scintillation counting.

Protein extraction and Western blotting

Cells were collected and lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Cell Signaling) and phosphatase inhibitors (sodium fluoride and sodium orthovanadate; Sigma‐Aldrich). After 30 min of incubation, cells were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 30 min. Supernatant was collected and passed through a 70 µm cell strainer to eliminate the debris. Protein quantification was performed using the bicinchoninic acid assay. Here, 20 µg of protein in each sample was resolved by electrophoresis using 4–12% Bis‐Tris gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Immunoblots were probed with antibodies (1:1000) from Cell Signaling Technologies, comprising insulin receptor‐β (23413S), Glut4 (2213S), P‐Akt (#4060P), Akt (#9272S) β‐actin (4967S) and sortilin (#20681S), as well as from Invitrogen, comprisng LRP‐1 (#PA5‐81212), GAPDH (#MA5‐15738) and Abclonal FATP1 (#NA12847), followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Proteins were visualized using chemiluminescence substrate. Blots were analyzed using ImageJ (NIH).

Acridine orange fluorescence

Here, 1 mg mL−1 Acridine Orange (AO) (Sigma‐Aldrich) stock solution was made in water and stored at 4°C. Just prior to imaging, 10 µm AO was added directly to the cell culture media and incubated for 10 min at 37°C. Post‐incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and then imaged directly using a SoRA SDC microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). To further evaluate the effect of pH, cells were also treated with 25 nm bafilomycin (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA) for 2 h before addition of AO and imaged as above.

Rescue of compartmental pH

Mature control or NHE6 knockdown (KD) adipocytes were incubated with either 1 µm monensin (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 16 h or 25 nm bafilomycin (InvivoGen) for 2 h. Following incubation, adipocytes were collected and total protein was extracted.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism, version 9.3.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). An unpaired Student's two‐tailed t test or ordinary one‐way ANOVA analysis was used to determine statistical significance of the data. Graphs show the mean ± SD (n = 3 or as specified in figure legend). Each data point shown is an average of three technical replicates. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

NHE6 null mice show age‐dependent reduction of body fat

Although similar in birth weight, Nhe6KO mice appeared smaller than their WT counterparts as adults. We monitored body weight, food intake, physical activity and whole‐body metabolic parameters using an open‐flow indirect calorimetry system, and measured fat, lean and water mass using EchoMRI as described in the Methods. At months of age, there was no significant difference in body weight or body fat between the two groups of mice fed on a standard (chow) diet (Fig. 1A–C ). Food intake was similar and differences in activity or energy expenditure did not rise to significance (Fig. 1D and E ). However, some differences became apparent as the animals aged. By 6–7 months of age, Nhe6KO mice weighed less and had lower fat mass than control animals of the same age and sex (Fig. 1F–H ). However, lean mass and total body water were unchanged (not shown). Food intake remained the same, although the Nhe6KO mice showed a larger range in energy expenditure (Fig. 1I and J ). Similarly, a previous study reported decreased bone weight and differences in trabecular structure in the vertebrae of Nhe6KO mice at 6 months but not at 3 months of age, although body weight differences were not observed (Schnyder et al., 2021).

Histological analysis by H&E staining was performed on adipose tissue isolated from age and sex matched mice. Although BAT has low fat content, lipid droplets were clearly visible in tissue sections from WT mice (Fig. 2A ). By contrast, lipid droplets were small and scarce in BAT of Nhe6KO mice, as confirmed by quantification of the droplet area (Fig. 2B ). In white adipose tissue (WAT), the lipid droplet is large, unilocular and equivalent to adipocyte cell size as seen in representative H&E stains of inguinal and gonadal WAT (Fig. 2A ). WAT adipocyte size increases with obesity and is accompanied by comorbidities such as dyslipidemia and diabetes. Thus, it is noteworthy that lipid droplet size, on average, was significantly smaller in inguinal (Fig. 2C ) and gonadal WAT (Fig. 2D ) from Nhe6KO mice.

Next, we considered whether the lower body weight in Nhe6KO mice could be compensated by a high‐fat diet (HFD) in which 60% of calories are derived from fat. Accordingly, 5‐month‐old mice were placed on HFD for 15 weeks and body weight was monitored weekly. Weight gain in control animals was significantly greater than in the Nhe6KO mice (Fig. 3A , inset), culminating in greater body weight and fat weight at 9 months (Fig. 3B and C ). Brown and white adipose tissue isolated from Nhe6KO animals weighed less than those from control (Fig. 3D–F ), as did the liver (Fig. 3G ). However, skeletal muscle and brain mass were not different (Fig. 3H and I ). H&E stains of brown and white fat (Fig. 4A ) showed significantly smaller lipid droplets (Fig. 4B–D ) in knockout mice. In summary, loss of NHE6 leads to a global decrease in fat accumulation that persists with HFD‐feeding.

Nhe6 null mice show aberrant glycogen stores in liver and muscle

We considered whether the robust effects of NHE6 on body weight and fat mass were associated with changes in diet‐related glycogen storage and systemic glucose regulation. Adipose tissue is the major fat depot, whereas skeletal muscle and liver are responsible for the bulk of post‐prandial glucose clearance, which is stored as glycogen. H&E stains of skeletal muscle in both chow and HFD fed mice were similar in control and Nhe6KO animals (Fig. 5A ). To reveal glycogen content, tissues were stained with periodic acid‐Schiff in the absence (PAS) or presence of diastase (PASD). Glycogen stores appeared to be modestly reduced in skeletal muscle of Nhe6KO mice on HFD, relative to control animals (Fig. 5A ). H&E staining of liver was similar in both groups of animals in chow diet but revealed visibly reduced fat accumulation in Nhe6KO compared to control in HFD (Fig. 5B ). Unexpectedly, glycogen stores in liver of Nhe6KO animals showed diet‐dependent differences from control, being visually lower in chow but higher in HFD (Fig. 5B ). This reversal prompted the evaluation of serum glucose levels in both groups of animals as described in the Methods. Baseline fasted levels of serum glucose was significantly higher in Nhe6KO compared to control animals fed on chow (Fig. 5C ), but lower in HFD‐fed Nhe6KO mice (Fig. 5D ). Following i.p. injection, glucose clearance was similar in both Nhe6KO and control, but peak serum levels were lower in Nhe6KO animals on HFD. Although not substantially different, glucose handling showed diet‐dependent alteration in Nhe6KO animals that correlated with changes in liver glycogen.

In liver, glucose transporter isoform 2 (GLUT2) is the glucose transporter that facilitates bi‐directional transport of glucose, enabling both glucose uptake for storage and release into the bloodstream. A HFD can significantly impact glycogen storage and GLUT2 expression (Jiang et al., 2019; Kim et al., 1995). We observed diet‐dependent changes in GLUT2 expression in liver tissue, with significant decrease in Nhe6KO under a HFD (Fig. 5E–H ). Downregulation of GLUT2 can impede both glycogen synthesis and mobilization, with severe deficiency leading to excessive liver glycogen in Fanconi–Bickel syndrome. Thus, our observations require more detailed investigation but point to a role for NHE6 in GLUT2 expression and liver glycogen storage.

Differentiated adipocytes selectively upregulate Nhe6

To evaluate cell autonomous roles of NHE6 on lipid and glucose accumulation and insulin regulation, 3T3‐L1 fibroblasts were differentiated using an adipogenic cocktail (Zebisch et al., 2012). Successful differentiation into adipocytes was confirmed by Oil Red O staining of cells (Fig. 6A ) and mRNA induction of canonical adipocyte genes Slc2a4 (GLUT4), Adipoq (Adiponectin), Plin1 (Perilipin‐1) and Plin‐2 (Perilipin‐2) (Fig. 6B ). Adipocyte differentiation was accompanied by an almost three‐fold increase in NHE6 transcript (Fig. 6C ). Although the magnitude of NHE6 increase is not on the same scale as the adipocyte markers, the high rate of ion exchange (∼1500 ion s−1) associated with this class of transporter (Lee et al., 2013) means that small changes in expression can have large consequences in the limited microenvironment of the endosomal compartment. By contrast, transcript levels of the closely related NHE9 isoform decreased by ∼50% upon adipocyte differentiation (Fig. 6D ) suggesting an isoform‐selective role for NHE6 in adipocyte function.

NHE6 knockdown reduces insulin‐dependent fat accumulation in adipocytes

We used either shRNA or siRNA to knockdown (KD) NHE6 in 3T3‐L1 cells, either before or after differentiation into adipocytes. In all cases, we confirmed that transcript levels of NHE6 decreased whereas NHE9 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 6E and F ). Similarly, expression of adipocyte markers was unchanged in NHE6 KD cells, except for a 1.5‐fold increase transcript levels for adiponectin, which is known to drive lipogenesis (Fig. 6G ). Despite the increase in adiponectin, Oil Red O staining of fat droplets was diminished in NHE6 KD adipocytes (Fig. 6H and I ). Electron microscopy confirmed that fat droplets were smaller and fewer in NHE6 KD adipocytes (Fig. 6J and K ). Thus, the in vivo findings from fat tissue in Nhe6KO mice were reproduced by NHE6 KD in 3T3‐L1 adipocytes, pointing to a cell autonomous effect.

Reductions in lipid content in NHE6 KD adipocytes could be the result of alterations in fatty acid uptake. Therefore, we monitored uptake of BODIPY‐labelled C12 fatty acid (2 µm for 15 min) in 3T3‐L1 adipocytes by confocal fluorescence microscopy. Mean fluorescence intensity in NHE6 KD was significantly diminished compared to control cells (Fig. 7A and B ). Insulin plays a crucial role in stimulating fatty acid uptake into adipocytes, driving the accumulation of fat. As expected, insulin treatment (100 nm for 20 min) increased fatty acid uptake in control adipocytes. However, fatty acid uptake in NHE6 KD adipocytes was not significantly elevated. Similar results were obtained by flow cytometry analysis of BODIPY C12 fluorescence. We observed time‐dependent fluorescence increase (15 min and 1 h) in both control and NHE6 KD adipocytes (Fig. 7C and D ). Cell associated BODIPY C12 fluorescence was lower for NHE6 KD, as seen by the left shift in peak distribution, with the difference increasing at the longer time point (Fig. 7E and F ). The percentage of cells showing high BODIPY C12 fluorescence (>2000 a.u.) was lower for NHE6 KD under all conditions (Fig. 7G ).

CD36 is a scavenger receptor that shuttles between endosomes and the cell surface where it facilitates uptake of long chain fatty acid into adipocytes. The rate of fatty acid uptake is increased by insulin‐stimulated translocation of CD36 to the plasma membrane (Glatz & Luiken, 2018). We used a dual labelled construct of CD36 with exofacial V5 tag and cytoplasmic GFP (Fig. 7H ) transiently expressed in 3T3L1 adipocytes to quantify CD36 translocation in unpermeabilized cells, as previously reported (Chan et al., 2024). Basal levels of surface to total fluorescence ratio in tagged CD36 were similar in control and NHE6 KD cells (Fig. 7I and J ). Insulin treatment elicited cell surface translocation of CD36 in control cells but not in NHE6 KD, as seen by representative confocal images, as well as the V5/GFP fluorescence ratio (Fig. 7I and J ). In summary, fatty acid uptake is decreased and less responsive to insulin following the knockdown of NHE6.

Insulin treatment increases NHE6 on the plasma membrane

A proteomic analysis of the adipocyte plasma membrane revealed NHE6 as a novel insulin‐responsive protein (Prior et al., 2011). As a first step toward probing the role of NHE6 in adipocytes, we compared the cellular localization of NHE6 under basal conditions and following insulin treatment. 3T3‐L1 cells with stable expression of NHE6 containing an external triple‐HA tag and cytoplasmic C‐terminal GFP marker (Fig. 8A ) were antibody labelled without permeabilization. NHE6 has been localized to early and recycling endosomes (Zhao et al., 2016), although a minor component may localize to the plasma membrane (Brett et al., 2002; Hill et al., 2006). Consistent with these findings, localization of NHE6‐GFP in 3T3‐L1 adipocytes was intracellular under basal conditions (Fig. 8B ). In the presence of insulin, there was a 2.5‐fold increase in cell surface NHE6 as revealed by punctate labelling of the extracellular HA epitope (Fig. 8B and C ). 3‐D visualization of confocal microscopy images reveals a small population of NHE6 that becomes surface membrane‐associated in response to insulin treatment (Fig. 8D ). The partial enrichment of NHE6 at the cell surface of insulin‐treated adipocytes that we observe closely replicates previous findings (Prior et al., 2011).

NHE6 colocalizes with GLUT4

A consequence of insulin signalling is the insulin‐dependent increase of selected endosomal proteins at the cell surface (Larance et al., 2005; Piper et al., 1991; Tanner & Lienhard, 1987; Volchuk et al., 1995; Wardzala et al., 1984), most prominently the glucose transporter isoform 4 (GLUT4). In the absence of insulin, GLUT4 is distributed between endosomes, trans‐Golgi network, and a heterogeneous mix of endosomal sorting intermediates and specialized GLUT4 storage vesicles (Leto & Saltiel, 2012). We investigated co‐localization of NHE6 and GLUT4 under basal and insulin‐stimulated conditions in 3T3‐L1 adipocytes stably transfected with a cMyc‐GLUT4‐mCherry construct (Fig. 8E ) (Lim et al., 2015; Lorenzo & Bennett, 2017). We observed partial co‐localization of NHE6‐GFP and GLUT4‐mCherry signals in the basal state (Pearson's correlation = 0.32) (Fig. 8F and G ) consistent with a previous report (Prior et al., 2011). Following insulin treatment, the extent of co‐localization remained the same (Fig. 8F and H ). This finding is consistent with translocation of the co‐localized intracellular pool of NHE6‐GFP and GLUT4‐mCherry to the plasma membrane.

NHE6 knockdown decreases insulin‐stimulated surface translocation of GLUT4

Previous studies in neurons and astrocytes showed that NHE6 regulates cell surface expression of transmembrane receptors and transporters (Ouyang et al., 2013; Prasad & Rao, 2018a). In adipocytes, basal surface expression of Myc‐GLUT4‐mCherry, in the absence of insulin, is low both in control and NHE6 KD cells (Fig. 9A ). Treatment with insulin (100 nm for 30 min) elicited robust translocation of cMyc‐GLUT4‐mCherry to the plasma membrane in control cells; however, NHE6 knockdown drastically reduced GLUT4 surface expression relative to control (Fig. 9B and E ). Insulin‐independent translocation of GLUT4 in response to metformin (1 mm metformin for 24 h) was also reduced, albeit less drastically (Fig. 9C and E ). Metformin has been reported to enhance insulin sensitivity in adipocytes by increasing the surface expression of GLUT4. However, metformin did not enhance plasma membrane GLUT4 in NHE6 KD adipocytes treated with insulin, pointing to a common point of disruption in the GLUT4 translocation pathway (Fig. 9D and E ).

Insulin enhances cell surface translocation of other membrane proteins, including the transferrin receptor (TfnR) (Tanner & Lienhard, 1987). Surface levels of TfnR under basal conditions, revealed by the binding of fluorescent transferrin (Tfn‐488) to non‐permeabilized adipocytes, were not significantly decreased in NHE6 KD cells, whereas insulin‐mediated increase was observed only in control cells (Fig. 9F–H ). Together, these findings indicate a broad requirement for NHE6 in the surface translocation of multiple insulin‐responsive proteins.

NHE6 knockdown alters glucose uptake in adipocytes

The reduced insulin responsive translocation of GLUT4 in NHE6 KD adipocytes suggested defective activation of the insulin signal transduction pathway. The serine/threonine kinase Akt is a key effector of insulin‐stimulated GLUT4 translocation (Fig. 10A ) (Kohn et al., 1998). Akt activation alone is sufficient to stimulate GLUT4 translocation in the absence of insulin (Ng et al., 2008). We found that phosphorylated Akt (p‐AktSer473) levels were reduced by 67% in insulin‐treated NHE6 KD adipocytes without a change in total Akt protein levels (Fig. 10B and C ). We conclude that the activation of this branch of the insulin signalling pathway is impaired in the absence of NHE6.

Insulin‐responsive 2‐deoxy‐d‐[1,2‐3H] glucose (2‐DG) uptake was monitored in control and NHE6 knockdown 3T3‐L1 adipocytes (Fig. 10D ). In control adipocytes, 2‐DG uptake was increased seven‐fold following acute treatment with insulin, as previously reported (Bryant et al., 2002). By contrast, only a two‐fold increase in 2‐DG uptake was observed in NHE6 KD adipocytes (Fig. 10D ), consistent with reduced insulin responsive signalling and surface expression of GLUT4 (Fig. 9B and E ). Unexpectedly, basal uptake of 2‐DG, in the absence of insulin, was increased four‐fold in NHE6 KD adipocytes. Because basal surface expression of GLUT4 was unaltered in these cells (Fig. 9A ), these observations suggest compensatory increase in other glucose uptake mechanisms. One probable mechanism may involve upregulation of the GLUT1 glucose transporter. Although mRNA levels of GLUT1 were unchanged (Fig. 10E ), protein expression of GLUT1 was increased in NHE6 KD adipocytes (Fig. 10F and G ). Thus, NHE6 has a selective effect on insulin stimulated surface trafficking of GLUT4. It is possible that other glucose transporters such as GLUT8 and SGLT2 (Scheepers et al., 2001; Xu et al., 2021) could also be upregulated in the absence of NHE6 and contribute to basal glucose uptake observed in 3T3L1 adipocytes.

NHE6 regulates proteostasis in adipocytes

In addition to defective insulin signalling, the drastic reduction in surface expression of GLUT4 resulting from NHE6 knockdown could also be a result of reduced GLUT4 expression. We show that NHE6 KD 3T3‐L1 adipocytes express 44% less GLUT4 under basal conditions (Fig. 11A and B ) despite no significant change in GLUT4 transcript (Fig. 11D ). GLUT4 exists in discrete organellar pools within the cell, with ∼40% of the total residing in the endo‐lysosomal system (Bryant et al., 2002; Thong et al., 2005), suggesting that NHE6 regulates the itinerary of GLUT4 through this pool. NHE6 KD adipocytes also showed a 30% reduction in protein levels of the insulin receptor‐β (IR‐β) subunit (Fig. 11A and C ), even though transcript levels were increased by 24% (Fig. 11E ). Thus, reduction of GLUT4 and IR‐β proteins are a post‐transcriptional effect of NHE6 on the GLUT4 pathway.

Next, we considered whether other membrane protein components of the GLUT4 storage vesicles (GSV) (Fig. 11F ) also show altered protein expression in response to NHE6 KD. One key component of GSVs is sortilin, a Vps10 domain containing protein that interacts with GLUT4 within the endosomal lumen and recruits components of the retromer (GGA1) and clathrin with its cytoplasmic tail (Shi & Kandror, 2005). Notably, GGA1 was reported to retain NHE6 in endosomes via an interaction with the NHE6 cytoplasmic tail (Ma et al., 2024). A proteomic analysis of GSVs revealed low density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1 (LRP1) to be another major, insulin‐responsive component (Jedrychowski et al., 2010). We found that NHE6 knockdown decreased protein levels of both sortilin (Fig. 11G and H ) and LRP1 (Fig. 11I and J ).

Fat accumulation requires uptake of long chain fatty acids through insulin‐regulated fatty acid transporters, including FATP1 in 3T3L1 adipocytes (Stahl et al., 2002). Here, we show that protein levels of FATP1 are reduced in NHE6 KD adipocytes (Fig. 11K and L ), consistent with reduced uptake of BODIPY C12 fatty acid shown in Fig. 7. Taken together, our data suggest that NHE6 exerts proteostatic control over multiple components in the insulin responsive pathway in adipocytes.

Chemical bypass of NHE6

The exchange of luminal protons for cations by the transport activity of NHE6 counters the proton pumping activity of the V‐ATPase to establish the pH balance of the endosome (Kondapalli et al., 2014; Prasad & Rao, 2015). Thus, loss of NHE6 results in hyper‐acidification of endosomal lumen by 1–2 pH units (Ouyang et al., 2013; Prasad & Rao, 2015). In adipocytes, NHE6 KD hyper‐acidified endo‐lysosomal compartments as indicated by increased acridine orange fluorescence (Brett et al., 2011) (Fig. 12A and B ). Treatment with the V‐ATPase inhibitor bafilomycin should abolish H+ pumping and alkalinize the endo‐lysosomal compartments. This is demonstrated by the disappearance of fluorescent puncta, resulting in diffused cytoplasmic distribution of acridine orange (Fig. 12C ).

Lysosomal turnover of proteins requires acidic compartmental pH. Treatment with bafilomycin treatment restored insulin receptor (Fig. 12D and E ) and LRP1 protein (Fig. 12F and G ) in NHE6 KD adipocytes to levels similar to control, as seen by western blot analysis. The ionophore monensin is a chemical mimetic of Na+/H+ exchangers and has been shown to block lysosomal delivery of cargo and reroute proteins to the cell surface (Muro et al., 2008), effectively reversing the effect of NHE6 KD in many cell types (Ko et al., 2022; Prasad & Rao, 2015, 2018a). Addition of monensin (1 µm for 16 h) to NHE6 KD adipocytes was sufficient to restore insulin receptor IRβ (Fig. 12H and I ) and GLUT4 protein (Fig. 12J and K ) to levels similar to control. These findings point to the role of NHE6 in regulating proteostasis of the insulin‐responsive pathway.

Discussion

NHE6 selectively regulates trafficking and turnover of insulin‐responsive cargo

Endosomal pH plays a key role in regulating the trafficking and turnover of plasma membrane receptors and transporters. Our data point to multiple steps involved in adipocyte insulin‐stimulated glucose and fat uptake that are affected by loss of NHE6, as summarized by the schematic in Fig. 13. Diminished activation of the insulin signalling pathway, as indicated by decreased phosphorylation of Akt, results in reduced exocytosis and delivery of cargo from the pool of insulin‐responsive storage vesicles to the plasma membrane, lowering uptake of glucose and fatty acids. Endosomal pH also directly impacts protein degradation by regulating the activity of luminal proteases and indirectly, by affecting trafficking to the lysosome. Hyper‐acidification of the endo‐lysosomal pathway, as a result of the loss of NHE6, enhanced the degradation of FATP1, GLUT4 and other components of GLUT4 storage vesicles such as LRP1 and sortilin. Consistent with the role of endo‐lysosomal pH, GLUT4 stability can be restored by agents that are known to alleviate hyperacidic compartmental pH (Prasad & Rao, 2015, 2018a, 2018b), such as monensin, a chemical mimetic for Na+/H+ exchange, and bafilomycin, a H+‐ATPase inhibitor, pointing to lysosomal turnover of cargo. We note that, in prior work, we have ruled out a role for the proteosome in endosomal NHE‐mediated protein turnover (Ko et al., 2022). The severe reduction in surface expression of GLUT4 in NHE6 KD adipocytes may be caused by a combined effect of reduced activation of the insulin signalling pathway (resulting from reduced expression of insulin receptor and Akt phosphorylation) and enhanced degradation of GLUT4 protein, although their relative contribution is not known. Intriguingly, NHE6 appears to selectively target the insulin responsive pathway, whereas levels of GLUT1 increased in the absence of NHE6, probably as compensation. We expect variable proteostasis susceptibility of vesicular cargo to NHE6 activity depending on the extent of co‐localization, compensatory effects of other pH regulators (such as NHE9 and PAC) and individual differences in protein half‐life. A comprehensive cataloging of proteins for which turnover could be altered by NHE6 may be required to better understand selectivity.

Role of NHE6 in adipocyte insulin response

Schematic shows the key steps in insulin‐mediated glucose and fatty acid uptake in adipocytes. Binding of insulin to its receptor (Step 1) activates downstream signalling pathways (Step 2) that result in exocytosis of endosomes including GLUT4 storage vesicles (Step 3) to the adipocyte membrane for rapid clearance of extracellular glucose, fatty acids and other nutrients (Step 4). The absence of NHE6 is accompanied by hyper‐acidic endosomal lumen (shaded red), increased degradation of GLUT4, insulin receptor and other cargo resulting in downregulation of key steps (Steps 1–4) in the insulin responsive pathway.

In pre‐adipocytes, both ectopically and endogenously expressed GLUT4 localizes to endosomes with a half‐life of only 2 h (Shi & Kandror, 2005). However, in differentiated adipocytes, GLUT4 is sorted into small (<100 nm) GLUT4 storage vesicles that represent the major insulin‐responsive compartment. This not only increases its insulin responsiveness, but also protects GLUT4 from lysosomal degradation. What controls this proteostatic switch? We suggest that induction of NHE6 upon adipocyte differentiation counters the acidification of lumen pH by V‐ATPase and protects endosomal cargo from lysosomal degradation.

NHE6 alters glucose handling and glycogen storage

Failure in insulin control of glucose transport is a chief component underlying insulin resistance in type 2 diabetes (Garvey et al., 1992; Klip et al., 2019). GLUT4 gene variants are rare (Stockli et al., 2011); therefore, understanding GLUT4 proteostasis could be key to our knowledge of metabolic disorders. GLUT4 expression is reduced in obese, prediabetic, insulin resistant and diabetic individuals (Cignarelli et al., 2019; Klip et al., 2019) and, based on the results from the present study, may be impacted by expression and activity of NHE6.

Biguanide drugs such as metformin are widely used to treat diabetes and work, in part, by increasing glucose uptake into adipose tissue (Kim & You, 2017). Specifically, metformin increases insulin‐independent plasma membrane expression of GLUT4 in adipocytes under basal conditions (Grisouard et al., 2010). Intriguing findings from worm and fly models show that eNHE mutants are resistant to metformin, probably through dysregulation of autophagy (Kim et al., 2016). Our observations revealed that metformin does not restore surface expression of GLUT4 in NHE6 knockdown adipocytes. Thus, similar to observations in C. elegans, NHE6 may be required for the ameliorating effect of metformin on GLUT4 in mammals. Identifying the factors that influence GLUT4 expression and trafficking could lead to a better understanding of insulin resistance in diabetes which is essential for more effective management of this disease.

In mouse models, heterozygous knockout of GLUT4 and conditional deletion in adipose tissue or skeletal muscle results in insulin resistance and propensity to develop diabetes (Abel et al., 2001; Rossetti et al., 1997). Intriguingly, fat‐specific insulin receptor knockout mice exhibit increased lean body mass and protection from glucose intolerance (Bluher et al., 2002; Minokoshi et al., 2003; Stenbit et al., 1997). Given our observation that adipocyte NHE6 KD decreased both GLUT4 and insulin receptor abundance, conferring insulin resistance at a cellular level, the glucose handling in whole body NHE6 knockout animals is difficult to interpret. Nhe6 null mice have lower body weight relative to control mice, but the same lean mass. Because mice were dosed per total body weight, Nhe6 knockout mice receive effectively less glucose than WT mice per lean mass. This may mask glucose intolerance, particularly in chow‐fed mice. Lack of a clear effect on glucose clearance could also point to opposing phenotypes from downregulation of both GLUT4 and insulin receptor in Nhe6KO animals and may require tissue‐specific knockouts and more careful analysis of glucose and insulin tolerance to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Implications for CS

The Nhe6KO mouse model of CS has been a useful tool to understand many of the pathophysiological effects of NHE6 loss. Similar to patients, the model shows a combination of neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental features, including delayed brain growth and Purkinje cell loss (Xu et al., 2017), altered nociceptor excitability and hyposensitivity to pain (Petitjean et al., 2020). Except for a report on skeletal abnormalities (Schnyder et al., 2021), there is a lack of research on non‐neuronal phenotypes that could inform on CS pathology. In this context, our observations relating to age‐dependent fat accumulation defects in the Nhe6KO mouse phenocopy the age‐dependent low body mass index comorbid with CS (Christianson et al., 1999; Gilfillan et al., 2008; Schroer et al., 2010), and reveal potential problems with insulin responsiveness, fat and glucose handling in patients. In part, these defects are cell autonomous, and the underlying dysregulation of proteostasis probably contributes to lower fat accumulation. However, tissue‐specific dysregulation of glucose or fat uptake (e.g. in skeletal muscle or intestine) may also be regulated by NHE6 and warrants further investigation. Another example relates to the CNS, where GLUT4 is enriched at active synapses to support energy demands during sustained activity (Ashrafi et al., 2017). Therefore, a question of interest is whether NHE6 regulates glucose uptake at active synapses.

In conclusion, the present study makes the case for further physiological studies of NHE6 in non‐neurological systems, in line with the ubiquitous tissue distribution and broad functional role of endosomal Na+/H+ exchange. We show data leading to a testable hypothesis of a mechanism linking endosomal pH to glucose and energy metabolism in adipocytes, offering new insights into obesity and insulin resistance that should spur additional studies in the future.
