Understanding bidirectional and transactional processes of child eating behaviours and parental feeding practices explaining poor health outcomes across infancy and early childhood in Australia: protocol for the Longitudinal Assessment of Children’s Eating (LACE) study
Authors: Alissa J Burnett, Katherine L Downing, Catherine G Russell
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionChild eating behaviours develop through interactions between the child’s characteristics, psychological factors and the child’s social environment and this affects the child’s diet and weight. To examine the currently existing birth cohort studies examining child eating behaviours, a review was conducted. There are currently no birth cohorts that concurrently examine child eating behaviours, dietary intake, growth and parental feeding practices from birth into early childhood. Therefore, the primary objective of the Longitudinal Assessment of Children’s Eating (LACE) study is to examine the bidirectional and transactional processes of child eating behaviours and parental feeding practices explaining poor dietary intake and excess weight across infancy and early childhood.Methods and analysisThe LACE study will be a prospective, longitudinal parent-reported study following infants from younger than 4 months of age across nine waves of data collection: younger than 4 months, 4 months, 6 months, 9 months, 12 months, 18 months, 2 years, 3 years and 5 years. Participants will be included if they are parents of infants younger than 4 months, 18 years or older, fluent in English and living in Australia at baseline. A sample size of 1210 is proposed. Participants will be recruited online via paid social media (Facebook and Instagram) advertisements. The study will examine child eating behaviours, body mass index Z-score, dietary intake, screen time, temperament, parent feeding practices and styles, and demographics. The data will be obtained using the online survey software Qualtrics. Data analyses will be conducted using Stata.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was granted by the Deakin University Human Ethics Advisory Group, Faculty of Health (HEAG-H 120_2022). The findings from this study will be disseminated via presentations at scientific conferences and published manuscripts in peer-reviewed journals. Findings will be disseminated to the general public via mainstream media and to participants of the study with a summary of the findings.Keywords: epidemiology, obesity, public health

Introduction

The dietary intake of children in Australia is suboptimal, with preschool children consuming one-third of their daily kilojoules from energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods and 72% of 2–3 years not meeting the vegetable intake recommendations.1 Suboptimal diets lead to overweight and obesity, with one in four children reported to be overweight or obese.2 Excess weight is shown to track into adolescence and adulthood,3 thereby increasing risk of later chronic disease and affecting quality of life.4,6 Preventing childhood overweight and obesity is, therefore, of high importance to public health. However, presently, efforts to prevent childhood obesity through the promotion of healthy diets have been met with limited success.7 From the evidence reviewed, there were only a few studies of high quality that concluded interventions were effective in showing any sustained impact.7 Children’s eating behaviours (ie, individual differences in patterns of behaviours and attitudes related to food and eating) are potentially malleable precursors to dietary intakes and weight that are shaped during childhood, yet have seldom been the target of child obesity-prevention interventions, as most interventions focus on the behaviours of parents.7 Understanding how and why child eating behaviours develop and affect children’s diets and weight will provide information to inform novel obesity-prevention interventions.

Biopsychosocial process models, such as the biopsychosocial model of child eating and weight,8 and the Chawner and Hetherington model of child vegetable intake9 provide a framework for understanding how child eating behaviours develop. Such models highlight the importance of understanding interactions between the child’s biological characteristics (eg, temperament, genetics), psychological factors (eg, parent cognitions, feeding practices) and the child’s social environment (eg, family food insecurity). These models emphasise the processes and pathways underpinning the development of eating behaviours and how they relate to child diet and weight outcomes. Understanding how these processes and pathways causally link early characteristics and behaviours with later outcomes allows for interventions to be more effectively targeted to unique child/parent dyads.810,14

There are several biological, psychological and social factors that have been identified as playing a role in the development of children’s eating behaviours. Important biological factors include temperament, genetics and neurocognitive functioning.10 Psychological factors include both the characteristics of parents and children and their behaviours, particularly parent feeding practices (ie, strategies that a parent engages in to influence their child’s dietary intake15). Feeding practices associated with diet and weight outcomes include pressure to eat, overt restriction and the use of screens during feeding.16,19 Also important are parenting styles (parenting behaviours and practices which influence the development of a child20 21) and parent/child cognitions (thoughts about food and eating such as parent concern for child eating10). Parent cognitions and styles are expected to affect their behaviours (feeding practices). Parent concerns about child eating will be affected by the child’s biological characteristics, as well as the child’s weight and eating behaviours and will also affect child weight and eating behaviours via transactional effects.10 Key social factors include the sociodemographic characteristics of families including parent education, family income and area-level disadvantage, which have been associated with child eating behaviours, diets and weight.10 While many biopsychosocial factors have been linked to the development of children’s eating behaviours and their links to diet and weight outcomes, the processes explaining how these factors causally affect child dietary intake and child overweight and obesity are still unknown.

Previous studies suggest that there are unique parent–child interactions of reciprocal influence,22 23 which evolve over time, with earlier experiences affecting later ones.1424,31 However, while progress is being made in understanding bidirectional processes in child eating and weight1424,31 understanding of these transactional processes is limited. Transactional processes may have their origins in infancy8 and result in different pathways to child dietary intake and weight status. It is essential that studies should begin in infancy and track children over time using developmentally appropriate repeated measures of key variables. This will increase our understanding of the origins of the processes and how they progress over time with the child and parents influencing each other in reciprocal ways and help inform the development of early preventative intervention programmes rather than corrective programmes later in life.

Effective and appropriate measurement of key variables is also central to understanding how children’s eating behaviours develop and influence diet and weight. There are a number of considerations in selecting the appropriate measures including the sample and informant characteristics, the use of questionnaire or other measurement instruments/procedures, whether measures are developmentally appropriate and how reliable and valid the measures are in the selected sample.32 While children’s eating behaviours as measured with parent-reported questionnaires (such as the Baby/Children’s Eating Behaviour Questionnaire (CEBQ)) have been shown to track over time (ie, months or years) the moment-to-moment changes in children’s expression of their appetitive traits in response to contextual factors have not been considered.32 Laboratory-based measures of child eating behaviours have demonstrated how contextual factors affect children’s responses to eating behaviour tasks such as the EAH paradigm.33 However, presently, it is unclear whether children’s trait-like eating behaviours are also affected by contextual factors. This information will add nuance to our understanding of whether the expression of some trait-like eating behaviours is affected by contextual factors or whether they are stable across contexts.

Identification of previous cohort studies

To examine the existing birth cohort studies examining child eating behaviours, we conducted a review. In consultation with a research librarian, a search of five electronic databases (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Global Health, MEDLINE Complete, PsycINFO and SocINDEX with Full Text) was conducted in August 2022. Search terms included keywords for cohort studies (“birth cohort” OR “mother–child cohort” OR “prospective cohort study” OR “longitudinal cohort study”), eating behaviours (“eating behaviour*” OR “eating behavior*") and infants (infant OR baby OR babies). Only cohorts that enrolled infants prior to 6 months of age and examined child eating behaviours were included. Cohort studies not in English, and treatment-seeking cohorts were excluded. Articles were initially screened by one reviewer (author 1), a second reviewer screened 10% of the articles at both the title and abstract and full-text stages to ensure consistency of the exclusion criteria. Once a cohort study was identified, the original protocol paper was sourced via back referencing. Data were extracted for each birth cohort rather than individual papers.

Birth cohort characteristics extracted included country, the age of the infants when they were enrolled in the study, timing of follow-up of each wave of the study, sample size at each wave, variables examined in the study, health outcomes measured in the study (ie, dietary intake and/or anthropometric measures).

The search identified 64 papers, which included 33 birth cohorts examining child eating behaviours (online supplemental figure 1). Most (50%) of the birth cohorts were located in the USA (n=7), the UK (n=6) or the Netherlands (n=4) (online supplemental table 1). The sample size of the birth cohorts at baseline ranged from 40 to 14 451. Only 25 birth cohorts assessed dietary intake,2634,97 while 32 birth cohorts included anthropometric measures,1126 34,58 60 and 15 examined parental feeding practices.1126 34 35 46,52 60 The timing of the follow-up of the birth cohorts ranged from 6 weeks to 4 years.

From this review, we identified that there are currently no birth cohorts that repeatedly measure child eating behaviours, parental feeding practices, child dietary intake and child weight simultaneously from birth into preschool years. Of the 33 birth cohort studies, 17 measured child eating behaviours at 6 months or earlier,1136 37 56 59 74 75 80,88 91 with only 16 of these studies also measuring child growth at the same time points.1136 37 56 74 75 80,88 91 However, only eight of these studies examined child dietary intake at the same time points,3637 56 59 74 75 87 88 91 93,97 and only five of these studies examined parental feeding practices at the same time points.11 74 75 87 88 92 103 This makes it difficult to understand the transactional effects. Establishing a longitudinal study that enables examination of the causal relationships and pathways between infants/children and their parents and how these relate to outcomes (diets, weight) will build our understanding of how and why eating behaviours are learnt and developed, and how these influence diet and weight into childhood.8 This study will help in determining the causal pathways between child eating behaviours, parent feeding practices, child weight status and dietary intake that could be disrupted with prevention programmes.

Therefore, the primary objective of the Longitudinal Assessment of Children’s Eating (LACE) study is to examine the bidirectional and transactional processes explaining poor dietary intake and excess weight across infancy and early childhood. It is hypothesised, based on previous research1424,31 and developmental literature,10 that transactional effects (parent/child) experienced early in life will be magnified later in life, explaining poor dietary intake and excess weight, for example, parent-led feeding practices in infancy will increase infant/child food approach appetitive traits, which will increase infant/child dietary intake and weight and the use of more parent-led feeding practices at later time points. Empirical evidence is needed to support this.

Methods

Study design

The LACE study will be a prospective, longitudinal parent-reported study following infants from younger than 4 months of age across nine waves of data collection: younger than 4 months, 4 months, 6 months, 9 months, 12 months, 18 months, 2 years, 3 years, and 5 years of age.

Sample selection

Parents of infants younger than 4 months of age will be recruited at wave 1 (baseline). Inclusion criteria are being 18 years or older, fluent in English and living in Australia at baseline.

Survey sample

This study will examine 121 parameters in total. As per Schreiber et al,108 a ratio of 10 participants to 1 parameter is recommended; therefore, a sample size of 1210 is proposed. Any missing data from drop-out between waves will be handled by estimating the parameters using the method of maximum likelihood which are iterative in nature.109

Recruitment

Participants will initially be recruited online via paid social media (Facebook and Instagram) advertisements, over a number of months (ie, rolling recruitment). Informed consent will be obtained from all participant’s legal guardian. After participants consent to participating in further waves of data collection, they will be sent emails with a personalised link to each iteration of the survey, based on the child’s age. Three reminder emails will be sent if a participant does not complete the survey. Each survey will take approximately 15 min to complete. In appreciation of their time, participants will be offered the opportunity to enter a lottery to win 1 of 20 US$50 gift cards at each wave of the study.

Measures

The measures examined at each wave are presented in online supplemental table 2.

Primary measures

Infant/child eating behaviours

Parents will report their child’s eating behaviours using the Baby Eating Behaviour Questionnaire (BEBQ) at waves 1–4. The BEBQ includes measures of satiety responsiveness, slowness in eating, food responsiveness, enjoyment of food and general appetite.63 The BEBQ originally asked parents to retrospectively rate their perceptions of their infant’s eating behaviours. However, the current study will ask parents about their infant’s current eating behaviours, therefore, wording will be changed to present tense (eg, ‘my baby loved milk’ will be changed to ‘my baby loves milk’). This approach has been used in previous studies.110 From wave 5, a shortened version of the CEBQ will be used,13 including satiety responsiveness, slowness in eating, food responsiveness, enjoyment of food, fussiness and emotional overeating. These subscales were chosen as these were identified as the subscales that were associated with a preference for energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods, and a lower consumption of fruit and vegetables.111,113 Both tools have previously been validated in similar populations.114 115 Most items of the BEBQ showed acceptable fit, except for satiety responsiveness.114 All factor-item loadings were significant for the CEBQ.115 Both tools showed reasonable internal reliability for the subscales (0.56–0.92).114 115 The responses will be recorded on 5-point Likert scales, ranging from 1 to 5 (never to always). The scores will be averaged to obtain a continuous score for each eating behaviour subscale.

Parent feeding practices

Parental feeding practices will be measured by self-report using the Feeding Practices and Structure Questionnaire (FPSQ) for infants and toddlers116 at waves 1–5. This questionnaire can be used with parents who currently predominantly milk-feed (18 items) or solid-feed their infant/child (34 items). Parents will be asked if they predominantly milk or solid feed and will be provided with the corresponding questionnaire. Four feeding practices will be measured via 4–7 items: feeding on demand versus feeding routine, using food to calm, persuasive feeding, and parent-led feeding. From wave 6, seven feeding practices will be measured via the FPSQ-28: structured meal timing, structured meal setting, persuasive feeding, covert restriction, reward for eating, reward for behaviour, and overt restriction.117 The responses will be recorded on 5-point Likert scales, ranging from 1 to 5 (never to always or disagree to agree), with the scores averaged to obtain a continuous score for each feeding practice. The questionnaires have previously been validated in similar populations and showed good internal reliability (Cronbach’s alphas >0.67).116 118 Each feeding practice consists of 4–6 items. The scores will be averaged to obtain a single continuous score for each feeding practice.

Body mass index Z-score

At each wave, parents will report their child’s most recent length/height and weight. They will also report the date the measurements were taken and whether these were taken by a health professional (eg, nurses, general practitioners). At wave 1, parents will also report their child’s length and weight at birth. This information will be converted into body mass index z-scores (BMIz) using the WHO’s age-specific and gender-specific growth charts.119

Child dietary intake

Child dietary quality will be assessed from wave 5 using a derived dietary quality index (DGI). To obtain the DGI, a 68-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) assessing the child’s dietary intake will be used.120 The FFQ contains questions relating to the child’s general eating habits, including usual intakes of fruit and vegetables, and the type of milk and bread usually consumed. The FFQ also contains questions relating to the consumption of 68 food items over the past month, which are rated on a 9-point Likert scale (‘never or less than once a month’ to ‘6 or more times a day’).

Secondary measures

Screen time

Screen time during meals will be measured from wave 1 by asking parents ‘During a typical week, how often do you feed your child (milk or solids) in front of a screen (television, tablet, smartphone, PC or other)?’ and ‘During a typical week, how often do you feed your child (milk or solids) with the television (or another screen) on in the background?’; adapted from previous studies.121 122 The responses will be recorded on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 to 5 (‘never or almost never’ to ‘during every meal or always’). Overall screen time will be measured from wave 3 by asking parents to report how many minutes or hours their child spends watching shows, movies or clips (on the television, computer, phone or tablet) or playing electronic games (this could be on a computer, console, phone, tablet or other device) on an average weekday and an average weekend day (based on items with established reliability123 124). These questions were adapted from previous studies.121,124

Open-ended questions regarding context of eating behaviours and feeding practices

Three open-ended questions constructed specifically for this study will be included at each wave of the study. All participants will be asked; ‘Does your baby have any other eating behaviours you would like to tell us about?’

If participants answer rarely, sometimes or often to any of the child eating behaviour questions, they will be asked to answer the following question:

Could you please give us some details about why these behaviours differ sometimes? For example, do the behaviours differ based on time of day, location, baby’s mood/emotions, your mood/emotions, baby getting distracted, baby’s tiredness, how much encouragement is provided, who is feeding them, baby’s temperament, baby’s hunger levels, how active baby has been, how much is offered, how much time is available for feeding, etc?

If participants answer rarely, sometimes or most of the time to any of the parental feeding practice questions, child eating behaviour questions, they will be asked to answer the following question:

Could you please give us some details about why these practices differ sometimes? For example, do the practices differ based on time of day, location, baby’s mood/emotions, your mood/emotions, baby getting distracted, baby’s tiredness, how much encouragement is provided, who is feeding them, baby’s temperament, baby’s hunger levels, how active baby has been, how much is offered, how much time is available for feeding, etc?

Breast/formula feeding

At waves 1–7, parents will be asked ‘What milk does your baby currently consume?’, with response options: breast milk only, formula only, and both breast milk and formula milk. If the child is formula or mixed fed the parents will be asked to report in days, weeks, or months, how old their child was when they stopped being (exclusively) breastfed.

Parenting styles

Parenting styles will be assessed based on parenting dimensions of warmth and control. The warmth parenting dimension will be assessed from wave 1 by averaging the scores of six items from the Child Rearing Questionnaire,125 for example: ‘How often do you have warm, close times together with this child?’. The control parenting dimension will be assessed from wave 7 by averaging the scores of five items from the National Longitudinal Survey of Children and Youth,126 for example: ‘When you discipline this child, how often does he/she ignore the punishment?’. The responses will be recorded on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 to 5 (never/almost never to all the time). The warmth and control dimensions will be divided and combined into four categorical parenting styles as per Wake et al127: authoritative, authoritarian, permissive, and disengaged.

Child temperament

Child temperament will be assessed from wave 6 using subscales from The Early Childhood Behaviour Questionnaire (ECBQ)–Short Form, including fear, impulsivity, inhibitory control, and shyness.128 These subscales were chosen as these were identified as the subscales that were associated with child eating behaviours.129 130 The tool has previously been validated in similar populations.131 The responses will be recorded on 7-point Likert scales, ranging from 1 to 7 (never to always). Each temperament subscale consists of 4–8 items. The scores will be averaged to obtain a continuous score for each eating behaviour subscale.

Parent concern for child eating

Parent concern for eating will be measured at wave 6 (once solid food eating is established) using items pertaining to food (items 1–15) from the Lifestyle Behaviour Checklist.132 Parents will be asked to rate the extent certain child behaviours (eg, eats too quickly) have been a problem for them on a 7-point Likert scale (1=not at all and 7=very much). Parents will also be asked to rate their confidence in dealing with the behaviour from 1 (certain I can’t do it) to 10 (certain I can do it).

Additionally, parents will be asked where they currently obtain information, support or advice about their child’s eating behaviours, and if they would like more information, support or advice about development of healthy eating behaviours.

Demographic characteristics

Parents will be asked to report several demographic characteristics including, age, gender, country of birth (Australia/not Australia), education level (four categories—high school or less, trade/certificate, undergraduate university degree, or postgraduate university degree), annual income, whether they have a healthcare card (a card given to citizens who recieve specific payments from the government, this will be used as a proxy for socioeconomic disadvantage) parenting arrangements (one or two parent home), and presence of siblings in the household (yes or no). Parents will also be asked to report their child’s sex and date of birth.

Health

Parents will be asked ‘Has your child been told by a doctor, nurse or other health professional that they have any of the following disorders or conditions that affect their eating?’, with response options; no or yes, which one/s?; Down’s syndrome, autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, sensory processing disorder, avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder, tactile defensiveness, anxiety, and other disorders.

Data analysis

The data will be obtained using the online survey software Qualtrics.133 Data analyses will be conducted using Stata V.17.134 Descriptive statistics (percentages and frequencies) will be used to describe the characteristics of the sample. Cross-lagged structural equation modelling will be used to simultaneously determine the bidirectional and transactional relationships between the child (eating behaviours, BMIz, dietary intake, breast/formula feeding information, and temperament) and parent variables (feeding practices (including screen time), parenting styles, and parent concern for child eating). The method of maximum likelihood will be used to handle missing data.109 A sensitivity subgroup analysis will be conducted to determine any difference between the overall sample and children who may have any developmental conditions that affect their eating.

Data management

REDCap (Vanderbilt University, Tennessee, USA) will be used for participant management (ie, to store and manage data relating to their contact details and the children’s age). Identifying data (ie, name and contact details) will be stored separately from their survey data. Whether a participant is due for a new iteration of the survey (based on their age), will be checked and sent weekly. Reminders to complete surveys will also be sent weekly. Once all participants are above the age of a timepoint (wave), the survey will be closed, and the data will be transferred from Qualtrics to Stata for cleaning and analysis. Discussion

The current body of literature points to several complex relationships between a number of infant/child and parent characteristics and behaviours in shaping children’s diets and weight. However, there is a lack of evidence explaining the causal processes linking these biopsychosocial factors and explaining diet and weight outcomes. There are likely many different pathways to poor dietary quality and overweight and obesity, and these pathways begin early in life.1424,31 In order to understand how parents and infants/children interact and influence each other over the developmental years, longitudinal studies are needed that begin in infancy, with repeated measures of key constructs that allow for causal processes to be examined.

The LACE study will make advances in understanding these complex pathways. In particular, the study will progress understanding of the bidirectional and transactional processes across infancy and early childhood explaining how child eating behaviours affect child diet and weight outcomes. The recruitment of infants from birth, the use of repeated developmentally appropriate measures of child eating and parent feeding, the consideration of contextual influences on child eating, and the recruitment of a large and diverse sample will enable the examination of these processes. The study will, therefore, provide novel information about the causal pathways explaining poor dietary quality and overweight and obesity across childhood for different parent–child dyads. This information is fundamental to the effective design and delivery of diet and obesity-prevention programmes which have, to date, rarely been tailored to parent–child dyads and their unique characteristics and interactions.
