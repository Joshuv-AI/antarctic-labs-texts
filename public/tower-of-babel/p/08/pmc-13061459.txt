A Survey to Evaluate the Knowledge and Attitude of Anesthesiology Resident Doctors towards Perioperative Pain Assessment in Children
Authors: Snigdha Singh, Anju R. Bhalotra, Rahil Singh, Shweta Dhiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Pain assessment in children is often inconsistent despite its recognition as the “fifth vital sign.” Untreated perioperative pain has adverse physiological and psychological effects. Although validated tools exist, their use in clinical practice remains limited. This survey evaluated the knowledge of anesthesiology residents towards pediatric pain assessment.Materials and Methods:A prospective, cross-sectional survey was conducted using a validated 15-item questionnaire (Cronbach’s α = 0.82). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and registered with the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2023/06/053717). The questionnaire was distributed electronically to anesthesiology residents (6 months–6 years of anesthesia experience, including ≥3 months in pediatric anesthesia) across teaching hospitals in North India.Results:Of 480 surveys circulated, 210 (43.7%) were returned. While 183 (87.1%) respondents reported awareness of pediatric pain assessment tools, knowledge was incomplete. Only 47% identified that the Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability scale is not a self-report tool. Most (85.7%) recognized that tool selection depends on age, cognition, and institutional protocols, but only 27.1% correctly noted that observational measures are inappropriate in older children capable of self-reporting. Just 24.3% knew that facial expressions are the most studied behavioral indicator of neonatal pain. Although 90% acknowledged the importance of documentation, responses indicated a gap between theoretical awareness and practical application.Conclusion:Anesthesiology residents possess partial but insufficient knowledge of pediatric pain assessment. Standardized training and institutional adoption of validated tools are needed to bridge the knowledge–practice gap and improve perioperative pain management in children.

INTRODUCTION

Pain is defined as “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage, or described in terms of such damage” (International Association for the Study of Pain [IASP], www.iasp-pain.org). Untreated pain in children has been shown to lead to both short- and long-term adverse consequences. In the short term, inadequate pain management may lead to heightened stress responses, increased anxiety, and behavioral distress,[12] while long-term exposure to unrelieved pain during childhood is associated with altered pain perception, central sensitization, needle phobia, increased healthcare avoidance, and a higher risk of developing chronic pain and psychological disorders such as anxiety and post-traumatic stress.[345] Therefore, effective pain assessment and management in pediatric patients is essential to prevent these physiological and psychological sequelae.

In 2020, the IASP revised the definition of pain and added explanatory notes to better reflect the multidimensional nature of pain. One of the key notes states that “verbal description is only one of several behaviors to express pain; inability to communicate does not negate the possibility that a human or a nonhuman animal experiences pain.”[6] This is particularly important in the pediatric population, where children – especially neonates and infants – may not have the ability to verbalize their pain. Other notes in the new definition that are relevant to pediatrics include: “pain is always a personal experience that is influenced to varying degrees by biological, psychological and social factors,” and “although pain usually serves an adaptive role, it may have adverse effects on function and social and psychological well-being.”[7] These statements emphasize that pain in children is subjective, multifactorial, and, if inadequately treated, may lead to significant physiological, psychological, and social consequences.

Assessment of pain is a fundamental component of effective pain management and is recommended to be monitored as the “fifth vital sign,” alongside temperature, pulse, blood pressure and respiratory rate.[8] However, pain assessment in children is frequently overlooked, inconsistently applied, and often suboptimal in clinical practice. A wide variety of pediatric pain assessment tools have been developed for different age groups and developmental stages. Although these tools are used effectively in many dedicated children’s hospitals worldwide, anesthesiologists working in general hospitals often have insufficient knowledge or training regarding appropriate pediatric pain assessment. This gap in knowledge and implementation may lead to inadequate pain management and negative outcomes in children. Therefore, we conducted this survey to evaluate the current knowledge of resident doctors regarding perioperative pediatric pain assessment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A self-prepared questionnaire containing 15 multiple-choice questions was prepared. Content validity of the questionnaire was established by experts from the Departmental Scientific Committee, who assessed each item for relevance, clarity, and appropriateness. Necessary modifications were made based on their feedback. Internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which was calculated based on the variance of individual items and the total test score. The value obtained (α = 0.82) indicated good internal consistency. After Institutional Review Board Approval, this Prospective, Cross-Sectional Survey was registered with Clinical Trial Registry, India (CTRI/2023/06/053717) and the questionnaire was circulated electronically on Google docs in various teaching hospitals in North India to anesthesiology resident doctors with 6 months to 6 years of experience in practicing anesthesia with at least 3 months of this in pediatric anesthesia.

Participants were informed about the objective of this survey, and it was conveyed that their participation was totally voluntary, no incentive would be provided for participation, and they would also be given anonymity assurance for their responses. Participants had to choose a single answer to all the questions. A reminder email was sent twice at an interval of 1 week, and the collected responses were analyzed and the data were updated on Google. The database was created, and responses were analyzed.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this survey has been calculated from the leading question, “Are you aware of any pain assessment tools for use in children?” Considering a population of 1000 anesthesiologists and an assumed prevalence of 40%, with a precision of 5%, a sample size of 270 respondents was calculated to achieve a 95% confidence level, 207 respondents for a 90% confidence level and 137 respondents for an 80% confidence level.

RESULTS

The questionnaire was sent to 480 clinicians through electronic media, of which only 210 (43.75%) responded. The responses are tabulated in Table 1. About 69% of the respondents had <3 years, 20% had 3–4 years, and 11% had >4 years of experience working in anesthesia. Most of the respondents, 183 (87.1%), were aware that there are pain assessment tools available for use in children. A majority of respondents, 126 (60%), knew that pain assessment in children was often ignored, inconsistent and suboptimal because of all three cited reasons, i.e. children have trouble understanding, expressing and communicating pain, there is a lack of awareness of pain-assessment tools for pediatric pain, and it is more time-consuming. The other 40% thought it was due to only two of the three reasons.

Almost all the respondents, 204 (97%), were aware that self-reporting, behavioral and physiological scales can be used in children. Most respondents, 180 (85.7%), correctly indicated that the choice of pain assessment tool depends on the child’s age, level of cognition, and institutional protocols. Again, most respondents, 198 (94.3%), correctly identified that only children of ages ≥3 years are able to self-report their pain.

The next question was – Which of the following scales was not a self-reporting tool – Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability (FLACC) scale, or Wong and Baker’s Faces Scale. Only 99 (47%) respondents could identify that the FLACC Scale was not a self-report tool, while the other three were. Many respondents, 126 (60%), knew that only children of ages >8 years with an understanding of numerical order and value can use numerical pain scales to express pain. One hundred and eighty-three (87.1%) of the respondents knew that observational pain measures take into consideration the behavior of the child and the physiological parameters. Others thought it was only one of these two parameters. Respondents were asked to identify those children in which observational pain measures are not typically appropriate for use. Only 57 (27.1%) respondents knew that observational pain scoring is not appropriate for older children who can self-report, but is appropriate to use in very young children, infants, toddlers, preverbal, nonverbal, very distressed or cognitively impaired children and children restricted by bandages/mechanical ventilation/sedation, etc.

Only 51 respondents (24.3%) correctly identified facial expressions as the most studied behavioral pain assessment measure in neonates, whereas the majority, 135 respondents (64.3%), believed that crying patterns – such as sobbing, whimpering, and screaming – were the most studied behavioral indicator. Respondents were asked to identify the false statement regarding physiological responses to pain in children. As many as 129 (61.4%) of respondents were not aware that, when used alone, physiological indicators are not specific and reliable indicators of pain in small children. When asked to identify the observational pain tools out of the modified Pain Assessment Tool, Faces Pain Scale-Revised, FLACC scale and Crying, Requires O2, Increased vitals, Expression, Sleeplessness (CRIES) scale, only 66 (31.4%) respondents correctly identified that the Faces Pain Scale–Revised is not an observational pain tool, while the others are.

Respondents were tested on their knowledge regarding factors determining the correct choice of a pain assessment measure in children. The false statement – that pain assessment tools do not depend on the age and cognitive ability of the child – was correctly selected by 171 (81.4%) of the respondents. In response to the question regarding documentation of pain assessment, the majority, 189 (90%) respondents correctly selected the option of “all of the above”, which shows that they were aware that documentation of pain assessment allows rapid and accurate communication amongst the health care team, encourages partnership working with patients/carers and professionals, contributes to safe, high-quality care and supports good clinical decision making and documents the efficacy of pain-relieving interventions. Few respondents chose only one of the individual options.

DISCUSSION

Pain must be assessed before it can be appropriately managed [Figure 1]. Accurate pain assessment requires a systematic approach. One suggested method is the QUESTT framework: Question the child; Use age- and development-appropriate pain scales; Evaluate behavior and physiological changes; Secure involvement of parents; Take the cause of pain into account; and Take action and evaluate results.[6] However, if the second step is omitted, the entire approach becomes ineffective.

Stages of pain management in children

We conducted this survey on 210 respondents, of whom 34.3% had 6 months–1 year of anesthesia experience, 34.3% had 1–3 years, 20% had 3–4 years, and 11.4% had more than 4 years of experience. For the purposes of this study, a resident was defined as either a postgraduate student or a senior resident. Many survey questions were basic, and it was anticipated that any resident working in pediatric anesthesia for more than 3 months would be familiar with these concepts. We did not perform subgroup analysis by years of experience, as the overall sample size was insufficient to ensure adequate numbers within each subgroup for meaningful statistical comparisons, representing a limitation of our study. It is plausible that knowledge levels may be lower among residents with fewer years of experience; however, this could not be formally assessed.

Our survey revealed a moderate level of awareness regarding pediatric pain assessment. Most respondents recognized the availability of pain assessment tools for children (87%), the factors contributing to inconsistent and suboptimal use (60%), the role of self-report, behavioral, and physiological parameters in pain evaluation (97%), and that tool selection should be guided by the child’s age, cognitive level, and institutional protocols (86%). In addition, 94% were aware that self-reporting tools can be used only in children aged ≥3 years, 87% correctly identified that observational tools evaluate both behavioral and physiological parameters, and 90% recognized that institutions should adopt standardized tools, ensure staff training, and promote consistent use. Similarly, 90% acknowledged the importance of proper documentation. These findings suggest that while awareness is fairly widespread, consistent implementation in clinical practice may still be limited. The questions most frequently answered incorrectly pertained to specific details of various pediatric pain assessment tools (Questions 7, 8, 10–13).

Acute pain resulting from injury, illness, or medical procedures is the most common adverse stimulus experienced by children.[9] If not treated promptly and effectively, it can lead to long-term physical and psychological sequelae.[10] These may include anticipatory anxiety during future procedures, a lowered pain threshold with increased sensitivity to subsequent pain, reduced analgesic efficacy, and higher analgesic requirements.[11]

Pain assessment in children is particularly challenging, as younger children often cannot verbalize pain adequately. Consequently, pediatric patients have historically been undertreated compared to adults.[12] Ongoing assessment is essential to evaluate analgesic needs and monitor response to treatment.[9] Because pain is subjective, self-reporting remains the gold standard for assessment.[7] Self-report tools involving numbers are reliable only after the age of 8 years, when children develop an understanding of numerical order and value.[6] At this stage, scales such as the VAS and NRS are appropriate. Most respondents (60%) were aware of this. Although over 60 self-report tools exist,[1314] commonly used tools for younger children include the Wong–Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale, the Pieces of Hurt Tool, and the Multiple-Sized Poker Chip Tool. Only 47% of respondents correctly identified that the FLACC Scale is not a self-report tool, unlike the VAS, NRS, and Wong–Baker FACES Scale.

For children unable to communicate pain due to age or developmental limitations, observational and behavioral tools are acceptable alternatives.[9] Very distressed or cognitively impaired children cannot reliably self-report; in such cases, clinicians must rely on observational tools that incorporate both behavioral cues and physiological parameters. Nearly half of respondents were unclear about this. Most (87%) recognized that observational scales consider both behavior and physiological parameters. Although physiological indicators – such as heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, heart rate variability, intracranial pressure, skin color, pupillary size, and palmar sweating – are monitored, they are nonspecific and may be influenced by stressors such as fever, anxiety, or exertion.[1415] Notably, 61% of respondents failed to identify that the statement “When used alone, physiological indicators are the most specific and reliable indicators of pain in small children” is incorrect.

Behaviors commonly evaluated in pediatric pain assessment include facial expressions; disturbed sleep; loss of appetite; hand and body movements such as unusual posture or reluctance to move; changes in behavioral state such as quietness, restlessness, or lethargy; increased irritability, aggression, or clinging; variations in crying patterns; consolability; and alterations in muscle tone such as appearing “scared stiff.”[151617] Among neonates, facial expressions are the most extensively studied behavioral indicators of pain[6] [Figure 2]. Only 24.3% of respondents were aware of this.

Neonatal pain assessment

In preverbal or non-verbal children, commonly used observational scales include the Premature Infant Pain Profile, Neonatal/Infant Pain Scale, Children’s Hospital of Eastern Ontario Pain Scale, Crying, Requires O2, Increased vitals, Expression, Sleeplessness, and FLACC scales.[1418] Notably, 69% of respondents were confused about whether the Faces Pain Scale–Revised is an observational tool; it is, in fact, a self-report tool.

Whenever feasible, a multidimensional or composite approach that integrates physiological and behavioral indicators along with self-report is preferred.[151617] In situations where pain is anticipated, parameters should be documented at 15-min intervals until optimal analgesia is achieved, with reassessment 30 min after rescue analgesia to evaluate effectiveness. Postoperatively, pain should be assessed hourly for the first 6 h and continued hourly in children receiving complex analgesia (intravenous opioids, ketamine, patient- or nurse-controlled analgesia, or regional/epidural infusions). Children receiving oral analgesics should have pain scored at least every 4 h during waking hours. Pain assessment should also be performed during activities such as physiotherapy. If pain fluctuates, assessment should continue for 48–72 h, after which pain intensity typically peaks and begins to subside.[1920]

Every institution should adopt a standardized set of scales appropriate for the children being assessed. The same scale should be used consistently to enable comparison. Documentation of pain scores and interventions is essential. As pain is considered the fifth vital sign, it must be assessed and documented alongside other vital signs.[18] Standardized forms, tools, and observation charts facilitate ongoing assessment and documentation in postoperative areas, intensive care units, high-dependency units, and pediatric wards. Proper documentation ensures rapid and accurate communication among the healthcare team, encourages collaboration with patients and carers, supports safe, high-quality care, guides clinical decision-making, documents intervention efficacy, and safeguards patients. In our survey, 90% of respondents acknowledged the importance of documentation, highlighting that much of their knowledge remains theoretical and may not consistently translate into practice.

Limitations: Our study had some limitations. Subgroup analysis based on residency year or clinical experience was not performed due to insufficient sample size to achieve adequate statistical power. Residents with less experience may have lower knowledge and confidence in pediatric pain assessment, but this could not be formally assessed. Future studies should include larger sample sizes and stratified analyses to explore the impact of training and clinical exposure on knowledge, attitudes, and practices. In addition, assessing pediatricians, pediatric surgeons, and pediatric intensivists could provide further insights into institutional knowledge and practice patterns.

CONCLUSION

Overall, our survey indicates moderate knowledge regarding pediatric pain assessment among pediatric anesthesiologists. While respondents demonstrated book knowledge, pain scores are not routinely used in many institutions. Despite progress in pediatric pain assessment and management, a knowledge-to-practice gap persists.[18] Pain management in preverbal children remains inadequate due to assessment challenges.[21] Untreated pain can result in long-term behavioral, neurodevelopmental, and cognitive consequences. Institutions caring for children must adopt and implement age-appropriate pain assessment tools, ensuring standardized tools are used consistently across operation theatres, postanesthesia care units, pediatric surgery, and medical wards.
