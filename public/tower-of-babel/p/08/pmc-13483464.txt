Age- and sex-dependent transcriptomic network alterations in sepsis
Authors: Collins K. Boahen, Andrian Fratea, Anca-Lelia Riza, Ioana Streata, Andra Grigorescu, Mihai G. Netea, Vinod Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis arises from a dysregulated immune response to infection, causing systemic inflammation and high mortality. Its nonspecific symptoms and complex molecular mechanisms make early diagnosis and therapeutic development challenging. The contribution of host factors to this heterogeneity is not fully understood. We investigated whether baseline gene co-expression networks are preserved or reorganized in sepsis and whether age and sex influence these networks by analyzing RNA-seq data from Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs) of healthy Romanian individuals and sepsis patients. Sixteen co-expression modules were identified in healthy controls. Most were preserved in sepsis, but four exhibited disrupted organization, indicating selective network reprogramming. Notably, the green module was strongly associated with age, sex, and sepsis. Within this module, 13 age-associated and 20 sex-associated hub genes were identified. Individual hub genes showed modest discriminative ability, whereas a multigene model achieved high accuracy (AUC = 0.988). Transcription factor motif enrichment highlighted STAT family and AP-1–related signals, while functional enrichment implicated chromatin remodeling, DNA repair, and immune pathways, consistent with hallmarks of aging. These results suggest that age and sex shape the molecular architecture of sepsis, emphasizing the need for demography-aware approaches to understand its biology and prioritize pathways and regulators for validation towards precision diagnostics and therapeutics.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, leading to systemic inflammation, multi-organ failure and high mortality1. Early diagnosis remains sometimes challenging due to its nonspecific and variable clinical presentation, with clinical scores needed to be applied systematically. Despite beneficial effects of antibiotics and intensive case therapy, specific immunotherapies are lacking, partly because the molecular underpinnings of sepsis are highly complex2. Recent literature highlights that the immune response in sepsis is highly heterogenous and patient-specific, influenced by genetic, epigenetic, age-related, and sex-related factors3,4. This heterogeneity hampers the prediction of clinical outcomes and the development of universally effective therapies. Even within the same clinical settings, patients may exhibit either a hyperinflammatory response or profound immune suppression or immunoparalysis5.

Age and sex are key modifiers of sepsis outcomes. Elderly adults experience markedly higher incidence and mortality, largely due to immunosenescence, which diminishes immune responsiveness6. Sex differences are also well established, with females generally exhibiting more favorable immune responses and lower case-fatality than males7. These demographic factors underscore the urgent need for individualized strategies for sepsis diagnosis and treatment.

Transcriptomic profiling has become an essential tool for investigating the systemic host response in sepsis, enabling genome-wide characterization of gene expression in blood and tissues. These studies have identified differentially expressed genes in blood or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients8–10, defined biologically distinct sepsis endotypes11,12, and revealed temporal dynamics of host transcriptional responses in sepsis13,14. However, many of these studies focus on individual genes, producing large list of differentially expressed genes that are difficult to evaluate15, and often overlook the coordinated activity of functionally related genes. Module-based approaches, which cluster co-expressed genes into biologically meaningful networks provide a systems-level perspective with greater reproducibility and interpretability16, yet remain underutilized in sepsis research.

Recent studies applying network-based methods to sepsis have uncovered modules related to mitochondrial dysfunction, leukocyte migration, and oxidative phosphorylation, as well as lncRNA-mediated regulatory networks17–19. However, to our knowledge these limited number of studies typically construct co-expression networks either within sepsis patients alone or across both sepsis patients and healthy controls. While such approaches are useful for identifying modules associated with sepsis, they do not directly assess how sepsis perturbs the normal or baseline architecture of the human transcriptome. Furthermore, few studies explicitly integrate age and sex into network-level analyses, despite evidence of profound demographic differences in immune regulation, including diminished expression of genes related to signaling, innate and adaptive immunity in critically ill older adults20 and greater organ dysfunction scores in male patients21.

In this study, we investigated sepsis-induced changes in transcriptional architecture using data from a Romanian Functional Genomics and Severe Infections (FUSE) cohort. We constructed co-expression networks from healthy individuals and evaluated their preservation in sepsis patients to identify modules specifically disrupted by infection. We further assessed associations of these disrupted modules with age and sex, characterized key hub-genes and their upstream transcriptional regulators, and conducted functional enrichment analyses to uncover underlying biological processes. This integrative approach aims to provide a systems-level understanding of sepsis heterogeneity and highlight demographic factors as critical determinants of disease biology.

Results

Study overview and analytical workflow

We analyzed PBMC transcriptomes from the FUSE cohort to examine sepsis-induced alterations in gene co-expression and their modulation by age and sex. Because sepsis patients differed from controls in age (Fig. S1A) and sex distribution (59% female vs. 41% male; Fig. S1B), we created an age and sex-matched subset (124 samples per group; Fig. S1C) ensuring no sex differences for various age categories for all downstream analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear separation between the sepsis and matched controls, supporting strong underlying transcriptional differences (Fig. S1D). As shown in Table 1, patients were combined across sepsis severity categories, including 94 with sepsis, 29 with severe sepsis, and one with sepsis shock. Principal components analysis did not demonstrate transcriptomic segregation among these groups. Additionally, not significant differences were detected according to smoking status, medications use or disease severity when group differences were assessed using the top principal components (PC1 and PC2). Transcriptomic co-expression module architecture in healthy controls

To establish a reference transcriptional framework, we analyzed gene expression profiles from healthy controls. We constructed a signed weighted gene co-expression network with a soft-thresholding power of 8 (Fig. 2A), to achieve a scale-free topology R² ≥ 0.8 with adequate mean connectivity. This analysis identified 16 distinct co-expression modules, collection of genes exhibiting highly correlated expression patterns under normal physiological conditions. Each module was assigned a unique color identifier for visualization. The resultant hierarchical clustering dendrogram along with the corresponding module colors, ranging from grey through to midnight blue, are shown in Fig. 2B. Genes that did not exhibit strong co-expression with any other genes were assigned the grey module. The modular structure, revealed through topological overlap-based clustering was explicitly demarcated, with modules varying in size and gene composition (Fig. 2C), indicating a diverse and organized transcriptional landscape under basal conditions. To assess the robustness of the identified modules, we compared the network and module assignments derived from the full dataset with those of randomly resampled subsets. The results demonstrated a remarkable stability across subsamples with modules with dominant colors such as purple, blue, yellow, and turquoise consistently preserved in the repeated analyses (Fig. S2A). Quantitative evaluation using network similarity metrices, including adjusted Ranked index and adjusted mutual information yielded values of 0.62 (Fig. 2D) and 0.56 (Fig. 2E) respectively, further supporting the robustness of the baseline architecture that serves as reference for preservation testing and detecting disease-associated disruptions in sepsis patients.

Sepsis selectively reorganizes gene co-expression modules

We tested preservation of control-defined modules in sepsis using NetRep (1000 permutations per module). A module was called preserved if all preservation statistics were significant at P < 0.01; otherwise, it was labeled non-preserved. Independent of the gray module, eleven of 15 modules met this criterion. In contrast, four modules (brown, magenta, salmon, and green) were non-preserved, indicating altered topological structure in sepsis (Fig. 3A). The grey module, representing unassigned genes was excluded from further analysis. Module-trait associations highlights age and sex effects

The module eigengenes (MEs), representing the overall gene expression profile for each module were correlated with individuals’ age and sex using Spearman’s correlation. Out of the fifteen identified modules (excluding the grey module), eight showed significant associations with age, while three were significantly correlated with sex (Fig. S2B). To better explore age trends, individuals were stratified into three age groups: young adult (20–40 years), middle-aged (41–64 years), and old (65 years and above). Age-positive modules (e.g., red, green, brown, pink, midnightblue) showed higher MEs in older adults; age-negative modules (yellow, black, cyan) were higher in young adults (Fig. S2C), suggesting an age-dependent decline in module eigengene expression patterns. Regarding sex differences, ME scores were higher in females for the black and cyan modules, whereas males showed elevated ME scores in the green module (Fig. S2D), indicating potential sex-specific transcriptional regulation.

Focusing on the four non-preserved modules (excluding grey), the brown and green modules were significantly associated with age, and only the green module was significantly associated with sex (Fig. 3D). To evaluate the robustness of these associations across demographic subgroups, we performed a sensitivity analysis stratified by age group and clinical status (sepsis vs. healthy controls). The green module showed a significant association with sepsis in both young adult and middle-aged groups (Fig. 3E), whereas no such significant association was observed for the brown module (Fig. S2E). In addition, males had significantly higher ME scores in the green module compared to females (Fig. 3F), consistent with age- and sex-dependent network reorganization.

To evaluate the predictive accuracy of these hub genes in distinguishing sepsis patients from healthy controls, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) for each gene. Individually, hubs showed modest discrimination between sepsis and controls (AUC ranges: 0.45–0.68 for age-associated; 0.49–0.83 for sex-associated; Figure S3A, B). Notably, ATXN7L3B demonstrated the strongest individual performance, achieving an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.78–0.88). We next tested whether combining hub genes could improve classification performance by conducting multivariate ROC analysis with all hub genes as input features. This integrative model substantially improved classification accuracy (AUC = 0.988; Fig. S3C, D). Additionally, DeLong’s test revealed statistically significant differences in AUC values among the individual hub genes (P < 0.05), indicating variability in their standalone diagnostic capabilities (data not shown). Because training and testing used the same data, these estimates are optimistic. We therefore interpret the signature as hypothesis-generating pending external validation or nested cross-validation.

For the age-associated hub genes, transcription factor (TF) motif enrichment analysis identified 86 significantly enriched DNA motifs (Normalized Enrichment Score > 3.0; Supplementary Data 1A). Discussion

We applied a systems-level transcriptomics approach to quantify how sepsis perturbs gene co-expression architecture and how these perturbations are modulated by age and sex. By constructing a robust reference gene co-expression network in healthy PBMCs and confirming its stability through subsampling (Adjusted Mutual Information = 0.56; Adjusted Rand Index = 0.62), we were able to assess which control-defined modules retain or lose their topological structure in sepsis. This design moves beyond gene-centric analyses and provides a coherent view of network reorganization in the disease state.

A central observation is that sepsis induces selective rather than global network remodeling. Specifically, the majority of co-expression modules, approximately 73.3% (11 out of 15, excluding the grey module) maintain their structural organization, indicating that large portions of the baseline transcriptional landscape are resilient to systemic inflammation. The preserved modules may therefore reflect intrinsic physiological or immune functions resistant to inflammatory perturbation22,23.

In contrast, four modules, most notably the green module showed altered topological structure, consistent with reorganization of co-regulation and offers a systems-level perspective on host response heterogeneity that is often overlooked by gene-centric approaches24,25. Our findings are consistent with previous studies. For example, an analysis of gene expression across different populations identified four gene modules, only one of which was associated with sepsis26. Similarly, in a pediatric sepsis cohort, 15 co-expression modules were identified, with four showing the strongest associations with sepsis compared to healthy controls27. These results collectively support the conclusion that sepsis selectively disrupts particular transcriptional programs, and underscore the value of topology-aware analyses for identifying disease-relevant circuitry.

Disrupted modules identified in our study reflect sepsis-specific deregulation and network reorganization driven by systemic inflammation, hallmarks of sepsis that warrant further investigation due to the condition’s complexity and heterogeneity. Notably, our findings indicate that transcriptional module activity correlates with host demographic factors, particularly age and sex. Specifically, the non-preserved green module showed associations with both variables. Although gene activity within this module generally increases with age, significant sepsis-induced alterations were only observed in younger or middle-aged adults when compared to healthy baselines. In contrast, the module’s lack of significance in older adults, who typically experience more severe disease and comorbidities, aligns with prior research showing that age weakens the immune transcriptomic response to sepsis, with diminished intensity of immune gene expression in adults versus children and neonates28. Furthermore, the relatively higher expression of this green module in male patients suggests the involvement of sex-specific pathophysiological mechanisms in sepsis. This is consistent with earlier findings of sexual dimorphism in sepsis progression and outcomes, where males often show higher incidence and mortality, likely due to underlying genomic and immune system differences29. Collectively, they emphasize that demographic context is not merely a confounder but a determinant of how transcriptional networks are rewired during infection.

Prioritization of hub genes within the green module suggests plausible biological mediators. FLNA emerged as a central node and has been implicated previously as a critical hub gene in sepsis30. The same study also highlighted MYH10 as a significant hub gene, which is physically and functionally associated with MYO18A, another hub gene showing age and sex interaction effects in our study. Another key hub gene, KLF13, has been described to offer a protective effect against myocardial damage secondary to sepsis by inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokine production and NF-kB signaling suppression31. Other candidates such as AMBRA1, a regulator of autophagy and apoptosis32, lack direct evidence in sepsis but are biologically credible by “guilt-by-association” within the co-expression context. These findings nominate concrete targets for mechanistic follow-up.

Transcription factor motif enrichment provides an upstream layer of plausibility. Interestingly, key transcription factors identified include members of the STAT family, and constitute the Jak/STAT pathway, a critical regulator of immune responses in sepsis33. The AP-1 components (FOS/JUN/ATF) also plays diverse roles in immune regulation, with the ATF2 transcription factor specifically reported as a therapeutic target for managing sepsis-associated neuroinflammation34. Additional transcription factors (e.g., RELA, STAT3, PITX3, ETS2, HNF4A, EP300) have been proposed as candidates for drug repurposing in the treatment of critical sepsis cases35. Although motif analyses rely on precompiled motif rankings, the convergence of JAK/STAT and AP-1 pathways supports a model in which a relatively small number of regulatory hubs coordinate broader module-level changes.

Functional enrichment of the green module is notable for chromatin remodeling, DNA damage/repair, and transcriptional regulation, alongside immune signaling pathways. These results are in line with earlier studies that epigenetic alterations are a component of cellular and molecular hallmarks of ageing which is a significant mechanism of induction of loss of cellular functions during ageing and ageing-associated disorders36, and occur in sex-dependent manner37. Telomere maintenance was another implicated pathway related to DNA repair, and loss in the length of telomeres beyond a critical point induce cellular senescence or apoptosis38. There are noteworthy sex differences in the efficiency of DNA repair and telomere dynamics so that adult women possess longer telomeres compared to adult men39,40. The observation that these processes are embedded within an age- and sex-modulated, sepsis-reorganized module provides a coherent mechanistic link between demographic modifiers and disease biology. It also raises the possibility that chromatin-level interventions or therapies tuned to biological age and sex could modulate host responses in a targeted manner.

Some limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. First, a validation cohort with similar demographic characteristics and full representation of the genes included in the network analysis is lacking, which is necessary to confirm the findings. Future studies incorporating larger, longitudinal cohorts with comprehensive clinical and laboratory data would help to better characterize modules that are independent of age and sex. Second, the relatively small sample size within subgroups, such as the adult sepsis patients, may limit the power to detect subtle but biologically relevant age-related differences. Third, the principal hub genes and transcription factors were identified through computational methods and supported by literature evidence, necessitating functional assays to establish their causal involvement in the pathophysiology of sepsis. Fourth, sepsis was diagnosed in the FUSE cohort using Sepsis-2 criteria, resulting in a sepsis population with relatively mild severity. The findings of our study need to be investigated and confirmed in more severe sepsis cohorts such as those diagnosed by Sepsis-3 criteria.

In summary, our data revealed that sepsis selectively reorganizes specific co-expression modules, in ways that are significantly age- and sex-modulated. Also, the discovery of central hub genes and transcription factors, especially those involved in inflammation, apoptosis, autophagy, immune signaling, underscores their usefulness as therapeutic targets. By integrating co-expression analysis with demographic factors, this study advances our understanding of sepsis biology and highlights the critical need to consider age- and sex-specific variables in precision diagnosis and lays the foundation for future mechanistic and therapeutic exploration for personalized treatment for sepsis.

Methods

FUSE cohort description and study design

The study included healthy individuals and patients with sepsis, meeting the Sepsis-2 criteria41, all of Romanian ethnicity, and enrolled as part of the Functional Genomics of Severe Infections (FUSE) project. A detailed description of the cohort, including inclusion and exclusion criteria has been described previously42. Briefly, patients’ enrollment took place between May 2017 and November 2019 at the Academic Hospital for Infectious Disease and Pneumology “Victor Babes” situated in Craiova, Romania. Individuals were excluded if they had a diagnosed inherited immunodeficiency or any condition associated with acquired immunodeficiency, including HIV infection, receipt of chemotherapy or prolonged corticosteroid therapy.

Healthy adult controls were recruited at the Human Genomics Laboratory of the University of Medicine and Pharmacy of Craiova. Eligibility criteria required no medical history and no use of prescription or self-administered medications at the time of enrollment.

The study protocol was approved by the committee of ethics and Academic and Scientific Deontology from the University of Medicine and Pharmacy of Craiova under the number 80/17.11.2016 and by the Ethics Committee of “Victor Babeş” Clinical Hospital of Infectious Diseases and Pneumology and performed in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki and guidelines for good clinical practice (GCP). All participants signed an informed consent form.

Overall, the FUSE cohort comprised 124 sepsis patients and 284 healthy controls with available transcriptomics data. Due to imbalance in sample size and differences in age and sex distributions between groups, all subsequent analyses were restricted to a subset of age- and sex-matched samples using the nearest-neighbor matching approach implemented in the MatchIt R package43. Participants were matched on exact sex and the closet available age. This ensures the distributions of the demographic variables are balanced between groups and to minimize confounding effects. Gene co-expression network construction, module preservation analysis, and all downstream analyses were conducted exclusively on these matched datasets.

RNA isolation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation of whole blood diluted 1:1 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) over a Ficoll-Paque gradient (GE Healthcare). The PBMCs were washed three times with cold PBS and resuspended in RPMI 1640 (Dutch modified) supplemented with 50 mg/L gentamicin, 2 mM L-glutamin (GlutaMAX), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Total RNA was extracted from PBMCs using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN), and only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) greater than 7 were used for downstream RNA sequencing.

RNA sequencing and preprocessing

RNA libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Kit (MGI Tech), and strand-specific RNA sequencing was performed on the DNBseq platform (paired-end, 100 bp read length), generating an average of 24.05 million reads per sample ( ± 0.54 M).

To obtain raw sequencing data, reads mapping to rRNA were removed. Quality filtering was conducted using SOAPnuke v1.5.2 (parameters: -l 15 -q 0.5 -n 0.1), eliminating adaptor-contaminated reads, reads with more than 10% unknown bases, and low-quality reads (more than 50% of bases with quality scores below 15). Post-filtering, the average number of clean reads per sample was 23.99 million ( ± 0.54 M), with an average clean read ratio of 99.7%. Sequencing quality was high, with an average Q20 of 98.3% ( ± 0.29%) and Q30 of 91.9% ( ± 0.99%). Additional quality assessments including the GC content distribution, sequencing duplication rates, adapter contamination and K-mer enrichment confirmed the high quality of the sequencing data.

Clean reads were initially aligned to the GRCh37 reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.4) for quality control and mapping assessment. Mapping quality was consistent across samples, with an average genome mapping rate of 93.63% and gene mapping rate of 78.71%. For gene expression quantification, reads were independently aligned to Bowtie2 (v2.2.5) as part of the RSEM (RNA-seq Expectation Maximization) pipeline.

A total of 19,379 genes were detected.

Data pre-processing and normalization

To exclude lowly expressed genes, raw counts data were first converted to counts per million (CPM) using the cpm function from the edgeR package44. Genes with CPM greater than 0.5 in at least two samples were considered expressed. This filtering step resulted in 15,544 genes being retained for downstream analysis. The filtered count data were then normalized across samples and transformed using the variance stabilization transformation (VS available in DESeq245.

Constructing gene co-expression network

To identify gene co-expression modules in the control group, we employed the Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) framework, specifically using the “blockwiseModules” function from the WGCNA package in R46. The analysis was conducted on the preprocessed expression data of 15,544 genes. The goodSamplesGenes function was used to check the quality of the samples and genes. We employed the biweight midcorrelation (bicor) method to generate a similarity matrix of gene expression data across all samples. The matrix was transformed into an adjacency matrix using a soft-thresholding power. Both the Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM) and network type were set to “signed” to enhance module specificity by preserving the sign of the correlation. The block size and minimum module size were set to 5000 and 30 genes respectively and module eigengenes were merged based on a dissimilarity threshold of 0.25. Each gene module was assigned a standard RGB color by default. The correlation between module eigen-gene and demographics was used to identify significant module-trait relationship (P < 0.05).

Evaluation of WGCNA module stability

To assess the stability of gene clusters, we repeatedly resampled 80% of the full dataset and performed network construction and module detection on both these subsamples and the original data, following the approach outlined in ref. 47. Modules identified in each of the 30 resampled datasets as well as the full dataset were then visualized. To quantitatively evaluate the consistency of the identified modules across the resampled and full datasets, we used two metrics: the adjusted Rand index (ARI) and adjusted mutual information (AMI). The adjusted Rand index (ARI) measures the similarity between two clusterings by considering all pairs of samples and determining how many are assigned to the same or different clusters. The adjusted mutual information (AMI) evaluates the amount of shared information between two clusterings of the same dataset. Both metrics range from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect agreement between the clusterings.

Module preservation analysis

To evaluate the preservation of gene co-expressed modules between the healthy controls and the sepsis samples, we utilized the NetRep software48, an R package designed for rapid and computationally efficient assessment of module preservation using a permutation-based framework. We first defined gene co-expression modules in the control group which served as the discovery set and the corresponding sepsis group was designated as the test set. NetRep calculates multiple module preservation statistics including measures of density and connectivity, to assess the stability of modules between the two groups. NetRep uses a non-parametric permutation approach to generate null distributions for each preservation statistic. For each module, we performed 1,000 permutations, during which the gene labels in the sepsis group network were randomly permuted, while keeping the control group network fixed. Empirical permutation p-values were computed for each module preservation statistic, and a module was deemed significantly preserved if all statistics yielded p-values less than 0.01.

Identification of hub genes

Hub genes of co-expression modules were established based on two key metrices: Module Membership (MM) and Gene Significance (GS). MM was computed as the correlation between a gene’s expression profile and their corresponding module eigengene, representing the gene’s connectivity and centrality within the module. GS is a measure of the correlation between individual gene expression levels and external trait of interest (e.g., age or sex), a measure of biological relevance. Spearman correlation analysis was applied to assess the relationship between module eigengenes and traits. Hub genes were defined as genes where MM > 0.8, indicating strong intramodular connectivity and GS > 0.2, indicating moderate to strong correlation with traits. Such thresholds are consistent with the respective values used in previous studies in the selection of target of genes49,50.

TF-Motif enrichment analysis

We utilized the RCisTarget R package51 to perform transcription factor (TF)-motif enrichment analysis on the identified hub gene set. RCisTarget is a computational framework that detects overrepresented TF-binding motifs within a given list of genes and predicts candidate TFs likely to regulate them. The analysis identifies DNA motifs significantly enriched near the transcription start sites (TSS) of input genes. These motifs are then mapped to their associated TFs, and a Normalized Enrichment Score (NES) is calculated. Motifs with an NES greater than 3.0 are considered significantly enriched.

Functional enrichment analysis of relevant/selected modules

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted to elucidate the biological functions of genes within key gene modules. The clusterProfiler R package52 was utilized to perform these analyses, offering gene-based classification based on specific GO levels. GO enrichment analysis encompasses all three GO sub-ontologies: biological process (BP), molecular functions (MF), cellular components (CC). Enrichment significance was determined using overrepresentation analysis with adjusted p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamin-Hochberg false discovery rate method (FDR) method.
