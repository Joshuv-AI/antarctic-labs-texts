Gall bladder sludge and biliary pseudolithiasis as rare complications of ceftriaxone therapy in adult patients – A case report including sonographic workup
Authors: Sven Jungblut, Sebastian Aland, Jens-Uwe Erk, Julia Aland, Hagen Frickmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCeftriaxone-induced biliary pseudolithiasis is a disease entity frequently observed in children, while it is rarely seen in adult patients. We provide a thorough sonographic workup of the case to guide sonographers confronted with it.Case reportA 41-years-old male patient with sonographically confirmed, culture-negative endocarditis accompanied by repeated fever episodes received 4g ceftriaxone per day next to ampicillin and gentamicin as an element of his antibiotic therapy. At day 4 of treatment, signs of gall bladder sludge and biliary pseudolithiasis were first seen in sonography. At day 17, abdominal pain over 30 min and increased liver and pancreatic enzymes pointed towards temporal duct obstruction as a complication and ceftriaxone therapy had to be preterm cancelled. A sonographic work-up of the stepwise improvement of the sonographic findings after the end of the ceftriaxone therapy is provided, a reemergence of endocarditis was not observed.DiscussionThe case reminds of a rarely recognized, usually reversible side effect of high-dose ceftriaxone therapy and its complications. The sonographic work-up may serve as guidance for clinicians during follow-up assessments.ConclusionsAlthough ceftriaxone allows usually well-tolerated antimicrobial therapies, biliary pseudolithiasis should be kept in mind as a rare but sometimes non-ignorable side effect.

Introduction

Ceftriaxone is a broadly applied 3rd generation cephalosporin which has been listed by the WHO (World Health Organization) as a so-called “essential medicine” due to its extraordinary importance for the treatment of a broad variety of bacterial infectious diseases (https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/371090/WHO-MHP-HPS-EML-2023.02-eng.pdf?sequence=1, last accessed 24th July 2025). Although side effects of ceftriaxone are usually mild and rarely observed [1–3], hepatobiliary disorders have been reported to be the second most frequent ceftriaxone side effect in children after gastrointestinal disorders with 24.6% and 37.4% of all registered pediatric side effects, respectively [2]. Within the subpopulation of children in whom ceftriaxone therapy had to be stopped due to the severity of side effects, reversible biliary pseudolithiasis (26.7%) had a relevant proportion [2]. Compared to the frequent occurrence of pediatric biliary pseudolithiasis induced by ceftriaxone administration as described in studies and case reports [2, 4–8], this condition is much less frequently seen in adult patients [9–15] with a significant age effect even within assessed adult populations [15]. While this medical condition is frequently mild and self-limiting, observed partly severe complications comprise epigastric colic [16] and biliary obstruction requiring endoscopic management [17] as well as acute cholecystitis with and without accompanying pancreatitis and partly severe courses including acute necrotizing calculous cholecystitis [18–20].

The sludge of ceftriaxone-induced biliary pseudolithiasis mainly consists of fine precipitates of ceftriaxone extracted into bile and calcium ions, ranging between 20 and 250 μm in size [21]. A hepatic effect of non-physiological secretion and a gall bladder effect supporting precipitation jointly lead to sludge formation [22, 23]. The precipitation process is dose-dependent, can facultatively be associated with abdominal pain, nausea as well as emesis and the sludge usually resolves within days to months after stopping drug exposure [24–29]. A meta-stable calcium ceftriaxone salt forms at 37 degrees centigrade with a formation constant C about 157.3 L mol−1 at a stoichiometry of 1:1; detectable precipitates require a [Ca2+] x [ceftriaxone] ion product exceeding the solubility product constant by factor 10.4. In vivo, a saturation index surpassing the metastability limit can be expected at dosages of 2g ceftriaxone infusion and higher, especially if gallbladder emptying is additionally impaired [30]. The phenomenon is addressed by professional societies as well. The WHO, for instance, mentions biliary lithiasis due to calcium ceftriaxone precipitates at increased ceftriaxone doses and that these precipitates dissolve after discontinuation of ceftriaxone therapy (https://extranet.who.int/prequal/sites/default/files/whopar_files/HA480part4v2.pdf, last accessed on 24th July 2025). Next to impaired gallbladder emptying, further risk factors for ceftriaxone-induced biliary pseudolithiasis comprise renal disease or even hemodialysis with associated electrolyte shifts [31, 32] but also fasting and bed rest [33] as well as surgical interventions [34]. Other 3rd generation cephalosporins like cefotaxime show a comparably lower risk of inducing biliary pseudolithiasis [35].

Abdominal sonography is the method of choice for diagnosing ceftriaxone-induced biliary sludge and pseudolithiasis [34, 36–38], although computed tomography has been assessed for this purpose as well [39]. In sonography, gallbladder sludge appears as echogenic material without associated acoustic shadowing, while the echogenic material in case of pseudolithiasis is associated with acoustic shadowing [37]. Many small echogenic particles but also larger echogenic foci within the gallbladder can be associated with the etiological entity. Sonography-based differentiation of these pseudo-stones from real gall bladder stones is usually unfeasible [38] and so, sonographers should be aware of it to avoid unnecessary surgical interventions. In computed tomography, detectable findings comprise sludge patterns in the most incidents, followed by stone plus sludge patterns and stone patterns; concomitant gall bladder enlargement can be associated with visible bile duct stones [39].

In the here presented case report, we contribute towards the so far scarce availability of clinical experience with ceftriaxone-induced biliary pseudolithiasis in an adult patient. Further, sonographic workup is presented to guide sonographers confronted with this clinical entity.

Case report

The 41-year old male patient presented at a medical inpatient department after repeated episodes of fever up-to 39 °C over several weeks. His medical history including history of travel was overwise non-conspicuous. In particular, there were no clues in his medical history on increased calcium intake or particular risk factors for stone-forming diatheses. At admission, an increased C-reactive protein (CRP) value of 50 mg L−1 (reference: <5 mg L−1) was recorded and echocardiography showed a filiform vegetation of the aortic valve with a measured maximum dimension of 24 mm (Fig. 1).

Leukocyte count values were never increased but remained close to the lower reference value of 3.8 giga-particles/L at the border of leukopenia. Repeatedly taken blood cultures remained negative and infectious disease work-up only revealed serological results indicative of previous infections with Legionella spp. and Mycoplasma spp. without signs of activity. No immunologically relevant contacts to adenovirus, Brucella spp., Coxiella burnetii, cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr-virus and enterovirus were suggested by respective serological assessments. Calculated therapy of culture-negative infectious endocarditis with ampicillin (12 g d−1), ceftriaxone (4 g d−1) and gentamicin (210 mg d−1) was initiated. Gentamicin was discontinued as planned after 14 days of treatment, ceftriaxone and ampicillin had to be preterm discontinued after 17 and 18 days of therapy, respectively, due to intolerable side effects. In spite of the short therapy duration, signs of infection resolved and follow-up echocardiography indicated regression of the vegetation.

Subcortical myoclonus was assumed to be associated with neurotoxic side effects of ampicillin and led to discontinuation of the aminopenicillin application. Biliary calcinosis and gall bladder sludge first observed during abdominal sonography at day 5 after hospital admission and thus day 4 after the start of antimicrobial therapy were considered to be associated with high-dose ceftriaxone treatment. While the effect was initially ignored as known and expected side effect of high-dose ceftriaxone therapy, an abdominal pain event over 30 min occurred after 17 days of treatment, associated with increased liver enzymes GPT at 1.85 μkat L−1 (reference <0.85 μkat L−1) and GOT at 1.07 μkat L−1 (reference <0.85 μkat L−1) as well as with increased pancreatic enzymes lipase at 19.9 μkat L−1 (reference <1,12 μkat L−1) and alpha-amylase at 6.16 μkat L−1 (reference 0.47–1.67 μkat L−1) in spite of a non-conspicuous abdominal CT-scan. This event led to the clinical suspicion of a transient block of the bile duct and the pancreatic duct. Consequently, ceftriaxone treatment was preterm canceled immediately.

Subsequently, biliary pseudolithiasis resolved over a period of 6 weeks as shown in sonographic follow up assessment and as indicated in the Figs 2–4. As exemplified in Fig. 2, sonographic visualization of the maximum dimension of biliary pseudolithiasis and gall bladder sludge before cancellation of ceftriaxone therapy showed echo-dense concrements of up-to 0.52 cm in size. In the first follow-up assessment of biliary pseudolithiasis and gall bladder sludge after therapy cancellation, the number of concrements was already reduced. Further, regression of concrement size was observed with a measured maximum dimension of 0.39 cm as shown in Fig. 3. Figure 4 shows the last sonographic follow-up assessment. Only a single concrement with a measured maximum dimension of 0.26 cm, which was merely half of the initially measured maximum size, was found at the end of follow-up, while all other concrements had already been dissolved. Signs of infections indicative of recurrent infectious endocarditis were not observed during control assessments.

Discussion

The presented case describes the rarely recognized side effect of biliary pseudolithiasis associated with ceftriaxone therapy. In line with this clinical entity's pathophysiology [24–32], a high ceftriaxone dose of 4 g per day was presumably the main reason for the impressive sonographic and clinical course. Other specific risk factors were not documented in the patient's medical history, especially no increased calcium intake. As minor factors of potential relevance, bed rest associated with in-patient treatment and renal function affected by nephrotoxic gentamicin therapy might have hypothetically played a role [31–33].

Epigastric pain and biliary as well as pancreatic obstruction have been described as complications of ceftriaxone-induced biliary pseudolithiasis [16, 17] and led to the preterm cancellation of ceftriaxone application in the here described case as well. Due to the merely temporal obstruction, endoscopic management was fortunately unnecessary and more severe complications like cholecystitis and pancreatitis could be circumvented. Nevertheless, the incidence was considered as a warning signal against proceeding with ceftriaxone therapy as before in order to avoid progression towards more severe side effects.

As impressively confirmed by the sonographic images presented, ceftriaxone-induced biliary pseudolithiasis in adult patients is reversible. This is in line with the above-mentioned WHO statement and experience from previous case reports [14]. Insofar, the here presented case in no exemption from this general rule. The provided sonographic imagines may help clinicians to evaluate the respective progress during follow-up assessments.

As a consequence of the observed side effects, the chosen antibiotic therapy of the patient's endocarditis was much shorter than recommended by current guidelines [40–42]. Fortunately, no signs of recurrence were seen during the follow-up assessments. The etiological agent of the endocarditis, in response to which the ceftriaxone therapy was conducted, remained undetected and the question of bacterial activity within potentially abundant and difficult-to-treat bacterial biofilms on the patients' aortic valve [43, 44] remained unresolved. The chosen dosages of antimicrobial therapy with ampicillin, ceftriaxone and gentamicin had been adapted from ESC (European Society of Cardiology) guideline recommendations on initial empirical treatment of infectious endocarditis [45]. Initial triple therapy including gentamicin was considered as sufficiently safe because of the normal kidney function of the young patient, making renal failure as a critical prognostic factor unlikely [46].

Conclusions

The case reminds of biliary pseudolithiasis as a rarely recognized side effect of ceftriaxone therapy as well as biliary obstruction as one of its more frequently reported complications. Further, the provided sonographic workup is intended to support result-interpretations and decision-making of clinicians during follow-up assessments of this generally mild and self-limiting medical condition.
