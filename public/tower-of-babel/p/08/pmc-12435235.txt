The molecular structure of the master growth regulator in plants emerges into the spotlight
Authors: Hideki Yoshida, Makoto Matsuoka
Published: 2025

DELLA proteins are key negative regulators of the phytohormone gibberellin (GA) signaling pathway and play a central role in a wide range of plant growth processes, including seed germination, stem elongation, floral transition, and fruit maturation in response to various internal and external signals such as light, temperature, and nutrient availability (1). A well-known molecular activity of DELLAs is to regulate transcription through physical interaction with transcription factors: This can interfere with the factors’ ability to bind to gene promoters, or they can directly activate transcription by acting as coactivators in partnership with transcription factors (2). The degradation of DELLA upon GA perception via physical interaction with the GA receptor GIBBERELLIN INSENSITIVE DWARF1 (GID1) and the Skp1-Cullin1-F-box (SCF) ubiquitin ligase has long been recognized as the core mechanism for enabling GA responses. Although the importance of direct interactions between DELLA and various other proteins has been revealed, the structure of DELLA proteins has remained unresolved except for the N-terminal DELLA domain in complex with GID1 (3). In addition, evidence from genetic mutants has hinted at an additional, proteolysis-independent repressive regulation for DELLAs, which has remained an unsolved question.

Dahal et al. (4) present high-resolution cryoelectron microscopy structures of the Arabidopsis thaliana DELLA protein REPRESSOR OF ga1-3 (RGA) protein in complex with GA3–GID1A, as well as with the F-box protein SLEEPY1 (SLY1) and its adaptor ARABIDOPSIS SKP1 HOMOLOGUE 1 (ASK1). These structures revealed a bipartite interaction mechanism in which the N-terminal DELLA domain acts as a “molecular bridge,” stabilizing the interaction between GID1A and the GRAS domain. Importantly, the C-terminal GRAS domain features a concave surface that accommodates direct binding by the F-box protein SLY1. Contrary to prior models suggesting that the GRAS domain undergoes conformational remodeling upon SLY1 binding, it remains structurally stable. This was further corroborated by experimental assays showing that disruption of the DELLA domain abrogates complex formation.

SLY1 binds to the GRAS domain via the hydrophobic concave surface of the GRAS domain, which is distinct from the GID1-binding surface. These structural analyses used SLY1 with gain-of-function mutation (E138K) which was previously reported by the genetic study (5). Structural analyses and yeast three-hybrid assays confirmed that this mutation eliminates a charge repulsion between SLY1 E138 and RGA E297 and strengthens hydrophobic interactions, thereby promoting SLY1–RGA interaction. Interestingly, a second conformation of the GA3–GID1A–RGA–SLY1–ASK1 complex was resolved, showing an ~8° torque-like rotation of the SLY1–ASK1 complex at the SLY1–RGA GRAS interface, with minimal disruption of the binding interface. This conformational elasticity may facilitate the ability of SCF complex to perform diverse polyubiquitination modifications to RGA.

Dahal et al. further validated this model using a split-RGA strategy, wherein the DELLA and GRAS domains of RGA were separately expressed in plant. Coexpression restored GA-dependent GRAS degradation, thus validating the cooperative action of both domains. This in vivo evidence adds a critical functional layer to the structural observations and highlights that the DELLA domain facilitates the interaction between GID1 and GRAS domain and the subsequent GA-dependent degradation of GRAS.

In addition to structural studies, Dahal et al. performed yeast three-hybrid assays that provided direct evidence for the proteolysis-independent mechanism for suppressing DELLA activity. The authors demonstrate that GID1, in the presence of GA, significantly reduces the interaction between RGA and the transcription factor IDD1, with which DELLA can interact. This interaction is mediated by the GRAS domain, which also serves as the docking site for GID1A in the GA-bound state. These data indicate that GID1 competes directly with IDD1 for GRAS binding. Further support comes from structural modeling, in which AlphaFold3 predicts that IDD binding sterically occludes the GID1-binding surface on the GRAS domain. Together, these findings establish a molecular mechanism by which GID1 suppresses DELLA transcriptional activity without inducing its degradation.

Dahal et al. (4) present high-resolution cryo-electron microscopy structures of the Arabidopsis thaliana DELLA protein REPRESSOR OF ga1-3 (RGA) protein in complex with GA3-GID1A, as well as with the F-box protein SLEEPY1 (SLY1) and its adaptor ARABIDOPSIS SKP1 HOMOLOGUE 1 (ASK1).

Concurrently, a separate study from another research group independently solved a cryo-EM structure of the GA3–GID1A–RGA and GA3–GID1A–RGA–SLY1–ASK1 complexes (6). Their findings are broadly consistent with those of Dahal et al., including two binding sites of GID1A, interaction of SLY1 with the GRAS concavity, and GID1A-mediated steric hindrance of transcription factor binding. However, Dahal et al. validated the molecular bridge role of the DELLA domain and the physical competition between GID1A and IDD through genetic and biochemical approaches. The concordance of structural insights and the complementarity of experimental evidence across these studies significantly strengthen the credibility of this revised GA signaling paradigm.

This study presents a reevaluation of how GA signaling balances growth regulation through both reversible and irreversible modes of DELLA control (Fig. 1). The ability of GID1 to repress DELLA activity without necessitating degradation introduces a layer of plasticity into the GA response. This is especially important under fluctuating environmental conditions, where rapid and transient modulation of transcriptional networks may be more advantageous than committing to proteolysis. Indeed, reversible suppression may serve as a priming mechanism, allowing plants to rapidly resume DELLA-mediated growth inhibition if GA levels drop.

In recent years, posttranslational modifications (PTMs) have emerged as a major focus in the molecular regulation of DELLA proteins (7). While ubiquitination has long been recognized as a prerequisite for DELLA degradation, additional modifications such as phosphorylation have also been implicated in modulating DELLA activity. More recently, glycosylation events, O-GlcNAcylation and O-fucosylation, have been reported as novel regulatory mechanisms (8). Although glycosylation is a well-established mechanism for regulating protein function and conformation, its role in regulating plant proteins has remained largely unexplored (9). The research on DELLA glycosylation has been almost limited to A. thaliana. However, given that both GID1 and DELLA proteins are evolutionarily conserved in all vascular plants (10, 11), there is ample opportunity to investigate whether such PTM-based regulatory mechanisms are conserved across diverse plant species, including crops, and to what extent they might impact agricultural traits. The detailed structural insights into DELLA presented in the studies could provide a foundation for elucidating how such PTMs influence its conformation and function.

Although this work has resolved the three-dimensional structure of the DELLA and GRAS domains of DELLA protein, the structures reported here represent its inactive and predegradation state, captured in complex with GID1 and SLY1. This study also shows that GID1 binding abolishes the interaction between DELLA and IDDs which acts as transcriptional scaffolds for DNA binding of DELLA (12). Therefore, the current structures do not depict DELLA in its active state. Future work will need to build upon these findings in order to capture structural snapshots of DELLA in its functionally active state and to fully understand how it serves as a master growth regulator in cooperation with various DELLA interactors.
