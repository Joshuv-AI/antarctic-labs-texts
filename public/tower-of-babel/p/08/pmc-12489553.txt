Refining the diagnostic strategy for malignant lymphoma: advantages of endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle biopsy over aspiration
Authors: Kyong Joo Lee, Se Woo Park
Published: 2025

Endoscopic ultrasound-guided tissue acquisition (EUS-TA) has become an indispensable tool for diagnosing deep-seated lesions in the gastrointestinal and mediastinal regions. Its role has traditionally been centered on solid pancreatic neoplasms, where it attains high sensitivity and specificity.1,2 However, as EUS-TA applications expand to hematologic malignancies, such as malignant lymphoma (ML), new questions have emerged regarding the optimal technique for procuring diagnostically adequate samples.

In this context, the study by Niiya et al.3 on the issue of Clinical Endoscopy provides timely and clinically meaningful data. The investigators retrospectively analyzed the diagnostic yield of EUS-guided fine-needle biopsy (FNB) compared with that of fine-needle aspiration (FNA) in 33 patients with histologically confirmed ML. Their findings support the hypothesis that FNB yields superior diagnostic performance and tissue quality compared to FNA, especially in the World Health Organization subclassification of lymphoma (71.4% vs. 31.6%, p=0.037). A significant strength of this study lies in its methodological design. Notably, the inclusion of a subgroup analysis that focused solely on 22-gauge needles minimized the bias related to needle size. Even in this subset, FNB was associated with a significantly higher subclassification accuracy (83.3% vs. 25%, p=0.019), thus reinforcing the robustness of this method across gauge types.

Histological subclassification is crucial in ML, where treatment regimens are increasingly subtype-specific. Immunohistochemical and molecular characterization often depend on the availability of core tissue with a preserved architecture. FNA, although effective for cytological evaluation, frequently falls short in yielding intact tissue cores, thus limiting the reliability of immunophenotyping.4,5 The findings of Niiya et al.3 corroborate this limitation and favor FNB, which retrieved high-quality histologic samples (score ≥4) in 100% of patients treated with 22-G, consistent with earlier studies that suggested higher tissue adequacy rates for FNB.6 Furthermore, the safety profile of EUS-TA was reassuring in both arms of the study, with no reported adverse events such as bleeding or perforation. This confirms the procedural safety of EUS-FNB for lymphadenopathy sampling7 and supports its broader adoption in routine diagnostic workflows.

One of the key considerations is the role of the needle gauge and its design. Although 19-G needles may provide larger sample volumes, they are less flexible and technically more challenging to deploy, particularly in angulated positions.8 The study’s subgroup analysis isolates needle design as a variable and suggests that 22-G FNB strikes a favorable balance between diagnostic yield and technical feasibility.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations warrant further discussion. The retrospective nature of the study and its relatively small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings. The FNB group had significantly larger lesions, which could have influenced the sampling success. Additionally, the lack of blinding in histological scoring and the absence of flow cytometry or cytogenetic analysis may have underrepresented the full diagnostic utility of the samples.9 Nonetheless, this study provides one of the few direct comparisons of FNB and FNA, specifically in ML, making it a valuable contribution to the literature.

In conclusion, this study supports the growing consensus that FNB should be considered the preferred EUS-guided modality for evaluating suspected ML. It offers superior diagnostic performance, especially for accurate World Health Organization subclassification, without compromising safety. As the therapeutic landscape for lymphoma becomes increasingly molecularly driven, the ability to obtain high-fidelity tissue samples via minimally invasive methods such as EUS-FNB becomes even more critical. Future prospective studies that incorporate genetic and cytometric analyses will be instrumental in validating these findings and establishing FNB as the standard of care for EUS-guided lymphoma diagnostics.
