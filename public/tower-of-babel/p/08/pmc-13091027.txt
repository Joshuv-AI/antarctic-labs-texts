Ecoflex-hydrogel bilayer soft robot for pH-controlled drug protection and delivery
Authors: Hanbi Park, Minseong Kim, Yeonjae Lee, Suho Lee, Jieun Kim, Jinseo Yoon, Eun Hui Jeong, Jiwoo Kim, Jun Dong Park, Jung Bok Lee, Donghoon Son, ChangKyu Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional anticancer therapies remain limited by inadequate tumor-specific delivery and substantial off-target toxicity. Although hydrogel-based drug delivery systems offer high biocompatibility and tunable release profiles, their clinical translation is often impeded by uncontrolled diffusion and premature drug leakage inherent to their porous networks. Here, we introduce a multifunctional silicone-hydrogel bilayer soft robotic platform designed to achieve precise, stimuli-responsive drug delivery with enhanced retention and spatiotemporal control. Unlike conventional hydrogel-based systems, the proposed platform employs an Ecoflex–hydrogel bilayer architecture in which the elastomeric Ecoflex layer acts as a protective barrier that mechanically confines the hydrogel and suppresses premature drug leakage. The bilayer architecture integrates a hydrophobic silicone layer, which effectively suppresses unintended drug leakage, with a pH-responsive poly(N-isopropylacrylamide-co-acrylic acid) (p(NIPAM-co-AAc)) hydrogel layer that enables on-demand release via reversible swelling-deswelling behavior. Incorporation of magnetic iron(III) oxide (Fe2O3) nanoparticles within the silicone matrix further allows remote, non-invasive actuation under external magnetic fields. The platform exhibits excellent thermal stability at physiological temperature (36.5 °C) and demonstrates selective drug release under alkaline conditions representative of the intestinal microenvironment. Repeated pH cycling confirms robust structural integrity and highly reversible actuation over multiple cycles. Collectively, this work establishes the silicone-hydrogel bilayer soft robot as a versatile, magnetically actuated, pH-responsive drug delivery system with significant potential for precision cancer therapy.

Introduction

Despite continuous progress in anticancer therapeutics, conventional chemotherapy remains limited by low therapeutic efficiency arising from insufficient tumor-specific accumulation and significant off-target toxicity to healthy tissues. Effective cancer treatment therefore requires drug delivery systems capable of localized, controlled release at diseased sites while minimizing systemic exposure. In response, targeted drug delivery strategies have been extensively investigated, including nanoparticle carriers, ligand-mediated targeting, and stimuli-responsive materials [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Among these, hydrogel-based drug delivery systems have emerged as one of the promising candidates due to their high water content, biocompatibility, and tunable responsiveness to physiological cues such as temperature and pH [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]].

However, the clinical translation of hydrogel-based platforms is often hindered by their intrinsic porous networks, which facilitate uncontrolled diffusion and premature drug leakage along the entire delivery pathway [12]. Such leakage not only reduces therapeutic efficacy but also increases the risk of adverse side effects at non-target sites [13]. Critically, gripper-type hydrogel soft robots reported to date share this fundamental limitation: their inherently porous structures permit continuous drug leakage prior to reaching the intended target, compromising delivery precision [14,15]. Addressing this limitation requires material systems that can simultaneously ensure drug retention under non-target conditions and enable selective release at the desired site. Hybrid architectures that combine hydrogels with complementary barrier materials provide a practical strategy to overcome this challenge [16,17].

Soft robots have facilitated targeted drug delivery in anatomically inaccessible regions by providing precise spatiotemporal control [18], which has driven growing research interest in biocompatible drug delivery systems [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. In this study, we develop a dual-layer soft robotic drug delivery platform engineered for site-specific drug release in alkaline environments. The proposed platform incorporates a non-porous Ecoflex silicone elastomer as an outer layer that serves a dual function: enhancing the mechanical stiffness of the gripper while acting as a diffusion barrier throughout transit. At the target pH, swelling of the poly(N-isopropylacrylamide-co-acrylic acid) (p(NIPAM-co-AAc)) hydrogel layer drives the release of the encapsulated payload.

The system integrates a hydrophobic non-porous silicone elastomer layer with a stimuli-responsive p(NIPAM-co-AAc) layer that encapsulates the drug payload (Fig. 1). The silicone layer functions as a diffusion barrier that suppresses premature drug leakage, while the hydrogel layer serves as an active reservoir for drug loading and release with thermal- and pH-responsive actuation. N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM)-based hydrogels are well known for their temperature-responsive behavior, exhibiting a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) near physiological temperature approximately 30–32 °C [23,24]. Above the LCST, the hydrogel network undergoes dehydration and collapse, whereas below the LCST it absorbs water and swells. This reversible swelling-deswelling transition, which is accompanied by a pronounced volume change, makes NIPAM-based hydrogels highly attractive as active materials for thermal-responsive actuation in soft robotic systems [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]], and they have also been specifically explored in biomedical applications such as healthcare devices and biosensors [13,[30], [31], [32]]. While poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (pNIPAM) is highly sensitive to temperature, the incorporation of acrylic acid (AAc) introduces significant pH-responsiveness to the system [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Specifically, the NIPAM-AAc hydrogel undergoes substantial swelling in solutions where the pH is above the pKa of the network which is approximately 4.5 [36,37]. Conversely, under acidic conditions the hydrogel contracts due to protonation of the AAc groups, resulting in pronounced deswelling of the polymer network. In this system, pH acts as the primary stimulus governing the swelling–deswelling behavior, while the thermo-responsive property assists the deswelling process near physiological temperature.

Under strongly acidic conditions such as those found in the stomach (pH 1.5–3.5), the hydrogel therefore remains in a compact state, and physiological temperature further promotes dehydration of the polymer chains, leading to enhanced deswelling. This cooperative effect facilitates tighter folding of the structure and improves retention of the encapsulated drug in the gastric environment. In contrast, in the mildly alkaline environment of the colon (pH 6.5–7.5), the hydrogel swells, enabling subsequent drug release.

A critical challenge in constructing such hybrid systems lies in achieving robust interfacial bonding between hydrophilic hydrogels and hydrophobic silicone elastomers. Hydrogels possess polar, water-rich surfaces [38], whereas silicone elastomers such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and Ecoflex exhibit intrinsically hydrophobic, low-surface-energy interfaces dominated by nonpolar methyl groups [39]. This large surface energy mismatch results in poor adhesion between hydrogels and silicone elastomers without targeted surface modification or interfacial engineering [40]. To address this incompatibility, a two-step surface treatment strategy was employed to significantly enhance adhesion between the two layers, ensuring mechanical stability during deformation and operation. When these two dissimilar materials are integrated, the resulting construct can minimize systemic exposure to drugs, reducing the risk of toxicity and improving patient safety.

In addition, comprehensive rheological and mechanical characterizations were conducted to evaluate materials printability and to predict deformation behavior during actuation supported by finite element analysis (FEA). These evaluations are critical for ensuring reliable three-dimensional (3D) fabrication and consistent performance. The fabricated soft robotic platform demonstrated controlled locomotion under magnetic guidance and enabled selective drug release in an alkaline environment representative of colonic milieu. Furthermore, the biocompatibility of the Ecoflex-p(NIPAM-co-AAc) bilayer was systematically evaluated. Cell viability analyses, including Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and live/dead assays, confirmed the absence of cytotoxic effects, thereby supporting the suitability of the bilayer soft robotic system for potential in vivo applications.

Overall, this work presents a bio-integrated Ecoflex-p(NIPAM-co-AAc) bilayer soft robotic system designed for intelligent, site-specific drug delivery. By combining barrier-assisted drug retention, pH-responsiveness, and magnetic locomotion, this platform offers a promising strategy for advancing precision drug delivery in cancer therapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

N-isopropyl acrylamide (NIPAM), Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (pNIPAM), Acrylic acid (AAc), Iron(Ⅲ) oxide (Fe2O3), Acetic acid solution, N, N′-methylenebisacrylamide (BIS), 2-Hydroxy-4’-(2-hydroxyethoxy)-2 methylpropiophenone (Irgacure 2959), Fluorescein O-methacrylate, Rhodamine 6G (Rh6G), 3-(Trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate, Isopropyl alcohol (IPA) were used as received from Sigma-Aldrich. Laponite RD (BYK), Ecoflex 00-30 (Smooth-On), and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, DAEJUNG) were also used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel and silicone elastomer

The NIPAM-based hydrogel was synthesized via free-radical photopolymerization using BIS as the crosslinker and Irgacure 2959 as the photoinitiator. In the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) network, NIPAM serves as the primary monomer, while AAc is incorporated as a comonomer to introduce ionic functionalities that confer pH-responsiveness. Laponite RD, a synthetic nanoclay, was included to govern the rheological behavior of the ink during printing. Upon extrusion through the printer nozzle, Laponite particles align along the flow direction, reducing viscosity to facilitate smooth extrusion. Once the shear stress is relieved post-extrusion, the particles rapidly reassemble into a network structure, restoring viscosity and providing shape stability to the printed construct [41]. To prepare the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) pre-gel solution, NIPAM, pNIPAM, BIS, Irgacure 2959, and Laponite RD were dispersed in deionized (DI) water under magnetic stirring for 30 min. AAc was subsequently added to introduce ionic functionalities into the copolymer network. For a total solution volume of 10 mL, the composition consisted of 0.833 g NIPAM, 0.02 g pNIPAM, 0.561 g Laponite RD, 0.02 g Irgacure 2959, 0.007 g BIS, 8.333 mL DI water, and 227 μL AAc.

The silicone elastomer ink was prepared by thoroughly mixing Ecoflex Part A and Part B at a 1:1 (wt%) ratio. For magnetic locomotion, Fe2O3 nanoparticles were incorporated at a concentration of 3 wt% relative to the total Ecoflex mass. The mixture was degassed by placing it in a refrigerator for 20 min to remove entrapped air bubbles prior to direct ink writing.

Characterization of materials

Mechanical properties and swelling behavior of the hydrogel

The Young's modulus (Ey) of photocured p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogels was measured using a stress-controlled rotational rheometer (HR-20, TA Instruments) equipped with a parallel-plate geometry at 37 °C. Prior to testing, samples were fully immersed in pH 2 and pH 8 buffers for 24 h to obtain a full equilibrium state. Specifically, compressive strain was applied by gradually reducing the gap at a rate of 12 μm/s, while the axial force was continuously recorded. Stress-strain curves (Note S1) were obtained by converting the axial force to stress and calculating the compression strain relative to the initial gap. The Young's modulus was determined from the slope of the initial linear region of the stress-strain curve, which extended to approximately 12 % compressive strain for all samples.

For swelling measurements, hydrogel samples were printed as cylindrical structures (radius: 0.5 cm; height: 1.2 mm; infill density: 70 %) and photocured in an Ultraviolet (UV) crosslinking chamber (Bio-Link 365, Vilber) for 10 min. After the curing process, samples were rinsed with DI water to remove unreacted monomers or impurities. The swelling behavior was evaluated by immersing the samples in buffer solutions of varying pH for 24 h. The equilibrium radius was measured using ImageJ software, and swelling ratios were calculated accordingly (Note S2).

Rheological characterization

The rheological properties of the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel and Ecoflex were analyzed using a stress-controlled rotational rheometer (HR-20, TA Instruments) with a parallel-plate geometry at room temperature (25 °C). To eliminate shear history effects, samples were pre-sheared at 10 s−1 and -10 s−1 for 60 s each, followed by a 180 s reset period. Strain-amplitude sweep tests were performed at a fixed angular frequency of 1 rad s−1 over a strain range from 0.001 to 10 to evaluate the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″). Flow curves were then obtained by measuring viscosity as a function of shear rate in the range of 0.1–1000 s−1. For the three-interval thixotropy test (3ITT), samples were subjected to 0.1 s−1 for 120 s, 500 s−1 for 10 s, and again 0.1 s−1 for 180 s. The structural recovery ratio was calculated as the ratio of the viscosity after high shear to the initial viscosity. All measurements were performed immediately after sample preparation.

Selection of silicone elastomer and magnetic integration strategy

Silicone elastomers were investigated as passive layers due to their mechanical compliance and inherent hydrophobicity. Ecoflex (Ey ≈ 32 kPa) was selected because its modulus closely matches that of the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel, enabling efficient and unobstructed bending deformation [42]. In addition to its mechanical properties, Ecoflex exhibits excellent biocompatibility [43], Its intrinsic hydrophobicity further reinforces its function as a diffusion barrier against premature drug leakage [44]. Additionally, Ecoflex 00-30 maintains structural integrity across a broad temperature range and under acidic gastric conditions [45]. Furthermore, 3 wt% magnetic Fe2O3 nanoparticles were incorporated exclusively into the Ecoflex layer rather than the hydrogel to prevent interference with hydrogel polymerization and to avoid structural defects, such as cracking, at high nanoparticle loadings (Note S3). This design ensured stable magnetic responsiveness within the silicone matrix while preserving hydrogel integrity.

Fabrication of the soft robot

The pH-responsive soft robot was fabricated using a direct ink writing (DIW)-based three-dimensional (3D) printing approach. DIW 3D printing enables precise and reproducible fabrication of complex 3D structures through high-resolution material deposition [[46], [47], [48]]. It allows straightforward integration of multiple functional inks including soft and stimuli-responsive materials, making it well suited for advanced biomedical applications such as tissue engineering, smart organoids, and surgical soft robotics [[49], [50], [51]]. In our study, an extrusion-based dual-head 3D bioprinter (Inkredible+, Cellink) was employed to enable precise deposition of multiple functional inks. The Ecoflex/p(NIPAM-co-AAc) bilayer soft robot was fabricated via a dual-material DIW printing process, in which the synthesized material inks were loaded into cartridges and mounted onto the printer extrusion heads. Detailed optimization of the DIW printing parameters and process conditions is provided in the Supporting Information (Note S4).

Bidirectional shape transformation characterization

To evaluate pH-responsive shape changing behavior, bilayer specimens (20 mm × 5 mm x 1.2 mm) were fabricated. Fluorescein O-methacrylate was incorporated into the hydrogel layer to enhance optical contrast. Samples were first equilibrated in pH 2 buffer at 36.5 °C for 24 h to achieve a fully contracted state. Subsequently, the bilayers were transferred to a pH 8 buffer, and the time-dependent change in tip-to-tip distance was recorded over 220 min using optical imaging to evaluate the pH-responsive shape transformation (Note S5 and S6).

Finite element analysis

Finite element analysis (FEA) was performed using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2 to simulate the deformation behavior of the pH-responsive bilayer soft gripper (Note S7). Both the hydrogel and Ecoflex layers were modeled using a Neo-Hookean hyperelastic formulation, which is not only suitable for deformation under 50 %, but also viable for large deformation around 100 % under volumetric constraints [52]. Although the hydrogel exhibited a swelling ratio of 111 % at pH 8, the Neo-Hookean model was adopted due to the volume-expansion constraints imposed by the non-pH-responsive passive silicone layer.

Symmetry boundary conditions were applied to reduce computational costs by modeling half or quarter geometries. Prescribed displacements were applied at the gripper center, and contact conditions were imposed at the tips to prevent interpenetration. The Young's modulus (Ey) of the hydrogel layer was respectively set to 11.2 kPa at pH 2 and 5.71 kPa at pH 8, based on experimental data (Fig. S1), while the Ecoflex modulus was fixed at 31.0 kPa as reported in previous research [42]. Detailed information on these FEA parameters, along with Poisson's ratio and density, is provided in Table S1. Especially, a Poisson's ratio (ν) of 0.5 was assigned to all materials with the incompressibility assumption commonly applied to soft hydrogels and silicone elastomers [53,54].

Functional evaluation of the soft robot

Magnetic locomotion and control

An in vitro testing platform was established using a 6-axis collaborative robot (A0509, Doosan Robotics) to evaluate magnetic controllability and environmental responsiveness of the pH-responsive soft robot (Note S8 and S9). A custom 3D printed fixture (PIS Combo 3D printer, Bambu Lab) housing a neodymium magnet (N52, diameter: 30 mm; thickness: 10 mm) was mounted on the robot arm's end-effector to enable non-contact magnetic manipulation. Locomotion experiments were conducted in DI water (pH 7) to mimic colonic conditions. Prior to testing, the soft robot was equilibrated in a pH 2 buffer to induce full contraction and to secure encapsulation of the cargo while minimizing leakage. The robot arm was programmed to follow a predefined trajectory for controlled magnetic guidance.

Drug loading and release characterization

Two fluorescent dyes were employed as model compounds to evaluate drug loading, retention, and release behavior. Among them, fluorescein O-methacrylate was incorporated into the hydrogel layer during fabrication for clear optical visualization of diffusion (Note S10).

For quantitative analysis, Rhodamine 6G (Rh6G) was employed as a drug surrogate. Rh6G shares comparable molecular size and aromatic group content with chemotherapeutic agents such as vincristine (VCR), and this structural similarity suggests analogous diffusion and release behavior [55]. Accordingly, Rh6G was used as a representative model compound for quantitative release studies. In addition, its strong absorbance in the visible region enables reliable concentration determination using UV–vis spectroscopy.

Drug loading was performed by immersing the fabricated Ecoflex/p(NIPAM-co-AAc) bilayer soft robots in a Rh6G solution (47.5 μM, 20 ml) prepared in pH 2 buffer for 48 h to allow diffusion into the hydrogel network. After loading, the samples were rinsed with DI water to remove loosely adsorbed dye. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) was determined by measuring the residual Rh6G concentration in the loading solution using UV–vis spectroscopy at 527 nm. To evaluate premature drug leakage under gastric-like conditions, Rh6G-loaded soft robots were incubated in pH 2 buffer and the released dye was quantified after 48 h. For drug release experiments, the loaded samples were transferred to pH 8 buffer at 36.5 °C, and aliquots of the release medium were periodically collected and analyzed by UV–vis spectroscopy. Encapsulation efficiency, leakage percentage, and cumulative release were calculated based on the measured dye concentrations. To further analyze the release mechanism, the experimental data were fitted using the Higuchi and Korsmeyer–Peppas kinetic models [56,57]. Detailed equations and fitting procedures are provided in the Supporting Information (Note S11).

Biocompatibility assessment

Extracts of the samples (soft robot, p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel, and Ecoflex) were prepared in accordance with the relevant ISO guidelines (ISO 10993-5). For the quantitative assessment, cell viability was measured using a metabolic activity-based assay (e.g., CCK-8 or MTT). In accordance with the ISO 10993-5 criteria, the material is considered non-cytotoxic if the cell viability remains above 70 % compared to the blank control. According to ISO guidelines, each sample was incubated in culture medium for 24 h at 37 °C under physiological conditions to enable the leaching of any potential residual components. After incubation, the extracts were collected and filtered for subsequent cell treatment. L929 fibroblasts, human dermal fibroblast (HDF), and adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) were employed to evaluate the biocompatibility of the soft gripper materials. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h to allow stable attachment. The culture medium was replaced with the prepared material extracts, and the cells were exposed for 24 h and 72 h of incubation. Cell viability was quantitatively evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. In parallel, a live/dead assay was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were incubated with a staining solution containing calcein acetoxymethyl ester (Calcein-AM) and Ethidium homodimer-1 (EthD-1) for 20 min at 37 °C, followed by three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove excess dye. Fluorescence images were acquired using confocal laser scanning microscopy (LSM900 with Airyscan, Carl Zeiss, NFEC-2024-12-301401) at Chronic and Metabolic Diseases Research Center, Sookmyung Women's University.

Results and discussion

Rheological characterization of Ecoflex/Fe2O3 and hydrogel

The two materials exhibit markedly different rheological responses under conditions relevant to extrusion-based 3D printing. In extrusion-based 3D printing, printability is governed by yielding behavior, shear-thinning, and structural recovery, as these properties control flow initiation in the reservoir, extrusion stability within the nozzle, and post-deposition shape retention and layer stacking [58,59].

As shown in the strain-amplitude sweep (Fig. 2a), the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel exhibits a substantially lower yield strain than Ecoflex/Fe2O3, indicating that its polymer network is disrupted at much smaller deformations. A lower yield strain facilitates an earlier transition to flow within the contraction zone, thereby reducing extrusion resistance during printing. Consistently, the flow curve (Fig. 2b) shows that p(NIPAM-co-AAc) undergoes significantly stronger shear-thinning than Ecoflex/Fe2O3. Rheological characterization of Ecoflex/Fe2O3and p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel.(a) Oscillatory strain amplitude sweep. (b) Flow curve. (c) Three-interval thixotropy test of p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel. (d) Three-interval thixotropy test of Ecoflex/Fe2O3.

The transient rheological behavior was evaluated using a three-interval thixotropy test (3ITT), designed to sequentially mimic the reservoir storage stage, nozzle extrusion, and post-extrusion recovery through low–high–low shear rate steps. As shown in Fig. 2c, the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel exhibits a structural recovery of approximately 140 %, indicating rapid reconstruction of the polymer network following high shear and even an increase in viscosity relative to the initial state. Rapid recovery is critical for limiting filament spreading immediately after deposition while maintaining sufficient interfacial contact between adjacent filaments, thereby promoting effective layer integration. Similarly, Ecoflex/Fe2O3 exhibits a structural recovery of approximately 95 % in its 3ITT response (Fig. 2d), indicating that its internal structure can be effectively restored after extrusion. Although its yielding and shear-thinning characteristics are less favorable than those of the hydrogel, the relatively high recovery ratio suggests that printability can be ensured through strategic control of processing parameters.

In practice, printability of Ecoflex/Fe2O3 was achieved by employing a smaller-diameter nozzle (0.25 mm) was employed, and partial curing at 4 °C was applied to increase viscosity and mitigate filament spreading (Fig. S3). These results indicate that although Ecoflex/Fe2O3 does not inherently satisfy the rheological criteria for optimal extrusion-based printing, its printability can be substantially enhanced through condition-specific process optimization. Overall, p(NIPAM-co-AAc) intrinsically offers more favorable rheological characteristics for extrusion-based fabrication, whereas Ecoflex/Fe2O3 represents a viable printable material when supported by tailored processing strategies.

pH-responsive actuation of p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel

To evaluate the suitability of the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel as a pH-responsive swelling/deswelling driven soft actuator, cylindrical structures (radius = 5 mm) were fabricated via extrusion-based 3D printing and subsequently UV-cured for approximately 12 min prior to actuation testing (Fig. S2a). The pH-responsive actuation amplitude was first tuned by systematically varying the AAc content, which directly governs the density of ionizable groups within the polymer network [60]. As shown in Fig. 3a, increasing AAc concentration resulted in a pronounced enhancement of swelling at pH 10, indicating an expanded actuation strain driven by electrostatic repulsion within the polymer network [37]. However, excessively high AAc content can lead to overly large swelling and reduced structural stability of the hydrogel, which may compromise the controlled deformation required for the bilayer soft robotic system. The dynamic swelling and deswelling kinetics, which directly determine actuator response time, are presented in Fig. 3b. Upon exposure to alkaline conditions (pH 10), the hydrogel actuator rapidly expanded, reaching near-equilibrium deformation within approximately 6 h and achieving maximum actuation strain after 24 h. Conversely, switching to acidic conditions (pH 2) triggered rapid contraction, with the actuator radius decreasing to approximately 0.55 cm within 8 h, demonstrating a clear and reversible pH-triggered shape transformation.

The steady-state actuation performance at room temperature (RT, 25 °C) and physiological temperature (36.5 °C) is summarized in Fig. 3c. Under alkaline conditions, deprotonation of -COOH groups induced strong electrostatic repulsion and osmotic pressure within the network, leading to volumetric expansion and macroscopic shape change [61]. A sharp increase in actuation strain was observed between pH 4 and pH 6, whereas minimal additional deformation occurred between pH 6 and pH 10, suggesting saturation of the pH-induced actuation mechanism. At pH 2, the hydrogel maintained a compact geometry close to its original printed dimension. Importantly, negligible temperature dependence was observed in the pH 6–10 range, indicating that the hydrogel actuator performance remains stable under physiologically relevant conditions (e.g., the colonic environments) and is not compromised by thermal fluctuations.

The long-term durability and reversibility of the pH-driven actuation were further evaluated through repeated cycling experiments (five cycles, pH 2 ↔ pH 10; 24 h per cycle), as shown in Fig. 3d. The hydrogel actuator consistently and reproducibly transitioned between an expanded state (radius = 0.95 cm) and a contracted state (radius = 0.54 cm) without mechanical degradation. A slight increase in post-cycle dimensions was observed, likely due to gradual network relaxation; however, this did not impair actuation fidelity. The absence of structural damage over multiple cycles confirms the mechanical robustness and functional reliability of the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel, underscoring its potential as a programmable, chemically driven soft actuator for applications in bio-integrated soft robotic systems.

To further promote covalent bonding at the Ecoflex and p(NIPAM-co-AAc) interface, a pre-hydrolyzed silane coupling solution consisting of isopropyl alcohol (9.5 mL), DI water (0.5 mL), acetic acid solution (0.5 mL), and 3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate (0.105 mL) was spin-coated onto the plasma-treated Ecoflex surface at 500 rpm. Immediately thereafter, a p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel layer with a thickness of approximately 0.8 mm was printed onto the modified silicone surface and photocured in a UV chamber at 365 nm for 15 min (Fig. 4c). The completed bilayer structure was subsequently immersed in a pH 2 buffer at 36.5 °C for 24 h to induce hydrogel contraction and achieve a fully closed initial configuration (Fig. 4d).

The resulting 3D-printed Ecoflex/p(NIPAM-co-AAc) bilayer exhibited pronounced and reversible pH-responsive bending behavior, as shown in Fig. S5 with supplementary movie (Note S6). The reversible actuation behavior originates from the intrinsic swelling-deswelling contrast between the pH-responsive hydrogel and the mechanically stable Ecoflex layer. Importantly, the structural stability of the bilayer interface was further assessed through repeated folding–unfolding actuation tests. The soft robot was subjected to 20 cycles of pH-driven deformation, during which no observable delamination or interfacial failure occurred. These results indicate that the plasma-assisted and silane-mediated bonding strategy provides sufficient interfacial stability for repeated actuation. This robust interfacial stability is attributed to the combined plasma activation and silane-mediated surface modification, which enables effective covalent anchoring between the hydrophobic silicone and the hydrophilic hydrogel [[62], [63], [64]]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the proposed bilayer architecture integrates high actuation sensitivity with structural durability, making it well suited for reliable operation in dynamic and physiologically relevant environments.

Finite element analysis (FEA) of the pH-responsive bilayer soft gripper

The finite element analysis (FEA) results, presented in Fig. 4e and f, reveal the mechanics underlying the pH-dependent morphing of the bilayer soft gripper. At pH 2, contraction of the active hydrogel layer generates a compressive strain mismatch with the non-stimuli responsive passive Ecoflex layer, driving inward bending and resulting in a fully closed configuration that completely encloses the internal cavity (Fig. 4e). Conversely, at pH 8, the hydrogel undergoes significant volumetric swelling, producing a tensile strain mismatch that reverses the bending direction and causes outward curling of the gripper tips (Fig. 4f). This inversion of curvature demonstrates the bilayer's predictable, strain-driven actuation. The simulations confirm that the soft gripper maintains mechanical stability and consistent curvature under acidic conditions, mimicking physiological environments. By effectively translating differential swelling-induced strains into controlled shape change, the bilayer architecture provides reliable and reversible actuation, making it a robust platform for pH-triggered soft robotic applications. Detailed parameters used to evaluate the pH-responsive shape transformation of the soft gripper are provided in the Supporting Information (Note S7 and Table S1).

Specifically, the pH-dependent mechanical behavior is attributed to variations in electrostatic interactions and osmotic pressure within the polymer chains, which alter network density and crosslinking efficiency [65]. Under acidic conditions, protonation of ionizable groups leads to reduced electrostatic repulsion and a more compact network structure, resulting in higher stiffness. In contrast, alkaline conditions promote deprotonation, increasing chain repulsion and network swelling, thereby decreasing the Young's modulus [66]. Such tunable mechanical properties highlight the potential of the hydrogel for applications requiring environment-responsive stiffness, particularly in biomedical engineering systems.

Evaluation of magnetic manipulation and pH-responsive actuation

The magnetic controllability and pH-responsive actuation of the soft robotic platform were evaluated through a maze navigation test to elucidate the interaction between locomotion and shape morphing. Magnetic responsiveness was achieved by incorporating Fe2O3 (3 wt%) nanoparticles into the non-pH-responsive passive Ecoflex layer, enabling remote manipulation of the bilayer structure via external magnetic fields without directly interfering with the hydrogel-based actuation mechanism. As shown in Fig. 5 together with Supplementary Information (Note S8) and the movie (Note S9), the soft gripper was guided through a confined maze by an externally applied magnetic field following a programmed trajectory generated by a collaborative robotic arm. To quantitatively characterize locomotion performance, the soft robot exhibited an average navigation velocity of 0.023 mm/s (total travel distance of 250 mm over 3 h) under a magnetic field strength of 30 mT at the robot's position. This relatively low velocity was intentionally selected to enable detailed observation of navigation behavior within the confined maze environment. The robot is capable of significantly higher locomotion speeds, as demonstrated in separate experiments where it traversed the same 250 mm path within 5 min, corresponding to a velocity that is more relevant for gastrointestinal applications. The geometry-limited minimum turning (steering) radius, constrained by the robot's maximum lateral dimension, was 3.5 mm in the folded configuration (pH 2) and 10 mm in the fully expanded cross-shaped state (pH 8). Furthermore, the operational precision was validated by comparing the positions of the robotic arm's end-effector and the soft robot, yielding a mean tracking error of 2.89 mm throughout the navigation. These results demonstrate that magnetic manipulation and pH-triggered actuation are functionally decoupled yet operate synergistically within the bilayer architecture. Specifically, magnetic fields enable precise and continuous control over the robot's position and orientation, whereas pH variations independently regulate the actuator state and shape transformation. This decoupling allows the soft robot to remain in a mechanically secured transformed configuration under acidic conditions (pH 2) while being magnetically navigated, and subsequently transition to an activated delivery configuration under neutral-to-alkaline conditions (pH 7–8) without loss of navigational controllability. The ability to independently yet concurrently regulate locomotion and actuation highlights the robustness and programmability of the platform, underscoring its suitability for targeted, on-demand drug delivery in the gastrointestinal tract. While magnetic manipulation using collaborative robotic arms has traditionally focused on the precise guidance of rigid magnetic agents [67], such rigid structures inherently lack the ability to deform, posing a risk of mechanical collision or injury in constrained and unstructured biological environments. In contrast, our platform leverages the precise magnetic steering principles but integrates a pH-responsive shape-morphing mechanism. This synergy allows the soft robot to maximize its navigability through narrow passages and ensure biomimetic safety, effectively overcoming the adaptability limitations of conventional non-deformable magnetic actuators [68].

pH-triggered drug release and leakage prevention

The pH-responsive drug release behavior of the soft robot composed of Ecoflex/p(NIPAM-co-AAc) bilayer was first qualitatively evaluated using a fluorescent dye as a model cargo. As shown in Fig. S8 with Supplementary Information (Note S10), the dye-loaded soft gripper underwent gradual structural unfolding when exposed to pH 8 at 36.5 °C, accompanied by progressive diffusion of the green fluorescent signal into the surrounding medium over a 6 h period. This synchronized coupling between pH-induced shape transformation and cargo release is consistent with the simulated deformation behavior, confirming the functional integration of actuation and release within the bilayer architecture.

The drug loading capability of the Ecoflex/p(NIPAM-co-AAc) bilayer soft robot was quantified using Rh6G as a model compound. From the loading experiment, the total amount of Rh6G encapsulated within the hydrogel layer was 0.20 mg, corresponding to an encapsulation efficiency (EE) of approximately 72.4 % relative to the initial Rh6G content in the loading solution. This relatively high loading efficiency indicates that the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel network effectively absorbs and retains small molecules through diffusion and physical entrapment within the polymer matrix.

The stability of the system under gastric-like conditions was assessed by incubating the robots in a pH 2 buffer for 48 h. The total Rh6G released was only 0.00473 mg, representing a negligible leakage percentage of 2.37 %. This behavior is attributed to the collapsed state of the hydrogel network in acidic environments, which restricts molecular diffusion. While this result indicates effective suppression of premature release at the material level, it is important to consider its potential implications under clinically relevant dosing conditions. For potent chemotherapeutic agents, which are typically administered on the order of mg/kg, even a small fraction of premature release in the gastric environment may correspond to a non-negligible absolute mass and could potentially lead to systemic toxicity.

Under alkaline conditions (pH 8, 36.5 °C), the robots exhibited a markedly increased release, reaching 0.157 mg (78.5 %) after 48 h (Fig. 6a), in sharp contrast to the minimal leakage observed under acidic conditions. To further investigate the drug release kinetics, the experimental release data were first analyzed using the Higuchi model, which describes diffusion-controlled drug release from a homogeneous matrix system. According to the Higuchi equation, the cumulative amount of drug released is proportional to the square root of time. As shown in Fig. 6b, a linear relationship was observed between the cumulative release percentage and the square root of time (t). Linear regression analysis yielded the equation y = 36.884x − 25.097 with a high correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.9892), indicating that the Higuchi model provides an excellent description of the release behavior within the studied time range. The strong linearity suggests that diffusion through the swollen hydrogel matrix plays a dominant role in controlling the release of Rh6G from the bilayer system. However, diffusion alone may not fully describe drug release in stimuli-responsive hydrogel systems, where polymer swelling and relaxation can also influence transport behavior.

To further elucidate the drug release mechanism, the experimental release data were fitted to the Korsmeyer–Peppas kinetic model [69]. The logarithmic plot of log(Mt/ M∞) versus log(t) showed a strong linear relationship (Fig. 6c), indicating that the model adequately describes the release behavior within the initial release region. Linear regression analysis yielded a release exponent (n) of 1.01 and a release rate constant (K) of 0.118. The obtained release exponent (n > 0.89) suggests a super case II transport mechanism, indicating that the release process is governed not only by molecular diffusion but also by significant polymer relaxation and swelling of the hydrogel network [69]. This behavior is consistent with the pH-responsive swelling characteristics of the p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel [70]. Consequently, drug release from the Ecoflex/p(NIPAM-co-AAc) bilayer system is primarily controlled by the combined effects of hydrogel swelling dynamics and polymer chain relaxation.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the Ecoflex/p(NIPAM-co-AAc) soft robotic platform exhibits high drug loading capacity, excellent leakage suppression under gastric-like conditions, and efficient drug release under intestinal condition. Furthermore, the hydrogel maintains its functional integrity at fever-range temperatures (39–40 °C) [71], ensuring reliable actuation even under pathological conditions. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the bilayer soft robotic system effectively combines secure cargo encapsulation under acidic conditions with environmentally triggered, on-demand release, underscoring its potential for controlled delivery in physiological environments, including pathological states accompanied by fever.

Biocompatibility and safety of the soft robotic platform

The in vitro cytotoxicity of the soft robotic platform was evaluated in accordance with international standards for the biological evaluation of medical devices (ISO 10993-5 and ISO 10993-12). Sample extracts were prepared following ISO 10993-12 recommendations using an extraction ratio of 0.2 g/mL (or 0.1 g/mL as specified) in cell culture medium, followed by membrane filtration for sterilization. In accordance with ISO guidelines, cytotoxicity was assessed using the standardized mammalian fibroblast cell line (L929), along with human primary cells, including human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) and adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), to ensure biocompatibility across multiple biologically relevant models.

Quantitative evalution using the CCK-8 assay revealed that cell viability following exposure to the soft robotic platform extracts was not significantly different from that of the control group (Fig. 6d–f), indicating negligible cytotoxicity across all tested cell types. Consistent with these findings, the live/dead assay further confirmed high cell viability, with the vast majority of cells exhibiting strong Calcein-AM (green) fluorescence and minimal EthD-1 (red) staining (Fig. 6g–i). No significant cytotoxicity was observed under either short- or long-term exposure conditions. Collectively, these results confirm the excellent biocompatibility of the soft robotic platform, supporting its suitability for integration into advanced biomedical and bio-integrated applications.

Conclusion

This study introduces a silicone/hydrogel bilayer soft robotic platform designed to overcome critical limitations of conventional hydrogel-based drug delivery systems, particularly unintended cargo leakage and limited controllability. By integrating a hydrophobic Ecoflex layer with a p(NIPAM-co-AAc) hydrogel, the proposed architecture enables programmable pH-responsive actuation that directly regulates cargo encapsulation and release, achieving a high encapsulation efficiency of 72.4 %. The strain mismatch generated by hydrogel swelling and deswelling drives reversible, large-amplitude shape transformations, allowing the system to transition between a mechanically secured transport state under acidic conditions and an activated release state under neutral-to-alkaline conditions.

The incorporation of magnetic nanoparticles within the Ecoflex layer provides an additional, independently addressable control modality, enabling precise, non-invasive navigation through confined environments without interfering with hydrogel-based actuation or release mechanisms. This functional decoupling of magnetic manipulation and chemical actuation allows robust positional control during transport while preserving stimulus-specific shape morphing and on-demand delivery. Combined experimental and finite element analyses confirm that the bilayer structure maintains mechanical integrity and predictable actuation behavior under conditions representative of the gastrointestinal tract.

Importantly, the bilayer design effectively suppresses premature cargo leakage in acidic environments through hydrogel contraction and pore densification, limiting leakage to a minimal 2.37 % at pH 2 over 48 h. These results demonstrate that the platform simultaneously achieves secure encapsulation, reliable actuation, and environmentally triggered release, which are key requirements for targeted oral or localized drug delivery, particularly in colonic applications. Furthermore, the soft robotic platform demonstrated excellent biocompatibility; CCK-8 and live/dead assays showed no significant cytotoxicity, maintaining high cell viability in various cells. pNIPAM and its copolymers have demonstrated no significant gastrointestinal (GI) tract toxicity. Previous reports indicate that these materials do not induce intestinal occlusion or leave polymer residues in animal models. Furthermore, histological examinations of GI tissues have revealed no distinct abnormalities, irritation, or inflammatory responses, confirming their excellent biocompatibility within the digestive system. In addition, Ecoflex-based devices exhibited no systemic toxicity or immunologic responses during a 4 week subcutaneous implantation in an in vivo model [72,73]. This safety profile, combined with its robust functional performance, underscores the platform's readiness for interaction with biological tissues. The proposed platform integrates reversible actuation, effective leakage prevention, and magnetic navigability within a single architecture, offering distinct functional advantages over conventional stimuli-responsive drug delivery systems. A comparative analysis with representative existing systems is further provided in Supporting Information (Note S12).

Despite the promising results demonstrated in this study, several translational challenges remain to be addressed. First, while direct ink writing enables precise fabrication, the scalability of this process for clinical manufacturing and reproducibility at larger scales remains to be established. Furthermore, as the current findings are based on in vitro experimental conditions, factors such as the long-term stability of the hydrogel and potential immune responses under in vivo conditions warrant further investigation. In particular, the chemical stability of the Ecoflex–hydrogel interface under gastrointestinal conditions, particularly in the presence of enzymes and bile salts, remains to be investigated. Additionally, while a fluorescent dye was employed as a model cargo in this study, it should be noted that the employed concentration may not reflect physiological dosing of actual therapeutic agents. Loading efficiency and release kinetics are expected to vary depending on the physicochemical properties of the intended therapeutic agent, including differences in molecular size and concentration. Nevertheless, the promising in vitro biocompatibility and drug release behavior observed in this study provide a strong foundation for future development; however, rigorous in vivo validation will be essential before this platform can be considered ready for interaction with biological tissues in clinical or preclinical settings.
