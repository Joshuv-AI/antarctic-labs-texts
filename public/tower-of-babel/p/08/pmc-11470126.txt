Tandem mobilization of anti-phage defenses alongside SCCmec elements in staphylococci
Authors: Motaher Hossain, Barbaros Aslan, Asma Hatoum-Aslan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent research has identified multiple immune systems that bacteria use to protect themselves from viral infections. However, little is known about the mechanisms by which these systems horizontally spread, especially among bacterial pathogens. Here, we investigate antiviral defenses in staphylococci, opportunistic pathogens that constitute leading causes of antibiotic-resistant infections. We show that these organisms harbor a variety of anti-phage defenses encoded within or near SCC (staphylococcal cassette chromosome) mec cassettes, mobile genomic islands that confer methicillin resistance. Importantly, we demonstrate that SCCmec-encoded recombinases mobilize not only SCCmec, but also tandem SCC-like cassettes enriched in genes coding for diverse defense systems. Further, we show that phage infection stimulates cassette mobilization (i.e. excision and circularization). Thus, our findings indicate that SCC/SCCmec cassettes not only spread antibiotic resistance but can also play a role in mobilizing anti-phage defenses.Subject terms: Bacterial genetics, Bacteriophages, CRISPR-Cas systems

Introduction

Staphylococci are ubiquitous skin-dwelling bacteria that play critical roles in health and disease. Over 40 different human-associated Staphylococcus species have been identified1,2, and the majority are considered skin commensals with neutral or even positive impacts3. However, two species in particular, S. aureus and S. epidermidis, have significant pathogenic potential—S. aureus causes a wide array of hospital- and community- acquired infections, including bacteremia, osteomyelitis, and skin and soft tissue infections4; and S. epidermidis is the most common cause of infections associated with implanted medical devices5. Compounding the problem, S. epidermidis harbors a reservoir of fitness/virulence factors which can be horizontally transferred to S. aureus2,6. These include genes responsible for methicillin resistance (mecA/C) encoded on SCC (staphylococcal cassette chromosome) mec cassettes7,8. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) poses a serious threat to global public health9,10 and the disease burden has only worsened following the COVID-19 pandemic11. Further, multi-drug resistant S. epidermidis strains constitute an emerging global threat12. Thus, in order to develop effective therapeutic alternatives, it is critical to understand the major pathways that control the horizontal transfer of fitness/virulence factors between these species.

Bacterial viruses (phages) and the immune systems targeted against them have profound and opposing impacts on bacterial survival, evolution, and horizontal gene exchange13. For instance, strictly lytic phages can kill their host within minutes of infection, and accordingly are being harnessed for therapeutic applications to eradicate staphylococcal infections14–16. In contrast, the lysogenic/temperate phages may integrate into the host chromosome and are known to boost pathogenic potential by transferring virulence factors or pathogenicity islands to the host13. In response to the constant pressure of phage predation, bacteria have evolved a variety of immune systems that counter these diverse effects. At least four such systems have been identified and functionally validated in S. aureus and S. epidermidis strains—restriction-modification (RM)17, CRISPR-Cas18, a mechanism of abortive infection (i.e. programmed cell death) facilitated by the serine-threonine kinase Stk219, and a unique innate immune system mediated by the nuclease-helicase Nhi20. These systems antagonize lytic and lysogenic phages alike, and therefore have the capacity to not only curb pathogenic potential, but also compromise the effectiveness of phage-based therapeutics. Although significant headway has been made in recent years towards identifying and functionally validating the diverse immune repertoire in a few model organisms21–25, the full battery of anti-phage defenses in staphylococci has not been systematically explored. Additionally, little is known about the predominant pathways by which these systems horizontally spread.

To shed light on these issues, we used bioinformatic and molecular approaches to investigate the inventory of defenses in staphylococci and their mechanisms of mobilization. Bioinformatic analyses of sequenced collections of S. epidermidis and S. aureus strains revealed that many of these organisms harbor homologs of diverse anti-phage defenses within/near the boundaries of SCC/SCCmec cassettes. Moreover, follow-up molecular analyses of representative staphylococcal species showed that overexpression of SCCmec-encoded recombinases CcrAB/CcrC stimulates the mobilization (i.e. excision and circularization) of not only SCCmec, but also tandem independent SCC-like elements that are enriched with diverse defenses. We also explored the impact of phage infection on cassette mobilization, and found that infection by diverse lytic phages causes release of ~10–200 -fold more circularized cassettes than does enzymatic lysis of cells. Taken together, our results identify SCC and tandem SCC-like elements as major hotspots for anti-phage defenses in staphylococci and suggest that phage-induced lysis constitutes an important path for their horizontal spread.

Results

Diverse anti-phage defenses are enriched within/near SCC/SCCmec cassettes

To investigate the breadth of defenses that staphylococci employ, we first examined the distribution and localization of homologs for all known anti-phage defenses in RefSeq collections of S. epidermidis (n = 89) and S. aureus strains (n = 982). This was accomplished by programmatically invoking MacSyFinder26 for each genome using the Hidden Markov Models and system definitions library of DefenseFinder27. The results revealed that staphylococci possess a diverse battery of defenses comprising 48 distinct immune system types (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1). Further, we noted that over 40% of the organisms in the dataset harbor 50% or more of their defenses within 300 genes downstream of rlmH (Fig. 1A). rlmH, also called orfX, is a core housekeeping gene downstream of which SCC/SCCmec cassettes are known to reside28,29. Here it bears mentioning that 300 genes (a number that was originally chosen to ensure sufficient scope of analysis downstream of rlmH) represents only ~14.6% of S. epidermidis genomes (σ = 0.9%), and ~12.3% of S. aureus genomes (σ = 0.6%). These initial observations alerted us to the possibility that SCC/SCCmec cassettes might be playing a significant role in the spread of anti-phage defenses.

To test this idea, we performed a more detailed analysis of the proteins proximal to RlmH—all proteins encoded by the 200 genes upstream and 500 genes downstream of rlmH were analyzed for their identities, levels of conservation, and predicted defense functions. The results show the protein content for the collections of S. epidermidis and S. aureus genome segments as a polar graph where every angle corresponds to a genome, and distance from the origin corresponds to a specific gene (Fig. 1B and C, respectively and Supplementary Data 2 and 3). The plots show that while RlmH (yellow ring around the origin) is preceded by highly-conserved (i.e. core) proteins upstream (dark blue bars in center), it is followed by a sharp transition into a region of poorly-conserved (i.e. accessory) proteins downstream (light blue bars). These accessory regions are flanked by another stretch of highly-conserved proteins further downstream (dark blue periphery). If we define the upstream boundary of the ‘accessory region’ as RlmH, and the downstream boundary as the occurrence of three consecutive proteins that exceed 95% conservation across all genomes, then the accessory region lengths exhibit a range (5th to 95th percentile) spanning 54 to 136 proteins for S. epidermidis and 16 to 83 proteins for S. aureus. In nucleotide terms, these segment lengths correspond to 3.4% of the S. epidermidis and 6.1% of the S. aureus genomes (95th percentile). Strikingly, genes that encode known defense systems (red bars) in this analysis window are highly concentrated in the accessory regions. Indeed, Chi-squared tests confirmed that the accessory regions downstream of rlmH of both species are highly enriched in defenses, against the null hypothesis of a uniform distribution of defenses across the genomes (Supplementary Data 4). Also, the majority of the analyzed genomes encode between one and five putative Ccr recombinases (black dots), the enzymes that mobilize SCC/SCCmec cassettes30,31 (Supplementary Data 2 and 3). This observation suggests that these genomes likely harbor at least one SCC element. There are at least 29 different defense types within the accessory regions, and interestingly, most of these (n = 16) are located exclusively in this region, including CRISPR-Cas, Stk2, and Nhi (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Data 1).

We also compared the diversity of anti-phage defenses encoded within versus outside of the accessory regions by adopting the ratio of exponentiated Shannon entropies (2H, where H is Shannon entropy), as a diversity metric32. This analysis suggests that defense systems inside the accessory regions are at least as diverse as defenses outside of these regions in the case of S. aureus, and almost three times more diverse in the case of S. epidermidis (Supplementary Fig. 1, inset). Taken together, these observations suggest that beyond carrying antibiotic resistance, SCC/SCCmec cassettes constitute a major hotspot for diverse anti-phage defenses. However, these cassettes are typically 24-68 kilobases (kb) in length and contain ~20–100 genes, respectively28,29, and many of the accessory regions downstream of rlmH extend well beyond these lengths (Fig. 1B and C). Therefore, it is unlikely that all defenses are encoded within the currently established bounds of the cassettes.

Ccr recombinases drive mobilization of defenses in tandem SCC-like elements

To investigate further, we examined more closely S. epidermidis RP62a as a representative of the sequenced set (number 43 in Fig. 1B). This organism harbors a Type II SCCmec cassette (~ 48 kb in length) that encodes ~50 proteins, including the CcrA and CcrB recombinases8. These enzymes bind attachment (att) sites flanking SCCmec30,31,33 and catalyze cassette excision and circularization as prerequisite steps for the inter-genus horizontal transfer of the entire cassette30,31. Our manual inspection of the RP62a SCCmec region revealed that only one known defense (stk2) lies within the bounds of the SCCmec cassette (Fig. 2A). However there appear to be at least three additional CcrAB att consensus sequences between SCCmec and the remaining defenses (Fig. 2A and B). These observations hinted at the compelling possibility that defenses encoded proximal to SCCmec may also be mobilized by CcrAB as separate/independent cassettes. To test this, we introduced the ccrAB operon from S. epidermidis RP62a under its native promoter into a multi-copy plasmid and transferred the plasmid (called pSepiCcrAB) into S. epidermidis RP62a. Cells were then grown to mid-log, and their DNA was extracted and subjected to PCR using a set of primer pairs specifically designed to detect excision and circularization of SCCmec and the putative defense-containing cassettes (Fig. 2A). The results revealed that overexpression of ccrAB indeed stimulates excision and circularization of not only SCCmec, but also two adjacent independent cassettes containing the Nhi, RM, and CRISPR-Cas systems (Fig. 2C–E). The Nhi-RM and CRISPR-Cas cassettes are ~17 kb and ~26 kb in length, respectively. The cassettes can be excised independently, in pairs, or all three can be found linked together (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B). Interestingly, the excision of all three cassettes could be readily detected by conventional PCR even without CcrAB overexpression (Fig. 2F and G).

We also examined the SCCmec regions of two additional clinical isolates which harbor Type III CRISPR-Cas systems—S. aureus MSHR1132, a community-associated MRSA strain recently re-classified as S. argenteus34,35, and S. aureus ST398 08BA02176, a livestock-associated strain recovered from a human surgical site infection36. MSHR1132 possesses a Type IVa SCCmec cassette (~ 24 kb) that encodes the CcrAB recombinases, and the Type III CRISPR-Cas system appears to be located downstream of the cassette flanked by additional att sites (Supplementary Fig. 3A and B). To test if CcrAB overexpression promotes excision/circularization of these tandem cassettes, the ccrAB operon with its native promoter was inserted into a multicopy plasmid to create pSarCcrAB, the plasmid was introduced back into the host, and DNA extracts were assayed for evidence of cassette excision/circularization using conventional PCR. The results showed that tandem cassettes are indeed generated, but unlike RP62a, cassette circularization in MSHR1132 can be detected even in the absence of pSarCcrAB (Supplementary Fig. 3C and D, EV lane). Accordingly, excision of both cassettes was also detected without ccrAB overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 3E and F). In lieu of CcrAB, some SCCmec cassettes are mobilized by a single serine recombinase, CcrC, and we wondered whether its overexpression stimulates defense mobilization. To test this, we examined S. aureus ST398 08BA02176, which harbors a Type III CRISPR-Cas system within the bounds of a Type V SCCmec cassette (Supplementary Fig. 4A and B)36. We created pSauCcrC (which bears ccrC under its native promoter), introduced the plasmid into ST398, and assayed DNA extracts for cassette mobilization via PCR amplification. We found that similarly to ccrAB in S. epidermidis RP62a, ccrC overexpression in S. aureus ST398 stimulates excision and circularization of the CRISPR-containing SCCmec cassette (Supplementary Fig. 4C and D).

Given that cassette excision occurs in the absence of Ccr overexpression, we sought to quantify the baseline excision frequencies in representative S. epidermidis and S. aureus strains. To do so, we used quantitative PCR (qPCR) to determine, in a given genomic DNA sample, the fraction of genome copies that have lost all cassettes. The results showed that in S. epidermidis RP62a, between one and six genomes per 100,000 copies exhibit spontaneous loss of all cassettes, and ccrAB overexpression causes over 10-fold increase in this excision frequency (Fig. 2H). A similar assay conducted for S. aureus ST398 revealed a baseline excision frequency of 10-fold lower (~ 1 ×10−6), and this value similarly increased by an order of magnitude in the presence of ccrC overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 4E). Drawing on these collective observations—particularly the facts that (1) cassettes are found to be excised in all possible combinations, and (2) overexpression of Ccr recombinases that reside within SCCmec increases excision frequencies by more than 10-fold—we arrived at one of our main conclusions: Ccr recombinases drive the mobilization (i.e. excision and circularization) of diverse defenses encoded both within the SCCmec cassette and just outside (i.e. proximal) of its boundaries in separate tandem cassettes.

Long-term overexpression of CcrAB causes loss of proximal defense-containing cassettes

In light of the above, we next wondered whether these enzymes can also cause excision and circularization of distal genomic loci. To investigate this possibility, we first took a bioinformatics approach to identify putative att consensus sites in the sequenced S. epidermidis collection by searching for the minimal CcrB binding motif30,31,33. This analysis identified 1,225 putative att sites across the 89 S. epidermidis genomes, with many sites appearing to reside outside the accessory region downstream of rlmH (Supplementary Fig. 5A and B and Supplementary Data 5 and 6). Moreover, the genomes harbor between one and six pairs of distal att sites within 150 kb (maximum) of each other on the same strand, thus demarcating putative distal cassettes (Supplementary Fig. 5A, red dots). These observations prompted us to investigate the extent to which CcrAB may mobilize additional genomic loci flanked by these putative att consensus sites.

To test this, we examined more closely S. epidermidis RP62a, which harbors 15 putative att sites. We found that nine of these sites exist within the accessory region, and a subset demarcate two additional putative cassettes that lie directly downstream of the CRISPR-containing cassette (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Further, six additional sites lie distal to SCCmec, and three of these demarcate two putative distal cassettes. To determine whether CcrAB can cause loss of these additional proximal/distal genomic loci, we used directed evolution to generate mutant strains that have lost all possible cassettes via long-term overexpression of ccrAB. Briefly, RP62a strains bearing pSepiCcrAB were passaged over ~50 generations, and colonies were screened for the loss of resistance to spermine, which is conferred by speG within the Nhi-RM cassette (Fig. 3A). Colonies that exhibited sensitivity to spermine were purified and confirmed for loss of all cassettes downstream of rlmH by PCR amplification (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 6). In addition, to rule out the possibility that cassettes might still be present in the cell, perhaps in an alternative location or in a circularized episomal form, we challenged the spermine-sensitive isolates with phage CNPx, which is targeted independently by defenses encoded within each of the three cassettes: Stk219, Nhi20, and CRISPR-Cas37. As expected, while CNPx cannot form plaques (i.e. zones of bacterial growth inhibition) on the ancestral RP62a strain, it forms ~108 plaques on three independently-generated evolved isolates (Fig. 3C), thus confirming the complete loss of all three cassettes. To determine the extent to which these mutants have lost additional genomic loci, we purified genomic DNA from the isolates, subjected the DNA to Illumina sequencing, and mapped the sequencing reads back to an assembly of the RP62a ancestral genome. The results showed that despite the presence of predicted att sites, CcrAB-mediated genomic loss in RP62a is restricted to SCCmec and the two proximal defense-containing cassettes (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 7).

Diverse antibiotics have little/no impact on cassette excision frequencies

We next explored the impacts of various environmental stimuli on cassette excision/circularization. In a previous report, DNA-damaging antibiotics were shown to stimulate SCCmec excision in S. aureus38. Thus, we wondered whether such stressors would have a similar impact on SCCmec and tandem defense-containing cassettes in our system. To test this, we measured cassette excision frequencies in S. epidermidis RP62a following treatment with a range of antimicrobials--oxacillin, spermine, trimethoprim, sulfamethoxazole, and ciprofloxacin. Oxacillin and spermine were chosen because SCCmec and proximal cassettes encode the mecA and speG genes, respectively, which confer resistance to these antimicrobials. The other three antibiotics were chosen because they are known to cause DNA damage, either directly or indirectly. We first determined the minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for each antimicrobial (Supplementary Data 7). Cells were then grown to mid-log and treated with a range of concentrations of each antibiotic (from 2 × MIC to 0.125 × MIC) for 30 minutes. DNA extracts from treated cells were then subjected to qPCR to measure cassette excision frequencies. The results showed that none of the antibiotics change the baseline excision frequencies of SCCmec and proximal cassettes (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Diverse phages stimulate release of circularized defense-containing cassettes

In light of our findings that tandem defense-enriched cassettes exist in an equilibrium of excised and integrated states in the absence of external stimulation (Fig. 2H and Supplementary Fig. 4E), we wanted to investigate the extent to which phages stimulate release of these cassettes. To test this, we designed an experiment to quantify copy numbers of circularized cassettes released from cells following challenge with a panel of diverse phages (Fig. 4A). The phages exhibit a range of sensitivities to defenses encoded within the cassettes—from completely resistant (e.g. ISP) to partially or fully sensitive (CNPx) (Fig. 4B). As controls, cells were grown in the absence of phage, or cells were enzymatically lysed by digesting heat-killed cells with lysostaphin (a cell wall-degrading enzyme). Following growth/lysis, cells and phage were pelleted and filtered out, and the DNA released from cells was extracted and subjected to qPCR with primers flanking the circularization junction of the largest cassette as a proxy for all other cassette variations. As expected, we found that while uninfected cells or cells challenged with the sensitive phage CNPx release ~103 cassettes, cells challenged with lytic phages Andhra, Southeast, Twillingate, and ISP release millions of cassettes (between 106−108, Fig. 4C). Notably, we also observed that phage-induced lysis causes release of significantly more cassettes (10-200 -fold) than does enzymatic lysis of cells, suggesting that beyond passively releasing cassettes via cell lysis, phage infection may stimulate cassette excision frequencies above the spontaneous rates.

Although the above experiment was designed to quantify cassettes released from cells, we considered the possibility that a fraction of the detected circularized cassettes might be encapsidated in phage particles that were inadvertently carried over into the supernatant. To explore this, we designed an experiment to quantify cassettes packaged in phage capsids (Supplementary Fig. 9A). In this experiment, cells (S. epidermidis RP62a or RP62a Δcassettes) were infected with the four phages that cause cell lysis (Andhra, Southeast, Twillingate, and ISP), phage particles in cell lysates were harvested, free (unpackaged) DNA was degraded using DNase, phages were subsequently precipitated, and DNA within phage particles was extracted and subjected to qPCR to quantify numbers of packaged cassettes. The data showed that while cassette numbers are below the limit of detection in phages following infection of S. epidermidis RP62a Δcassettes, some cassettes (just above the limit of detection) were detected in all phages following infection of the wild-type strain (Supplementary Fig. 9B). However, since the capsids of phages Andhra and Southeast (with genome sizes of ~18.5 kb and ~47.2 kb, respectively) are too small to accommodate the triple-linked cassette (~ 90.5 kb), we speculate the latter signal likely originates from contaminating/undigested DNA carried over from the cell lysate, rather than from cassettes packaged within capsids.

The results thus far demonstrate that infection and cell lysis by diverse phages stimulates the release of triple-linked SCCmec-Nhi-RM-CRISPR cassettes. However, we wondered about the extent to which other cassette combinations are released into the cell lysate. To test this, we repeated the experiment in Fig. 4A using phage Andhra as a representative (in six independent trials), and examined all possible combinations of cassettes released into the supernatant by subjecting the released DNA to Illumina sequencing. The resulting reads were mapped to each predicted circle junction and corresponding scar junction formed as a result of cassette excision. We then quantified normalized counts of reads that cross each circle and scar junction. In addition to analyzing the SCCmec-proximal cassettes, we looked for evidence of excision and circularization of the two predicted distal cassettes (Supplementary Data 6), as well as five randomly-generated cassettes of similar sizes from regions overlapping the distal cassettes as controls (Supplementary Data 8). The results showed that while random cassettes are largely undetectable, there is clear evidence of circularization of all other cassette combinations, including the two distal cassettes (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, there are about twice as many copies of the triple-linked cassette than any other cassette combination (significance measured in pairwise t-tests, p < 0.0005). Taken together, these observations support a model in which diverse phages stimulate the excision and release of SCCmec and tandem defense-enriched cassettes (Fig. 4E).

Discussion

Here, we investigate anti-phage defenses in staphylococci, with a focus on S. epidermidis and S. aureus, two staphylococcal species with the highest pathogenic potential in humans4,5. We demonstrate that these organisms harbor homologs for diverse defenses within/near SCC/SCCmec cassettes (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), and overexpression of SCCmec-encoded Ccr recombinases causes mobilization (i.e. excision and circularization) of not only SCCmec, but also independent tandem defense-containing cassettes (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 2–4). Moreover, we showed that while diverse phages are unlikely to package these cassettes following infection of a representative S. epidermidis strain (Supplementary Fig. 9), they stimulate the release of significantly more cassettes (~ 10-200-fold) than does enzymatic lysis of cells (Fig. 4). Taken together, our observations provide strong support for the hypothesis that phage infection facilitates the spread of diverse defenses by making them available in compact circularized cassettes, ready for uptake by neighboring cells via natural transformation.

Although SCCmec cassettes have been studied for decades28, and their capacity to be transferred between different staphylococcal species is well-established39, the predominant pathway(s) by which they are transferred from donor to recipient cells in a clinical context remains unclear. For instance, one study demonstrated SCCmec transfer can occur by recombining into a conjugative plasmid and entering recipient cells via conjugation40. Another study showed that SCCmec cassettes (Types I and IV) could be packaged and transferred by transducing phages when donor cells are grown in the presence of methicillin at a low temperature, but transfer is restricted to small SCCmec cassettes (< ~40 kb) due to phage packaging constraints41. In a third study, Type II SCCmec uptake from DNA extracts was observed when cells were grown in a specific media, presumably via natural transformation42. Similarly, a fourth study demonstrated SCCmec transfer between specific S. aureus mating pairs in biofilm growth conditions when donors are subjected to heat-killing43. Notably, in all of these studies, transfer frequencies are exceedingly low (ranging from 10−8 to 10−10), highlighting the difficulty of reconstituting the transfer process in a laboratory setting. Nonetheless, inspired by these studies, we set up a system and attempted to demonstrate cassette transfer between S. epidermidis RP62a and S. aureus Newman44 (a strain devoid of SCCmec and other detectable cassettes) under various stress conditions (e.g. during phage challenge or the presence of cell wall targeting antibiotics) through multiple pathways, including transduction by a panel of phages (Andhra, Southeast, CNPx, and Twillingate), and natural transformation through pairwise mating experiments on filters or in a biofilm context. However, multiple attempts proved unsuccessful in recovering S. aureus recipients that have taken up any of the cassettes (Supplementary Data 9). It is possible that our experimental setup and selection scheme are not sensitive enough to detect the rare transformants, or the mating pair may be otherwise incompatible. Nonetheless, in light of the high prevalence of SCCmec cassettes in clinical S. epidermidis and S. aureus isolates28,45, the technical difficulty of tracking the released cassettes from donor cells to their final destinations in a lab setting does not diminish the significance of our findings or its implications (discussed below).

While functional analyses of SCCmec cassettes have historically focused on the cargo of antibiotic resistance genes they carry, other accessory elements (encoded in so-called ‘junkyard’ or ‘joining’ regions) have remained largely uncharacterized28,29. Here, our findings strongly suggest that beyond spreading antibiotic resistance, SCCmec cassettes and the recombinases within play a central role in mobilizing diverse anti-phage defenses encoded both within and outside the bounds of the SCCmec cassette (Figs. 1 and 2). Our observations support the notion that SCCmec and surrounding regions constitute fertile grounds for new defenses and other fitness factors yet to be identified. Moreover, the ability of SCCmec-encoded recombinases to mobilize additional genomic segments blurs the definition of what precisely constitutes an SCCmec cassette. The prevailing view is that SCCmec cassettes are discrete mobile elements with well-defined boundaries28,29; however, our observations hint at a more expansive view of these elements as contiguous collections of accessory genes flanked by Ccr att sites, where Ccr recombinases need only be present in a single cassette to facilitate movement of the rest. Our data shows that all combinations of cassettes are generated by Ccr recombinases, including individual and double-linked defense-containing cassettes that are devoid of Ccr recombinases. Although the latter cassettes are at a clear disadvantage for being transferred and integrated into recipients on their own, it is conceivable that they would be capable of being incorporated into recipient genomes via Ccr recombinases in the recipient host should it already possess an SCC/SCCmec cassette. In agreement with this notion, some S. epidermidis and S. aureus strains have been found to harbor ACME (arginine catabolic mobile element) and COMER (copper and mercury resistance) cassettes flanked by att sites downstream of SCCmec46–49. Similar to the defense-enriched cassettes described in this study, ACME and COMER elements lack their own recombinases and are mobilized by those encoded within SCCmec.

The evolution of such modularity in cassette architecture may confer multiple advantages in facilitating cassette transmission, reception, and retention. For instance, collections of cassettes have the benefit of maintaining high carrying capacity while also keeping open the option of being mobilized as smaller nested segments which are more amenable to acquisition by the next host. Indeed, staphylococcal transducing phages that have been shown to transfer SCCmec cassettes41 have strict packaging constraints, thus making smaller genomic segments more amenable to packaging and transfer via transduction. Moreover, from a risk mitigation perspective, genomic segments traveling independently as smaller cassettes maximize their potential to survive in transit while being challenged with RM, CRISPR-Cas, or other nucleic acid degrading defenses. Finally, harboring multiple copies of att sites throughout nested cassettes ensures that at least a subset maintains mobility should random mutagenesis render one or more sites unrecognizable.

Our observation that phage lysis stimulates the release of defense-enriched cassettes alongside SCCmec (Fig. 4) allows for an unsettling prediction—the very same mechanisms that mediate the spread of methicillin resistance are likely to compromise the long-term effectiveness of phage-based therapeutics. These findings underscore the need for the preemptive development of adjunctive therapeutic strategies that intervene with the mobilization and transfer of these cassettes. It is also imperative to identify the other locations where anti-phage defenses reside. Indeed, not all of the analyzed staphylococcal genomes maintain the bulk of their defenses within/near SCC/SCCmec cassettes (Fig. 1A). This observation raises the question—where are the other defenses located? A recent study showed that S. aureus pathogenicity islands (SaPIs) constitute hotspots for anti-phage defenses50. SaPIs are a family of short (< 20 kb) mobile elements that parasitize specific helper phages to facilitate their own packaging and spread. Indeed, there has been an increasing awareness that diverse bacteria harbor an assortment of defenses within mobile elements (MGEs) including similar parasitic phage-like elements (also known as satellites)25,50,51, plasmids52, transposons53, integrative conjugative elements54, and integrated temperate phages (prophages)25,55,56. Such defenses are thought to play major roles in inter-MGE conflicts57. Our ongoing work continues to investigate the localization of defenses across staphylococci and seeks to identify new immune systems. These efforts will not only shed light on the predominant pathways that mediate phage-host interactions but are also likely to enable the development of more effective and robust alternative therapeutics.

Methods

Computational analyses

For analysis of defense types and localization in staphylococci (Fig. 1), the RefSeq collections of S. epidermidis and S. aureus genomes were downloaded in GenBank format on Nov. 21, 2021 and July 5, 2022, respectively. RlmH was located in each genome, and all proteins were oriented in the same direction such that RlmH can be read from left to right. To generate the polar plots in Fig. 1B and C, the sequences of the 200 proteins upstream and 500 proteins downstream of RlmH were extracted and stored in a matrix where the rows correspond to protein positions (0 to 700), and the columns to the genomes in the dataset. To determine conservation levels, all protein sequences in the matrix were processed as a unified dataset using a hierarchical clustering algorithm we implemented. The algorithm dynamically aggregates protein sequences into clusters based on their pairwise similarity using the concept of Levenshtein edit ratio58 and a threshold value of 0.95. To determine the defense-relatedness flag (binary) for each protein sequence element of the matrix, we used MacSyFinder version 2.126 and Hidden Markov Model (HMM) system definition library of DefenseFinder27 (updated on January 14, 2024) to scan each genome for the presence of complete (known) defense systems. Only the component genes of a fully detected system were flagged as defense-related in the protein sequence matrix. In the end, the resulting matrix comprises protein sequences associated with two attributes: their conservation level, represented as a percentage value between 0 and 100%, and a binary indicator (0 or 1) that denotes whether a given protein sequence corresponds to a defense-related gene, along with the name of the corresponding HMM model. The other attributes such as whether the protein sequence is RlmH or identified as a Ccr recombinase are determined through the presence of associated protein family (pfam) domains using hmmer 3.3.259.

To assess the enrichment of defense systems within a region of interest (ROI) (300 genes downstream of rlmH or the variable accessory region), a Chi-squared (χ²) test was employed. A hypothetical stochastic process was assumed in disbursing the total number of known defenses evenly across a given genome, without favoring any specific region (the null hypothesis). As such, expected frequencies inside and outside the ROI were calculated by partitioning the total number of defenses accordingly: N × τ defenses inside and N × (1 - τ) outside, where N is the total number of defense systems in a specific genome and τ is the nucleotide length of the ROI divided by the nucleotide length of the entire genome. Since τ varies from genome to genome, we adopted a conservative approach by using τ = μτ + 3στ, where μτ and στ are the mean and standard deviation. Further, we repeated the analysis after removing from consideration those genomes that have more than half their defenses in the ROI and still were able to reject the null hypothesis in favor of enrichment for both species (See Supplementary Data 4). It should be noted that a per-genome multiple testing approach was deemed not feasible since individual total defense system counts do not provide sufficient sample sizes to yield acceptable statistical power (see Supplementary Data 2 and 3).

To quantify diversity of defenses, we calculated Shannon entropy (H) for defense system distributions both inside and outside the accessory region32. The Shannon entropies of defense systems were calculated as the sum of psys x log2psys, where psys is the probability of the defense system in a given partition (e.g., inside or outside). Diversity ratio is the ratio of the exponentiated entropies: 2H_in / 2H_out.

For the att consensus site analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5), a two-step iterative approach was employed. A consensus (counts matrix) was derived from 16 predicted and/or experimentally-verified CcrB binding sites compiled from the literature31,33,47 and our own observations (Fig. 2B), and a preliminary position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM) was calculated using the background nucleotide frequencies derived from the 89 S. epidermidis genomes. Next, rlmH was located in each of the 89 S. epidermidis genomes, genome nucleotide sequences were oriented such that rlmH can be read from left to right, and the rlmH translational start site was designated as position 1. Programmatically invoking FIMO60 (MEME suite) using the preliminary PSSM, every preprocessed genome sequence was searched for matching motif hits (in both forward and reverse complement directions) with p-values < 1 × 10−6. A final PSSM was created from these hit sequences (Supplementary Data 10) and the search was repeated for hits with p-values < 1 × 10−6, which were subsequently clustered into two categories—those falling inside the ‘accessory region’ (i.e. light blue region downstream of rlmH in Fig. 1 B), and those falling outside. For hits outside the accessory region, a one-dimensional clustering algorithm was used to detect motif instance pairs on the same strand that were close enough to demarcate a putative cassette (i.e. < 150 kb in length). It should be noted that, instead of extending the motif (the counts matrix) in all directions using arbitrary pseudocounts (which is equivalent to Bayesian inference with an assumed uniform prior), we opted for an iterative motif refinement approach (conceptually akin to PSI-BLAST). This strategy serves as an alternative to address the data sparsity of the original 16 att sites (experimentally verified) while staying true to the underlying signal captured in the original motif. In the final set, p-values are still bounded by 1 × 10−6, but with some genomes, associated q-values61 up to 0.25 were also observed.

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

All parental bacterial strains were a generous gift from Luciano Marraffini. S. epidermidis RP62a62 and S. argenteus MSHR113234 were grown in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI, BD Diagnostics). S. aureus RN422063, ST39836, and Newman44 were grown in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB, BD Diagnostics). Growth media was supplemented with 10 μg/mL chloramphenicol (for selection of pC194-based plasmids) and 15 μg/mL neomycin (for S. epidermidis strains). All bacterial strains were grown at 37 °C. Liquid cultures were propagated with agitation in an orbital shaker set to 180 rpm. Strains were routinely authenticated via PCR amplification and sequencing genomic regions unique to each strain.

Constructing ccr overexpression plasmids and strains

Plasmids pSepiCcrAB, pSarCcrAB, and pSauCcrC were created to overexpress Ccr recombinases. These plasmids were created using the multicopy plasmid pC19464 as backbone and genomic DNA from S. epidermidis RP62a, S. argenteus MSHR1132, and S. aureus ST398, respectively, to amplify ccr inserts. Plasmids were assembled via two-piece Gibson Assembly65 using the primers listed in Supplementary Data 11. Following Gibson assembly, constructs were introduced into S. aureus RN4220 by electroporation. Electrocompetent cells were prepared as described by Monk and colleagues66. For transformation, competent cells were thawed on ice for 5 min and left at room temperature for another 5 min. Cells were then pelleted via centrifugation at 5000 × g for 1 min. The pellet was resuspended in 50 μL of sterilized 10% glycerol containing 500 mM sucrose, and the dialyzed Gibson assembly mix was added into the cell suspension. The mixture was then transferred into a 2 mm electroporation cuvette (VWR) and pulsed at 2.1 kV/cm, 100 W, and 25 μF with a GenePulser Xcell instrument (Bio-Rad). Cells were then allowed to recover in 1 mL of sterile TSB containing 500 mM sucrose at 37 °C with agitation for 2 h. Recovered cells (200 μL) were plated on TSA or BHI agar supplemented with appropriate antibiotics and incubated at 37 °C. Transformants were recovered the following day and confirmed for the presence of the intended plasmid by PCR amplification of the junctions of the assembled plasmids and Sanger sequencing using primers MH070 and F016 (Supplementary Data 11). At least three transformants were confirmed, confirmed plasmids were purified from S. aureus RN4220 using the E.Z.N.A.® Plasmid DNA Mini Kit I (Omega Bio-Tek, Inc, GA, USA), and purified plasmids pSepiCcrAB, pSauCcrC, and pSarCcrAB, were transferred into the appropriate strain (RP62a, ST398, and MSHR1132, respectively).

Detecting cassette circle and excision junctions using conventional PCR

Amplification of circle and excision junctions was performed in 25 μL PCR reactions containing 25–100 ng of DNA template (plasmid miniprep), primers listed in Supplementary Data 11, and Phusion high fidelity DNA polymerase (NEB) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR products were resolved on 1% agarose gels. For sequence confirmation, PCR products were purified using the E.Z.N.A.® Cycle Pure kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Inc, GA, USA), product concentrations were measured using the NanoDrop™ 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and products were submitted for Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics, Louisville, KY) and/or Illumina sequencing (SeqCenter, Pittsburgh, PA).

Preparing bacterial genomic DNA for Illumina sequencing and qPCR

Overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 in TSB/BHI and grown at 37 °C until OD600 reached 1. Cultures (20 mL) were then transferred to 50 mL conical tubes and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were discarded, washed once with 20 mL fresh TSB/BHI, and pellets were stored at −80 °C. For DNA extraction, cell pellets were resuspended with 200 μL of sterile water and transferred into microtubes. Resuspended pellets were then incubated with lysostaphin (100 μg/mL) and MgCl2 (5 mM) at 37 °C for 2 h. The Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega Corporation, WI, USA) was used to extract the genomic DNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Final DNA pellets were dissolved in 50–60 μL of prewarmed DNase-free water. DNA concentrations were measured using the NanoDrop™ 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the samples were stored at 4 °C for short-term use or at −20 °C for long-term use.

Constructing plasmids for qPCR standard curves

All PCR primers used for plasmid constructs are listed in Supplementary Data 11. Plasmids pSepiStdEx, pSauStdEx, and pSepiStdCirc were created to use as qPCR standards (Std) to measure numbers of cassette excision (Ex) and circle (Circ) junctions in S. epidermidis RP62a (pSepi) and S. aureus ST398 (pSau). All plasmids were constructed via two-piece Gibson assembly65 using PCR primers listed in Supplementary Data 11. Plasmid pC19464 was used as backbone, and genomic DNA for inserts as follows: The 894 bp insert for pSepiStdEx was amplified from the genomic DNA preparation of S. epidermidis RP62a Δcassette strain (which contains the chromosomal junction formed upon the loss of SCCmec and tandem cassettes) using primers MH067 and MH068. The 960 bp insert for the pSepiStdCirc construct was amplified from a genomic DNA preparation of the S. epidermidis RP62a strain using primers MH109 and MH110. Similarly, the 858 bp insert of pSauStdEx was amplified from the genomic DNA of S. aureus ST398 using primers MH085 and MH086. All Gibson assembled constructs were transferred into S. aureus RN4220 and confirmed as outlined in the section above. Confirmed plasmids were purified, quantified, and used directly in qPCR assays to create standard curves.

Measuring cassette excision frequencies and circle numbers using qPCR

All qPCR primers are listed in Supplementary Data 11. To determine cassette excision frequencies, primer pairs MH065/MH066 and MH089/MH090 were designed as controls to measure total genome copy numbers—these primers amplify the 5’-end of rlmH in S. epidermidis RP62a and S. aureus ST398, respectively. In addition, primer pairs MH081/MH084 and MH092/MH096 were designed to measure the numbers of genome copies that have lost all cassettes—these primers flank the cassette excision junctions in S. epidermidis RP62a and S. aureus ST398, respectively. To determine the number of released circularized cassettes, primers MH112/MH113 were designed to amplify the circle junction of all three cassettes in S. epidermidis RP62a. Each qPCR reaction (25 μL) consisted of 100 ng of genomic DNA as template, 0.4 μM control primers or 1 μM excision/circularization primers, and 1X PerfeCTa SYBR Green SuperMix (Quanta Biosciences). Separate sets of wells were prepared with appropriate standard reactions consisting of 10-fold dilutions of standard plasmids (pSepiStdEx, pSauStdEx, or pSepiStdCirc) containing 109−102 DNA molecules. The copy number of the standard plasmid molecules was calculated from the concentration and size of the standard plasmids using the URI Genomics & Sequencing Center online formula (https://www.technologynetworks.com/tn/tools/copynumbercalculator). Each qPCR plate also included negative control wells containing nuclease-free H2O. The DNA templates were amplified using a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) under the following conditions: one cycle of 95 °C for 3 min; and 40 cycles of 95 °C (10 sec) and 56.4 °C (30 sec). At the end of the run, melt curves were generated to confirm homogenous products by exposing samples to a final temperature gradient of 65 °C to 95 °C. Following the reaction, standard curves were created using the CFX Maestro software version 3.1 (Bio-Rad), and a linear regression model was used to extrapolate product copy numbers. Excision frequencies represent the ratios of qPCR product copy numbers generated from the excision and control primer pairs.

Constructing RP62a Δcassette strains via directed evolution

The RP62a/pSepiCcrAB strain was grown overnight and subcultured each day for seven consecutive nights in BHI (1:100) supplemented with neomycin and chloramphenicol at 37 °C in a shaking incubator. Since the doubling time for S. epidermidis RP62a under these growth conditions is ~40 minutes, and bacteria remain in exponential phase ~300 minutes before growth plateaus, we estimate cells double ~7.5 times per subculture. Thus, after seven consecutive subcultures, cells have doubled 52.5 times (~50 generations). The grown culture on the seventh day was serially diluted (100 − 10−7) and 100 μL of the 10−5 dilution was spread onto BHI agar plates. The plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C, and colonies were selected for further analysis. The colonies were resuspended into fresh BHI medium and then spotted onto both BHI agar and BHI agar supplemented with spermine (Acros Organics) at a final concentration of 0.9 mg/mL. Plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight, and colonies that grew on BHI agar but not on BHI agar with spermine were selected. The selected colonies were confirmed for the loss of cassettes by amplifying the excision junction using PCR primers p1 and p8 and sequencing the products using Sanger sequencing. Colonies were also confirmed by plating with dilutions of phage CNPx according to the protocol described in the section on phage enumeration below.

Mapping and assembling Illumina reads

For confirming cassette circle and excision junction PCR products via Illumina sequencing (Fig. 2), Illumina paired-end reads were mapped onto the expected product sequence with Bowtie 2 v. 2.4.4. For analyzing genomic sequences of S. epidermidis RP62A/pSepiCcrAB ancestral and Δcassette evolved isolates (Fig. 3), paired-end reads for the ancestral genome were first assembled with SPAdes v. 3.15.3 using the reference sequence (NC_002976.3) as an untrusted contig (i.e., with the –untrusted-contigs flag for gap closure and repeat resolution assistance). Then, paired-end reads from three independently generated RP62A Δcassette isolates were mapped back to the ancestral genome assembly using Bowtie 2 v. 2.4.4. Coverage was calculated from the resultant bam file (after sorting and indexing using samtools v. 1.13) using the “igvtools count” command, and normalized plots of coverage data were generated as a function of position, measured in reads per million. For mapping reads to released cassettes (Fig. 4) each target cassette, circle and scar junction sequences were synthesized from the RP62a genome and exported in FASTA format. Paired-end sequencing reads corresponding to each biological replicate were then aligned to these sequences using Bowtie2, and the resultant alignments were processed into sorted BAM files via samtools. Our custom Python script analyzed these BAM files to count uniquely mapped reads that span the junction (e.g., the region where the cassette’s terminal ends meet upon circularization) as evidence of circularization and scarring. To minimize spurious alignments, the script required that reads that are to be counted as evidence must traverse the midpoint with a flanking region exceeding a predefined tolerance of 30 nucleotides on either side. This threshold was empirically chosen to balance false positive and false negative rates. Alignments with bowtie2 mapping quality score of minimum 0 were accepted to allow for single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) arising from recombination processes. Importantly, to set the baseline for the background noise from random chance alignments, five additional control sequences—randomly generated cassettes of analogous length (Supplementary Data 8)—were also analyzed. Inspired by the well-established differential gene expression analysis pipeline as implemented in DESeq2, the counts were first organized into a matrix with circle/scar junction sequences as rows (akin to gene names) and replicates as columns, and subsequently normalized using the ‘median of ratios’ method which corrects for library size and composition variations so that the counts could be compared across replicates.

Phage propagation and enumeration

Phages CNPx19 (NC_031241) and ISP67 (NC_047720) were generous gifts from Luciano Marraffini. Phages Southeast (OQ623150), Andhra68 (NC_047813), and Twillingate69 (MH321491) were discovered in-house. All phages were propagated using S. epidermidis LM168070 as host. To prepare the phage stocks, 1-5 purified phage plaques were combined into 500 μL TSB and vortexed for 30 sec. The suspension was then centrifuged at ~15,000 × g for 2 min to pellet agar and cells, and the resulting phage lysate (i.e., supernatant) was passed through a 0.45 μm syringe filter. Next, the phage lysate was combined with overnight host culture (diluted 1:100) in 7 mL of Heart Infusion Agar (HIA) prepared at 0.3 x concentration and supplemented with 5 mM CaCl2. The phage-host mixture was poured onto a solid layer of Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) supplemented with 5 mM CaCl2 and allowed to solidify for 10 minutes at room temperature. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, the top agar layer was harvested and resuspended in 10 mL of fresh TSB. The suspension was vortexed for 5 min to release phages from the agar, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min to remove agar and cell debris. The resulting concentrated phage lysates were passed through a 0.45 μm bottle filter to obtain a purified phage stock. Phage concentrations were determined by plating 10-fold dilutions of the phage suspension atop a lawn of cells using the double-agar overlay method as described by Cater and co-workers68. Phage stocks were stored at 4 °C, and phages were routinely authenticated through PCR amplification and sequencing of genomic regions unique to each phage.

Determining the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of antimicrobials

MICs for the antibiotics used in this study were determined using the protocol outlined in ref. 71. Briefly, S. epidermidis RP62a was cultured overnight at 37 °C in BHI containing 15 μg/ml neomycin. Overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 in fresh BHI with 15 μg/ml neomycin and grown as above until OD600 reached 0.5. Cells were then diluted 1:100 in fresh BHI containing two-fold dilutions of each antimicrobial covering a range of concentrations (from 0–10,000 μg/ml), and 200 μL aliquots were distributed into a 96-well plate. The plate was incubated overnight (16–18 h) at 37 °C without shaking. The absorbance (OD600) was then recorded using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite® M Plex). The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of the antibiotic at which no bacterial growth was observed.

Measuring cassette excision frequencies in cells treated with antibiotics

S. epidermidis RP62a cells were grown overnight in BHI supplemented with 15 μg/ml neomycin. The cells were allowed to grow until the OD600 reached 1.0, corresponding to 108 CFU/mL. The cell cultures were then distributed into six separate 50-mL conical tubes, each containing 20 mL of cells. The required volumes of each antimicrobial were added to achieve the desired concentrations (0, 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, and 2 times the MIC). The cells were incubated at 37 °C with shaking for 30 minutes and then pelleted at 5000 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was discarded, and pellets were washed twice with an equal volume of PBS. The supernatant was discarded, and cell pellet was resuspended in 200 μl of water and genomic DNA was extracted according to the protocol above titled “Preparing bacterial genomic DNA for Illumina sequencing and qPCR”. The resulting genomic DNA was used for quantifying the excision frequency of cassettes using qPCR as described in the section above titled “Measuring cassette excision frequencies and circle numbers using qPCR.”

Phage challenge in semi-solid agar and extraction of released DNA

The high titer phage lysates (≥ 1010 pfu/mL) propagated in S. epidermidis LM1680 were used to challenge S. epidermidis RP62a at a phage:bacteria ratio of 10:1 within soft agar overlays. Briefly, each phage lysate was combined with 300 μL of overnight host culture in 7 mL of Heart Infusion Agar (HIA) prepared at 0.3 x concentration and supplemented with 5 mM CaCl2. The phage-host mixture was poured onto a solid layer of Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) supplemented with 5 mM CaCl2 and allowed to solidify for 10 min at room temperature. After 16–18 h of incubation at 37 °C, the top agar layer was harvested and resuspended into 10 mL of fresh TSB. The suspension was vortexed for 5 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min to remove agar and cell debris. Finally, the resulting supernatant was passed through a 0.2 μm bottle filter to obtain a purified supernatant with no cellular debris. Lysates were further centrifuged at 22,000 × g and 4 °C for 1 h to pellet phages. The top portion of the supernatant from each tube was carefully collected in a 50 mL conical tube without disturbing the pellet. Next, 5 mL of the supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of Phenol:Chloroform:Isoamyl Alcohol (25:24:1) and vortexed for 1 minute. The mixture was subjected to centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 5 minutes at room temperature (RT), and the upper aqueous layer was transferred into a fresh tube. The aqueous phase was then mixed with sodium acetate (pH 5.2) to a final concentration of 200 mM, and two volumes of 100% ethanol was added. The tube was inverted 3-4 times and kept on ice for 10 minutes before centrifuging at 20,000 × g for 5 minutes at RT. The supernatant was gently decanted, and the pellet was washed with 3–5 mL of 75% ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 5 min at RT. After decanting the supernatant, the remaining liquid was carefully aspirated from the pellet, which was air-dried for 5-10 minutes. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of DNase-free dH2O, and its concentration was measured using NanoDrop™ 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA extracts were subjected to qPCR to quantify numbers of released circles as described in the section above.

Cell lysis by heat and lysostaphin treatment

Overnight S. epidermidis RP62a cultures (300 μL) were added to 7 mL of HIA prepared at a concentration of 0.3X. The mixture was poured onto a solid layer of TSA supplemented with 5 mM CaCl2 and allowed to solidify for 10 minutes at room temperature. Following 16–18 h of incubation at 37 °C, the top agar layer was harvested and resuspended in 10 mL of 1X PBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 1.8 mM KH2PO4). The suspension was vortexed for 5 minutes to facilitate cell release and subsequently heated at 95 °C for 10 minutes in a water bath, followed by placement on ice to cool down. The cell lysate was then incubated with lysostaphin (40 μg/mL) and MgCl2 (5 mM) at 37 °C for 2 h. The lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes and the resulting supernatant was passed through a 0.2 μm bottle filter to obtain a purified supernatant with no cellular debris. DNA was then extracted from 5 mL of the supernatant according to the protocol described in the section above.

Extraction of DNA packaged in phage capsids

DNA was extracted from phages amplified on S. epidermidis RP62a and S. epidermidis RP62a Δcassettes strains following the method outlined in Cater and co-workers68. Briefly, a 20 ml phage lysate (~109 pfu/ml) was subjected to digestion with DNase I and RNase A (10 μg/ml each) for 30 minutes at 37 °C. The digested lysate was then mixed with 10 ml of precipitant solution (30% [wt/vol] polyethylene glycol 8000 and 3 M NaCl) and left to incubate at 4 °C overnight, allowing phages to precipitate. The resulting precipitated phages were pelleted by centrifugation for 10 minutes at 10,000 × g and 4 °C. Subsequently, the phage pellet was resuspended in 250 μl of resuspension buffer (5 mM MgSO4, 10 mM EDTA, pH 8.0), and incubated with proteinase K (100 μg/ml) at 50 °C for 30 minutes. The phage suspension was then combined with 500 μl of resin contained in the Promega Wizard DNA cleanup kit. After inverting the mixture several times, it was applied to the minicolumn within the kit. The resin was washed with 2 ml of 80% isopropanol and dried by centrifugation for 2 minutes at 13,000 × g. Finally, the DNA was eluted from the resin using 100 μl of distilled water preheated to 80 °C.

Evaluating cassette transfer

To demonstrate cassette transfer, a system was created using S. epidermidis RP62a as a donor and S. aureus Newman as a recipient. Importantly, the recipient is devoid of SCCmec and other cassettes and harbors an intact attachment site downstream of rlmH. This system relies upon selective markers naturally found in each of the three cassettes—oxacillin resistance conferred by mecA in SCCmec; spermine resistance afforded by speG in the Nhi-RM cassette, and phage resistance conferred by the CRISPR system in the CRISPR cassette. For the latter selection to work, the recipient strain harbors a plasmid, pcrispr-spcI1, which encodes a CRISPR repeat and a spacer targeting phage ISP72. This mini repeat-spacer array complements the CRISPR system and confers resistance to ISP should the CRISPR cassette be transferred into the recipient. S. aureus Newman/pcrispr-spcI1 (the recipient) is sensitive to oxacillin, spermine, and ISP, but resistant to chloramphenicol owing to the plasmid, thus allowing the use of appropriate combinations of selective markers to separate wild-type donors and recipients from recipients that may have taken up one or more cassettes.

To demonstrate cassette transfer via transduction, overnight cultures of S. epidermidis RP62a were diluted in 2 ml of BHI (1:100 dilution) and 20 µL of phage lysate (Andhra, CNPx, Southeast, and Twillingate, ~109 pfu/mL) was added. A control culture without phage was also included. The cultures were grown at 37 °C in a shaking incubator until phage clearing was observed (~ 4–6 h). The cells were then pelleted, and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.45-micron filter. 100 µL of the supernatant was combined with 1 ml of S. aureus Newman/pcrispr-spcI1 cells grown to mid-log (OD600 = 1.0) and the mixture was incubated for 20 minutes at 37 °C in a shaking incubator. The cells were then pelleted, resuspended in 100 µL of TSB, and 10-fold dilutions were spotted onto pre-warmed TSA plates with appropriate antibiotics or an overlay of phage ISP for selection (described in the paragraph above). The plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight, and representative colonies from selective plates were purified and tested for the presence of each cassette using PCR with primers listed in Supplementary Data 11.

To demonstrate cassette transfer through filter mating, the protocol outlined in ref. 73 was used. Briefly, S. aureus Newman/pcrispr-spcI1 was streaked on a TSA plate containing 10 μg/ml chloramphenicol and incubated overnight at 37 °C to obtain single colonies. The next day, individual colonies (> 1 mm in diameter) were inoculated into 2 ml of TSB with 10 μg/ml chloramphenicol. Concurrently, S. epidermidis RP62a overnight cultures were diluted (1:100) in fresh BHI with 15 μg/ml neomycin. Both donor and recipient cultures were grown at 37 °C with shaking until OD600 reached ~2.0. The cultures were then combined to achieve a 1:1 ratio (recipients:donors) for filter mating. The combined cultures were washed to remove antibiotics by centrifuging at 6200 × g for 2 minutes, discarding the supernatant, and resuspending the pellets in 1 ml of TSB without antibiotics. This centrifugation was repeated, and the pellet was dissolved with 100 μl of TSB. The resulting mating mixture was aseptically transferred atop a 0.45 μm membrane filter atop a TSA plate. In one iteration, the TSA plate had no antibiotics, in a second iteration, the plate contained 5 mM H2O2 according to74, and in a third iteration, the plate had a layer of HIA top agar infused with phage Andhra (~109 pfu/mL). The filter mating experiment was also conducted in L-form inducing media containing the cell wall-targeting antibiotic penicillin, as well as in a control media containing all the ingredients of the L-form inducing media without penicillin75. As controls, donor and recipient strains alone were also placed on separate filters. Plates were left on the benchtop until the suspension dried, then incubated upside-down at 30 °C overnight. The following day, the mating mixtures were resuspended in 3 ml of TSB, serially diluted, and spotted atop pre-warmed TSA plates containing appropriate antibiotics or an overlay of phage ISP for selection. Plates were dried on the benchtop and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Representative colonies from selective plates were purified and tested for the presence of each cassette using PCR with primers listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Cassette transfer via natural transformation under biofilm conditions was also evaluated following the protocol outlined in ref. 43 with slight modifications. Briefly, overnight cultures of donor and recipient strains were diluted 1:100 and combined in a 1:1 ratio, with individual cultures serving as controls. 200 μl of each combined/individual culture were added into a 96-well plate in triplicate with or without phage Andhra (~109 pfu/ml). The cells were incubated statically at 37 °C for 24 h. After the incubation period, the biofilm was harvested by extensive pipetting and scraping with the pipette tip. 100 μl of each biofilm suspension was spread onto pre-warmed TSA plates with appropriate selective antibiotics or an overlay of phage ISP. The plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight, and representative colonies from selective plates were purified and tested for the presence of each cassette using PCR with primers listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Statistical analyses

All statistics associated with the computational analyses in Figs. 1 and 3 (and associated Supplementary Figs.) are detailed in the section above titled “Computational analyses”. Graphed qPCR data represents the mean (± S.D.) of three replicates. Average values were analyzed in pairwise comparisons using two-tailed t-tests, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Sample sizes were empirically determined, and no outliers were observed or omitted. Graphed normalized read counts (in Fig. 4D) show data for six independent trials with box and whisker plots indicating the minimum, maximum, median, first quartile, and third quartile of the range. The whiskers extend to the furthest data points within 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR) from the quartiles, excluding any outliers beyond this range.
