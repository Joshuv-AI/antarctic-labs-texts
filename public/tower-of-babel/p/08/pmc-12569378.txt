Synergistic role of potassium fertilizer and kinetin in mitigating iron toxicity and enhancing yield in lowland rice
Authors: Jevelin Swain, R K Panda, R K Nayak, M R Satapathy, Jyotirmayee Das, Madhusmita Panigrahy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLowland rice in humid Asian regions is prone to iron toxicity. High Fe2⁺ in the soil can reduce yield by 12–100% depending on genotype, stress severity, and soil fertility. Accordingly, two years of field experiment coined with split-plot design was conducted during the wet seasons of 2022 and 2023 at the Central Farm, Odisha University of Agriculture and Technology, Bhubaneswar. Where three replicated plots with five rice genotypes as main, while subplots received five K fertilizer levels with or without foliar kinetin (200 mg/L). Percent yield increased with both sole K application (62.42%–72.85%) and combined K with kinetin (86.39%–113.36(%), indicating that K alone was insufficient to overcome iron toxicity. Kinetin enhanced sink capacity and promoted efficient translocation of photosynthates into filled grains, improving grain weight per panicle. Increasing K doses with kinetin improved morphological traits, photosynthetic pigments, and translocation of proteins and carbohydrates from source to sink. The combined treatment alleviated Fe toxicity by limiting Fe uptake, reducing ROS, MDA, proline, and phenolics and regulating antioxidant enzyme activity. Tejaswini, treated with 100–120 kg K₂O/ha and two kinetin sprays, achieved the highest yield gains (110–113%), demonstrating its potential for sustainable rice production in iron-toxic lowlands.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21573-7.Keywords: Iron toxicity, Kinetin, Potassic fertilizers, Nutrient use efficiency, Sink capacity, Reactive oxygen species (ROS)Subject terms: Plant sciences, Biogeochemistry

Introduction

Approximately half of the world’s population currently consumes rice as a staple food. Rice is cultivated over more than 160 million hectares across a variety of climatic conditions. India is the second-biggest producer of rice in the world and has the largest area. Over an area of over 43 million hectares, the crop is farmed in a variety of ecologies with differing soil and climate conditions. However, poor soil health, inefficient use of nutrients, and climate change provide additional problems for rice producers and researchers. Lowland rice in India is grown across an area of around 14.0 Mha, or 32% of the country’s total area, mostly in the east. The region is characterized by poor soil quality and frequent floods and droughts caused by irregular rainfall.

Flooded soil can lead to iron toxicity, which negatively impacts lowland rice production. Toxic oxygen radicals are produced when rice roots absorb excessive Fe2+ and then transport it into the leaves, damaging cell structure and impairing physiological functions. This problem can be mitigated by developing tolerant cultivars, applying appropriate fertilizers, managing water efficiently, and adopting suitable cropping patterns1. So far, approximately 1200 rice varieties have been released in the country, including about 240 varieties by ICAR and around 100 varieties by OUAT, adapted for different ecologies. These cultivars are also able to withstand various biotic and abiotic stresses. Iron toxicity often leads to elemental imbalances in the soil solution due to the high concentration of Fe2⁺. Studies have shown a strong correlation between the concentration of Fe2⁺ in the soil solution, the cultivar’s tolerance level, and reduced rice yield1. Nevertheless, several challenges related to the widespread prevalence of iron toxicity in lowland rice remain to be addressed urgently.

Applying nutrients such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and zinc (Zn) can help reduce the negative effects of iron toxicity in rice fields2. However, many resource-limited rice farmers apply less fertilizer than recommended. This nutritional problem is often exacerbated by the microbial reduction of insoluble iron-III (Fe3⁺) to soluble iron-II (Fe2⁺) under flooded conditions, which leads to excessive Fe2⁺ uptake by rice plants. In such conditions, some soil bacteria carry out anaerobic respiration, using the reductive process to generate energy (ATP), thereby producing significant amounts of soluble ferrous ions (Fe2⁺) in the soil solution2,3.

It appears that potassium deficiencies in rice fields may exacerbate iron toxicity; as a result, using K fertilizers raises the concentration of potassium ion (K+) in plants, reducing iron toxicity. By boosting photosynthate translocation and improving gas exchange, protein synthesis, enzyme activity, and stomatal conductance, potassium application can help reduce abiotic stress. Potassium also lengthens the root system, lessens water damage, and activates anti-stress enzyme systems. This promotes crop development and lowers lodging by increasing translocation, enhancing water absorption, lowering respiration, and increasing photosynthetic activity. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are produced less frequently in plants when the K status is upregulated4. In the enzymatic processes involved in photosynthesis, stress management, and the production of carbohydrates, potassium is a cofactor. POX (Peroxidase) primarily oxidizes OH-. into H2O, whereas CAT (a tetrameric haem-containing enzyme) detoxifies reactive oxygen species by turning hydrogen peroxide into water. In the chloroplast, where CAT (Catalase) is absent while manufacturing dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR), super oxide dismutase (SOD), mono-dehydroascorbate reductase (MDHAR), and GR(Glutathione reductase) following potassium administration, ascorbic acid-dependent APX (Ascorbate peroxidase) is the sole enzyme capable of scavenging H2O2. Exogenous K+ significantly aids in counteracting the inhibitory effects of Fe2+ during photosynthetic pigment synthesis. Exogenous K+ alleviated Fe2+-induced membrane damage, resulting in lower MDA (Melondialdehyde, a by-product of lipid peroxidation) levels and higher chlorophyll concentrations. The administration of varying concentrations of K+ can partially mitigate this stress5. Potassium (K), however, is insufficient on its own to prevent iron toxicity in low-lying rice.

Other than nutrients, plant growth regulators are organic substances that alter physiological processes in plants and are active in very small amounts. Nine kinds of phytohormones, including cytokinin (CK), auxins, gibberellins (GA), salicylates (SA), ethylene (ET), jasmonates (JA), strigolactones (SL), and brassinosteroids (BR), have been identified too far. Plant growth is influenced by cell division, which is stimulated by cytokinin (CKs). They are produced from the adenine precursor in the roots of plants. Furthermore, they go through the woody tissue, or xylem, in the direction of the leaves and fruits, where they are used for cell differentiation and plant growth. When cytokinin are applied, a number of characteristics related to plant development, physiology, and growth change (i.e., flower and fruit formation, leaf senescence, and apical dominance). They strengthen the sink and promote cell proliferation, which enhances photosynthesis6. Kinetin (Kn), a cytokinin subtype, increases the amount of chlorophyll in plants by inducing the synthesis of photosynthetic proteins, speeds up cell division, and alters the apical dominance of plants. The application of a small amount of plant growth regulators affects physiological activities and helps to absorb applied nutrients leading increased yield and quality7. The goal of the experiment is to determine which rice genotypes are best suited to investigate the potential effects of varying K fertilizer levels and exogenous kinetin (Kn) application on leaves in lowland rice soil that is iron-toxic in order to boost rice grain yield and quality.

Results

Screening of rice genotype suitable to Iron toxicity under K nutrition

Thirty-two rice genotypes were evaluated under different doses of potassium fertilizer in iron-rich, acidic soils with low available NPK and low organic carbon (OC). The results of the scatter plot analysis (Fig. 1) showed that the genotypes were irregularly distributed across the different quadrants. In addition to distributing genotypes in two groups, the cluster analysis (Fig. 2) created accounting "grain yield, straw yield, HI, root length, tiller number, SPAD, Fe uptake and K uptake" all genotypes, however, displayed decreased percent yields (Supplementary Table 2) when compared to their corresponding theoretical yields, suggesting that potassium alone is insufficient to counteract iron toxicity in low-lying soils as no change in post-harvest soil PH was found due to potassium doses but DTPA-Fe was found to decreased over initial Fe concentration with increased potassium doses (Supplementary Table 1). The genotypes Lalat, Manaswini, Hiranmayee, Pratikshya, and Tejaswini that performed well throughout the same time span (133–138 days) were selected for additional research and their reactions to various dosages of potassium fertilizer and kinetin were examined.

Discussion

A significant amount of the global farmland, primarily in Africa and Asia, has already been harmed by iron toxicity, a developing and extremely powerful mineral toxicity stress that occurs in low-lying, moist, and acid soil8. The most financially viable course of action to prevent severe crop loss or total crop failure is to manage excess Fe toxicity. Excess iron damages the photosynthetic system, metabolic processes, membranes, organelles, and other vital biomolecules by producing ROS via the Fenton reaction8,9. While these impacts on later growth stages (vegetative and reproductive) result in poor growth, development, and a large loss in productivity, they also contribute to poor germination, weak seedling establishment, and weak seed vigor10,11. Adequate K reduces Fe2⁺ translocation from roots to shoots12, but K alone cannot fully prevent Fe toxicity13. Differences in cultivar K-use efficiency may explain yield variation. Cytokinins, particularly kinetin (Kn), enhance photosynthesis, morphology, nitrogen metabolism, spikelet number, sink capacity, and photosynthate translocation, improving yield and stress adaptation. However, the combined effect of K and kinetin under elevated Fe remains unclear, and this study is the first to evaluate different K levels with exogenous kinetin foliar application across popular rice cultivars.

Given that soil properties play a critical role in Fe availability and nutrient uptake, understanding the physical and chemical characteristics of the experimental soil is essential to interpret the observed varietal responses to potassium and kinetin. The soil in this study is an acidic inceptisol (pH-4.82) with high levels of Fe toxicity (458.6 mg/kg) of diethylene triamine penta-acetic acid (DTPA-Fe). Low land rice genotypes were grown under the influence of varying K levels, and then K + Kinetin (Kn) was applied to develop strategies to counteract iron toxicity. In the present study, a considerable decrease in DTPA extractable Fe was observed in post-harvest soil compared to the initial soil Fe, which might be due to the overall effect of K application. Apart from this overall view soil is strongly acidic, non-saline with low-moderate organic carbon and the cationic micronutrient (Fe2+, Zn2, Cu2+, Mn) status of soil was mostly high with low K showing negative interaction as K is also a cation.

The 32 rice genotypes that were evaluated showed varying overall trends in mean performance and growth metrics when compared to yield. In order to draw distinct lines between K uptake and Fe uptake in relation to grain yield, the genotypes with vegetative growth in terms of plant height, tiller number, root length, and SPAD value were analysed using scatter and cluster analysis, a useful technique in screening studies for grouping rice genotype against one or more factors (Different levels of K nutrition). The genotypes were chosen based on the bottom limit of scatter plot. According to the silhouette index, the hierarchical cluster analysis divides the data into two categories. Two genotypes (Pratikshya, Manaswini) were selected from one group and three genotypes (Lalat, Tejaswini, Hiranmayee) from another group. The results of the cluster analysis also show good agreement between the genotype under test and its growth and yield when clustered under 40, 80, 100, and 120 mg/kg of K. Five genotypes belonging to medium duration (133–138 days) of crop growth were identified on the basis of grain yield performance, iron and potassium uptake in grain. Finally, analysis of five rice genotypes revealed a noteworthy heterogeneity in genotype responsiveness to various K treatments.

The above five cultivars were studied again in same low-land iron toxic conditions using varying K fertilizer levels along with kinetin (Kn), excluding the control. The results showed a significant increase in the overall growth, yield and its components, physiological and biochemical traits of cultivars. On the other hand, elevated iron concentration was harmful and had a negative effect on yield and growth in the control (K0-Kn), supporting beneficial interaction of potassium (K) with Kn to reduce iron toxicity. K + Kn markedly induced plant height, tiller number, root growth under critical Fe2+ concentration (Table 1).

In the K40 + Kn, K80 + Kn, and control treatments, loose root hairs and bronzing leaves were signs of iron toxicity; however, in the K100 and K120 treatments, there were no signs of toxicity (data not shown). ROS are produced in cells as a normal by-product of plant metabolism, but their concentration suggests a stressful situation. H2O2, O2·-, OH·, proline, phenol and scavenging enzymes considerably rose in the control treatment leaf and then decreased as K dosages with Kn increased. According to our research, a variety of physio-biochemical strategies are employed by stressed plants as tolerance mechanisms. These strategies include the use of non-enzymatic components like carotenoids and phenol, as well as enzymatic components like APX, DHAR, SOD, CAT, POX, GPX, and GR (Table 2 and 3). The antioxidant defence system shields enzymes, unsaturated membrane lipids, nucleic acids, and other cellular components from the damaging effects of ROS14. POX (peroxidase) and CAT activities play a significant role in plant resistance to Fe2+ toxicity15. Increase in protein, carbohydrate content, and chlorophyll indicate that combined application of exogenous K and Kn may be able to lessen the harm that Fe2+ induced oxidative stress on the leaf photosynthetic machinery causes. The fact that Fe2+ toxicity (control) causes enormous ROS formation in plants (K0-Kn), enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity, and increased MDA content all of which contribute to lipid peroxidation corroborates our findings. Reduced chlorophyll content is the result of ROS destroying chl a and directly harming Chlorophyll. The application of various K + concentrations can reduce such stress to a limited extent5,16. However, we discovered that the combined action of K + Kn is more relevant to check Fe toxicity.

The control showed the lowest values for panicle/m2, panicle length, panicle weight, number of filled grains/panicle, and 1000 grain weight. In two years, these findings remained consistent despite large variations in the yield percentage. The reason for variations in yield percentage is the contribution of kinetin (Kn), which enhances yield and yield-attributing components when the weather conditions are similar between two years and yields consistent trends every time. In 2022, K40 was used as the control, and there was a noticeable difference in the application of K with or without Kn in both years.

Application of 200 ppm kinetin to rice during the mid-tillering stage disrupted apical dominance and stimulated lateral growth, leading to the production of effective tillers (panicles) and leaves. This effect was associated with enhanced cell division and elongation, which also influenced chlorophyll content, photosynthesis, and the subsequent distribution of assimilates among plant organs, thereby increasing panicle number. Potassium application further increased the number of tillers by promoting node activation, resulting in a greater number of productive early tillers. However, as observed in these experiments, the increase in tiller number could not be explained by potassium application alone, supporting earlier findings7,17. Significant interactions between K + Kn and genotype were observed for yield and yield-attributing traits, with Tejaswini recording the highest grain yield (58.69 q/ha) and Lalat the lowest (31.58 q/ha). Although grain yield increased significantly with treatments, harvest index (HI) remained non-significant because total biomass increased proportionally. This indicates that the treatments enhanced overall growth and yield, but did not alter the relative partitioning of assimilates between grains and vegetative tissues. Panicle weight also increased markedly when kinetin was applied together with potassium, whereas the lowest values were recorded without kinetin. Since kinetin promotes cell division and elongation, this likely contributed to the observed increase in panicle weight and grain size. As high sink capacity is a key driving force in stabilizing rice seed set and improving yield, the results clearly demonstrate that sink capacity can enhance rice production (Table 4) by promoting nutrient uptake and dry matter accumulation.

At the maturity stage, a greater proportion of dry matter is allocated to the ears in rice genotypes with higher sink capacities, which may be regulated by kinetin18,19. One possible explanation for the increase in grains per panicle following K + Kn application is the role of kinetin in promoting the growth of various plant organs, including the panicle. Kinetin enhances panicle length, number of branches, number of spikelets, and spikelet size. In addition, several enzymes in rice are co-activated by potassium, including transfer enzymes. Application of potassium together with kinetin significantly increased the number of filled grains per panicle compared with the control. The increases in grain carbohydrate and protein content are therefore linked to starch biosynthesis, which is stimulated by both potassium and kinetin20,21. Higher potassium fertilization also reduces the percentage of empty spikelets in rice22. A continuous supply of potassium throughout different growth stages promotes the translocation of carbohydrates from stems, leaves, and other storage organs to grains, thereby enhancing sink capacity. Kinetin further regulates key developmental processes such as cell division, cell elongation, and protein synthesis, leading to higher grain yield. Consequently, grain yield was significantly greater in all K + Kn treatments compared with the control23. Potassium and kinetin, which show a strong positive relationship with grain yield and yield attributes (with the lowest values recorded in the control), also contribute to an increase in total biomass.

Given that percent yield (Table 4) with and without Kn fluctuated markedly between the two years, relying solely on potassium nutrition under iron-toxic lowland conditions could lead to the erroneous conclusion that high potassium alone suppressed iron toxicity. Previous studies have reported that spraying with potassium and kinetin enhanced seed yield in three mung bean genotypes24 and that yield improvement was achieved through the combined application of potassium fertilizer and cytokinin18. In the present study, for example, the genotypes Tejaswini and Lalat exhibited the highest and lowest yield performance, respectively, across K levels with Kn, particularly at K100 and K120. However, the overall trend of mean genotype performance for growth parameters differed from that of yield and its components. Genotypes with better vegetative growth, such as increased plant height, tended to show significantly higher yield performance. Moreover, different K levels with Kn were strongly correlated with plant height, tiller number, root length, and root density (Table 5). These results are consistent with earlier findings25,26.

The fact that higher doses of K cause rice to have a higher root oxidizing capacity, which oxidizes Fe2+ to Fe3+ and excludes this ion from absorption, indicates that higher doses of K inhibit the accumulation of Fe. ROS build-up and root hair growth are impacted by the decreasing level of Kn brought on by K insufficiency (control). Thus, exogenous Kn reacts to low K levels by promoting K absorption.

The application of the recommended fertilizer doses aimed to eliminate any nutritional stress, supporting the idea that the effects of kinetin were more physiological than nutritional. However, kinetin influences plant functions in conjunction with, or in contrast to, other growth regulators, and does not act independently on physiological processes of plants27. As a result, the observed effects in this study may be due to both kinetin and its interactions with potassium.

The field experiment was carried out during wet seasons of 2022 and 2023 at Central experimental farm, Odisha University of Agriculture and technology, Bhubaneswar, Odisha, (200 15’ N latitude, 850 52’E longitude, elevation 25.9 m above mean sea level) which belongs to east and south eastern coastal plain agroclimatic zone of Odisha and falls under the east coastal plain and hills zone of humid tropics of India. Experimental design and field management

Screening test of ideal genotype (Field Trial-1st year)

The initial screening test of genotypes was done on the basis of gain yield performance, iron and potassium uptake in grain during Kharif-2022 where the field experiment was carried out in a split plot design having 32 genotypes in main plot and 4 doses of potassic fertilizer (40, 80, 100 and 120 kg K2O/ha) in sub plot along with recommended doses of N (80 kg/ha) applied in 2 splits and P (40 kg P2O5/ha) and was replicated twice. The potassium was applied in 3 splited doses of 25%, 50% and 25% of K requirement.

Field trial (2nd year)

Five genotypes viz; Lalat (G1), Manaswini (G2), Hiranmayee (G3), Pratikshya (G4), Tejaswini (G5) were selected on the basis of grain yield performance, iron and potassium uptake in grain as per screening performance from scatter and cluster analysis (Fig. 1 and 2) for further study in the same iron rich soil during Kharif-2023. The experiment was trialled in a split plot design having 5 levels of potassic fertilizer (0, 40, 80, 100, 120 kgK2O/ha) along with N and P as per previous practice and was replicated thrice. Further at mid-tillering stage, the crop was foliar sprayed twice with kinetin (Kn) (dissolved in 1 M KOH) with a concentration of 200 mg/L in a gap of 10 days to the first spray except control (K0-Kn).

Conclusion

The integration of iron-tolerant rice cultivars with appropriate nutrient and hormonal management provides an effective strategy to alleviate iron toxicity in lowland soils. Potassium fertilization at rates of 100–120 kg K₂O/ha helps maintain ionic balance, enhances root activity, and improves nutrient uptake under stress. Simultaneously, foliar application of kinetin at the mid-tillering stage improves sink capacity and promotes efficient translocation of assimilates from source to sink, resulting in higher grain filling, panicle weight, and overall yield. Together, these practices significantly enhance root growth and development, increase photosynthetic pigment content, and strengthen antioxidant defense systems, thereby reducing oxidative stress under high Fe conditions. Iron-tolerant cultivars such as Tejaswini demonstrate the greatest response, highlighting their potential in iron-toxic soils. This combined approach offers a practical and sustainable strategy for low-input farmers and provides a basis for precision nutrient management, integrating physiological resilience with agronomic practices to improve rice productivity under challenging soil conditions.
