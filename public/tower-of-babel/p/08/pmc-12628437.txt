Increased all-cause mortality following proximal humerus fractures in Danish adults: a register-based matched cohort study of 93,952 fracture cases and 469,760 controls
Authors: Helle Kvistgaard Østergaard, Cecilie Rud Budtz, Bakir O Sumrein, Merete Nørgaard Madsen, Stig Brorson, Antti P Launonen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSummaryMortality after proximal humerus fractures (PHF) has received less attention compared to hip fractures. We found higher all-cause mortality among PHF patients in Denmark compared to matched controls, with the highest rates within 30 days post-fracture, especially in men. Further research is needed to identify factors associated with this increased mortality.PurposeTo assess all-cause mortality among adults with PHFs in Denmark, compared to age- and sex-matched controls from the general population.MethodsThis population-based matched cohort study retrieved data from the Danish National Patient Register and the Danish Civil Registration System from 1998–2018. Adults with a PHF were included and matched 1:5 on age and sex. Outcomes were death at 30 and 90 days, one and two years. Poisson regression analysis accounting for interactions was performed, and age- and sex-specific mortality rates (MRs) per 1,000 persons were calculated for PHFs and controls. Mortality rate-ratios (MRRs) were calculated for all age groups.ResultsWe included 93,952 PHF patients and 469,760 age- and sex-matched controls. Across all time periods, MRs were higher among PHF patients, with MRs consistently higher for men than women. MRs increased with age in both PHF patients and controls. For individuals aged ≥ 60 years, the MRRs among PHF patients and controls were highest within the first 30 days post-fracture, particularly for the age group 60–69 years, where the MRR was 16.32 (95% CI: 14.47–18.40) for males and 7.82 (95% CI: 7.15–8.54) for women. Between 31 and 90 days, the MRR decreased to 6.75 (95% CI: 6.07–7.52) for men and 3.87 (95% CI: 3.58–4.19) for women. After one year, the MRR stabilized and remained relatively unchanged throughout the two-year follow-up.ConclusionDanish patients with PHF demonstrated substantially higher all-cause MRs than their controls, with the highest rates within the first 30 days after the fracture, especially among men.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00198-025-07629-4.Keywords: Cohort study, Denmark, Mortality, Population-based, Proximal humerus fractures

Introduction

Proximal humerus fractures (PHFs) are among the three most common non-vertebral fractures in older adults, with women accounting for over 70% of all fractures[1–3]. The lifetime risk of sustaining a PHF for women aged 50 years or older is estimated to be 13%[2]. A recent population-based study from Denmark, which utilized registry data from 1996–2018, reported an overall incidence rate of 138 per 100,000 person-years, with the highest rates observed in women aged 60 and older at a rate of 500 per 100,000 person-years[1]. PHFs are closely associated with osteoporosis[4], and some patients with PHF also exhibit underlying frailty.

While increased mortality after hip fractures in older adults is well-documented and a recognized concern in the aftercare of this patient group, mortality following PHF has gained comparatively less attention[5–8]. Recent population-based studies from Nordic countries, however, have reported increased mortality among patients with PHF relative to the general population[9–14]. As an example, Swedish registry studies have reported a five-fold increase in 30-day mortality and a two-fold increase in one-year mortality following PHF, compared to the expected mortality in the general population[9, 11]. Similar trends have been observed in Finland and Denmark[8, 10, 12, 14]. However, the Danish studies either focused specifically on PHF patients who received primary shoulder arthroplasty[12], a procedure accounting for only a small proportion of PHF cases, or the studies did not distinguish between PHFs and other humeral fractures, such as humeral shaft fractures [8, 14]. Since these are distinct patient groups, where epidemiology, biomechanics, management, prognosis, and complications differ, their results cannot be generalized to the PHF population[1, 15]. Thus, large-scale nationwide investigations of mortality following PHF remain relatively limited, particularly in the Danish population, to adequately inform clinicians managing this patient group.

Using longitudinal nationwide registry data spanning 21 years, this study aimed to assess all-cause mortality among adults with a PHF in Denmark, compared to age- and sex-matched controls from the general population. Additionally, we aimed to stratify mortality rates (MRs) by sex and age groups. We hypothesize that adults with a PHF in Denmark have higher all-cause MRs than the general population of the same age and sex.

Methods

Study design

This population-based matched cohort study retrieved data from the Danish National Patient Register (DNPR) and the Danish Civil Registration System (CRS) from 1998 to 2018 [16, 17]. Data are presented according to the RECORD statement[18].

Setting

Denmark has a population of approximately 6 million people, all of whom have access to healthcare, including free emergency services, inpatient care, and outpatient visits. Nearly all patients with a PHF are diagnosed and treated at public hospitals. Although a minority of patients may receive treatment in private hospitals, all fractures confirmed through radiographic evaluation, regardless of the setting, must be registered in the DNPR[16].

Data source

The DNPR was used to identify the PHF population. The registry contains data from all Danish hospitals, both public and private, covering inpatient care and, since 1995, outpatient care. It provides information such as diagnoses, surgical procedures, treatments, complications, and hospital admissions[16]. The validity of PHF diagnosis in the DNPR is high[19]. After identifying the PHF population in the DNPR, it was linked to the CRS by a unique 10-digit personal identification number (CPR) assigned to all Danes at birth or immigration since 1968[17]. The CPR makes it possible to link each individual across all Danish medical databases until death or emigration. The CRS provides information on date of birth, age, sex, and vital status, and was used to identify age- and sex-matched controls from the general population[17]. Data access was gained through the Danish Health Data Authority where Danish health data is collected, stored, and managed. Approval was achieved by the Danish Data Protection Agency (reference no. 680317, case no. 1–16-02–100-20).

Participants

Based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) code S42.2*, all adults (≥ 18 years) with a PHF were identified in the DNPR from 1998 to 2018. Only the patients´ first fracture was included. The CRS was used to identify a general population cohort comprising all adults (≥ 18 years) during the same year span. The two registries were linked, and a comparison cohort of age- (on exact birth date ± 30 days) and sex-matched controls without PHF was randomly selected from the CRS at a 1:5 ratio. If five matches were not eligible, the cases were excluded. To ensure independence of data, patients and controls could only appear once in the dataset. To avoid survival bias, we aligned the data prospectively before matching, thereby ensuring that controls who later had a fracture would remain control patients. Both PHF cases and controls were assigned a specific inclusion date (index date) in the study. For the PHF cases, the index date was the first date the S42.2* code was registered, and for the controls, the index date was the date equal to the index date for the case, to which each control was matched.

Variables

The outcomes included all-cause death episodes registered at 30 and 90 days and one and two years after the index date of fracture. Covariates extracted for the analysis included age (calculated on the index date and categorized into 10-year age bands), sex, and, for PHF patients, whether surgery was performed within 30 days after the index date.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) or as proportions (%). Patients and their matched controls were categorized into the following age groups: 18–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, 80–89, and ≥ 90 years. Poisson regression analysis was performed to account for interactions between groups (fracture cases and controls) and time periods (30-, 90-, 365-, and 730-day mortality), assuming that MRs differ between groups and vary over time. Age- and sex-specific MRs per 1,000 persons, with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), were calculated for the restricted time periods of 0–30, 31–90, 91–365, and 366–730 days for both PHF cases and controls. The restricted time periods were chosen to identify when MRs and mortality rate-ratios (MRRs) were most pronounced during the two-year follow-up. In addition, dividing the follow-up into shorter time periods ensured that the assumption of stable mortality within each specified period was met, increasing the validity of the statistical analysis and results. MRRs were calculated for all age groups, however, we primarily focused on the 60–69, 70–79, 80–89, and ≥ 90-year categories in our presentation of results, as the majority of patients with fragility fractures fall within these age groups. Additionally, age-specific MRs per 1,000 persons and MRRs were calculated for cumulative endpoints at 30 days, 90 days, one year, and two years for the PHF cases and controls. To explore potential time trends in mortality over the 21 years, stratified analyses were conducted, presenting MRRs with 95% CIs by fracture year: 1998–2002, 2003–2008, 2009–2013, and 2014–2018 for the age groups 60–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years. Data cleaning, preprocessing, and all statistical analyses were performed using the Stata software package (version 17, StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

We included 93,952 patients with PHF and 469,760 age- and sex-matched controls from the general Danish population in this analysis. A total of 12,630 PHF cases were excluded: 12,610 cases had previously been selected as controls before their fracture date (of whom 2,851 sustained a fracture within 730 days and 393 died within two years of the index date), and an additional 20 cases were excluded due to the lack of five eligible age- and sex-matched controls.

The mean age among PHF patients was 67 years (SD 15.9), with women accounting for 69% of all fracture cases. The majority of the PHF patients were aged 60 years or older (69%). The mean age of the group of excluded patients was 73.0 years (SD 11.8). During the study period, 87% of the included PHF patients received non-operative treatment (Table 1).

With a primary focus on age categories from 60 years and above, we found that within the first 30 days after the fracture, the MRRs were particularly high in the 60–69 years age group with a MRR of 16.32 (95% CI: 14.47;18.40) for men and 7.82 (95% CI: 7.15;8.54) for women. Between 31 and 90 days, the MRR decreased to 6.75 (95% CI: 6.07;7.52) for men and 3.87 (95% CI: 3.58;4.19) for women, with further decreases between 90 and 365 days, after which it stabilized. In the ≥ 90 years age group, the MRR within the first 30 days was 7.29 (95% CI: 6.37;8.33) for men and 5.07 (95% CI: 4.64;5.54) for women. After the 31–90-day time period, the MRRs stabilized towards the 366–730-day time period, with MRRs of 1.43 (95% CI: 1.30;1.57) for men and 1.27 (95% CI: 1.21;1.34) for women (Table 4 and Fig. 2).

Over the study period from 1998 to 2018, there was no consistent decline in MRRs for any sex or age group. MRRs in 2013–2018 remained relatively similar to, or slightly higher than those in 1998–2002.

Discussion

We assessed all-cause mortality among adults with a PHF in Denmark, compared to age- and sex-matched controls from the general population between 1998 and 2018. Furthermore, we quantified differences for both men and women and across different age groups. Our findings showed substantially higher MRs in patients who sustained a PHF compared to their matched controls over the two-year follow-up period. The MRR was highest within the first 30 days after the fracture. Focusing on the patients aged 60 or more, the MRR was particularly high for the 60–69 year age group, with a 16.32-fold higher MR among men and a 7.82-fold higher MR among women. The MRR decreased with each subsequent time period and stabilized after one year, remaining relatively unchanged throughout the two-year follow-up period. MRs and MRRs were consistently higher among men than women, across all time periods.

Due to differences in methodologies, our estimates cannot be directly compared to those reported in other Nordic studies, with study populations comparable to the Danish. For instance, Bergdahl et al., Sumrein et al., and Somersalo et al. reported mortality results as standardized mortality ratios (SMRs), which compare the observed number of deaths in a patient cohort to the expected number of deaths in the general population, adjusted for age and sex[9–11]. This represents a different statistical approach from the use of mortality rate ratios (MRRs) in the present study, which directly compares mortality rates between PHF patients and the general population. Amundsen et al. reported a sixfold increase in 30-day mortality among patients who underwent shoulder arthroplasty after PHF compared to the general Danish population[12]. However, this subgroup represents only a minority of PHF cases and cannot be generalized to the entire PHF population. Neither can results from Tran et al. and Christensen et al., who showed similar results, as their fracture cohorts included both PHFs and humerus shaft fractures[8, 14]. Although these methodological differences limit direct comparisons, our findings align with the overall trend observed across Nordic studies, demonstrating a notably higher mortality among PHF patients compared to the general population[9–12], particularly within the first 30 and 90 days after the fracture, with a decreasing trend onwards[8, 10, 11]. In addition, previous studies also reported consistently higher MRs among men than women[9–11]. Our age-stratified analysis, using 10-year age bands, showed that the MRR was highest in the younger age groups, gradually decreasing with advancing age. Similar trends were also found in previous studies[9, 10].

A novel feature of our study is stratification into restricted time periods, allowing precise identification of the most critical period after the fracture, during which patients with PHF faced the highest risk of mortality, as well as the period in which this risk declined the most. Similar time-specific analyses were not reported in any of the aforementioned studies, where mortality estimates were most commonly reported as accumulated endpoints[9–12].

Our findings demonstrate a temporal trend in mortality following PHF, with the highest risk occurring within the first 30 days post-fracture, followed by a gradual decline, as also seen in other fragility fractures such as hip fractures[13]. PHF patients are most often managed non-operatively [1, 20, 21], typically discharged shortly after emergency department evaluation, with follow-up in the outpatient clinic. As treatment strategies and care trajectories differ across fracture types, a better understanding of the timing and magnitude of increased mortality following PHF is important in its own right. These insights may help inform targeted follow-up, risk stratification, and clinical decision-making in this patient group.

Stratification by sex and age groups enabled the identification of relatively younger age groups sustaining a PHF, with men accounting for the highest MRRs. While fractures in older adults are often attributed to osteoporosis and frailty, younger patients may experience PHF as a result of high-energy traumas. The relatively high MRRs observed across all age groups are likely attributable to person-related factors, such as comorbidities, underlying frailty, or, in rare cases, the trauma mechanism rather than the type of treatment[22]. In addition, socio-economic factors, including addictive behavior, may also be a potential contributing factor to the risk of dying[23]. Results from a recent study by Brorson et al. demonstrated that, in a cohort of PHF patients aged 60 years or older with Neer 2-part surgical neck fractures, health-related quality of life at six months was comparable to the Danish background population[24]. Although these findings could be interpreted as contradictory to our observation of increased all-cause mortality following PHF, several important distinctions must be considered. Brorson et al. excluded patients with concomitant fractures, dementia, and early mortality, resulting in a healthier and less comorbid cohort than the unselected, nationwide population included in our study. Furthermore, their study focused exclusively on Neer 2-part fractures, whereas our cohort encompasses the full spectrum of PHFs and comorbidity burden. Taken together, these distinctions suggest that the increased mortality risk we observed reflects underlying patient vulnerability and overall health status, rather than a direct consequence of the fracture itself.

Strengths and limitations

A methodological strength of this study is that our findings are derived from a large, longitudinal dataset obtained from the DNPR, covering the entire population living in Denmark. The validation of orthopaedic diagnoses and procedures in this register is generally high, with PHF diagnoses demonstrating strong accuracy[16, 19]. Thus, the DNPR is considered suitable for register-based research. An additional strength is the matching of PHF patients and controls by birthdate (± 30 days) rather than by birth year. While biological age is increasingly recognized as a meaningful indicator of individual health status, chronological age remains the most widely used and measurable proxy for mortality risk in epidemiological studies. Although the reliance on chronological age does not confirm its validity as the optimal predictor, striving for the most precise matching, such as minimizing even small differences in chronological age, may help reduce potential outcome differences in older populations, where the progressive accumulation of health risks with advancing age can influence mortality risk.

To ensure methodological consistency, we excluded 20 PHF cases where five age- and sex-matched controls could not be identified. To avoid immortal time bias, we excluded 12,610 cases that had previously been selected as controls prior to their fracture index date. We acknowledge that this reduced our sample size, however, with a final sample size of 93,952 PHF cases and 469,760 matched controls, we are still able to present robust estimates and narrow 95% CIs. The higher mean age in the group of excluded PHF patients explains why the PHF population included in this study has a lower mean age (67 years) than the mean ages of the PHF populations reported in other Nordic studies[9, 11].

A limitation of this study is the lack of data on the cause of death. Additionally, the dataset lacked information on comorbidities, preventing us from drawing conclusions about causality. To avoid confounding by indication, we refrained from stratifying by treatment subtypes, as potential differences in mortality among treatment subtypes more likely reflect trends in patient selection, preferences, and clinical practices rather than the actual mortality risk associated with specific treatment types.

Our study addresses a knowledge gap concerning mortality in the Danish PHF population. In this study, we investigated mortality exclusively following PHFs, including both operatively and non-operatively treated patients. This approach ensured that our findings are representative of the entire PHF population in Denmark, the majority of whom are treated non-operatively. The high MRRs observed during the early post-fracture period raise the question of whether certain subgroups, particularly male PHF patients, may benefit from more structured follow-up or early geriatric assessment, which could potentially contribute to reducing excess mortality.

Conclusion

In conclusion, patients with PHF in Denmark exhibited notably higher all-cause MRs compared to age- and sex-matched controls, with the highest MRRs observed within the first 30 days after the fracture. Among individuals aged 60 years or older, mortality was particularly high in the 60–69-year age group. Both MRs and MRRs were consistently higher in men than in women, suggesting that men sustaining a PHF represent a particularly frail subgroup, especially within the first 30 days after the fracture. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the relationship between PHFs and associated mortality risk factors, emphasizing the need for further research in this area.
