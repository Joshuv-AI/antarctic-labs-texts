Guineenoside B a triterpenoid saponin from Piper guineense (Piperaceae) triggers apoptosis and PI3K/Akt/mTOR inactivation and inhibits metastasis
Authors: Eutrophe Le Doux Kamto, Anne Offermann, Olivier Placide Noté, Joséphine Ngo Mbing, Mohamed Haddad, Dieudonné Emmanuel Pegnyemb, Dieudonné Njamen, Susan L Bonnet, Sven Perner, Stephane Zingue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOver 1.4 million new cases of prostate cancer (PCa) and 375,304 deaths were recorded in 2020. Piper guineense Schum and Thonn is used to treat various diseases including cancer. Three triterpenoid saponins namely guineenosides (Guin) A, B & C were recently isolated from this plant. This study marks the initial documentation of the anticancer potential of these compounds.MethodsAnti-cell growth (MTT), proliferation (CCK-8), clonogenic assays were evaluated. Furthermore, the most potent compound (Guin-B) was investigated for its potential to induce apoptosis, inhibit cell migration/invasion as well as cell adhesion. Some key proteins in cell proliferation and migration were evaluated.ResultsOut of the 3 saponins isolated from P. guineense, Guin-B exhibited significant and concentration-dependent inhibition of PC3 (IC50 =10 µg/mL or ~ 4.82 × 10− 6 µM) and LNCaP (IC50 = 8 µg/mL or 3.86 × 10− 6 µM) cell growth and proliferation as well as a potent inhibition (p < 0.001) of PC3 cell clone formation. Guin-B induced a potent (near 50%) increment of apoptotic cell population in PC3 and LNCaP cells at both 5 and 10 µg/mL; which was accompanied by a significant increase in caspase-3 activity. Guin-B induced a potent inhibition of PC3 cell migration and invasion and an increase in adherence of PC3 cells to the extracellular collagen and fibronectin matrix. A decrease in the expression of pAKT, AKT, mTOR and VEGF proteins was noticed following the treatment with Guin-B.ConclusionGuineenosides B triggers apoptosis via mitochondrial pathway and PI3K/Akt/mTOR inactivation and inhibits metastasis at a low concentration, thus encouraging future investigation of this saponin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05329-y.Keywords: Piper guineense, Triterpenoid saponin, Prostate cancer, Apoptosis, Metastasis

Introduction

Cancer ranks as the second leading cause of mortality worldwide, manifesting in diverse forms and impacting various tissues, with the most common types being prostate cancer (PCa) in men and breast cancer in women [1, 2]. The global prevalence of cancer is projected at 28.4 million by 2040, with approximately 20 million new cases and over 9.7 million cancer-related deaths. Prostate cancer will contribute significantly to these projected values given that 1.5 million new cases were recorded in 2022 [3]. Factors such as population growth, aging, socioeconomic conditions, and heightened cancer risk factors collectively contribute to the rapid rise in cancer incidence worldwide. In sub-Saharan Africa alone, there were 26,392 new PCa cases, resulting in 7709 fatalities [4]. There were over 1.5 million new cases of PCa and 396,792 (4.1%) deaths in 2020, and it is predicted that by 2040, there 523,233 will be 2,293,818 new cases and 500,000 deaths, primarily driven by population growth and aging [5]. Comprehensive research on PCa and its associated mortalities, aimed at establishing guidelines and treatment/control strategies, especially in low and middle-income countries, represents a critical global health priority [6, 7].

Changes in cellular functions and genetic mutations predominantly underlie the initiation of cancers, influenced by environmental stressors, smoking, and various carcinogenic chemicals [8]. These chemicals can directly or indirectly impact cellular structures, leading to gene mutations and genetic disorders predisposing individuals to cancer [9–11]. Additionally, factors such as radiation exposure, heavy metals, and certain viruses contribute to carcinogenesis, collectively accounting for approximately 7% of cancer cases [12]. Cancer disrupts normal cellular activities and gene functions involved in the cell cycle and division, resulting in abnormal cell proliferation [13]. In summary, sedentary lifestyles, alcohol consumption, smoking, sunlight exposure, chemical exposure, hormonal factors, dietary habits, obesity, and consumption of certain foods are among the risk factors associated with cancer [14, 15].

Despite significant progress in medical care, fatalities due to PCa continue to rise. This is especially evident in developing nations, where governments are less prepared to tackle this challenge due to limited access to diagnostic tools and the high expenses associated with treatments [16]. It is then urgent to find alternative solutions (preventive and curative) such as bioactive secondary metabolites from medicinal plants, which could be used in chemotherapy and hormone therapy and therapies focused on antioxidants present a compelling rationale for cancer treatment [17, 18]. Natural products exhibit significant tumor-inhibiting effects, leading to interest in developing novel, cost-effective, and highly efficient anticancer medications from natural sources [19–21].

In continuation of the search for new natural anticancer drugs, we focused on Piper guineense Schum and Thonn known as the “Ashanti” or “Guinea pepper”, used for the treatment of various ailments including cancer [22]. In previous works, cyclitol and quinic acid derivatives, terpenes, lignans, amide alkaloids, and flavanoids were purified from Piper species [23–26]. Kamto et al. [26] reported for the first time the isolation and structural characterization of three (3) triterpenoid saponins namely guineenosides A–C (Fig. 1), which are highly polar compounds formed from glycosylated terpenoids purified from the n-butanol fraction of P. guineense stem bark. These compounds belong to those recognized to have anticancer potential [27], so there are increasingly researched for their health benefits [28]. In this study, we report for the first time the anticancer potential of the three triterpenoid saponins previously isolated from P. guineense stem bark, focusing on their molecular mechanism of action on cell proliferation and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT).

Methods

Reagent and compounds

The triterpenoids saponins namely Guineenoside A (Guin-A, C86H140O46, MW = 1909.8470), Guineenoside B (Guin-B, C92H150O51, MW = 2071.9383) and Guineenoside C (Guin-C, C64H104O30Na, MW = 1375.6485) were all white amorphous powders isolated from the n-butanol fraction of P. guineense stem bark methanolic extract as described by Kamto et al. [26].

Cell lines and cell culture

Cell lines

Human androgen-insensitive (PC3) and -sensitive (LNCaP) cell lines were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) Promochem located in Wesel, Germany.

Cell culture

LNCaP and PC3 cells were grown and propagated in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), along with 1% penicillin (100 U/mL)/streptomycin (100 µg/mL). They were cultured under controlled conditions of 37 °C, 5% CO2, and a pH of 7.4 in a humidified environment. Cell passages were conducted every two days by refreshing 90% of the culture medium. Prior to each experimental procedure, the number of viable cells was assessed using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) Multiskan TECAN reader counter system.

Assessment of cell growth and proliferation

MTT to assess cell growth

Cell growth was evaluated using the mitochondrial tetrazolium test (MTT) purchased from Roche Diagnostics (Penzberg, Germany). LNCaP and PC3 cells, both treated and untreated, were plated onto 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL in 100 µL. Saponins (Guin-A, Guin-B & Guin-C) extracted from P. guineense were dissolved individually in DMSO and evaluated at final concentrations ranging from 0.5 µg/mL (equivalent of 2.61 × 10− 7 µM, 2.41 × 10− 7 µM and 3.69 × 10− 7 µM for Guin-A, Guin-B and Guin-C, respectively) to 60 µg/mL (equivalent of 3.14 × 10− 5 µM, 2.89 × 10− 5 µM and 4.43 × 10− 5 µM for Guin-A, Guin-B and Guin-C, respectively). Control groups were exposed solely to the vehicle (0.01% DMSO). At 0, 24, 48, and 72-hour intervals, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 2 h. Following this, cell lysis was performed using a buffer containing 10% SDS in 0.01 M HCl for an additional 2 h. Absorbance for each well was measured at 550 nm using a microplate ELISA TECAN© SPARK reader (Crailsheim, Germany).

CCK-8 to assess cell proliferation

The saponins’ ability to inhibit cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) from GLPBio (Hamburg, Germany). Treated and untreated LNCaP and PC3 cells (1 × 104 cells/mL in 100 µL) were seeded onto 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Subsequently, 10 µL of the tested compound (Guin-B) was added at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 60 µg/mL, while the control group was exposed to the vehicle (0.01% DMSO). Following a 48-hour incubation period, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 hours under the same conditions. The plates were gently shaken on a shaker, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate TECAN reader.

Assessment of the inhibition of clone formation

PC3 cells, both treated and untreated, were seeded onto 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well. Guin-B was subsequently introduced at the recommended concentrations [2.5 (1.20 × 10− 6 µM)-10 µg/mL (4.82 × 10− 6 µM). After incubation for 7 days, colonies containing ≥ 50 cells were enumerated. The count of clones in the control group was designated as 100% and compared with the count of clones in the treated PC3 cells.

Assessment of apoptosis

Flow cytometry for detection of apoptotic cells

Apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) staining obtained from BD Pharmingen (Heidelberg, Germany). PC3 and LNCaP cells were exposed to Guin-B at their respective effective concentrations [5 µg/mL (~ 2.41 × 10− 6 µM) and 10 µg/mL (~ 4.82 × 10− 6 µM)] for 48 h. Subsequently, they were washed twice with PBS and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min with 5 µL each of Annexin V-FITC and PI. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted on the cells using FACS caliber from BD Biosciences (Heidelberg, Germany). The percentages of cells undergoing early and late apoptosis, as well as those necrotic or viable, were calculated.

Caspase-3/CPP32 colorimetric assay

The ability of Guin-B to induce caspase-3 activation, a characteristic feature of intrinsic apoptosis in mammalian cells, was evaluated utilizing the Caspase-3/CPP32 colorimetric assay kit (Ilmenau, Germany). This assay detects the chromophore p-nitroaniline (pNA) upon cleavage from the labeled substrate DEVD-pNA. Initially, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in a T75 cm3 culture flask and treated with Guin-B at their respective effective concentrations [5 µg/mL (~ 2.41 × 10− 6 µM) and 10 µg/mL (~ 4.82 × 10− 6 µM)] 24 h later for a duration of 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were lysed on ice and centrifuged (12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min) to isolate proteins (supernatant). Following protein concentration measurement, 100 µL of proteins in 50 µL were transferred to a 96-well plate and diluted with 50 µL of 2× Reaction buffer containing 10 mM of DTT. Next, 5 µL of the 4 mM DEVD-pNA substrate was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 2 h. The emission of pNA light was quantified at 405 nm using a microplate TECAN reader.

Assessment of anti-metastatic potential

Wound-healing assay

This assay evaluates the potential of Guin-B to impede PC3 cell migration. PC3 cells were seeded onto 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well and allowed to grow until reaching confluence. Four hours prior to inducing the wound, the medium was substituted with serum-free RPMI-1640 medium. Subsequently, a scratch wound was generated using a 100 µL pipette tip, followed by two washes with PBS to mechanically remove detached cells. Guin-B or the control solvent (DMSO) was then added in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium, and cells were permitted to incubate for 72 h. The alteration in the healing of the wounded area due to cell migration was observed under a fluorescent microscope (10×) Olympus (Olympus, Japan). Microphotographs were captured every 24 h, and the extent of wound closure was assessed using ImageJ® software.

Chemotaxis assay

The migration of cells induced by serum was evaluated using 6-well transwell chambers from Greiner (Frickenhausen, Germany) with 8 μm pores. PC3 cells were seeded into the upper chamber and allowed to incubate for 24 h in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium, while the lower chamber contained RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS. After the 24-hour incubation period, non-migrating cells on the upper surface of the transwell membranes were gently eliminated using a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were then stained with hematoxylin and counted separately under an Olympus microscope.

Cell adhesion to extracellular matrix components assay

We used pre-coated 6-well plates containing extracellular matrix components like collagen or fibronectin. PC3 cells were then exposed to the respective effective concentrations of Guin-B for 48 h in T25 cm3 culture flasks. After that, the plates, regardless of whether they were made of plastic or coated with collagen or fibronectin, were subjected to incubation with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1 h at room temperature to prevent nonspecific cell adhesion. Following this incubation, 1 × 105 treated or untreated (control) cells were added to each well and allowed to adhere for 60 min. Once the adherence period was over, non-adherent cells were carefully washed off with PBS, and the adherent cells were fixed with 1% glutaraldehyde. Subsequently, cell counts were carried out in five different fields using an Olympus microscope (20× objective) to determine the average rate of cellular adhesion.

Expression of cell signaling proteins

Sufficient number of cells were grown in culture flasks filled with RPMI-1640 and treated with Guin-B at their respective efficacy concentrations for 48 h. Lysing and centrifugation of the cells were done at 14,000 rpm/10 min at 4 °C to extract proteins. After measuring the protein concentration, they were separated by electrophoresis at 120 V (400 mA, 150 watts) for 90 min using a 10% NuPAGE Bis-Tris Plus Gel of 10 wells. Thereafter, the proteins were blotted onto nitrocellulose membranes (115 V, 115 mA, 150 watts, 75 min), which were blocked for nonspecific proteins with Tris buffer containing tween and 10% skimmed milk for 1 h. The membranes were then incubated with pAKT (IgG1, monoclonal, dilution 1:2000), AKT (IgG1, monoclonal, dilution 1:1000), pmTOR (IgG1, monoclonal, dilution 1:1000), mTOR (IgG1, monoclonal, dilution 1:1000), VEGF (IgG1, monoclonal, dilution 1:1000), ZO-1 (IgG1, monoclonal, dilution 1:1000), β-Catenin (IgG1, dilution 1:1,000), N-Cad (IgG1, dilution 1:1,000), vimentin (clone D21H3, dilution 1:1,000) and β-actin (1:20,000) at 4 °C for 24 h; all from Invitrogen or BD Biosciences (Heidelberg, Germany). The secondary antibodies used in this study were HRP (Horseradish peroxidase)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Upstate Biotechnology, Lake Placid, NY, USA; dilution 1:5,000) or anti rabbit IgG, and incubation was done for 1 h. The membranes were briefly incubated with ECL (Enhanced chemiluminescence) detection reagent (ECLTM, Amersham/GE Healthcare, Munich, Germany) to visualize the proteins and then analyzed by the Fusion FX7 system (Peqlab, Erlangen, Germany). β-actin served as the internal control.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software 5.00. To ensure accuracy and reliability, each experiment was repeated at least three times. For data sets comprising three or more groups with a single variable, analysis was carried out using ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was determined at a p-value below 0.05 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 or ***p < 0.001 compared to control cells).

Results

Antiproliferative activity

Effect of compounds on prostate cancer cell growth

All the 3 saponins from P. guinensis tested showed a concentration-dependent and significant inhibition of LNCaP and PC3 prostate cancer cells after 48 h of incubation. However, the stronger (p < 0.001) one in the both cell lines was Guin-B (Fig. 2A-D) with IC50 of 10 µg/mL (~ 4.82 × 10− 6 µM) and 8 µg/mL (~ 3.86 × 10− 6 µM) in PC3 and LNCaP cells, respectively after 48 h.

The magnitude of growth suppression was time-dependent (Fig. 2E-F). The optimum concentrations were 5 (p < 0.05) and 10 µg/mL (p < 0.001).

Effect of compounds on Apoptosis and caspase-3 assay

The Figs. 3C, D and E show the type of cell death induced by Guin-B on LNCaP and PC3 cells. In PC3 cells, Guin-B induced a 35–40% increment of apoptotic cell population at both 5 and 10 µg/mL. As far as the LNCaP cells are concerned, there was a concentration-dependent and significant increment of the number of apoptotic cells (40% at 5 µg/mL and 55% at 10 µg/mL) (Fig. 3E).

In line with this observation, the caspase-3 activity was significantly increased in both PC3 (p < 0.05 at 5 µg/mL and p < 0.01 at 10 µg/mL) and LNCaP (p < 0.01) cells at the both tested concentrations (Fig. 3F-G).

Discussion

PCa is characterized by a strong proliferation of malignant cells in a gland localized just below the bladder so-called prostate. Despite advancements in prostate cancer (PCa) management, it remains the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men. It is projected that by 2040, this disease will result in 2,293,818 new cases and 500,000 deaths, primarily due to population growth and aging [5]. While current treatments effectively address 90% of patients with androgen-sensitive PCa, effective therapies for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) are lacking [29]. Today, a significant number of cancer cases are managed through the implementation of complementary and alternative preventive strategies. Plant-based medications have emerged as promising options for preventive chemotherapy in both developing and developed nations. Alkaloids, flavonoids, triterpenoids, and saponins are among the key targets in the search for potential anticancer agents, with approximately half of all approved anticancer drugs originating from natural sources [30, 31]. For some time, the African forest has been a rich reservoir for discovering effective anticancer agents [32]. In our recent exploration of plants with anticancer potential, we focused on Piper guineense, from which three triterpenoid saponins were isolated [26]. Given recent studies suggesting that this class of natural biomolecules may interfere with the initiation, development, and progression of various cancers [33], this study was aimed at evaluating their anticancer potential and elucidating the mode of action of guineenoside-B, the most active compound among them.

Common features of cancer cells include resistance to antigrowth signals and uncontrolled production of growth signals, inhibition of apoptosis, continuous cellular replication, angiogenesis and metastasis [34]. In this work, out of the 3 saponins tested, guineenoside-B showed better cytotoxic potential on both androgen sensitive (LNCaP) and non-sensitive (PC3) cells in comparison with other compounds with CC50 of 7.5 µg/mL in LNCaP and 9 µg/mL in PC3 cells. Guineenoside-A and guineenoside-C exhibited a CC50 of 20 µg/mL (LNCaP cells) and 18 µg/mL (PC3 cells) and 16 µg/mL (LNCaP cells) and 13 µg/mL (PC3 cells), respectively. This was the first time that this saponins were isolated and no studies have been done to evaluate its cytotoxic potential so far. These saponins share the same aglycone and are characterized by the length of the oligosaccharide inserted in the C-3 of the aglycone skeleton with often, up to two or three sugars, and up to three or six sugars on the oligosaccharide chain at C-28. Guineenoside-B has more oligosaccharides (4) inserted in C-28 than the other ones, and this may contribute to its potent cytotoxic effect as compared to the others. Saponins have proven efficacy and acceptability for cancer treatment in various types of cancers both in vitro and in vivo, through antiproliferative and anti-metastasic effects [35]. This result is in accordance with many other saponins, such as Diosgenin, which induced a CC50 of 47 µM in LnCaP cells, Cholestanol Glucoside induced Cell Death in PC3; Polyphyllin I had a CC50 with 2.13 µM in PC3 cells [36, 37], . The fact that Guineenoside-B exhibited anticancer effects against androgen-sensitive LNCaP and non-sensitive PC3 cells, indicates that it can be used in the development of therapy for breast cancer independently of the hormonal pathway.

In this study, cell growth arrest and antiproliferative activity of guineenoside-B were evidenced by its ability to induce apoptosis, a critical process that maintains tissue homeostasis and eliminates potentially harmful cells. Apoptosis is triggered when proapoptotic proteins of the Bcl2 family cause permeabilization of the mitochondrial outer membrane, leading to activation of caspases such as caspases 3, 6, and 9, or when cell-surface death receptors such as Fas are bound by their ligands [38]. The balance between apoptosis and cell survival is typically modulated by signaling pathways such as the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, a key intracellular signal transduction pathway [39]. AKT plays an anti-apoptotic role by phosphorylating the Ser136 site of Bcl-2-associated death promoter (BAD), leading to the dissociation of BAD from Bcl-2 and inhibition of BAD-induced apoptosis. Additionally, AKT phosphorylates the Ser184 site of Bax to inhibit its pro-apoptotic effect, or prevents the release of cytochrome c, an apoptosis-inducing factor, from the mitochondria [40, 41]. In our study, guineenoside-B induced cell growth arrest, inhibition of cell proliferation, and suppression of clone formation, along with downstream regulation of pAkt/Akt and mTOR. This suggests that guineenoside-B may exert its proapoptotic effect by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. These findings are further supported by the increased number of apoptotic cells observed after treatment of PCa cells with guineenoside-B at concentrations of 5 and 10 µg/mL, accompanied by an increase in caspase-3 activity. This effect bears resemblance to protodioscin, a well-known steroidal saponin that induces apoptosis by inactivating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway [42]. Thereby, in order to strongly suggest the intrinsic apoptosis as cell death mechanism related to cytotoxicity or inhibition by guineenoside-B of PC3 cell growth in the previous MTT, CCK-8 and clonogenic assays, the caspase 9 activity with pro- (Bax) and anti-apoptotic (Bcl-2, Bcl-XL) proteins levels of Bcl-2 gene family in addition to effector caspase-3 activity, should be further investigated.

A common feature shared among malignancies is their metastatic potential, define as their capability to spread throughout the body and colonize other tissues. This is the major reason of their lethality and anticancer drugs which target metastatic hallmarks are very crucial [43]. This refers to a condition where tumors dissemble from the primary tumor and find their way either in the blood stream or in different organs other than the initial one. The initial step in the EMT process is the acquisition of characteristics from an epithelial to a mesenchymal/invasive phenotype, described as EMT, which allows the cells to invade and disseminate [44]. During epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), there is a downregulation of genes/proteins encoding epithelial junction proteins such as E-cadherin and cytokeratin, leading to the disassembly of adherent junctions, desmosomes, and tight junctions, as well as the loss of apical–basal polarity [45]. Conversely, the expression of mesenchymal genes such as vimentin, fibronectin and N‐cadherin is upregulated, promoting cell migration and adhesion [46]. Guineenoside-B at concentrations of 5 and 10 µg/mL decreased cell migration, invasion, and mesenchymal transition, as well as angiogenic markers such as vimentin, VEGF, and β-catenin in PC3 cells, indicating its potential as an anti-metastatic compound. This effect was accompanied by a significant increase in PC3 cell adhesion to collagen and fibronectin extracellular matrix. Similarly, polyphyllin I, a saponin, is known to deactivate EMT and VEGF, a pro-angiogenic ligand overexpressed in many cancers [47]. Furthermore, Terrestrosin D, a saponin from Tribulus terrestris, also inhibited angiogenesis in PC3 cells through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways [48].

Conclusion

This is the first report of the anticancer activity of three (3) triterpenoid saponins (Guineenosides A, B and C) recently isolated from P. guineense. Guineenoside-B exhibited significant and concentration-dependent inhibition of PC3 and LNCaP cell growth and proliferation as well as a potent inhibition of PC3 cell clone formation. It induced a potent (50%) increment of apoptotic cell population in PC3 and LNCaP cells at both 5 and 10 µg/mL; which was accompanied by a significant increase in caspase-3 activity. Guineenoside-B induced a potent inhibition of PC3 cell migration/invasion and an increase of adherence of PC3 cells to the extracellular collagen and fibronectin matrix. A decrease in the expression of pAKT, AKT, mTOR and VEGF proteins was also noticed. Guineenoside B triggers apoptosis and PI3K/Akt/mTOR inactivation and inhibits metastasis at a low concentration. This encourages future investigation on this saponin.
