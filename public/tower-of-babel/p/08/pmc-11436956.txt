Construction of store-operated calcium entry-related gene signature for predicting prognosis and indicates immune microenvironment infiltration in stomach adenocarcinomas
Authors: Zichao Zhang, Chenglong Wang, Wenzheng Shi, Zhihui Wang, Weihua Fu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Gastric adenocarcinoma (STAD) is the most prevalent malignancy of the human digestive system and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death. Calcium pools, especially Ca2+ entry (SOCE) for storage operations, play a crucial role in maintaining intracellular and extracellular calcium balance, influencing cell activity, and facilitating tumor progression. Nevertheless, the prognostic and immunological value of SOCE in STAD has not been systematically studied. The objective of this study was to develop a risk model for SOCE signature and to explore its correlation with clinical characteristics, prognosis, tumor microenvironment (TME), as well as response to immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted drugs. We used the TCGA, GEO (GSE84437 and GSE159929), cBioPortal and TIMER databases to acquire mRNA expression data for STAD, along with patient clinical indicators, single-cell sequencing data, genomic variation information, and correlations of immune cell infiltration. An analysis of SOCE genes based on tumor vs. normal tissue comparisons, pan-cancer dimension, single-cell sequencing, DNA mutation, and copy number variation revealed that SOCE genes significantly impact the survival of STAD patients and are differentially involved in the immune response. SOCE co-expressed genes were identified by Pearson analysis, and subsequently protein-protein interaction (PPI) and gene function enrichment analysis indicated that coexpressed genes were associated with multicellular pathways. Based on TCGA and GSE84437 datasets, a multifactor Cox proportional hazard regression analysis was conducted to identify SOCE co-expressed genes associated with overall survival in STAD patients. Several mRNA prognostic genes, including PTPRJ, GPR146, LTBP3, FBLN1, EFEMP2, ADAMTS7 and LBH, were identified, which could be used as effective prognostic predictors for STAD patients. In both training and test datasets, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to evaluate and illustrate the predictive capability of this characteristic in forecasting overall survival of STAD patients. The qPCR and human protein atlas (HPA) were employed to assess mRNA expression and protein levels. Furthermore, the ESTIMATE, TIMER, CIBERSORT, QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER, xCell and EPIC algorithms were utilized to perform immune score and analyze immune cell infiltration. It effectively revealed the difference in prognosis and immune cell infiltration in TME between high-risk and low-risk groups based on the risk signature associated with SOCE. Notably, significant differences in tumor immune dysfunction and rejection (TIDE) scores between the two groups, suggesting that patients in the low-risk group may exhibit a more favorable response to ICIS treatment. The GDSC database and R packages for predictive analysis were utilized to analyze responses to chemotherapy and targeted drugs in high-risk and low-risk groups. In summary, the 7-gene signature associated with SOCE serves as a significant biomarker for evaluating the TME and predicting the prognosis of STAD patients. In addition, it may provide valuable insights for developing effective immunotherapy and chemotherapy regiments for patients with STAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73324-9.

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common malignant tumor worldwide and is considered to be one of the most significant factors affecting patient prognosis1. Stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), which accounts for 95% of GC cases, is the predominant histological type of gastrointestinal malignancies. Due to the lack of obvious or asymptomatic early symptoms, there are currently no sensitive and specific diagnostic markers for early STAD detection. This limitation greatly hinders the diagnosis and treatment of STAD2–4. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify effective prognostic markers for STAD and develop new therapeutic approaches and strategies.

Studies have demonstrated the significance of gene expression in predicting tumor clinical prognosis and identifying biomarkers. ALOX12B and PACSIN1 were found to be significantly higher in STAD patients with metastases, leading to lower survival rates5. EMCN/MUC15 combination was able to predict overall survival (OS) in STAD patients6. Ye et al. constructed a metabolic-based 13-gene risk model using the TCGA dataset to predict survival in STAD patients7. As a crucial second messenger, calcium ions play a precise role in regulating various physiological activities within cells and are essential for tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Store-operated calcium entry (SOCE) represents a unique mode of intra- and extracellular signaling. Ca2+ is induced to flow into cells by extracellular signaling molecules such as epidermal growth factor receptor activator. SOCE can be expressed in various cell types including immune cells, smooth muscle cells, and endothelial cells, contributing to the regulation of immune function, vasoconstriction, cell proliferation, and migration.

Research has indicated that elevated expression of Orai1 and STIM1 upregulates MACC1 expression leading to increased proliferation, metabolism, migration, and invasion of GC cells8. Overexpression of STIM1 promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition and is associated with poor prognosis in STAD patients9. Orai2 is associated with PI3K/Akt signaling pathway as well as MAPK-dependent focal adhesion dissociation which promotes tumorigenicity and metastasis in STAD10. Therefore, the targeted inhibition of calcium channels represents a promising new direction for tumor therapy. However, the value of SOCE in prognosis and immunity has not been systematically investigated in STAD.

In this study, a comprehensive analysis of gene expression, gene mutation, copy number variation, immune infiltration, and survival was conducted for the SOCE gene across various cancer types using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) STAD datasets. The analysis was performed using the R language package (Fig. 1). Co-expressed genes of SOCE were identified through Pearson correlation analysis, and their biological functions and associated signaling pathways were explored using GO and KEGG enrichment analysis methods. Prognostic features for stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) based on SOCE co-expressed genes were established using the multivariant Cox risk regression method with TCGA dataset as the training set and validated by dataset GSE84437 as an external dataset. Based on the risk score derived from these prognostic features, STAD patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. The correlation between prognostic signature and clinical characteristics of STAD patients was investigated. Furthermore, mRNA and protein levels of the signature were assessed through qPCR and Human Protein Atlas (HPA). Immune scoring and immune cell infiltration analysis were performed using ESTIMATE, TIMER, CIBERSORT, QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER, XCELLL, and EPIC algorithms. Additionally, Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Rejection (TIDE) score was utilized to evaluate immunotherapy efficacy. Response to chemotherapy and targeted drugs in high- or low-risk groups was analyzed using the GDSC database and the R package pRophetic prediction.Fig. 1The workflow of this study.

Materials and methods

High-throughput sequencing data collection and processing

The expression data of SOCE gene in 17 cancer types, including bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), esophageal cancer (ESCA), head and neck cancer (HNSC), kidney chromophobe (KICH), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), STAD, thyroid carcinoma (THCA), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), was be analyzed. The RNA-Seq expression levels generated by the workflow were read and counted for normalization, i.e., the transcriptional expression value FPKM-UQ was obtained, and the expression difference between the tumor and the corresponding normal tissues was calculated. High-throughput sequencing gene expression data and clinical information of STAD patients were obtained from the TCGA and NCBI GEO database. The TCGA-STAD dataset included 326 STAD tissues and 29 normal gastric tissues. Datasets GSE15992911 and GSE8443712 were obtained by downloading from the NCBI’s GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). They are based on GPL20795 HiSeq X Ten and GPL6947 (Illumina HumanHT-12 V3.0 Expression BeadChip) for sequencing, respectively. The TCGA-STAD dataset were utilized to analyze gene expression differences between STAD tissues and normal tissues using the R language limma package13. A false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and a relative expression value (|log2 fold change (FC) |) > = 1.5 were employed as thresholds for significant gene differences, with the results of gene difference analysis being presented using the ggplot2 package14. Furthermore, a single-cell database including healthy human tissues, the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE159929 was used to analyze the expression of the SOCE genes. The TCGA dataset was also served as the training dataset for constructing multiple genes signature. Finally, the GSE84437 dataset, comprising 433 STAD samples, was used as a validation set to assess the validity and accuracy of the prognostic features.

Gene mutation and copy number variation analysis

The cBioPortal database (http://www.cbioportal.org/), a data-mining web tool based on the TCGA database and developed by researchers at Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center, was utilized to analyze mutations and copy number variants (CNVs) in the SOCE gene in STAD.

Immune infiltration analysis

The TIMER database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) was used to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the infiltration levels of various immune cells. In this study, we employed the TIMER database to investigate the correlation between SOCE genes and immune cell infiltration in STAD. The RNA data of SOCE genes were quantified using the HPA online database, and different immune cluster genes were categorized based on their expression in diverse samples. The resulting clusters were manually annotated to characterize the function and specificity of the SOCE genes. An annotation of an immune cluster was presented alongside a confidence score assigned to that gene. The confidence score was determined by calculating how many times the gene was assigned to that immuno-infiltrating cluster in repeated counts, with reported values ranging from 0 to 1, where 1 indicated the highest possible confidence level. The clustering results were visualized as UMAP plots, with each gene’s assigned cluster highlighted as a region of corresponding color.

Survival analysis

To investigate the relationship between gene expression levels and STAD patients’ OS, the expression profiles were normalized to the logarithm. Univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted with a significance level of P value < 0.05, and hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The STAD samples were categorized into high/low expression level groups using the median value of expression levels as the cut-off point. Genes that showed significant association with OS were identified as prognosis-related genes in STAD patients. Survival curves for these genes were assessed and plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method and Log-rank test based on the R package Survival (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survival). The Log-rank test was utilized to determine statistical significance, with a cut-off threshold at P < 0.05.

Correlation and protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis

Co-expressed genes associated with SOCE genes were identified through Pearson correlation analysis. Data sets were truncated using a threshold of |Pearson R|> 0.4 and P < 0.01. Subsequently, the co-expressed genes were organized into PPI networks utilizing the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) online program, and then visualized using Cytoscape software with the MCODE plug-in for module and hub gene screening.

Functional enrichment analysis

The biological functions of SOCE co-expressed genes were enriched in terms of cellular composition (CC), molecular function (MF) and biological processes (BP). Additionally, enrichment analysis was conducted for biological pathways, diseases, and drugs using the R language packages ClusterProfile and ggplot2, based on Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)15.

Construction and evaluation of identified prognostic signature

Based on the SOCE co-expressed genes, the TCGA-STAD dataset was utilized as a training set to develop risk ratio-based patient prognostic features. The GEO dataset GSE84437 was used as a validation set to assess the validity and accuracy of the prognostic features. Using the Survival and Survminer packages in R, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conduceted on the SOCE co-expressed genes obtained in the previous step to identify statistically significant genes (P < 0.05) based on the clinical data of STAD patients in the TCGA dataset. Then, multivariate COX proportional hazards regression analysis was performed using bivariate regression to screen out genes associated with prognosis in STAD patients. Consequently, a predictive polygenic profile of OS time in STAD patients was established, and the risk score (RS) was calculated according to the following formula.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Risk~ score=\sum _{i=1}^{n}{Coef}_{i}* {x}_{i}$$\end{document}

Based on the median RS value, STAD patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. The survival of STAD patients was assessed using Kaplan–Meier method using Log-rank test. The prognostic signature’s validity and sensitivity were evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) for both the training and validation sets of STAD patients. Subgroups based on lymph node metastasis, clinicopathology, histological stage, and age were established in both the training and validation sets to analyze the relationship between the prognostic signature and clinical factors in STAD patients.

Cell culture and expression verification of signature genes

The gastric epithelial cell line (GES-1) and GC cell lines (NCI-N87, AGS, and MKN-45) were procured from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai, China. The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (BasaMedia, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, BasalMedia), 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin. All cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and quantified by UV spectrophotometer. Endogenous cDNA was generated by reverse transcription of total RNA and subjected to real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR, Table S1) using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit (TaKaRa, Otsu, Shiga, Japan). The mRNA expression was normalized using GAPDH as an internal reference, and the relative mRNA expression level was calculated by the 2-ΔΔCt method. The entire experiment was biologically repeated. The HPA online tool was used to obtain immunohistochemistry-based protein expression profiles in normal gastric tissue or STAD tissue.

Assessment of the tumor microenvironment (TME)

Based on RNA-seq data from the TCGA-STAD cohort, we utilized the ESTIMATE algorithm to compute the ESTIMATE score, tumor purity, stromal and immune score for charactering the TME16. The stromal and immune score indicate the degree of stromal and immune cell infiltration in the tumor tissue. The ESTIMATE score integrates measures of stromal score and immune score, along with the tumor purity score as indicators of tumor purity17. Higher tumor purity scores and lower ESTIMATE, stromal and immune scores indicate higher tumor purity and reduced levels of stromal and immune cell infiltration in tumor tissue. Violin plots were employed to visualize disparities in microenvironmental scores between high- and low-risk groups as well as among subgroups within each clinical factor.

Expression abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TIICs)

We utilized six algorithms to assess tumor immunity, including TIMER18 , CIBERSORT19, QUANTISEQ20, MCPcounter21, XCELL22, and EPIC. Thses were used to evalute the expression abundance of 22 TIICs in all samples and to analyze prognostic characteristics and immunity using the R package “IOBR”. Heatmaps were employed to present a correlation matrix of risk scores, prognostic marker genes, tumor purity, immune scores, stroma scores, ESTIMATE scores, and abundance of TIICs. Subsequently, box plots were utilized to demonstrate the variance in abundance of TIICs between high- and low-risk groups as estimated by CIBERSORT. Violin plots were employed to visualize differences in immune infiltration-related genes between the two groups.

Evaluation of immunotherapy response

We conducted a comparison of the expression levels of common immune checkpoints, immunostimulatory, immunoinhibbitors, chiemokines, MHC, etc. in the two risk groups. The predictive efficiency of STAD prognostic traits was evaluated using the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion Algorithm (TIDE, http: //tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) to analyze differences in immunotherapy biomarkers between the high- and low-risk groups23. High TIDE scores were correlated with a higher probability of being non-responder to immunotherapy, whike the opposite was observed for low TIDE scores.

Evaluation of chemotherapy response

According to the GDSC database (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer, https://www.cancerrxgene.org/), the response to chemotherapy or targeted drugs was predicted and analyzed. Subsequently, the sensitivity of high and low risk populations to the drugs was assessed by comparing the half inhibitory concentrations (IC50 values) using the R package pRophetic24.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance of two groups of variables fitted to a normal distribution was assessed using the unpaired Students’ t-test. Additionally, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for variables in more than two groups. The correlation between risk scores and clinicopathological parameters was analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and Wilcoxon rank sum test. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was utilized for assessing the correlation between two groups of variables.

Results

Expression of SOCE genes in STAD tissues

First, we conducted a pan-cancer study of SOCE gene expression, revealing significant differences in the expression levels of SOCE genes in most cancer types compared to their normal tissues, including BRCA, UCEC, CHOL, KICH, LIHC, STAD, and THCA. Specifically, in STAD, the expression levels of STIM2, ORAI1 and ORAI2 were up-regulated while STIM1 was down-regulated relative to normal tissues (Fig. 2A). Analysis of SOCE expression in relation to clinical parameters revealed significant differences in STIM1/2 and ORAI3 among different clinical subgroups and showed associations with tumor grade development (Fig. S1). KM analysis demonstrated that the expression of STIM2 and ORAI3 was associated with patients’ OS (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, exploration into the impact of immune infiltration on patient survival in STAD indicated that macrophage infiltration significantly affected patient survival; specifically low-abundance macrophage infiltration was associated with longer survival (P = 0.004, Fig. 2C). Additionally, correlation analysis between SOCE gene expression levels and immune infiltration revealed significant positive correlations between SOCE genes except for ORAI2 with various immune cell types such as B cells, CD8 + cells, CD4 + cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells; notably ORAI2 (cor = 0.40, P = 2.72e-05) and ORAI3 (cor = 0.40, P = 2.72e-05) were significantly positively correlated with CD4 + cell infiltration. We then selected five STAD datasets and conducted an analysis of the DNA polymorphisms of SOCE genes. The results are presented in Fig. 2D and E, indicating that mutations were commonly found in SOCE genes (Fig. S2), along with varying degree of amplification and deep deletion. Furthermore, sequence polymorphisms were examined for their correlation with immune cell infiltration, revealing a significant association between arm-level deletion and arm-level gain with immune cells such as B cells, T cells, macrophage, neutrophil, and dendritic cells (Fig. 2F). In the STAD tissues from HPA database, it was observed that all SOCE genes except ORAI1 exhibited different protein expression levels (Fig. 2G). Establishment of multigene Cox prognostic signature based on SOCE-related genes

To investigate the impact of SOCE-related genes on patient survival, we conducted Cox one-way regression analysis on 357 co-expressed SOCE genes. Among these, 23 genes exhibited P-values less than 0.05. Subsequently, multi-way Cox regression analysis was performed on these 23 genes, revealing that 7 of them had P-values less than 0. 05. The results of the analysis indicate that these 7 genes are correlated with patients’ OS. Specifically, PTPRJ (HR = 0.809, P < 0.001) may be benign prognosis, while GPR146 (HR = 1.3, P = 0. 002), LTBP3 (HR = 1.2, P = 0.019), FBLN1 (HR = 1.13, P = 0.007), EFEMP2 (HR = 1.19, P = 0.02), ADAMTS7 (HR = 1.2, P = 0.041) and LBH (HR = 1.4, P < 0.001) may suggest poor prognosis.

A risk-prognosis signature for mRNA was developed based on a set of 7 genes. The prognostic scoring formula was determined as follows: RS = -0.25 × PTPRJ expression + 0.176 × GPR146 expression—0.07 × LTBP3 expression + 0.09 × FBLN1 expression + 0.34 × LBH expression—0.27 × EFEMP2 expression + 0.12 × ADAMTS7 expression. Based on the median RS value (1.024), the TCGA dataset consisting of 326 patients was stratified into low-risk and high-risk groups. Survival analysis revealed a significantly higher survival rate in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group (Logrank P < 0.0001, Fig. 5A). Furthermore, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of STAD patients were assessed, and the time-dependent ROC curves demonstrated AUC values of 0.615, 0.635, and 0.758, respectively, indicating a strong prognostic capability for this prognostic feature (Fig. 5B). Additionally, Fig. 5C illustrates the gene expression of the prognostic signature in both high-risk and low-risk groups. The findings suggest that higher the signature prognostic score is associated with worse the prognosis and increased mortality among STAD patients. The accuracy of the 7-gene prognostic profile was further validated using the GEO external dataset GSE84437. Based on the median RS value (0.975) calculated by the prognostic score formula, 433 STAD patients were categorized into low-risk and high-risk groups. As depicted in Fig. 5D, STAD patients in the low-risk group exhibited significantly higher OS compared to those in the high-risk group (P < 0.0001). Analysis of patient survival at intervals of 1, 3, and 5 years revealed AUC values for the prognostic feature as follows: 0.582, 0.654, and 0.735, respectively, suggesting robust prognostic ability (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, the results indicated that higher the prognostic score were associated with increased mortality among STAD patients in the GSE84437 dataset (Fig. 5F). Moreover, the mRNA expression levels of these seven genes were examined across different cell lines using qPCR. Compared to the GES-1 cell line, the expression levels of PTPRJ, LTBP3, ADANTS7, EFEMP2, and LBH showed an overall increasing trend in the GC cell line; conversely, GPR146 and FBLN1 showed an overall decreasing trend (Fig. 5G). Next, we conducted an investigate into the correlation between 7-mRNA prognostic features and OS of STAD patients in various clinical subgroups. The findings revealed that within the TCGA-STAD dataset (Fig. 6A), there was a significant increase in clinical stage (I-II and III-IV), lymph node metastasis stage (N0, N1-N3), tumor grade (T1-T2 and T3-T4), non-distal metastasis (M0), pathological grade (G1-G2 and G3-G4), age (young and old), and gender (male and female) within different clinical subgroups. Subsequently, we conducted an analysis of the expression differences of prognostic signature genes in the aforementioned subgroups with significant variances in pathological indicators. We found that most of the genes exhibited significant differences in pathological and T stage subgroups. Specifically, the expression levels of GPR146, LTBP3, FBLN1, EFEMP2 and LBH were significantly higher in the advance stages compared to the early stages. In summary, our findings indicate a notable association between the expression of 7-mRNA prognostic features and the OS of STAD patients across different clinical subgroups.

Discussion

STAD is one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting the digestive system and carries one of the highest incidence and mortality rates among all cancers worldwide. The development and progression of STAD involve a complex multistage process characterized by numerous genetic and epigenetic changes. The effectiveness and approach to cancer treatment often hinge on the stage at which the cancer is diagnosed. Unfortunately, diagnosis of STAD typically occurs at an advanced stage, leading to challenging diagnosis, poor prognosis, and high mortality. Molecular markers derived from coding or non-coding genes hold significant potential in predicting cancer prognosis.

Studies have demonstrated that SOCE serves as a crucial pathway for calcium signaling in nearly all animal cells, playing diverse roles in gene expression, motility and secretion to tissue and organ development and immune responses25. However, the progress of STAD research has yet to systematically address the analysis of SOCE gene expression and variation affecting survival, prognosis, and immune regulation. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of the molecular features, functional mechanisms, clinical significance, and immune infiltration relevance of STIM1 in human tumors based on pan-cancer data from TCGA database26. In this study, a series of analyses revealed significant differences in the expression of SOCE genes between STAD tissues and normal tissues, with the exception of ORAI1. Additionally, there were significant differences in the expression levels of ORAI3 and STIM1 with the development of histological grading stages of STAD. When combined with the expression results of cell populations and immune cell populations, as well as the correlation analysis of SOCE gene polymorphisms with immune cells, it was found that the expression levels and gene polymorphisms of SOCE were heterogeneous and related to tumor immune infiltration. These findings suggest that SOCE is involved in the process of tumor development and immune response, ultimately affecting patient survival outcomes. Furthermore, a prognostic signature containing seven genes (PTPRJ, GPR146, LTBP3, FBLN1, EFEMP2, ADAMTS7, and LBH) was constructed based on co-expressed genes related to SOCE. This signature can serve as an effective prognostic predictor for STAD patients.

Studies have revealed that the 7-mRNA signature genes are linked to tumor progression and prognosis. Ptprj has been identified as a candidate for the mouse colon-cancer susceptibility locus Scc1 and has been targeted by miR-155 in CRC as a tumor suppressor27,28. LTBP3 has been shown to promote early metastatic events during the early dissemination of primary cancer cells, particularly in the intravasation step of the metastatic cascade29. HBx-related long non-coding RNA MALAT1 promoted cell metastasis via up-regulating LTBP3 in HCC30. Fibulin-1 (FBLN1) is a major component of the extracellular matrix in lung tissue, and its levels have been reported to be downregulated in non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLC), while also being identified as a potential biomarker for early detection of cervical cancer31,32. EFEMP2 expression was lower in BRCA and closely associated with patient prognosis; it promoted CRC cell invasion and growth through the ERK1/2 signaling pathway, serving as a tumor suppressor gene that inhibited the progress of lung cancer33–35. ADAMTS7-mediated degradation of Comp may offer a potential therapeutic target for osteosarcomas treatment36. LBH was identified as a potential independent prognostic biomarker that promotes proliferation and invasion of GC cells by activating the integrin/FAK/Akt pathway; it also inhibits cellular migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in nasopharyngeal carcinoma via downregulating αB-crystallin expression37,38. The above reports suggest the potential impact of the 7-mRNA prognostic signature constructed for STAD. However, the 7-gene prognostic signature based on SOCE co-expressed genes established in this study has not been previously reported as a reliable predictor of prognosis in STAD. Nevertheless, this signature shows promise as a potential marker for predicting the prognosis of STAD and provides theoretical support for identifying relevant therapeutic target drugs.

Tumor, stromal and infiltrating immune cells are intricately coordinated within TME. Given the resolution of TME and the mechanisms of immune escape, tumor immunotherapy plays a pivotal role in clinical treatment39. Firstly, we noted a significant increase in immunization scores, interstitial scores, and assessment scores in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group. Secondly, we conducted an analysis on the disparity in infiltration levels of 22 immune cells between the high- and low-risk groups. Through immunotherapy, memory CD8 + T cells are generated to infiltrate the tumor parenchyma and consistently prevent metastasis and recurrence. M2 macrophages, often considered as tumor-associated macrophages, can create an immune barrier to CD8 + T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune responses40. In this study, we observed that although patients in the high-risk group had a high proportion of CD8 + T cells, immunosuppressive cells such as M2 macrophages were widely distributed and exhibited a relatively active anti-tumor immune response, thereby promoting tumor progression. The resting MC cells in the high-risk group were significantly higher than those in the low-risk group, while activated MC cells were significantly lower than those in the low-risk group. This suggests that tumor progression is inhibited by activated MC within the tumor microenvironment. In addition, the higher proportion of memory B cells in the low-risk group facilitated rapid reactivation to form plasma cells, thereby promoting an effective immune response and inhibiting tumor progression. Previous studies have explored the potential role of metabolic reprogramming in driving the shift between activated and resting CD4 + memory T cell infiltration in the GC tumor microenvironment. The results are consistent with our present study, which found that the low-risk group had lower levels of resting CD4 + memory T cells and higher levels of activated CD4 + memory T cells, suggesting a correlation with a better prognosis for patients. We further investigated the relationship between prognostic signatures and the TME. Our findings revealed a significant increase in immunization scores, interstitial scores, and assessment scores in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group.

In addition, the TIDE results revealed that the score values of the high-risk group were significantly higher than those of the low-risk group. Furthermore, common immune checkpoint-related genes (PD-L1, CTLA-4, IDO1, and TIGIT) were found to be highly expressed in the low-risk group. This suggests that immunotherapy may be more suitable for the low-risk group. To provide better guidance for STAD treatment, drug sensitivity analyses were conducted in both the high- and low-risk groups. A variety of anticancer drugs were studied, revealing that the low-risk group showed sensitivity to 27 anticancer drugs while the high-risk group was sensitive to only 4 anticancer drugs. These findings offer valuable insights for clinical selection of chemotherapeutic agents. In future studies, we aim to further explore the clinical significance of these drugs for STAD patients. Overall, our results indicate that the prognostic profile established in this study can serve as a rational and effective method for screening patients undergoing treatment. Additionally, targeting SOCE-related genes in combination with immune checkpoint blockade therapy and/or chemotherapy may represent a promising therapeutic strategy to improve outcomes for STAD patients.

Despite our significant findings, this study does have certain limitations. Specifically, our analysis and conclusions are based on data obtained from public databases, which may introduce inherent case selection bias. Furthermore, while our findings were validated through evaluations using multiple external datasets, it is imperative to gather gastric cancer cases and clinical information, validate the risk scoring model, and compare it with the gold standard for clinical diagnosis in order to ensure that the risk model developed in this study can be applied to clinical practice and further validate the accuracy of our results. Lastly, additional in vivo and in vitro experiments are necessary to explore the function of prognostic signatures in STAD.

Conclusion

Through pan-cancer analysis, we have identified significant differences in SOCE gene expression across multiple cancer types. Furthermore, both the expression levels and gene polymorphisms of SOCE are associated with patient survival and tumor immunity levels. Subsequently, based on the TCGA-STAD and GSE83347 datasets, a 7-mRNA prognostic signature was constructed. This signature includes seven SOCE-correlated genes: PTPRJ, GPR146, LTBP3, FBLN1, EFEMP2, ADAMTS7 and LBH. Functionally, the risk score derived from this prognostic signature is highly correlated with the TME and immune cell infiltration in STAD patients. Combined analysis of the risk score with stromal score or immune score could more accurately predict the response to immunotherapy in STAD patients. In terms of drug sensitivity, we have identified a series of sensitive drugs for patients in both high-risk and low-risk groups respectively. In summary, our prognostic risk model can serve as a reliable biomarker for individualized treatment of STAD patients.
