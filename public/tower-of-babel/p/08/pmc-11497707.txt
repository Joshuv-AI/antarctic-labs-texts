INTRODUCTION

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations including behavioral, lingual, and executive‐functional deficits. 1 Based on the dominant clinical manifestations, patients with FTD can be classified into three subtypes: the behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD), the nonfluent variant primary progressive aphasia (nfvPPA), and the semantic variant PPA (svPPA). 2 Among them, the bvFTD subtype is characterized by apathy, lack of empathy, impulsivity, and execution‐oriented cognitive dysfunction; the nfvPPA subtype is characterized by linguistic difficulties including hesitant speech, agrammatism, and dysarthria; and the svPPA subtype is characterized by semantic dysfunctions such as impaired object recognition, dyslexia, and difficulty in naming. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 Besides the distinctive clinical presentations, the three FTD subtypes also differ in terms of the severity of functional impairments, 6 , 7 clinical courses, and prognostic outcomes. 8 , 9 , 10 For example, patients with the bvFTD subtype are often associated with more pronounced functional disabilities, faster clinical progression, and shorter survival relative to the other two subtypes, 11 , 12 suggesting disparate neural substrates among subtypes.

Neuroimaging studies have revealed divergent gray matter volume (GMV) reductions in the three subtypes, with the bvFTD subtype mainly involving the frontal and anterior temporal lobes, the svPPA subtype mainly involving the anterior temporal lobe, and the nfvPPA subtype mainly involving the inferior frontal and insular regions. 13 , 14 However, the interpretation of GMV changes assessed using voxel‐based morphometry can be difficult because GMV is a mixture of cortical thickness, surface area, and/or cortical folding. 15 , 16 Indeed, the three constituent components of GMV are both phenotypically and genetically independent and likely to be affected by different neuropathologic events. 15 No prior studies have simultaneously characterized the subtype‐specific changes in the three morphological features of FTD. Studies as such may provide new insights into the neural mechanisms underlying the clinical heterogeneity of this disorder.

Apart from the GM changes, studies have uncovered divergent white matter (WM) abnormalities in FTD subtypes. 14 , 17 For example, a recent study reported varying degrees of WM lesions among FTD subtypes, with the bvFTD subtype exhibiting more severe WM hyperintensities (WMH) than other subtypes. 18 , 19 Meanwhile, FTD patients with GRN gene mutation were prone to have more severe WMH than those without, suggesting a genetic basis for the WM lesions. 20 , 21 Questions however remain unanswered as to whether and how WM lesions and cortical morphological abnormalities in FTD are spatially and causally related. Considering the essential role of WM in metabolite provision, 22 signal transduction, 6 and neurotrophic support to GM, 23 elucidating the interplay between WM lesions and GM morphological abnormalities may uncover potential mechanistic pathways mediating these endophenotypic abnormalities in FTD.

Recent studies have found that changes in GM and WM are tightly linked to a disrupted glymphatic system, 24 which plays crucial roles in the removal of neurotoxic protein aggregates in neurodegenerative diseases. 25 In FTD, around half of cases are accompanied by neuronal inclusions of toxic TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43) aggregates, while 36%–50% of cases are accompanied by aggregation of hyperphosphorylated tau proteins. 4 , 26 Neuropathologic abnormalities as such have been linked to glymphatic dysfunction that occurs as a result of AQP4 polarization and vascular defects. 27 , 28 Moreover, studies also found a significant spatial overlapping between regions with trapped contrast agents along perivascular space and cortical regions with profound proteinopathy in FTD. 29 , 30 Intriguingly, a recent study demonstrated abnormal glymphatic function in the bvFTD variant using diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI‐ALPS) index. 25 Whether changes in the glymphatic system are subtype‐specific and related to GM and WM abnormalities in FTD remain unknown.

This study aimed to investigate the subtype‐specific changes in cortical morphology, WMH, glymphatic function, and their associations in FTD. We hypothesized that (1) cortical morphological changes, severity of WMH, and glymphatic dysfunctions would be subtype‐specific in FTD, with the bvFTD subtype exhibiting the most prominent changes; (2) cortical morphological changes would relate to whole‐brain WMH volume and DTI‐ALPS in FTD; (3) the neural underpinnings of cortical morphological deviations among FTD subtypes would encompass biological processes/pathways critical for the development and spread of TDP‐43 and/or tau pathologies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

Participants enrolled in the Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration Neuroimaging Initiative (FTLDNI) (https://4rtni‐ftldni.ini.usc.edu/) were considered for inclusion in this study. The FTLDNI database is a multicentric database that collects Positron Emission Tomography (PET), MRI, and Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) biomarkers of patients with FTD and matched healthy controls (HCs). Patients with FTD were diagnosed according to the Neary criteria. Exclusion criteria for FTD patients included major psychiatric or neurologic comorbidity, sustained hypertension, and vascular diseases. 31 The severity of cognitive impairment and clinical dementia in patients with FTD was assessed using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scales, respectively. Inclusion criteria for HCs encompassed cognitive normality, as indicated by a CDR score of 0 and an MMSE score ranging from 26 to 30, along with normal physical health and no prior history of mental illness. 13 After excluding patients with poor image quality, inaccurate cortical surface reconstruction, and local gyrification index (LGI) computational errors, a total of 122 FTD patients were included in this study, consisting of 56 bvFTD, 30 svPPA, and 36 nfvPPA. Considering the significant differences in age and sex composition among the three subtypes, we meticulously selected three age‐ and sex‐matched control groups, comprising a total of 101 HCs from the FTLDNI database, for comparison with each FTD subtype.

MRI data acquisition

The multimodal MRI data were acquired at distinct locations using a Siemens 3.0T Tim Trio scanner with a 12‐channel receiver head coil. T1‐weighted MRI data were collected using a three‐dimensional magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.98 ms, flip angle = 9°, slice thickness = 1 mm, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. T2‐weighted MRI data were collected using a fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery sequence with the following parameters: TR = 6000 ms, TE = 389 ms, inversion time = 2100 ms, flip angle = 120°, slice thickness = 1 mm, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. Diffusion‐weighted MRI data were collected using a single‐shot echo‐planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: b‐value = 0, 1000 s/mm2, gradient directions = 44, TR = 9200 ms, TE = 82 ms, slice thickness = 2.7 mm, voxel size = 2.73 × 2.73 × 2.70 mm3, phase encoding direction = anterior‐posterior.

Cortical morphological analyses

T1‐weighted MRI data were processed using FreeSurfer (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) to obtain each participant's cortical thickness, surface area and LGI maps. In brief, the T1‐weighted images were segmented to identify the gray/white matter boundary, which was further tessellated and topologically corrected to generate a refined surface representation of the boundary (i.e., the white surface). This surface was used as the initial shape for a deformable model to reconstruct the pial surface. From these cortical surfaces, we can derive the three cortical morphological indices. Specifically, the cortical thickness map was obtained by measuring the distance between the pial and white surfaces; the cortical surface area map was computed by allocating one‐third of the area of each triangle to each of its vertices; the LGI map was computed by measuring the ratio of the pial surface area to the outer hull surface area in a vertex‐wise fashion across the cerebral cortex. The maps of cortical thickness, surface area, and LGI were resampled onto the “fsaverage” template in FreeSurfer and then smoothed using Gaussian‐kernels with full‐width‐at‐half‐maximum (FWHM) of 20, 20, and 15 mm, respectively. We chose a lower FWHM for LGI as LGI is an inherently smoothed metric. 32 , 33 Prior to statistical analysis, all the surfaces used for the calculation of the morphological indices were visually checked. Participants with poor surface reconstruction or LGI calculation error in at least one cortical hemisphere were excluded. The number of excluded participants, along with the specific reason for their exclusion, can be found in supplementary methods.

Vertex‐wise contrast of cortical thickness, surface area, and LGI maps were carried out between each FTD subtype and their matched healthy controls using SurfStat package (http://www.math.mcgill.ca/keith/surfstat/). For every vertex, a general linear model (GLM) was applied with covariates including age, sex, and group. Clusters of interest were firstly defined using a vertex‐wise p < 0.001 and then corrected for multiple comparisons across the entire brain at the cluster level using random field theory (RFT). 34 The significance level for these clusters was set at p < 0.05 after multiple‐comparison correction.

To examine the morphological differences among the three subtypes, vertex‐wise analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted respectively for cortical thickness, surface area, and LGI using SurfStat package with age and sex as covariates of no interest. Clusters of interest were firstly defined using a vertex‐wise p < 0.001 and then corrected for multiple comparisons across the entire brain at the cluster level using RFT. The significance level for these clusters was set at p < 0.05 after multiple‐comparison correction. To explore the confounding effect of WMH burden on the between‐group comparisons, we replicated the above surface‐based analyses while incorporating whole‐brain WMH volume as an additional covariate of no interest.

Using the pooled data of the three subtypes, we computed the Spearman's partial correlation coefficient between the global mean value of each of the three morphological indices and the clinical measurements (the CDR and MMSE scores) while adjusting for age and sex as covariates of no interest.

WMH analysis

WMH volumes were measured using a fully automated toolbox, UBO Detector. 35 Briefly, individual T2 images were first registered to their corresponding T1 images. Tissue segmentation was then conducted, and the Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie (DARTEL) algorithm was employed to warp the T1 images onto a study‐specific template in the DARTEL space. To warp the T2 images onto the same DARTEL template, the field maps generated by warping individual T1 images to the DARTEL space were applied to the T2 images that had already been registered to the T1 images. Following the removal of non‐brain tissue and correction for bias field on the T2 images in DARTEL space, a supervised machine learning algorithm, k‐nearest neighbor (k‐NN), was employed to identify the WMH clusters, which were further categorized based on their anatomic location into periventricular, deep, frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital WMH. The quality control HTML webpages generated by UBO Detector during the registration, segmentation, and extraction of WMH were meticulously reviewed and evaluated by an experienced radiologist (Y.M. Zhang) to detect any potential processing errors. Prior to statistical analysis, the WMH volumes were log‐transformed to improve the normality of their distribution.

WMH volumes for a particular brain region and the whole brain were compared between each subtype and their matched HCs as well as among the three FTD subtypes using the GLM in SPSS, with age and sex as covariates of no interest. Using the pooled data of the three subtypes, we computed Spearman's partial correlation coefficient between whole‐brain WMH volume and clinical measurements (the CDR and MMSE scores) while adjusting for age and sex as covariates of no interest.

DTI‐ALPS analysis

DTI‐ALPS index was computed from DTI data to assess the glymphatic function of each participant. Briefly, DTI data were denoised, head movement and eddy current corrected. The fractional anisotropy (FA) and x‐axis, y‐axis, and z‐axis diffusivity maps were estimated and transformed into the JHU‐ICBM‐FA template space 25 using linear and nonlinear registration tools in FSL. The projection and association fibers (i.e., the superior corona radiata [SCR] and the superior longitudinal fasciculus [SLF]) at the level of lateral ventricle body were identified based on the JHU‐ICBM‐DTI‐81‐white‐matter Labeled Atlas. Four 5 mm‐diameter spherical masks located in the areas of bilateral SCR and SLF were created and applied to each participant's diffusivity maps. The mean diffusivity values in the x‐axis (Dxx), y‐axis (Dyy), and z‐axis (Dzz) of the masks on projection and association fibers were computed and denoted respectively as Dxxproj, Dyyproj, Dzzproj, Dxxassoc, Dyyassoc, and Dzzassoc. Subsequently, the DTI‐ALPS index can be calculated as a ratio of [(Dxxproj + Dxxassoc) / (Dyyproj + Dzzassoc)]. 36 The diffusion maps for each participant were visually checked to identify and exclude any potential processing errors or images with poor quality (see supplementary methods).

Contrasts of the DTI‐ALPS index were carried out between each subtype and their matched HCs as well as among the three FTD subtypes using the GLM in SPSS, with age and sex as covariates of no interest. Using the pooled data of the three subtypes, we computed Spearman's partial correlation coefficient between the DTI‐ALPS index and clinical measurements (the CDR and MMSE scores) while adjusting for age and sex as covariates of no interest.

Intermodal correlation analyses

Spearman's correlation coefficient was computed using the pooled data of the three subtypes to examine the associations among the global mean value of each morphological index, the whole‐brain WMH volume, and the DTI‐ALPS index. The correlation analyses were further corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. Using the “mediation toolbox” within Matlab, we also explored the mediated relationships among the imaging and clinical measurements in FTD.

Neuroimaging‐transcriptional association analysis

A neuroimaging‐transcription association analysis was performed to explore the potential biological pathways accounting for the morphological deviances among the FTD subtypes. Specifically, the mean f‐value for each of the 68 cortical regions of the Desikan–Killiany Atlas was extracted from the F‐map of the aforementioned vertex‐wise analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) respectively for the three morphological indices, resulting in three 68 ×1 vectors of regional morphologic deviance. Next, gene expression data were extracted from the Allen Human Brain Atlas transcriptomic dataset and mapped onto 68 cortical regions of the Desikan–Killiany Atlas using the abagen toolbox (https://github.com/rmarkello/abagen), resulting in a 68×16533 regional transcription matrix. Subsequently, a partial least square (PLS) regression was conducted to explore the association between regional transcription data and each of the three regional morphologic deviance vectors, with the regional transcription data as the predictor variables and the regional morphologic deviance vector as the response variable. Using PLS regression, we can maximize the covariance between predictor and response variables and identify a set of latent components. In neuroimaging transcriptomic analysis, the first component (PLS1) represents the linear combination weights of the gene expression data that best predict the neuroimaging feature across regions. A permutation test with 1000 replications was used to evaluate the statistical significance of PLS1. The error estimating each gene's PLS1 weight was measured through bootstrapping (resampling the 68 cortical regions 1000 times with replacement). A Z‐score was then calculated for each gene as the ratio of the weight to its bootstrap standard deviation. Genes with a Z‐score > 4 for each of the three morphological indices, indicative of pronounced morphological deviances among subtypes, were amalgamated for subsequent enrichment analysis. After obtaining these genetic ‘hits’, we used Metascape (https://metascape.org/) to calculate enrichments of gene ontology (GO) biological processes and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways.

WMH burdens in variants of FTD

Compared with HCs, the bvFTD subtype showed higher volumes of WMH across the whole brain (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.595), periventricular WM (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.582), deep WM (p = 0.002, Cohen's d = 0.574), left frontal lobe (p = 0.006, Cohen's d = 0.492), right frontal lobe (p = 0.003, Cohen's d = 0.546), left temporal lobe(p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.751), right temporal lobe (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.795), left parietal lobe (p = 0.011, Cohen's d = 0.454), right parietal lobe (p = 0.035, Cohen's d = 0.373), left occipital lobe (p = 0.030, Cohen's d = 0.405) and right occipital lobe (p = 0.008, Cohen's d = 0.506); the svPPA showed higher volumes of WMH across the periventricular WM (p = 0.036, Cohen's d = 0.560) and left temporal lobe (p = 0.017, Cohen's d = 0.649) (Table 2). No significant differences in WMH volumes were observed between the nfvPPA subtype and its corresponding matched HCs. ANCOVA among the three subtypes revealed significant differences in the WMH volumes of the whole brain, periventricular WM, Deep WM, frontal lobe, temporal lobe, parietal lobe, and right occipital lobe, overall showing the most severe WM lesions in the bvFTD subtype and the least severe WM lesions in the nfvPPA subtype (Figure S8).

Changes in DTI‐ALPS in variants of FTD

Each of the three FTD subtypes exhibited a significantly lower DTI‐ALPS index compared to their corresponding matched HCs (mean DTI‐ALPS = 1.217 for bvFTD, mean DTI‐ALPS = 1.362 for matched HCs, FDR‐corrected p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.233; mean DTI‐ALPS = 1.232 for nfvPPA, mean DTI‐ALPS = 1.322 for matched HCs, FDR‐corrected p = 0.003, Cohen's d = 0.796; mean DTI‐ALPS = 1.295 for svPPA, mean DTI‐ALPS = 1.364 for matched HCs, FDR‐corrected p = 0.034, Cohen's d = 0.609). ANCOVA among the three subtypes indicated noteworthy differences in the DTI‐ALPS index (p = 0.017). Pair‐wise intergroup comparisons revealed significant differences between svPPA and bvFTD (FDR‐corrected p = 0.014, Cohen's d = 0.678), between svPPA and nfvPPA (FDR‐corrected p = 0.034, Cohen's d = 0.564), but not between nfvPPA and bvFTD (FDR‐corrected p = 0.751) (Figure S9).

Associations among imaging and clinical data

In patients with FTD, higher cortical thickness related to lower CDR scores (r = −0.312, FDR‐corrected p = 0.006) (Figure 5A) and higher MMSE score (r = 0.228, FDR‐corrected p = 0.029); higher LGI related to lower CDR score (r = −0.285, FDR‐corrected p = 0.008); higher surface area related to lower CDR score (r = −0.205, FDR‐corrected p = 0.043); higher volume of WMH across the whole brain related to higher CDR score (r = 0.227, FDR‐corrected p = 0.029) and lower MMSE score (r = −0.275, FDR‐corrected p = 0.010) (Figure 5B). Higher volume of WMH across the whole brain related to lower cortical thickness (r = −0.169, uncorrected p = 0.044), surface area (r = −0.243, FDR‐corrected p = 0.016), and LGI (r = −0.268, FDR‐corrected p = 0.011) (Figure 5C) in the patients with FTD. Lower DTI‐ALPS related to lower cortical thickness in patients with FTD (r = 0.318, FDR‐corrected p = 0.007) (Figure 5D). Whole‐brain WMH volume had a significant mediating effect on the association between cortical thickness and MMSE score, while both cortical thickness and LGI had a significant mediating effect on the association between whole‐brain WMH volume and CDR score (Figure S10).

Neuroimaging‐transcriptional association analysis

The regional PLS1 scores, computed as a weighted sum of the 16533 gene expression dataset, demonstrated significant correlations with the f‐values representing regional deviations in cortical thickness, surface area, and LGI among the three FTD subtypes, accounting respectively for 51.8%, 28.4%, and 23.8% of the variance of the corresponding ANCOVA. Using a threshold of normalized weights Z > 4, we pinpointed a collective count of 2825 pivotal genes linked to regional cortical thickness, surface area, and LGI deviances in patients with different subtypes of FTD. Utilizing the identified gene list, functional enrichment analysis unveiled an array of critical biological processes/pathways crucial to the pathophysiology of FTD, encompassing “protein phosphorylation”, “protein localization to plasma membrane”, “oxidative phosphorylation”, “fatty acid metabolism”, “MAPK pathway”, “ leukocyte activation involved in immune response”, “axonogenesis”, and “actin filament organization”, among others (Figure 5E).

DISCUSSION

Capitalizing on the multimodal MRI data of the FTLDNI, this study examined the subtype‐specific changes in cortical morphology, WMH, and perivascular glymphatic changes, as well as their interrelationships and associations with clinical data in FTD. Compared with HCs, the three FTD subtypes showed differential topographic patterns of reductions in cortical thickness, surface area, and LGI, with their global mean related to the CDR and/or MMSE scores. Compared with HCs, the bvFTD subtype showed a higher WMH volume across the whole brain, whereas such results were not found in the nfvPPA and svPPA subtypes. Using the pooled data of the three subtypes, the whole‐brain WMH volume was negatively correlated with the MMSE score and positively correlated with the CDR score. In addition, patients with FTD also showed subtype‐specific reductions in DTI‐ALPS. In FTD, higher whole‐brain WMH volume was related to lower cortical thickness, surface area, and LGI, while lower DTI‐ALPS indexing sluggish glymphatic flow was related to lower cortical thickness in FTD. Neuroimaging‐transcriptional association analysis on regional cortical morphological deviances among FTD subtypes has identified fundamental biological processes/pathways closely linked to the formation and spread of TDP‐43/tau pathologies.

Compared with HCs, we found distinct topographic patterns of cortical thickness reductions in the three FTD subtypes, which are in good agreement with previous studies. 13 , 37 Specifically, the nfvPPA subtype showed predominant thickness reductions in the bilateral frontal cortex, an area critical for language production and executive function; 38 the svPPA subtype showed predominant thickness reductions in the bilateral anterior and middle temporal cortex, regions that are fundamental for semantic knowledge, single word comprehension, and memory 39 , 40 , 41 ; the bvFTD subtype showed more extensive thickness reductions in the bilateral frontal, temporal and parietal cortices, which involve almost all key hubs of the default mode network and have key roles in cognitive processes such as thinking, self‐awareness, executive control, and emotional processing. 42 Using the pooled data of three subtypes, we revealed significant correlations of the global mean cortical thickness with the MMSE and CDR scores, suggesting that the cortical thickness reductions may have contributed to the cognitive impairments in FTD. In parallel, we found a similar pattern of surface area reductions as the thickness reductions in the three subtypes, although the affected cortical regions in general were less widely distributed. These results may indicate a coexistence of both radial and tangential (relative to the cortical surface) pathological changes in the FTD subtypes, with the tangential changes being relatively milder than the radial ones. This study also revealed distinct patterns of LGI reductions in the three subtypes, involving predominantly the posterior cingulate and precentral cortices in the nfvPPA subtype, the bilateral orbitofrontal and inferior frontal cortices in the svPPA subtype, and more widespread LGI reductions of the insula and neighboring areas extending to the bilateral frontal, pre‐ and post‐central and inferior parietal in the bvFTD subtype. Considering that gyrification has been thought to be a surrogate measure of underlying brain connectivity, 43 the observed results of LGI reductions thus may signify varying degrees of brain dysconnectivity in the three subtypes, in keeping with a previous study showing similar patterns of WM microstructural abnormalities as the LGI changes in the three subtypes. 17 , 18 In addition, we uncovered a negative correlation between LGI and CDR score in FTD, indicating that the gyrification reduction may contribute to the progression of dementia in FTD. Intriguingly, all three subtypes exhibited frontotemporal involvement, as evidenced by changes in the three morphologic indices. This pattern likely suggests that changes in these three morphological indices could represent distinct endophenotypic manifestations of shared neuropathological abnormalities existent in relevant frontotemporal regions of FTD.

Compared with HCs, we found a higher volume of WMH across the whole brain in the bvFTD subtype but not in the nfvPPA and svPPA subtypes. This finding is in line with previous studies and indicates that the white matter lesions of FTD are subtype‐dependent. 17 Given that the bvFTD subtype had a higher CDR score than the other two subtypes, a higher level of white matter lesions in the bvFTD subtype may, to some extent, account for the more severe dementia in this subtype. The significant correlations between whole‐brain WMH volume and MMSE score, as well as between whole‐brain WMH volume and CDR score in FTD support this speculation. We also found significant negative correlations of whole‐brain WMH volume with the global mean values of cortical thickness, surface area, and LGI in FTD, suggesting that the subtype‐specific WM lesions may have a role in the cortical morphologic changes of the three subtypes. These findings, along with the observed mediated relationships among these variables, indicate that WMH lesions affect cognitive dysfunctions in FTD by disrupting the morphology of cortical regions connected to the affected WM tracts, whereas the morphological abnormalities further exacerbate the cognitive impairments by impacting relevant WM connections. Compared with HCs, this study also revealed decreased DTI‐ALPS in the three subtypes, indicating compromised glymphatic function in FTD. The glymphatic system plays pivotal roles in the clearance of waste products (such as tau and TDP‐43 aggregates) in the brain and has been associated with MMSE scores in neurodegenerative diseases. 28 , 44 Presumably, the compromised glymphatic function may have contributed to the dementia across the three subtypes. This speculation is partially supported by the results of the lowest DTI‐ALPS and the highest CDR score in the bvFTD subtype. The speculation, however, is at odds with our findings of the lowest CDR score (i.e., least severe dementia) but comparable DTI‐ALPS in the nfvPPA subtype compared to the bvFTD subtype. This suggests that additional pathological factors, such as WMH (being least severe in the nfvPPA subtype), may have a greater impact on the varying severity of dementia across the three subtypes. In addition, lower DTI‐ALPS was associated with lower cortical thickness in patients with FTD. This finding may indicate that the dysfunctional glymphatic system cannot effectively eliminate the continuous accumulation of toxic tau and/or TDP‐43 protein, which consequently may lead to severe neuronal losses, manifesting macroscopically as cortical thinning in FTD.

Neuroimaging‐transcriptional association analysis on regional cortical morphological deviances among the three subtypes has uncovered essential biological processes/pathways highly relevant to FTD pathologies. On the one hand, we identified a series of biological processes vital for the formation of TDP‐43 and/or tau aggregates and their intracellular transport. Specifically, the “protein phosphorylation” process, as a ubiquitous post‐translational modification of TDP‐43 and tau, could affect their activity, stability and aggregation propensity, 45 while the “protein localization to plasma membrane” process may have a role in the cytoplasmic mislocalization and accumulation of the predominately nuclear RNA‐binding TDP‐43. 46 , 47 Pathological changes as such can be exacerbated by disrupted cellular homeostasis that arises from a cascade of metabolic and inflammatory events. Indeed, disruptions in “oxidative phosphorylation” and “fatty acid metabolism” may elevate the level of reactive oxygen species (i.e., oxidative stress), 48 which likely triggers the “MAPK pathway” and initiates “leukocyte activation involved in immune response” process, culminating in a state of neuroinflammation. 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 Such a pro‐inflammatory environment may further aggravate cytoplasmic accumulation of TDP‐43 or tau protein aggregates, which appears to interact with processes such as “axonogenesis” and “actin filament organization”, enabling their intracellular transport along axons or cellular cytoskeletal structures. 53 , 54 On the other hand, we also identified several biological processes that support intercellular communication or facilitate the exchange of biological information, such as “vesicle‐mediated transport” and “endocytosis or exocytosis”. These processes are crucial for moving macromolecules, such as proteins, from within cells to the extracellular space, and disruptions in these processes might hasten the entry of TDP‐43 and tau aggregates into the extracellular environment. 55 , 56 , 57 Such detrimental effects could be amplified by the inefficient glymphatic waste clearance, 58 resulting in heightened extracellular accumulation and facilitating the intercellular transmission of TDP‐43 and/or tau pathologies. Altogether, these findings indicate that the cortical morphological changes observed in various FTD subtypes might represent macroscopic manifestations of disturbed biological processes or pathways that play a critical role in the development and spread of TDP‐43 and/or tau pathologies.

Some limitations should be mentioned for this study. First, the uneven sample sizes of the three patient groups may potentially affect the results of the between‐group contrasts. Second, there was a significant imbalance in the sex distribution among the three groups. Thus, sex was incorporated as a covariate of no interest to alleviate its impact on the inter‐group contrasts. Third, given that there was no significant correlation between DTI‐ALPS and clinical scores in these patients, the interpretations of the relevant results in this study are somewhat speculative and, therefore, should be taken cautiously. Fourth, the gene expression data were acquired from six post mortem donor brains, whereas the brain structural MRI data were obtained from 223 living participants. Such inconsistency may obscure gene expression‐cortical morphology associations with small effect sizes. Fifth, the present study was carried out cross‐sectionally. Future longitudinal studies are needed to explore the dynamic profiles of the morphologic abnormalities, WMH, and glymphatic dysfunction.

In conclusion, the present study revealed subtype‐specific changes in cortical morphologic indices, WMH and DTI‐ALPS, as well as significant associations among them in FTD. Our findings insinuate differential pathophysiology underwriting the distinct symptom profiles in the three FTD subtypes and may contribute potential pathways for the development of personalized treatment strategies for this disorder.
