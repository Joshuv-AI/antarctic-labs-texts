Eco-Corona of Microplastics in Soil Inhibits Their Role as a Contaminant Vector While Enhancing Their Role as a Microorganism Vector
Authors: Shi Yao, Tao Wang, Qicheng Bei, Xiangwei Wu, Xin Jiang, Yongming Luo, Yang Song, Scott X. Chang, Hans Peter H. Arp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics are an environmental vector for both chemical contaminants and microorganisms. Key to this role is the formation of an eco-corona on microplastic surfaces, which influences surface interactions with the surrounding environment. To better understand this, we investigated the composition and formation mechanism of the eco-corona on the surface of microplastics from soil metabolites, and how this affected the sorption of organic contaminants and the colonization of early biofilms. Soil metabolites formed an eco-corona on microplastics primarily through van der Waals interactions, resulting in hard and soft eco-corona layers via multilayer adsorption. The eco-corona not only acted as a barrier, partially preventing exogenous organic contaminants sorbing onto microplastics, but also functioned as a novel sorption layer for organic contaminants. Through this combined effect, the eco-corona reduced organic contaminant sorption by 14.4–19.8%. Additionally, the eco-corona accelerated and enhanced the biotic vector role of microplastics by regulating their initial microbial colonization, thereby enhancing deterministic processes in community assembly. Our findings demonstrate that the eco-corona, a surficial component of microplastics, serves as a “second surface” in governing the environmental behavior of microplastics, necessitating its incorporation in risk assessments of microplastics in terrestrial environments.

Introduction

Microplastics are accumulating in terrestrial ecosystems, particularly in soils, at an increasing rate. , The persistence of microplastics in soils has spurred investigations into their potential impacts on ecosystem processes and soil microbial communities. , In particular, the interactions between increasing microplastics and other factors of global change, such as biodiversity loss, climate change, and other forms of pollution, need to be studied to fully understand the ecological consequences of plastic pollution. , Far from being inert particles in the soil, microplastics actively participate in soil ecosystems by serving as dual vectors for both chemical contaminants and microorganisms, through their unique capacity to adsorb diverse environmental constituents. This adsorption behavior facilitates the formation of a dynamic and ecologically complex interface that emerges between microplastics and the surrounding environment. These interface interactions would exacerbate the environmental risk of complex contamination, mediate soil microbe-derived carbon and nitrogen cycling, and result in substantial uncertainties regarding the ecological risks of microplastics. −

Generally, interactions between microplastics and contaminants/microorganisms depend on the surface properties of microplastics. Herein, a key yet frequently overlooked driver of these interfacial interactions is the formation of eco-corona on microplastics. The eco-corona is the first layer of biomolecular compounds, including metabolites, proteins, and extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), etc., that form on microplastics after their environmental exposure. , The formation of the eco-corona alters the surface properties and confers biological identity to microplastics, thereby mediating their subsequent interactions with contaminants and microorganisms. , For instance, it was found that an eco-corona composed of humic acid can enhance the binding of hydrophilic contaminants, such as 4-nonylphenol, on polystyrene (PS) nanoplastics. The eco-corona formed on polyethylene (PE) mulch microplastic surfaces with soil metabolites inhibited the sorption of hydrophobic contaminants such as dibutyl phthalate (DBP). Notably, the eco-corona formation and its interaction with contaminants occur simultaneously on microplastic surfaces. The eco-corona consists of both so-called “soft” and “hard” fractions, differentiated by the affinity and stability of the adsorbed molecules. The soft eco-corona comprises loosely bound biomolecules that undergo dynamic and reversible exchange, − while the hard eco-corona is characterized by tightly adhered, stable biomolecules resistant to detachment. − Although the sorption of contaminants to microplastics has been extensively studied, the processes and mechanisms of interaction between distinct fractions in the eco-corona and organic contaminants remain unclear.

In addition to the influence of the eco-corona on chemical contaminants, it can also influence the biotic vector role of microplastics by shaping microbial colonization. Microplastics’ surfaces provide unique niches for diverse microorganisms that ultimately form biofilms. , Although surface roughness and hydrophobicity are known physicochemical factors influencing microbial attachment, , the eco-corona likely acts as a key driver governing the microbial assembly on microplastics. , For example, the eco-corona may recruit the pioneer colonizing microorganisms by providing labile organic substrates from its biomolecular constituents, as well as altering the surface properties of the microplastics. These processes mediated by the eco-corona may function as a form of environmental filtering that enhances the deterministic assembly of microbial communities on the biofilm; however, this has currently not been explored. A matured eco-corona may drive predictable biofilm development, which is essential for understanding the interfacial transformation of microplastics in the environment.

This study explored two hypotheses to elucidate how eco-corona can impact the vector roles of microplastics in soil and thus bridge the research gaps: (i) the eco-corona, especially the hard eco-corona, may become a new surface for microplastics and influence the role of microplastics to act as a vector for organic contaminants; and (ii) the eco-corona creates interfacial hotspots for initial colonization of microbes and therefore shifts the assembly of microbes on microplastics. To test the above hypotheses, we selected white polyethylene film microplastics (PEwhite‑film) and pure polyethylene microplastic granules (PEpure‑granule) as representatives of the most prevalent microplastics found in agricultural soils, due to practices such as plastic mulching. Soil metabolomes, the most bioactive small-molecule metabolites in the soil, were selected as the source components for the eco-corona. The composition and formation mechanism of the hard and soft eco-corona on microplastics were identified by nontargeted metabolomics, molecular dynamic simulations, and multiple spectroscopic techniques. To investigate the effect of eco-corona components on the adsorption of contaminants, fomesafen, a broadly used herbicide, and DBP, a widely used plasticizer that often ends up in soil, were selected as proxy soil contaminants. Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM), flow cytometry, high-throughput and metagenomic sequencing techniques were used to determine the effects of eco-corona on the formation and community assembly of biofilms on the surface of PE microplastics. This investigation was done to understand the mechanisms and impacts of the eco-corona’s mediation on both the fate of contaminants and microbes, to holistically assess their eco-environmental risks.

Materials and Methods

Soils and Reagents

Farmland soil samples were collected from three distinct regions in Jiamusi in Heilongjiang Province (47°30′ N, 134°13′ E), Xinxiang in Henan Province (35°00′ N, 114°24′ E), and Yingtan in Jiangxi Province (28°15′ N, 116°55′ E), China. These soils were classified as Mollisol (M), Fluvo-aquic (F), and Red soil (R), respectively, according to the Chinese soil taxonomy. All soil samples were immediately transported to the laboratory for storage and processing. A portion of the soil samples was air-dried and sieved through a 2 mm mesh to determine their physicochemical properties. The remaining soil was stored at −80 °C until further analysis. White PE film was obtained from Shandong Minfeng Plastic Film Company (China); this plastic is designated as PEwhite‑film. Pure PE granules, used for plastic product manufacturing, were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (China), and this plastic is designated as PEpure‑granules. PE microplastics were prepared through a series of steps, including freezing, milling, and sieving, to achieve a particle size range of 250–600 μm (CryMill 6870D, China). Fomesafen (purity ≥99%, CAS 72178-02-0), LC–MS grade water, and methanol (chromatographic purity) were procured from Ehrenstorfer GmbH (Germany) and Accustandard Inc. (USA), respectively. Sodium azide (NaN3) was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (China).

Polyethylene Microplastics Encapsulated with Eco-Corona

Soil metabolites were extracted from ethanol-free chloroform-fumigated soil using ultrapure water as the extraction solution. Eco-corona encapsulated microplastics were prepared from soil metabolites derived from the three distinct soil types: Mollisol, Fluvo-aquic, and Red soil. For each experiment, 10 mg of PEwhite‑film or PEpure‑granules was weighed and transferred into an amber glass vial containing 8 mL of the respective soil metabolite solution. The microplastic concentration (equivalent to 2.4 × 104 items kg–1 dry soil) was selected based on reported levels in polluted agricultural soil hotspots. , The vials were then placed in a rotary oscillator and incubated at 25 °C for 72 h in the dark. After incubation, the samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, and the eco-corona encapsulated microplastics were collected, freeze-dried, and stored at −20 °C (the experimental procedures of this study are shown in Figure S1, Supporting Information).

Characterization of the Microplastics

The surface morphology of the microplastics was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU8010). The surface elemental composition was determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Escalab 250 Xi) using a monochromatic Al source (1486.6 eV). Raman spectra of the microplastics’ surface were measured by confocal Raman microscopy (Renishaw inVia). Additionally, the zeta potential and static contact angle were measured by a particle size analyzer (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90) and a drop shape analyzer (DSA100S, Krüss), respectively.

Analysis of the Hard and Soft Eco-Corona

The eco-corona fractions of PE microplastics were extracted using a two-step extraction method involving oscillation and ultrasonication, with a 1:1 (v/v) water–methanol mixture serving as the extractant. According to the operational definition, the fraction extracted by oscillation was designated as the soft eco-corona, while the fraction extracted by ultrasonication was designated as the hard eco-corona. Briefly, 10 mg of PE microplastics, either with or without eco-corona, were added to 8 mL amber glass vials containing 8 mL of the extractant. The microplastics were first subjected to oscillation for 48 h. After oscillation, the solution and particles were separated, and the supernatant was collected as the soft eco-corona fraction. Subsequently, the remaining PE microplastics were extracted again by ultrasonication for 30 min. The resulting solution was separated and designated as the hard eco-corona fraction. Each treatment was replicated in triplicate. For further analysis, 4 mL of each extracted solution was freeze-dried. The dried samples were then redissolved in 120 μL of a 1:1 (v/v) acetonitrile: water solution, followed by low-temperature ultrasonication (5 °C, 40 kHz) for 5 min. The samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 13,000g for 5 min. Finally, the supernatant was collected and transferred to vials for untargeted metabolomics analysis (Text S1).

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Based on the molecular composition of soft and hard eco-coronas, eight metabolites were selected to investigate the eco-corona formation process on PE microplastics through molecular dynamics simulation. The selected metabolites represented diverse chemical classes, including: organooxygen compounds (raffinose), benzenoids (acetyl vitamin K5), organonitrogen compounds (stearoylethanolamide), benzopyrans (rhodamine b), glycerophospholipids (lysope (18:1­(9z)/0:0)), carbonyl compounds (maltotriose), purine nucleosides (adenosine), and carboxylic acids (l-glutamic acid). These metabolites were selected according to the following criteria: they (i) were metabolites from eco-corona that are common to at least two soil types; (ii) belonged to different classes of compounds; (iii) were among the top 20 metabolites in abundance of eco-corona components in each soil; (iv) have existing Chemical Abstracts Service (CAS) registry numbers. These metabolites were subsequently categorized into soft and hard eco-corona components. The PE polymer chains (n = 300) were constructed using Materials Studio. The surface charges of the PE polymer and the 8 molecules were calculated employing the 6-311G­(d,p) basis set in Gaussian 16. All molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2021 with the OPLS-AA for atomic parametrization (Text S2).

Influence of the Eco-Corona on the Sorption of Organic Contaminants

The adsorption of low-hydrophobic fomesafen (K ow = 2.38) onto microplastics in soil metabolites, and in water (containing 0.02% NaN3 as a biocide and 0.01 mol L–1 CaCl2 to support the ionic strength) was investigated. In the sorption kinetic experiments, 0.4 mL of 100 mg L–1 fomesafen standard solution was added to 19.6 mL of background solution to obtain a fomesafen concentration of 2 mg L–1 in the vials. Then, 10 mg of microplastics (i.e., PEwhite‑film or PEpure‑granule) was added to each vial. The vials were oscillated in a rotating oscillator at 50 revolutions min–1 at 25 °C under dark conditions. Samples were collected destructively at predetermined time intervals (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h) for analysis. In the sorption isotherm experiments, fomesafen concentrations were set at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 mg L–1, respectively. To assess the influence of eco-corona on fomesafen sorption onto microplastics, eco-corona encapsulated microplastics were used in the sorption experiment, with water (containing 0.02% NaN3 and 0.01 mol L–1 CaCl2) as the background solution, and fomesafen concentrations were set at 2, 5, and 10 mg L–1. Following equilibration, samples were centrifuged at 10,000g for 3 min, and the supernatant was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) for fomesafen quantification (Text S3). The data on the influence of the eco-corona on the sorption of highly hydrophobic DBP (K ow = 4.27) was cited from our previous study. For molecular dynamics simulations, the interactions between PE microplastics and two contaminants (DBP and fomesafen), along with eight selected metabolites mentioned above, were investigated. The PE polymer construction and simulation parameters were identical to those described in the previous section.

Influence of Eco-Corona on Biofilm Colonization on Microplastics’ Surfaces

A mesh bag incubation method was used to conduct the biofilm formation experiment on PE microplastics in soil. All nylon mesh bags (pore size 150 μm) were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 100 mg of PE microplastics, encapsulated with or without eco-corona, were weighed into the sterile mesh bags. The soil solution was prepared in 1 L amber glass vials at a soil-to-water ratio of 1:5 (w/v), using 100 g of soil and 500 mL of ultrapure water. Five bags of PE microplastics were placed in each vial of soil solution, and they were incubated at 25 °C on a 150 revolutions min–1 oscillator for 35 days. Each treatment was replicated in triplicate. Mesh bag samples were dynamically collected destructively on days 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35. Following collection, each mesh bag was rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution, and the PE microplastics in the bags were collected. Some of the collected PE microplastics were used for flow cytometry, SEM, CLSM, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis for characterizing the biofilm (Text S4). The remaining PE microplastics were stored at −80 °C until all samples were collected and analyzed by 16S rRNA (Text S5) and metagenomics sequencing (Text S6).

Quality Control and Data Analysis

The methodology for analysis of the metabolomics raw data is presented in Text S7. Orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed for statistical analysis to identify global metabolic differences between comparable groups. Variable importance in the projection (VIP) was calculated in OPLS-DA model. The p values were estimated with a paired Student’s t-test on single-dimensional statistical analysis. Metabolites with VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 were considered to show a significant difference. Significant differences in species composition among microbiomes were analyzed using LEfse software. First, the nonparametric factorial Kruskal–Wallis test was used to detect species with significant differences in abundance between subgroups. Then, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to determine between-groups variability. Finally, the effect size of species with significant differences was assessed by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) with a default filter value of 2. Unigenes were compared in the KEGG and eggNOG databases using Diamond software for metagenomic analysis. All statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted using the vegan, dplyr, ggcor, ggplot2 packages of R Studio (version 4.0.2), and Origin Pro (version 8.5). The co-occurrence network analyses were executed in Cytoscape (version 3.10.3) and visualized by Gephi (version 0.9.2). Spearman’s correlations with p < 0.05 and r > 0.8 were included in the network analyses.

To determine the potential importance of stochastic processes in community assembly, the neutral community model (NCM) was adopted. The calculation and visualizations were performed in R Studio using the devtools, ggplot2, ggimage, and plyr packages. Additionally, the normalized stochasticity ratio (NST) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was employed to quantify the relative importance of deterministic versus stochastic processes. An NST value below 50% indicates that deterministic processes dominate, while a value above 50% indicates that stochastic processes dominate. This calculation was performed using the “nst” package in R Studio. The phylogenetic bin-based null model analysis (iCAMP) was used to quantify ecological processes, including homogeneous selection, heterogeneous selection, dispersal limitation, homogenizing dispersal, drift and others.

Results

Composition and Formation of the Eco-Corona on Microplastics

Several lines of evidence confirm the formation of an eco-corona on the surface of microplastics. First, the surface wrinkles of microplastics changed after interaction with soil metabolites (Figure S2). Second, the presence of soil metabolites induced a notable increase in N content and specific peak intensities on the surface of microplastics (Figure S3A,B). Third, the static contact angle decreased on PE microplastics (Figure S4), while the zeta potential of the microplastics’ surface increased after interacting with soil metabolites, particularly with PEpure‑granule (Figure S5). Through untargeted metabolomics, we identified 544 compounds comprising the extractable eco-corona on microplastics. The composition varied significantly between PE microplastic types and across different soil environments (Figure A). The soft eco-corona was predominantly composed of lipids and lipid-like, benzenoids, nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogues, organoheterocyclic, organic acids, and organooxygen compounds. The hard eco-corona exhibited greater complexity, containing additional classes of phenylpropanoids and polyketides, as well as soil-specific hydrocarbons and organohalogen compounds (Figure A). Notably, even within shared compound classes, specific metabolite profiles differed substantially between soft and hard eco-corona fractions. Comparative analysis revealed a greater similarity in hard eco-corona composition across soil types than in soft eco-corona (Figure S6), suggesting that the composition of the hard eco-corona is more influenced by the surface properties of microplastics than by soil type.

To elucidate the eco-corona formation mechanism, we performed molecular dynamics simulations on eight representative metabolites from soft and hard eco-corona components. The metabolites spontaneously migrated and clustered around PE surfaces, forming stable interfacial structures (Figure B). Heterogeneous aggregation occurred through mutual interactions between different metabolites and PE microplastics. Hard eco-corona components (stearoylethanolamide, lysope (18:1­(9z)/0:0), rhodamine b) showed direct surface adsorption, whereas soft eco-corona components formed peripheral distributions around the microplastics (Figure S7). Both van der Waals and electrostatic forces facilitated the adsorption of metabolites onto PE microplastics, with van der Waals interactions dominating, especially for hydrophobic, high-molecular-weight, hard eco-corona components (Figure C and Table S1). It could be observed that the solvent accessible surface area (SASA) and radius of gyration (R g) value of stearoylethanolamide, a hard eco-corona component, were the lowest among eight metabolites (Figure S8), thereby rendering the hard eco-corona more capable of spontaneously adsorbing onto the surface of PE microplastics to form stable molecular structures.

Eco-Corona Modulates the Abiotic Vector Role of Microplastics

The eco-corona reduced adsorption of both fomesafen (19.8% inhibition) and DBP (14.4% inhibition) on PE microplastics (Figure A). It was noteworthy that the eco-corona plays a dual role in regulating microplastics’ capacity to adsorb organic contaminants. The first role is that the eco-corona components outcompete contaminants for surface adsorption sites on PE microplastics. Van der Waals interactions dominated contaminants’ adsorption (Table S2), and the binding energy of PE with certain hard eco-corona components, e.g., stearoylethanolamide, was significantly higher than that with tested contaminants (Figure C and Table S2). Of these two tested contaminants, DBP exhibited a stronger aggregation affinity on PE microplastics than fomesafen (Figure S9). We also confirmed that soil metabolites in solution could reduce the adsorption of organic contaminants on microplastics via enhancing their cosolubility; for example, the combined effect of soil metabolites and the eco-corona can inhibit the adsorption of fomesafen by 61.9% (Figures B, S10, and S11). The second role of the eco-corona in adsorption is that the eco-corona acts as an alternative sorption layer. Both fomesafen and DBP partitioned into the eco-corona matrix, forming microplastic-eco-corona-contaminant complexes (Figure C). The average total binding energy of contaminants with hard eco-corona components was higher than that of those with soft eco-corona components (Figure D and Table S2). The DBP bound more strongly to pristine PE (−317.79 kJ mol–1) than to eco-corona components (−155.66 to −245.62 kJ mol–1). In contrast, fomesafen, a compound with low hydrophobicity, exhibited higher affinity for the eco-corona (−194.26 to −225.27 kJ mol–1) than for pristine PE (−152.30 kJ mol–1) (Figure D and Table S2).

Eco-Corona Facilitates the Early Formation of Biofilm on Microplastics

To validate the second hypothesis regarding the biotic vector role of microplastics, we investigated biofilm development on PE microplastics with and without an eco-corona. The microbial communities observed on the surface of PE microplastics displayed distinct morphological preferences. Filamentous microorganisms preferentially colonized the PEwhite‑film surface, while spherical and rod-shaped microorganisms dominated the PEpure‑granule surface (Figures S12–S14). The formation of biofilm was also tracked by the emergence of a new absorption peak at 1045 cm–1 (C–O bonding in polysaccharides) in the FTIR spectrum (Figure S15). The CLSM images of the biofilm, which included live cells, dead cells, and their EPS, exhibited an overall succession pattern in which live cells gradually transformed into EPS and dead cells (Figures S16–S18). The presence of the eco-corona resulted in higher overall biofilm and EPS coverage on microplastics after 7 days of incubation (Figures A and S19), signifying that the eco-corona contributes to the development of early biofilms on microplastics’ surface. The volume of cells in the biofilm quantified by flow cytometry also confirmed that the cell volume on the PEwhite‑film surface increased significantly after 7 days of incubation when the eco-corona was present, especially in the Mollisol and Fluvo-aquic soils (p < 0.05), increasing by 18.5–43.5% (Figures B and S20).

The 16S rRNA sequencing data revealed that the predominant bacterial phyla on the surface of PE microplastics were Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes, comprising more than 50% of the total microbial community (Figure S21). Compared with PEpure‑granule, the PEwhite‑film showed greater responsiveness to the eco-corona. The eco-corona enhanced both the richness and diversity of microorganisms on the PEwhite‑film surface during early colonization (day 7) (Figures B and S22). The eco-corona significantly altered the microbial community composition at the genus level, enriching microbes such as Flavobacterium, Conexibacter, Phenylobacterium, Bradyrhizobium, Mesorhizobium, Caulobacter, Roseiarcus, Devosia, etc., while suppressing the microbes such as Pullulanibacillus, Mycobacterium, Marmoricola (Figure S23). The eco-corona exerted time-dependent effects on microbial functions. During the early stages of biofilm formation, the eco-corona demonstrated a noticeable influence on the performance of certain ecological functions associated with biofilm colonization (Figure C), including lipid metabolism, energy metabolism, glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, cell motility, membrane transport, and signal transduction. During the subsequent stages of biofilm colonization, however, differences in microbial function diminished, indicating that the primary eco-corona influence occurred during the initial stages of biofilm colonization (Figure D).

Eco-Corona Enhances the Determinacy of the Microbial Community Assembly on Microplastics

We further analyzed the assembly process of microbial communities to comprehensively investigate the effect of the eco-corona. After 35 days of incubation, the NST indicated that the biofilm formation process on the microplastics’ surface is influenced by stochastic processes (NST > 50%). However, the eco-corona reduced the stochasticity of the biofilm formation process (Figure A,B). Based on iCAMP analysis, homogeneous selection and dispersal limits were more important than other processes in biofilm community assembly (Figure S24). It is noteworthy that the presence of the eco-corona decreased the relative importance of dispersal limits and increased homogeneous selection. Additionally, the NCM provided a better fit for the microbial assembly process on microplastics (R 2 = 0.83) than for the eco-corona encapsulated microplastics (R 2 = 0.80) (Figure C,D). These results collectively indicated that the eco-corona enhances the determinacy of the biofilm formation process. The co-occurrence network analysis indicated that microbes involved in early biofilm development were more closely correlated with the hard eco-corona than with the soft eco-corona components (Figure S25).

A novel finding from this study is that the microplastics’ surface biofilm transitioned from a deterministic to stochastic assembly processes over 35 days (Figure B). In the early stage of biofilm colonization (Day 7), biofilm assembly on the surface of microplastics was primarily driven by deterministic processes, and the eco-corona significantly enhanced this effect, increasing the relative importance of homogeneous selection (Figure E). As incubation time increased, the relative importance of dispersal-limited processes in biofilm assembly increased, and the stochasticity of biofilm assembly increased accordingly. Meanwhile, the presence of the eco-corona decreased microbial mobility (m-value), with lower m-values indicating greater limitations on microbial community dispersal, across different stages of biofilm formation (Figure S26). Further, the co-occurrence network of microorganisms exhibited an increase in the degree, clustering coefficient, and positive correlations during the development of early biofilms on eco-corona encapsulated microplastics (day 7 of the incubation), especially in Mollisol and Fluvo-aquic soils, suggesting that the eco-corona can increase synergistic interactions of microorganisms on microplastics’ surface (Figure S27 and Table S3).

Discussion and Environmental Implications

Microplastics emitted into the environment undergo surface modification through adsorption of compounds such as soil metabolites, leading to the formation of both hard and soft eco-coronas via heterogeneous aggregation and multilayer adsorption processes. − Our study found that van der Waals interactions serve as the primary driving force for the adsorption of metabolites on PE microplastics. The eco-corona substantially alters the physicochemical surface properties of microplastics, including hydrophobicity, functional group composition, and charge distribution. Most importantly, our findings demonstrate that the eco-corona profoundly influences the roles of microplastics as vectors for abiotic and biotic entities, such as organic contaminants and microorganisms, respectively. These results advance our understanding of microplastic–environment interactions by highlighting the eco-corona as the critical interface that influences the behavior of microplastics under realistic environmental conditions.

Our results support the first hypothesis that the eco-corona, especially the hard eco-corona, alters the surface sufficiently to have a quantifiable impact on the abiotic vector role of PE microplastics in the transport of organic contaminants. Based on the results of adsorption and molecular dynamics simulations, the impact of eco-corona on the adsorption of organic contaminants on PE microplastics involves three pathways: (1) soft/hard eco-corona components compete with organic contaminants for adsorption sites on the surface of the microplastics; (2) soft/hard eco-corona components sorption reduces hydrophobicity of PE microplastics’ surface; and (3) the eco-corona components, especially the hard eco-corona, combine with organic contaminants (Figure ). Although the formation of eco-corona significantly reduced the total adsorption of organic contaminants on microplastics, we found a novel result: both the two tested contaminants, fomesafen and DBP, could be combined within the eco-corona, especially in the hard eco-corona fractions, forming molecular clusters. For hydrophilic contaminants such as fomesafen, its combination with hard eco-corona components is stronger than that with PE, meaning that the eco-corona could act as a novel sorption layer and thus capture a part of fomesafen that was less readily desorbed. This is expected to increase the probability that contaminants or their metabolites are internalized in cells through internal exposure carried by eco-corona-encapsulated microplastics. Therefore, the dual role of eco-corona, i.e., simultaneously inhibiting bulk adsorption while creating a new binding compartment, presents a complex exposure scenario regarding the abiotic vector role of microplastics. These findings highlight the need for a comprehensive toxicological evaluation of microplastic-contaminant complexes that account for eco-corona mediated effects on bioavailability, internal exposure, and toxicity pathways to terrestrial organisms. It is worth noting that, as this study focuses on PE microplastics of a single particle size, future research should also consider the effects of different types of microplastics and their particle sizes.

Our findings also support the second hypothesis, demonstrating that eco-corona creates interfacial hotspots for initial colonization of microbes that shifts the assembly of microbes on microplastics (Figure ). Lipids and lipid-like, organic acids, organic oxygen compounds, nucleosides, and nucleotide compounds within the eco-corona serve as key signaling molecules that regulate interactions between microplastics and microbial communities, while also being the most readily available growth substrates for soil microorganisms. , In the early stage of biofilm colonization (e.g., Day 7), the eco-corona serves as a growth substrate and physicochemical modifier, increasing the cell volume within the biofilm and accelerating initial biofilm formation on microplastic surfaces. Biofilms are mainly colonized by substrate utilization; , thus, the influence of the eco-corona tends to be more prominent when biofilms start to form. We observed a characteristic enrichment of Flavobacterium, Phenylobacterium, Roseiarcus, and Devosia on eco-corona encapsulated microplastics, with Phenylobacterium, Roseiarcus, Devosia, members of the Alphaproteobacteria, serving as pioneer colonizersa phenomenon previously documented in the marine environment and now confirmed in a soil system. The Flavobacterium is able to degrade complex carbohydrates, and its early attachment is ascribed to the utilization of the carbonyl compounds within the eco-corona. Additionally, the physicochemical properties of microplastics also affect the microbial colonization on the surface of microplastics. , The eco-corona increases the hydrophilicity of the PE microplastics, which may also promote the early colonization of biofilms on their surfaces.

Regarding microbial community assembly, it was recognized that a higher proportion of stochastic processes than deterministic processes influences the assembly of microbial communities on the surfaces of microplastics in an urban river, a natural coastal watershed, and soil, respectively. − While stochastic processes dominated overall community assembly across the 35 days of incubation, we identified a critical early phase (before Day 7) window where deterministic selection prevailed. During this phase, the eco-corona increases the relative importance of the homogeneous selection of microorganisms on microplastics, suggesting that it actively attracts certain microbial communities on microplastics’ surface. As noted above, the lipids and lipid-like, organic acids, nucleosides, and nucleotide compounds within the eco-corona can provide available growth substrates for microorganisms. Simultaneously, the eco-corona alters the surface properties of microplastics. Together, these effects act as an environmental filter, which increases homogeneous selection of microorganisms on the microplastic surface, thereby enhancing the deterministic process of community assembly on microplastics. Further consideration, therefore, needs to be given to the influence of substances coexisting with microplastics, e.g., eco-corona, on their biological effects in the ecosystem.

The eco-corona was found not only on PE microplastics in soil but also on polypropylene, PS, and polyethylene terephthalate in natural water, suggesting that different microplastics could form eco-coronas in diverse environments. The formation of the eco-corona on microplastics represents a ubiquitous and environmentally significant process that fundamentally transforms particle surfaces by altering the surficial properties. This can be considered a “second surface” of the microplastics. Critically, the eco-corona comprises bioactive molecules that mediate biological interactions by facilitating cell-to-particle signaling, enhancing cellular internalization of microplastics, and promoting trophic transfer across biological systems. ,, These surface modifications can impact the risk of microplastic toxicity to soil biota, as the eco-corona could mask the inherent toxicity. The strong combination of hydrophilic organic contaminants with microplastics’ eco-corona undoubtedly impacts their complex risks beyond conventional microplastic-contaminant interactions. This “second surface” also contains more abundant and diverse substrates than the pristine polymer surface, facilitating early microbial colonization. The subsequent formation of biofilm would greatly alter the surficial properties and environmental behavior of microplastics in the soil, shaping the plastisphere (Figure S28), − and also serve as a potential center for carbon cycling. , This study clarifies the eco-corona’s role in mediating both the abiotic and biotic vector effects of microplastics, which should be accounted for in how we assess and regulate microplastic pollution. This shift moves beyond considering pristine particles in terrestrial environments, but rather as eco-corona covered particles with surface properties that bare some similarities with each other but are unique from those of pure polymers. And we should also recognize that the formation of eco-corona, biofilm and plastisphere will be in a dynamic process when the soil conditions changed, such as the release of plant root exudates. The complexity and dynamics of surficial properties of microplastics in natural filed should be studied in future.
