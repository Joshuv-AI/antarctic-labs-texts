Timing and clinical burden of febrile neutropenia and treatment discontinuation during neoadjuvant chemotherapy
Authors: Kei Nakada, Koji Takada, Ayako Gose, Mai Nishimoto, Mariko Nishikawa, Asuka Kochi, Chika Watanabe, Yukie Tauchi, Kana Ogisawa, Haruhito Kinoshita, Masatsune Shibutani, Tamami Morisaki, Shinichiro Kashiwagi

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFebrile neutropenia (FN) remains a major complication of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) and is a frequent cause of treatment disruption. However, FN is a heterogeneous clinical entity, and the impact of its timing and clinical severity on treatment continuity has not been fully elucidated. This study aimed to characterize the timing and clinical burden of FN and to clarify their associations with treatment discontinuation during NAC.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 137 consecutive patients with HER2-positive breast cancer who received NAC. FN timing was assessed by chemotherapy cycle and days from NAC initiation, with early FN defined a priori as FN occurring at cycle ≤ 2. FN severity was evaluated based on the requirement for hospitalization. Treatment discontinuation was defined as premature discontinuation of NAC due to chemotherapy-related adverse events; dose reductions or treatment delays without permanent discontinuation were not included in this endpoint. Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with treatment discontinuation.ResultsFN occurred in 18 patients (13.1%). The median time to FN onset was 46 days (interquartile range, 19–112), with half of FN events occurring by cycle 2. Although FN events were evenly distributed across chemotherapy cycles, cumulative incidence analysis revealed early clustering on a time-based scale. Hospitalization was required in 50.0% of patients with FN. Treatment discontinuation occurred in 8.4% of patients without FN, 11.1% of patients with non-hospitalized FN, and 44.4% of patients with hospitalized FN. Exploratory multivariable analyses demonstrated that hospitalized FN was associated with treatment discontinuation after adjustment for age and host-related factors (odds ratio, 11.20-13.33 across models), whereas low body mass index and reduced renal function were not independent predictors.ConclusionsThe impact of FN on treatment continuity during NAC appears to be determined by both its timing and clinical severity. FN requiring hospitalization, particularly when occurring early during NAC, was associated with an increased likelihood of treatment discontinuation. These findings underscore the importance of early identification and prevention of severe FN to optimize treatment delivery during neoadjuvant chemotherapy, while recognizing the statistical uncertainty inherent to the limited number of events.Keywords: Febrile neutropenia, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Treatment discontinuation, Timing and severity, Hospitalization, Breast cancer

Background

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) has become an integral component of modern breast cancer management, providing opportunities for tumor downstaging, in vivo assessment of treatment sensitivity, and optimization of subsequent surgical and systemic strategies [1–3]. Importantly, the clinical efficacy of NAC is highly dependent on the delivery of the planned chemotherapy regimen with adequate dose intensity and continuity [4–6]. Accumulating evidence indicates that treatment interruption or premature discontinuation during NAC adversely affects pathological response and may compromise long-term oncological outcomes, highlighting treatment completion as a clinically meaningful endpoint rather than a purely procedural measure [4–6].

Febrile neutropenia (FN) represents one of the most serious and clinically consequential toxicities associated with cytotoxic chemotherapy [7, 8]. Beyond its immediate morbidity, FN frequently necessitates dose reduction, treatment delay, or discontinuation, thereby directly threatening the continuity of NAC [4–6, 9]. Previous studies have focused primarily on identifying host-related risk factors for FN development, including low body mass index, impaired renal function, and other markers of physiological vulnerability [7, 10–13]. While such factors are useful for predicting susceptibility to FN, they do not fully explain the downstream clinical consequences once FN occurs. Notably, FN is not a uniform clinical entity. The clinical implications of FN may differ substantially depending on when it occurs during the course of chemotherapy and how severe its clinical presentation is [9, 14]. Early-onset FN may interrupt treatment before sufficient cumulative dose intensity is achieved, whereas FN requiring hospitalization reflects a degree of severity that often mandates treatment interruption regardless of prophylactic or supportive measures [8, 9, 14]. Despite these clinically intuitive distinctions, most previous analyses have treated FN as a binary event, overlooking the potential importance of its timing and clinical burden [7, 10, 11].

This gap is particularly relevant in the neoadjuvant setting, where early treatment disruption may have disproportionate consequences compared with later phases of therapy [4–6]. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether treatment discontinuation is primarily driven by host vulnerability itself or by the occurrence of clinically severe FN that overwhelms supportive care strategies [7, 8, 10, 11]. Disentangling these factors is essential for refining risk stratification and for identifying clinically actionable targets to improve treatment continuity [8, 10, 15]. In this context, we sought to characterize the timing and clinical burden of FN during neoadjuvant chemotherapy and to clarify their associations with treatment discontinuation. By focusing on when FN occurs and whether it requires hospitalization, rather than on FN occurrence alone, this study aims to provide a clinically grounded framework for understanding how FN compromises treatment delivery during NAC.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective observational study included consecutive patients with HER2-positive breast cancer who received NAC at Osaka Metropolitan University Hospital between January 2019 and March 2025. HER2 positivity was defined in accordance with the American Society of Clinical Oncology/College of American Pathologists (ASCO/CAP) guidelines applicable at the time of diagnosis [16]. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they received at least one cycle of NAC containing cytotoxic chemotherapy in combination with anti-HER2 therapy. Patients were excluded if they had received prior systemic chemotherapy for breast cancer, had incomplete clinical or laboratory data before initiation of NAC, or discontinued treatment for reasons unrelated to chemotherapy-related toxicity. After applying these criteria, a total of 137 patients were included in the final analysis. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of Osaka Metropolitan University Hospital (approval number: #2025-045). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy and supportive care

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens consisted of standard anthracycline- and/or taxane-based chemotherapy administered in combination with anti-HER2 agents, including trastuzumab with or without pertuzumab, according to institutional protocols and contemporary clinical guidelines [1–3, 17, 18]. The selection of specific chemotherapy regimens and treatment schedules was determined by the treating physicians based on tumor characteristics and patient condition. Primary prophylactic granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) at the initiation of NAC was not part of the routine institutional protocol. Therapeutic G-CSF or secondary prophylactic G-CSF was administered at the discretion of the treating physicians after the development of neutropenia or FN, or when subsequent cycles were considered to carry a clinically relevant risk of recurrence, in accordance with established clinical guidelines [8, 15, 19, 20]. In the present analysis, G-CSF use was summarized as any G-CSF administration during NAC. Because G-CSF administration was not randomized and was frequently triggered by neutropenia, FN, or physician-perceived risk, it was interpreted as a treatment process variable rather than an independent baseline covariate.

Definition and assessment of febrile neutropenia

Febrile neutropenia was defined based on standard clinical criteria for severe neutropenia accompanied by fever, consistent with established guidelines [8]. For patients who developed FN, detailed information regarding the timing and clinical burden of FN was collected. FN timing was assessed based on both the chemotherapy cycle at FN onset and the number of days from initiation of NAC to FN occurrence. Early FN was defined a priori as FN occurring during chemotherapy cycles 1 or 2. This cutoff was selected because the initial two cycles represent the early vulnerability window before individualized dose modification or secondary prophylactic strategies can be fully implemented; in the present cohort, this cutoff also corresponded to the median cycle of FN onset and captured 50.0% of FN events. FN severity was evaluated based on whether hospitalization was required for clinical management.

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was treatment discontinuation, defined as premature discontinuation of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) before completion of the planned regimen due to chemotherapy-related adverse events, including febrile neutropenia. Dose reductions or schedule delays that did not result in permanent discontinuation were not counted as treatment discontinuation. This endpoint therefore captured the most clinically consequential form of treatment delivery failure, whereas lesser modifications of treatment delivery were outside the predefined primary endpoint. Treatment discontinuation for reasons unrelated to chemotherapy toxicity was not considered treatment discontinuation in this analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median with interquartile range or range, as appropriate, and categorical variables were summarized as number and percentage. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were additionally calculated to describe the magnitude of baseline differences between patients with and without FN. To identify factors associated with treatment discontinuation, logistic regression analyses were performed. Univariable analyses were first conducted and are presented together with the multivariable analyses. Given the limited number of treatment discontinuation events, multivariable analyses were considered exploratory, and parsimonious models were constructed to avoid overfitting and collinearity. Model 1 included hospitalized FN, age, and low body mass index, whereas Model 2 included hospitalized FN, age, and reduced renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²). Early FN was not included in the multivariable models because of its overlap with hospitalized FN. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 16 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Timing of febrile neutropenia during neoadjuvant chemotherapy

The timing of FN onset is illustrated in Fig. 1 and summarized in Table 2. FN occurred at a median of 46 days (interquartile range [IQR], 19–112) from the initiation of NAC, with a median onset at chemotherapy cycle 2 (range, 1–6). The cumulative incidence curve demonstrated a steep early rise, indicating that a substantial proportion of FN events occurred early during NAC. Consistent with this observation, half of all FN events occurred by cycle 2, with early FN (cycle < = 2) accounting for 50.0% of cases. When FN events were examined by cycle group, early and late FN events were evenly distributed, with 9 events (50.0%) occurring at cycle < = 2 and 9 events (50.0%) occurring at cycle ≥ 3. These findings indicate that although FN events are evenly distributed across chemotherapy cycles, they tend to cluster early when evaluated on a time-based scale from NAC initiation.

Clinical burden of febrile neutropenia

The clinical burden of FN was substantial. Overall, hospitalization was required in 9 of 18 patients with FN (50.0%). Importantly, hospitalization was more frequently observed among patients with early FN than among those with late FN (early FN with hospitalization: 6 of 18 FN cases [33.3%] vs. late FN with hospitalization: 3 of 18 FN cases [16.7%]). Quantitatively, 6 of 9 patients with early FN (66.7%) required hospitalization, and 6 of 9 patients with hospitalized FN (66.7%) experienced FN during cycles 1 or 2. Thus, early FN and hospitalized FN overlapped in 6 of 18 FN cases (33.3%), supporting the decision not to include both variables in the same multivariable model. This distribution highlights the coexistence of early onset and increased clinical severity in a subset of patients who developed FN during NAC.

Association between febrile neutropenia and treatment discontinuation

Treatment discontinuation according to FN severity is shown in Fig. 2. Treatment discontinuation occurred in 10 of 119 patients without FN (8.4%), 1 of 9 patients with FN who did not require hospitalization (11.1%), and 4 of 9 patients with FN requiring hospitalization (44.4%). Thus, treatment discontinuation was higher in patients who experienced hospitalized FN. Further stratification by FN timing and severity demonstrated that treatment discontinuation was most frequent among patients with early FN requiring hospitalization. In contrast, no treatment discontinuation was observed among patients with late FN who did not require hospitalization. These findings suggest that the combination of early onset and severe FN is particularly disruptive to the continuation of NAC.

Factors associated with treatment discontinuation

To identify factors associated with treatment discontinuation, logistic regression analyses were performed (Tables 3 and 4). In univariable analyses, hospitalized FN and increasing age were associated with a higher risk of treatment discontinuation. In multivariable analyses, hospitalized FN remained associated with treatment discontinuation after adjustment for age and low BMI (Model 1: OR, 13.33; 95% CI, 2.23–79.62; P = 0.005). A similar association was observed when low BMI was replaced with reduced renal function (eGFR < 60) in an alternative multivariable model (Model 2: OR, 11.20; 95% CI, 2.07–60.76; P = 0.005). In contrast, low BMI and reduced renal function were not independently associated with treatment discontinuation in the multivariable models. Because only 15 treatment discontinuation events were observed, these effect estimates should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in view of the wide confidence intervals. Overall, the results suggest that while host-related factors may contribute to FN development, treatment discontinuation during NAC is more closely associated with the occurrence of FN requiring hospitalization than with baseline patient vulnerability alone.

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively examined the timing and clinical burden of FN during NAC and clarified their associations with treatment discontinuation. FN is increasingly recognized as a determinant of treatment delivery and relative dose intensity in breast cancer chemotherapy [4–6, 22, 23]. Our findings demonstrate that FN is a heterogeneous clinical phenomenon whose impact on treatment continuity is strongly influenced by when it occurs and how severe it is, with hospitalized FN being associated with an increased likelihood of treatment discontinuation.

A key observation of this study is the discrepancy between cycle-based and time-based assessments of FN onset. Although FN events were evenly distributed across chemotherapy cycles, the cumulative incidence curve revealed that FN clustered early when evaluated on a time scale from NAC initiation. This finding suggests that cycle number alone may inadequately capture the vulnerability window for FN, whereas time-based evaluation provides a more clinically relevant perspective. Early FN may disrupt chemotherapy delivery before sufficient cumulative dose intensity is achieved, thereby exerting a disproportionate effect on treatment completion.

Equally important is the role of clinical burden. Guidelines emphasize that FN requiring hospitalization represents a high-severity clinical scenario often necessitating treatment interruption [8, 15, 19, 20]. Patients with non-hospitalized FN exhibited treatment discontinuation rates comparable to those without FN, whereas nearly half of patients with hospitalized FN failed to complete NAC. This distinction highlights that the severity of FN, reflected by the need for inpatient management, may represent a critical threshold beyond which supportive care strategies may no longer preserve treatment continuity.

Notably, although low body mass index and impaired renal function were associated with FN development [7, 12, 13, 21], these host-related factors were not independently associated with treatment discontinuation after adjustment. These findings suggest a hierarchical relationship in which host vulnerability predisposes patients to FN, but treatment discontinuation is more closely linked to the occurrence of clinically severe FN [8, 9, 15]. This concept is further supported by the consistency of hospitalized FN across multiple multivariable models, although the magnitude of the estimated effects should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of outcome events and wide confidence intervals [4–6, 22, 23].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design may limit generalizability, and unmeasured confounding cannot be fully excluded. Second, only 15 treatment discontinuation events and 9 hospitalized FN events were observed, which constrained the complexity and stability of multivariable modeling. Although parsimonious models were constructed, the wide confidence intervals indicate considerable statistical uncertainty, and the present analyses should be interpreted as exploratory association analyses rather than as definitive predictive models. Third, the primary endpoint was restricted to permanent treatment discontinuation. Dose reductions or treatment delays without discontinuation were not included, and the overall clinical burden of FN may therefore have been underestimated. Fourth, G-CSF administration was performed at physician discretion and was frequently influenced by evolving clinical status, including neutropenia or perceived risk. Accordingly, residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded, and the present study was not designed to determine the causal effect of G-CSF use on FN severity or treatment continuity. Finally, detailed data on hospitalization duration and relative dose intensity were not available, precluding a more granular assessment of clinical burden.

Despite these limitations, this study offers a clinically grounded framework for understanding how FN compromises treatment delivery during NAC. By moving beyond a binary definition of FN and incorporating both timing and severity, our findings provide a rationale for future prospective studies and risk stratification strategies that focus on early and severe FN rather than on FN occurrence alone [8, 10, 15].

Conclusions

Febrile neutropenia during neoadjuvant chemotherapy is a heterogeneous event whose impact on treatment continuity appears to be influenced by both timing and clinical severity. FN requiring hospitalization, particularly when occurring early during NAC, was associated with an increased likelihood of treatment discontinuation, whereas baseline host-related factors alone did not independently predict failure to complete treatment in the present exploratory analysis. These findings highlight the importance of early identification and prevention of severe FN to optimize treatment delivery during neoadjuvant chemotherapy.
