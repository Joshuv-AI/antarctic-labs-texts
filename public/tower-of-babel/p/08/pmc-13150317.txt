Phosphate starvation induces root cell‐type‐specific transcriptional responses and alternative splicing
Authors: Mary‐Paz González‐García, Mónica Lanza, Victoria Baca‐González, Estefano Bustillo‐Avendaño, Laura Serrano‐Ron, Sara González‐Bodi, Jose M García‐Mina, Ángel M Zamarreño, María Garnica, Miguel A Moreno‐Risueno, Elena Caro, Juan C del Pozo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Phosphate (Pi) is an essential nutrient for plant growth, and understanding how Arabidopsis thaliana root cells respond to Pi deficiency is crucial to decipher whole plant responses.We perform high‐resolution transcriptomic profiling across five distinct root cell types, identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differential alternative splicing (DAS) events that define cell‐type‐specific Pi‐responsive regulatory layers.We identify over 7000 DEGs and 733 DAS that act largely independently and are cell‐type‐specific, affecting distinct loci and biological pathways. Transcription factors such as LHY, REV7 and MYB88, linked to circadian rhythms, stomatal regulation and ABA perception, acquire novel functions under Pi starvation. We show that the splicing factor SR45, previously associated with iron homeostasis, regulates Pi allocation. The sr45‐1 mutant displays increased root‐to‐shoot Pi ratio and reduced biomass. A substantial number of Pi‐responsive DAS transcripts are SR45 bound, indicating that SR45 operates as a regulatory hub for Pi transport and homeostasis.This cell‐type‐resolved dataset provides a detailed molecular map of Pi deficiency responses and highlights the importance of post‐transcriptional regulation in shaping root adaptive strategies. Transcriptional regulation and alternative splicing emerge as coordinated mechanisms orchestrating plant adaptation to Pi starvation. Keywords: alternative splicing, Arabidopsis root development, cell type, phosphate response, transcriptomics

Introduction

Optimal plant nutrition is essential for plant growth, development and yield. Phosphorous (P) is one of the most vital and nonreplaceable macronutrients for living organisms, including plants (Bindraban et al., 2020). Normally, natural soils contain sufficient P sources for plant growth requirements, but in many cases, this P is nonaccessible for plant uptake (Smil, 2000). Global P fertilization has increased fivefold in five decades, with a high economic and ecological cost. Unfortunately, a large part of the P applied is rapidly bound to soil, creating a low‐accessible residual P, or vanished by leaching, runoff and/or erosion, reaching and producing fresh water eutrophication (Conijn et al., 2018). P is acquired as inorganic phosphate (Pi) by plants from the rhizosphere, a small area surrounding the root system, in which local Pi depletion is rapidly generated. Pi starvation has a significant impact on root and shoot development in plants, leading to a series of adaptive responses aimed at enhancing Pi acquisition and utilization (Bouain et al., 2016; Paz‐Ares et al., 2022). It has been largely published that under Pi starvation, the elongation of the primary root is inhibited, while the development of lateral root and root hair formation is enhanced. This adaptive response increases the root surface area, allowing the plant to explore a larger soil volume for Pi acquisition (Peret et al., 2011; Gruber et al., 2013; Paz‐Ares et al., 2022; Madison et al., 2023). However, the strong inhibition of primary root growth observed in in vitro assays has been suggested to result, at least in part, from the negative effects of root illumination under laboratory conditions (Silva‐Navas et al., 2019). To overcome this limitation, and grow the roots in darkness, a novel device, called D‐Root, was developed (Silva‐Navas et al., 2015). A comparative transcriptomic analysis performed on dark‐grown root and light‐grown root seedlings under both Pi‐sufficient and Pi‐deficient conditions revealed a large set of novel genes exclusively regulated by Pi starvation in roots that were not previously identified, providing a more accurate assessment of gene expression under Pi deficiency (Silva‐Navas et al., 2019). In recent decades, numerous transcriptomic analyses have been conducted to unravel the molecular responses to Pi starvation, often using either the whole plant or the entire root system (Baek et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2018; Silva‐Navas et al., 2019; Rico‐Reséndiz et al., 2020; Satheesh et al., 2022; Scheible et al., 2023). However, an organism's response to environmental stimuli is shaped by the integrated activity of its diverse cell types. In plants, fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) has been also adapted to enrich and analyze the gene expression of distinct cell types from organs, allowing for more precise and comprehensive transcriptomic profiling (Birnbaum et al., 2003; Nawy et al., 2005; Brady et al., 2007, 2011; Gifford et al., 2008). The response to Pi starvation has been transcriptionally analyzed at the cell‐type level using fluorescent‐reporter lines that specifically label the cortex (pCO2:YFP) and nonroot hair‐epidermis (nrh‐epidermis; pWER:GFP) cell lineages in Arabidopsis (Cederholm & Benfey, 2015). This study found that ROOT GROWTH FACTOR1, 2 and 3 genes are induced in the cortex, controlling root meristematic activity and radial growth during the Pi starvation response. An in silico analysis combining Pi scarcity responsive gene expression and cell‐type‐specific enrichment identified a set of root cell–specific regulator genes involved in the Pi starvation response and Pi homeostasis (Linn et al., 2017).

In this work, we performed a comparative transcriptomic analysis of five distinct dark‐grown root cell types and identified more than 7000 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in response to Pi deficiency. By characterizing selected mutants, we uncovered novel functions for genes not previously associated with the Pi starvation response. Notably, mutation of the SR45 gene, which encodes a splicing regulatory factor, led to increased Pi accumulation in roots, suggesting a role for SR45 in Pi homeostasis. Computational analysis revealed that 733 unique transcripts are differentially regulated by alternative splicing (AS) in response to Pi deficiency across various root cell types. Interestingly, most of these AS‐regulated transcripts are not affected at the transcriptional level. Moreover, the Gene Ontology (GO) categories associated with AS‐regulated transcripts differ from those linked to transcriptional regulation. While transcriptional changes predominantly affect genes involved in stress responses, AS appears to modulate developmental processes, suggesting that expression and AS act as independent regulatory pathways.

Overall, we present a high‐resolution, cell‐type dataset that captures plant adaptive responses to P deficiency under conditions closely resembling natural environments (roots in darkness). Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that both transcriptional and post‐transcriptional responses to Pi starvation are largely cell‐type specific and highlight the complexity and specificity of the regulatory mechanisms involved in this response.

Materials and Methods

Plant growth and medium

We used Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh Columbia‐0 (Col‐0) background as wild‐type (WT) plants. To analyze the response in different root tissues, we used five different cell‐type marker lines: WER::GFP (in Col‐0 background) expresses green fluorescent protein (GFP) in epidermal nonhair atrichoblast cells (Lee & Schiefelbein, 1999); J0571 (in C24 background) expresses GFP in cortex/endodermis cells (Malamy & Benfey, 1997); J0661 (in C24 background) expresses GFP in pericycle and procambium cells in the differentiated part of the root (Supporting Information Fig. S1a; Laplaze et al., 2005); SHR::GFP marker (in Col‐0 background) expresses GFP in the stele (Helariutta et al., 2000); and J3411 (in C24 background) expresses GFP in the lateral root cap (LR cap) cells (Birnbaum et al., 2003). The GAL4‐VP16/UAS enhancer trap lines J3411, J0571 and J0661 are in the C24 background (Laplaze et al., 2005). To facilitate identification, the marker lines were named in this manuscript as follows: WER::GFP, nrh‐epidermis; J0571, cortex‐endo; J0661, proc‐pericycle; SHR::GFP, stele; and J3411, LR cap. Mutant lines were ordered from the Salk Institute for Biological Studies T‐DNA collection (SALK) collection, genotyped by PCR (see Table S1 for primer sequences) and selected as homozygous stable alleles. The sr45‐1 mutant and sr45‐1/SR45.1‐GFP and sr45‐1/SR45.2‐GFP lines used in this work were generated and described by Zhang & Mount (2009); the line pMYB88::GUS‐GFP by Vanneste et al. (2011), pyl112458 mutant (Gonzalez‐Guzman et al., 2012) and the pLHY:LUC line by Baudry et al. (2010).

Seedlings were sterilized with 2 ml of 70% bleach solution, mixed continuously for 5 min in a laminar flow hood and then washed. The bleach was removed, and the seeds were washed five times with 1.5 ml of sterile water. Afterward, the clean seeds were sown on vertically oriented 12 × 12‐cm plates containing ½‐strength Murashige & Skoog medium (½MS) without Pi, 0.05% MES, 1% sucrose and 1% agar (Difco, Sparks, MD, USA). The pH was adjusted to 5.7, and after autoclaving, filtered‐sterile KH2PO4 was added to the autoclaved medium at the indicated concentrations: high phosphate medium (HPi = 625 μM Pi), low phosphate medium (LPi = 5 μM Pi) or moderate phosphate medium (MPi = 40 μM Pi). In all cases, potassium was compensated by using the same amount of KCl to maintain the overall potassium level. To facilitate root collection for cell sorting, a sterile Nitex mesh (SEFAR Nitex 03‐100/44; 10 × 5 mm) was placed on top of the medium in each plate. All seedlings were germinated and cultivated using the D‐Root device (Silva‐Navas et al., 2015) in a germination chamber under a 16 h : 8 h, light : dark photoperiod (107.45 μmol m−2 s−1) at 22°C.

Cell sorting and RNA sequencing

Seedlings from the five markers were grown for 6 d in HPi or LPi medium. Protoplasts from the root tip (c. 2 cm) were generated for 60 min, and cells marked with the respective GFP lines were sorted by passing them through a FACS device (FACSAria; Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) fitted with a 100‐μm nozzle and a 20‐psi sheath pressure at a flow rate of 1000–2000 events per second, following the protocol described by González‐García et al. (2020). The efficiency (Methods S1) of viable protoplasts after digestion was between 80 and 96%, with an average of 91% for all cell types. Protoplasts were collected directly into 300 μl of lysis buffer (Qiagen RLT buffer, Germantown, MD, USA) plus 3 μl of β‐mercaptoethanol and frozen on dry ice.

Total RNA from sorted protoplasts was isolated using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA integrity was determined using the Agilent 6000 RNA Nano Kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and Qubit™ RNA Assay Kits (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer, according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cDNA synthesis and library preparation for all samples were performed using the SMART‐Seq v.4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit and the SMARTer® ThruPLEX® DNA‐Seq Kit (Takara, Mountain View, CA, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. Sequencing of the libraries was performed by the NC State University Genomic Sciences Laboratory using a NovaSeq SP 100 SE flow cell.

For C24 ecotype, seedlings were grown in ½MS containing HPi or LPi for 6 d. Afterward, RNA was extracted using the miniRNA Plant Extraction Kit (Qiagen) and the quantity and quality was analyzed by qubit. RNA‐seq was performed by Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI) using DNBSEQ platform and PE150 paired reads, and after filtering and quality analyses, DEGs were identified with these cutoff parameters (log2 fold change (log2FC) > 0.8 or < −0.8 with an adjusted P adj ≤ 0.05).

Transcriptional and alternative splicing computational analyses

For read alignment, FastQC (v.0.11.9; Andrews, 2010) and multiqc (v.1.7; Ewels et al., 2016) were used for quality control analyses. For each sample, RNA‐seq raw reads (single‐end reads of 100‐bp), the Trimmomatic (v.0.38; Bolger et al., 2014) software was used for Illumina adapters and low‐quality base trimming with specific command line parameters: leading:3 trailing:3 sliding window:4:15 minlen:36. The remaining reads were aligned to the A. thaliana TAIR10 reference genome using the Araport11 annotation (Cheng et al., 2017) and the Star aligner (v.2.5.3a; Dobin et al., 2013) and specific command line parameters: ‐outFilterMultimapNmax 20 ‐alignSJoverhangMin 8 ‐alignSJDBoverhangMin 8 ‐outFilterMismatchNmax 8 ‐alignIntronMin 35 ‐alignMatesGapMax 100 000 ‐alignIntronMax 20 000.

To identify DEGs, first the HTSeq (v.1.99.2; Anders et al., 2015) was used with the ‘union’ option to generate read counts per gene based on RNA‐seq mapped reads and Araport11 annotation. Normalization and statistical analysis of differential gene expression were performed using the DESeq2 Bioconductor package in R (v.1.38.3; Love et al., 2014). Genes were considered differentially expressed, either up‐ or downregulated, if they had a log2FC > 0.58 or < −0.58 with an adjusted P‐value ≤ 0.05. Hierarchical K‐means clustering was performed using the factoextra R package (v.1.0.7; Kassambara & Mundt, 2020). Data were visualized using complexheatmap R package (v.2.1.4; Gu et al., 2016).

As the library preparation kit does not produce libraries for stranded RNA sequencing, it should be noted that AS analysis on this dataset could be limited. Aligned and sorted bam files from STAR alignment were used to detect splicing events and significant splicing differences between control and Pi‐deficient sample groups using rmats turbo (v.4.1.2; Wang et al., 2024). The five main types of AS events from replicate RNA‐Seq data (A3SS, A5SS, SE, RI and MXE events) were defined by considering only junctions‐spanning reads (JC) as defined by rMATS. AS event statistics were performed and plotted using the R package maser (v.1.23.0; Veiga, 2024). We defined as differentially alternatively spliced (DAS) if they showed significant AS features identified in all three samples of each replicate (control or Pi‐deficient) that were detected in at least two or more replicates with more than five reads, and had a differential accumulation (deltaPSI) of > 0.15 or < −0.15, indicating a significant 15% increase or decrease in the splicing inclusion level between treatments, and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Gene ontology and transcription factor analysis

Gene Ontology categorization was performed using metascape tool (https://metascape.org/) (Zhou et al., 2019) using the following custom enrichment parameters: (1) Min Overlap = 3; (2) P‐Value Cutoff = 0.05; and (3) Min Enrichment = 1.5. Additionally, a multiple test correction was applied using the FDR method to control Type I errors. The parameters were selected based on previous studies and the nature of the dataset, ensuring a balance between statistical significance and biological relevance. Venn diagrams were generated with the interactive Venn tool (http://www.interactivenn.net/).

Transcription factors (TFs) were identified by matching our gene sets with the curated Arabidopsis thaliana TF annotations provided in PlantTFDB (https://planttfdb.gao‐lab.org/index.php?sp=Ath).

Free phosphate quantification

Arabidopsis plants were grown on ½MS for the indicated days. Pi quantification followed the colorimetric method of Ames (1966) with minor modifications. Shoots and roots were collected separately in 1.5‐ml tubes, frozen in liquid nitrogen and subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles (40 min at 37°C) to disrupt cell walls. Tissue was extracted with 1% acetic acid (500 μl for shoots, 200 μl for roots). For measurement, aliquots of HPi samples (3.75 μl shoots, 5 μl roots) and LPi samples (65 μl shoots, 75 μl roots) were adjusted to 75 μl with 1% acetic acid. Then, 175 μl of reagent mix (36.6 ml solution A + 10 ml solution B) was added (Solution A: 2.1 g ammonium molybdate, 18.3 ml H2SO4, 347.7 ml water; Solution B: 1 g ascorbic acid, 10 ml water). Samples were incubated for 1 h at 37°C in the dark. Pi was quantified by absorbance at 820 nm (SPECTROstar Nano) using a Pi standard curve and dilution factors. Due to the variability in Pi concentration observed across different experiments, we also normalized Pi concentration relative to the control, as indicated.

The LPi : HPi ratio of Pi content was calculated by dividing the Pi concentration (nmol per mg fresh weight) in seedlings grown under LPi conditions by the mean Pi concentration in seedlings grown under HPi conditions. The root‐to‐shoot Pi ratio (Pi‐R : S) was determined by dividing the Pi concentration in roots by that in shoots from the same seedling pool.

ABA quantification

Cis‐ and trans‐abscisic acid (ABA) levels were quantified as described in Silva‐Navas et al. (2019) using 0.25 g of frozen tissue. Arabidopsis plants were grown in ½MS supplemented with either 625 μM or 5 μM Pi and then root and shoot were separated and immediately frozen. The cis‐, trans‐ABA standard was purchased from OlChemin Ltd (Olomouc, Czech Republic) and endogenous ABA was quantified using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC device coupled to a Q Exactive Focus Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), equipped with an HESI(II) source, a quadrupole mass filter, a C‐Trap, an HCD collision cell and an Orbitrap mass analyzer. A reverse‐phase column (Synergi 4 mm Hydro‐RP 80A, 150 × 2 mm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) was used.

Phenotypic analysis of roots and confocal analyses

Plants were scanned using an Epson 600 V scanner with high resolution, and primary root and LR length and LR number were quantified using Fiji software. For confocal microscopy, roots were stained in 10 μg ml−1 propidium iodide (PI) for 2–5 min, rinsed, mounted in dH2O and imaged in a Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). For the cell‐type markers and sr45‐1/SR45.1‐GFP or sr45‐1/SR45.2‐GFP and/or PI, the excitation wavelengths were 488 and 514 nm, and fluorescence was collected in the ranges of 493–540 nm (rendered in green) and 600–650 nm (rendered in red), respectively. Root meristem size was calculated based on the number of meristematic cortex‐endo cells and/or the distance from the quiescent center to the last meristematic cell using confocal images taken in a Leica SP8 microscopy. The end of the meristem zone was taken as the point where a meristematic cortical cell doubled in size relative to the previous one, as described (Tellez‐Robledo et al., 2019).

The LPi : HPi ratio for primary root length (PRL) and lateral root number (LRs) was calculated by dividing the values obtained from seedlings grown in LPi medium by the average values from seedlings grown in HPi medium and then calculating the average ratio. The fresh weight root‐to‐shoot ratio (FW‐R : S) was determined by dividing the root weight (mg) by the shoot weight from the same pool of seedlings.

Luciferase imaging

pLHY:LUC seedlings (Baudry et al., 2010) were grown on ½MS under HPi or LPi conditions. After 6 d, plates were sprayed with 1 ml of 2.5 mM potassium luciferin (Gold Biotechnology, St Louis, MO, USA). After 10 min of incubation, luminescence was recorded using a Night Owl II imaging system (Berthold), following the procedure described in González‐García et al. (2024). Expression levels were quantified by selecting a defined region of interest (ROI) and measuring the analog–digital units per pixel using the MetaMorph Image Analysis Software. The relative integrated intensity profile along the root was also extracted using the MetaMorph software.

Statistical analyses

The overall data were statistically analyzed using the Graphpad 5 software. Comparisons between two groups were performed with the Student's t‐test, while multigroup comparisons were performed using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and indicated by different letters. The overlap between the different transcript sets was evaluated using Fisher's exact test. The background was defined as all transcripts detected in the experiment (or the reference transcriptome, depending on dataset and analysis). Two‐tailed Fisher's exact test was used to assess whether the observed overlap was greater or lesser than expected by random chance and P‐values were computed using the exact hypergeometric distribution. Odds ratios (OR) were reported as a measure of effect size. All statistical analyses were performed in R using the corresponding built‐in functions for Fisher's exact test.

Results

Cell‐type expression analyses reveal specific responses to Pi starvation

To investigate cellular mechanisms underlying the Pi starvation response, we performed cell‐type‐specific transcriptomic analyses using the D‐Root system to avoid root illumination (Silva‐Navas et al., 2019). We used five GFP reporter marker lines to label specific root cell types: atrichoblast epidermal cells (nrh‐epidermis), cortex‐endo/endodermis (cortex‐endo), procambium–pericycle (proc‐pericycle), meristematic stele (stele) and LR cap (Fig. S1a). After 6 d of growth under HPi (625 μM) or LPi (5 μM) Pi conditions, root meristem size remained and GFP expression patterns were unchanged across treatments (Fig. S1a,b).

Given the different genetic backgrounds of the marker lines (Col‐0 for nrh‐epidermis and stele; C24 for cortex‐endo, proc‐pericycle and LR cap), we first compared the DEGs in the whole root in response to Pi starvation across both ecotypes (Methods S1). Approximately half of the C24 DEGs were also differentially regulated in Col‐0 (Fig. 1a; Dataset S1a–c). Notably, a substantial number of DEGs were induced or repressed in an ecotype‐specific manner, likely reflecting long‐term adaptive differences between ecotypes. Among the DEGs shared by both ecotypes, we observed a significant enrichment of functional GO categories or Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways associated with Pi starvation (Fig. S2a). Remarkably, most Pi‐related genes (involved in Pi starvation response, Pi metabolism and homeostasis) were differentially expressed and significantly enriched in response to Pi starvation in both Col‐0 and C24 ecotypes (Fig. S2b,c). In addition, these Pi‐related genes showed similar expression patterns, with few exceptions, and were differentially expressed in both ecotypes (Dataset S1d,e). Taken together, these data indicate that the Pi starvation response is partially ecotype‐dependent, although a core gene involved in Pi starvation is similarly expressed in both ecotypes, Col‐0 and C24.

Next, using seedlings grown under HPi and LPi conditions, we sorted GFP‐positive root cells from each marker line and performed comparative transcriptomic analyses. These analyses revealed 7818 DEGs across all cell types in response to Pi starvation that correspond to 7171 unique transcripts (Fig. 1b; Dataset S2a). Notably, proc‐pericycle cells exhibited the largest number of DEGs, followed by the lateral root cap, cortex‐endo, stele and the nrh‐epidermis (Fig. 1b; Dataset S2a–f). Comparison of these cell‐type DEGs with the whole‐root response to Pi starvation in the corresponding ecotype revealed significant overlap across all of them (Fig. 1c; Dataset S1f–k), confirming that the observed transcriptional changes at the cell‐type level are indeed related to Pi deficiency. Nevertheless, a large number of transcripts did not overlap, suggesting that they might have specialized functions in these specific cell types.

Next, to determine how similarly each cell type contributes to the Pi starvation response, we compared cell‐type DEGs from a similar ecotype. Only a limited number of transcripts showed a shared response to Pi starvation across multiple cell types (Fig. 1d). However, we observed a significant overlap between cell‐type DEGs and Pi‐related genes in cortex‐endo, proc‐pericycle, but not in the other cell types analyzed, although they showed an enrichment tendency (Fig. S3a). Biocomputational analyses of these cell‐type DEGs revealed that most of the enriched GO categories and KEGG pathways were also specific to individual cell types, with only a few GO terms shared across multiple types (Fig. S3b). These results indicate that analyzing transcriptional changes at single‐cell‐type resolution may enable the identification of genes and regulatory pathways that have remained undetected using bulk approaches.

Intriguingly, we identified 336 genes that were upregulated in some cell types but downregulated in others (Dataset S2a). This striking pattern of opposing regulation reinforces the notion of a highly complex and strongly cell‐type‐specific transcriptional response to Pi starvation, indicating that individual root cell types may deploy distinct, and in some cases contrasting, regulatory programs to coordinate the overall plant adaptation to P deficiency.

TFs are well known to play a central role in plant responses to nutrient deficiencies, including Pi starvation (Shin, 2011; Jain et al., 2012; Das et al., 2022). Among the DEGs identified, we found a higher number than expected of TFs that were either up‐ or downregulated in response to Pi starvation than statistically expected across different cell types (Dataset S2g). Notably, the nrh‐epidermis showed the fewest TFs affected, consistent with its overall lower transcriptional responsiveness to Pi deficiency (Dataset S2g,h). The strongest enrichment of upregulated TFs was observed in the stele and LR cap, with the ERF and bHLH families being the most represented. By contrast, downregulated TFs showed lower overall enrichment across all cell types. To investigate functional associations, we constructed interaction networks comprising all upregulated TFs (Dataset S2i). GO terms analysis of this network revealed enrichment in categories related to stress responses, hormonal signaling (ethylene and ABA), cell fate specification, hypoxia response, root development and meristem maintenance (Dataset S2i). When this network was generated using TFs from specific cell types, we found that ABA signaling was significantly enriched in proc‐pericycle, while ethylene was enriched in all cell types except in nrh‐epidermis and cortex‐endo (Dataset S2i).

Guided by these observations, and to validate our hypothesis that cell‐type‐resolved transcriptomic analysis can uncover novel genes involved in Pi starvation, we selected mutants for some differentially expressed TFs not previously linked to Pi deficiency. These included LBD41, a gene involved in lateral root formation and hypoxia response (Meng et al., 2009; Mustroph et al., 2009); MYB88, a regulator of stomata development that also functions in establishing the gravitropic set‐point angles of lateral roots (Wang et al., 2015); and REV7 and LHY, known for their roles in circadian rhythm regulation, thermomorphogenesis and ABA biosynthesis (Alabadí et al., 2002; Tian et al., 2022).

Analyses of root architecture revealed distinct phenotypic responses among the mutants. Under HPi, all mutants displayed enhanced primary root growth than WT (Fig. 2a). Under LPi conditions, WT did not significantly modify root growth. The rev7 and myb88 mutants showed a mild, nonsignificant reduction, while lbd41 and lhy displayed a significant increase in root length (Fig. 2b). Accordingly, the LPi : HPi growth ratio significantly increased in lbd41 and lhy, while it decreased in rev7 and myb88 compared with the WT ratio (Fig. S4a), indicating gene‐specific regulatory roles in root adaptation to Pi deficiency.

Under LPi condition, lateral root density (LRD) was increased in WT seedlings, but was unaffected in lbd41, myb88, and lhy mutants, whereas rev7 showed a significant reduction (Fig. 2a). However, the LPi : HPi LRD ratio diverged, increasing in lbd41 and decreasing in myb88 and rev7 (Fig. S4b). These differences reflect an uncoupling between primary root elongation and lateral root formation during Pi deficiency stress.

Pi content measurements revealed further functional divergence. Under HPi conditions, lbd41 showed a slight increase in root Pi without altering shoot levels; myb88 accumulated less Pi in both organs, showing a reduced root‐to‐shoot Pi (R : S‐Pi) ratio (Fig. 2c–e). Both rev7 and lhy displayed elevated root Pi content, resulting in higher R : S‐Pi ratios (Fig. 2c–e). Under LPi conditions, lbd41 mutant did not show any significant difference in Pi level compared with WT, while the Pi level in the myb88 mutant was significantly reduced in roots, leading to a lower R : S‐Pi ratio (Fig. 2d–f). The rev7 mutant accumulated significantly less Pi in shoots, leading to a higher R : S‐Pi ratio than WT. Conversely, lhy showed a significant decrease in root Pi levels, lowering its R : S ratio (Fig. 2d–f). These contrasting patterns indicate distinct contributions of REV7, LHY and MYB88 to Pi allocation and homeostasis.

Using the pMYB88::GUS‐GFP (Vanneste et al., 2011) and the pLHY:LUC (Baudry et al., 2010) reporter lines, we observed that both MYB88 and LHY were induced by Pi starvation (Fig. 2g,h). Since this induction is observed in both ecotypes, C24 (transcriptomic data) and Col‐0 (reporter lines), the Pi‐dependent activation of both genes is unlikely to be an ecotype artifact and instead likely reflects a robust, cell‐type‐specific response to Pi availability.

To further validate the role of these TFs in the Pi starvation response, we tested whether cell‐type DEGs were enriched among their predicted direct targets of these TF, as defined by DAP‐seq data (Methods S1; https://connectf.org/). As shown in Fig. S5 and Dataset S2k, we observed significant overlap across all cell types, except between REV7 and nrh‐epidermis, where a significant gene depletion was observed. Notably, LHY, REV7 and MYB88 showed the greatest overlap in the stele, followed by proc‐pericycle. Taken together, these data suggest that these TFs play an important role in the root response to Pi starvation.

ABA signaling is important for Pi starvation response

LHY has been described as a negative regulator of the biosynthesis of ABA (Adams et al., 2018), a hormone that has previously been related to the Pi starvation response (Woo et al., 2012; Kawa et al., 2016; Lei et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022; Nagatoshi et al., 2023). In our study, ABA‐related processes emerged as one of the enriched functional categories in the TF network analysis (Dataset S2i), suggesting a role of ABA under Pi deficiency. However, since these works have used light‐grown root seedlings, a condition that largely alters Pi responses, we decided to investigate the role of ABA in dark‐grown roots under Pi starvation. ABA quantification showed that Pi starvation significantly increased ABA levels in shoots, with a moderate increase in roots (Fig. 3a). To further explore the role of ABA, we analyzed the Pi starvation response in the sextuple ABA‐receptor mutant pyr1 pyl1 pyl2 pyl4 pyl5 pyl8 (referred here to as 112458), which has impaired ABA perception and signaling (Gonzalez‐Guzman et al., 2012). WT plants exhibited a slight reduction in root growth under Pi starvation (Fig. 3b). By contrast, the 112458 mutant, which already had shorter roots than WT, did not exhibit further root growth inhibition under LPi (Fig. 2b). In response to Pi starvation, LRD increased in WT seedlings, but not in the 112458 mutant, which displayed a modest but statistically nonsignificant reduction (Fig. 3c). Under HPi, but not LPi, conditions, the mutant accumulated more shoot biomass than WT plants (Fig. 3d), although the root‐to‐shoot fresh weight (R : S‐FW) ratio remained similar in both genotypes (Fig. 3e). Interestingly, the Pi content analysis revealed that, under HPi, 112458 mutants accumulated more Pi in shoots than WT seedlings, while in roots both genotypes showed similar levels (Fig. 3f). Under LPi, the mutant accumulated significantly less Pi than WT in roots (Fig. 3g), resulting in a significant lower Pi‐R :S (Fig. 3h), suggesting disrupted Pi allocation during Pi scarcity.

Further supporting the role of ABA in the Pi starvation response at the cell‐type level, we found a significant overlap between ABA‐induced genes and Pi starvation‐induced TFs, except for nrh‐epidermis cells. Notably, the most significant overlap occurred in the stele (Dataset S2j), a cell layer previously shown to be highly responsive to ABA signaling (Duan et al., 2013).

Cell‐type alternative splicing reveals specific responses to Pi starvation

Our analysis revealed that AS is among the significantly enriched GO/KEGG functional categories in response to Pi starvation across distinct cell types. Since AS is important for the control of plant development and the response to stresses (Laloum et al., 2018; Barff et al., 2025), we wondered whether the response to Pi starvation in different cell types could be augmented by generating new isoforms through AS. Through bioinformatic analysis, we evaluated all five major types of AS: intron retention (IR), exon skipping (ES), alternative 5′ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3′ splice site (A3SS) or mutually exclusive exons splicing (MXE) across the five cell types analyzed in response to Pi starvation. A total of 999 significant AS events, affecting 733 unique transcripts, showed differential accumulation in response to Pi starvation, indicating DAS (Dataset S3; Fig. 4a). Consistent with previous studies (e.g. Li et al., 2016), IR was the most common AS type across all cell types (Fig. 4b), with c. 52% of the total number of events. The remaining events that included A3SS, A5SS, ES and MXE were also identified but in a lower percentage (20.5, 12, 14 and 1.5%, respectively). To assess cell‐type contributions to AS changes under Pi starvation, we compared DAS for nrh‐epidermis and stele (both derived from Col‐0) and separately for cortex‐endo, proc‐pericycle and LR cap (derived from C24). We found a limited overlapping between different cell types (Fig. 4c). This observation indicates that AS in response to Pi starvation seems to be also cell type‐specific, mirroring the cell type‐specific nature of the transcriptional response (Fig. 1). GO and KEGG pathway analyses of the DAS transcripts revealed significant enrichment in processes, such as epigenetic regulation of gene expression, the transition from vegetative to reproductive meristem, protein phosphorylation, RNA splicing and mRNA surveillance, among others (Fig. 4d). Next, we examined the overlap between DEGs and transcripts regulated by DAS, revealing that 68.48% of DAS‐regulated transcripts were not differentially expressed (Dataset S4a,b). Among the transcripts shared between DEGs and DAS, several belonged to functional categories relevant to the Pi starvation response, such as phosphoric ester hydrolase activity and organophosphate metabolic processes. Furthermore, transcripts regulated exclusively by AS were enriched in functions, including phospholipase D activity and organophosphate metabolism, both key components of the cellular response to Pi deprivation (Cruz‐Ramírez et al., 2006), reinforcing the idea that AS is also important for the plant Pi starvation response. These findings underscore the distinct yet complementary roles of gene expression and AS in orchestrating the Pi starvation response, with marked cell type‐specific regulatory differences.

Among the DEGs associated with alternative splicing, we found that SR45, a key regulator of AS during plant development and stress responses, was induced in the proc‐pericycle, a cell layer that accounted for nearly 20% of all AS events triggered by Pi starvation (Dataset S2; Fig. S6a,b). SR45 mRNA is post‐transcriptionally processed, generating two isoforms (SR45.1 and SR45.2) that differ in eight amino acids (Zhang & Mount, 2009). Supporting the computational AS analysis (Fig. S6c), reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis using isoform‐specific primers revealed that under HPi conditions SR45.1 and SR45.2 accumulated at similar levels in proc‐pericycle cells (Methods S1; Fig. S6d,e). However, under Pi starvation, SR45.1 levels were significantly higher than SR45.2 (Fig. S6e).

Consistent with previous findings (e.g. Zhang & Mount, 2009), we found that a mutation in SR45 significantly reduced root growth under HPi conditions compared with WT seedlings c. 33% (Fig. 5a). Notably, this reduction was even more pronounced in response to LPi (Fig. 5b), suggesting an enhanced sensitivity of sr45‐1 mutant to Pi deficiency. This mutant also developed fewer LRs than WT, but this number was unaffected in response to LPi (Fig. 5c,d). We found that, under HPi, the sr45‐1 mutation reduced root apical meristem (RAM) size by c. 30% compared with WT, correlating with the shorter root phenotype (Fig. 5e,f). Under LPi conditions, both sr45‐1 and WT seedlings suffered similar RAM reductions (35.3% in WT vs 33.3% in sr45‐1; Fig. 5e,f). However, under moderate Pi (MPi, 40 μM), WT seedlings showed no significant RAM reduction (6.5%), whereas sr45‐1 mutant exhibited a significantly greater reduction (c. 27%), indicating hypersensitivity to Pi limitation. Under HPi conditions, sr45‐1 mutant accumulated significantly higher Pi levels in roots than WT, while no differences were observed in shoots (Fig. 5g,h), suggesting a potential role for SR45 in organ Pi allocation.

It has been demonstrated that specific overexpression of the SR45.1‐GFP isoform rescues the salt‐sensitive phenotype (Albaqami et al., 2019), while SR45.2‐GFP appears to partially restore the short‐root phenotype of the sr45‐1 mutant (Zhang & Mount, 2009). Given the abnormal Pi allocation in sr45‐1, we examined the effects of ectopically expressing both isoforms, SR45.1‐GFP and SR45.2‐GFP, in the sr45‐1 mutant. These genotypes were grown in a range of different Pi concentrations, from extreme high Pi (6000 μM Pi) to low Pi (5 μM HPi). As shown in Fig. 6a, Pi levels above 1200 μM significantly inhibited root growth in WT, but not in the sr45‐1 mutant or sr45‐1/SR45.1‐GFP or sr45‐1/SR45.2‐GFP lines. It is well established that in response to Pi starvation, both R/S‐FW ratio and root : shoot Pi ratio (R/S‐Pi) increase (for full numerical values supporting these ratios, see Dataset S6). Under Pi‐deficient conditions, WT seedlings increased in R/S‐FW, whereas this response was absent in the sr45‐1 mutant (Fig. 6b). Notably, overexpression of either SR45.1‐GFP or SR45.2‐GFP in the mutant did not significantly restore R/S‐FW (Figs 6b, S7). Consistent with previous findings (e.g. Silva‐Navas et al., 2019), R/S‐Pi increased under LPi conditions in WT seedlings (Figs 6c, S7). We showed, for the first time, that R/S‐Pi reached its maximum peak under moderate Pi of 40 μM (MPi) rather than 5 μM of Pi and an intermediate ratio at high‐Pi conditions (Fig. 6c). However, this trend was not observed for sr45‐1, although the ratio was significantly higher under MPi and LPi. Intriguingly, R/S‐Pi was markedly elevated in both sr45‐1/SR45.1‐GFP and sr45‐1/SR45.2‐GFP lines under MPi. Taken together, these results suggest that neither isoform alone fully rescues the Pi‐related phenotype of the sr45‐1 mutant.

The observation that both isoforms were induced by Pi starvation raises the possibility that concurrent expression of SR45.1 and SR45.2 may be required for full phenotypic restoration. To test this hypothesis, we crossed sr45‐1/SR45.1‐GFP (female) with sr45‐1/SR45.2‐GFP (male) and analyzed root growth and Pi levels in the F1 progeny. Our results showed that simultaneous overexpression of both isoforms enhanced the recovery of the sr45‐1 phenotype by increasing root growth and reducing the R : S‐Pi ratio under HPi conditions to nearly WT levels (Fig. S8), suggesting that both isoforms function in the Pi response.

Through mRNA immunoprecipitation, many SR45‐bound RNAs were identified (Xing et al., 2015). By overlapping the SR45‐bound RNAs with our DEGs and DAS datasets, we found a higher intersection with DAS than with DEGs (Figs 7a–c, S9; Dataset S5a,b). Analyses of cell‐type DAS‐SR45‐bound GO/KEGG functional categories showed a specific enrichment among the different cell types, although some categories were shared between cell types. Among them, it is worth highlighting the spliceosome and mRNA surveillance, organophosphate metabolic processes, circadian rhythm or phosphatidylinositol (PI) binding. Notably, the latest GO is related to the plant response to Pi deficiency, since it links Pi status with lipid‐mediated control of membrane trafficking and cytoskeletal organization, both of which are key drivers of root remodeling under Pi stress (Verma et al., 2021; Watari et al., 2022). Notably, most transcripts within these GO categories were AS‐regulated in the cortex–endodermis and LR cap, showing increased processing under LPi conditions (Fig. 7e). Taken together, our data indicate an important role for AS regulation and SR45 function in Pi homeostasis and adaptive responses to Pi deficiency in Arabidopsis.

Discussion

Plants dynamically adjust their root features at molecular, morphological and physiological levels to cope with Pi deficiencies (Plaxton & Tran, 2011; Lopez‐Arredondo et al., 2014; Madison et al., 2023). These adaptations enhance their ability to explore new soil zones to improve Pi acquisition and use efficiency and plant fitness. Many transcriptomic studies have deciphered the main molecular changes at the gene expression level in response to Pi starvation, either using the whole root system (Baek et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2018; Silva‐Navas et al., 2019; Rico‐Reséndiz et al., 2020; Satheesh et al., 2022; Scheible et al., 2023), specific root cell types (Cederholm & Benfey, 2015) or biocomputational analyses (Linn et al., 2017). The whole plant response to Pi starvation involves the coordination between various organs, tissues and cell types. It was proposed that plants interpret changes in Pi availability at the cellular level and, as a consequence, modify the root morphology by inducing radial divisions in the cortex‐endo or specification and formation of root hairs (Kwak & Schiefelbein, 2007; Cederholm & Benfey, 2015). Thus, to obtain a more precise root map of Pi starvation response and tackle changes with cell‐type resolution, we have carried out a transcriptomic analysis using different root cell types from plants that grew in HPi or LPi in the medium and using the D‐Root device to avoid the negative impact of root illumination (Silva‐Navas et al., 2019). Despite our cell‐type markers derived from two different ecotypes, overall, our results indicate that Pi‐responsive transcriptional programs are partially conserved between Co‐0 and C24. Nevertheless, each ecotype also displays a distinct set of uniquely regulated genes, which may underlie ecotype‐specific mechanisms for long‐term adaptation to Pi deficiency. This is consistent with recent studies showing that ecotypes exhibit distinct transcriptional and proteomic adaptations in response to environmental stresses (Miao et al., 2018; Leschevin et al., 2021) or the control of long noncoding RNA expression between Columbia and Landsberg erecta ecotypes in response to Pi starvation (Blein et al., 2020).

Although bulk RNA sequencing has traditionally been used to profile transcriptional changes, cell‐type‐specific transcriptomics (via cell‐type‐seq, scRNA‐seq and snRNA‐seq) reveals heterogeneity stress responses among different cell types and enables identification of novel and dynamic cell transcriptional state, noncanonical pathways and regulatory networks (Dam et al., 2023). Thus, cell‐type resolution provides a more accurate and meaningful view of how distinct cell populations perceive and adapt to nutrient stress, adding depth to existing whole‐root transcriptomic analyses. It is important to emphasize that, despite the ecotype influence, the transcriptional responses of the specific cell types analyzed here were enriched in Pi‐related genes in the cortex–endodermis and procambium–pericycle cells. Although our analysis focuses on a defined time point, it provides a valuable snapshot of gene expression under Pi starvation, establishing a framework for future multi‐time‐point studies or single‐cell transcriptomic analyses to fully capture the temporal and spatial complexity of the response (Contreras‐López et al., 2022). Despite this, our cell‐type‐specific transcriptomic framework, derived from roots grown in darkness, provides a coherent and biologically meaningful set of Pi‐responsive genes and complements bulk transcriptomic studies, offering high‐resolution insight into cell‐specific adaptations.

This higher resolution allows the identification of novel gene functions in the Pi starvation response that might have been overlooked in whole organ transcriptomic analyses, such as MYB88 or circadian rhythm TFs LHY and REV7. MYB88 is a regulator of stomatal function and also in the angle formation of LRs (Wang et al., 2015). Based on our data, MYB88 also plays a role in Pi root/shoot allocation and might connect stomatal regulation and transpiration‐driven nutrient flow with Pi homeostasis, or influence the gravitropic set‐point angles of lateral roots (Wang et al., 2015), a trait that is also relevant for nutrient foraging. In addition, MYB8 functions in the response to iron deficiency (Gong et al., 2024) and PHR1, a MYB‐CC family member, regulates the Pi starvation response (Rubio et al., 2001), suggesting that other MYB‐related proteins, such as MYB88, could also be implicated in the regulation of the iron‐Pi nutrition in plants. However, these possibilities will require further investigation.

The circadian clock components such as CCA1, LHY, PRRs, or TOC1 have been shown to regulate nutrient‐responsive genes, including those involved in Pi and nitrogen metabolism, likely to synchronize nutrient uptake and metabolic processes with daily environmental cycles (Grundy et al., 2015; Haydon et al., 2015; Xiong et al., 2026). Furthermore, the plant clock orchestrates photosynthesis, carbohydrate allocation, mitochondrial activity and ATP/ADP balance over the day, a process that requires high levels of Pi (Cervela‐Cardona et al., 2021). Recently, it has been shown that MAKK‐dependent phosphorylation of CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1) stabilizes it and triggers a transcriptional response connecting nitrogen sensing and lateral root growth (Zhang et al., 2026). Our data showed that two circadian rhythm TFs, LHY and REV7, also function in the Pi starvation response, supporting the idea that circadian clock components are essential for optimal plant nutrition, probably by synchronizing nutrient uptake with growth during specific phases of the circadian cycle.

LBD41 and LHY have been associated with iron deficiency responses (Xu et al., 2019; Telara et al., 2025). It is also noteworthy that a mutation in SR45 leads to a constitutive iron deficiency phenotype, characterized by altered iron distribution within the plant (Fanara et al., 2022). Given the well‐established interconnection between Pi deficiency and iron homeostasis (Guo et al., 2022), it is plausible that the coordinate function of these TFs and SR45‐mediated alternative splicing contribute to the Pi starvation response by modulating iron levels in a cell‐type‐specific manner. However, this hypothesis requires further investigation.

Hormonal crosstalk plays a key role during Pi starvation by integrating signals such as auxin, cytokinin, ethylene and other phytohormones to modulate root architecture and coordinate systemic Pi starvation responses (Madison et al., 2023). It is known that ABA also plays a role in Pi homeostasis by facilitating Pi acquisition, and impairment of ABA biosynthesis leads to decreased Pi levels due to reduced expression of the PHT1;1 and PHT1;4 transporters (Fang Zhu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2022). The ABA receptor pyl112458 mutant displays a shorter root phenotype (Gonzalez‐Guzman et al., 2012) and increased LRD (Li et al., 2020), phenotypes that are not affected by Pi starvation. The lhy mutant, which should have higher ABA levels (Alabadí et al., 2002), enhances root growth in response to Pi starvation, corroborating our finding with the ABA receptor pyl112458 mutant. The observation that this ABA‐receptor mutant reduces Pi content under Pi‐starvation conditions but increases it under HPi conditions suggests that ABA functions as a context‐dependent ‘brake‐and‐fuel’ regulator, fine‐tuning Pi homeostasis in response to external Pi availability. Furthermore, ABA appears to be involved in the reutilization and transport of Fe from root to shoot under Fe deficiency in Arabidopsis (Lei et al., 2014). Therefore, it is tempting to speculate that ABA might play a role in shaping the root system and coordinating Pi‐Fe homeostasis during the response to nutritional stresses, including Pi starvation.

Gene transcription and AS are pivotal mechanisms that orchestrate a plant's response to nutrient availability and abiotic stress in general (Liu et al., 2022). Alternative splicing enhances proteome diversity, enabling plants to amplify their ability to respond to environmental stimuli or developmental cues, including plant nutrition‐related responses (Dong et al., 2018) and fine‐tune their responses to environmental challenges. In plants, Pi deficiency is known to enhance AS activity (Satheesh et al., 2022; Scheible et al., 2023; Reis et al., 2024; Prabha et al., 2025). Tissue‐ and cell‐type‐specific AS produces isoforms that fine‐tune gene expression, driving specific developmental programs and facilitating whole organism adaptation to stress conditions (Martín et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025). A systematic analysis of RNA‐seq datasets recently showed limited overlap between DEGs and DAS‐regulated transcripts, indicating that these mechanisms frequently target different transcript sets (Dam et al., 2023). In line with previous findings, our analysis also showed limited overlap between DEGs and DAS, reinforcing the notion that gene expression and AS likely convey distinct biological signals and functions aimed at increasing plant adaptation to Pi starvation.

In this work, we present evidence that SR45, a key AS regulator, controls Pi allocation in Arabidopsis. The phytohormone ABA induces dephosphorylation of SR45, reducing its ubiquitination and degradation, and increasing SR45 protein accumulation and functionality (Albuquerque‐Martins et al., 2023). Furthermore, sr45‐1 also shows hypersensitivity to ABA and controls gene expression in response to glucose (Carvalho et al., 2010). Consequently, impaired ABA perception, as in the pyl112458 mutant, may result in reduced SR45 protein stabilization, promoting its degradation and functionally mimicking the sr45‐1 loss‐of‐function phenotype. Notably, both pyl112458 and sr45‐1 mutants accumulate higher levels of Pi under high‐Pi conditions, suggesting a mechanistic link between ABA signaling, SR45 activity and the regulation of Pi homeostasis.

Alternative splicing generates multiple mRNA variants from a single locus, and these isoforms may have distinct functions in response to nutrient availability (Satheesh et al., 2022; Scheible et al., 2023). Here, we identified SR45 as an important gene regulating Pi homeostasis as the sr45‐1 mutant accumulated higher levels of Pi in the roots than the WT under HPi conditions. SR45 has also been involved in Fe homeostasis, tending to accumulate more Fe in sr45‐1 roots than WT plants (Fanara et al., 2022), suggesting a defect in Fe translocation from root to shoot. Transcriptomic analyses of the sr45‐1 mutant using different iron concentrations shows alterations in several Pi transporters, PIP5K6, which is implicated in PI biosynthesis, and several genes related to Pi homeostasis (Fanara et al., 2022) that could be involved in the Pi defects observed in the sr45‐1 mutant. As this mutant also showed alteration in copper, manganese or zinc levels (Fanara et al., 2022), it is possible that SR45 acts as a hub for nutritional homeostasis and transport from root to shoot, although additional experiments will be needed. In Arabidopsis, there are two isoforms, SR45.1 and SR45.2, which arise from AS of the SR45 transcript. SR45.1 is predominantly involved in splicing events related to stress responses, while SR45.2 has more specific roles in development (Albaqami et al., 2019). We show that the SR45.1 isoform expression slightly increases under Pi starvation, in the proc‐pericycle, indicating that it may play a more prominent role in the adaptive response to Pi deficiency. However, our genetic data suggest that overexpression of neither SR45.1‐GFP nor SR45.2‐GFP isoforms fully restores the WT phenotype. Simultaneous overexpression of both isoforms increases the sr45‐1 mutant phenotype recovery, though not to WT levels. These findings suggest that SR45 expression from its specific full promoter or the presence of additional SR45 isoforms may be required to maintain Pi homeostasis, although further studies are needed to clarify these possibilities.

Environmental stresses can reshape AS patterns of genes involved in root development, enhancing plant adaptability (Liu et al., 2022; Innes et al., 2024). In our analysis, overlapping AS transcripts from Pi starvation and SR45‐bound targets identify several transcripts involved in the cell cycle, chromatin remodeling, mRNA surveillance and phosphatidylinositol Pi signaling among others. AS of developmental regulators, particularly those controlling the cell cycle and meristem activity, modulates lateral root initiation and emergence (Wang et al., 2014; Li et al., 2022; Misra et al., 2023). It is noteworthy that AS events related to the cell cycle in our study occur preferentially in the LR cap and cortex–endodermis, a cell layer that increases radial cell divisions in response to Pi starvation (Cederholm & Benfey, 2015). Remarkably, we found preferential enrichment of genes encoding Pi‐binding proteins that are regulated by AS in the LR cap. Since PI controls endo‐/exocytic machinery (Lin et al., 2022) and Pi starvation involves extensive remodeling of membrane trafficking and composition (Shemi et al., 2016), PI signaling might be essential to reposition transporters and nutrient‐responsive receptors during Pi limitation. Under Pi starvation, plants activate phospholipase‐mediated hydrolysis of membrane phospholipids to release free Pi: This hydrolysis requires the replacement by P‐free lipids, such as galacto‐ and sulfolipids (Verma et al., 2021). Remarkably, many phospholipases are regulated by AS, particularly in the cortex–endodermis and lateral root cap, suggesting that alternative splicing contributes to membrane adaptation during Pi starvation.

These findings suggest that AS regulates distinct biological processes in a spatially resolved manner during Pi starvation and highlights the complexity of post‐transcriptional regulation in nutrient‐stress responses.

Based on our data, we conclude that cell‐type‐specific transcriptomics provides valuable insights into how different root cell populations orchestrate the plant's adaptive response under Pi starvation. By resolving the regulatory landscape at cellular resolution, this approach not only deepens our understanding of nutrient‐stress adaptation but also uncovers previously unknown gene functions and isoform‐specific roles that remain hidden in bulk analyses. Our findings reveal that the root's response to Pi scarcity is not uniform across all cell types, but a coordinated, multilayered strategy shaped by the unique contributions of individual cell types. This high‐resolution framework provides a foundation for understanding root responses to Pi starvation and for future translational efforts to engineer crops capable of thriving in low‐Pi soils.
