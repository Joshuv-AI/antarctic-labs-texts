Artificial neural network analysis of a fractional cyber-epidemic model in wireless sensors under the proportional Hadamard–Caputo operator
Authors: Mohamed A. Barakat, Abd-Allah Hyder, Tarek Aboelenen, Naveed Iqbal, Ahmed Shafee, Noorullah Noori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a fractional order model for the spread of malware in wireless sensor networks that builds memory directly into the dynamics through the proportional Hadamard–Caputo operator. The network population is organized into six groups, namely susceptible, exposed, infectious, quarantined, recovered, and vaccinated devices. We recast the system as an integral equation using a logarithmic change of time and we prove two fixed point results, where existence follows from the nonlinear Leray–Schauder alternative and uniqueness is obtained by Banach’s contraction principle. We then establish stability in the sense of Ulam–Hyers and in its extended form, showing that small modeling or data errors lead to proportionally small changes in the solutions. For computation, we build a predictor and corrector scheme in the modified Adams Bashforth Moulton framework adapted to the proportional Hadamard Caputo kernel with exponential memory in logarithmic time. Simulations show that stronger memory or a lower fractional order slows decay and extends spread while values near the classical case bring rapid stabilization.

Introduction

Wireless sensor nodes track diverse environmental and physical signals such as pollution and temperature and humidity and sound and pressure. When deployed in a region they interconnect wirelessly to form a sensor network that may include a few devices or scale to many thousands across the target area. Measurements reach base stations by direct transmission from nearby nodes or by relays through intermediate nodes. Radios can use standard local area and wide area protocols suited to the environment. Deployment can be random with limited later supervision or planned to support easy supervision and reduced maintenance1. Planned or pilot deployments often achieve strong coverage with relatively few devices while random deployment can leave uncovered regions2. Sensor networks may use a flat design with little difference among nodes or a layered design with several levels that assign sensing and aggregation and administrative roles. As the field matures the choice between planned placement and random placement and the choice of a suitable system architecture remain central decisions that ensure effective monitoring3. At the same time research on artificial neural networks is extensive and often difficult to transfer into practice and active studies appear in disease modeling and in models of random processes and in deterministic chaos and in other complex systems4–6.

In earlier centuries many communities faced severe outbreaks of influenza and malaria and cholera when medical knowledge was limited and transmission was rapid. People recognized that protection after recovery could weaken and that vulnerability could return7,8. A widespread belief held that recovered individuals were no longer infectious and close contact then increased spread in many settings. Over time advances in biomedical science altered epidemic response as diagnostics improved and vaccines emerged and treatments advanced and public health practice became more coordinated. The eighteenth century introduced a shift in mathematics as real populations began to be described by explicit equations that started with simple growth patterns and then moved to nonlinear systems and later to predator and prey dynamics. By the late twentieth century these developments produced compartmental models that organized individuals by infection status and created a common framework for modern epidemic analysis. Subsequent research examined how infections propagate in populations and how behavior and awareness modify that process and how model behavior remains stable or changes under different conditions9–11. These models were adapted to many contexts that include pine wilt and leishmaniasis and HIV and tuberculosis and other viral infections and COVID 1912–14. This broad work identified threshold conditions and clarified long term dynamics and guided effective control strategies15,16. As the field matured researchers incorporated age structure and measures of predictability and qualitative dynamics through bifurcations and detailed predator and prey interactions15,17,18. Related methodologies then informed studies outside communicable disease and described social processes such as smoking behavior and alcohol use in distinct populations. These advances show that mathematical modeling can reveal and organize complex behavior in many domains and can support sound decisions in science and policy.

Fractional calculus extends classical differentiation and integration to real orders and captures memory and hereditary effects that integer order models do not represent well. Recent studies introduced new operator definitions and broadened admissible kernels beyond simple power laws19–22. This expansion supports wider physical and biological modeling and motivates new nonlinear differential and integral formulations23–25. Further developments proposed additional operator variants that enhance flexibility for complex dynamics26. Exact solutions are rare for these models, so researchers rely on numerical schemes and approximation methods that reproduce intricate behavior such as chaotic limit cycles. Atangana presented operators with fractal derivatives that combine power law, modified exponential decay, and Mittag Leffler patterns27. Applications in cyber epidemiology established conditions for existence, equilibria, stability, and reproduction thresholds within the Atangana and Baleanu framework28. An antivirus model on a computer network produced a nonautonomous ordinary differential formulation from a fractional system and reported numerical results under Caputo and Caputo Fabrizio settings29. A fractional computer virus epidemiology model provided existence results and numerical experiments30. Qualitative analysis of fractional computer virus spread examined existence, uniqueness, and numerical behavior31. A wireless sensor network model verified the reproduction threshold and stability properties32. Existence and uniqueness for a fractional computer virus model with a Mittag Leffler kernel were established using fixed point techniques33.

Fixed-point theory provides a unified analytical mechanism for proving well-posedness of nonlinear dynamical models by converting the governing equations into an operator equation of the form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z=\mathcal {T}z$$\end{document} on a suitable function space. In fractional settings, this viewpoint is particularly natural because the nonlocal operator often admits an equivalent Volterra-type integral representation, so existence can be obtained via compactness-based principles and uniqueness via contractive mappings. Beyond existence and uniqueness, fixed-point estimates supply explicit bounds that are routinely reused in stability analysis and in the justification of iterative numerical schemes.

Recent developments illustrate the breadth of fixed-point techniques across domains and modeling paradigms, including common fixed-point results in generalized metric structures34, contractive frameworks with applications to boundary value problems35, and fractional epidemic modeling where existence–uniqueness is established via fixed-point arguments36. Fixed-point ideas also appear in data driven and applied analyses connected to epidemic dynamics37 and in fractional-order neural-network models where stability and numerical behavior are derived through contractive mappings38.

The motivation of this study is threefold. First, malware transmission in wireless sensor networks is inherently history-dependent, so classical Markovian models may underestimate persistence and delayed effects. Second, the proportional Hadamard–Caputo operator offers a suitable mechanism to encode memory on a logarithmic time scale. Third, we aim to provide a unified analytical–computational framework that ensures well-posedness and stability while remaining numerically implementable via a predictor–corrector scheme. This study develops a fractional malware model for wireless sensor networks that encodes memory through the proportional Hadamard Caputo operator. The network state is organized into six device classes which are susceptible, exposed, infectious, quarantined, recovered, and vaccinated. The differential system is rewritten as an equivalent Volterra integral equation under a logarithmic change of time which makes the role of memory transparent and supports the later analysis. Existence of solutions is obtained through the nonlinear Leray Schauder alternative and uniqueness is established by the Banach contraction principle. Rigorous bounds demonstrate Ulam Hyers stability and its extended form so small modeling errors lead to proportionally small deviations of the solutions. A modified Adams–Bashforth–Moulton predictor–corrector is built for the proportional kernel, yielding weights that are simple to implement and encode exponential memory in logarithmic time. Computational simulations show a clear memory effect since stronger memory or a smaller fractional order slows decline and prolongs the active spread phase while orders that approach the classical case produce rapid stabilization. Together these elements form an integrated analytical and computational framework that explains how fractional memory shapes malware propagation and that provides a practical tool for control design in sensor networks.

This article moves from foundations to analysis, computation, and conclusions in a clear sequence. Section “Preliminaries” presents the background on fractional integration and differentiation and introduces the proportional Hadamard Caputo operators used later. Section “Description of the model” builds the wireless sensor network malware model with six device classes, defines the contact factor, reformulates the dynamics with proportional memory on a logarithmic time scale, and provides a table that explains each state and parameter. Section “Main results” sets the functional framework and assumptions, proves existence by the nonlinear Leray–Schauder alternative, establishes uniqueness through Banach’s contraction, and derives Ulam–Hyers and extended Ulam–Hyers stability. Section “Numerical simulations” develops a predictor and corrector method of the modified Adams Bashforth Moulton type adapted to the proportional kernel and reports simulations on how memory strength and the proportional parameter shape the system. Section “Conclusion” gives a brief conclusion and points to future directions.

Conclusion

This paper develops a six-compartment malware propagation model for wireless sensor networks under the proportional Hadamard–Caputo (pHC) operator. The proposed framework is most suitable when the malware dynamics exhibit history-dependence, such as delayed patching, persistent contacts, or non-Markovian containment effects, where an integer-order model may underestimate persistence. By recasting the system as an equivalent Volterra integral equation on a logarithmic time scale, we established well-posedness (existence via the nonlinear Leray–Schauder alternative and uniqueness via Banach’s contraction principle) and proved Ulam–Hyers-type stability, which quantifies robustness of trajectories under small data, modeling, or implementation perturbations.

A modified Adams–Bashforth–Moulton predictor–corrector scheme was constructed for the pHC kernel. In simulations, fractional regimes (with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha <1$$\end{document} and/or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta <1$$\end{document}) typically lead to slower transients and longer persistence compared with the classical limit \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\alpha ,\beta )=(1,1)$$\end{document}, while preserving an implementable time-stepping structure. The main advantages of the approach are the tunable and interpretable memory control through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\alpha ,\beta )$$\end{document} together with rigorous robustness guarantees; the main trade-offs are the added calibration effort for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\alpha ,\beta )$$\end{document} and higher computational cost than standard ODE solvers. Hence, the integer-order model is preferable when memory effects are negligible or fast real-time computation is the priority, whereas the pHC model should be preferred when delayed effects and long tails are observed and conservative planning is required. For \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta =1$$\end{document}, the framework reduces to the Hadamard–Caputo setting and recovers the special-case results reported in45.

Future work will address parameter identification of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\alpha ,\beta )$$\end{document} from network traces, systematic sensitivity and uncertainty quantification, and the design of mitigation and control strategies that explicitly account for fractional-memory effects. Additional directions include extensions with time varying topology, communication delays, stochastic disturbances, and benchmarking against alternative fractional operators.
