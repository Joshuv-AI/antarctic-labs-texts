Perspectives on workplaces choices among physicians and nurses in peripheral hospitals
Authors: Orna Tal, Esti Reic, Yanit Avitan, Sarit Azar, Galit Hasin, Michal Bitan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe shortage of healthcare professionals and the need for committed hospital workers is a global challenge, particularly in peripheral and rural areas, where workforce distribution disparities lead to unequal access to care. Various factors influence healthcare workers’ choice of workplace, including external factors (residency location, economic incentives, family needs) and internal motivations such as sense of duty, perception of accomplishment, sense of belonging, social cohesiveness, professional and academic aspirations, and perceived opportunities. This descriptive cross-sectional study aimed to describe patterns of workplace considerations among physicians and nurses in two peripheral hospitals and to explore differences by profession and hospital context.MethodsDuring January-February 2023 a survey was conducted in two public hospitals in southern country district, comprising 42 questions across five domains: career opportunities, initial incentives, personal/family-based considerations, values, and demographics.ResultsMajor incentives for workplace choices in peripheral hospitals include a sense of mission, professional enthusiasm and organizational values. Physicians prioritized unique specialties and academic engagement, while nurses emphasized professional teamwork and a sense of duty. Workers from the larger hospital scored accomplishment and prestige as significant influential factors.ConclusionsChoices of peripheral hospital workplaces are shaped by personal and professional factors, including a sense of mission, self-empowerment, governmental economic incentives and lifestyle considerations. These domains shape workplace preferences among workers in two peripheral hospitals, by professional, organizational and personal factors. These findings improve insight, generate hypotheses and inform locally tailored workforce policies, and may further contribute to national strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14763-0.Keywords: Workforce, Preferences, Peripheral hospitals, Professional perceptions

Background

The demand for well-trained, dedicated hospital professionals is a global challenge driven by population growth, aging chronically ill patients, and innovative medical technologies. Unexpected events, such as pandemics, natural disasters, and military conflicts further strain healthcare systems by increasing the demand for specialists in specific fields. Thus, across all socioeconomic levels healthcare systems encounter challenges, to varying extents, in workforce education, employment, distribution, performance and retention [1] struggling to avoid compromising patient care. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) estimate, there will be a shortfall of 11 million health workers by 2030, mostly in low- and lower-middle income countries [1].

Disparities in access to healthcare between rural and urban areas exist around the world, particularly in developing countries. Although half of the world’s population lives in rural areas, only 23% of the global health workforce is deployed to such areas [2]. While this is particularly enhanced in low- and middle-income countries, as in the Asia-Pacific region [3], such gaps also exist in Canada and in European countries, reported in: Hungary, Norway, France, Germany, and the United Kingdom [4]. The uneven distribution of the healthcare workforce can limit access to physicians [5], reducing the use of health services and impacting patient outcomes. Studies in Germany [6], Russia [7], and the United States [8] have reported disparities in health indicators between rural and urban populations. To address these gaps and promote healthcare equity, medical authorities aim to encourage physicians to work in peripheral hospitals, many countries struggle to bridge the gap in physician availability between rural and urban areas [4].

Israel faces similar challenges. Its publicly funded health system comprises 24 general hospitals, yet only seven are located in peripheral northern (4 hospitals) and southern districts (3 hospitals). Consequently, accessibility in these regions is reduced. While hospital managers strive to attract specialists, foster academic activity, and maintain excellence in care, many professionals hesitate to relocate or work in peripheral hospitals. Physician density in central Israel is 5.0 per 1000 residents compared to only 1.1 per 1000 in the southern district (OECD, 2021).

The medical leadership worldwide, including Israel, is facing physician attrition due to burnout, lack of residents’ positions, emigration for training abroad, and an aging physician population cohort [9]. Israel has the highest percentage of medical students studying abroad among Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries [10] and a growing percentage of elder physicians that tend to retire within 5 years [11]. Moreover, governmental incentives to lure medical residents to peripheral districts are limited, particularly secondary to security threats in these areas, that pose a barrier to choosing a workplace by young families.

The specialization education track also differs between hospitals and community training. Naturally, central large hospitals offer extensive clinical exposure and innovative treatment opportunities. Moreover, residents and interns that are posed in central hospitals due to mandatory training requirements prefer to continue working in these hospitals after their residency [12] and avoid choosing peripheral hospital although available. To address this, the Israeli government and the Israeli Medical Association (IMA) have introduced incentives to encourage healthcare professionals to work in peripheral areas, both during training and as specialists [13–15].

Although demand for central hospital positions exceeds supply, since 1976, national guidelines are used to limit the adjusted staff per bed ratio [16]. Accordingly, there is a formula to calculate the accumulative number of physicians to hospital size. This regulatory threshold limits the number of available positions in central hospitals, even medical graduates apply, while in peripheral hospitals a shortage of physicians is an ongoing challenge. Hospital directors constantly seek for residents and experts to feel this gap.

To explore real world evidence regarding incentives and considerations, we conducted a descriptive, cross‑sectional survey among physicians and nurses in two peripheral hospitals in southern Israel and to compare perceptions by profession and hospital.

The aim of this study was to explore professionals’ considerations and workforce patterns t peripheral hospital settings.

Methods

Design, setting and participants

This study consisted of a descriptive cross-sectional survey among physicians and nurses working in two public general hospitals in the southern district of the country: a large tertiary 1200-bed hospital (Hospital A) located in the central southern regional city, and a medium size 560-bed hospital (Hospital B) located in a southern coastal city. Both hospitals are located in peripheral areas at a considerable distance (approximately 63–113 km) from the central metropolitan region, and the geographic distance between them is 72 km. Both hospitals are considered “first-line emergency medical centers” and provide similar ER and hospitalization health services without discrimination to all patients within the framework of National Insurance Coverage. However, they differ in size (number of beds and personal) and complexity of services provided (such as neurosurgery).

The population surrounding both hospitals has low socio-economic status (5 of 10 on the national scale) [17], with a large share of minorities, including Bedouin, Arabs and Ethiopian-Israelis [18].

During January–February 2023, members of the research team personally approached staff in wards and clinical departments, briefly explained the objectives of the study and invited them to complete an anonymous paper-based questionnaire. aiming to maximize compliance. All physicians and nurses employed at the two hospitals were eligible to participate in the survey, and recruitment procedure constitutes a non-probability convenience sample based on voluntary participation; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as description of responding population rather than as statistically representation of the entire hospital workforce and cannot be generalized.

The target sample size was 150 participants in each hospital (75 physicians and 75 nurses), based on a priori power considerations. Assuming a two-sided alpha of 0.05, a power of 80% and equal group sizes, this sample was calculated to detect medium-sized differences in mean workplace consideration scores between professional groups and between hospitals. Due to feasibility and anticipated response rates, the expected number of completed questionnaires was approximately 100 participants per hospital.

Study tools and data collection

Survey

A structured questionnaire was personally disseminated during January and February 2023. The questionnaire was specifically developed for this study by the research team. The full survey instrument is available as Additional file in the supplementary materials. It comprised 42 items across five domains: The first four sections examined the considerations of choosing a workplace: (a) career opportunities (items 1–8) (b) incentives (items 9–15), (c) external and family consideration (items 16–19), and (d) personal preferences and values (items 20–23). The participants were asked to rank each item on a scale ranging from 1 (least important) to 10 (most important). The fifth section of the questionnaire comprised questions on participant demographics.

One central variable was ‘Concept of Mission’ (COM) defined as enthusiasm, commitment and devotion toward one’s workplace and task [19], representing pride of belonging or being educated as [20] a member of the profession and organization, overarching policy objective aimed at tackling a societal challenge within a specified timeframe [21].

This definition was explained to the participants prior to the response.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and professional characteristics of participants, stratified by hospital and by profession. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. Given the non-probability sampling, all analyses are primarily descriptive and exploratory in nature. Group comparisons using non-parametric tests and multivariable regression models are intended to highlight patterns within the participating workforce, rather than to support formal population-level inference.

Differences between hospitals and between professions were assessed using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables (Fisher’s exact test was applied when any expected cell frequency was < 5), and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (for two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (for more than two groups) for continuous variables. These tests were also used to examine differences in workplace choice considerations by hospital and by profession.

For each workplace consideration score, we fitted a multivariable linear regression model including profession (nurse vs. physician) being the main independent variable, adjusted for gender, age, and marital status, known to be associated with both professional role and work–family preferences.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics of the study population

A total of 307 participants completed the survey: 151 physicians and 156 nurses with no statistically significant differences between the hospitals in the number of professionals in each group (Hospital A: 75 (50%) physicians and 75 (50%) nurses. Hospital B: 76 (48.7%) physicians and 80 (51.3%) nurses) (Table 1). Physicians were predominantly male (66.2%), while most nurses were female (85.2%), with no significant differences between the two hospitals. The mean age was 39.3 years (SD 9.2) with no statistically significant differences between the professions; however, the respondents working in Hospital A were statistically significantly older compared to those working in Hospital B (41.8 [SD 9.4] vs. 36.8 [SD 8.3]. p < 0.001).

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants by hospital and profession

Two-thirds of respondents (65.5%) were married and most (76.7%) were Jewish (65.3% of physicians and 88.3% of nurses, p < 0.001). Most respondents (68.1%) lived in urban areas with no statistically significant differences between physicians and nurses.

Over half of the respondents (51.8%) reported working in surgical departments and 25.1% reported working in internal medicine or pediatric departments. The respondents in Hospital A had statistically significantly greater professional experience compared to those in Hospital B (12.9 years [SD 8.7] vs. 8.2 years [SD 8.2], p < 0.001). Most of the participants (90.2%) were involved in daily clinical work, 9.8% were managers or administrators, with no differences between hospitals.

Almost half of the respondents (49.0%) working in Hospital B and 56% of those working in Hospital A resided less than 20 min away from the hospital, while 26.5% and 37.3% of those working in Hospital B and Hospital A, respectively, resided 20–40 min away. A statistically significantly higher proportion of nurses lived less than 20 min away from the hospital compared to physicians (62.6% vs. 42.3%, p < 0.001).

Workplace considerations

Analysis of workplace preference considerations (Table 2) revealed that the most important factors influencing workplace choice among the participants were professional activity (mean 7.6, SD 2.3) and salary (mean 7.4, SD 2.4). Followed by the concept of mission, workplace values and flexibility of shifts scored 7.2 [SD 2.8, 2.7. 2,5 respectively]. Social factor, such as sense of cohesiveness were rated less important across both hospitals (means: 4.7, SD 2.2 and 4.4, SD 3.1).

Differences between the two hospitals presenting family reasons held greater importance among Hospital B workers compared to Hospital A (6.0 [SD 3.4] vs. 5.0 [SD 2.6], p = 0.009). Prestige, concept of mission, and workplace values appeared to be more important by Hospital A respondents compared to Hospital B and these differences were statistically significant (prestige: mean 8.5, SD 1.7 vs. 5.3, SD 2.8; p < 0.001; concept of mission: 8.5, SD 1.6 vs. 6.1, SD 3.1; workplace values: 8.5, SD 1.6 vs. 5.9, SD 3.0).

Comparison between professions indicated no statistically significant differences in the value assigned to professional elements (perceived professional level, clinical interest, academic opportunities, adoption of innovations) or to social factors (family and peer influence, self-fulfillment). However, physicians rated the hospital’s research capacity, sense of cohesiveness, and opportunities to specialize in unique departments more highly than nurses (research: mean 6.5, SD 2.7 vs. 4.4, SD 2.6; p < 0.001; cohesiveness: 5.0, SD 2.6 vs. 4.2, SD 2.8; p = 0.007; specialization: 7.7, SD 2.4 vs. 6.4, SD 2.8; p < 0.001). Different importance was noted by nurses, emphasizing family reasons and geographic location (family: 6.0, SD 3.2 vs. 4.9, SD 2.8; p = 0.003; location: 6.8, SD 3.1 vs. 5.1, SD 2.8; p < 0.001).

Multivariable analyses

In multivariable models adjusted for gender, age and marital status, showed a similar pattern comparing physicians and nurses resembling the unadjusted analyses regarding most workplace considerations. Family-related reasons that presented differences between physicians and nurses in the crude analysis, was attenuated and became non‑significant after adjustment. In these models, age emerged as the main predictor: older participants assigned higher importance to family-related reasons when choosing a workplace, independent of profession and gender (β = 0.04, p = 0.042). In contrast, for perceived professional level, profession became a statistically significant predictor only after adjustment (β = 0.89, p = 0.004), suggesting that demographic adjustment revealed a professional difference that was not presented in the crude comparisons (Table 3).

Discussion

Peripheral hospital managers, challenged by shortage of professional personnel, tend to relay on external barriers such as geographic distance, un favored working or residency conditions, work overload and undertreated patients’ complexity. This study offers a more comprehensive insight into components influencing workplace choices among physicians and nurses already occupied in two peripheral hospitals. Although the hospitals differ in size and performance, the sociodemographic characteristics of the workers (age, gender, marital status and religion) reflect similar workers’ characteristic although professionals in the larger, tertiary hospital were older and more experienced. These findings, although significant may seed from the sampling methodology, using on a convenience sample and may not be generalized to a broader perspective.

Our primarily assumption was that differences will be found between professions (physicians versus nurses), not between hospitals, as we choose two hospitals located in the same district of the country (the southern periphery), that operate under the same conditions and regulations (public funding), provider care to similar populations, facing a similar environment (ecosystem) and exposed to similar terror threats.

We revealed workplace preferences were primarily guided by a mixture of professional activity, remuneration and values such as concept of mission and organizational culture. While these priorities were consistent across professions, inter-institutional differences were evident: workers in Hospital A placed significantly greater value on prestige, mission, and workplace values compared to Hospital B. This suggests that hospital size, complexity, and perceived professional status as steering healthcare leadership may shape the motivational landscape for clinical associates, particularly in environments with differentiated opportunities for specialization and advancement. Further, the finding that nurses prioritize family reasons and proximity to the workplace more than physicians underscores the impact of personal circumstances on occupational decision-making. Physicians, in contrast, displayed greater preference for research capacity, unique specialization opportunities, and team cohesiveness, pointing to a professional orientation toward academic development and organizational integration. These patterns are likely influenced by the peripheral location of both hospitals at a considerable distance from the central metropolitan region, where clinicians must weigh the professional advantages of working in a regional tertiary hub or medium-sized general hospital against commuting time, family needs and distant residency. This geographic context may partly explain why some participants emphasized values and aspirations like prestige and mission, while others carried more practical thoughts prioritizing family needs and commuting considerations.

The multivariable analyses provide additional nuance to these patterns. Family-related reasons showed difference between physicians and nurses in the crude analysis that disappeared once age and marital status were included in the model. This indicates that family, rather than profession, is the dominant incentive in workplace choice. We noticed that older clinicians, both physicians and nurses are more influenced by family-related factors, In contrast, the fact that perceived professional level became significantly associated with profession only after demographic adjustment suggests that variability in age and marital status had partly masked an underlying professional contrast. Once this demographic variability was accounted for, the distinct professional orientations of physicians and nurses with respect to perceived professional level became clearer, reinforcing the idea that professional identity and career trajectories shape how clinicians evaluate the professional attributes of peripheral workplaces.

Social factors, including sense of team and influence of peers or family, were generally scored of lower importance for workplace preferences, however, geographic and family considerations remained relevant, particularly for nurses and older staff. These trends highlight the need for tailored recruitment and retention strategies that address both professional and personal motivators, meeting institutional characteristics and requirements.

Collectively, these findings highlight the complexity of workforce dynamics in peripheral hospitals and underscore the importance of aligning human resource policies with the diverse needs and aspirations of clinical staff.

Conclusions

Our research demonstrates that workplace preferences among physicians and nurses in peripheral Israeli hospitals are shaped by a multiple ideas, perceptions, aspirations and values of professional, organizational, and personal factors. Key motivators, including clinical professionalism, earning potential, concept of mission, and workplace culture, feature prominently across both hospitals and professions. However, institutional characteristics such as prestige and opportunities for advancement, as well as personal considerations like proximity to home and family needs, also play significant roles and vary in relative importance for physicians versus nurses.

Workers in larger hospitals tend to prioritize prestige, mission, and organizational culture, whereas workers in smaller medical facilities value family-related factors more strongly. Physicians are more likely to seek research opportunities, specialization, and team cohesiveness, seeding from their education designing role features that prevail over their personal needs. In comparison, nurses emphasize geographic location and family considerations. These differentiated patterns reflect the need for targeted workforce strategies.

Efforts to address workforce shortages and retention in peripheral regions should therefore be multifactorial, integrating organizational incentives, opportunities for academic and professional growth, and support for personal and family needs. Recognizing and responding to these nuanced preferences will be essential for sustaining a committed and effective healthcare workforce in underserved areas. The healthcare leadership should prioritize closing the gaps of medical professionals, especially in health systems with scares resources, high demand and multiple strains.

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations to this study. First, the cross-sectional design captures workplace preferences at a single point in time and cannot account for changes in attitudes or motivations that might occur longitudinally. Second, data were obtained through a self-administered survey, which may introduce response bias. Third, participants were recruited using non-probability convenience sampling based on voluntary participation. As a result, selection bias cannot be excluded, and the findings should be interpreted as descriptive of respondents from the two hospitals rather than as statistically representative of all physicians and nurses working in peripheral settings. The study was conducted in two hospitals located within the same geographic district enabling the comparison, yet, the results may not be representative or comparable to other peripheral regions or diverse healthcare settings. Moreover, the sample size, while substantial, may still be insufficient to capture smaller subgroups or rare perspectives among hospital staff, especially as rural hospitals may occupy a relatively high percentage of workers from minority groups that are less responsive to surveys. Lastly, the character and nature of the healthcare system and the hospitals may vary between countries, as well as local specific policy guidelines so that generalization of findings should be carefully addressed. Future research is required to fully interpreted these findings using mixed‑methods designs, combining multi‑center and longitudinal approaches with qualitative components analyzing open‑ended questions or interviews. This may deepen the understanding of the motivations behind the quantitative patterns observed and to further validate and refine context‑sensitive recruitment and retention strategies.
