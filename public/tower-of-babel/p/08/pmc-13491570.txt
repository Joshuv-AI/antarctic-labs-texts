Five Milestones to Addressing Rural Veteran Care Coordination, Care Management, and Case Management Needs: An Integrated Case Management Practice Framework
Authors: Christine Melillo, Heidi Sjoberg, Marcie Lee, Cristina Parsons C, Claire Palmer E, Etana Disasa, Theodore Warsavage, Laura Williams, Elizabeth Sprinkle, Kimberly S. Hall, Francesca Bryan‐Couch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeVeterans often experience fragmented care across clinics and health systems, with rural veterans facing additional challenges such as navigating community care. To address these needs, the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) implemented Care Coordination and Integrated Case Management (CCICM), a standardized, clinical practice framework that assigns high‐needs veterans a lead coordinator to assess needs, coordinate services, and reduce fragmentation. Evidence describing CCICM implementation in rural settings is limited. This project examined CCICM implementation across rural facilities.MethodsThis multi‐methods project assessed CCICM implementation at six rural VHA facilities participating in an Office of Rural Health Enterprise‐Wide Initiative (EWI) October 2024 through September 2025. Implementation proceeded through five milestones: (1) Leadership Awareness; (2) Facility Readiness; (3) Preparing to Implement; (4) Systems and Clinical Integration; and (5) Establishment of a Governance Structure. Structure and process evaluation used quantitative data and qualitative interviews, focus groups, and observations. VA Generative Pre‐trained Transformer transcript summarization with human review supported rapid matrix content analysis.FindingsAll six sites achieved clinical integration; one site will not continue due to staffing constraints. Over 80% of veterans identified for care coordination had moderate or complex, most commonly medical or physical, needs. Veterans assigned a Lead Coordinator (n = 245) were predominantly older, male, White, and rural. Four elements associated with successful implementation were strong leadership, effective change‐management, clear communication, and tailored veteran engagement. Enhanced EWI supports facilitated workflow alignment and sustainability planning.ConclusionsStructured, leadership‐supported system changes can improve rural veteran care coordination. CCICM offers a reproducible model for coordinated care across diverse settings.

Introduction

Veterans receiving care through the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) often have needs distinct from those of non‐veterans [1]. Military exposures contribute to higher rates of morbidity and mortality [2, 3, 4]. VHA clinicians address co‐occurring conditions—such as traumatic brain injury, chronic pain, and mental health disorders—within the context of military culture, values, and preferences. VHA clinicians coordinate care across clinics and healthcare systems. This specialized approach is essential veteran‐centered treatment [5].

Nearly 3 million rural veterans rely on VHA and Non‐VHA healthcare services [6]. Non‐VHA providers and healthcare systems deliver community care services in veterans’ local areas. Rural veterans face access barriers, including navigating community care, traveling long distances for specialty services, and limited access to telehealth and reliable internet [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. These barriers increase the need for care management, specifically care coordination and integrated case management. Although these strategies overlap, each includes distinct components that require structured assessments, methods, and documentation. Table 1 provides descriptions for each approach.

To meet these needs—particularly among rural veterans—VHA developed a national practice change: Care Coordination and Integrated Case Management (CCICM) [12]. CCICM is a clinical practice framework led by staff and committees to reduce care fragmentation. Local facility staff identify veterans who may benefit from CCICM through risk stratification and assessment of care coordination needs then meet as a multidisciplinary team to temporarily assign a Lead Coordinator for the veteran. Because rural facilities operate in different contexts, VHA program offices partnered with the Office of Rural Health (ORH) to provide additional support to selected sites. ORH Enterprise‑Wide Initiatives (EWIs) expand national access efforts in facilities that serve rural veterans. The CCICM EWI advances VHA and ORH priorities by improving access to care, strengthening care coordination, and supporting care tailored to rural communities. Rural EWI sites received additional support to clinically integrate CCICM, as well as evaluation of implementation and sustainability. This manuscript outlines CCICM implementation steps at six rural facilities participating in the ORH EWI, describes veteran cohorts, and presents PRISM‐informed findings from staff interviews.

Methods

Project Design

This implementation project used a multi‐methods approach. The CCICM program office led the implementation effort, using the Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement (ADKAR) change management model [13]. Evaluation methods leveraged Practical, Robust, Implementation, and Sustainability Model (PRISM) [14, 15, 16] due to its focus on multi‐level shareholder engagement and large‐scale innovation assessment. This manuscript follows Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQuIRE) 2.0 (Supplement A) [17].

Implementation Methods

The national CCICM program office led implementation at the six selected ORH EWI sites. CCICM subject matter experts applied evidence‐based interventions and change‐management principles to create a standardized implementation structure. Strategies such as engaged leadership and enhanced communication [18] were integrated into the implementation structure to support inter‐professional collaboration and reduce redundancy.

All five milestones (Figure 2) were completed resulting in clinical integration at each site. Each milestone included critical actions.

CCICM Five Milestones and Critical Actions to practice implementation.

A national dashboard completed by each facility tracked facility progress. The CCICM Clinical Integration Implementation Guide (Appendix 1) outlines each milestone and was provided to Co‐Coordinators to guide actions. Requirements for each milestone (Figure 2) are outlined below.

Milestone 1: Leadership Awareness

Leadership awareness cultivates facility awareness by building knowledge of CCICM outcomes and benefits to the organization. Four critical actions were required to enhance leadership awareness and understanding of CCICM. This milestone was intended to support engagement in CCICM education and collaborative opportunities and enhance buy‐in from executive leadership.

Milestone 2: Facility Readiness

Facility readiness focuses on evaluating how prepared the facility is to implement and clinically integrate CCICM. Six critical actions were embedded into this milestone. An Executive Sponsor and two Co‐Coordinators were selected at each site to implement and clinically integrate CCICM.

Milestone 3: Preparing to Implement

Milestone 3 consists of three critical actions. Leadership and frontline staff collaborated to determine best strategies for CCICM implementation within their facility. Educational needs were identified and CCICM informative activities and materials were distributed.

Milestone 4: Systems and Clinical Integration

The Co‐Coordinators and Executive Sponsor at each site developed a process to standardize veteran risk stratification. Some veterans were identified by consultation and others through predictive analytics tools. The Complexity Assessment Review (CAR), a standardized assessment tool, was completed by clinicians to determine the veteran's most prominent area of care management need (e.g., self‐management, mental health/behavioral health, social, and medical/physical) and stratify the veteran's care coordination needs as basic, moderate, or complex. Veterans with moderate and complex scores were reviewed and assigned an appropriate LC at a multidisciplinary Care Coordination Review Team (CCRT) meeting.

Milestone 5: Establish Governance Structure

Key shareholders within all available clinical programs were represented as members of the facility CCICM Committee. The committee supported clinical integration, provided facility‐wide education, completed annual resource surveys, monitored facility metrics, evaluated veteran outcomes, reviewed monthly data, and identified areas for enhancement. The committee regularly reported to site executive leadership.

Completion of Milestone 5 is designated as “full implementation.” Clinical integration is one step beyond full implementation and includes an active CCICM committee and at least monthly CCRTs with LC assignments.

National Data Infrastructure

National CCICM developed a comprehensive data infrastructure to support implementation and sustainment that features Power BI, Palantir Technologies's Foundry (Palantir Foundry), and PowerApps. These platforms integrate veteran, facility, and national data from the Veterans Administration Corporate Data Warehouse, a comprehensive veteran and administrative data repository accessed for business intelligence and analytics purposes. The CCICM Dashboard (Power BI) was established to provide close to real‐time reporting on metrics, such as the number of veterans assigned lead coordinators and veteran engagement levels. Routine reports included comparisons of emergency department and inpatient utilization, readmissions, and Ambulatory Care Sensitive Conditions (ACSC) Admissions. Palantir Foundry software enables complex data integration and was primarily used for identifying veterans who could benefit from care coordination by capturing service usage data in both VHA and non‐VHA facilities. Finally, PowerApps were established to standardize documentation of veteran identification, outcomes, and Co‐Coordinator non‐clinical workload (e.g., tracking logs).

Additional EWI‐Specific Activities

Implementation at the EWI sites included all the above implementation and infrastructure supports. EWI sites received additional enhanced support, including full‐time nurse and social work Co‐Coordinator salaries, tailored data tools, and consultative site visits. EWI site co‐coordinators were required to complete additional documentation including a veteran tracking log, which contains standardized information from veteran identification through outcomes of Lead Coordinator assignment. EWI site co‐coordinators were required to complete routine chart audits to confirm fidelity to standard CCICM practices. Additional routine meetings were used to develop community, share outcomes, and learn from other co‐coordinators. Mentorship plans were developed for each site to provide guided support to co‐coordinators to develop a dyad relationship, establish function clarity and communicate within their facility. Mentorship plans paired each co‐coordinator dyad with national CCICM staff to build relationships and improve CCICM integration at facilities.

Evaluation Methods

The interdisciplinary evaluation team includes nurses, one social worker, one occupational therapist, statisticians, programmers, qualitative researchers, implementation scientists, and evaluation experts. Each member of the evaluation team has multiple years of experience within the VHA system. The evaluation team used a multi‐methods approach to evaluate the implementation and sustainability of the CCICM initiative at six rural sites participating in the ORH EWI throughout fiscal year 2025 (October 2024 to September 2025). Each participating site included at least 51% of catchment veterans living in rural or highly rural communities. Rurality within the VA is classified following Rural–Urban Commuting Area Codes [19]. Working in collaboration with Co‐Coordinators and national CCICM team leads, the evaluation was guided by the RE‐AIM framework [20]. The evaluation team adopted a pragmatic approach focusing on outcomes (what works, what does not work) while understanding unique, lived experiences are key to actionable knowledge informing adoption and maintenance of interventions [21]. Individual outcomes and experiences were explored through interviews, focus groups, observations and relationship building. This evaluation protocol was reviewed by the VA Eastern Colorado Health Care System regulatory board and determined to be non‐research.

Data Collection and Analysis

Primary and secondary data were collected October 2024 through March 2026.

Quantitative

Data were collected and summarized at the site level, using the CCICM Power BI Dashboard based on the Re‐engineered Primary Care Management Module and Text Integration Utilities domains, along with other commonly used Corporate Data Warehouse domains. These sources captured structured and unstructured data such as veteran demographics, CCICM team member identifiers, encounter‐level details, and clinical documentation. Data tracking begins upon identification of potential care coordination needs through risk stratification or referral and is logged in the Power BI dashboard.

All data describe the active panel at the time of the writing of this paper, March 2026. Demographic characteristics of veterans engaged in CCICM include age, sex, and race. Rurality of veterans assigned a lead coordinator is reported as a discrete count and percentage of the active panel.

Qualitative

Representatives on the CCICM national implementation team and evaluation qualitative team conducted two‐day site visits at six ORH EWI rural sites between October 2024 and September 2025 to explore determinants of CCICM implementation and clinical integration through observations, scheduled events, informal conversations, interviews, and focus groups. The qualitative evaluation team developed semi‐structured guides informed by PRISM constructs (Appendices 2 and 3) to conduct interviews and focus groups [14, 15, 16, 22, 23, 24].

Sampling and Recruitment

The qualitative evaluation tasked Co‐Coordinators to provide a list of individuals who were instrumental in CCICM implementation at all levels. Analysts populated these lists with email addresses and used them for purposive sampling followed by snowball sampling. Participants were invited to one of two focus groups: a “Manager‐Supervisor Focus group” or a “Lead Coordinator Focus group” depending on their role. If a participant held both a Manager‐Supervisor role and a Lead Coordinator role, they were invited to choose which data collection event to attend, ensuring not to include the participant's supervisor in the same focus group if possible. Select individuals were invited to an individual interview based on their unique contributions to implementation of CCICM (e.g., Co‐Coordinators, seminal leaders, department buy‐in, LC experience) and personal scheduling preferences.

Data collection: Interviews and focus groups were conducted virtually via Microsoft Teams or in person. Sessions were recorded using Microsoft Teams or a secure, password‐protected recording device. Audio recordings were stored behind the VHA firewall, de‐identified, and transcribed verbatim.

Data Management

To manage the large volume of qualitative data efficiently while maintaining analytic rigor, Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) Generative Pre‐trained Transformer (GPT) was selected as a tool to support rapid qualitative data management because it supports timely generation of structured summaries while preserving key content and context [25]. VA GPT was used per VA Guidance [26] to generate preliminary transcript summaries. This approach is consistent with emerging best practices for integrating artificial intelligence (AI)‐assisted methods in qualitative research, particularly for large‐scale implementation studies [27, 28]. The prompt “I need to quickly summarize this transcript using the PRISM framework. Act as a qualitative researcher and summarize this transcript, organizing information into PRISM constructs (Characteristics of Organizational Recipients, Program/Intervention‐Organizational Perspective, Implementation and Sustainability Infrastructure, Characteristics of Patient Recipients, Additional Observations and Recommendations)” was entered and individual transcripts were uploaded into VA GPT.

Data Analysis

The qualitative evaluation team employed rapid matrix content analysis with deductive coding to efficiently identify themes across interview data between site visits [29]. Rapid matrix content analysis provides efficiency and speed in understanding site experiences generating findings, which can be shared for immediate decisions and adjustments. An a priori codebook was developed based on PRISM constructs and core text segments of the interview guide. To ensure familiarity with the data and site‐level context, team members first reviewed full transcripts.

Summaries were organized in a Microsoft Excel matrix where each row represented one transcript, and each column corresponded to PRISM‐informed codes. Each cell contained corresponding VA GPT‐generated summary text that was representative of the a priori codes. An additional column captured verbatim salient quotes to facilitate accurate reporting and interpretation. Three qualitative analysts then compared GPT‐generated summary test against full transcripts to confirm fidelity and completeness and to analyze themes across sites.

The evaluation qualitative team met 1–2 h weekly to discuss coding decisions, refine themes, and resolve discrepancies. Codes were iteratively grouped into broader PRISM aligned themes. Ideas and patterns across sites were analyzed to identify themes and contextual variations.

Results

In this section, authors provide a description of our project findings, organized into two main areas: implementation findings and evaluation findings. The implementation findings explore the implementation process employed nationally, while the evaluation findings describe select outcomes of CCICM within the six EWI participating sites.

Implementation

EWI site co‐coordinators attended additional monthly community of practice calls with standardized slides to share site metrics and challenges. As the year progressed, additional mentorship needs were identified. The national implementation team developed and implemented site co‐coordinators trainings to improve leadership and communication skills. All six sites achieved clinical integration. One site will not continue in the initiative because staffing demands elsewhere prevented the site from maintaining the required two full‑time Co‑Coordinators by the end of fiscal year (FY)25.

Four actionable PRISM‐informed themes describing successful implementation and clinical integration strategies were identified: (1) leadership engagement and support; (2) supportive change management strategies; (3) effective communication; and (4) veteran engagement strategies.

Leadership Engagement and Support

When asked about their experience with CCICM, participants noted executive leadership, service chiefs, and middle‐level management support and engagement were critical elements to the success of CCICM. Leadership members were initially supportive of CCICM and took steps to apply for and agree to support the EWI at their site. Across sites, facility leadership tended to be actively engaged to coordinate resources and provide necessary support at all levels. Executive sponsors commonly facilitated policy development, conflict resolution, and inclusion of CCICM outcomes in facility‐wide communications. One staff member described this top‐down approach: We're very veteran‐focused and veteran‐centric and that flows from top down […] Dr. [redacted] and the whole [executive leadership team] team […] they're all pulling the same rope for doing what's best for the Veteran.(SW)

Support from immediate supervisors was seen as essential for effective implementation and clinical integration. Immediate supervisors of lead coordinators set the tone for how CCICM was received within their programs. Some supervisors enabled workflow adjustments and celebrated lead coordinators’ achievements. Leadership support often prompted updates to standard operating procedures and charter documents to align CCICM activities within existing workflows. In contrast, supervisors who were unclear about lead coordinator responsibilities may have withheld lead coordinator assignments or created unintentional barriers to clinical integration.

Supportive Change Management Strategies

Change management strategies were noted to be essential. These strategies included ongoing training on CCICM and VHA resources, clear role expectations, and accessible resources to smooth model of care transitions. Co‐coordinators delivered brief training, typically 15 min or less, tailored to departments and service needs and new staff concerns. Staff reported that frequent ongoing education improved understanding of CCICM and its value. Education occurred in town halls, lunch and learn sessions, and impromptu sessions. Participants also described the importance of building trust with veterans and providing consistent, supportive contact to reduce resistance to participation. A change in practice can be disruptive to a veteran's sense of routine. Taking the time to use change management techniques to build trust was seen as essential to engaging veterans in CCICM.

Effective Communication

Effective communication was foundational across all levels of staff, from national CCICM partners and facility leaders and between lead coordinators and veterans. Participants reported increased success with CCICM clinical integration when mode, frequency, or communication style were well aligned with partner needs. Co‐coordinators initiated conversations with the executive leadership team where clear communication helped secure leadership understanding and support. One Co‐Coordinator provided insight into why frequent clear communication with leadership is important: [CCICM] is different, so keep going back like [Redacted] said, it took me a number of times to really grasp [CCICM], so be willing to have conversations with senior leaders and continue to follow up with them. Veteran Engagement Strategies

Authors did not interview veterans for this manuscript; however, interview and focus group participants shared veteran feedback they had received about CCICM. Veterans at times were unsure of this new approach, and participants described several engagement strategies to increase engagement. These strategies included using veterans’ preferred communication mechanisms (phone, text), setting Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time‐Bound (SMART) goals, and supporting veteran autonomy when working with a lead coordinator. Because veterans may hesitate to express their needs, staff often relied on targeted verbal assessment techniques, particularly during telephone or telehealth encounters. One home telehealth nurse shared one of her strategies for garnering a more detailed assessment. I find that especially the elderly sometimes don't like to let on how difficult things are for them. Lead coordinators are most successful when assigned based on matching veteran needs (e.g., medical, psychosocial, mental health) to lead coordinator skill sets and existing relationship between veteran and lead coordinator. Staff reported poor veteran engagement when an unknown VHA staff member reached out to offer support. Lead coordinators create a communication plan with the veteran, which is typically a weekly phone call. Veterans are reportedly more engaged when a familiar voice is on the other end of the phone. Providing consistent, supportive contact helps build trust and reduce miscommunications.

Discussion

Our findings suggest a structured, milestone approach and infrastructure can enable clinical integration of CCICM across rural VHA facilities. These findings are consistent with recommendations from the VHA State of the Art care coordination meeting that aligning care coordination context, locus, and design features with local needs facilitates the translation of theory into practice [30]. The national CCICM infrastructure (e.g., implementation guide, communities of practice, and data dashboards) reflects a learning health system approach to measure progress and standardize core processes while tailoring workflows to rural contexts [31]. This balance of standardization and tailoring is consistent with PRISM's emphasis on integrating design with organizational context to support adoption and sustainability.

CCICM builds on VHA's established risk‐informed veteran identification processes. Predictive models such as the Care Assessment Need score reliably identify patients at increased risk of hospitalization or death, supporting the early identification and interventions core to CCICM [32]. Despite these predictive models, system‐level evidence suggests variation in care coordination delivery among high‐risk veterans: across 1.8 million high‐risk veterans, only 31% received any coordination service in the year after identification, and service varied markedly across settings [33]. Our data suggests CCICM's standardized structures directly address this inconsistency by defining who may benefit, who acts, and how progress is monitored. These results also complement the stance that community care expands care options but may increase potential risks (e.g., communication gaps, fragmented care), reinforcing the need for standardized processes and data exchange—elements embedded in CCICM [12].

Rural context may amplify communication gaps and service challenges. More than 2.7 million rural and highly rural veterans rely on VHA care, yet distance to healthcare systems, workforce constraints and digital connectivity remain persistent barriers [6]. Additionally, adults with chronic disease experience higher rates of adverse social determinants of health and health related social needs including cost barriers, transportation, and isolation which can disrupt care continuity [31]. Challenges in the rural context may include provider shortages and staff serving in multiple roles, often out of facility need and not staff choice. This project's implementation strategies are foundational to CCICM's processes and focus on rural veterans with care coordination needs complementing VHA's initiatives to engage rural veterans [34]. Project findings reinforce prior VHA work demonstrating pre‐implementation assessments, local tailoring, and continuous feedback improve feasibility and scalability of complex practice frameworks in rural settings [35]. Building on this foundation, CCICM incorporates additional structured processes that translate PRISM constructs into routine clinical practice. Literature indicates that experienced coordinators enhance integrated care experience [12]. CCICM addresses this via ongoing staff education, formalized materials, and mentorship.

Project approach to qualitative analysis also contributes to emerging methodological literature. The use of VA GPT to summarize interview transcripts was intended to save time and manage the substantial volume of qualitative data in this project [25]. AI‐assisted summarization allowed the research team to rapidly generate structured summaries while preserving key content, which facilitated timely matrix development and theme identification consistent with rapid analysis methods used in large‐scale implementation studies where rapid turnaround is critical [27, 28]. However, AI‐assisted data management methods introduce potential limitations, including risk of omission or misinterpretation of nuanced data. To mitigate these risks, all VA GPT‐generated summaries were verified against full transcripts by three experienced qualitative analysts, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus. While VA GPT improved analytic efficiency, human oversight remained essential to ensure rigor and trustworthiness. Future work should further evaluate the reliability of AI‐assisted qualitative data management and establish standards for transparency and validation in peer‐reviewed work.

This project has limitations typical of quality improvement initiatives: outcomes were descriptive and focused on early implementation and integration rather than comparative effectiveness. Resource limitations, including workforce turnover and variable staff experience with case management, may influence implementation of practice change frameworks such as CCICM. Nonetheless, the findings highlight several proactive sustainment steps for rural VHA facilities and are consistent with earlier VHA work refining care coordination initiatives [30]. Future research should incorporate veteran‐reported outcomes, examine equity across subgroups, and test combinations of care coordination and targeted social drivers of health services in rural populations [36, 37].

Conclusion

CCICM demonstrates how structured system change, supported by leadership engagement, governance, role clarity, and data‐driven feedback, can enhance care coordination for rural veterans while addressing drivers of fragmentation identified in the VHA literature. By integrating implementation science principles with standardized national infrastructure and milestones, CCICM offers a reproducible model for strengthening coordinated care in a variety of care environments.
