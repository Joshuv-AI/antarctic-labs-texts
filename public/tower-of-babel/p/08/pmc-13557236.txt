Dermoscopy of Transient Lingual Papillitis: The ‘Jellyfish in a Sea of Red’ Sign
Authors: Srishti Dabas, Sree Parvathy, Vishal Gaurav, Krishan Deb Barman
Published: 2026

Case Presentation

A 25-year-old male presented with complaints of burning sensation over the tip of his tongue for the past 7 days. On mucosal examination, there were multiple pink-to-erythematous enlarged, grouped fungiform papillae over the tip and dorsolateral aspect of the anterior tongue (Figure 1A). Dermoscopy revealed multiple white ovoid projections ( hypertrophied fungiform papillae) on a background of punctate red dots, producing the characteristic “jellyfish in a sea of red” appearance (Figure 1B) [1]. There was no history of similar complaints in other family members, and no systemic signs or symptoms were noted. Based on clinical and dermoscopic features, a diagnosis of transient lingual papillitis was made. The patient was prescribed triamcinolone acetonide 0.1% oral paste for local application, three times a day for 1 week, resulting in complete resolution of the lesions.

Teaching Points

Transient lingual papillitis is a common but often over-looked, short-lived condition, most frequently described in children but also seen in adults, as in our case. It presents as sudden hypertrophy and inflammation of fungiform papillae on the tongue’s tip and sides. While some cases may be associated with fever, difficulty eating, excessive salivation, cervical lymphadenopathy, and angular cheilitis, in adults it can be idiopathic and occur without any systemic signs or symptoms. The condition typically resolves spontaneously within 2–15 days. While the exact cause is unclear, a viral etiology is suspected [2]. Differential diagnoses include scarlet fever’s “strawberry tongue,” which features a white coating with enlarged fungiform papillae and a skin rash, and guttate psoriasis, in which fungiform papillae prominence fluctuates with cutaneous lesions.
