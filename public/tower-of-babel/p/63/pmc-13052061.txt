From Immunization to Autoimmunity: A Deep Dive Into Post‐COVID‐19 Vaccine Reactions—A Narrative Review
Authors: Mohammad Javad Yousefi‐Hashemabad, Saeed Hashemi, Kiarash Saleki, Seyed Mohamad Kazem Nourbakhsh, Andia Saleki, Zahra Hassanzadeh, Abbas Azadmehr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCoronavirus disease‐2019 (COVID‐19) emerged in 2019 from Wuhan, China, leading to the accelerated development of vaccines, which have proven instrumental in controlling the spread of the virus. While vaccination ceased the increasing trend of the viral infection, rare adverse autoimmune events emerged as important considerations. The present review captures the current evidence on reported cases of post‐COVID‐19 vaccination autoimmune and clinical presentations and their potential immunological mechanisms. We aim to inform healthcare providers about the spectrum of potential vaccine‐related autoimmune responses, support timely diagnosis, and suggest considerations for patient management.MethodsWe comprehensively searched Google Scholar, Scopus, and PubMed to find relevant studies on autoimmune events after COVID‐19 vaccination. We prioritized studies that help understand the mechanisms, manifestations, and clinical management of autoimmune events associated with COVID‐19 vaccines. Since this is a narrative review, we synthesized findings qualitatively from diverse study types, such as case reports and cohort studies, with no formal reanalysis and pooling.ResultsAlthough causality relationship needs more studies for clarification, we found an increasing trend of reporting COVID‐19 vaccine‐associated autoimmune events, such as vaccine‐induced immune thrombotic thrombocytopenia (VITT), Guillain‐Barré (GBS), Graves' disease (GD), and autoimmune hepatitis (AH). We reported clinical manifestations, risk factors for developing the disease, and the importance of proper management in each autoimmune disease. Finally, we guided future investigation to clarify the risk factors and mechanistic pathways linking COVID‐19 vaccination to autoimmune responses to have safer vaccinations in the future.ConclusionAutoimmune reactions to COVID‐19 vaccination are rare but need consideration. We suggest that each of these events can be controlled through personalized risk assessment and management of the autoimmune events. This aim can be achieved by focusing on unique aspects of post‐vaccine autoimmune events, using novel approaches, risk assessors, and predictor tools such as AI‐based analysis of predictive biomarkers.Keywords: autoimmunity, COVID‐19 vaccination, vaccines

Introduction

In December 2019, a new strain of the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS‐CoV), categorized as SARS‐CoV‐2, caused the third major human coronavirus outbreak that caused an infection named COVID‐19 (Coronavirus Disease 2019) [1]. The Virus spread from Wuhan, China, and became a global pandemic [2, 3]. The rise of COVID‐19 cases and related deaths (775 million cases and 7 million deaths until the latest update on 2 May 2024), global mental health threats, and their impact on the worldwide economy necessitated the rapid development of vaccines to curb the pandemic [4, 5, 6].

Vaccines are among the most effective health technologies for controlling and eradicating diseases. The vaccination program halted the annual deaths of 2–3 million people in the last 50 years. Vaccines prevent hospitalization with a high rate of success [7, 8]. Within 11 months of the identification of SARS‐CoV‐2, intense efforts by scientists in labs led to the presentation of 300 vaccine projects with diverse methods, such as using protein‐based, viral vector, nucleic acid, and weakened virus vaccines [9]. Obtaining global approval for a vaccine is a significant challenge, ensuring the vaccine's efficiency and safety. Eighteen of the presented vaccines progressed rapidly due to accumulated knowledge and existing platforms [4]. Collectively, COVID‐19 vaccines are new, fast‐developed products, with limited evaluation of long‐term effects [10].

The progression of the vaccination has coincided with the rise of vaccine‐induced health complications [11]. The association between vaccination and rare autoimmune diseases has long been studied, contributing to hesitancy in vaccination programs [12]. Concerns regarding autoimmune diseases and their association with COVID‐19 vaccination have increased because of the lack of long‐term studies on COVID‐19 vaccines [13]. The affiliation of Autoimmune diseases with vaccine administration is covered by two theories, including cross‐reactivity and bystander immune system activation. mRNA vaccines possibly work under the cross‐reactivity mechanism, and adjuvant‐included vaccines are more potent in causing effects through bystander activation. Adjuvants can trigger a vast range of lymphocyte colons, including self‐reacting colons leading to autoimmune diseases. This activation is because of the increased secretion of pro‐inflammatory mediators such as IL‐6, IL‐8, chemokine CCL‐2, CCL‐3, and CCL‐4 [14]. Molecular mimicry is a potent stimulator of the cross‐reactivation mechanism leading to the secretion of autoantibodies and activation of T cells against self‐antigens [13, 14]. Interestingly, Fas ligand (FasL), a classical apoptosis factor, could promote hyperinflammation and cytokine production in COVID‐19 through interaction with MMP7/9. Therefore, its investigation into the autoimmune sequelae of COVID‐19 is recommended [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Multiple sclerosis, autoimmune rheumatic diseases, autoimmune hepatitis (AH), thrombotic thrombocytopenia, and Guillain‐Barré Syndrome (GBS), are among the most commonly reported autoimmune diseases [13, 21].

In this narrative review, we provided updated insights into the association of COVID‐19 vaccination with autoimmune diseases. We performed a comprehensive search on PubMed and Google Scholar to identify studies on autoimmune events associated with COVID‐19. We used systematic reviews, cross‐sectional, and cohort studies to provide an overview of each autoimmune event's characteristics. We selected case reports providing insights into the underlying mechanisms, unique aspects, and differences of these autoimmune events. Being alert to the mechanisms and manifestations of these autoimmune diseases can help clinicians reach a timely diagnosis and select optimal treatments.

Vaccine‐Induced Thrombocytopenia

Emerging incidence of reports of unusual thrombosis after the administration of the AstraZeneca (ChAdOx1 nCoV) vaccine prompted pharmacovigilance alerts in 2021. Subsequent cohort studies also demonstrated a rare association between the vaccine and thrombosis incidence. For instance, a prospective UK cohort identified 220 VITT cases (170 definite cases) among approximately 24 million AstraZeneca (ChAdOx1 nCoV‐19) first doses administered in the UK. VITT was demonstrated as a rare but severe complication, occurring 5–30 days post vaccination, and needs clinicians to maintain a high suspicion for timely diagnosis and management [22]. Thrombocytopenia is characterized by a low blood platelet count (below 100,000/mL). Thrombocytopenia may be asymptomatic or may increase the risk of bleeding or even the occurrence of thrombosis [23]. Vaccine‐induced thrombocytopenia usually emerges in two distinct phenotypes, immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), and vaccine‐induced thrombotic thrombocytopenia (VITT). Although ITP and VITT are distinguished by the reduced number of platelets, the underlying mechanism of each condition is different. ITP occurs as a result of an autoimmune response against platelets, which is more likely to cause the deletion of platelets without activating coagulation pathways [24]. On the other hand, VITT is a condition that usually causes clots in unusual locations [25]. It is clinically important to distinguish the clinical manifestations and serological differences between ITP and VITT to properly manage these conditions (Table 1).

One probable cause of the ITPs following the administration of the COVID‐19 vaccines may be related to the vaccine type. For instance, adenoviruses were shown to be associated with platelet activation, which is a possible trigger for ITP [34]. A cohort study designed by Sue Pavord on hospitalized ITP patients who were administered ChAdOx1 nCoV‐19 (AstraZeneca) indicated a 22% mortality rate among 220 patients, mostly with a manifestation of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST), thrombocytopenia, and cerebral hemorrhage. The presence of 41% of patients with no previous diagnosis highlights a potential association between COVID‐19 vaccines and the occurrence of ITP [26]. On the other hand, although fewer studies are available on mRNA vaccines' association with ITP events compared to the adenovector‐based vaccines, several have reported cases of thrombocytopenia following their administration [35]. Vaccine‐Induced Thrombotic Thrombocytopenia (VITT)

Thrombocytopenia can simultaneously occur with thrombosis in cerebral veins and intracranial hemorrhage in people with deficient amounts of platelets. While thrombosis is unusual in ITP, it is more prevalent and distinguished in a newly discovered syndrome, VITT [24]. Studies were reporting thrombosis with thrombocytopenia after the first dose administration of AstraZeneca and Johnson & Johnson vaccines, termed VITT, in some patients a few months after the administration of COVID‐19 vaccines. Additionally, an increased incidence of the rare post‐VITT thrombosis known as CVST following AstraZeneca administration was also reported in studies [42].

To date, most patients are female, and youths are at a greater risk of developing the disease after vaccine administration [25]. Intense headache and abdominal pain are two chief complaints, and other critical signs of pulmonary embolism and vascular collapse must be considered [43]. Early case series reports on the mortality rate were high (50%), but early recognition and treatment reduced it to 5% in some countries, such as Australia [42].

A study reported 5 cases of thrombosis thrombocytopenia with high levels of anti‐PF4 antibodies following AstraZeneca vaccine administration. All patients responded well to IVIG and steroid therapies, indicating the disease's immune‐mediated etiology. Four patients developed CVST, and one patient developed multiple peripheral thromboses. Headache was the most prevalent complaint for the first group, and backache was the complaint of the remaining patients. Contradictory response to heparin and estimated platelet aggregation in four named patients and a typical HIT serum suggested a rise of HIT‐like syndrome after administration of adenoviral vaccines. Despite a good response to IVIG and steroid treatment, the use of heparin is in doubt and needs caution because of the contrast between thrombocytopenia and thrombosis [44]. mRNA vaccines are not exempt, and a few cases are available in sources. In another case report, a 78‐year‐old patient was admitted to the hospital with shortness of breath and right foot swelling. He received two doses of the Pfizer mRNA vaccine. The interval between the last dose administration and the admission was 5 days. He developed thromboses in the right great saphenous vein and gastrocnemius. Pulmonary embolism was demonstrated in both subsegmental arteries. PF4 antibodies were positive despite no heparin use within the last 10 years [45].

Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

MS is a debilitating inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS) with an autoimmune etiology. The underlying mechanism is hypothesized to be peripheral autoreactive T‐cell activation and their further infiltration in the CNS, which subsequently activates B cells and macrophages, leading to myelin damage. (Figure 1) [46]. Approximately 2.9 million individuals are diagnosed with MS worldwide, with a peak onset between 25 and 35 years of age [46, 47]. Several factors influence the incidence of post‐vaccine MS, including an absence of Disease Modifying Therapies (DMT), younger age, higher Expanded Disability Status Scale score, and relapse history 1 year before vaccine administration [48].

Hypothesized schematic mechanisms underlying post‐vaccination autoimmune diseases and common manifestations. Different COVID‐19 vaccine platforms may trigger autoimmune responses through distinct pathways; therefore, certain autoimmune diseases may be more likely to occur following specific vaccine types.

Post‐COVID vaccination cases of MS are divided into two groups: new‐onset MS and relapses in pre‐existing disease. New‐onset cases need more diagnostic evaluation to confirm the disease, while relapse cases can be diagnosed when worsening of preexisting symptoms or even new MS‐related symptoms occur. MRI findings showed multiple enhancing/non‐enhancing lesions; however, old lesions can be observed in cases with relapsed MS [49, 50].

Studies Examining MS Cases Occurred Post‐COVID‐19 Vaccine

According to a retrospective cohort study of 1661 vaccinated MS patients (90% receiving BNT162b2), a mild but significant increase (3.79% to 5.30% within 90 days post‐COVID‐19 vaccination) in relapse incidence was observed [51].

Role of DMTs

Reduce vaccine‐induced immune responses but suppress neuroinflammation, which lowers relapse risk. In a larger longitudinal cohort study with a 1‐year follow‐up of 2466 MS patients, while the incidence of relapses after COVID‐19 vaccine administration was rare, relapses were more associated with other baseline factors of the disease situation, including the use of DMTs [52]. Although there are limited studies on the risk of MS relapses following COVID‐19 vaccines in patients who were not using DMTs, it is suggested to initially control the disease status in MS patients, as it can influence post‐vaccine reactions [52, 53, 54].

Similar to the post‐COVID‐19 vaccine relapse of MS, new‐onset cases were also reported in several studies. Although the exact underlying mechanism of the new‐onset MS events has not been fully understood, some risk factors have been suggested as probable determinants of the risk of developing the disease. For instance, in a study on 266 pre‐ and post‐COVID‐19 vaccine MS cases found no increase in MS onset post‐vaccination. Interestingly, in a subgroup analysis including 14 cases of post‐vaccination MS diagnosed and 17 pre‐COVID MS patients, they found that none of the vaccine‐onset patients carried the common MS‐risk allele, HLA‐DRB115, whereas HLA‐DRB108 was present in only the post‐vaccine onset group [55].

In addition to the findings of larger cohort studies, we have explored peer‐reviewed case reports and provided a summary table exhibiting the summary features of both post‐vaccine relapse and new‐onset MS incidences (Table 3). These case series lack control groups and do not establish causality; however, they may improve the understanding of the expected presentations and management approaches following vaccination.

Autoimmune Hepatitis (AH)

Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a chronic, severe hepatitis caused by an autoimmune‐mediated response affecting host liver cells [63]. AH is more prevalent among females and can occur at any age [64]. Potential triggers such as viral infections and vaccines can induce autoreactive T‐cell responses, leading to AH [65]. AH is classified into two types. The presence of anti‐smooth muscle (SMA) and/or anti‐nuclear (ANA) antibodies indicates AH type 1, and a positive test for anti‐liver kidney microsomal antibody type 1 (anti‐LKM1), anti‐LKM3, and/or anti‐liver cytosol type 1 antibody (anti‐LC1) indicates AH type 2 [66].

Disseminated reports exist on the occurrence of AH after vaccinations, but there is a lack of studies on the evaluation of post‐vaccine incidence. Previous case reports have indicated a coincidence of AIH occurrence following the administration of vaccines against papillomavirus, hepatitis A and B, and influenza [67, 68, 69, 70]. In a study based on 8,562,584 WHO‐reported vaccine adverse events, the hepatitis B vaccine (ROR 3.52, 95%CI 2.50–4.95), the COVID‐19 vaccine (ROR 2.95, 95% CI 2.73–3.18), and the papillomavirus vaccine (ROR 2.13, 95% CI 1.45–3.13) were the vaccines with the highest risk of developing AH [71]. Additionally, a study identified 27 confirmed cases of AH following COVID‐19 vaccination and demonstrated clinical features, treatments, and outcomes of these patients [72]. In a systematic review of 275 post‐COVID‐19 vaccine liver disease cases, AH was the most reported pathology with 138 cases. Most of the cases were new‐onset, and only four cases were relapses in previously diagnosed AH [73, 74]. Most of the patients were consistent with having AH type 1, supported by the presence of IgG immunoglobulin and antinuclear antibodies [73]. In another systematic review involving fewer cases (32 cases with AH‐like syndrome), the onset of the symptoms was estimated to be 21.1 ± 15.0 (mean + SD) [75]. Symptoms were nonspecific and included icterus, choluria, loss of appetite, fatigue, and abdominal pain [75].

Guillain‐Barré

GBS is a deteriorating polyradiculoneuropathy autoimmune disease that causes impairment in controlling the muscles, sensation, and other peripheral nervous system functions, with a prevalence of 1.91 cases per 100,000 people per year [82]. GBS is caused by Immunological responses targeting the peripheral nervous system. Investigations indicated some possible pathogenesis of the GBS, such as Inflammation‐related infiltration of T cells and macrophages leading to demyelination, release of some IgGs against GM1, GD1a, and GalNAc‐GD1a, and multiple hypothesized immune responses to myelin proteins (PMP22, P0, and P2) [82, 83]. There are two different phenotypes of GBS: acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) and acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (AIDP). The disease is determined by Bifacial weakness with paresthesia (BFP), paraparesis, GBS, Miller‐Fisher syndrome, and other rare subtypes of GBS (Figure 1) [84]. The diagnosis of GBS is a combination of inspection and examination, electrophysiological assessment, cerebrospinal fluid evaluation, and serum immune molecule estimation [85].

The hypothesized association between vaccination and the occurrence of Guillain‐Barré has long been intriguing, accompanied by conflict between vaccination proponents and opponents [86]. Due to the importance of this debate, large population‐based cohorts have been investigated to assess the risk and association of vaccination programs and the occurrence of GBS for long periods. Regarding these kinds of studies, a narrative review of these large longitudinal cohorts restated that most of these vaccines were not significantly associated with an increased risk of GBS, and any potential risk was outweighed by the public benefits of immunization [86].

The COVID‐19 vaccination program, concomitant with new reports of GBS cases, led to studies on the incidence of GBS in different populations. It has been clearly stated in several studies that most of the GBS incidences were after the first dose of COVID‐19 vaccines, regardless of vaccine type [84]. In 2023, Ryan Y. S. Keh et al. analyzed population‐based data from UK hospitals and the National Health Service to investigate the incidence of GBS after COVID‐19 vaccination. They observed an increased incidence of GBS during 6 weeks following the first dose of vaccination. ChAdOx1 nCoV‐19 (AstraZeneca) and tozinameran (Pfizer) were associated with most occurrences, with 176/198 and 21/198 of total cases, respectively [87]. A published systematic review and meta‐analysis analyzed verified GBS cases reported to the U.S (from December 2020 to January 2022). The review examined GBS incidence within 21 and 42 days after three types of COVID‐19 vaccine administrations (487,651,785 doses). The reporting rate of GBS was 9–12 times higher for Ad26.COV2.S compared with mRNA vaccines, and 2–3 times higher than the expected GBS background incidence rate [88]. A systematic analysis of 41 studies (from 17 countries) with 88 cases of GBS following COVID‐19 vaccination found that AIDP was the most common type among GBS cases. Additionally, the BFP variant was more prevalent than its typical prevalence. Since these data were aggregated from individual cases, larger population‐wide cohorts are needed for confirmation [89].

Post‐Vaccine GBS Findings

Anti‐GM1 and anti‐SS‐A antibodies associated with AMAN after mRNA vaccines [90]. Also, a report of a severe case after the second dose of mRNA vaccine (Pfizer) with the absence of anti‐GM1, GM2, GD1a, and GQ1b diagnosed as AIDP type of GBS demonstrates the variety of responses to even one type of hypothetical stimulation [91]. The reason behind the macrophage‐related cause of demyelination of neurons in AIDP is not clear, and this tendency of macrophages to attach and destroy these fibers remains indistinct [92]. Noteworthy, most of the post‐vaccine GBS cases are AIDP cases [93]. More general muscle dysfunction, paraplegia, diarrhea, and dysphagia were observed in AIDP patients, and the need for ventilators was lower than in non‐AIDP patients [85].

The AMAN subtype is usually more severe and morbid, and some AMAN cases have been reported in several reports [94]. More sensational signaling is disrupted in non‐AIDP cases and causes some more frequent symptoms such as vision and diplopia, l, back pain, and Bilateral facial weakness [85].

Treatment

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG); most patients achieve complete or partial recovery [93, 94].

Epidemiology and Vaccination Impact

A worldwide vaccination program will lead to reporting the simultaneous occurrence of different diseases and vaccine administration even through the chance. To date, despite the incidences of GBS post‐vaccination, vaccine administration has not been established to be a significant risk factor for GBS [95]. On the other hand, COVID‐19infection itself is a strong risk factor for GBS, and in a comparative study, COVID‐19infection was correlated with an increased risk of GBS while the vaccine was not [96].

Graves' Disease and Other Rare Autoimmune Diseases

Graves' disease (GD) is an autoimmune disease characterized by the production and presence of anti‐TSH‐R (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone Receptor) autoantibodies (TRAb) [97, 98]. GD causes hyperthyroidism more than other factors. Thyroid Stimulating Antibody (TSAb) is a subtype of TRAb that acts as an agonist to Thyroid Stimulating Hormone. TSAb induces thyrocyte proliferation, increases thyroid hormone secretion, and promotes thyroid growth (Figure 1) [98]. Excessive stimulation of TSH‐R by TSAb causes Graves orbitopathy (GO) due to the synergetic role of the autoantibodies and insulin‐like growth factor 1 [99]. The presence of Thyroid Peroxidase Antibody (anti‐TPO) is a biomarker of thyroid autoimmune diseases and can also be observed in GD [100].

The annual rate is 19 cases per 100,000, with women having a six times greater risk [101]. During the pandemic, some cases were being reported as diagnosed with GD [102] with greater severity among youths [103]. Despite the existence of contradictory studies that deny the association between COVID‐19 vaccination and GD incidence [104]. There are several reports of new‐onset and relapsed GDs after the administration of the COVID‐19 vaccine [105]. Among various vaccines, mRNA vaccines are the most prevalent vaccines in patients diagnosed with GD post‐vaccine administration [106]. Patrizio et al. expressed concern about the emerging reported new‐onset and recurrence of GD post‐vaccination [107]. Flippo et al. observed some differences between post‐COVID‐vaccine GD cases and other GD cases. They reported older cases with a greater proportion of male patients in post‐vaccine GD incidences [108].

Perspectives and Future Directions

Several mechanisms have been hypothesized to trigger the adverse autoimmune responses after vaccination. Molecular mimicry, where immune responses to viral proteins resemble self‐antigens, bystander activation of self‐reactive lymphocytes, and cytokine storms post‐vaccination, particularly with adenoviral vectors, are more probable mechanisms involved in post‐COVID‐19 autoimmune events [13]. There are innovative and novel approaches to investigating the adverse effects of vaccines. It is elucidated that some adverse effects of vaccines are strongly associated with a genetic background or impaired immunological pathways. System biology‐based approaches will help future studies on vaccine safety by analyzing complex interactions in immunological pathways and their effects on immune responses to vaccines. Additionally, genomic analysis can predict the risk of adverse events such as autoimmune diseases in individuals [117]. For instance, it has been suggested that the simulation of the immune system through vaccines may increase the risk of SLE in individuals with genetic susceptibility [118]. Molecular mimicry is a well‐known mechanism by which vaccines can lead to the production of autoantibodies. It has been shown that some sequences of the spike protein can increase the possibility of producing autoantibodies against several organs in the human body. A study demonstrated that anti‐Peptide 2 is an antibody that targets a sequence outside the receptor‐binding domain of spike protein in SARS‐CoV‐2. While this antibody is not successful in fighting against the virus, it can target similar sequences in the human unc‐80 protein. Novel in silico approaches can improve vaccine production by detecting these sequences and developing safer vaccines [119].

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools are now being developed to detect immunological signs of diseases, including autoimmune diseases [120]. These tools can facilitate detecting autoimmune responses in individuals, post‐vaccination. Yang et al. investigated the promising role of two deep‐learning models to predict autoimmune diseases. Although some limitations affected the significance of these models, they could predict autoimmune diseases effectively (AUC 0.93 and 0.99) by analyzing T‐cell receptor sequences [121]. These approaches provide fast and effective management of post‐vaccination adverse events. More research is needed to elucidate the efficacy and precision of these innovative approaches in the clinic, and there is a long way to develop proper tools to be used as public health services.

We suggest more transparent long‐term studies on vaccinated populations to assess autoimmune risks over time, reveal the underlying mechanisms, and define risk factors in individuals. Genetic screening and enhanced monitoring systems are highly suggested to prevent adverse events post‐vaccination [122]. In addition, systematic reviews of clinical cases of autoimmune events should be prioritized, as they provide critical information on clinical manifestations, disease progression, patterns of immune responses, management and treatments, and emerging trends in autoimmune diseases [123]. We encourage future case reports to be more focused on: 1. Including a detailed genetic profile. 2. Doing long‐term follow‐ups to see the progression and response to treatment of patients. 3. Providing detailed histories of patients to elucidate other triggers of autoimmune events. Moreover, in times where rapid discovery of pathological mechanisms and treatment effects is necessary, computational Immunology research could be useful to address a wide range of disorders [124, 125, 126], such as neurological disorders, which could be potentially immune response‐related, are prevalent in aging populations as well as COVID‐19 patients [127, 128]. The effect of hyperinflammatory and autoimmune reactions in COVID‐19 cases with cancer could be studied in the future as well [129, 130]. In the context of COVID‐19, drug discovery approaches could focus on autoimmunity and the prediction of immune response in various populations in cases where large‐scale prospective studies are not feasible.

Additionally, the use of medicinal plants and natural products is important in the prevention and treatment of diseases. We recommend research to dampen autoimmunity in COVID‐19 patients. The use of immunomodulatory herbal products has been investigated in many contexts, including COVID‐19 [131, 132, 133, 134, 135, 136, 137].

Conclusions

While autoimmune events following COVID‐19 vaccination are rare, these events could be detrimental to affected individuals. There is evidence on a wide range of autoimmune events following COVID‐19, involving skin, cardiovascular, nervous, or endocrine systems, as well as other body organs. However, a great portion of emerging studies are preliminary case reports with heterogeneous results corresponding to different vaccine types from various manufacturers. Large longitudinal studies, although with limitations, demonstrated low incidences of these autoimmune events in various populations. On the other hand, it is hard to determine whether an autoimmune event is an immune dysregulation due to vaccine administration or is just a coincidental occurrence. Identifying markers with good predictive and diagnostic values guides personalized risk assessments. Novel computational technologies can be used to investigate the auto reactions to different substances entering the body. We suggest using bioinformatics and AI‐based approaches to reveal serological markers of adverse events following vaccination to diagnose the disease clearly and manage the autoimmune event even before it develops. Translational immunological research to uncover the molecular basis of clinical manifestations in the context of post‐COVID‐19 vaccination averse immune responses is also advised.
