Immediate and Longitudinal Outcomes of a National Hands‐On Basic Research Course in Rhinology

Introduction

Opportunities for hands‐on basic research training have become increasingly limited for early‐career clinicians, particularly in surgical subspecialties such as rhinology. Clinical workload, reduced protected research time, and the highly specialized nature of modern laboratory techniques have made it challenging for clinicians to gain practical exposure to basic research [1]. This decline in research engagement has raised concerns regarding the sustainability of the clinician–scientist workforce and the integration of scientific inquiry into clinical practice.

To address this gap, various educational interventions have been developed to introduce clinicians to research methodologies. However, most evaluations of such programs have focused on short‐term outcomes, including participant satisfaction, self‐reported knowledge acquisition, or perceived confidence [2]. While these measures provide insight into immediate educational impact, they do not capture whether participation leads to sustained research engagement or tangible academic output. Moreover, there is a paucity of longitudinal evaluations of research training programs organized by professional societies, despite their potential to provide standardized, specialty‐specific education and facilitate cross‐institutional networks.

The Japanese Rhinologic Society organized a short‐term, society‐led hands‐on basic research course aimed at providing early‐career clinicians with practical research experience, mentorship, and opportunities for peer networking since 2014. The present study evaluated both the immediate and long‐term outcomes of this program using postcourse and follow‐up surveys. Specifically, we assessed participant satisfaction, perceived educational value, sustained involvement in basic research, and subsequent academic outcomes. By examining downstream behavioral and scholarly outcomes, this study aimed to evaluate the association between participation in a society‐led hands‐on research initiative and subsequent research engagement in rhinology.

Methods

Course Structure and Format

The course consisted of two 90‐min sessions, each comprising a 30‐min didactic lecture and 60 min of hands‐on practical training. Each session was designed for approximately six active participants, although observation by additional attendees was permitted without a strict limit. Approximately 15 faculty members served as instructors, and the number of faculty members was often nearly equivalent to the number of active participants, allowing close supervision. Participants were selected on a first‐come, first‐served basis after open registration. All participants completed the course. The program was designed to provide practical exposure to basic research techniques rather than to require completion of an independent research project during the session. Faculty members provided direct technical instruction and research advice, and discussions during or after the course sometimes facilitated postcourse networking and subsequent collaborative research.

Study Design and Survey Instruments

This study was conducted as a mixed‐methods program evaluation of a society‐led, short‐term hands‐on basic research course organized by the Japanese Rhinologic Society. The course aimed to provide early‐career clinicians with practical exposure to basic research techniques, experimental design, and opportunities for mentorship and peer networking.

Two questionnaire‐based surveys were conducted from September to October 2025. An immediate postcourse survey was distributed at the conclusion of the course to assess participant satisfaction, perceived educational value, motivation for future research activities, and perceived networking benefits. Items were primarily measured using Likert‐scale responses, supplemented by selected multiple‐choice questions.

A long‐term follow‐up survey was conducted to evaluate sustained research engagement and academic outcomes after course participation. This survey assessed current involvement in basic research, perceived long‐term utility of the course, and subsequent scholarly activities, including conference presentations, manuscript preparation or submission, initiation of new research projects or collaborations, and grant applications or funding acquisition. Participants were asked whether their experience in the seminar had led to subsequent academic outcomes, using the following item: “Did your experience in the seminar lead to any of the following outcomes? Conference presentations, manuscript preparation or publication, collaborative research, grant applications or funding acquisition, etc.” Multiple responses were allowed for relevant items. All surveys were administered electronically and participation was voluntary.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan. The study protocol and survey procedures were reviewed and approved by the Asahikawa Medical University institutional review board (#25176).

Participation in both the immediate and long‐term surveys was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all respondents prior to survey completion. Survey responses were collected anonymously, and no personally identifiable information was recorded or analyzed.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize survey responses. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, calculated based on the number of respondents for each survey item. Likert‐scale responses were summarized using frequency distributions, with emphasis on the highest and top two response categories where appropriate to illustrate overall trends. Given the exploratory nature of this program evaluation and the limited sample size, no formal hypothesis testing was performed. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software (GraphPad Prism version 11.0.1, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA), and results are reported in accordance with recommendations for reporting educational and survey‐based research.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Survey Response

The hands‐on seminar covered diverse experimental techniques across multiple technical domains (Figure 1A). The details are provided in Table S1. Among the training topics, Western blotting was the most frequently implemented technique, followed by immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry. Training modules were categorized into four domains: cell biology, molecular biology, animal experiments, and computational/research skills. Molecular biology–related techniques were consistently represented across years, while cell biology– and animal experiment–related modules were offered intermittently. In later years, computational and research skill–related topics, such as bioinformatics and grant proposal writing, were also incorporated.

A total of 62 participants completed the immediate postcourse survey. As baseline research‐related information, 41 of 62 participants were already conducting research, and 28 of 62 had already started animal experiments at the time of course participation. Of these, 27 participants responded to the long‐term follow‐up survey and were included in the final analysis (Figure 2). A comparison between long‐term follow‐up responders and nonresponders is provided in Table S2. Participation year, primary affiliation, doctoral degree status, and baseline research‐related status did not differ significantly between the two groups, whereas responders showed more favorable immediate postcourse evaluations for several items. The majority of participants were in their 30s (13, 48%), followed by those in their 40s (9, 33%) and 20s (5, 19%). Most participants were male (77.8%) and affiliated with university hospitals (77.8%). The majority of respondents had participated in the course between 2022 and 2025 (77.8%) (Table 1). Preferences for training topics varied by age group. Participants in their 40s most frequently selected cell biology–related modules, followed by molecular biology and computational/research skills. In contrast, participants in their 30s most commonly chose animal experiment–related topics. Among participants in their 20s, computational and research skill–related modules were the most frequently selected, followed by animal experiment–related topics (Figure 1B).

Immediate Postcourse Outcomes

Immediately after completion of the hands‐on basic research course, participants reported high levels of satisfaction and perceived educational benefit. Overall, 98.4% of respondents rated the educational content within the top two categories of the Likert scale, and 91.9% assigned the highest rating to instructor explanations. In addition, 96.8% of participants indicated that they obtained useful insights applicable to future research activities.

Regarding future intentions, most respondents agreed that the course should be continued (93.5%), and 71.0% expressed willingness to participate again in similar programs. These immediate postcourse outcomes are summarized in Figure 3.

Immediate postcourse evaluation of the hands‐on basic research course. Participant responses to the postseminar questionnaire are shown using a 5‐point Likert scale. Most respondents selected the highest rating (Score 1) across all evaluation items, including willingness to participate again, the need for continuation of the seminar, usefulness for research, instructor explanation, and educational content. Scores were based on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = most positive response, 5 = least positive response).

Networking and Perceived Scalability

Beyond individual satisfaction and perceived educational value, the postcourse survey also assessed the extent to which the program facilitated networking among participants and instructors. Overall, 59.0% of respondents reported that they were able to establish cross‐institutional connections through the course. Furthermore, 85.0% indicated that they would participate in future programs if a structured system enabling longitudinal networking and collaboration were available (Figure 4).

Networking outcomes and perceived scalability of the hands‐on research course. Immediately after course completion, 59.0% of participants reported establishing horizontal connections with instructors or peers. In addition, 85.0% indicated willingness to participate in future programs if a structured system enabling longitudinal networking and collaboration were available.

Long‐Term Research Engagement and Academic Outcomes

At long‐term follow‐up, 62.9% of respondents were actively engaged in basic research, either as team members (40.7%) or as primary investigators (22.2%). An additional 14.8% reported involvement in clinical research only, while 22.2% were not engaged in research activities at the time of follow‐up (Figure 5A).

(B) Academic outcomes achieved after course participation, including conference presentations, manuscript preparation or submission, initiation of new research collaborations, and grant applications or funding acquisition. The most frequently reported long‐term benefits of course participation were networking with instructors and peers (74.1%) and increased motivation for research (63.0%). Subsequent academic outcomes included conference presentations (37.0%), manuscript preparation or submission (33.3%), initiation of new research projects or collaborations (18.5%), and grant applications or funding acquisition (14.8%). No academic outcomes were reported by 40.7% of respondents. Multiple responses were permitted for applicable items (Figure 5B). Quantitative distributions of the number of academic outputs are provided in Figure S1 and Table S3. To evaluate the potential influence of follow‐up duration on academic outcomes, respondents were stratified into those with a follow‐up period of 1–2 years (n = 15) and those with a follow‐up period of 3–7 years (n = 12). Academic outcomes were more frequently reported among participants with longer follow‐up periods, including conference presentations, manuscript preparation or submission, grant applications or funding, and new collaborations, whereas no academic outcomes were more common among participants with shorter follow‐up periods (Figure S2).

Discussion

This mixed‐methods program evaluation demonstrated that a short‐term, society‐led hands‐on basic research course in rhinology was associated with both immediate educational benefits and sustained research engagement. Immediately after course completion, participants reported high levels of satisfaction, perceived educational value, and motivation for future research activities. Importantly, long‐term follow‐up revealed that a substantial proportion of participants remained actively involved in basic research and achieved tangible academic outcomes, including conference presentations, manuscript preparation, and initiation of new research collaborations. These findings suggest that even brief, well‐structured hands‐on training programs may be associated with sustained research engagement among early‐career clinicians.

The present study has several novel features. First, it provides one of the few longitudinal evaluations of a society‐led hands‐on basic research course within a surgical subspecialty, where opportunities for structured basic research training are limited. Second, by incorporating both immediate postcourse assessments and long‐term follow‐up data, this study extends beyond traditional satisfaction‐based evaluations and provides a more meaningful assessment of downstream impact through tangible academic outcomes, including sustained research engagement, conference presentations, manuscript preparation, and research collaborations. Third, the findings underscore the role of professional societies as effective platforms for fostering clinician–scientist development through mentorship, peer networking, and exposure to practical research skills. Unlike single‐institution programs, society‐led initiatives can facilitate cross‐institutional networking and provide training on broadly relevant topics that are selected through annual discussion by a society committee. Together, these results highlight the potential value of scalable, society‐driven educational interventions in strengthening the research workforce in rhinology and other procedural disciplines.

Previous studies evaluating research training programs for clinicians have primarily focused on short‐term outcomes, such as participant satisfaction, self‐reported knowledge gain, or perceived confidence [2, 3]. While these metrics are useful for assessing immediate educational impact, they do not necessarily reflect sustained engagement in research or downstream academic productivity. In contrast, the present study incorporated long‐term follow‐up and demonstrated that participation in a brief, hands‐on research course was associated with continued involvement in basic research and measurable scholarly outputs. This distinction is particularly relevant in surgical subspecialties, where clinical demands often limit opportunities for prolonged research training.

From an educational theory perspective, the findings can be interpreted through the framework of experiential learning and self‐efficacy theory. Hands‐on training provides learners with concrete experiences and active experimentation, which are key components of experiential learning [4]. Additionally, early exposure to achievable research activities, supported by mentorship and peer interaction, may enhance self‐efficacy and reduce perceived barriers to engaging in basic research. Networking and mentorship may represent particularly important components of society‐led research education. In the present study, networking with instructors and peers was the most frequently reported long‐term benefit, suggesting that the value of the program extended beyond technical instruction alone. In addition, participants who established horizontal connections during the course were more likely to respond to the long‐term follow‐up survey, indicating that cross‐institutional networking may help sustain postcourse engagement and research motivation. Future seminars should therefore place greater emphasis on actively facilitating networking among participants and instructors through structured mentoring, online platforms, and periodic follow‐up opportunities.

Professional societies may play a unique and underrecognized role in addressing structural gaps in clinician–scientist training. Unlike institution‐based programs, society‐led initiatives can offer standardized, specialty‐specific curricula and facilitate cross‐institutional networks that persist after course completion. The present findings suggest that such programs may complement traditional academic pathways by providing accessible entry points to research for clinicians without formal research appointments or protected time. This model may be particularly valuable in disciplines facing a decline in the number of clinician–scientists and could be adapted to other procedural specialties seeking to strengthen their research capacity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study relied on self‐reported questionnaire data, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. In addition, participation in both the course and the follow‐up surveys was voluntary, raising the possibility of selection bias. The long‐term follow‐up response rate was 43.5%, and nonresponse bias should be considered when interpreting the findings. Although available aggregate data allowed comparison of participation year, primary affiliation, doctoral degree status, baseline research‐related status, and selected immediate postcourse responses between responders and nonresponders, some demographic variables, including age and sex, were not collected in the immediate postcourse survey. Because the number of participants in each annual course was small, these variables were not collected from the perspective of protecting participant privacy. Importantly, participants who reported more favorable immediate impressions of the course, particularly regarding willingness to participate again and establishment of cross‐institutional connections, were more likely to complete the long‐term follow‐up survey. Therefore, the rates of sustained research engagement and academic outcomes may have been overestimated, and future seminars should place greater emphasis on actively facilitating horizontal networking among participants and instructors.

Although the survey asked whether the seminar experience had led to subsequent academic outcomes, the outcomes were self‐reported and may have included research activities that had already been underway before course participation. In addition, because it was not feasible to construct an age‐ and sex‐matched control group from otolaryngologists who did not participate in the course, the findings should be interpreted as an association between course participation and subsequent research engagement rather than evidence of a causal effect. Participants may have had a stronger preexisting interest in research than nonparticipants, introducing selection bias.

Follow‐up duration also influenced the interpretation of academic outcomes. When respondents were stratified by follow‐up period, participants with longer follow‐up periods reported more academic outputs, whereas those with shorter follow‐up periods more frequently reported no academic outcomes. These findings are consistent with the time‐dependent nature of scholarly activities such as conference presentations, manuscript preparation, grant applications, and collaborative research. Therefore, academic productivity among recent participants may be underestimated, and future studies should use standardized follow‐up intervals or time‐adjusted analyses. Finally, the sample size was relatively small, and the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides valuable insight into the potential educational impact of short‐term, society‐led hands‐on research training. The results support further prospective evaluation of similar programs using larger cohorts, standardized outcome measures, and where feasible, comparative study designs.

Conclusions

A short‐term, society‐led hands‐on basic research course was associated with sustained research engagement and tangible academic outcomes among clinicians in rhinology. Although causality cannot be established, these findings suggest that professional societies may contribute to clinician–scientist development through scalable, specialty‐specific hands‐on training programs, mentorship, and cross‐institutional networking, thereby complementing traditional institution‐based research pathways.
