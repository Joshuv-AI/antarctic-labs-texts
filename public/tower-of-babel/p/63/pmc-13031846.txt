Small eye movements during convergence and divergence in individuals with intermittent exotropia
Authors: Yoshihito Mochizuki, Akiko Kimura, Yoichi Okita, Akiko Masuda, Hiroyuki Kanda, Fumi Gomi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We explored eye movement disorganization during convergence and divergence in individuals with intermittent exotropia (iXT), using a 300-Hz Tobii Pro Spectrum eye-tracking system. The participants comprised 20 patients with iXT, who were evaluated both before and three months after strabismus surgery, aged 15–40 years and 20 controls with orthophoria or horizontal exophorias of ≤ 8 prism diopters, exhibiting latent exo-deviations under dissociated viewing conditions. The participants alternated their gaze between a target at 30 cm and a monitor at 60 cm every 5 s. The convergence time was significantly longer in the iXT group than in the control group. We identified three types of small, conjugate eye movements in the iXT group that occurred less frequently in the control group: small, back-and-forth movements during convergence or divergence (“notch events”); transient, brief movements that occurred immediately before the initiation of convergence or divergence (“static notch events”); and overshooting of the target followed by correction at the end of the vergence response (“overshoot events”). The iXT group showed significantly more static notch events during convergence, as well as more notch and overshoot events during divergence. The static notch events during convergence significantly decreased after strabismus surgery. Small eye movements were associated with preoperative symptoms and postoperative Adult Strabismus-20 Questionnaire (AS-20) improvement. Frequent vergence-related movements in iXT may impair daily activities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39497-1.

Introduction

Intermittent exotropia (iXT) is the most common type of strabismus in Japan1. Individuals with iXT experience both exotropia and exophoria; binocular vision is maintained when phoria is controlled, but exotropia becomes apparent when the phoria decompensates2. Intermittent exotropia is commonly classified into three types: basic type, divergence excess type, and convergence insufficiency type, based on the relationship between near and distance deviation angles. Although patients with iXT do not experience diplopia, they have clinical symptoms that impact their quality of life, such as asthenopia related to maintaining phoria, photophobia, and a tendency to keep one eye closed3.

Patients with iXT are believed to use accommodative convergence to maintain phoria and exhibit fusional convergence when focusing on a close target. When divergence occurs, ocular alignment shifts to exotropia, suggesting that eye movements during divergence differ from those in individuals without iXT. Although many studies have used eye trackers to examine saccades and reading behavior in individuals with iXT4,5, no studies have assessed eye movement associated with convergence and divergence. Previous studies have focused on variables such as saccadic velocity, fixation stability, and binocular coordination, whereas vergence-related fine eye movements during gaze shifts remain less well characterized. Therefore, understanding eye movements during convergence and divergence in individuals with iXT could provide valuable insights into this condition and inform potential treatments.

Vergence eye movements are disjunctive eye movements in which the two eyes rotate in opposite directions to adjust binocular alignment for changes in viewing distance, including convergence for near targets and divergence for far targets. The angle of convergence is commonly used to quantify the magnitude of vergence and reflects the binocular alignment required to maintain single vision at a given viewing distance. In this study, we focused on the “distance to the gazing point,” which represents the instantaneous binocular point of gaze defined by the intersection of the two lines of sight, rather than the physical distance to the visual stimulus. This variable provides a dynamic measure of binocular vergence behavior during gaze shifts. Vergence responses are typically elicited by step changes in vergence demand and are expressed as smooth vergence eye movements with dynamics distinct from saccadic eye movements6. Distinguishing between the stimulus profile (step change in vergence demand) and the resulting eye-movement response is important for accurate interpretation of vergence behavior in eye-tracking recordings.

To explore this, we analyzed eye movements during convergence and divergence in individuals with and without iXT using an eye-tracking system to identify any characteristic eye movements associated with iXT7,8. We also looked for possible associations between eye movements and patient-reported visual complaints in daily life.

Results

The iXT group comprised 20 patients (9 male, 11 female) with a mean age of 22.5 ± 6.5 years. The mean horizontal deviation was 43.4 ± 16.4 prism diopters (PD) for near objects and 35.8 ± 15.6 PD for far objects before strabismus surgery. The control group comprised 20 participants (9 male, 11 female) with a mean age of 26.2 ± 5.1 years. Their mean horizontal phoria was 2.0 ± 2.5 PD at near and 1.7 ± 2.4 PD at distance, with ranges of 0 to 8 PD at near and 0 to 7 PD at distance, confirming that all control participants were either orthophoric or exophoric.

Duration of ocular movements

During convergence, the iXT group took 0.30 ± 0.19 s to focus on the near target, while the control group took 0.19 ± 0.06 s (Fig. 1, left). This delay in the iXT group was statistically significant (p = 0.030). During divergence, the iXT group took 0.36 ± 0.28 s and the control group 0.23 ± 0.09 s; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.078) (Fig. 1, right). Both groups took longer to reach divergence than convergence, although these differences were not statistically significant (iXT group, p = 0.180; control group, p = 0.112). In the iXT group, no significant correlation was found between the magnitude of exotropic deviation and the duration of convergence or divergence movements (convergence: r = − 0.217, p = 0.437; divergence: r = − 0.1644, p = 0.558). The recordings did not show complete divergence to the exotropic position in the iXT group.

Small ocular movements in the iXT group

During convergence or divergence, we observed that the angle of convergence sometimes briefly decreased or increased in direction (Fig. 2A). These momentary directional movements, occurring within 0.1 s with a change of 0.1-meter angle (MA) or more, were referred to as “notches.” Evaluation of the movements of each eye revealed that both eyes performed the same directional movement (Fig. 2B). Among the notches, we identified momentary eye movements during which the angle of convergence briefly decreased in direction immediately before the start of convergence or divergence (Fig. 3A). In these cases, both eyes moved in the same direction, similar to other notch events, indicating conjugate movement (Fig. 3B). These were referred to as “static notch” events. Additionally, at the end of the divergence movement, some participants overshot the final fixation point and then returned to it (Fig. 4A,B). When the angle of convergence was overcorrected by 0.2 MA or more, these eye movements were defined as “overshoot” events4. This threshold was set based on the standard deviations of small eye movement changes, with the higher cutoff (0.2 MA) chosen to differentiate overshoots from minor vergence noise near movement endpoints. Unlike notch and static notch events, overshoots were disjunctive, involving asymmetric movements between the two eyes.

Notch events were observed during convergence and divergence in 20% and 35% of participants in the iXT group and in 15% and 5% of participants in the control group, respectively. The difference in frequency during divergence was significant between the two groups (p = 0.031). Static notch events were typically seen during convergence, and their frequency was significantly higher in the iXT group (70.0%) than in the control group (30.0%) (p = 0.022). Overshoot was a characteristic eye movement during divergence, with a significantly higher frequency in the iXT group (35%) than in the control group (10%) (p = 0.047).

Relationship between eye movements and characteristics of baseline iXT

Among the iXT cases, 16 were of the basic type and 4 were of the convergence insufficiency type; none were of the divergence excess type. There were no significant differences between the types in terms of the duration of ocular movements (convergence: p = 0.801, divergence: p = 0.794) or the frequency of small ocular movements (convergence: notch, p = 0.650; static notch, p = 0.794; overshoot, p = 0.735) (divergence: notch, p = 0.357; static notch, p = 0.114; overshoot, p = 0.384).

Additionally, no correlation was found between the degree of horizontal deviation, as measured by the alternate prism cover test at distance, and the frequency or presence/absence of any small eye movements (convergence: notch, r = − 0.049, p = 0.844; static notch, r = − 0.050, p = 0.840; overshoot, r = − 0.278, p = 0.250) (divergence: notch, r = 0.201, p = 0.409; static notch, r = − 0.014, p = 0.954; overshoot, r = − 0.084, p = 0.734). We investigated the relationship between small eye movements and the baseline angle of exotropia, and no significant correlations were found.

Postoperative assessment

Three months after surgery, ocular deviation was significantly improved compared with the preoperative measurements, with a mean of 13.9 ± 7.0 PD for near and 9.7 ± 6.2 PD for distance (p < 0.001 for both). The time required for ocular movements during convergence and divergence was 0.28 ± 0.16 and 0.30 ± 0.19 s, respectively. There were no significant changes compared with baseline.

The postoperative frequencies of small eye movements were as follows. During convergence, static notch events were observed in 35% of patients, notch events in 20%, and overshoot events in 5%, indicating a significant reduction in static notch events (p = 0.025). During divergence, no static notch events were observed; however, notch events were seen in five (25%) participants and overshoot events in three (15%), showing a tendency toward a lower frequency of overshoot events (p = 0.073) (Table 2).

Evaluation with the adult Strabismus-20 (AS-20) questionnaire

Table 3 shows the mean scores for the 20 subscale items on the AS-20 Questionnaire in patients with iXT preoperatively and 3 months postoperatively.

In the preoperative assessment, the total score for the psychosocial subscale was 19.5 ± 8.2 of 50, and the functional subscale scored 21.9 ± 8.4. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated between each AS-20 Questionnaire item and the frequency of specific eye movement events. No corrections for multiple comparisons were applied. Significant correlations were observed between static notch events and two reading-related items—Q16: “I have problems reading because of my eye condition” (r = 0.54, p = 0.003) and Q20: “I need to take frequent breaks when reading because of my eyes” (r = 0.53, p = 0.003). Additionally, a significant correlation was found between overshoot events and one concentration-related item—Q13: “I stop doing things because my eyes make it difficult to concentrate” (r = 0.53, p = 0.003).

Following surgery, the AS-20 Questionnaire results showed improvements in both psychosocial and functional subscale scores, with the functional subscale score demonstrating significant improvement (psychosocial, p = 0.111; functional, p = 0.001). Specifically, there were marked improvements in the subscales for depth perception and reading.

We examined the relationship between changes in AS-20 Questionnaire scores before and after surgery and changes in the frequency of small eye movements using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. A significant negative correlation was found between the postoperative reduction in static notch events during convergence and improvement in responses to item Q11: “It is easier to function with one eye closed” (r = − 0.6694, p = 0.024). In addition, the reduction in overshoot frequency during divergence after surgery was significantly correlated with improvement in the reading-related item Q12 (r = 0.817, p = 0.002) and showed a trend toward correlation with item Q13, which relates to concentration (r = 0.552, p = 0.079).

Discussion

We conducted this study to elucidate abnormalities in eye movements during convergence and divergence in individuals with iXT. Previous reports have shown that individuals with iXT may experience discomfort during reading or dissatisfaction with their vision while driving9,10. In these situations, they may engage in horizontal eye movements or perform repetitive convergence and divergence movements. Even if exotropia is not readily apparent, there may be underlying abnormalities different from those in healthy individuals, leading to a perceived inconvenience in visual experiences as evidenced by the lower functional scale scores on the AS-20 Questionnaire.

Using an eye-tracking device, we were able to identify characteristic changes in eye movements during convergence and divergence in individuals with iXT. Additionally, we observed the effects of strabismus surgery on these small eye movements.

Regarding the velocity of eye movements, previous studies have shown that convergence is often, though not always, faster than divergence in normal individuals and athletes11–15. In our study, both the control group and the iXT group tended to take less time for convergence than for divergence. Notably, significantly more time was required for convergence in the iXT group than in the control group. During gaze shifts between targets at 30 cm and 60 cm, the observed eye movements were likely influenced by a combination of fusional, proximal, and accommodative vergence mechanisms. In patients with iXT, we hypothesize that a greater reliance on fusional convergence, rather than accommodative or proximal components compared with controls, may have contributed to the prolonged convergence time. This interpretation is based on the task demands and temporal characteristics of the observed responses and should be regarded as speculative.

In terms of fine eye movements during convergence and divergence, we identified three types of small eye movements, which we referred to as “notch,” “static notch,” and “overshoot.” The frequency of notch events during convergence was not significantly different between the two groups; however, during divergence, the frequency of notch events was significantly higher in the iXT group (35%) than in the control group (5%) (p = 0.031). This suggests that smooth divergence fusion may be more challenging in some individuals with iXT. Static notch, an eye movement seen during convergence, was observed in 70% of the iXT group but in only 30% of the control group, with a significant difference (p = 0.022). Static notch can be explained by the slight abduction of the non-dominant eye that occurs immediately after initiating the convergence movement, contributing to the prolonged convergence time in the iXT group. These movements may reflect saccade–vergence interactions, as reported in previous studies16–18. In particular, Zee et al.17 demonstrated that vergence responses are often accompanied by transient saccadic signals, resulting in an initial conjugate movement followed by a vergence-driven adjustment. Semmlow et al.18 further noted that such saccade-induced midline errors can occur even during pure vergence tasks, followed by corrective disjunctive responses. The static notch events we observed—brief, asymmetric movements followed by a pause—may reflect this type of interaction, where incomplete suppression of saccadic activity at the onset of vergence causes a transient misalignment, which is then corrected by a delayed fusional response.

This interpretation aligns with the possibility that conjugate “notch” and “static notch” responses may represent forms of saccade–vergence interaction, as previously suggested. Such interactions may involve initial conjugate components followed by disjunctive adjustments, potentially accounting for the characteristic patterns observed in our recordings. We acknowledge that this interpretation is speculative and that further studies using high-resolution oculomotor recordings will be necessary to validate these mechanisms. Overshoot events, which reflect less coordinated or inefficient control of eye movements, occurred at the end of divergence in the iXT group, appearing in 40% of this group compared with only 10% in the control group (p = 0.047). Eyes with iXT may temporarily return toward the original exotropic position after divergence and then try to maintain the normal position with fusional convergence, which could explain the overshoot events.

According to Schor’s model, vergence control is governed by the interplay of tonic innervation and phasic inputs such as retinal disparity, blur, and proximal cues. This model explains the mechanisms underlying both accommodation and convergence19,20. In our experiment, a change in fixation distance simultaneously provided retinal disparity, blur, and proximal cues, all of which contributed to the vergence response.The model suggests that a neural integrator is located in the brainstem, with each control system incorporating both a fast and a slow neural integrator. These integrators transmit signals to the ciliary muscle and extraocular muscles. When a change in viewing distance creates a disparity, the fast neural integrator activates, followed by compensatory accommodative and convergence movements. The static notch is likely a result of inaccuracies in the fast neural integrator, whereas the notch and overshoot are attributed to errors occurring during compensatory accommodative and convergence movements.

In the AS-20 Questionnaire, individuals with iXT scored poorly on reading-related items within the functional subscale, with both static notch and overshoot correlating with these reading items. Convergence and divergence movements are crucial for smooth impulse eye movements. Static notch and overshoot events may interfere with near-vision tasks, such as reading, in some individuals with iXT.

Following surgery, the frequency of static notch events during convergence and overshoot events during divergence significantly decreased. These results may indicate that improving eye alignment is beneficial for visual performance. Mihara et al.4 also reported that strabismus surgery decreased the increased peak velocities of horizontal saccades in the operated eye, although the velocities did not fully reach those observed in the control group.

The marked improvements in depth perception and reading subscales on the AS-20 Questionnaire after surgery support the importance of normalizing eye alignment. We previously observed improvements in eye fatigue-related pain and general visual perception after strabismus surgery10. Thus, the reduction in the frequency of abnormal small eye movements after surgery may contribute to subjective improvements in visual comfort or reduced eye fatigue, although a direct causal relationship has yet to be established.

A major limitation of this study was the small number of participants. Additionally, we excluded children younger than 15 years with iXT. Further investigations may contribute to a better understanding of the pathophysiology of iXT. Another limitation of this study is the absence of an unoperated control group for comparison with the postoperative results of the iXT patients.

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted at Hyogo Medical University Hospital in Japan. The research protocol adhered to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hyogo University of Medicine Hospital (Review Board Number 3499).

Participants

We recruited patients with iXT who were scheduled to undergo strabismus surgery at the Department of Ophthalmology, Hyogo Medical University Hospital between April 2021 and January 2022. The inclusion criteria were an age of 15–40 years, the ability to maintain fusion and convergence, corrected visual acuity better than 0.15 logMAR, measured monocularly for distance vision with the naked eye or contact lenses, and no history of surgery for ocular diseases, such as retinal detachment. Age-matched controls with orthophoria or a horizontal exophoria of ≤ 8 PD at both near and distance were also included. All participants underwent an assessment of their eye movements during convergence and divergence using an eye-tracking system (Tobii Pro Spectrum; Tobii Pro AB, Danderyd, Sweden).

In addition to eye movement examinations, the AS-20 Questionnaire was administered to the patients with iXT both preoperatively and 3 months postoperatively. Every participant provided written informed consent upon their inclusion in the study.

Clinical examination

The angle of deviation was assessed using the alternate prism cover test (APCT) for both near and distance. APCT was performed at 5 m for distance fixation and 30 cm for near fixation. The type of iXT was classified into three categories: basic, divergence excess, and convergence insufficiency.

AS-20 questionnaire

The AS-20 Questionnaire (available at http://www.pedig.net) assesses both the functional and psychosocial aspects of strabismus21. Respondents answer each question on a 5-point scale: “never,” “rarely,” “sometimes,” “often,” and “always.” A score of 1 was assigned to “never,” and a score of 5 was assigned to “always.”

Measurement of eye movements during convergence and divergence

Eye tracking was performed using the Tobii Pro system at a sampling frequency of 300 Hz. Visual stimuli were presented on a 23.8-inch monitor (EIZO FlexScan EV2451; EIZO Corporation, Hakusan, Japan) running at 300 Hz with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels (16:9 aspect ratio). The participants sat with their heads on a chin rest positioned 60 cm from the monitor, with a 60-cm rail installed from the screen to the chin rest. A visual target for near sight was presented 30 cm in front of the screen and 30 cm from the participant (Fig. 5). The near target consisted of a black asterisk (1 cm in diameter) printed on white paper and suspended from a rail. It was carefully positioned so that the participant’s line of sight and the asterisk were aligned along the same straight axis. Both targets were placed along the participant’s midline. When an asterisk appeared on the monitor 60 cm ahead, the participants were instructed to gaze at the asterisk. When the asterisk disappeared, they were expected to gaze at the target 30 cm in front of them. The size of the target on the screen and the near target was the same, with a visual angle of 1.9°.

We did not quantify the proportion of time that exotropia was manifest during free viewing.

Analysis of eye movements

The eye tracker provided the three-dimensional eye position and gaze direction for each eye. We defined the “distance to the gazing point” as the distance from the midpoint between the two eyes to the intersection point of the binocular lines of sight, rather than the physical distance to the fixation stimulus. This value represents the instantaneous binocular point of gaze and was used to calculate the angle of convergence. A right-handed coordinate system was defined such that the horizontal direction on the monitor corresponded to the X-axis and the direction perpendicular to the monitor corresponded to the Z-axis (Fig. 6). The positions of the right and left eyes were denoted as (XR, ZR) and (XL, ZL), respectively. For each eye, the gaze direction was expressed as a unit vector derived from the measured gaze angle (θR for the right eye and θL for the left eye), with X and Z components denoted as (direction XR, direction ZR) and (direction XL, direction ZL).

The distance to the gazing point was necessary to calculate the angle of convergence; this angle was defined as 1 m divided by the distance to the gazing point. Overshoot events were defined as disjunctive movements in which the convergence angle exceeded the final steady-state value by ≥ 0.2 MA at the end of the vergence response.

The primary outcome variable was movement time, defined as the duration between the onset and endpoint of the eye movement.

The start and end of eye movements were defined as the 10% point between the beginning and end of the variable angle of convergence, and the time required from the start to the end was used as the duration for each convergence and divergence (Fig. 7).

We compared the time required for convergence and divergence, along with the associated eye movements, between the iXT group and the control group. To analyze the time required for convergence and divergence, data from 4 of 10 trials in which the eye position values were accurate and not missing were used to calculate the average value. If characteristic small ocular movements were detected in two or more of the four trials, the eye was defined as positive.

Statistical analysis

We used JMP Pro 16 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) to determine the significance of differences, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the time during convergence and divergence, and the chi-squared test was used to compare the frequency of occurrence for each type of eye movement. The correlation between the AS-20 Questionnaire results and the duration of eye movements, as well as small eye movements, was examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

To investigate the relationship between small eye movements and the baseline angle of exotropia, we performed point-biserial correlation analyses, using the presence or absence of each eye movement type (notch, static notch, and overshoot) as a binary variable and the baseline deviation angle as a continuous variable.
