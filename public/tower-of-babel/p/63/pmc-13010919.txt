Dietary effect of nano-encapsulated probiotics and alpha-lipoic acid in improving blood parameters, immunity, gut health, gene expression, and growth performance in heat-stressed broilers
Authors: Haifa Ali Alqhtani, Hadeel A Almamoory, Ahmed M Elbaz, Hany A Thabet, Ahmed Ateya, Ayman Abd El-Aziz, Ahmed M Elkanawaty, Ahmed Sabry Arafa, Rowa K Zarah, Elham Mohammed Alzahrani, Fatmah Ahmed Safhi, Manal A Babaker, AbdelRahman Y Abdelhady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat stress in broilers disrupts vital functions, leading to decreased performance and overall health, as well as significant economic losses. To alleviate the negative influences of heat stress on broilers, several strategies have been employed, most notably dietary supplementation. The present study investigates the impact of supplementing nano-encapsulated probiotics and alpha-lipoic acid on growth performance, gut microbiota, immune response, and gene expression in heat-stressed broilers. A total of 360 one-day-old male Ross 308 broiler chicks were randomly assigned to four groups (6 replicate per group): a control group fed a basal corn–soybean diet (CON) and three treatment groups receiving the basal diet supplemented with nano-encapsulated probiotics (NC-P, 1 × 106 CFU/g of diet), alpha-lipoic acid (ALA, 300 mg/kg), or their combination (CNPA). Supplementing the CNPA mixture significantly improved growth performance, as evidenced by enhanced feed conversion ratio, body weight gain, carcass traits, and up-regulated IGF-1 gene expression (P < 0.05). Moreover, the mixture decreases cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-cholesterol, and uric acid levels (P < 0.05). CNPA mixture improves the ability to adjust heat stress by enhancing antioxidant status, via increased superoxide dismutase (SOD), and decreased malondialdehyde (MDA, P < 0.05). Furthermore, CNPA mixture reduces pathogenic bacteria and up-regulates the expression of cationic amino acid transporter-1 (CAT-1, P < 0.05) and mucin 2 (MUC-2) genes, enhancing intestinal health. Overall, the combination of nano-encapsulated probiotics with α-lipoic acid effectively mitigates heat stress impacts in broilers.Keywords: Broiler performance, Nano-encapsulated probiotics, Alpha-lipoic acid, Gut integrity, Heat stress

Introduction

Despite significant advancements in the poultry industry, including the provision of favorable conditions for optimal production performance through the construction of barns equipped with modern technologies (ventilation and pad cooling), poultry farmers still face numerous challenges (Wasti et al., 2021), including environmental heat stress, which causes substantial economic losses (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2021). Exposure to heat stress in birds causes numerous negative changes in behavior, neuroendocrine responses, physiology, and immunity (Elbaz et al., 2025a; Fathi et al., 2025), thus deteriorating productive performance. This has recently prompted researchers to investigate dietary additives capable of alleviating heat stress, including antioxidants, enzymes, herbal extracts, and organic acids (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2024; Elbaz et al., 2025b,c; Fathi and Rezaee, 2025), which have been shown to improve growth performance and health status in poultry.

Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) has recently attracted attention for its antioxidant properties and its involvement in fatty acid metabolism and energy metabolism (Wasti et al., 2021). Alpha lipoic acid is produced in small quantities by the body's cells and achieves high bioavailability due to its water and fat solubility, as well as its easy absorption in the intestine (Sohaib et al., 2018). Previous reports have demonstrated numerous properties of ALA, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immune-stimulating, and growth-promoting effects (Guo et al., 2014). Most important properties of ALA are its antioxidant properties, particularly in its oxidized and reduced forms, which work to eliminate free radicals and produce other antioxidants, as well as play a role in glucose metabolism and have demonstrated metal-chelating activity (Sohaib et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2020).

Probiotics have emerged as a safe alternative to antibiotic growth promoters for controlling pathogens (Ismail et al., 2023). They are live microorganisms that enhance host health and productivity (Febrian et al., 2025), mainly through modulation of gut microbiota, intestinal colonization, and competitive exclusion of harmful bacteria (Elbaz et al., 2025a,c). Moreover, their antimicrobial activity involves production of antibacterial metabolites, competition for nutrients, and inhibition of pathogen adhesion to epithelial receptors (Śmiałek et al., 2021). Additionally, probiotic supplementation improves immune response, intestinal morphology, and expression of genes associated with gut integrity and health in chickens (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2025; Febrian et al., 2025).

Nano-encapsulation has emerged as a promising technology in poultry nutrition (Wu et al., 2024). Its importance lies in preserving the stability and bioactivity of encapsulated compounds while protecting them from adverse environmental conditions encountered during feed processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit (Pateiro et al., 2021; Ismail et al., 2023). Many reports have demonstrated that encapsulation enhances the efficacy, bioavailability, and stability of dietary supplements (Ismail et al., 2023; Febrian et al., 2025). The nano-encapsulation protects probiotic bacteria from stomach acid and bile salts, enabling the bacteria to survive and multiply more effectively in the intestines.

Despite the documented individual benefits of probiotics and alpha-lipoic acid, information available on their use together in nano-encapsulation technology under conditions of heat stress is limited. Therefore, we hypothesized that combining nano-encapsulated probiotics and alpha-lipoic acid would exert synergistic effects, improving immune-oxidant status and intestinal integrity, thereby mitigating the negative effects of heat stress in broiler chickens. Thence, the objective of the current study was to evaluate the impact of supplementing nano-encapsulated probiotics, alpha-lipoic acid, and their combination on performance, blood parameters, immune response, and gut health of heat-stressed broilers.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental chicks were cared for in accordance with the regulations of the Institutional Animal and Poultry Ethics Committee of Desert Research Center and Ain Shams University, Cairo, Egypt (approval No, #5-2025-43), regarding animal welfare.

Materials preparation

Nano-encapsulated probiotics were obtained from the laboratories of the National Research Centre, which include Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis, ATCC 19659) and Lactobacillus plantarum (L. plantarum, ATCC 8014). Alpha Lipoic Acid was purchased from Doctor's Best, Inc., headquartered at 2742 Dow Street, Tustin, California, 92780. The manufacturing steps for Nano-encapsulated probiotics were as follows: B. subtilis was prepared and activated by inoculating it on LB agar and incubating it at 37°C for 24 hours under aerobic conditions. L. plantarum was prepared and activated by inoculating it on MRS agar and incubating it at 37°C for 72 hours under anaerobic conditions. Different bacterial cultures were collected to obtain a cell density of approximately 1 × 1010 colony-forming units per strain. Bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Chitosan (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) was prepared by dissolving it in 100 ml of distilled water and then acidifying it with 0.4% (w/v) glacial acetic acid. The pH of the chitosan solution was adjusted to 6.0-6.5 before adding the bacteria. The final concentration of bacterial cells was adjusted to be approximately 1 × 106 CFU/ml. Chitosan (low molecular weight) was combined with a dual-strain probiotic (freeze-dried) at room temperature and stirred at 300 rpm for 20 minutes to homogenize by using a magnetic stirrer at room temperature, with the addition of 5% skim milk (cryoprotectant) to protect bacterial viability during encapsulation. The mixture was then stored for 24 hours at 8°C before being lyophilized to improve stability.

Broilers care and experimental design

A total of 360 one-day-old Ross 308 male chicks were used in a completely randomized design with four experimental groups and 6 replicates of 15 chicks each. The control group (CON) received a basal diet consisting of corn-soybean meal without additives, and another three experimental groups fed nano-encapsulated probiotics (NC-P, 1 × 106 CFU/g of diet), alpha-lipoic acid (ALA, 300 mg/kg), or a combination of both (CNPA, at the same doses), respectively. All chicks were individually weighed upon arrival and fed the starter and grower diets. According to the nutrient requirements of Ross 308 chicks, a basal diet was formulated according to NRC (1994), as shown in Table 1. Chicks were allowed to consume fresh water and crumbled (1 to 10 days) and pelleted feed (11 days to the end) ad libitum. In the experiment, for the first two days, the temperature was set at 32.5°C and then gradually lowered to 21°C by day 35. To simulate summer conditions, the birds were exposed to 31.5±1.0°C (artificial gradual increase) from 12 p.m. to 5 p.m., starting from 10 days of the broiler’s age until the end of the experiment, according to the previously described thermal stress models through Fathi et al., (2025). In addition, the average relative humidity was 57 ± 10%, and the base temperature was maintained at 21 ± 1°C. Relative humidity and temperature were recorded as previously mentioned to calculate the temperature and humidity index (THI) according to the equation of Tao and Shen (2003) as shown in Figure 1. The average light intensity was set to 20 lux, and the birds were then exposed to light for 24 hours during the first week, then to complete darkness for two hours and light for 22 hours until the end of the experiment. Chicks had been vaccinated against Newcastle disease (NDV) and infectious bronchitis at the hatchery. Also, at day 7, chicks were injected subcutaneously in the back of the neck with inactivated avian influenza (H9N1) plus inactivated NDV vaccines, in addition to being vaccinated against infectious bursal disease at day 13 and NDV at day 9 and 19.

Performance

Broiler chicks were received, weighed, and randomly distributed to experimental treatments. Feed intake (FI) and live body weight (LBW) were recorded on days 21 and 35. Average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated. As well as, the European production efficiency factor (EPEF, Elbaz et al., 2025) were determined and the mortality rate was recorded daily. On day 35, Forty-eight broilers (12 chickens/group) were randomly selected for experimental sampling. These broilers were slaughtered, weighed, and dissected for carcass traits, including carcass yield, liver, breast, intestinal, thigh, and abdominal fat, as well as spleen, thymus, and bursa of Fabricius.

Experimental samples

At the end of the trial period (35 d), 12 chicks from each experimental group were randomly selected to take blood, intestinal, and liver samples before slaughter (at night). To isolate plasma, blood samples were collected from the jugular vein before slaughter into tubes containing an anticoagulant (EDTA). Blood samples were centrifuged at 3500 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C and then stored at -20°C. During slaughter, the intestinal bacterial contents were collected from 12 chickens per group. One gram of intestinal content was placed in sterile glass jars, and the necessary dilutions and homogenization were then performed to count the target bacteria. Duodenal content samples were obtained at slaughter to measure digestive enzyme activity and were stored at −20°C until further analysis. At slaughter for gene expression measurement, samples were taken from the small intestine and liver of 12 chickens per group, stored at −80°C, and transported immediately to the laboratory for gene expression analysis.

Measurements

Serum lipid profiles were measured by estimating the levels of cholesterol, glucose, and triglycerides using commercial kits (Spinreact Co., Girona, Spain) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Renal and liver functions were measured in serum by estimating total protein, albumin, creatinine, and uric acid levels, as well as aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) using commercial kits (Bio Diagnostic, Cairo, Egypt) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Serum immunoglobulin concentrations (IgA, IgM, and IgG) were determined using microtiter plates and chicken-specific ELISA quantitation kits (Bethyl Laboratories Inc., Montgomery, TX, USA) following the sandwich ELISA procedure. Blood stress markers, including triiodothyronine (T3) and corticosteroids (COR), were measured using commercial kits. The activities of oxidative enzymes, including glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), malondialdehyde (MDA), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), were determined. Serum cytokine levels of interleukin-10 (IL-10), tumor necrosis factor a (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were detected using commercially available ELISA kits (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA). Using special commercial kits to measure the activity of lipase, protease, and amylase (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) in the contents of the intestines. Additionally, 1 g of each cecal sample was homogenized in 9 ml of sterile peptone saline, stirred for 30 min to obtain a 10⁻¹ dilution, and then successive tenfold dilutions were prepared. From each dilution, 0.1 ml was transferred using a sterile pipette and distributed among three Petri dishes containing selective bacterial media specific for each microbe of interest, which included: Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Escherichia coli (E. coli), Clostridium perfringens (C. perfringens), and total coliforms. The inoculated plates were incubated under appropriate conditions for each microbe. Furthermore, the adequate ileum digest contents were collected and transferred into clean centrifuge tubes containing distilled deionized water. The mixture was homogenized thoroughly so that pH values could be measured in the ileum digest contents. The pH level was measured using a pH meter (Basic 20, Crison, Spain). In samples of the cecum contents, volatile fatty acids (VFAs) concentrations were determined using a gas chromatograph coupled with a mass spectrometer (Agilent 5975C, Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, Delaware, USA), following the procedures described by Shehata et al. (2022). Total RNA was extracted from eight replicates of liver and intestinal tissue in each group and homogenized in TRIzol buffer (Invitrogen, CA, USA) to assess the expression of hepatic insulin growth factor-1 (IGF-1), intestinal cationic amino acid transporter-1 (CAT-1), and intestinal mucin 2 (MUC-2) genes. The RNA was re-suspended in nuclease-free ultrapure distilled water, quantified, plotted, and concentrated using a NanoDrop ND-2000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Description of the genes and corresponding primers is as follows: and IGF-1 forward: 5′-GGTGCTGAGCTGGTTGATGC-3′, reverse: 5′-CGTACAGAGCGTGCAGATTTAGGT-3′ (JN942578), MUC2 forward: 5′-CATTCAACGAGGAGAGCTGC-3′, reverse: 5′- TTCCTTGCAGCAGGAACAAC-3′ (JX284122.1), CAT-1 forward: AGGCTTCCCATCTCACCTCA, reverse: TCTTCACATGCCATCCAGGT (NM_001398060.1), and GAPDH gene (NM_204305.2, housekeeping gene) were formed as follows: AGTCAACGGATTTGGCCGTA, reverse: ACAGTGCCCTTGAAGTGTCC, with the thermocycler protocol. The relative mRNA expression of target genes was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, as reported by Livak and Schmittgen, (2001).

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS software (Version 20.0), employing a completely randomized design. Differences between means were evaluated using the LSMEANS option in SPSS. Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used to separate the means. The normality of the experimental data was verified using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ±SD. Data were analyzed using the following general linear model: Yij​=μ+Ti+eij

Where: Yij; observed value of the dependent variable, μ; overall mean, Ti; fixed effect of the ith dietary treatment (i = 1–4), eij; random error associated with each observation, assumed to be independent and normally distributed.

Results

Productive performance

Broilers were exposed to heat stress, as indicated by the THI value, which varied among 28.8 and 30.8 during the entire experimental period, as shown in Figure 1. Table 2 illustrates the effect of nano-encapsulated probiotic and alpha-lipoic acid supplementation on growth performance. During the period 1–21 days, ADG was significantly higher in chickens receiving CNPA, NC-P, and ALA than in the control group (P < 0.05), while FCR tended to be better in chickens receiving CNPA (P = 0.016) compared to the other groups. During the period 22–35 days, ADG was significantly increased in chickens receiving CNPA, NC-P, and ALA compared to the control group, while ADG was highest in chickens receiving CNPA. Furthermore, FCR and ADFI were not affected (P = 0.078) between the experimental groups. During the period 1–35 days, ADG was significantly increased in CNPA-fed chickens compared to the other groups; the corresponding improvement percentages for ALA, NC-P, and CNPA were 2.83, 4.24, and 6.46%, respectively. FCR was better in chickens receiving CNPA, NC-P, and ALA compared with the CON group; with enhancement percentages for ALA, CNPA, and NC-P were 2.98, 5.36, and 3.57 %; and the best FCR was in chickens fed CNPA. However, ADFI was unaffected between the experimental groups during all experimental periods (P ≥ 0.05). Over the entire period, the mortality rate was lower (P = 0.010) in the groups receiving the experimental supplements compared to the control group; with reductions of for NC-P, ALA, and CNPA were 40, 20, and 40, respectively; however, the lowest mortality rate was observed in the NC-P and CNPA groups. Additionally, EPEF was higher in the NC-P, ALA, and CNPA groups than in the control group (P < 0.05), with corresponding improving by 10.87, 7.61, and 15.22 % for NC-P, ALA, and CNPA, respectively.

Blood biochemical indices

Biochemical indices were significantly influenced by adding nano-encapsulated probiotics and alpha-lipoic acid in heat-stressed broiler (Table 4, Table 5, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Triglycerides, LDL-cholesterol, and cholesterol levels significantly decreased in broilers fed NC-P, ALA, and CNPA compared to control groups. HDL levels were not changed among the experimental groups (P ≥ 0.05). However, there was an increase in the level of glucose in broilers fed NC-P and CNPA (P = 0.020) compared to ALA and the control groups. Creatinine levels increased (P =0.046) in chickens fed NC-P and CNPA compared to other groups, while uric acid and ALT significantly decreased in chickens fed NC-P, ALA, and CNPA (P =0.031) compared to the control. However, AST, total protein, and albumin concentrations were not changed among the experimental groups (P ≥ 0.05). The experimental supplementation significantly affected the stress markers, increasing triiodothyronine (T3, Fig. 3A) and corticosteroid (COR, Fig. 3B) levels in the NC-P, ALA, and CNPA groups compared to the CON group (P < 0.05). The highest T3 levels and the lowest COR levels were also found in the CNPA groups (P < 0.05).

Intestinal integrity

Table 6 and Figure 5 (A, B, and C) illustrate the effect of experimental dietary supplements on intestinal integrity and function. Chickens fed NC-P, CNPA, and ALA showed a significant decrease in pH compared to the control group. C. perfringens and E. coli counts also decreased (P < 0.05) in the groups fed NC-P, CNPA, and ALA supplements compared to the control group. Additionally, the Lactobacillus population increased in the broilers fed NC-P, CNPA, and ALA supplements, and the Bifidobacterium population also increased (P =0.002) in the NC-P and CNPA-fed broilers compared to the ALA and control groups. Furthermore, the coliform population remained unchanged between the experimental groups (P ≥ 0.05). The experimental supplementation increased the production of volatile fatty acids, as butyric acid and acetic acid increased in chickens fed NC-P, ALA, and CNPA compared to the CON group (P < 0.05). Propionic acid tended to increase in chickens that received ALA and CNPA (P =0.031) compared to the control and NC-P groups. Upregulation of gene expression of MUC2, IGF-1, and CAT-1, was observed in the NC-P, ALA, and CNPA groups compared to the control group (P < 0.05). MUC2 and IGF-1 gene expression were highest in the NC-P and CNPA groups, while CAT-1 gene expression was highest in the CNPA group (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Despite significant advancements in the poultry industry, heat stress remains a major challenge in poultry production (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2021). Dietary strategies utilizing supplements with antioxidant, immune-boosting, and antimicrobial properties are effective means of mitigating its effects. Therefore, this study evaluated nano-encapsulated probiotics and alpha-lipoic acid, which reduced indicators of heat stress such as panting, wing spreading, decreased triiodothyronine (T3) levels, and decreased antioxidant activity (Ma et al., 2020; Ismail et al., 2023), demonstrating the practical potential of CNPA supplementation in mitigating heat stress in broiler chickens.

In the current study, chickens fed a supplement-free diet and subjected to heat stress exhibited reduced productivity, accompanied by decreased immune response and oxidative stress. Dietary inclusion of nano-encapsulated probiotics with α-lipoic acid (CNPA) improved productive performance by enhancing body weight gain, feed conversion ratio, and increasing EPEF value, while simultaneously reducing mortality. These results are consistent with the observed improvement in gut health in the current study, manifested in reduced pathogenic microbes and inflammation, enhanced oxidative stability, and increased upregulation of CAT-1 and MUC2 genes. Furthermore, stimulated the immune response, increased the production of digestive enzymes, and upregulated the IGF-1 gene, thus promoting feeding efficiency and growth. These findings are consistent with previous reports on probiotics or alpha-lipoic acid (Guo et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2024). This is attributed to the numerous properties of the experimental additives, including their antimicrobial and immunomodulatory effects (Elbaz et al., 2021; Febrian et al., 2025). Furthermore, their role in promoting oxidative stability and reducing inflammation (Elbaz et al. 2025c) improves intestinal health. Additionally, they enhance microbial fermentation, volatile fatty acid production, and support intestinal integrity (Sohaib et al., 2018; Abdel-Moneim et al., 2025), thereby improving intestinal metabolic functions (Pateiro et al., 2021; Wasti et al., 2021; Shehata et al., 2022). These are key elements in the EPEF assessment, resulting in a significant improvement in EPEF. Based on the above, the CNPA blend effectively alleviates the detrimental physiological and metabolic effects of heat stress by maintaining oxidative balance, immune response, and intestinal integrity, thereby supporting growth performance and well-being of broilers.

Data from the current trial showed that CNPA supplementation positively influenced carcass traits, increasing carcass and breast yield while reducing abdominal fat. The improvement in carcass weight is attributed to increased nutrient availability, improved protein accumulation, and enhanced energy distribution through the role of probiotics in stabilizing gut microbiota and reducing intestinal inflammation (Elbaz et al., 2021; Shehata et al., 2022), which increases nutrient digestion and amino acid absorption (Wasti et al., 2021; Miao et al., 2025), thus providing more materials necessary for muscle protein synthesis. Meanwhile, alpha-linolenic acid plays a role as a cofactor in maintaining cell integrity by promoting oxidative stability and reducing fat deposition by scavenging free radicals, which protects muscle tissues and further supports growth (Sohaib et al., 2018). As well as its modulated metabolic processes, thereby improving cellular energy production and protein synthesis, and supporting muscle growth. Our results are consistent with previous reports on the role of probiotics in promoting carcass weight (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2024). This complementary effect improved nutrient utilization efficiency, thereby increasing muscle growth, raising the carcass composition and meat yield, and reducing fat accumulation, especially under conditions of heat stress.

Additionally, our study showed that taking CNPA blend supplements significantly increases the activity of amylase and protease enzymes. This improvement can be explained by several complementary mechanisms related to the synergistic effect of probiotics with alpha-lipoic acid. This is due to the role of probiotics in modifying the gut microbiota composition and stimulating exocrine pancreatic function by increasing the gene expression of digestive enzymes, which increases endocrine enzyme production. Furthermore, the antioxidant properties of ALA protect pancreatic tissue and the intestinal mucosa, thus preserving enzyme synthesis and secretion capacity. Similarly, Miao et al. (2025) and Idowu et al. (2025) reported that probiotic and ALA supplementation effectively enhanced the secretion of digestive enzymes such as amylase and protease, which support nutrient digestion and absorption. Furthermore, some beneficial bacteria produce extracellular enzymes that promote nutrient digestion (Bromfield et al., 2024). Probiotics can also indirectly stimulate the pancreas and intestinal mucosa to produce more digestive enzymes by stimulating the secretion of certain hormones that regulate pancreatic enzyme secretion. The combination of probiotics and ALA is likely to produce synergistic effects by enhancing intestinal functions, protecting cellular structures and metabolic processes from oxidative damage, and thus increasing the activity of digestive enzymes, improving nutrient digestion, raising feed conversion efficiency, and thus promoting growth, particularly in conditions of heat stress where digestive efficiency is usually affected (Elbaz et al., 2021; 2025a).

Furthermore, the results showed that CNPA supplementation modulated broilers' stress responses by influencing endocrine and metabolic pathways, which increased T3 levels and decreased COR levels. The probiotics enhance intestinal barrier integrity, reduce systemic inflammation, and regulate gut microbiota (Elbaz et al. 2025a; Shehata et al. 2022), thereby decreasing the activity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the subsequent reduction in corticosteroid release. Additionally, they support thyroid function, promoting T4-to-T3 conversion, thereby improving growth regulation and energy metabolism (Elbaz et al., 2025a), ultimately improving metabolic efficiency. Concurrently, ALA plays a key role in alleviating physiological stress thanks to its potent antioxidant and metabolic regulatory properties (Ma et al. 2020; Wasti et al. 2021). Therefore, CNPA enhances stress tolerance, metabolic efficiency, and physiological adaptation in heat-stressed broilers.

In broilers, fat metabolism is negatively affected by heat stress through increased oxidative stress, impaired lipid regulation in the liver, disruption of endocrine regulation, and promotion of lipid deposition (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2021), which is evident in elevated blood cholesterol and triglyceride levels, that is consistent with the findings of the current study. Supplementation with probiotics or α-lipoic acid mitigated these effects by lowering triglyceride and cholesterol levels. Probiotics modify the gut microbiota composition, which supports bile salt hydrolase activity, production of SCFAs, and reduces cholesterol reabsorption, thereby lowering serum total cholesterol and supporting liver lipid metabolism (Elbaz et al., 2021, 2025; Liu et al., 2025). On the other hand, ALA inhibits fatty acid synthesis and AMP-activated protein kinase (Chen et al., 2012), exhibiting lowering serum triglyceride, total cholesterol, and LDL-cholesterol levels (Mousavi et al., 2019).

Nitrogen metabolism balance and antioxidant status are indicators of kidney function, with blood uric acid reflecting protein breakdown or renal excretion and creatinine reflecting muscle metabolism (Cui et al., 2025). Our study results showed that adding the CNPA mixture reduced uric acid levels and slight increasing creatinine levels, indicating improved kidney function. This is consistent with several reports indicating that probiotic supplementation or ALA enhances kidney function by lowering blood uric acid levels (Sugiharto et al., 2018; Latipudin et al., 2024). The probiotics modulate the gut microbiota and reduce the absorption of toxic metabolites such as uric acid and nitrogen retention (Bi et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2025), thereby lowering serum uric acid concentrations and reducing the metabolic burden on the kidneys. Furthermore, ALA, as a potent antioxidant and metabolic cofactor (Guo et al., 2014), mitigates oxidative damage to kidney tissue, thus supporting greater filtration and excretory efficiency in the kidneys. Additionally, a slight increase in blood creatinine may indicate the mixture's role in improving muscle metabolism, thus increasing protein metabolism, as well as rising renal urea excretion rate (Latipudin et al., 2024), which supports kidney function.

Liver enzymes are among the most important indicators of liver health. The current study showed a decrease in ALT levels in the blood of chickens fed the CNPA mixture, while AST levels remained unchanged. This demonstrates the potential of probiotics with alpha-lipoic acid mixture supplementation to improve liver function in broilers. This positive impact of probiotics may be attributed to their primary role in promoting intestinal health by modifying the intestinal microbial composition (N. Elbaz et al., 2025), as well as their antioxidant role through stimulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes, thus these changes reduce inflammation and oxidative stress, contributing to the maintenance of liver cell function and integrity. Consistent with our findings, several reports have found that probiotic supplementation reduces ALT levels (Elsaadawi et al., 2022; Shani et al., 2024), thus decreasing hepatocyte leakage and improving liver integrity.

Under heat stress conditions, broiler chickens suffer from immunosuppression due to hormonal imbalances and inflammation (Fathi et al., 2025), which impairs antibody production and immune cell function. In the current study, supplementing the CNPA mixture enhanced the immune response by increasing the relative weight of the bursa of Fabricius and levels of IgA, IgG, and IL-10, while decreasing IL-6 levels. The improved immune response may be attributed to the role of probiotics in enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, modifying gut microbiota composition, activating intestinal-associated lymphoid tissue, and promoting the production of SCFAs (Wu et al., 2024), thus enhancing both innate and adaptive immunity. In parallel, ALA acts as a potent antioxidant, regenerating cellular antioxidants, directly removing reactive oxygen species, and decreasing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Sohaib et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2020), thereby protecting immune cells and increasing immunoglobulin production. Similarly, some reports indicate that the adding probiotics or ALA has a positive effect on stimulating the immune response (Sohaib et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2024; Febrian et al., 2025) via increasing immune organ function, antibody production, and reducing inflammation. Together, these mechanisms explain the improved immune competence observed in heat-stressed broilers.

The antioxidant defense system in heat-stressed chickens is disrupted (Fathi and Rezaee, 2025), leading to oxidative stress that impairs cell integrity and physiological functions. Our results showed that supplementing the CNPA mixture enhanced the oxidative stability of heat-stressed broilers by increasing the activity of oxidative enzymes, including GSH-Px, CAT, and SOD, while decreasing MDA levels. Similar effects have been reported for probiotics and α-lipoic acid (Elsaadawi et al., 2022; Abdel-Raheem et al., 2024). This improvement in the antioxidant system is attributed to several mechanisms: probiotics modulate the composition of the gut microbiota, promoting beneficial bacteria that produce many bioactive compounds and SCFAs with antioxidant properties (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2025; Elbaz et al., 2025a) which improves the absorption of nutrients, particularly vitamins and minerals (Ismail et al., 2023), which are vital cofactors for the activity of antioxidant enzymes. Alpha-linolenic acid, on the other hand, acts as a powerful natural antioxidant that enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes (Dai et al. 2020; Ma et al., 2020; Wasti et al., 2021), and it plays a key role in neutralizing free radicals, restoring redox balance, and preventing electron leakage by promoting mitochondrial function (Parveen et al., 2013).

Heat stress disrupts the balance of the intestinal environment, including microbial content, acidity, and production of VFAs (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2021; Wasti et al., 2021), negatively affecting the intestinal health of poultry. In the current study, the CNPA mixture increased the Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium count and VFAs concentrations while decreasing pathogens and the pH value, which is consistent with many previous reports (Wasti et al., 2021; Abdel-Raheem et al., 2024). Probiotic supplements can play a role in enhancing the poultry gut environment through several mechanisms, including microbial balance, intestinal pH, and producing antimicrobial metabolites (like SCFAs) (Zhu et al., 2017), creating favorable conditions for the growth of acid-resistant beneficial bacteria and inhibiting harmful microbes (Han et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2021). Meanwhile, ALA can improve the gut environment of heat-stressed chickens by lowering intestinal pH through promoting microbial balance (Li et al., 2019; Wasti et al., 2021). This increases the growth of beneficial bacteria that produce SCFAs, which in turn promotes the growth of fermentative species that produce VFAs (Wasti et al., 2021). VFAs also modulate immune responses and strengthen the intestinal barrier, thus promoting intestinal integrity (Naeem and Bourassa, 2025).

At the molecular level, heat stress exerts numerous effects on gene expression patterns involved in stress response, immune regulation, and metabolic pathways, aiming to restore internal balance and protect vital functions. In the current study, CNPA supplementation modifies gene expression by up-regulating CAT-1, IGF-1, and MUC2 gene expression, which supports intestinal integrity and growth performance in heat-stressed broiler chickens. In agreement with these results, Wasti et al. (2021) and Elbaz et al. (2025a,c) found increasing CAT-1, IGF-1, and MUC2 gene expression in chickens fed a diet including probiotics and alpha-lipoic acid. IGF-1 is a key gene for regulating growth through hormonal and metabolic processes (Fujita et al. 2019), and its expression is often reduced under heat stress. The IGF-1 gene-enhancing effect of CNPA supplementation may be attributed to its role in gut health and endocrine balance, thus supporting protein synthesis and growth. The MUC2 gene is also key in maintaining the mucous barrier (Zeinali et al., 2017). CNPA combination stimulates MUC2 gene expression by reducing intestinal inflammation, enhancing goblet cell activity, and boosting mucosal immunity, collectively protecting epithelial cells and facilitating nutrient absorption. The CAT-1 gene is responsible for the absorption of cationic amino acids (Elbaz et al., 2025a). CNPA supplementation has been shown to enhance CAT-1 gene expression by improving intestinal morphology and redox balance (Yao et al., 2021), ensuring efficient nutrient transport. These results indicate that the CNPA mixture can mitigate the harmful molecular effects in heat-stressed broiler chickens.

In conclusion, adding a CNPA mixture to broiler chicken diets effectively improves growth rate, feed efficiency, and carcass characteristics in heat-stressed broilers. Meanwhile, it enhances immune response, oxidative stability, and gut health by regulating enzyme activity, gene expression, and reducing pathogenic microbes. Furthermore, the mixture positively regulates key blood parameters, supporting overall physiological health. Therefore, adding a CNPA mixture to broiler chicken feed is a practical and effective strategy to mitigate the negative effects of heat stress in commercial production. Findings are limited to the experimental duration and conditions.
