Can automated copy number variation pathogenicity prediction tools replace manual review under the ACMG framework? A clinical validation
Authors: Yingying Zhang, Xiuming Zhang, Dayang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCopy Number Variation (CNV) is associated with numerous complex diseases, yet their pathogenicity assessment remains heavily dependent on manual expert review, a process that is cumbersome and time-consuming. Although a variety of automated tools for CNV pathogenicity prediction have been developed, systematic assessments of their performance and clinical generalizability under the five-category classification framework defined by ACMG standards remain scarce.MethodsThis study constructed an expert-reviewed CNV benchmark dataset. Under the ACMG five-category system, it systematically compared the classification prediction capabilities of five prediction tools across both deletion and duplication CNV samples. Furthermore, it assessed their applicability in real clinical samples and evaluated potential biases towards specific variations.ResultsThe study indicates that different tools exhibit performance variations and specialised strengths across different classification tasks, yet overall capability in distinguishing clinical risk boundaries across the five categories remains inadequate. Among these, AnnotSV demonstrated outstanding performance in predicting pathogenic samples, achieving accuracy rates of 95.16% and 86.39% on the deletion and duplication benchmark datasets respectively. ClassifyCNV demonstrated optimal performance in variant of uncertain significance classification with an accuracy of 95.33%. Furthermore, all models exhibited significantly superior five-category discrimination capabilities for deletion samples compared with duplication samples, revealing pronounced performance biases across variant types.ConclusionCurrent automated CNV prediction tools demonstrate higher discriminatory capacity for deletion variants than for duplications; however, they remain insufficient to fully replace manual expert review within the ACMG five-category framework. Further optimisation using multicentre, consistently curated clinical gold-standard datasets is required to improve their reliability and clinical applicability.

Introduction

Copy number variation (CNV) refers to structural genomic variation involving the deletion or duplication of genomic segments, typically ranging from tens of base pairs to several megabases in size [1]. A growing body of evidence indicates that CNV is associated with numerous complex disorders, including intellectual disability, neurological diseases, and tumors, holding significant implications for understanding disease pathogenesis, clinical diagnosis, and prognostic assessment [2], [3]. Consequently, the precise detection and scientific interpretation of CNV are particularly crucial in clinical practice. This is especially true in prenatal screening, where CNV detection can effectively identify foetal chromosomal abnormalities, providing evidence for early intervention and clinical decision-making [4]. Advances in next-generation sequencing technology have markedly enhanced the detection efficiency and resolution of CNV, establishing a technical foundation for its clinical application [5]. Nevertheless, interpreting the pathogenicity of CNV remains a core limitation in current clinical practice. In clinical workflows, the assessment of CNV pathogenicity primarily relies on standardized interpretation by clinical professionals using the guidelines for interpreting CNV jointly published by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Clinical Genome Resource (hereafter referred to as the ACMG standards). This guideline employs a semi-quantitative scoring system, categorizing CNV pathogenicity into five levels. However, the guideline-based interpretation process still exhibits significant constraints. Clinicians must retrieve evidence from multiple databases and literature databases to integrate findings for scoring. The cross-validation and integration of evidence across databases are complex and time-consuming, while also being susceptible to human factors that compromise result consistency [6].

Automated tools for CNV pathogenicity interpretation have become an important strategy to improve efficiency in clinical genomics. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into two major paradigms: (1) rule-based automated systems that computationally operationalize ACMG standards into structured scoring and decision frameworks, and (2) data-driven machine learning-based models that infer pathogenicity patterns from labeled CNV datasets. Rule-based tools facilitate standardized and reproducible implementation of expert-curated interpretation criteria, thereby reducing manual workload and inter-operator variability. In parallel, data-driven machine learning-based models have shown advantages in handling high-dimensional genomic features and capturing non-linear relationships from large-scale datasets [7]. Such models have been explored in multiple clinical risk prediction tasks, including tumor risk assessment and prenatal screening for trisomy 21. For example, random forest–based models have achieved detection performance comparable to existing Down syndrome screening software under identical false-positive rates, supporting the feasibility of learning-based approaches in clinical risk stratification [8].Together, these developments highlight the emergence of automated CNV pathogenicity interpretation tools that offer complementary solutions to alleviate the bottlenecks of manual CNV interpretation in large-scale genomic testing.

Currently, predictive models for classifying the pathogenicity of CNV have been extensively developed. However, existing models for CNV pathogenicity classification generally lack of external validation based on independent third-party clinical datasets, and their generalisation capability and stability in real clinical settings remain insufficiently verified. Given this situation, this study conducted independent, systematic clinical validation of multiple published models for predicting CNV pathogenicity. We constructed and utilised a CNV dataset derived from real-world clinical testing to comprehensively evaluate the classification performance of each model. Furthermore, we conducted an in-depth analysis of their advantages and potential limitations in clinical applications. This study aims to provide a reference framework and practical support for the clinical interpretation and widespread adoption of CNV prediction models.

Methods

Screening and acquisition of automated CNV prediction models

A systematic literature search strategy was employed to identify prediction models related to CNV pathogenicity classification. In PubMed, we searched the keywords “SV clinical interpretation”, “CNV clinical interpretation”, “CNV Classification”, “CNV pathogenicity prediction”, and “CNV classify model” in titles and abstracts (Fig. 1). Abstracts were preliminarily screened, identifying 19 candidate models. Subsequently, exclusion criteria were established based on the timeliness of model interpretation standards, reproducibility, and clinical applicability to further refine the selection. Exclusion reasons included: (1) outdated interpretation systems, such as CNV-Webstore [9] published in 2011, which significantly diverged from current CNV clinical interpretation standards; (2) overlap with included models, such as CNV-ClinViewer [10] utilizing ClassifyCNV for output classification; (3) failure to provide executable model invocation methods or model invocation failures, e.g., SG-ADVISER CNV [11], SVInterpreter [12], CNVisi [13], REDBot [14]; (4) models reliant on manual or semi-automated scoring, such as CNVxplorer [15] and AutoCNV [16]; (5) outputs not compliant with the ACMG five classification system, such as ISV [17], SCIP [18], StrVCTVRE [19], TADeus2 [20], and CNVoyant [21] which generate other labels or risk coefficients. Establishing a clinical CNV benchmark dataset

To validate the potential application of these models in real-world clinical settings, this study retrieved and included five independent studies from PubMed that involved populations or patients with CNV and employed manual review for pathogenicity classification. All included studies classified CNV according to the ACMG standards during data construction. Prior to data integration, non-CNV, such as point mutations, SNVs, and other non-CNV data, were filtered out to ensure data type consistency and the reliability of clinical labels. Additionally, the internal dataset included cases detected as CNV-seq positive by the Central Laboratory of Third Affiliated Hospital of Shenzhen University between December 2019 and November 2025. Following sequencing completion, this internal cohort underwent manual interpretation and classification by clinical experts adhering to ACMG standards. Prior to data entry, all patient identifiers were removed, retaining solely CNV coordinates and clinical classification labels to construct an internal de-identified dataset. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Shenzhen University (Approval No.: 2026-LHQRMYY-KYLL-001). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of fully anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived in accordance with the applicable ethical guidelines.

The reference pathogenicity classifications were established through two pathways based on ACMG standards. For the internal clinical cohort, all CNVs were independently interpreted by two clinical geneticists. Both reviewers strictly followed the ACMG standards for CNV interpretation, incorporating weighted evidence categories including CNV length, gene content, population frequency, overlap with established pathogenic/benign regions, familial evidence, and phenotype consistency. For external cohorts derived from published studies, only datasets in which CNV classifications were generated according to ACMG standards were included. Furthermore, all source data underwent standardization procedures, including: (1) removing duplicate variants with identical chromosomal locations and variant types; (2) excluding records with conflicting clinical classifications within the same genomic region; (3) standardizing gene coordinate formats and variant type expressions.

Model operation and results collection

To ensure uniformity in genome versions, this study employed Python's pyliftover and UCSC LiftOver tools to batch-convert hg19 genome coordinates from the standardized dataset to the hg38 version. Subsequently, following each model's published calling protocols, the processed CNV data were input into AnnotSV, ClassifyCNV, dbCNV, MarCNV-ISV, and X-CNV for predictive analysis. All above models were obtained and run via GitHub or the official web interface, following the usage instructions provided in the original literature. In addition, MarCNV-ISV provided three operation modes (conservative, balanced, and progressive). In this study, all three modes were systematically evaluated. The balanced mode achieved the best overall performance across the main evaluation metrics and was therefore selected as the default configuration for all subsequent analyses in the main text. Results obtained from the conservative and progressive modes are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Upon completion of model predictions, the five-category pathogenicity prediction results for CNV output by each model were extracted and recorded for subsequent statistical analysis and performance evaluation.

Data analysis

The classification performance of the models was quantitatively assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), specificity, sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), false positive rate (FPR), and F1 score. Except for dbCNV, which solely outputs five-category CNV labels, all other models additionally provide risk scores or risk coefficients. To ensure comparability across all models within the same evaluation framework, when calculating AUC-ROC, the five-category outputs from dbCNV were mapped to an ordered numerical scale of 1–5: benign= 1, likely benign= 2, variant of uncertain significance (VUS)= 3, likely pathogenic= 4, pathogenic= 5.

All statistical analyses were performed in Microsoft Excel 2021. Model prediction result visualization and chart generation were conducted using Python 3.12.7. The generated charts facilitated comprehensive comparative analysis of model predictive performance and interpretation of results.

Overall discriminatory performance of all models

We plotted One-vs-Rest ROC curves to evaluate the overall discriminative capability of each model on this dataset (Fig. 3). Results indicate that across the duplication dataset, the overall discrimination ability of all models approached near-random discrimination levels, suggesting duplication variants exhibits heightened classification ambiguity in multi-class discrimination tasks. By contrast, the MarCNV-ISV ensemble model achieved relatively higher consistency on the duplication data (Macro AUC = 0.52), though the overall improvement was limited. Within the deletion dataset, all models demonstrated superior overall discriminative capability compared with duplications, exhibiting a more stable trend of class separation. Among these, the MarCNV-ISV model performed optimally on deletions (Macro AUC = 0.62), indicating its enhanced capability to establish reliable diagnostic prediction boundary separation when dealing with deletion CNV.Fig. 3The overall ROC curves for each model in the benchmark dataset. Consistency and error profile of predictions

We analyzed the consistency of model predictions and the characteristics of error distributions (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Regarding the reproducibility of benign labels, dbCNV demonstrated the highest classification stability. For both duplication and deletion types, the majority of benign records were correctly classified as benign (1096/1303, 84.11%). For VUS data, all models except ClassifyCNV and MarCNV-ISV consistently exhibited high error rates and minimal predictive convergence. Notably, X-CNV demonstrated error biases towards pathogenic and likely pathogenic classifications for both duplication and deletion events, resulting in a systematic overestimation tendency during VUS variant interpretation. Regarding consistency in pathogenic labeling, AnnotSV maintained optimal convergence in both duplication and deletion classification tasks (349/362, 96.41%). Furthermore, other models exhibited tight prediction flow and minimal divergence in deletion-pathogenic classifications, indicating stronger model separability within this dataset. Furthermore, analysis of misclassified cases revealed that prediction errors were predominantly enriched in duplication CNV (Fig. 5F). Taking AnnotSV as an example, the misclassification rates for duplication and deletion CNVs were 41.41% and 35.98%, respectively, indicating that duplication CNVs pose greater challenges for accurate interpretation than deletions. With respect to CNV size, although small-sized variants accounted for the largest proportion of all misclassified cases (Fig. 5G), stratified error rate analysis demonstrated that misclassification was mainly concentrated in CNV of intermediate length. Across all five evaluated tools, CNVs with sizes ranging from ≥ 5 Mb to < 10 Mb consistently exhibited the highest error rates, with dbCNV showing a particularly elevated misclassification rate of 58.82%, underscoring the increased difficulty of automated interpretation for intermediate-sized CNVs.

Within the benign category, dbCNV and X-CNV demonstrated higher benign recognition rates for both duplication and deletion variants, suggesting heightened sensitivity to benign CNV signals and clearer classification boundaries. In contrast, the clinically annotated tool ClassifyCNV exhibited similar benign recognition rates (49.90%, 53.10%), indicating persistent ambiguity in classifying benign variants.

In the pathogenic classification task, AnnotSV achieved high accuracy in both duplication and deletion variants (86.39%, 95.16%), demonstrating its strongest positive signal aggregation capability for high-confidence pathogenic duplications. Furthermore, both dbCNV and X-CNV demonstrated exceptionally high accuracy on the deletion-pathogenic dataset, suggesting that these models exhibit strong agreement in deletion pathogenicity classification.

The VUS dataset exhibited the greatest inter-model variation. ClassifyCNV achieved the highest accuracy for both duplication and deletion (94.92%, 95.74%), suggesting this model tends to retain a large proportion of CNV within the VUS category over time, exhibiting a stronger ‘conservative label persistence effect’. Conversely, X-CNV demonstrated the lowest accuracy in this classification (23.48%, 1.22%), with substantial samples being assigned to either benign or pathogenic categories. This indicates a more aggressive classification strategy, favouring the assignment of samples of uncertain significance to definitive conclusions.

Discussion

In this study, we observed that regardless of the comparison strategy employed, the discrimination ability of each model was significantly superior for deletion CNV samples compared to duplication CNV samples. This trend likely stems from deletion variants being biologically more prone to pathogenic effects, whereas duplication variants typically exhibit greater human tolerance. Consequently, deletion datasets are enriched with more clearly haploinsufficient genes [33]. These genes constitute key evidence for pathogenicity within the ACMG standards, which stipulate that a single confirmed haploinsufficient gene provides strong evidence for classifying a CNV as pathogenic. They also represent crucial training features for multiple models incorporated in this study. Consequently, the higher and more consistent classification resolution demonstrated by all models on deletion samples is anticipated [6]. This characteristic also enables dbCNV and X-CNV models centred on multi-dimensional genomic evidence to demonstrate significantly superior accuracy in the deletion-pathogenic category. Overall, deletion variants demonstrate markedly superior separability in models compared to duplication variants, suggesting existing AI methods are more sensitive to deletion-related evidence in multi-class CNV classification tasks. Conversely, duplication variants remain challenging to predict under a five-class labeling system, necessitating future integration of additional structural variation features or more refined evidence frameworks to enhance discriminative capability.

In addition, we found that CNVs with sizes ranging from ≥ 5 Mb to < 10 Mb represent the category most prone to misclassification across all prediction models. This vulnerability may arise because CNVs within this size range reside in a zone of evidentiary conflict and rule instability: they lack the clear benign evidence commonly observed in small CNVs, yet do not exhibit the strong pathogenic signals typically associated with large CNVs. As a result, such CNVs are particularly susceptible to classification drift within both the ACMG rule-based framework and model decision boundaries, making them a hotspot for prediction errors. Further analysis revealed that in the benchmark dataset, benign variants accounted for 47.10% of CNVs smaller than 5 Mb, whereas CNVs in the ≥ 5 Mb to < 10 Mb range were predominantly classified as VUS, likely pathogenic, or pathogenic. Small CNVs are more likely to be predicted as benign due to their limited gene coverage and the frequent absence of dosage-sensitive genes. By contrast, medium-sized CNVs may encompass a considerable number of genes but often lack genes with well-established disease associations, thereby substantially increasing the risk of misclassification by prediction tools.

Furthermore, different models exhibited distinct strengths across pathogenicity levels and CNV types. Some tools showed higher sensitivity in identifying benign variants, others performed better in distinguishing VUS, while certain models demonstrated superior performance in separating benign from pathogenic CNVs. Taking ClassifyCNV as an example, this tool relies exclusively on ACMG standards for classification; however, only approximately 50% of the guideline criteria can currently be implemented through automated scoring. As a result, a substantial proportion of CNVs cannot be fully evaluated using the complete set of ACMG rules and are consequently classified as VUS, a limitation commonly observed in rule-based automated interpretation systems [7]. In contrast, the three operating modes of MarCNV-ISV, a hybrid framework integrating rule-based ACMG standards with data-driven machine learning predictions, exhibited clear and clinically meaningful trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity (Supplementary Table S1). These modes correspond to different weighting coefficients assigned to the rule-based and machine learning components, thereby modulating the relative contribution of conservative guideline-driven evidence versus data-driven decision boundaries. Specifically, the balanced mode achieved the highest overall performance, with the best accuracy (69.81%), F1 score, and MCC, indicating superior global discriminative ability and prediction stability across classes. In contrast, the progressive mode, which assigns a higher weight to the data-driven component, showed relatively higher sensitivity (43.12%) at the expense of lower specificity (87.64%) and PPV (39.49%), reflecting a more aggressive classification strategy that favors reducing false negatives but introduces more false positives. Conversely, the conservative mode emphasizes rule-based ACMG evidence through higher weighting of guideline-derived scores, prioritizing specificity (88.28%) and PPV (50.53%) while sacrificing sensitivity (39.34%), making it more suitable for clinical scenarios requiring strict control of false positive pathogenic calls. These mode-dependent performance characteristics provide practical guidance for clinical users to select appropriate operating configurations according to different application scenarios and risk preferences. Importantly, the balanced mode consistently outperformed the other two modes across composite metrics, providing an objective basis for its selection as the primary configuration for subsequent comparative analyses in this study. This additional analysis reduces concerns regarding arbitrary parameter selection and strengthens the robustness of our evaluation framework. Nevertheless, despite these model-specific strengths, the overall clinical discriminatory power of all evaluated tools under the five-category framework remains limited, indicating that current algorithmic inference is not yet capable of fully replacing manual expert review.

Regarding the quality of model training data, its implications warrant careful consideration. Among the five evaluated models, AnnotSV and ClassifyCNV are primarily rule-based systems, whereas the remaining tools incorporate data-driven machine learning components. Notably, their training data were primarily sourced from public repositories such as ClinVar, ClinGen, and DGV. These databases may be affected by delayed updates, inconsistencies in evidence frameworks, and legacy annotations, with certain pathogenicity labels dating back more than a decade [34]. Given that the ACMG standards underwent a major revision in 2019 and that subsequent studies have continually identified novel pathogenic genes and dosage-sensitive regions, reliance on historical labels may introduce systematic noise. Such label heterogeneity can constrain model accuracy and blur the discriminative boundaries of the five-category clinical classification system. Within this context, data-driven machine learning approaches theoretically offer advantages. By integrating multidimensional genomic features and modelling continuous risk scores, these models can capture dosage sensitivity patterns and subtle evidence interactions that may not be fully represented in rule-based systems. However, despite these methodological strengths, the clinical deployment of existing models remains constrained. Reduced performance on independent validation cohorts suggests limited generalisability, while discrepancies among models likely arise from differences in feature engineering, training labels, and algorithmic design. Furthermore, limited interpretability of complex learning-based models continues to impede their direct adoption in clinical decision-making environments.

The benchmark dataset employed in this study also exhibits certain limitations. Notably, benign samples constitute nearly half of the duplication cohort, resulting in significant category imbalance within the evaluation set. Under these conditions, when a model demonstrates insufficient predictive capability for benign labels, its overall performance metrics, such as accuracy, F1 score, and ROC results, become substantially diluted by the abundance of benign samples, yielding outcomes that underrepresent its true predictive ability. Furthermore, although both the incorporated clinical research data and hospital cases were manually graded according to ACMG standards, these guidelines inherently constitute a semi-quantitative, multi-evidence cross-referencing scoring system reliant on expert judgement. This approach unavoidably introduces subjective decision-making bias during information retrieval and synthesis processes [35]. Consequently, even though manual interpretation is widely regarded as the "gold standard" in current clinical practice, it remains impossible to entirely eliminate potential errors in manual labeling, nor can manual interpretation guarantee absolute uniformity and objective accuracy in classifying all cases within the constructed dataset [36]. This factor similarly constrains the model's ability to demonstrate stronger discriminatory power in the five-classification task.

The cohort is substantially enriched for pregnancy-loss–related and prenatal structural anomaly cases. Such enrichment may influence model performance in several ways. First, prenatal and severe structural phenotypes are more frequently associated with large-effect or syndromic CNV, which may be more readily detected by both rule-based and learning-based models, potentially inflating sensitivity in this clinical context [7]. Second, the relative underrepresentation of milder neurodevelopmental phenotypes and asymptomatic individuals may limit the model’s exposure to smaller, borderline, or low-penetrance CNVs, which could affect specificity and performance stability when applied to postnatal diagnostic or population-based screening cohorts. Therefore, while the dataset reflects realistic referral patterns in prenatal and reproductive medicine, caution is warranted when extrapolating the present benchmarking results to cohorts with substantially different phenotype distributions.

In summary, this study finds that deletion exhibits higher detectability, which to some extent aligns with the biological characteristics of CNVs and the training feature configuration. The limitations in the model's clinical five-category classification performance may stem from multiple factors: outdated training data labels, delayed database updates, imbalanced dataset categories, and the inherent subjectivity of manual labeling. This underscores the need for future CNV pathogenicity prediction model development to: (1) incorporate more timely, high-quality, expert-consensus data resources; (2) enhance multi-class boundary modelling capabilities and improved interpretability of VUS classification features; and (3) ideally, continue developing highly reliable CNV clinical gold standard datasets and multi-centre label validation systems. This would enhance complementarity and consistency between manual and algorithmic approaches, thereby advancing the reliable clinical application of predictive models.

Conclusion

This study constructed a CNV benchmark dataset based on manual review and systematically compared the predictive performance of five models within the ACMG five-category classification system. Results indicate that while the models demonstrated significantly superior discriminatory ability for deletion samples compared to duplication samples, their ability to distinguish clinically borderline categories within the five-category framework remains relatively limited. Limitations in model training data sources, particularly public databases such as DGV and ClinVar, coupled with delayed label updates, may constitute key factors constraining model performance. This underscores a challenge in CNV interpretation: although recent technical advances have greatly expanded the potential clinical utility of CNV analysis, the translation of genomic data into reliable clinical insights still depends on overcoming persistent obstacles related to data standardization, evidence integration, and reduction of inter-operator variability. Future optimization efforts will require multi-centre gold-standard datasets with higher data quality, more timely updates, and annotation systems aligned with ACMG standards to support more objective, efficient, and clinically dependable AI-driven CNV pathogenicity prediction.
