Atrophy signature for alpha‐synuclein copathology in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Diana Esteller‐Gauxax, Núria Guillén, Neus Falgàs, Beatriz Bosch, Adrià Tort‐Merino, Sergi Borrego‐Écija, Roger Puey, Raquel Ruiz‐Garcia, Laura Naranjo, Aida Niñerola‐Baizán, Andrés Perissinotti, Núria Bargalló, Anna Antonell, Albert Lladó, Raquel Sánchez‐Valle, Agnès Pérez‐Millan, Mircea Balasa, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBrain atrophy patterns in Alzheimer's disease (AD) are well established, however, the contribution of alpha‐synuclein (α‐syn) copathology to these patterns remains unclear. This study investigates magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) ‐based atrophy patterns in AD patients with α‐syn copathology.METHODSWe study two independent cohorts: Hospital Clinic Barcelona (N = 139) and Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) (N = 191). Participants were classified into AD–α‐syn seed amplification assay positive (αSAA+), AD‐αSAA‐, and healthy controls (CTR). Group comparisons of MRI regions were conducted using permutation tests adjusting for age and sex.RESULTSCompared to CTR, both AD‐αSAA‐ and AD‐αSAA+ showed widespread cortical thinning. Direct comparison of AD‐αSAA+ versus AD‐αSAA‐ revealed differential involvement of insular, entorhinal, lateral temporal, pre and postcentral gyrus, frontal cortices, and amygdala, although with minimum size effect.DISCUSSIONα‐Syn copathology was associated with more extensive and pronounced atrophy, especially in frontal, lateral temporal, entorhinal, and amygdalar regions. This suggests that α‐syn is associated with greater neurodegeneration beyond typical AD patterns.

BACKGROUND

Alpha‐synuclein (α‐syn) copathology is found in approximately 50%–60% of Alzheimer's disease (AD) cases at post mortem. 1 , 2 Copathologies are increasingly recognized as key contributors to AD clinical heterogeneity and have been linked to faster cognitive decline, a higher burden of neuropsychiatric symptoms—particularly hallucinations, attentional fluctuations, and sleep disturbances—and changes in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker profiles. 3 , 4 Moreover, α‐syn copathology has also been associated with faster amyloid‐driven tau accumulation and α‐syn–tau synergy, which may amplify neurodegeneration. 5 , 6 , 7 However, its impact on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) ‐derived neurodegeneration patterns remains poorly understood.

Patterns of brain atrophy in AD are well established, typically starting in medial temporal lobe structures and spreading to temporoparietal association cortices, posterior cingulate, and frontal regions. In contrast, dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), the prototypical α‐synucleinopathy, lacks a consistent atrophy signature. Comparative studies suggest partially overlapping but distinct patterns, with AD showing greater cortical atrophy (especially medial temporal and posterior parietal) and DLB showing relatively greater striatal and insular involvement. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Notably, some subcortical regions commonly affected by α‐syn pathology, such as the palladium, often appear structurally preserved in DLB, suggesting a dissociation between α‐syn burden and macroscopic atrophy. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Other reports describe greater occipital and sensorimotor (precentral/postcentral) atrophy in DLB, 16 , 17 while orbitofrontal findings remain inconsistent. 8 , 18 , 19 Importantly, DLB with concomitant AD pathology shows faster and more widespread atrophy than either condition alone, with prominent involvement of medial temporal structures (entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, amygdala), insula, temporoparietal regions (parahippocampal, middle/inferior temporal, inferior parietal, fusiform, lingual) 13 , 15 , 20 , 21 and subcortical structures including caudate and putamen, 13 , 15 , 22 with less consistent reports of posterior cingulate, occipital, and left frontal involvement.

Until recently, MRI studies of α‐syn copathology in AD were limited by the lack of reliable in vivo α‐syn biomarkers, and most evidence relied on post mortem confirmation, making it unclear whether copathology was present at the time of imaging. The emergence of α‐syn seed amplification assays (αSAAs) now enables sensitive and specific in vivo detection of misfolded α‐syn aggregates, providing an opportunity to assess the structural brain changes associated with α‐syn copathology in AD in vivo. 23 , 24

In this study, we aim to characterize the MRI‐based atrophy patterns in AD patients with and without α‐syn copathology, defined by CSF αSAA status, in a tertiary memory clinical cohort and the ADNI cohort. We hypothesize that α‐syn copathology would be associated with differential atrophy in regions previously implicated in both AD and DLB. By identifying these patterns, we seek to clarify the anatomical differences associated with mixed pathology and contribute to a more nuanced understanding of its structural phenotype.

METHODS

Study design

We conducted a retrospective neuroimaging study using two independent cohorts: (i) a real‐world clinical sample from the Alzheimer's and other cognitive disorders unit—Hospital Clínic Barcelona (Clinic Barcelona cohort), and (ii) the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI cohort). Eligible participants required clinical and biological evidence consistent with AD and availability of a T1‐weighted (T1w) MRI. Using both a real‐world clinical cohort and a multicenter research cohort aimed to enhance the generalizability and validate findings across different settings.

Clinic Barcelona cohort

The study included 139 participants recruited from the Hospital Clínic de Barcelona between 2009 and 2023, comprising AD participants and healthy controls (CTR) with baseline T1w MRI. All AD participants fulfilled criteria for dementia due AD 25 , 26 supported by an AD CSF biomarkers profile according to local cutpoints (Aβ42 < 600pg/mL, p‐tau181 > 65pg/mL, and t‐tau > 362pg/mL). CSF samples collected at the initial evaluation were analyzed using αSAA, classifying AD participants as AD‐αSAA‐ (negative) or AD‐αSAA+ (positive). All AD participants had a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score > 1.

CTR were research volunteers without memory complaints, normal comprehensive neuropsychological testing, normal AD‐CSF biomarkers and negative αSAA. Exclusion criteria were major stroke, traumatic brain injury, major psychiatric disorder, or alcohol abuse. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki (Fortaleza, 2013) and Spanish regulations, was approved by the Hospital Clínic de Barcelona Ethics Committee (HCB/2020/1410), and all participants provided written informed consent.

ADNI cohort

This study included 191 participants from the ADNI cohort, a project launched in 2003 as a public–private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD, and funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) (adni.loni.usc.edu). We included individuals from ADNI 1–3 with available T1w MRI, CSF Aβ42, p‐tau, t‐tau, and αSAA results. Exclusion criteria were: (i) cognitively unimpaired individuals with positive biomarkers (AD or α‐synuclein), (ii) individuals with amyloid deposition (abnormal CSF Aβ42 or positive amyloid positron emission tomography [PET]) without other biomarker alterations (p‐tau181 and t‐tau), and (iii) cognitively impaired individuals with negative AD biomarkers (suggesting alternative pathology). Participants were classified using AD biomarker status and αSAA results:

AD group: Participants were included in the AD group if they had a CDR score > 1 and fulfilled both amyloid and tau biomarker criteria consistent with AD pathology (A+T+). Amyloid positivity was defined as either abnormal CSF Aβ1–42 according to ADNI cutoffs together with a positive amyloid PET (Centiloid [CL] > 20), or a borderline CSF Aβ1–42 value (defined as within approximately ± 150 pg/mL of the pathological cutoff) together with a positive amyloid PET. Borderline cases were adjudicated using amyloid PET because CSF Aβ1–42 alone may be more susceptible to pre‐analytical variability and less robust than ratio‐based measures for amyloid classification. Participants were also required to have elevated CSF p‐tau181 according to ADNI cutoffs, and individuals with evidence of amyloid deposition in the absence of tau pathology were excluded. AD participants were further subdivided into AD‐αSAA+ and AD‐αSAA‐ according to αSAA results, and those with indeterminate αSAA were excluded.

MCI group: Additionally, an MCI‐stage AD group was selected from ADNI using the same biomarker criteria, but limited to individuals with a CDR score of 0.5, consistent with mild cognitive impairment.

CTR group: Individuals without evidence of amyloid deposition (normal CSF amyloid and CL < 20), normal CSF p‐tau181, and αSAA‐ (A–T—αSAA‐); if CSF amyloid was borderline, inclusion required a negative amyloid PET (CL < 20). Individuals with indeterminate biomarker profiles were excluded. All CTR had preserved cognition (Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE] ≥ 27), no subjective memory complaints, and CDR 0.

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

Clinic Barcelona cohort

T1‐weighted MRI was acquired on a 3T scanner using a high‐resolution 3D magnetization prepared rapid gradient Echo (MPRAGE) sequence (repetition time = 2.300 ms; echo time = 2.98 ms; 240 slices; field‐of‐view = 256 mm; voxel size = 1×1×1 mm), and processed as previously described. 27 Images were analyzed with FreeSurfer v6.0 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu.sire.ub.edu/) for automated cortical and subcortical reconstruction/parcellation using available atlases and following standard procedures. 28 , 29 We extracted mean cortical thickness (CTh) for 68 Desikan–Killiany regions and gray matter (GM) volumes for 16 subcortical structures, 30 including bilateral summary measures. Subcortical volumes were normalized by total intracranial volume (ICV), as a known confound in neuroimaging studies. All segmentations were visually inspected and manually corrected when needed.

ADNI cohort

T1‐weighted MRI was acquired following the ADNI protocol 31 and screened by the ADNI imaging core for artifacts and protocol compliance. ADNI scans had been preprocessed by the imaging core using FreeSurfer v7.2 to perform subcortical and cortical reconstruction of the T1w acquisitions and obtain cortical and subcortical measures with automated atlas‐based parcellation, and volumes were normalized by ICV to avoid the confounder.

Biochemical biomarkers

Clinic Barcelona cohort

CSF αSAA: The αSAA was performed in‐house using real‐time quaking‐induced conversion assay (RT‐QuIC) with full‐length recombinant human α‐syn (amino acids 1–140; Sigma, Poole, UK) as substrate. CSF samples were run in triplicate in 96‐well plates and seeding reaction was conducted in an Omega FLUOstar reader at 35°C for 90 h with intermittent shaking cycles (1 min double orbital shaking at 400 rpm, 14 min rest). Thioflavin T fluorescence was recorded every 15 min (Excitation wavelength 450 nm/Emission wavelength 480 nm).

Results were dichotomized as positive/negative; positivity was defined as relative fluorescence unit (RFU) > 2 SD above the mean of internal negative controls at 90 h across CSF replicates. Samples were classified as positive when 2 or 3 replicate curves were positive. Samples with one positive and two negative replicate curves were retested in triplicate. Samples with fluorescence curves not reaching the positivity cutoff but not clearly negative were considered indeterminate and excluded. The final fluorescence value was the mean RFU at 90 h, and T50 was the time to reach 50% of the maximum RFU. This assay only detects PD/DLB‐like α‐synuclein aggregates.

CSF AD biomarkers: Aβ1–42, p‐tau181, t‐tau were measured with Lumipulse following the manufacturer's instructions (Fujirebio, Belgium) using local cutoffs. Amyloid positive (Aβ+) status was defined as Aβ1‐42 < 600 pg/mL; for borderline Aβ1–42, an Aβ1–42/Aβ1–40 ratio < 0.07 defined Aβ+.

ADNI cohort

The CSF αSAA was performed at the Amprion Clinical Laboratory (CLIA ID No. 05D2209417; CAP No. 8168002) using a clinically validated method in accordance with Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) requirements, as previously described. 4 Results were classified as positive/negative, with positivity defined by significant fluorescence according to the Amprion protocol after 20 h of incubation and negativity defined as absence of significant fluorescence.

Under the Amprion classification system, samples were categorized as “PD/DLB‐like Detected” (‘’Detected‐1″) when α‐synuclein aggregates showed a Type 1 seeding profile consistent with PD/DLB, “MSA‐like Detected” (‘’Detected‐2″) when aggregates showed a Type 2 profile consistent with MSA, or “Not Detected” when no α‐syn aggregates were identified. Because the Barcelona in‐house RT‐QuIC assay is designed to detect PD/DLB‐like but not MSA‐like α‐syn seeds, only ‘’Detected‐1″ samples were considered positive and “Not Detected” samples negative for the purposes of cross‐cohort comparison. Samples classified as ‘’Detected‐2″ were not included in these analyses. When no definitive result could be established, testing was repeated, and samples that remained unresolved after two rounds of testing were classified as indeterminate and excluded.

CSF AD biomarkers were measured following ADNI protocols using single‐molecule array technique (Simoa); Aβ42, p‐tau181, and t‐tau were analyzed by the ADNI Biomarker Core laboratory at the University of Pennsylvania Medical Center. 32

Statistical analysis

We compared the demographic and clinical data among groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, and the Fisher test for discrete variables. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD.

To assess the differences between MRI measurements, we used a permutation test with 10000 permutations adding age and sex as covariates. All analyses were corrected for multiple comparisons across the MRI regions (CTh and GM volumes independently) using the Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment. Cohen's distance (Cohen's d) was used to quantify the magnitude of between‐group differences in atrophy (|d|≈0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 indicating small, medium, and large effect sizes).

We implemented the statistical analyses in R version 4.2.1. The significance level was set in all the analyses at a p‐value < 0.05.

As expected, CSF and plasma biomarker levels differed significantly between diagnostic groups. No significant age or sex differences were observed within cohorts. Across cohorts, ADNI participants were older than those in the Clinic Barcelona cohort (p < 0.0001), with no other relevant demographic or clinical differences.

AD‐αSAA− versus CTR

In both cohorts, AD–αSAA‐ participants showed significantly lower CTh across most cortical regions and reduced GM volumes in nearly all regions examined compared with CTRs (Figure 1A). In the Clinic Barcelona Cohort, the few regions without significant differences were mainly concentrated in medial occipital areas and selected medial/fronto‐basal regions, as well as the caudate, pallidum and ventral structures. The largest effect sizes (|Cohen's d| ≥ 1.2) were observed in parietal regions, amygdala and hippocampus. Other regions with large effect sizes (|Cohen's d| ≥ 0.8) included the temporal structures, posterior and superior frontal cortex, posterior cingulate and lateral occipital cortices (Figure 1A). Lateral ventricles were enlarged in AD–αSAA‐ compared with CTRs, consistent with global atrophy.

Similar findings were observed in the ADNI cohort (Figure 1B). The few regions without significant differences were mainly concentrated in the anterior and isthmus cingulate, caudate and ventral structures. In this case, the largest effect sizes (|Cohen's d| ≥ 1.5) were concentrated in the temporal cortices, inferior parietal and medial orbitofrontal cortex. Other regions with large effect sizes (|Cohen's d| ≥ 0.8) included frontal, cingulate, occipital and insular areas (Figure 1A). Ventricular enlargement was also observed in AD–αSAA‐ versus CTR in ADNI.

Across cohorts, the overall pattern was consistent, with relative preservation of the anterior cingulate and robust involvement of hippocampus and amygdala. Differences were mainly quantitative and topographic: the Clinic Barcelona cohort showed a more parietal‐centered profile, whereas ADNI displayed higher peak effects led by temporal/medial temporal regions and a slightly broader spatial distribution involving fronto‐basal regions and medial occipital areas (Figure 1).

AD–αSAA+ versus CTR

When comparing AD–αSAA+ with CTR, both cohorts showed a similar but more extensive atrophy pattern than that observed for AD–αSAA‐ (Figure 2). In the Clinic Barcelona cohort, AD‐αSAA+ participants exhibited lower CTh and reduced GM across most cortical regions and structures, with non‐significant findings again largely restricted to medial occipital, more limited medial/fronto‐basal regions, pallidum, and ventral regions. Notably, the effect‐size range and the number of regions exceeding high thresholds (e.g., > 1.5) were greater in AD–αSAA+ than in AD–αSAA‐. The largest effect sizes (|Cohen's d| ≥ 1.8) were again localized in parietal cortices (including precuneus), amygdala, hippocampus and, additionally, pallidum. Large effect sizes (|Cohen's d| ≥ 0.8) were also seen in temporal/medial temporal (including entorhinal/fusiform), frontal, cingulate, insular and occipital regions.

In the ADNI cohort, the subset of regions not reaching significance were more reduced and included anterior cingulate, caudate and ventral structures (Figure 2B). The largest effect sizes (|Cohen's d| ≥ 1.8) encompassed temporal and medial temporal cortices, parietal association areas (inferior parietal/precuneus/supramarginal), limbic structures (amygdala/hippocampus), the insula, and frontal association cortices (middle and superior frontal), with the strongest effect in the parietal cortex (precuneus; peak > 2). Large effects extended into the other frontal regions, cingulate and occipital areas. Lateral ventricles were also enlarged supporting a more pronounced global atrophy.

Comparing cohorts, both showed an overlapping parietal–temporal–limbic signature with additional frontal/cingulate/insula involvement. ADNI presented higher peak effects (to aprox. −2.71) and a broader set of high‐effect cortical regions, whereas the Clinic Barcelona cohort showed stronger parietal predominance and comparatively more marked subcortical involvement (pallidum, thalamus, caudate, and putamen) (Figure 2).

AD–αSAA‐ versus AD–αSAA+

Direct comparison between AD subgroups revealed more modest differences than those observed against CTRs, but with consistent directionality across cohorts: AD–αSAA+ showed greater atrophy than AD–αSAA‐ in selected cortical and subcortical regions (Figure 3). In the Clinic Barcelona cohort, lower CTh in AD–αSAA+ was detected mainly in insular, entorhinal/parahippocampal and superior temporal cortices and selected frontal regions (paracentral, precentral, postcentral, orbitofrontal, and rostral middle and superior frontal). Subcortical differences were more evident, particularly involving lateral ventricles and limbic/subcortical structures (hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, and putamen). Effect sizes were predominantly low‐to‐medium, with medium effects concentrated in temporo‐medial and subcortical regions (Figure 3A).

In ADNI, AD–αSAA+ likewise showed greater cortical atrophy in insular, entorhinal, superior/middle temporal regions, and selected frontal regions (precentral, postcentral, orbitofrontal, prefrontal and rostral middle and superior frontal), with a smaller set of subcortical differences (primarily lateral ventricles and amygdala). Effect sizes were again mainly low‐to‐medium, with medium effects concentrated in fronto‐temporal regions (superior frontal, middle temporal, pars orbitalis, rostral middle frontal and pars opercularis).

Across cohorts, differences consistently involved the insula, entorhinal cortex, superior temporal cortices, precentral/postcentral gyri, orbitofrontal cortex, and rostral middle/superior frontal regions. Clinic Barcelona additionally showed minor involvement of the paracentral cortex and right parahippocampal gyrus, whereas ADNI showed more prefrontal/fronto‐basal differences (pars opercularis/triangularis/orbitalis). Subcortical effects were broader in Clinic Barcelona (most structures affected) but limited in ADNI (right amygdala and lateral ventricles). Medium effects were mainly subcortical/temporo‐medial in Clinic Barcelona (hippocampus, putamen, thalamus, entorhinal) and predominantly fronto‐temporal in ADNI (superior frontal, middle temporal, pars orbitalis). Lateral ventricle enlargement was observed in AD‐αSAA+ in both cohorts, indicating greater global atrophy.

In the ADNI cohort we explored MCI individuals, the anatomical cortical differences between MCI–αSAA‐ and MCI–αSAA+ were broadly similar to that observed in the dementia‐stage analysis. The absolute Cohen's d values were uniformly small (< 0.25), indicating that these differences were of very limited magnitude compared to AD dementia participants.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we characterized patterns of brain atrophy in individuals with AD with and without α‐syn copathology, as determined by CSF αSAA, across two independent cohorts (Clinic Barcelona and ADNI). Across cohorts, both AD–αSAA‐ and AD–αSAA+ showed broadly similar CTh patterns relative to CTR, consistent with core AD‐related neurodegeneration; however, AD–αSAA+ exhibited larger and more widespread effect sizes, including stronger frontal and insular involvement and broader temporal extension, supporting a more aggressive neurodegenerative profile and greater neuroanatomical heterogeneity associated with α‐syn copathology. A notable divergence was subcortical: the caudate was relatively preserved in AD–αSAA‐ compared with CTR but was affected in AD–αSAA+, particularly in the Clinic Barcelona cohort, aligning with neuropathological evidence of caudate/striatal involvement in AD cases with α‐syn copathology and with reports that the striatum is more affected in DLB than in AD, in which remains relatively preserved. 12 , 13 , 15 , 22

When comparing AD–αSAA+ and AD–αSAA‐, the most robust and reproducible differences spanned multiple brain networks, with prominent cortical effects in the insula, entorhinal and superior temporal regions (including right bankstss), precentral/postcentral gyri, orbitofrontal cortex, and rostral middle frontal regions. Subcortical differences included lateral ventricles enlargement and greater amygdala involvement. These findings were largely replicated across cohorts, with some cohort‐specific variation in the magnitude and topography of atrophy, particularly in frontobasal and parahippocampal regions. Overall, the consistency of findings across samples supports a robust association between α‐syn copathology and more severe and spatially extensive structural brain changes in AD, particularly in regions commonly implicated in both AD and DLB.

This observation aligns with clinicopathological evidence that overlapping neuropathologies accelerate neurodegeneration and are associated with faster clinical progression in AD. 33 Accordingly, α‐syn copathology may contribute and modulate the brain atrophy patterns observed in AD. Because α‐syn copathology is the most frequent comorbid lesion in AD brains, defining its atrophy signature may improve diagnostic precision, help explain variability in clinical presentation, disease course, and treatment response, and support patient stratification for clinical trials and targeted therapies, particularly as disease‐modifying approaches increasingly focus on specific pathological processes.

Previous MRI studies of AD with concomitant α‐syn pathology have been limited and have yielded mixed results. Despite this, several regions have been repeatedly implicated, particularly the amygdala, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampus. 13 , 15 , 20 , 21 , 34 , 35 Our findings converge with this prior evidence: the amygdala and entorhinal cortex were consistently more affected in AD–αSAA+ individuals across both cohorts, while hippocampal involvement was evident in the Clinic Barcelona cohort. Neuropathological studies further support medial temporal and limbic structures as frequent convergence sites for amyloid, tau, and α‐syn pathology. 36 , 37

Beyond medial temporal regions, AD–αSAA+ individuals showed a cortical pattern resembling that reported in the literature for DLB and related synucleinopathies. Greater atrophy in the precentral and postcentral gyri was observed across both cohorts, consistent with reports of stronger sensorimotor cortex involvement in DLB than in AD and with similar findings in Parkinson's disease. 16 , 17 Insular atrophy also consistently distinguished AD–αSAA+ from AD–αSAA‐, in line with MRI and neuropathological evidence identifying the insula as a vulnerable region in DLB, including prodromal stages and particularly in the presence of concomitant amyloid pathology. 8 , 11 , 38 , 39 Superior frontal cortex involvement was likewise consistent across cohorts, in agreement with prior DLB reports. 10 , 17 , 40 , 41

Orbitofrontal differences were consistently observed across cohorts, although their extent differed. In ADNI, orbitofrontal involvement extended into frontobasal regions, including pars opercularis, triangularis and orbitalis, in line with previous MRI reports, 5 , 8 , 17 , 18 , 19 whereas these effects were more restricted in Clinic Barcelona. Broader prefrontal involvement was also seen in ADNI, a pattern that has likewise been noted by other authors and may reflect cohort‐related factors such as older age, differences in disease stage, or clinical composition. 18 , 40 In addition, we observed greater atrophy in the right bankstss and superior temporal gyrus, consistent with prior descriptions. 5 , 8 , 41

At the subcortical level, AD‐αSAA+ individuals showed greater alterations than AD–αSAA‐, consistent with the proposed vulnerability of subcortical structures to α‐syn deposition. In the Clinic Barcelona cohort, the pallidum was relatively preserved, as previously reported in DLB, 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 whereas putaminal involvement was more apparent than in ADNI, consistent with prior neuroimaging studies of α‐syn copathology. 13 , 15 This findings further supports the notion that α‐syn copathology shifts AD toward a more DLB‐like neuroanatomical profile.

Additional MCI analysis in the ADNI cohort showed a largely overlapping regional pattern, but with uniformly very small Cohen's d effect sizes. This suggests that the effect of α‐syn copathology on cortical structure may differ across disease stages and may increase as disease progresses.

Across cohorts, atrophy patterns were broadly consistent, but differed in effect magnitude and topography, with a more frontobasal signature in ADNI. This was also reflected in group‐to‐control contrasts: Clinic Barcelona showed relatively larger parietal effects, whereas ADNI showed stronger temporal and frontobasal involvement. These differences may relate to cohort composition, as Clinic Barcelona is younger and enriched for early‐onset AD, which is associated with a more widespread neocortical/parietal pattern and greater white matter and subcortical involvement 42 , 43 and posterior hypometabolism on FDG‐PET, 44 whereas ADNI is older and more representative of late‐onset AD, typically showing relatively greater inferior frontal and medial temporal (orbitofrontal/cingulate/parahippocampal/hippocampal) hypometabolism. 44 Another difference between cohorts is that, in the Clinic Barcelona cohort, all MRI scans were acquired at 3T, whereas in the ADNI cohort, approximately half of the scans were acquired at 1.5T and the other half at 3T. Previous structural MRI studies have reported no significant differences in subcortical volumes or cortical thickness measures attributable to field strength. 45 , 46 Nevertheless, it should be noted that our analyses did not include external covariates such as acquisition site or scanner type, which could potentially introduce additional variability.

Key strengths of this study include the use of CSF αSAA to detect α‐syn copathology in vivo at the time of MRI and the replication of findings across two independent cohorts. Limitations include the qualitative and threshold‐dependent nature of αSAA, which may miss cases with low‐burden or amygdala‐predominant Lewy pathology, 34 , 47 the absence of neuropathological confirmation and the cross‐sectional design, which limits inference about the rate of neurodegeneration and precludes conclusions about accelerated progression. Although different α‐syn–SAA platforms were used across cohorts, both assays have demonstrated high diagnostic performance in prior studies (Amprion: ∼96% sensitivity and ∼92% specificity; in‐house RT‐QuIC: 95.7% sensitivity and 93.2% specificity 4 , 48 ). In addition, the absence of a pure DLB comparison group represents an important limitation. Therefore, the interpretation that AD–αSAA+ individuals exhibit a “DLB‐like” atrophy pattern should be considered cautious and understood as an inference based on previously reported DLB neuroimaging findings rather than on a direct within‐study comparison. The age difference between cohorts should also be considered when interpreting the findings. The effect of α‐syn copathology on atrophy may vary across the age spectrum, potentially reflecting differences in mixed pathology burden in older individuals or disease aggressiveness in younger cases.

In conclusion, α‐syn copathology in AD is associated with a distinct and more widespread pattern of cortical and subcortical atrophy than AD without α‐syn. The consistent involvement of the amygdala, entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and insula supports the idea that overlapping proteinopathies accelerate neurodegeneration in key convergence zones, while additional frontal, temporal, and subcortical differences point to broader network vulnerability. These findings highlight the need to consider copathology when interpreting AD neuroimaging and may help explain clinical heterogeneity and variability in response to anti‐abeta and future anti‐tau therapies. Longitudinal studies with neuropathological confirmation are needed to define the temporal trajectory of these changes and assess their value for early identification and stratification of AD patients with α‐syn copathology.
