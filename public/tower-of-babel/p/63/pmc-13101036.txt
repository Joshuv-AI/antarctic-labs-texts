Distinct Host Gene Expression Patterns Induced by Covert Deformed Wing Virus Infections in Honeybees (Apis mellifera)
Authors: Ali Sinan Kara, Samet Okuyan, Hatice Nur Kaynak, Serap Sakarya, Zeynep Ebrar Yavuz, Salim Aktürk, Gökhan Akdeniz, Aydin Turkec, Stuart J Lucas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeformed Wing Virus (DWV) is a widespread insect pathogen and a major contributor to honeybee colony losses. While overt infections cause characteristic deformities and behavioral changes, covert or low‐level infections remain poorly understood. Here, we hypothesize that nonsymptomatic DWV infections induce distinct host transcriptional responses compared to symptomatic cases, which may influence the ultimate outcome of infection. Transcriptomic analysis of Apis mellifera anatoliaca worker bees from an experimental apiary revealed a high DWV RNA load in a minority of colonies. Assembly of the viral genome from these asymptomatic colonies identified a novel DWV‐A/DWV‐B recombinant (DWV_Ord1; GenBank Accession PX394756). Comparative gene expression analysis showed that covertly infected bees upregulated several genes associated with venom production, cuticular integrity, and light perception, suggesting heightened defensive readiness. These results provide insight into early molecular responses to DWV and identify potential biomarkers of low‐level infection in honeybee colonies.Keywords: Apis mellifera, covert infection, DWV, gene expression, recombinant virus

Introduction

The honeybee (Apis mellifera) is a key pollinator species, providing essential ecological and economic benefits. Viral infections, particularly Deformed Wing Virus (DWV; Iflavirus aladeformis), are major drivers of colony collapse (Şevik et al. 2024). DWV is a positive‐sense single‐stranded RNA virus encoding four structural (VP1–VP4) and four nonstructural proteins. Typical symptoms associated with DWV infection include deformed wings, shortened abdomens, precocious foraging, reduced flight capability, and shortened lifespan (Benaets et al. 2017). Four major DWV genotypes (A–D) and numerous recombinant variants have been described (De Miranda et al. 2022), with DWV‐B and its recombinants now dominant in Europe and the United States (Hesketh‐Best et al. 2024; Sircoulomb et al. 2025). The DWV‐B strain was originally described as Varroa destructor virus‐1 (VDV‐1), since it was first identified in weakened colonies heavily infested by the parasitic mite Varroa destructor, and it is thought that increasing prevalence of this strain is driven by Varroa‐mediated transmission. Şevik et al. (2024) recently reported the first detection of DWV‐A/DWV‐B(VDV‐1) recombinants in Turkish apiaries, but the distribution of these types across Türkiye is not known.

Previous studies have demonstrated that increasing DWV viral loads in both DWV‐A and DWV‐B infections are accompanied by the activation of immune‐related genes in honeybees. These responses have been reported to be proportional to the severity of the infection (Ryabov et al. 2019; Norton et al. 2025). This pattern suggests that host immune responses intensify in parallel with viral replication, marking the transition from covert to overt infection.

In 2023, during transcriptomic profiling of experimental colonies at the Ordu Apiculture Research Institute (Türkiye), we detected high levels of DWV RNA in asymptomatic bees. Covert DWV infections are common in adult honeybees, with symptoms only becoming obvious if the virus is transferred to larvae (Benaets et al. 2017). Honeybee responses to covert DWV infections remain poorly characterized at the transcriptomic level, although it is likely that these responses determine whether the infection progresses to the symptomatic stage.

We therefore hypothesized that low‐level DWV infection elicits a host gene expression profile distinct from that reported for symptomatic infections (Norton et al. 2025). We used the transcriptome data to assemble the genomes of the DWV present in our colonies, defining a new recombinant variant and also gaining insights into viral genome dynamics during infection. We then explored how the honeybee gene expression profile is affected by asymptomatic DWV infection, and our findings suggest that there is little or no detectable activation of classical immune pathways, while genes associated with nonspecific defense mechanisms such as bee venom production are upregulated. We also noted that multiple long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) genes were downregulated during infection, suggesting that DWV interferes with RNA‐mediated transcriptional regulation.

Materials and Methods

Colony Establishment and RNA Sequencing

To minimize genetic variation, all colonies were established from virgin queens instrumentally inseminated with semen from a single drone. One queen showing stable egg‐laying performance was selected, and larval grafting from this queen was used to produce super‐sister queens for the founding of all experimental colonies. The colonies were maintained at the Ordu Apiculture Research Institute, Ordu, Türkiye (40°54′30.73″ N 37°49′58.36″ E) (Supporting Information S1: Figures 1 and 2). Before the start of the experiment, routine spring Varroa destructor screening was carried out as part of standard colony management; no detectable infestation was observed. A final mite assessment performed immediately before the experiment again showed no detectable Varroa infestation. All colonies were maintained under identical field conditions, and worker bee samples (~50/hive) were collected from those gathered on brood frames containing eggs and young larvae, in order to enrich for nurse bees. All bee samples were collected on the same day and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve RNA integrity.

All bees from a single hive were pooled and macerated together to control for individual differences, after which total RNA was extracted (EcoPure Total RNA Kit; EcoTech Biotechnology, Türkiye) and sequenced (Illumina NovaSeq 6000, Biomarker Technologies, Germany; ≥ 20 million 150 bp paired‐end reads per sample).

Bioinformatics and Differential Expression Analysis

RNA‐seq reads were aligned with Hisat2 (Kim et al. 2019) against a combined database of the honeybee genome (Amel_HAv3.1; Wallberg et al. 2019) and 13 common bee viruses (GenBank accessions: AB070959.1, NC_002066.1, NC_002548.1, NC_003784.1, NC_004365.1, NC_004807.1, NC_004830.2, NC_006494.1, NC_009025.1, NC_014137.1, NC_027631.1, NC_032433.1, NC_035184.1, NC_010711.1, and NC_010712.1). Samples with 3%–9% of total reads aligned to viral genomes were assigned as “infected”; all other samples had ≤ 0.02% viral reads (Table 1). Viral reads were extracted from the alignment files using samtools, and then assembled using rnaSPAdes (Bushmanova et al. 2019). Viral genetic diversity within and between samples was assessed using V‐pipe 3.0 (Fuhrmann et al. 2024). For host transcriptome analysis, four DWV‐positive samples were compared to four virus‐free controls with matched genetic background and apiculture treatments. Read counts that aligned uniquely to annotated bee genome features were obtained using htseq‐count (Anders et al. 2015) and analyzed by DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined by having FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05 and ≥ 1.5‐fold change between infected bees and controls. Visualization was performed using iDEP2.1 (Ge et al. 2018) and EnhancedVolcano (Blighe 2018).

Results and Discussion

Identification of a Novel DWV Recombinant

A consensus viral genome (DWV_Ord1, 10,153 nt; GenBank PX394756) was assembled, encoding the canonical 2,893 amino acid polyprotein (Figure 1A). Viral reads from each infected hive were also assembled separately; those from hives C2 and M2 also gave complete DWV genomes, whereas the assembly from D2 had a ~330 nt gap covering the Leader Peptide (LP) region, while Q2 lacked the entire 5′ untranslated region (UTR; Supporting Information S3: Table 1). Assembled genomes were recombinants between DWV‐A and B lineages, with recombination breakpoints in the 5′ UTR/LP, VP1, helicase region, and 3′ UTR, although the exact position of the breakpoints differed between genomes assembled from each sample (Figure 1B and Supporting Information S3: Table 1). In all assemblies, the 5′ UTR and most of the viral capsid protein sequences matched DWV‐B, while the start of the LP region and the coding sequences downstream of the helicase domain matched DWV‐A. This structure mirrors European DWV‐B/A recombinants previously reported to be increasingly dominant in managed colonies (Sircoulomb et al. 2025). Intra‐sample analysis using V‐pipe 3.0 (Fuhrmann et al. 2024) also revealed high sequence diversity within each hive, especially in the VP1–VP3 and helicase regions (Figure 1C,D), consistent with rapid viral diversification under RNA interference (RNAi) pressure (Ryabov et al. 2019). All the genomes reported here aligned closely to each other (Supporting Information S2: Figure 3) and phylogenetic analysis placed them in a single cluster located between DWV‐A and B accessions (Figure 2), consistent with the possibility that the viruses were contracted from a single source. However, the genetic variations also suggest that diversification and selection of multiple viral strains was taking place in each colony. In particular, the majority DWV genome from hive D2 was missing the LP region and contained a frameshift mutation in its helicase domain, which may indicate that this strain was no longer able to replicate and would eventually be cleared from the colony.

DWV_Ord1 had highest similarity to a meta‐transcriptome assembled DWV accession reported in Israel in 2018 (GenBank MW397639.1), while recombinant strains with breakpoints in the helicase region have also been detected in apiaries in southern Türkiye (Şevik et al. 2024). Our infection may originate from such a strain being spread north by Varroa destructor; although Varroa mites were not detected in our colonies, they may have been present in the environment and so a low‐level infestation cannot be ruled out.

Taken together, our findings suggest that DWV_Ord1 and the sequences isolated from individual colonies should not be considered as single virus accessions, but rather the consensus of a dynamic population of DWV‐A/B variants in which the capsid proteins from DWV‐B are recombined with viral replication enzymes from DWV‐A, aligning with the growing prevalence of such recombinant DWV variants in European honeybee populations (Sircoulomb et al. 2025).

Host Transcriptomic Response During Covert Infection

Compared to uninfected controls, DWV‐positive bees exhibited 52 upregulated and 37 downregulated DEGs (Figure 3A and Supporting Information S3: Table 2). Among the most highly upregulated was LOC406145 encoding Secapin, a serine protease inhibitor found in bee venom (Figure 3B). On investigation, genes encoding other major protein components of bee venom (Melittin, Apamin, C1q‐VP, MCDP) were also upregulated, but at a lower level of significance (FDR‐adjusted p < 0.1; Figure 3C). Similarly, a recent study found that honeybee brains infected with high DWV loads upregulated expression of antimicrobial peptides, although not those reported here (Loughran et al. 2025). Surprisingly, the most statistically significant upregulated DEG was Lop2, which encodes a nonvisual opsin. This light‐sensitive protein is expressed in the honeybee brain and directs behavioral responses to light levels (Velarde et al. 2005). Taken together, these findings suggest that the DWV‐positive honeybees exhibited heightened defensive readiness than those from hives with minimal viral load. These patterns suggest a primed defensive state in covertly infected bees, involving different genes from those observed in more heavily infected colonies (Norton et al. 2025). Interestingly, two of the other most significantly affected DEGs were members of the CPR family of cuticular proteins, with CPR5 upregulated but CPR12 downregulated in the DWV+ samples (Figure 3B). It has been previously reported that CPR genes from other insects interact directly with plant viruses, facilitating viral transmission (Mallick and Eleftherianos 2024), although it is not known whether insect viruses can also bind to these proteins. More generally, changes in cuticular structure to reduce permeability are part of the insect defense response against microbial infection. The other most significantly downregulated DEGs encode proteins with potential signaling roles; a LRR‐type transmembrane receptor (LOC113218784) and the Nach sodium channel (LOC100577346). However, the function of these proteins in honeybees is not yet known. Strikingly, the majority of the other downregulated DEGs were uncharacterized lncRNA (Supporting Information S3: Table 2). Several lncRNAs were also differentially expressed in honeybee brains in response to high levels of DWV (Loughran et al. 2025) while other studies have reported differential expression profiles of lncRNAs during fungal infection (Ye et al. 2022). The predicted functions of these lncRNAs are yet to be validated experimentally, although it is thought that lncRNAs regulate the expression of other genes via several different mechanisms (Statello et al. 2021). The downregulation of multiple lncRNAs observed in this study may indicate that DWV suppresses lncRNA production to promote viral replication; or alternatively, that infected host cells redirect noncoding RNA production toward antiviral RNAi mechanisms. Detailed functional investigation is needed to elucidate the roles of honeybee lncRNAs in the DWV defense response.

Conclusion

This study reports the first DWV‐A/DWV‐B recombinant genome identified in northern Türkiye and characterizes host transcriptional responses during asymptomatic infection. Previous studies of honeybees suffering from symptomatic DWV infections have reported differential expression of host genes associated with RNAi, production of antimicrobial peptides, and various immune signaling pathways (Loughran et al. 2025; Norton et al. 2025). Therefore, we were interested to explore whether different genes are implicated during a covert DWV infection. Our data represents a snapshot of such an infection in which the virus population is diverse and dynamic, and the infected bees are initiating defense responses that appear well suited to fight off possible invaders but unlikely to prevent viral proliferation. Our results indicate that low‐level DWV infection primarily triggers the upregulation of venom‐associated and cuticular genes, suggesting a defensive response in specific to asymptomatic infections rather than overexpression of immunity‐related genes. Future work should validate whether these expression changes are direct responses to DWV or Varroa and assess their potential as biomarkers for covert viral infection in honeybee colonies. Further research could explore the use of these venom‐related genes as potential analyte elements in biosensor systems, providing a diagnostic tool capable of detection of DWV variants in honeybee populations. Such an approach could support early intervention strategies in apiculture, helping to prevent both economic losses and colony declines.
