Artificial Intelligence for Mammography Ready for the Real-World: Turning Promise Into Reality
Authors: Jung Hyun Yoon, Eun-Kyung Kim
Published: 2026

Population-based breast cancer screening using mammography is currently performed worldwide [1]. Considerable debate continues over the benefits and harms of mammography screening, including false-positive recalls, missed cancers in dense breasts, and cost-effectiveness in resource-limited areas [2]. As part of efforts to address these limitations, artificial intelligence (AI) for mammography has been developed and validated and is now commercially available for clinical use. Prospective trials are currently underway in order to demonstrate the clinical feasibility of AI for mammography [3,4,5].

RESULTS OF THE MASAI TRIAL EVALUATING THE EFFICACY OF AI-ASSISTED SCREENING

Recently, the results of the MAmmography Screening with Artificial Intelligence (MASAI) trial were reported [5]. This was a randomized controlled trial that evaluated the screening efficiency of using AI for triaging cases to either AI-supported mammography screening or standard double reading, that is, having two readers interpret the mammograms [6]. Interval cancer rates were compared between AI-supported screening, where mammograms with AI scores of 1–9 were assigned to single reading plus AI and mammograms with AI score of 10 were triaged to double reading plus AI. The outcomes of AI-supported screening were compared to those of double reading without AI, which is the standard in European screening programs. Results of the MASAI trial showed that AI-assisted mammography screening had non-inferior interval cancer rates to standard double reading: 1.55 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.23–1.92) vs. 1.76 (95% CI: 1.42–2.15) per 1,000 examinations, non-inferior proportion rate of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.64–1.18, P = 0.41), respectively. Sensitivity was significantly higher in AI-assisted screening vs. standard double reading: 80.5% (95% CI: 76.4–84.2) vs. 73.8% (95% CI: 68.9–78.8, P = 0.031) with no significant differences regarding specificity between the two arms: 98.5% (95% CI: 98.4–98.6, P = 0.88), findings consistently observed across age and breast density subgroups. Additionally, AI-supported screening resulted in 12% fewer interval cancers, 16% fewer invasive cancers, and 27% fewer non-luminal A subtypes.

INTEGRATING AI INTO MAMMOGRAPHY INTERPRETATION IN KOREA

Breast cancer screening practices in Asia differ from those in Europe in terms of the ages for screening, screening intervals, and imaging modalities used [7]. Specifically, in Korea, where biennial screening with mammography is recommended for women aged 40–69 years [8], single reading is the standard of care for mammography interpretation. In the single reading strategy, the main purpose of AI is to enhance reader performance rather than reduce the workload. Recently, the preliminary results of the Artificial Intelligence for Breast Cancer Screening in Mammography (AI-STREAM) trial [4], a prospective multicenter trial conducted in Korea, compared the diagnostic accuracy of radiologists with and without AI for interpretation using a single reading strategy. The cancer detection rate was found to be significantly increased to 13.8% for breast radiologists when using AI versus breast radiologists alone (5.70 vs. 5.01 per 1,000, P < 0.001), without differences in recall rates (P = 0.564) (Table 1). Furthermore, in the AI-STREAM trial, the standalone cancer detection rate for AI was significantly higher than that of general radiologists (5.21 vs. 3.87 per 1,000, P = 0.027) and was similar to that of breast radiologists (5.21 vs. 5.01 per 1,000, P = 0.752). This shows that AI has achieved cancer detection rates comparable to or exceeding those of radiologists, supporting its safe use in assisting with mammography interpretation.

FUTURE CONSIDERATIONS

Mounting evidence supports the robustness of the AI performance for safe use in real-world settings. In Korea, the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety issued a notice in August 2024, stating that AI systems approved for commercial use may be used in the market as non-reimbursable services if they meet certain conditions [9]. Therefore, we expect a significant increase in the use of AI systems for mammography interpretation in Korea. However, several factors must be considered to ensure the appropriate use of AI systems.

First, we need to consider the different consequences of AI usage according to readers. In the MASAI trial, the specificity (and false-positive rates) for AI-assisted screening did not show differences to double reading, thus indicating that using AI did not induce automation bias. Most readers (14 of 16) in the MASAI trial were experienced breast radiologists who were confident in neglecting false-positive AI marks. Additionally, readers were aware of the ongoing trial, which would have made them more attentive to the mammographic or AI findings. In contrast, in the AI-STREAM trial, the recall rates significantly increased (from 6.31% to 6.89%, P < 0.001) when general radiologists used AI for assistance. The recall rate for standalone AI (6.25%) was similar to that of general radiologists, which may have led to higher recall rates after using AI. Whether the outcomes of both trials will be sustained in real-world practice can only be determined through long-term monitoring after AI deployment.

Second, differences in cancer characteristics between humans and AI may influence screening efficacy. Both the MASAI and AI-STREAM trials reported relatively increased detection of in situ cancers in AI-supported screening [4,5], which may add to the overdiagnosis burden of screening with mammography. On the other hand, cancers missed by radiologists but detected by AI present as subtle soft tissue lesions, such as masses, asymmetry, and distortions, all later confirmed as invasive cancers [10]. Further cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to determine whether incorporating AI into mammography screening workflows contributes to the goal of breast cancer screening and ultimately reduces breast cancer–related mortality.

Finally, educational programs should be developed to instruct potential users on the correct use of the system. As observed in the MASAI and AI-STREAM trials, the point at which AI is integrated into the clinical workflow varies according to the screening and interpretation environments, thus leading to different consequences [11]. Users should understand which abnormalities or cancer types AI reliably detects so that they can confidently incorporate or disregard AI findings during interpretation. Most importantly, users should be educated on the use of an AI system, how it operates, and how it will be integrated into their workflow to ensure proper operation, maintenance of AI systems, and human competence. We anticipate that the results of ongoing research on AI uncertainty measures and human-AI interaction will provide ideas for effective allocation of roles between humans and AI [11]. AI for mammography interpretation is on the way for settlement in real-world practice. For successful AI adoption, continued deliberation is needed on human-AI interaction and the roles that can safely be delegated to AI for screening efficacy.
