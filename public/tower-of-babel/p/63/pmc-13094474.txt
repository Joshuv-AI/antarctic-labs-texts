Identification of key volatiles affecting the flavour of Hainan ‘Shanyou’ (Camellia hainanica) oil by GC–MS
Authors: Yuanhao Ding, Shihan Zhou, Mengyu Zhang, Yiwei Song, Pengmeng Du, Fei Chen, Ping Li, Qicheng Han, Jinping Liu, Jian Wang, Wenqiang Wu, Xiaolong Huang, Wenquan Wang, Dongyi Huang, Hanggui Lai, Haiyan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShanyou oil (SYO), extracted from the seeds of Camellia hainanica in Hainan, is highly valued for its distinctive flavour, compared with that of other camellia seed oils (CSOs). To investigate the chemicals that contribute to its unique aroma, oils from 13 regions across Hainan, Guangdong, and Guangxi were collected. Sensory evaluation revealed that the SYO has intense coffee-like, caramellic, nutty, and potato-like aromas, which are significantly stronger than those of CSOs from other regions. Headspace solid-phase microextraction combined with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS–SPME–GC–MS) revealed the presence of 103 volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Differential analyses of VOCs between samples revealed that acetoin and 2,3-butanediol were markedly abundant in SYO and served as key contributors to its characteristic caramel-like aroma. This study provides critical insights into the flavour chemistry of SYO, facilitating its authentication and quality evaluation.Keywords: C. Hainanica; Shanyou oil; VOCs; Sensory evaluation; Acetoin; 2,3-butanediol; GC–MS

Introduction

Camellia species have been cultivated as perennial woody oil crop in the mountainous areas of southern China for more than 2000 years, yielding a natural high-quality edible oil named camellia seed oil (CSO) (Jia et al., 2021). Owing to its high levels of unsaturated fatty acids such as olive oil, CSO is known as ‘Oriental olive oil’ worldwide and is widely used in the food industry (Chen et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024; Yang, Zhang, Li, et al., 2018). CSO from Hainan (HN), which is locally referred to as ‘Shanyou’ oil (SYO), is derived from a newly identified decaploid species, Camellia hainanica (Zhenggang et al., 2020). Compared with other CSOs, SYO has superior quality: it is a golden yellow in colour, has a relatively high unsaturated fatty acid content, has a unique aroma, and lacks bitterness (Zhenggang et al., 2021). However, the aromatic differences between SYO and other CSOs remain ambiguous, and their underlying causes and chemical basis have yet to be elucidated.

Flavour is a critical aspect of edible oil quality, and aroma plays a critical role in defining its sensory characteristics and overall acceptability (Sung et al., 2025). The aroma of CSO is affected by multiple factors, including plant variety (Fang et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2023), processing methods (Cai et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023), and geographic origin (Wang et al., 2022).Owing to unique geographical environment of Hainan Island, SYO has distinctive qualities and flavour that surpass those of other varieties grown in tropical China (Ding et al., 2025); however, the key volatile organic compounds (VOCs) responsible for the characteristic aroma of SYO remain unidentified.

The aroma of edible oils is determined primarily by their VOCs (​He, Wu, Zhou, and Chen, 2021​), and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) has been widely used for the detection, identification and quantification of VOCs in CSO (Gai et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2021). Using headspace-gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS–GC–IMS), researchers have further identified aldehydes and ketones as major classes of VOCs in CSO (Fang et al., 2022). Recent data suggests that the “roasted” and “nutty” flavour in fragrant CSO originate from stir-frying or heating processes, which generate heterocyclic compounds, such as pyrazines, pyrroles, and furans, via the Maillard reaction or the degradation of lipids and amino acids (Jia et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2025).

To address the gap in aroma related studies of SYO, this study investigated the content and composition of VOCs in CSO from HN, Guangdong (GD), and Guangxi (GX) using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS–SPME–GC–MS). Sensory evaluation was conducted to assess the flavour profile of SYO, and correlations between sensory attributes and VOCs were analysed. These results suggest that acetoin and 2,3-butanediol are key VOCs contributing to the unique flavour of SYO, distinguishing it from other CSOs. This study elucidates the chemical basis of the flavour of SYO and highlights the principal differences in VOCs between SYO and other camellia seed oils.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

To evaluate the flavour profiles of CSO from different tropical regions and varieties, a total of 14 representative mature camellia fruit samples were collected from HN, GD and GX from October to November. Detailed sample information is listed in Table S1. Additionally, mature seeds from a green tea variety, locally known in HN as ‘Daye Cha’ (C. sinensis, diploid) were included as an outgroup control. At each sampling location, 10 oil camellia trees were randomly selected, and three fruits were collected in triplicate from each tree to ensure a representative sample. Detailed sampling information, including the precise position (address, latitude and longitude), climate conditions and soil types, is provided in Table S1.

CSO extraction by the traditional hot-press method

Undamaged and disease-free fruit samples were manually shelled to obtain the seeds, which were then transferred to an oven at 75 °C for hot air drying until they reached a constant weight. The dried seeds were cooled to room temperature and subsequently dehulled to obtain mature kernels. In accordance with a previously reported traditional hot-pressing method (Xia et al., 2024), the camellia kernels samples were stir-fried by experienced operators under controlled conditions. The oil was extracted using an LYF-898 screw oil press machine (Dongguan Minjian Electric Appliance Industrial Co., Ltd.). The stir-frying time and temperature (150 °C) for each sample were strictly monitored to ensure consistency, and the press machine was cleaned for each pressing process. The resulting CSO samples were then stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis.

Sensory evaluation

A total of 22 panelists were recruited for sensory evaluation of the CSO samples. All assessments were conducted in a controlled laboratory environment maintained at 25 °C. To reduce cross-interference between samples, panelists rinsed their mouths and noses with water before each oil was evaluated. On the basis of documented aroma profiles of camellia seed oil, other plant seed oils, and the primary odours identified in preliminary assessments (Chetschik et al., 2010; He, Wu, and Yu, 2021; Jia et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2024), the sensory evaluation focused on the following aroma attributes: green, potato-like, coffee-like, caramellic, nutty, and fatty. Descriptions of each aroma attribute are provided in Table 1. Before each session, assessors briefly calibrated and anchored a 0–10 intensity scale (0 = none; 10 = very strong) using low/mid/high items from the dilution series (e.g., tubes 1, 2 and 3). Assessors refrained from flavour drinks and fragrances for ≥1 h before testing. Orthonasal sniffing was used (2–3 s per vial), with ≥30 s of rest between samples. A blank was reinserted after every 3–4 samples to monitor carryover. Each coded sample was rated within the session by all assessors and, when necessary, across multiple sessions.

C–MS analysis

The VOCs in CSO were analysed using an Agilent 7890 A gas chromatograph combined with an Agilent 7000D mass selective detector (GC–MS; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). An HP-5 MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to separate the VOCs. The GC temperature program was optimized as follows: the initial oven temperature was held at 40 °C for 3 min, increased at a rate of 2 °C/min to 160 °C, and then rapidly increased at 50 °C/min to a final temperature of 300 °C, which was held for 3 min to ensure complete elution. The samples were injected in splitless mode with the injector set to 270 °C, using a 0.75 mm internal diameter deactivated inlet liner (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Ultra-high purity helium (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, whereas high-purity nitrogen (99.999%) was used as the collision gas at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. Mass spectrometry detection was performed in full-scan mode with a mass range of 40–500 m/z. Electron ionization (EI) was conducted at 70 eV, with a filament current of 0.1 μA. The ion source temperature was maintained at 230 °C, and both MS1 and MS2 quadrupoles were held at 150 °C. The scanned range was 50 to 650 m/z, with a solvent delay of 5.4 min. To ensure reproducibility, a high-purity HS–SPME fibre (5191–5874, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for VOCs extraction and was replaced after 70 uses. Each sample analysis was performed in three independent biological replicates. Quality control (QC) samples were analysed after every ninth injection to monitor instrument performance and ensure analytical consistency.

Qualitative and quantitative evaluation of VOCs in the CSO

A widely targeted metabolomics method was implemented for VOC detection between different CSO samples (Yuan et al., 2022). The raw data were processed using Agilent MassHunter Quantitative Analysis (ver B.09.00). A signal was considered to be detected when it met the following criteria: a retention time (RT) within the same range (a peak shift ≤0.2 min) and similar peak shape and detectable responses for both precursor–product ion pairs. These detected signals were annotated according to the reported MS2T library (Yuan et al., 2022), and peak areas were then integrated and manually adjusted.

Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and screening of differential VOCs

To investigate the metabolomic characteristics of the VOCs in the CSO, the quantified volatile data were processed using LOWESS/Spline normalization software (version 1.1). The normalization parameters were follows: span = 0.57, number of quality control samples (QC number) = 8, minimum data point size = 0.32, and maximum size 1. The normalized data were subsequently analysed by PLS-DA (Szymańska et al., 2012) and clustering heatmap visualization using the MetaboAnalyst online platform (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). Differential abundance analysis was further performed using the online data analysis platform Genescloud (https://www.genescloud.cn).

Results and discussion

Polyploidy variation and fruit phenotypic differentiation in collected camellia accessions

To date, the unique germplasm responsible for producing SYO, derived from the decaploid C. hainanica, remains relatively unknown, both to the general public and among researchers. Although C. hainanica was recently identified as a distinct species (Zhenggang et al., 2020), it is still frequently misclassified as C. drupifera (an octoploid) in many studies (Wang et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2022). The characteristics of SYO, a newly recognized germplasm, have not been thoroughly characterized.

Polyploidy is atypical and defining trait among oil camellia species (Lin et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025; Zhang, Lai, et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024); thus, we determined the ploidy levels of all the samples using flow cytometry. The results confirmed that all the samples collected from HN were decaploid (Table S2), and belonged to C. hainanica. The variety ‘Xianghua’ (C. osmantha) collected from GX was identified as a hexaploid, whereas samples from GD included two polyploid types: an octoploid from ‘Wangsha’ (C. drupifera) and a decaploid from ‘Longtang’ (C. hainanica). Notably, the decaploid sample from ‘Longtang’ is the first recorded case of C. hainanica outside Hainan Island, suggesting that this species is predominantly distributed within Hainan Province (Zhang, Shi, et al., 2024).

In this study, polyploidy variation was found to be strongly associated with differences in fruit size. The mature fruit of ‘Daye Cha’, a diploid species, is the smallest among the collected samples (Fig. 1A) and has a dark green skin. Whereas the fruits of ‘Xianghua’ (hexaploid, Fig. 1B) are larger and have a glossy light green skin. Samples from GD, specifically the octoploid ‘Wangsha’ (Fig. 1C) and the decaploid ‘Longtang’ (Fig. 1D), produce even larger fruits than both ‘Daye Cha’ and ‘Xianghua’. The remaining samples from HN are decaploid (Fig. 1E–N) and generally have large fruit sizes comparable to those from GD, although some variation exists among the HN samples. These observations are consistent with previous reports indicating that fruit size in oil camellia increases with ploidy level (Ye et al., 2024).

Sensory evaluation of CSO

To evaluate the flavour characteristics of collected samples through sensory analysis, the CSO was first extracted using a local, traditional hot-pressing method as described previously (Xia et al., 2024). Six common aroma descriptors were used on the basis of established references (Cai et al., 2025; He et al., 2021; Jia et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024): green, potato-like, coffee-like, caramellic, nutty, and fatty (Fig. 2). The seed oil from ‘Daye Cha’ had a mild green aroma, with sensory indices near or below 5 (Fig. 2A). In contrast, oil-camellia samples from GX, GD, and HN had subtle coffee-like and caramellic notes, distinguishing the aroma of green tea seed oil from that of oil-camellia products (Fig. 2A). The presence of coffee-like and caramellic aromas is consistent with the findings of previous studies on hot-pressed CSO (Cai et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2024). However, compared with SYO, the CSOs from GX and GD received relatively lower sensory scores (Fig. 2B), suggesting a more delicate flavour profile.

Sensory evaluation of CSO from different regions. (A) Sensory evaluation was conducted on ‘Daye Cha’ as well as CSO from GX and GD provinces. (B) Sensory evaluations with 10 SYO samples from HN.

The SYO samples were characterized by distinct caramellic and coffee-like aromas (Fig. 2B). Caramellic notes appeared as an essential aroma in SYO; most samples scoring above 4, with the highest reaching 7.18 (HN-Pozhai). Several SYO samples also exhibited distinctive aromatic traits: the aroma of the HN-Baomeiling sample showed the strongest coffee-like aroma (score of 7, whereas the scores of most other samples were less than 5); the HN-Jilan sample showed the most prominent potato-like note (score of 4.82), and the HN-Meilue showed the strongest fatty aroma (score of 5.06) (Fig. 2B).

In conclusion, ‘Daye Cha’ seed oil was characterized by a distinct green aroma, whereas compared with oils from GD and GX, SYO presented a more complex and intense flavour profile. SYO is distinguished by its dominant caramellic and coffee-like attributes, complemented by unique nutty, fatty, and potato-like nuances.

VOC identification and composition in CSO

To investigate the VOCs responsible for the flavour formation of CSO from different regions, we analysed VOCs using HS–SPME–GC–MS. Total ion chromatograms revealed distinct differences in the VOC profiles among the CSO samples from different regions and varieties (Supplementary Fig. S1). Notably, the VOC profile of ‘Daye Cha’ seed oil was distinctly different from that of tropical oil camellia species. Furthermore, compared with oils from GX and GD, SYO samples exhibited a greater number and intensity of peaks (Supplementary Fig. S1), suggesting that both the diversity and concentration of VOCs were greater. Among all samples, SYO from HN-Baomeiling presented the greatest number of peaks, indicating a particularly a rich VOC profile.

GC–MS total ion chromatogram of camellia seed oil. A-N represent total ion chromatograms of the VOCs in 14 CSO samples. GX, GD and HN indicate Guangxi, Guangdong and Hainan, respectively. 2×, 6×, 8×, and10× represent diploid, hexaploid, octoploid and decaploid ploidy, respectively.

Although CSO from GD and GX shared similar chromatographic profiles, despite differing in ploidy and genetic background, this similarity suggests the possible role of geographic and environmental factors in shaping aroma composition (Hsu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022). Geographical and climatic conditions affect both volatile and non-volatile components in camellia seeds (Wang et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2023), although germplasm differences are often overlooked. In this study, VOC profiling revealed that SYO is distinctly different from other tropical CSOs, even from the decaploid sample in GD (GD-Longtang), which shares the same ploidy level. This finding suggests that both genetic background and geographic origin contribute to the unique flavour profile of SYO. These findings highlight the potential of SYO as a geographical indication product of Hainan and provide a scientific basis for its further development and authentication.

Using HS–SPME/GC–MS, a total of 150 signals corresponding to volatile compounds were detected across the 14 CSO samples. From these signals, 103 VOCs were identified and classified into 11 categories: esters, ketones, aldehydes, alcohols, pyrazines, alkenes, benzenes, alkanes, acids, phenols, and furans (Table S3, Fig. 3A). In contrast to previous studies reporting heterocyclic compounds and aldehydes as the dominant VOCs in CSO (Xia et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2025), esters were the most abundant class in SYO, accounting for 27 compounds (26% of the total), followed by ketones (18, 17%), aldehydes (15, 14%), alcohols (12, 12%), pyrazines (9, 9%), and benzenes (7, 7%) (Fig. 3A). The overall categories are consistent with previously reported profiles (He, Wu, and Yu, 2021; Xiong et al., 2025).

Composition and content of the VOCs from 14 CSO samples. (A) Classification of detected VOCs from the CSO samples into 11 categories. (B) Relative abundance of VOCs from 14 CSO samples. (C) Percentage of 11 VOC categories in 14 CSO samples.

Quantitative analysis revealed that SYO samples contained significantly higher total VOC levels than GX and GD samples did, with particularly prominent abundances in five Hainan samples: HN-Xinwen, HN-Baomeiling, HN-Hailin, HN-Jiatou, and HN-Pozhai (Fig. 3B, Table S4). Among all samples, the total content of HN-Baomeiling was the highest, reaching 3.44 × 108, which was approximately 3 to 6 times greater than that of GX-Xianghua, GD-Wangsha, and GD-Longtang (Fig. 3B, Table S4). In terms of relative abundance, pyrazines, alcohols, ketones, phenols, and benzenes were the most dominant classes of VOCs across the samples (Fig. 3B, Table S4).

Notably, the composition and proportional distribution of VOCs differed considerably among the samples. For example, alcohols were highly abundant in HN-Songpo and HN-Jianfengling but were present at much lower levels in GX-Xianghua, GD-Wangsha, GD-Longtang, HN-Jiatou, and HN-Changshu (Fig. 3C). Pyrazines, heterocyclic compounds associated with roasted and nutty aromas (Guo et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2024), were significantly more abundant in ‘Daye Cha’ than in other CSOs (Fig. 3B–C). Similarly, benzenes and ketones were notably enriched in ‘Daye Cha’ seed oil (Fig. 3B–C). Substantial variations were also observed in the proportions of phenols and aldehydes across samples (Fig. 3C). These findings highlight the crucial role of VOC profiles in distinguishing CSOs from different varieties and growing regions.

Differential VOCs in CSO samples

PLS-DA, a method widely used for group separation and biomarker screening in metabolomics (Gao et al., 2018; Yang, Zhang, Yin, et al., 2018), was applied to explore differences in VOC profiles among CSO samples. The PLS-DA score plot revealed distinct separation: ‘Daye Cha’ seed oil exhibited a distinct cluster (red), whereas samples from GD and GX (green) and HN (blue) were grouped by geographical origin (Fig. 4A). To further characterize sample clustering, a hierarchical clustering heatmap was used to classify the 14 samples into three groups, which is consistent with the results of the PLS-DA (Supplementary Fig. 2). VOCs were divided into four clusters (I–IV); Cluster I was predominantly enriched in GD and GX samples, whereas Clusters III and IV were specific to HN and ‘Daye Cha’. These results indicate significant differences in VOC composition between green tea seed oil and oil-camellia CSOs, as well as between regions.

To identify representative VOCs contributing to the distinctiveness of ‘Daye Cha’, variable importance in projection (VIP) scores from the PLS-DA model were analysed (Du et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2025). The compounds with the highest VIP scores included C0001 (tadecanoic acid, (2-phenyl-1,3-dioxolan-4-yl)methyl ester, cis-), n-heptadecane, nonadecanediol-1,2, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, 1-hexacosene, durene, n-heptaldehyde, isopropenylpyrazine, trans-p-mentha-1(7),8-dien-2-ol, c0088 (oleic acid, 3-(octadecyloxy)propyl ester), and glyceryl behenate (Fig. 4B), suggesting their key roles in distinguishing ‘Daye Cha’ from oil-camellia varieties.

Significance analysis further revealed the top five VOCs that were significantly enriched (P < 0.05) in ‘Daye Cha’: nonadecanediol-1,2 (alcohol), 2,5-dimethylpyrazine (pyrazine), trans-p-mentha-1(7),8-dien-2-ol (alcohol), 1-hexacosene (alkene), and glyceryl behenate (ester) (Fig. 4C). Among these compounds, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine was the most significant (P < 0.001). Owing to its very low flavour threshold, this compound is known to contribute nutty, grilled, bready, and earthy aromas, primarily derived from Maillard reactions (Ren et al., 2024; Scalone et al., 2020). The presence of this compound may underlie the subtle coffee-like and caramellic notes detected in ‘Daye Cha’.

To examine the relationships between VOC abundance and sensory attributes, Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated between VOC levels and aroma descriptors (Du et al., 2023). A correlation heatmap revealed that the VOCs most enriched in ‘Daye Cha’ were strongly associated with a green aroma (Fig. 4D), indicating that alcohols, esters, and alkenes collectively contribute to its distinctive green scent (He, Wu, and Yu, 2021).

VOCs contributing to the distinctiveness of the SYO compared to the CSO from GD and GX

To identify the key volatile organic compounds (VOCs) responsible for distinguishing SYO from CSO samples originating from GD and GX (as shown in Fig. 4A), variable importance in projection (VIP) scores (>1) were extracted from the PLS-DA model. A total of 15 VOCs, including acetol, 2-hexadecoxyethanol, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, and ethylnitrite were identified, and acetol contributed the most (Supplementary Fig. 3). Acetoin, which is biosynthesized in various plant species, imparts a pleasant yogurt-cream aroma and a buttery flavour (Xiao & Lu, 2014). Notably, acetoin has also been detected in almond flavour studies, suggesting its potential association with nut-like aromas (Franklin & Mitchell, 2019).

Subsequent significance analysis revealed that the relative abundance of nine VOCs significantly differed (p < 0.05) between SYO and the CSO samples from GD and GX. These VOCs include ethylnitrite, isovaleraldehyde, acetol, acetoin, 2,3-butanediol, pseudocumene, 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine, ethylpyrazine, and 1,6-dimethyl-3-piperideine (Fig. 5A). The effects of acetoin and 2,3-butanediol were the most significant (p < 0.001). 2,3-butanediol occurs in CSO processed via traditional hot-pressing (Cai et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024) and can be biosynthesized from acetoin through the action of α-acetolactate decarboxylase (ALDC, EC: 4.1.1.5) in bacteria (Cui et al., 2020).

Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis (r) between the VOC levels and sensory evaluation scores indicated that isovaleraldehyde, acetoin, and 2,3-butanediol were positively correlated with the potato-like aroma. In contrast, ethylnitrite, acetol, 2-furanmethanol, (Z)-2-heptene, ethylpyrazine, pseudocumene, and 1,6-dimethyl-3-piperideine were associated with the coffee-like aroma. Additionally, acetoin, (2S,3S)-(+)-2,3-butanediol, 2,3-butanediol, and (Z)-2-heptene were positively correlated with the nutty aroma (Fig. 5B). These findings are consistent with the sensory evaluation results, which indicated that most SYO samples from HN have possess a stronger coffee-like aroma (Fig. 2).

VOCs contributing to the aroma formation of SYO

While significant differences in aroma and VOC profiles were observed between ‘DaYe Cha’, GD/GX CSO, and SYO from HN, the key VOCs responsible for the characteristic aroma of SYO remain to be elucidated. Sensory evaluation indicated that the SYO sample from HN-Baomeiling exhibited the strongest coffee-like aroma (Fig. 2). Similarly, this sample contained notably high levels of ethylnitrite, acetol, 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine, and 2,3-butanediol (Fig. 6). Acetol is known to cause via carbohydrate fragmentation or degradation during non-enzymatic browning (Maillard reaction) (Mohamad et al., 2011). In combination with the positive correlations observed between these VOCs and the coffee-like aroma (Fig. 5B), ethylnitrite and acetol were significantly positively correlated with the intensity of the coffee-like aroma, suggesting that they may be important components contributing to this aroma characteristic.

Relative abundance of VOCs in CSO from GDGX and SYO from HN.

Furthermore, SYO samples from HN-Baomeiling and HN-Songpo had the most intense nutty aroma (Fig. 2), which coincided with their highest content of 2,3-butanediol (Fig. 6). 2,3-butanediol is a significant alcohol compound in sauce-flavour baijiu, where it is produced through fatty acid oxidation and is frequently detected in heated CSO (Ebeler & Thorngate, 2009; Feng et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2024). Notably, acetoin and 2,3-butanediol were the only two VOCs that were both highly enriched and predominantly present in SYO samples, suggesting their potential use as chemical markers for distinguishing SYO from other camellia seed oils.

Conclusion

Shanyou oil (SYO) from Hainan had higher concentrations and a greater diversity of volatile compounds than camellia seed oils from GD and GX. The oil from C. sinensis (‘Daye Cha’) was distinguishable from oil-camellia varieties on the basis of its volatile profile, which corresponded to a mild green aroma. Sensory evaluation confirmed that SYO has a more distinct and complex flavour profile, likely attributable to local environmental conditions and the distinctive decaploid germplasm of C. hainanica. Acetoin and 2,3-butanediol were identified as critical volatile markers that differentiate SYO from other tropical camellia oils, providing a scientific basis for its geographical authentication and quality evaluation.
