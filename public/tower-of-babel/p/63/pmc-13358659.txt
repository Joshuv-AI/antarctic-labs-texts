Visibility of breathlessness in fibrotic interstitial lung disease: perceptions of international healthcare professionals
Authors: Meena Kalluri, Nanna Makholm, Thomas Skovhus Prior, Elisabeth Bendstrup, Huzaifa Adamali, Shaney Barratt, Natasha Lovell, Anne-Marie Russell, Charlotte Pooler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreathlessness is prevalent in fibrotic-interstitial lung diseases (F-ILD) but remains underrecognised and undertreated. The purpose of this study was to examine perceptions of visibility of breathlessness among healthcare professionals experienced with managing patients with F-ILD.MethodsFour focus groups and three key-informant zoom interviews were held with 28 multinational participants from diverse practice settings and health disciplines. Using a qualitative inductive approach, themes were developed and confirmed through open coding and ongoing content analysis.ResultsThe inevitability and invisibility of breathlessness in F-ILD were central and continuous throughout the three themes of the study: invisibility and visibility of breathlessness; complexity of breathlessness assessment; and becoming aware and prioritising breathlessness. Patients normalise and adapt to minimise their breathlessness. Exertional breathlessness and hypoxaemia are less understood by clinicians, friends, and family, contributing to its invisibility. Symptom assessment and management are complex and limited, reliance may be through an objective lens. There is a need for an easy-to integrate tool to assess and manage breathlessness. All participants were unprepared by their initial education to recognise or prioritise breathlessness as a symptom. Some sought specific training, most gained competency and awareness through role modelling and mentorship from experts. These healthcare professionals did not accept breathlessness as inevitable in F-ILD and urged others to not ignore it.ConclusionsOur study suggests perceptions of the invisibility and inevitability of breathlessness in F-ILD can change. Management of breathless patients can improve through awareness, education and training.

Introduction

In fibrotic interstitial lung disease (F-ILD), characterised by progressive scarring, breathlessness is a defining symptom contributing to hospitalisation, mortality, and poor quality of life [1, 2]. Patients with F-ILD experience distress from breathlessness, while caregivers report feeling overwhelmed, anxious and depressed due to lack of support [3–5]. Without preparation to manage breathlessness at home, patients often seek emergency care, leading to respiratory hospitalisations and escalating costs [6]. Median survival post-hospitalisation is 2.8 months, compared to 27.7 months for nonrespiratory hospitalisation [7], highlighting a need for dedicated dyspnoea care. Despite guidelines that recommend symptom management and quality-of-life measures with disease-modifying therapy [8, 9], breathlessness remains an unmet need.

Patients want healthcare professionals (HCPs) to specifically address breathlessness and provide relief [10]. Holistic F-ILD care integrating breathlessness assessment with how patients “feel and function” can address this gap [11]. Yet breathlessness remains invisible, underrecognised and undertreated in practice, with clinical assessments disconnected from lived experience [12–15]. Furthermore, multiple factors contribute to invisible suffering with persistent breathlessness. Patients adapt to breathlessness by slowing down or avoiding activities that cause distress [14, 16]. Systematic symptom enquiry by HCPs is often lacking. Patients may indicate they are fine or must initiate discussions, even when suffering from moderate to very severe exertional breathlessness [10, 15–17].

HCP attitudes, competency and practice are critical determinants of care. Responsiveness to breathlessness significantly influences patient coping and help-seeking behaviours, with breathlessness remaining invisible if clinicians perceive nothing can be done or lack the skills to manage it [18]. This study examined perceptions of visibility of breathlessness by HCPs experienced in managing patients with F-ILD.

Methods

A purposive sample of HCPs with expertise in F-ILD from diverse settings were recruited via email from all participating sites in the Edmonton Dyspnea Inventory (EDI) validation study. Invitations were sent to F-ILD specialists and generalists who collaborated with specialists. The health research ethics boards at the University of Alberta and Bristol NHS Trust, UK (IRAS 321211) granted ethics approval as part of the EDI study (PRO00119668), with waiver by Aarhus, Denmark Health Ethics Board.

Three to five focus groups were anticipated due to multinational participation [19]. Janis Cole (University of Alberta, Alberta, Edmonton, Canada) obtained informed consents. C. Pooler conducted the zoom focus groups and interviews (supplementary material). HCP-participants (HCP-Ps) were asked about perceptions of visibility and invisibility of breathlessness and practices of breathlessness assessment in F-ILD, without recommendation or suggestion of methods or tools. Transcripts were anonymised, verified and uploaded into NVivo 11 for data management. Using a qualitative inductive approach [20], C. Pooler and M. Kalluri developed and confirmed themes. C. Pooler did initial line-by-line and paragraph open coding; C. Pooler and M. Kalluri re-read the transcripts and codes and met regularly for ongoing inductive content analysis of transcripts and phrases. Focus groups were discontinued when no further insights, categories or themes emerged [19, 20]. All authors confirmed wording of final themes. Findings are reported in alignment with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [21].

Invisibility and visibility of breathlessness

HCP-Ps described breathlessness as both visible and invisible: presenting as normal; seemingly comfortable at rest; and visible with exertion and disease progression.

Presenting as normal

Several aspects contribute to patients presenting as normal. They minimise symptoms and manage activities “so that they can continue to function and not be so breathless”. (HCP-P17) Stoicism creates invisibility of both breathlessness and F-ILD, “especially the seniors, they often have been brought up with, ‘Don't complain’” (HCP-P16). Feelings related to breathlessness, such as getting scared or anxious, were “not spoken about”; nor are patients asked. Patients look deceptively normal using these strategies, with underlying distress overlooked:

It can be detrimental just because they may present normally to their healthcare practitioner, office, or at bedside, and so they may not get the proper tests to help diagnose them or help treat whatever is exacerbating. (HCP-P22)

Seemingly comfortable at rest

People with F-ILD are breathless with exertion but often look comfortable at rest. HCP-P16 noted that even to the “trained observer”, breathlessness is visible and invisible. Usually at rest in chairs or beds when seen by HCP-Ps in clinics and hospitals, patients need to be observed when moving or explicitly asked about exertional breathlessness:

I think it's very, very much an invisible symptom. It's invisible if people aren't asked about their shortness of breath with different activities. So usually, when they're seen by a healthcare professional, patients with severe dyspnoea, which is primarily on exertion, appear to be comfortable until they move around and that's when the shortness of breath is noticeable. But those are not the times when patients are usually seen by healthcare professionals. As a result of that, many times, patients are seemingly comfortable at rest. So, I would a hundred percent agree that shortness of breath is an invisible symptom. (HCP-P12)

Visible with exertion and disease progression

Breathlessness becomes overt with exertion, starting with higher metabolic demand activities, such as climbing stairs.

I would say that breathlessness is certainly not invisible when patients are on the move. Quite debilitating both for themselves and for their loved ones and family members. (HCP-P5)

With severe F-ILD, breathlessness is no longer masked or absent due to activity avoidance. It becomes clearly visible with talking, eating, and even at rest when attention is paid.

Complexity of breathlessness assessment

HCP-Ps used various strategies to assess breathlessness. Four subthemes revealed nuances of breathlessness assessment: need to probe to uncover; recognise dichotomy of aims; limitation of assessing subjective symptom through objective lens; and need for breathlessness assessment tools.

Need to probe to uncover

HCP-Ps must probe to reveal underestimated, hidden, ignored or normalised breathlessness. HCP-P15 shared an example:

I had a client having lots of trouble with breathing. I sent her to the specialist, and she came back and said, “Well, the specialist did nothing”. I said, “Well, when he asked you how you were doing, what did you say?” She said, “Well, I told him I was fine” (chuckles). I said, “Well, that's why he didn't do anything”. (HCP-P15)

HCP-P16 described practical probing in breathlessness assessment, “I will ask them, ‘Well, are you stopping? Are you taking breaks? Do you have to sit down to walk down the hall?’”

Recognise dichotomy of aims

At times a dichotomy arose between alleviating distress and managing disease, as HCP-P11 noted when “not concurrently managing the symptom at the same time as trying to figure out why the symptom is occurring”. Respirologists attributed it to their role:

We are often asking for breathlessness because we want to assess severity of disease. So, we have another aim, you could say. Whereas you [palliative physicians] are there to help patients manage their breathlessness, so for you, it's more important to learn the impact of breathlessness which we should also be very interested in. (HCP-P6)

This dichotomy also related to managing severe breathlessness.

I think most clinicians who see someone [with] severe breathlessness are probably going to have more of an anxiety provoked response on the clinician side. “So okay, what is the aetiology, and how do we treat whatever this respiratory event is?” Rather than, “Oh, goodness! You feel really breathless. How can we help you? Do you need a dose of opioid?” (HCP-P12)

Limitation of assessing subjective symptoms through objective lens

In describing breathlessness assessments, HCP-Ps most often stated objective findings first and in more detail than subjective, including the experience or effect of breathlessness. HCP-P8 relayed:

We use the pulse oximeter. We look at the patients, how do they look, how they breathe. The colour on their face. And the movements of breathing. Is it the thorax and abdomen or a more diaphragmatic breathing? Use of auxiliary respiratory muscles? We look at the breathing frequency. Also of course, we ask, “How do you feel when you are breathing?” (HCP-P8)

HCP-Ps noted that diagnostic tests were needed for differential diagnosis and disease progression. Breathlessness severity, patient goals, and function were rarely described in symptom assessment, nor were symptoms such as cough, anxiety or fatigue assessed. Objective assessments provide visibility and credibility to HCP-Ps, families, and friends, but patients with F-ILD “get a little bit dismissed, because it is something subjective, not visible necessarily to the people who are observing [them] when at rest” (HCP-P11). The importance of subjective findings were emphasised, helping patients “being heard” as “it's a very subjective versus objective assessment. And how do you combine the two to make it so that you get the right outcome for your patient?” (HCP-P13). HCP-P18 stated:

If they're still short of breath with a high O2 Sat, it's remembering that those are numbers. But it's the clinical assessment to try to figure out what's happening with that person (HCP-P18)

HCP-P15 voiced “real disconnect” when patients feel short of breath when objective findings are normal:

Now, with all our technology, you can reassure them that their oxygen levels are okay. But I say, “If you're still feeling short of breath, I still want to help you feel better regardless of what's showing”. (HCP-P15)

Need for breathlessness assessment tools

HCP-Ps used a variety of approaches and tools to assess breathlessness, noting limitations with widely used tools. HCP-P6 observed: “We're so pressured for time that we end up not necessarily, always choosing the best tools. I mean the [Medical Research Council] MRC has its uses, but it also has its limitations”. The MRC was acknowledged for its prognostic value, “It's not surprising, because if you are MRC 5 [with] breathlessness at rest, your disease is more advanced” (HCP-P1), and the Borg scale with activities and function, rather than breathlessness. The Integrated Palliative Outcome Scale was beneficial because it asks, “what is important to the patient”, but is incomplete with “few questions on breathlessness”. (HCP-P6)

HCP-P12 noted regarding the Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS): “I thought some of the symptoms that I was assessing were a little bit too pertinent for patients with cancer, and a little bit less pertinent for patients with lung disease”. HCP-P11 expressed that the ESAS is challenging for patients with F-ILD “because everything is ‘How do you feel right now?’, and generally they're just sitting there at rest, so their breathlessness is not a major thing”. HCP-P28 agreed, the ESAS is “is a little bit more general palliative care oriented, as opposed to breathlessness. So, I always kinda try and dig little bit, even if they aren't recording [breathlessness] on the ESAS, I do ask about exertion”.

Several used the EDI to assess breathlessness severity with activities and crises. HCP-P27 stated, “It's time well spent talking to the patient, what their understanding of stairs is, and then the number that you get will determine how uncomfortable they are, how risky that is for them”. HCP-P17 described its use: “I think the EDI for severity really helps us identify when patients may have a crisis and come up with interventions to help prevent those crises, so they can self-manage those episodes”, whereas “the MRC doesn't identify some of those more critical times”.

Becoming aware of and prioritising breathlessness

These HCP-Ps were ill-prepared initially to recognise or prioritise breathlessness. Engaged and experiential learning helped them assess and manage breathlessness. Some sought formal training and workshops.

Not knowing and not prepared

Initial education and training focused on signs, pathophysiology and disease management. All agreed they had minimal information on breathlessness as a symptom, and none recalled learning breathlessness management. HCP-P17, registered nurse, described:

I would say I didn't have much training. My training for shortness of breath was to be scared of it. I would see people being really short of breath. That was the time to worry. But in terms of strategies of dealing with it, other than applying oxygen, was minimal. (HCP-P17)

HCP-P12, physician, concurred:

I did pulmonary and critical care training and felt like I did not in any way know how to treat symptoms of cough or breathlessness other than doing my best to optimise treatment of their underlying lung disease. But, in terms of getting extra tools in the tool belt of medications purely aimed at symptom management, I really didn't feel like I knew what to do, and so that was the motivating factor for pursuing formal training. (HCP-P12)

Furthermore, participants perceived HCPs continue to have minimal understanding of breathlessness in F-ILD, “they're not educated about the uniqueness of pulmonary fibrosis, the oxygen needs, shortness of breath, and how burdensome and profound it is, in this patient population especially” (HCP-P27). They recommended education:

The gap that I'm hearing is that the providers don't seem to have that education. Maybe we need to create like some sort of provider education to be able to help spread some of that information so that we could fill those gaps. How to handle dyspnoea, and those end stage problems that they don't really see in acute care and help that transition [home]. (HCP-P22)

Engaged and experiential learning

HCP-Ps learned from each other and “observations on the job” (HCP-P21). Learning was gained with ILD-specialists in acute care, community care, and outpatient clinics. HCP-P8 confirmed their “very basic” “entry level education”, and “mostly just learned [by being] with the patients for many years”. HCP-P9 concurred: “And so, you are taught more by experience, colleagues, and also by reading about pulmonary fibrosis and not so much from education”. HCP-P13 relayed

My background is Registered Nurse. This is not something that I learned. So, from a pulmonary perspective, this was definitely a learning curve, and mine was mostly education from those around me. Dr XX was a really big part. It is very different in terms of understanding the diagnoses, when patients are ready for discharge, where I can understand my patients and what they are feeling, and I can imagine the anxiety that comes with not feeling like you are able to fully breathe. (HCP-P13)

Intentional learning

When HCP-Ps became aware of breathlessness, and patient distress became visible, they engaged in opportunities to gain further understanding. HCP-P8 described various learning strategies:

We have so few nurses in Denmark who work with IPF. We have a friendship group with the UK nurses. So once in the year we go to the UK and we have speeches about lung disease for two days, and how to manage dyspnoea. Different workshops about how to use oxygen for these patients and so on. (HCP-P8)

Two indicated they did additional training in palliative medicine as stated by HCP-P12: “I did not learn (about breathlessness management) during my medical education in pulmonology, but I did in medical education in hospice and palliative care”.

Discussion

The central theme of inevitability and invisibility of breathlessness in F-ILD aligns with evidence that breathlessness is frequently missed, unacknowledged, and accepted and misunderstood by patients, caregivers, clinicians, and society [13–16, 22, 23]. Themes of invisibility and visibility of breathlessness, complexity of breathlessness assessment, and becoming aware and prioritising breathlessness provide opportunities to understand and address the inevitability and invisibility of breathlessness in F-ILD.

These HCP-Ps reported that patients minimise, normalise, and adapt to breathlessness, and accept their inevitably shrinking world [24]. Importantly for education and practice, they emphasised that patients are often seen at rest rather than upon exertion, obscuring the distress, burden and severity of breathlessness. Direct enquiry about intensity with exertional activities can make breathlessness visible [10, 14, 24, 25]. However, few HCPs explore breathlessness severity during daily activities, associated emotional distress and limitations, or patients’ goals and wishes. Thus, exertional dyspnoea and hypoxaemia remain hidden, even delaying diagnosis of F-ILD and access to care [26].

Description of breathlessness assessment as complex, requiring a systematic approach, optimised tools, and clinician engagement, aligns with the literature [10, 13, 16, 27]. Some HCP-Ps listed multidimensional tools to assess breathlessness, yet chose tools such as the modified MRC due to time constraints. The disconnect between “lived symptoms” and objective measures was stressed, necessitating integration of patient narratives to avoid symptom minimisation. HCP-Ps emphasised need for easy-to-integrate scales that assess symptom intensity across activity levels and crisis events, as recommended in the literature [9, 27–29]. Such detailed and explicit probing is vital for shared understanding of patient experience and personalising breathlessness interventions [10, 29]. Yet most HCPs rely on objective tests and many do not use any severity scales or tools in practice [10, 27, 30–32].

Unexpected findings were descriptions of dichotomy of aims in diagnosing and assessing disease severity versus relieving distress. As reported in the literature [13, 16, 22, 24], these HCP-Ps indicated that diagnosis and disease-specific treatments may be prioritised over breathlessness severity and impact, relegating symptoms to a secondary concern rather than a therapeutic goal. This is important to recognise, as beliefs are key barriers to changing behaviour. For example, a survey of clinicians revealed that those who believed that breathlessness relief is central to management were more likely to believe that patients should be asked about breathlessness severity (61% versus 30%) [33]. Thus, imparting knowledge and skills alone may not be sufficient without changing HCP attitudes and beliefs regarding professional roles and acceptance of dyspnoea relief as central to management in F-ILD.

These HCP-Ps from diverse disciplines and settings all reported inadequate preparation to assess and address breathlessness. Lack of competency results in clinician anxiety, frustration, and helplessness in alleviating patient suffering [16, 24], and describing such situations as a “pain in our neck” (p. 56 in [16]). Such acquiescence may lead to apathy, disengagement and relinquishment of HCP responsibility to provide relief, furthering the inevitability and invisibility of breathlessness. HCP-Ps in our study who received informal mentoring from ILD experts who explicitly focused on breathlessness relief recognised and assessed breathlessness systematically, and applied evidence-based interventions. They became competent and confident displayed positive attitudes towards breathlessness and encouraged others to do so. They observed and learned to accept the responsibility to address patient distress and ultimately derived professional satisfaction on seeing their patients’ relief. These novel findings suggest improving HCP competency in breathlessness management may, change longstanding perceptions of its inevitability and invisibility, improve management, and ease professional frustrations.

Participant recommendations included improvement of breathlessness preparedness and understanding of F-ILD in education and practice. Traditional teaching methods such as lectures, conferences, courses and simulations that impart knowledge and skills, may not sufficiently sensitise and prepare learners to address distressful breathlessness, a symptom not universally experienced [34]. Structured reflective practice, narrative understanding and role-modelling are important to develop necessary attitudes, values and beliefs [35–38]. Prioritisation in practice requires individual and institutional role-modelling to support long-term practice culture that values patient-centred outcomes. Our findings suggest interdisciplinary clinics, hospital units, and community teams with F-ILD specialists with expertise in symptom management offer valuable opportunities for experiential and collaborative learning in breathlessness management.

Inclusion of experienced F-ILD practitioners from different disciplines, settings and countries are important strengths of the study. Most participants were female; however, impact of gender on symptom recognition is unclear [39]. Limitations include not collecting demographic details of participants, including experience and additional training. Another limitation is that recruitment did not include general practitioners, who play a major role in F-ILD care.

Conclusion

Healthcare professionals described the burden of breathlessness they perceive as invisible, inevitable, hidden and ignored in patients with F-ILD. They revealed the need for increased awareness and recognition of breathlessness, including tools that assess exertional breathlessness and breathlessness crises. Initial symptom management education may be insufficient, whereas engaged and reflective education through mentorship with expert clinicians facilitated knowledge, skills and compassion to holistically assess breathlessness and relieve suffering.
