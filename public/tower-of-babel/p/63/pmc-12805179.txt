Improved Survival in Octogenarians With Heart Failure
Authors: Viana Copeland, Boris Fishman, Assi Milwidsky, Noam Makmal, Ranel Loutati, Shir Elimeleh, David Hochstein, Alexander Fardman, Yishay Wasserstrum, Amitai Segev, Orly Weinstein, Amit Segev, Michael Arad, Elad Maor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOctogenarians (aged ≥80 years) represent a significant subpopulation of heart failure (HF) patients yet remain underrepresented in clinical trials.ObjectivesThis study evaluates trends in mortality among octogenarians with HF between 2009 and 2024.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of HF patients treated at a single tertiary center. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied, stratifying patients by age (octogenarians or older and nonoctogenarians), by time period (before and after June 2016), HF type (heart failure with preserved [HFpEF] and reduced ejection fraction), and frailty status.ResultsAmong 32,892 patients (median age 72 years, IQR: 64-82; 58% [19,168] males), 11,013 (33%) were octogenarians or older. Over a 5-year median follow-up (IQR: 1-7), 18,558 (56%) died. Five-year mortality was 69% (7,599/11,013) in octogenarians or older vs 37% (8,095/21,879) in nonoctogenarians (P < 0.001). Use of guideline-directed medical therapy increased over time. Adjusted HRs for mortality in octogenarians or older, compared with nonoctogenarians, decreased between the early period (HR: 2.1; 95% CI: 2.03-2.19; P < 0.001) compared to the later period (HR: 1.6; 95% CI: 1.57-1.75; P < 0.001; P for interaction <0.001). In the overall cohort, mortality HR declined in HFpEF patients (HR: 2.02-1.62, P for interaction <0.001), but not in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction patients (HR: 2.25-2.00, P for interaction = 0.400). In the overall cohort, decreases in mortality HR between the early and late period were significant in nonfrail patients.ConclusionsOver a 15-year period, mortality risks among octogenarians and older with HF decreased, especially in HFpEF and nonfrail patients.

As the global population ages, the prevalence of heart failure (HF) is expected to rise, cementing its status as a major public health challenge.1,2 The clinical complexities of advanced age, including noncardiac comorbidities, necessitate tailored strategies to address the increasing frailty, morbidity, and mortality burden in this vulnerable group.3, 4, 5 Older adults presenting with HF are becoming increasingly common.6 Despite major advances in cardiovascular care, older adults remain underrepresented in HF trials, limiting the evidence base for guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) in octogenarians.2,7 Additionally, the impact of frailty, symptomatic relief, and optimal medication constellation and dosing has not been well studied, creating significant challenges for clinicians taking care of this population.4,8

The past decade has ushered in a paradigm shift in HF management with the introduction of GDMT and device therapies, including angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), cardiac resynchronization therapy, and mitral edge-to-edge repair.9 ARNI, SGLT2i, and MRA therapies have shown robust mortality and morbidity benefits across HF phenotypes and are now foundational treatments for both HF with reduced and preserved ejection fraction (HFrEF and HFpEF).10,11 HFpEF, particularly prevalent among octogenarians, has only recently seen the emergence of effective therapies, further complicating clinical decision-making.10,12,13 However, real-world uptake among the older adults, particularly octogenarians or older age groups, has lagged significantly,2,7,14 with concerns about safety, tolerability, renal function, polypharmacy, and frailty often contributing to therapeutic inertia or underuse. This disconnect may limit the potential benefits of contemporary therapies in older adults.

To address these gaps, we conducted a large retrospective cohort study using real-world data from a national referral center to evaluate longitudinal trends in treatment patterns and mortality among octogenarians or older with HF.15

Methods

Study population

This is a retrospective cohort study of all adult patients (>18 years) evaluated at Sheba Medical Center between January 2009 and August 2024 with a clinical diagnosis of HF. The study is based on the SHEBAHEART big data registry. The electronic medical records of all patients are the source for this study. Data were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical records, and diagnoses were consistent with the European Society of Cardiology HF guidelines.15,16 Diagnosis was based on personal medical records (International Classification of Diseases-9th Revision [ICD-9] codes 404.X and 428.x), hospitalization records, laboratory tests, medications, physiological signals including electrocardiograms, echocardiograms, and reports from investigations and procedures. HF diagnosis was then validated by comparing the registry-identified ICD-9 codes with diagnoses and other relevant clinical and personal data derived from a comprehensive review of the patient's medical records and by performing logic tests on aberrant results. Patients were stratified into 2 age groups: octogenarians or older (≥80 years) and nonoctogenarians. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board with a waiver for informed consent, given its retrospective design and adherence to strict anonymity protocols.

Heart failure definition

HF was defined based on multiple criteria. A confirmed diagnosis of HF, cardiomyopathy, or cardiorenal syndrome by a cardiologist was required. HFrEF was characterized by an EF ≤40% without improvement within 2 weeks, along with symptoms such as orthopnea, pulmonary congestion, or edema within a month before or after the echocardiogram, or furosemide treatment or elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) within a year before or after. BNP values within 14 days before or after the echocardiography study were excluded. HFpEF was defined as EF ≥50% with diastolic dysfunction or elevated filling pressures by echocardiography, together with furosemide treatment within a year before or after. Patients under chronic ARNI treatment or cardiac resynchronization therapy were also identified.

Study variables and outcomes

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were extracted, including comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), and atrial fibrillation (AF), as well as laboratory and imaging measures such as glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and left ventricular EF. Left ventricular hypertrophy was defined as either the presence of a thickened left ventricular posterior wall (≥12 mm) or a thickened interventricular septum (≥12 mm). GDMT was defined as the use of either angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker, ARNI, beta-blocker (BB), MRA, and SGLT2i. Frailty status was based on the widely used Norton score. The Norton score is a mandatory field for all admission in Sheba Medical Center with a range between 5 to 20 and is available for more than 99% for our study population (where higher scores represent better function and lower frailty). It was originally developed to evaluate the risk for pressure ulcers during hospitalization and includes 5 components: physical condition, mental condition, mobility, activity, and incontinence. As it evaluates major determinants of wellbeing, it was later shown to serve as a good frailty index in the older population and particularly among those with cardiovascular conditions.17 For the current analysis, the Norton index was categorized into 3 mutually exclusive frailty groups: low-nonfrail (18-20 points), medium (15-17), and high (≤14).18 Follow-up began at the first date of HF diagnosis ranging from 2009 to 2024. Patients were censored at death or study end (August 31, 2024). The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, which was available for all patients and retrieved from the Israeli Ministry of Interior through August 2024.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher exact tests, while continuous variables were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U tests. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to compare survival rates by age group and study period, with differences assessed using log-rank tests. To assess temporal differences, analyses were stratified by 2 time periods: before and after June 2016 (median diagnosis date) with interaction analysis used to estimate how the association of older age with mortality was modified by time periods. Cox regression was used to estimate HRs for time-to-event outcomes consisting of all-cause mortality, accounting for time-dependent effects. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression models was evaluated using both graphical and statistical approaches. Log-minus-log survival plots were visually inspected for key covariates. Schoenfeld residuals were examined to formally test the assumption. No significant violations were detected. Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes (1- and 2-year mortalily) between the 2 periods. Model selection was based on outcome type and study design. Parameters included in the multivariable model were based on statistical significance (P < 0.05) and clinical relevance, guided by prior HF literature. Finally, several sensitivity analyses were employed. This included models excluding patients with oncological disease or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), both major causes of mortality in older adults, as well as separate analyses by HF subtype (HFpEF vs HFrEF) and by frailty status in octogenarians or older within the overall cohort. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The final study cohort included 32,892 patients with a mean age of 72 ± 14 years (IQR: 64-82), of whom 19,168/32,892 (58%) were male and 11,013 (33%) were octogenarians and 2,302 (7%) were nonagenarians. Compared with nonoctogenarians, octogenarians or older were more likely to have HFpEF (66% [7,349/11,013] vs 49% [10,773/21,879], P < 0.001) and hypertension (76% [8,394/11,013] vs 63% [13,678/21,879], P < 0.001) but were less likely to have DM (48% [5,276/11,013] vs 56% [12,234/21,879], P < 0.001). Notably, octogenarians or older were also less likely to receive MRAs (19% [2,063/11,013] vs 29% [6,276/21,879], P < 0.001), SGLT2i (4% [390/11,013] vs 8% [1,691/21,879], P < 0.001), and ARNI (3% [279/11,013] vs 10% [2,235/21,879], P < 0.001). Differences in baseline characteristics in the early and late time periods

The study cohort was stratified into 2 equal groups based on time period. The early period included 16,458 patients (mean age 72 ± 13 years), of whom 5,540/16,458 (34%) were octogenarians or older (mean age 85 ± 4.3 years). The late period included 16,437 patients (mean age 72.5 ± 13 years), of whom 5,473/16,437 (33%) were octogenarians or older (mean age 86 ± 4.5 years). In both periods, octogenarians or older were less likely to receive GDMT, including MRAs, SGLT2i, and ARNI, while more frequently prescribed loop diuretics.

Compared to the early period, octogenarians or older from the later period had a higher mean GFR (52 ± 20 vs 55 ± 23 mL/min/1.73 m2, P < 0.001), a lower mean HbA1c level (6.42 ± 1.43-6.1 ± 1.07, P < 0.001), a lower left atrial volume index (52 ± 29 vs 47 ± 24 mL/m2, P < 0.001), a lower systolic pulmonary artery pressure (47 ± 14 vs 46 ± 14 mm Hg, P < 0.001), and more left ventricular hypertrophy (50% [2,798/5,540] to 60% [3,283/5,473], P < 0.001). The prevalence of DM (40% [2,213/5,540] to 56% [3,063/5,473], P < 0.001) and AF (62% [3,445/5,540] to 64% [3,490/5,473], P = 0.031) was higher, while CVA (27% [1,481/5,540] to 25% [1,363/5,473], P = 0.017), COPD (16% [1,777/5,540] to 11% [597/5,473], P < 0.001), and ischemic heart disease (48% [2,654/5,540] to 43% [2,332/5,473], P < 0.001) were lower. The use of evidence-based therapies, including SGLT2i (0.02% [1/5,540] to 7% [389/5,473], P < 0.001), BB (63% [3,495/5,540] to 66% [3,616/5,473], P < 0.001), and ARNI (0.2% [13/5,540] to 5% [266/5,473], P < 0.001), was higher, while calcium-channel blocker (CCB) use declined from 38% (2,092/5,540) to 33% (1,797/5,473) (P < 0.001). Notably, the proportion of patients with a low frailty index rose substantially from 28% (1,530/5,540) to 49% (2,702/5,473) (P < 0.001) and the proportion of patients hospitalized in the year prior to diagnosis declined from 37% (2,031/5,540) to 31% (1,711/5,473) (P < 0.001) (Tables 2 and 3). Mortality during follow-up

During a median follow-up of 5 years (IQR: 1-7), totaling 149,460 person-years of follow-up, 18,558/32,892 patients (56%) died. Of these, 8,474/11,013 (46%) were octogenarians or older, and 10,084/21,879 (54%) were nonoctogenarians. Overall, in the cohort, one- and 2-year mortality rates were 19% (6,250/32,892) and 8% (2,632/32,892), respectively. One-year mortality rates were 34% (3,744/11,013) and 12% (2,628/21,879) in octogenarians or older and nonoctogenarians, respectively. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that the cumulative probability of death at the median follow-up time of 5 years was 69% ± 0.5% (95% CI: 68%-70%) vs 37% ± 1% (95% CI: 36%-38%), in octogenarians or older and nonoctogenarians, respectively (p Log rank <0.001) (Figure 1). Graphical assessment of log-minus-log survival plots demonstrated parallel curves for the main covariates. Schoenfeld residuals testing indicated no significant violation of the proportional hazards assumption for the primary models (global test P > 0.05). Differences in mortality in the early and late time periods

In the early period, during a median follow-up of 6.00 years (IQR: 2.00-10.00), 72% (11,927/16,458) of patients died, of whom 34% (5,540/16,458) were octogenarians or older. One-year mortality was 29% (1,606/5,540) in octogenarians or older and 14% (1,529/10,918) in nonoctogenarians. In the late period, during a median follow-up of 3.00 years (IQR: 1.00-5.00), 40% (6,631/16,437) of patients died, of whom 33% (5,473/16,437) were octogenarians or older. One-year mortality declined to 24% (1,313/5,473) in octogenarians or older and 13% (1,426/10,964) in nonoctogenarians. Interaction analysis, adjusting for established predictors of mortality, revealed a period-dependent attenuation in the prognostic impact of age. In the early period, octogenarians or older exhibited a 2.1-fold increased risk of death (95% CI: 2.03-2.19; P < 0.001) compared with nonoctogenarians, which declined to a 1.6-fold increased risk in the late period (95% CI: 1.57-1.75; P < 0.001; P for interaction <0.001) (Figures 2 and 3, Supplemental Tables 1 to 3) (Central Illustration). Consistent findings were observed in binary logistic regression models assessing 1-year mortality: in the early period, octogenarians or older had 2.4-fold higher odds of death at 1 year (OR: 2.41; 95% CI: 2.19-2.67; P < 0.001), compared to only 1.4 times increase in odds in the late period (OR: 1.43; 95% CI: 1.30-1.58; P < 0.001; p for interaction = 0.005). A similar trend was observed for 2-year mortality, with ORs of 2.22 (95% CI: 1.95-2.55) in the early period and 1.42 (95% CI: 1.23-1.64) in the late period (P for interaction <0.001) (Supplemental Tables 29 to 33).Figure 2Kaplan–Meier Survival in Early vs Late PeriodsUnadjusted Kaplan–Meier survival curves comparing mortality by age group in 2 time periods: early (January 2009–June 2016) and late (June 2016–August 2024).Figure 3Spline Chart of Adjusted HR Among Octogenarians or Older Throughout the StudyThis spline chart illustrates the adjusted HR for all-cause mortality among octogenarians or older with HF Abbreviation as in Figure 1.Central IllustrationSurvival in Octogenarians or Older With Heart FailureKaplan-Meier curves shows 5-year survival and HRs in octogenarians or older vs nonoctogenarians in the early (2009-2016) and late periods (2016-2024) SGLT2i = sodium glucose co-transporter-2; GDMT = guideline-directed medical therapy; other abbreviation as in Figure 1.

Additional comparisons

Excluding medical conditions known to be associated with non-CVD death yielded consistent results: when COPD and oncological patients were excluded, temporal trends remained unchanged, with significant reductions in overall, 1-year, and 2-year mortality (P for interaction <0.001 for all) (Supplemental Tables 10 to 16). A subgroup analysis was performed examining patients with HFpEF (N = 18,122/32,892 [55%]) vs HFrEF (N = 14,984/32,892 [45%]). While temporal trends were consistent among HFpEF patients with a reduction in mortality rates over time (early period HR: 2.02; 95% CI: 1.92-2.13 vs late period HR: 1.62; 95% CI: 1.51-1.73; P for interaction <0.001), such a trend was not significant among HFrEF patients (early period HR: 2.25; 95% CI: 2.07-2.46 vs late period HR: 2.00; 95% CI: 1.77-2.27; P for interaction = 0.400) (Supplemental Tables 4 to 9). A subgroup analysis of nonagenarians presented a similar trend as octogenarians (early period HR: 2.45; 95% CI: 2.15-2.75 vs late period HR: 2.25; 95% CI: 1.99-2.55; P for interaction <0.001) (Supplemental Tables 26 to 29). A subgroup analysis by frailty status among octogenarians or older demonstrated that temporal trends were consistent among nonfrail patients with reduced mortality rates over time (early period HR: 2.21; 95% CI: 2.11-2.32 vs late period HR: 1.53; 95% CI: 1.41-1.66; P for interaction <0.001). This, however, was not seen among medium (early period HR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.40-1.70 vs late period HR: 1.47; 95% CI: 1.30-1.64; P for interaction = 0.310) or low frailty groups (early period HR: 1.77; 95% CI: 1.58-1.99 vs late period HR: 1.62; 95% CI: 1.44-1.80; P for interaction = 0.330) (Supplemental Tables 17 to 25).

Discussion

In this contemporary cohort study of over 32,000 patients, including more than 11,000 octogenarians or older, we observed an improvement in survival among older adults over a 15-year period. Survival gains were most pronounced in the later period, coinciding with broader adoption of evidence-based therapies and improved comorbidity management.

While prior registries noted the low adoption of contemporary therapies in older adults, the trajectory of mortality improvement and narrowing of survival disparities have been poorly characterized.6,17,19

Our findings have several plausible explanations. First, the association of better outcomes with increased utilization of evidence-based therapies, such as SGLT2i and ARNI, could be explained by targeting neurohormonal dysregulation, systemic inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction.10,20, 21, 22, 23, 24 Second, our findings reflect advancements in the management of common HF-related comorbidities. While the later period was associated with higher prevalence of DM and AF, the same period was also associated with lower HbA1c levels, suggests better disease awareness, and improved glycemic control, which is pivotal in HF prognosis and reducing cardiovascular risk.3,8,19 Improved renal function, as indicated by higher values of GFR at baseline, may suggest early referral of HF patients prior to disease progression, a key prognostic factor in HF. Simultaneously, the declining prevalence of CVA, COPD, and ischemic heart disease highlights broader gains in vascular and pulmonary health, likely influenced by preventive care. Addressing polypharmacy is also central in octogenarians or older patients. The shift from angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and CCB to newer therapies enables a more tailored and appropriate pharmacological management, which may support better adherence, fewer side effects, and improved clinical outcomes.25,26 Additionally, fewer prior hospitalizations and a higher proportion of patients with low frailty scores at diagnosis indicate earlier referral and a healthier baseline population, further enhancing survival.4,27,28

The most pronounced mortality reductions were in HFpEF, which was more prevalent among octogenarians or older. HFpEF, marked by systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and metabolic disturbances, responds well to SGLT2i.5,9,21,29 This agent provides natriuresis, enhanced vascular compliance, reduced myocardial stiffness with subsequent improved diastolic function, and mitigates congestion, addressing key HFpEF pathophysiologic mechanisms.5,9,20,21,29 Furthermore, the potential use of ARNI, which has been noted to enhance natriuretic peptide activity and counteract fibrosis and hypertrophy, might be beneficial among select HFpEF patients and should be further studied.9,22,23 In HFpEF, improved management of core pathophysiological drivers, reflected by better glycemic control, renal function, and fewer vascular comorbidities, likely contributed to the observed mortality reductions.20,29,30 Conversely, improvements in HFrEF outcomes were more modest. This likely reflects the relative saturation of effective therapies in HFrEF and the limited incremental benefit of newer agents in patients with advanced myocardial injury.10,11 The pathophysiology of HFrEF remains less amenable to reversal compared to the more dynamic, comorbidity-driven substrate of HFpEF.17,20,29

Older adults with HF represent a heterogeneous, comorbidity-driven population marked by advanced cardiac remodeling and a predominance of HFpEF.24,31,32 Despite advances, we show how GDMT underutilization persists in octogenarians or older compared to their younger counterparts, which is consistent with prior registries.6,7,11,13,17,19 As this population is frequently excluded from large trials and has limited dedicated studies, evidence on emerging treatments remains scarce, highlighting the importance of the present analysis.

The therapeutic approach in older adults is further challenged by a heightened risk of polypharmacy, adverse drug reactions, and frailty, with older adults consistently demonstrating higher frailty indices.4,27 Frailty emerged as a key modifier of treatment benefit. While older adults with low frailty scores experienced significant survival gains, these improvements were attenuated or absent among more frail individuals. Frailty, which encompasses sarcopenia, impaired mobility, and systemic vulnerability,4,18,27 may limit tolerance to pharmacotherapy and diminish physiological reserve, thereby blunting the impact of GDMT. Our findings underscore the need to integrate frailty assessment into routine HF care to optimize therapeutic decisions. Nonetheless, additional research is needed to refine our understanding, of which older adults derive the greatest benefit from GDMT. Subgroup analyses focusing on older adult patients with lower frailty scores could identify patients most likely to benefit from optimized GDMT. Conversely, older adults perceived as less able to tolerate GDMT due to frailty or poorer prognosis may benefit more from nonpharmacological interventions.

Polypharmacy remains a pervasive challenge in this population, increasing the risk of drug interactions, nonadherence, falls, electrolyte disturbances, and treatment discontinuation.11,17,32 This further poses an issue in obtaining adequate dosage and up-titration.33 The frequent use of loop diuretics in older adults likely reflects persistent congestion and reduced tolerance to neurohormonal therapies. The observed decline in CCB use, agents associated with adverse hemodynamic profiles in HF, suggests more judicious medication selection over time, reflecting a continued shift away from traditional therapy use in favor of contemporary therapies.20,29 These challenges underscore the need for a multidisciplinary approach, integrating cardiology, geriatrics, and allied specialties, to address the complex clinical, functional, and therapeutic needs of this vulnerable population and ensure optimal care delivery.34

Given the underrepresentation of older adults in clinical trials and the increasing number of individuals reaching this age, pragmatic trials designed specifically for this population are urgently needed. Such trials could provide robust evidence to guide individualized, frailty-centered strategies, addressing the current evidence gap and optimizing outcomes for this vulnerable group. This study is among the largest contemporary real-world analysis to demonstrate temporal mortality reductions in octogenarians or older, stratified by frailty and HF phenotype. These findings advance current knowledge by illustrating how targeted therapy uptake and improved risk factor control can yield substantial survival gains, particularly in HFpEF. They reinforce the urgency for prospective, frailty-adapted therapeutic trials in the older HF population, particularly in an aging population.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias and residual confounding, although adjustments for key clinical variables were performed to mitigate these risks. Second, reliance on ICD-9 coding and electronic health records could result in some degree of diagnostic misclassification, particularly in older adults with complex comorbidities, however, validation efforts and cross-referencing of multiple data sources aimed to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Third, as a single-center study conducted at a national referral hospital, findings may not fully reflect variations in health care delivery or patient demographics elsewhere, although the breadth and heterogeneity of the cohort strengthen its generalizability. Nevertheless, this study poses several notable strengths. First, it represents one of the largest contemporary cohorts of octogenarians or older with HF, including over 11,000 patients with comprehensive clinical, echocardiographic, and laboratory data spanning a 15-year period. Second, mortality outcomes were ascertained through linkage to a national registry, ensuring complete follow-up and minimizing outcome misclassification. Third, the inclusion of both inpatient and outpatient populations allows for a more representative and generalizable evaluation of real-world HF management across the disease spectrum. Finally, the robustness of our findings was confirmed through multiple sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of patients with major noncardiovascular comorbidities and validation among nonagenarians.

Conclusions

In this large, real-world cohort, survival among octogenarians or older patients with HF improved over a 15-year period, particularly among HFpEF and nonfrail patients.
