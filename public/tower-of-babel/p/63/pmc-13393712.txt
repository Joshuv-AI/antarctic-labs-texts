A meta-analysis of Origanum vulgare impact on egg production and quality in laying hens
Authors: Hossein Hassanpour, Mehraban Salsali, Tahereh Karimi-Shayan, Mohammad-Saeed Heydarnejad, Aziz A Fallah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Oregano supplementation enhances egg production, weight, shell thickness, and yolk color.•No significant effects were observed on egg mass, yolk index, Haugh unit, or shape index.•The powder form increases egg weight, yolk color, and shell quality but decreases Haugh units.•Improvements in shell quality and yolk color require over 9 weeks of supplementation, emphasizing long-term use.Keywords: Origanum vulgare, Laying hens, Essential oil, Powder, Egg quality

Introduction

Rising demand for natural feed additives as alternatives to synthetic products has recently emerged within the poultry sector, motivated by concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance and shifting consumer preferences toward cleaner production methods (Vlaicu et al., 2021). Phytobiotic compounds such as herbs, spices, and essential oils offer a sustainable means of improving bird health and performance without exacerbating drug resistance (Wang et al., 2024). Among these, phytogenic additives are especially attractive due to their bioactive constituents, which support digestion, gut health, and immunity (Shehata et al., 2022). Beyond serving as natural growth promoters, these supplements can also enhance the quality of poultry products, making them increasingly relevant to modern animal production (Mohamed & Hassan, 2023).

The historical trajectory of poultry nutrition has shifted decisively from the widespread use of sub-therapeutic antibiotics as growth promoters (AGPs) in the late 20th century to the present pursuit of natural alternatives. The European Union's 2006 ban on AGPs served as a watershed moment, catalyzing global research into phytogenic feed additives (Castanon, 2007; Phillips, 2007). This regulatory shift has been particularly consequential for laying hen production, where sustained productivity over long production cycles demands careful nutritional management. Coupled with rising consumer demand for antibiotic-free eggs, this has positioned precisely characterized phytogenics such as Origanum vulgare as promising alternatives that align with sustainable production goals (Alian et al., 2026).

Origanum vulgare (oregano) is a perennial plant classified under the mint family (Lamiaceae) and is extensively acknowledged for its gastronomic and therapeutic applications. Originating from the Mediterranean and western Asia, this aromatic plant has been used since antiquity in classic therapeutics and alimentary conservation, due to its potent bioactive compounds (Hassanpour et al., 2024; Maithani et al., 2023; Sharifi‐Rad et al., 2021). Over recent decades, oregano has drawn considerable attention in poultry nutrition, where its antimicrobial and antioxidant characteristics provide a sustainable alternative to synthetic growth promoters (Bozakova & Ivanov, 2022; Cui et al., 2024; Nikolova & Puvača, 2022). The main bioactive constituents responsible for oregano's effects are phenolic compounds, notably carvacrol (60–80%) and thymol (5–10%), concentrated in its essential oil (Gholami‐Ahangaran et al., 2022). These agents exert powerful, extensive antibacterial effects on prevalent disease-causing organisms in poultry namely Salmonella, Escherichia coli, and Clostridium perfringens rendering oregano a viable natural option for managing gut health. (Al-Mnaser et al., 2022). Furthermore, carvacrol and thymol exhibit radical-neutralizing capabilities, eliminating unstable molecules and alleviating oxidative damage in avian species, an essential determinant for maintaining egg integrity and prolonging storage duration (Gul et al., 2019; Migliorini et al., 2019).

Beyond antimicrobial and antioxidant actions, oregano influences poultry physiology through multiple pathways. It facilitates the production of enzymatic agents (e.g., protease, amylase) within the digestive tract, thus augmenting feed component hydrolysis and assimilation (Basmacioğlu Malayoğlu et al., 2010; Madkour et al., 2024). Its anti-inflammatory effects help regulate immune responses, potentially mitigating chronic inflammation that could otherwise limit productivity (Moghrovyan et al., 2023; Sidiropoulou et al., 2022). In laying hens, these combined effects translate into improved egg production (via enhanced metabolic efficiency) and better egg quality (Bozkurt et al., 2014). Oregano can be added to chicken rations in several presentations desiccated plant matter, aromatic oils, or solvent isolates each displaying distinct phytochemical dosages. Numerous studies have highlighted oregano's potential as a multifunctional feed additive that supports productivity, product quality, and sustainability (Alagawany et al., 2018).

Although oregano in bird diets has been studied more, results are not consistent. This variability may arise from differences in experimental designs, including the Origanum species used, the form of oregano, dosage levels, supplementation duration, as well as hen breeds and basal diet composition. Such inconsistency underscores the need for a thorough synthesis of existing evidence to offer clearer guidance on optimal application methods and expected outcomes. Oregano improved eggshell thickness in some trials (Abdel-Wareth et al., 2013; Botsoglou et al., 2005) but showed no effect in others (Cufadar, 2018; Demirel & Denli, 2019). Likewise, effects on Haugh units varied depending on the form of oregano used (Feng et al., 2021; Johnson et al., 2025). Clarifying these form-dependent effects is economically important, as shell thickness directly influences breakage rates during handling and transport, while Haugh units determine albumen quality and consumer acceptance, ultimately affecting egg shelf-life and market value. Given these inconsistent findings, a narrative review alone would be inadequate to resolve the controversy. Meta-analysis was necessary to quantify effect sizes, identify sources of heterogeneity, and provide reliable guidance on optimal oregano form and duration.

Recent meta-analyses have confirmed the efficacy of thyme (Thymus vulgaris) (Hassanpour et al., 2025b) and rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis) (Hassanpour et al., 2025a) in improving egg production and quality in laying hens, with powdered forms and longer supplementation durations yielding greater benefits. However, no similar synthesis exists for Origanum vulgare (oregano), and crucially, none of these reviews have directly compared the differential effects of essential oil versus powdered forms of the same plant a distinction with significant implications for bioavailability and practical application. The present study fills this gap by providing the first meta-analysis that systematically compares oregano essential oil and powder, while also assessing the influence of supplementation duration. This study fills these gaps by systematically reviewing and analyzing oregano use in laying hens. The goal is to measure oregano's impact on key factors like egg production, average egg weight, yolk characteristics (color and height-to-diameter ratio), albumen quality (Haugh units), and shell properties (thickness, strength, and weight percentage). This comprehensive assessment specifically examines how these parameters are influenced by the form of supplementation (essential oil versus powder) and the duration of administration. Ultimately, this study provides practical recommendations for the commercial use of oregano, identifying effective forms and cost-efficient strategies to sustainably enhance egg production and quality.

Materials and methods

Study eligibility and data extraction

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines to ensure transparency and rigor (Parums, 2021), and the study plan was registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) and is available at: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/QNKVT. We searched several major online databases for studies (Fig. 1) using phrases derived from earlier work examining oregano's effects on egg traits and laying performance. The search strategy combined terms related to oregano with egg-related outcome terms previously used by our group (Hassanpour et al., 2025a).

Studies had to meet the following inclusion criteria to be included: (1) they used a controlled experiment with laying hens, comparing a group fed oregano to a group without oregano; (2) they measured how oregano affected at least one of these: how many eggs were laid, total egg mass, shell thickness, egg weight, yolk color, yolk index, Haugh unit score, egg shape, or shell weight; (3) used oregano without mixing with any other plant-derived substances or additives (i.e., multi-ingredient blends containing oregano with probiotics, other herbs, organic acids, or minerals were excluded); (4) were published in English. Only outcome measures reported in at least six individual studies were included. This threshold was established to ensure sufficient statistical power for random-effects models and to enable meaningful subgroup analyses. It was indicated that meta-analyses with few studies produce unstable estimates of between-study heterogeneity and unreliable confidence intervals (Mathes & Kuss, 2018). For each eligible study, data were extracted at the longest supplementation period reported and organized according to the structure shown in Table 1.

Data analysis and assessment of heterogeneity, sensitivity, and bias risk

Statistical analyses followed the approach described by Hassanpour et al. (2025a). We reported effect sizes as unstandardized mean differences (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was determined when the confidence interval excluded zero (P < 0.05). Effect magnitudes were interpreted based on biological and practical significance. For consistency, standard errors were converted into standard deviations where necessary.

For studies reporting multiple oregano doses against a single control group, we included each dose as a separate comparison to preserve dose-response information. However, to avoid artificial inflation of sample sizes and unit-of-analysis errors since the same control group would otherwise be counted multiple times, we divided the control group sample size proportionally among the number of dose groups included from that study. This conservative approach effectively distributes the shared control contribution across comparisons while preventing overestimation of statistical precision, as recommended for meta-analyses with shared control groups (Chandler et al., 2019).

Differences between studies were checked using Cochrane's Q-test and I² values, with variability classified as low (≤ 25%), moderate (≈ 50%), or high (≥ 75%). For the Chi-square (Cochran's Q) test, a P-value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate significant heterogeneity, consistent with Cochrane recommendations, thereby justifying the subsequent subgroup analyses. The I² statistic was used to quantify the proportion of total variation attributable to heterogeneity.

Two predefined factors were examined in subgroup analyses: the physical form of oregano (essential oil, powder, or extract) and the length of supplementation (≤ 9 weeks versus > 9 weeks). Sensitivity analyses were performed to determine whether any individual study disproportionately influenced the overall effect sizes.

To further investigate the sources of heterogeneity and quantify the influence of study-level moderators, meta-regression analyses were performed using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation. The following moderators were examined: (1) supplementation duration (coded as 0 = ≤9 weeks, 1 = >9 weeks) and (2) oregano form (coded as 0 = essential oil, 1 = powder). Each moderator was tested separately in univariable models for each outcome. The regression coefficient (β) represents the mean difference in effect size between moderator categories (e.g., powder vs. essential oil, or >9 weeks vs. ≤9 weeks). A positive β indicates a greater effect size for the category coded as 1, while a negative β indicates a greater effect for the reference category (coded as 0). Meta-regression results with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, indicating that the moderator significantly influenced the observed effect size.

We checked for publication bias using Egger's and Begg's tests, and used the Trim-and-Fill method to adjust if needed. All statistics were done with Stata version 11.2 (StataCorp), and results were considered significant when P < 0.05.

Risk of bias was assessed using the SYRCLE's Risk of Bias tool for animal intervention studies (Zhang et al., 2019), which evaluates ten domains: (1) sequence generation, (2) baseline characteristics, (3) allocation concealment, (4) random housing, (5) blinding of investigators and caregivers, (6) random outcome assessment, (7) blinding of outcome assessors, (8) incomplete outcome data, (9) selective outcome reporting, and (10) other sources of bias. Each domain was rated as 'low risk,' 'high risk,' or 'unclear risk' based on the information available in the included studies.

Results

Study features

After removing duplicate records and irrelevant entries from the electronic database searches, 19 studies met the rules to look at oregano's effects on eggs (Abdel-Wareth et al., 2013; Arpášová et al., 2013a, 2014, 2013b; Botsoglou et al., 2005; Cufadar, 2018; Demirel & Denli, 2019; Emmanuel et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2021; Florou-Paneri et al., 2005; Gao et al., 2022; He et al., 2017b; Johnson et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2023; Migliorini et al., 2019; Radwan Nadia et al., 2008; Reshadi et al., 2020; Soliman et al., 2016; Xian et al., 2024). As mentioned in Table 1, the majority of these studies employed oregano essential oil (78.9%), while the remainder used powdered oregano (21.1%). Supplementation dosages varied according to the form administered powdered oregano was added at 3 to 30 g per kg of feed., whereas essential oil dosages ranged from 25 to 930 mg per kg of feed. The duration of supplementation spanned from 5 to 24 weeks, with 26.3% of the studies lasting 9 weeks or less and 73.7% continuing beyond 9 weeks.

Publication bias

Egger's and Begg's tests found no sign of publication bias in the variables of egg production, egg weight, Haugh unit, yolk index, yolk color, shell weight, and shell thickness (P > 0.05). In contrast, these tests revealed a publication bias for egg mass and egg shape indices (P < 0.05). However, the application of the Trim-and-Fill method did not indicate the need to incorporate additional studies to correct for the identified bias. This result implies that, despite the detection of publication bias, there may be insufficient evidence to support the existence of missing studies that would necessitate adjustments to the analysis.

Oregano's effect on egg output and quality

As shown in Table 2, oregano supplementation significantly improved egg production (WMD: 2.14; 95% CI: 1.29, 2.99), egg weight (WMD: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.14, 1.02), shell thickness (WMD: 0.01; 95% CI: 0.002, 0.02), yolk color (WMD: 0.13; 95% CI: 0.03, 0.23), and shell weight (WMD: 0.31; 95% CI: 0.05, 0.56) (P < 0.05). Across all measured traits, considerable heterogeneity was detected using the I² index and Cochrane's Q test. However, sensitivity analyses did not pinpoint any individual study as a primary contributor to this variability.

With respect to oregano form, powdered supplementation led to significant enhancements in egg weight, egg mass, egg shape index, yolk color, shell thickness, and shell weight, although it concurrently reduced Haugh unit values (P < 0.05). Discussion

This study confirms that Origanum vulgare supplementation improves both egg production and quality in laying hens, with outcomes depending on the form in which oregano is administered (essential oil versus powder) and the length of the treatment period. These form- and duration-dependent effects are likely explained by differences in the chemical makeup and bioavailability of oregano's active constituents, especially carvacrol and thymol, as well as secondary components such as γ-terpinene and p-cymene (Kosakowska et al., 2024; Nurzyńska-Wierdak & Walasek-Janusz, 2025). These data match earlier studies indicating that phytogenic additives, oregano in particular can boost poultry productivity and egg quality through antioxidant mechanisms and stimulation of digestive processes (Safavipour et al., 2022).

These findings reflect the broader shift in poultry nutrition from antibiotic growth promoters to phytogenic alternatives. Our meta-analysis confirms that oregano can effectively replace synthetic additives while improving productivity and egg quality.

The meta-analysis revealed that both oregano essential oil and powder significantly improved egg production rate, supporting earlier studies that attributed this effect to enhanced nutrient absorption and gut health (He et al., 2017a). The improvement in egg production is economically meaningful for commercial operations and highlights oregano's promise as an eco-friendly substitute for synthetic growth promoters. Mechanistically, this enhancement is mediated by carvacrol-induced digestive enzyme secretion (e.g., protease, amylase) as shown in vitro, and by thymol-driven increases in villus height and intestinal absorptive surface area (Gholami‐Ahangaran et al., 2022; Hashemipour et al., 2013; Li et al., 2023).

Mechanistic studies established that carvacrol and thymol disrupt bacterial cell membranes via permeabilization, depolarization, and potassium ion leakage (Oussalah et al., 2006; Xu et al., 2008). The phenolic hydroxyl group is essential for this activity, as O-methyl derivatives lack comparable efficacy (Ergüden, 2021), providing a biochemical basis for oregano's gut health benefits.

At the molecular level, carvacrol and thymol enhance laying hen performance through three main pathways: (1) stimulation of pancreatic enzyme secretion via TRP channel activation, improving nutrient digestion and absorption (Gholami‐Ahangaran et al., 2022; Premkumar, 2014); (2) upregulation of intestinal calcium-binding proteins (e.g., calbindin-D28k), enhancing calcium transport for eggshell formation (Olayiwola & Adedokun, 2025); and (3) reduction of oviduct oxidative stress through endogenous antioxidant upregulation, preserving albumen quality (Hadef et al., 2025).

Notably, the powdered form exhibited broader benefits, significantly increasing egg weight, egg mass, yolk color, shell thickness, and shell weight. These improvements may stem from oregano's bioactive compounds, which enhance nutrient metabolism and calcium utilization, crucial for shell formation (Cengiz et al., 2016; Emmanuel et al., 2022). The powder's higher fiber content may provide a slower release of phenolic compounds compared to essential oil, potentially allowing more sustained exposure throughout the digestive tract (Bampidis et al., 2005; Świątkiewicz et al., 2014). However, it should be emphasized that this represents a hypothesis based on fiber chemistry principles; no study included in this meta-analysis directly measured phenolic release kinetics or calcium-binding protein expression. Direct evidence for these mechanisms in laying hens remains limited, and future research should explicitly investigate the temporal dynamics of phenolic absorption from different oregano forms. Conversely, oregano powder reduced Haugh unit scores, pointing a possible negative impact on albumen quality. This might be reflected by the powder's higher tannin content (2–3% vs <0.5% in oil), which could interfere with protein sulfhydryl groups crucial for albumen stability (Mueller‐Harvey, 2006; Rawel et al., 2005). This contrasts with the essential oil form, which significantly increased Haugh units. A plausible hypothesis is that essential oils provide more rapid systemic distribution of antioxidant phenolics, offering better protection against albumen protein oxidation. However, this mechanism remains speculative, as no included study measured plasma carvacrol or thymol concentrations in laying hens. Confirmation requires pharmacokinetic studies. The reduction in yolk index with essential oil supplementation further highlights the form-dependent effects of oregano. While one possible explanation is that thymol may interact with yolk lipoprotein structures, an effect that has been demonstrated in vitro (Gholami‐Ahangaran et al., 2022), this mechanism has not been directly confirmed in laying hens. Alternative explanations cannot be excluded, including essential oil-induced changes in lipid metabolism or hepatic yolk precursor synthesis. However, the practical application of oregano essential oil is constrained by the thermal instability of carvacrol and thymol during feed pelleting (70–90 °C), which can diminish its albumen-quality benefits. Recent studies have shown promise in protecting volatile phenolics: micro-encapsulation with chitosan or maltodextrin coatings has improved thermal stability and sustained release (Fontana et al., 2025; Movahedi et al., 2024), while nano-emulsion formulations have enhanced bioavailability and oxidative stability (Rajabi et al., 2026). Lipid-based carriers, including solid lipid nanoparticles, have also demonstrated effective protection during feed processing (Saba et al., 2026).

Subgroup analyses indicated that supplementation periods exceeding 9 weeks were necessary to observe significant improvements in egg shape index, yolk color, shell thickness, and shell weight. This temporal progression can be attributed to two key physiological processes: First, the observed duration-dependent effects may relate to the time required for microbial adaptation. Studies in poultry have suggested that 6–8 weeks may be necessary for stable modulation of cecal microbiota (Fathima et al., 2022), although this timeframe is derived from studies using different phytogenic compounds and should not be directly generalized to oregano without confirmation. Second, one included study (Migliorini et al., 2019) reported cumulative improvements in yolk antioxidant status after 8 weeks of oregano supplementation, suggesting that some physiological effects may require sustained exposure. However, the specific claim regarding vitellogenin synthesis reaching "peak increases of 20–25%" after 8 weeks is not supported by the data from any included study. To move beyond phenotypic observations, future research should integrate metagenomic and metabolomic analyses to decipher how oregano alters cecal microbial ecology and gut-barrier gene expression. Recent study employing 16S rRNA sequencing have demonstrated that oregano modulates the cecal microbiota in laying hens by increasing beneficial genera such as Faecalibacterium and Lactobacillus while reducing potentially pathogenic taxa (Tian et al., 2022). Concurrently, oregano upregulates tight junction proteins (occludin, claudin-1) and downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) in the intestinal mucosa (Valdez et al., 2023). Such integrative approaches reveal the molecular cascades that translate into improved egg production and quality. These data emphasize the need for long-term supplementation strategies in commercial poultry operations to maximize egg quality enhancements.

Meta-regression confirmed that oregano form is a stronger moderator of laying hen performance than supplementation duration for most outcomes. Powdered oregano consistently outperformed essential oregano oil across production and shell-related traits, whereas essential oregano oil demonstrated a distinct advantage for albumen quality (Haugh units). Duration beyond 9 weeks significantly predicted improvements in egg production, egg shape index, and shell weight, supporting the biological rationale for sustained supplementation. These form-dependent effects likely reflect differences in bioavailability and release kinetics, powder provides sustained modulation of calcium metabolism, while essential oregano oil offers rapid antioxidant protection for albumen proteins. These findings provide an evidence-based framework for tailoring oregano supplementation: powder with extended duration for maximizing production and shell integrity, versus essential oregano oil for preserving internal egg quality.

Despite significant findings, substantial heterogeneity was observed. Dosage differences likely contributed considerably, as essential oregano oil doses ranged 25–930 mg/kg (37-fold) and powder 3–30 g/kg (10-fold). This wide spectrum likely produced nonlinear, biphasic dose-response relationships, with optimal effects at intermediate doses (200–400 mg/kg for oil; 10–20 g/kg for powder) and diminished responses at higher levels due to toxicity or palatability issues (Ding et al., 2022; Ghanem et al., 2024). Additional heterogeneity sources included variations in carvacrol/thymol ratios, basal diet composition, genetic variations in hepatic metabolism of phytochemicals among hen breeds, and environmental conditions. High I² values do not invalidate our findings but underscore the context-dependent nature of oregano's effects and the need for standardized dosing strategies (Toncer et al., 2009; Zinno et al., 2023)

From a practical perspective, oregano powder seems more effective in enhancing overall egg quality, particularly in terms of shell metrics. In contrast, essential oregano oil may be more suitable for maintaining albumen integrity, as measured by the Haugh unit. This difference suggests that the slower release of oregano powder better supports long-term calcium metabolism, while the rapid absorption of essential oregano oil provides acute antioxidant protection for proteins.

The cost-effectiveness of powdered oregano is notable due to lower processing costs compared to oil extraction, combined with its broader benefits. This makes it a viable option for large-scale applications. However, producers should consider the duration of supplementation, as benefits become more pronounced after extended use. Nevertheless, while this meta-analysis focused on pure oregano supplementation, commercial applications increasingly employ blended phytogenic formulations. Recent studies have demonstrated synergistic effects when oregano essential oregano oil is combined with organic acids or other botanicals such as thyme, rosemary, or cinnamaldehyde, often yielding greater improvements in performance and gut health than individual components (Puvača et al., 2022; Rossi et al., 2020). Such multi-ingredient strategies reflect the current trajectory of commercial poultry nutrition, warranting comparative studies between pure and blended products. Sustained supplementation through multiple production cycles is likely necessary to achieve the full economic return.

Future industrial adoption of oregano will require integrated economic assessments that account for processing costs, reduced egg breakage, and extended shelf-life, alongside sustainable sourcing strategies utilizing non-food-grade biomass, agricultural by-products, or marginal lands for cultivation to avoid competing with human food supply chains. To support these practical applications, future research should employ dose-response modeling to establish optimal inclusion levels, systematically compare formulations, and investigate interactions with diet composition or other additives, thereby enabling precision-based recommendations for commercial use.

The publication bias observed for egg mass and egg shape index reflects a broader challenge in phytogenic feed additive research: the preferential publication of positive production outcomes, particularly those with direct economic. This tendency may disproportionately affect secondary or derived parameters such as egg shape, which are often reported only when results are favorable, while primary metrics like egg production and egg weight are more consistently documented regardless of outcome. Although the Trim-and-Fill correction confirmed that this bias does not alter the study's principal conclusions, the possibility of inflated effect estimates for these two parameters should be acknowledged. More importantly, this finding underscores the need for prospective trial registration and mandatory reporting of all measured outcomes in poultry nutrition research to strengthen the evidence base for future meta-analyses.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across all outcomes, reflecting considerable variability beyond random sampling error. This heterogeneity likely arises from multiple interconnected factors: (1) wide dosage ranges that may produce nonlinear dose-response relationships; (2) variations in oregano chemotype, particularly carvacrol/thymol ratios, which significantly influence bioactivity; (3) differences in basal diet composition affecting phenolic bioavailability; (4) genetic variations in hepatic metabolism of phytochemicals among hen breeds; and (5) environmental conditions and hen age at supplementation initiation. While subgroup analyses addressed form and duration, the limited number of powder studies (21.1%) and short-term trials (26.3%) constrains the generalizability of these findings. Second, the inability to perform formal dose-response meta-analysis represents a significant gap. This was precluded by inconsistent dosage reporting units, the limited number of studies employing multiple dose levels within the same experiment, and insufficient studies per narrow dose range for meaningful stratification. Third, publication bias was detected for egg mass and shape index, though Trim-and-Fill adjustment did not alter conclusions, suggesting limited clinical impact. However, language bias cannot be excluded, as only English-language studies were included. Additional limitations include inconsistent reporting of hen age, housing systems, and environmental conditions, as well as the need for standard error conversion in some studies where standard deviations were not directly reported. These constraints highlight the urgent need for standardized phytochemical profiling, consistent dosage reporting, and comprehensive outcome documentation in future research to enable more precise meta-analytic synthesis and dose-response modeling.

Future research priorities

Several critical knowledge gaps warrant investigation to advance commercial application. First, standardization of oregano-based additives is urgently needed, including minimum potency thresholds for carvacrol and thymol, validated analytical methods, and chemotype-specific efficacy data. Second, dose-response studies with multiple intermediate doses are required to establish optimal inclusion levels and plateau points for each outcome measure. Third, delivery system innovation, particularly protective encapsulation technologies to enhance thermal stability during feed pelleting requires systematic evaluation. Fourth, mechanistic studies employing metagenomic, metabolomic, and transcriptomic approaches are needed to elucidate how oregano modulates gut microbial ecology and host gene expression to improve egg production and quality. Fifth, large-scale commercial trials incorporating economic outcome measures are essential to validate research findings and guide producer decisions. Finally, the field should adopt standardized reporting guidelines, including prospective trial registration, detailed phytochemical profiling, and complete outcome data with variance measures, to enhance comparability and future meta-analytic synthesis.

Conclusions

Oregano in chicken feed of laying hens represents an effective strategy for boosting egg production and enhancing egg quality traits. The form of oregano used (essential oregano oil versus powder) and the duration of its administration significantly influence the outcomes. Powdered oregano appears more effective for improving overall egg quality, particularly shell metrics, while essential oregano oil is preferable for maintaining albumen integrity. Long-term supplementation strategies exceeding 9 weeks are recommended to achieve optimal results and economic returns. While variations in oregano composition, diet, and hen genetics contribute to heterogeneity across studies, oregano supplementation, especially in powdered form, offers a viable, cost-effective option for sustainably improving egg production and quality in commercial poultry operations. The meta-regression quantitatively confirmed these form-specific effects, providing a robust evidence base for tailoring oregano supplementation to specific production goals, powder with extended duration for maximizing shell integrity and production efficiency, or essential oregano oil for preserving albumen quality. To translate these research findings into consistent commercial outcomes, we emphasize the urgent need for industry-wide standardization of oregano-based feed additives. Future efforts must focus on establishing minimum potency thresholds for carvacrol and thymol, implementing routine quality-control assays, and developing stable delivery systems that preserve bioactivity. Such regulatory and technical advancements are essential to guarantee product reliability, enable precise dosing, and maximize the economic and performance benefits reported in this meta-analysis.
