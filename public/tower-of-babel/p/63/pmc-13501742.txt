Investigating the expression of CTLA-4 and CD28 genes as immune regulators in patients with colorectal polyps
Authors: Marziyeh Etesami, Flora Forouzesh, Amirreza Hooshmand, Mahsa Saeedi Niasar, Pardis Ketabi Moghadam, Ehsan Nazemalhosseini-Mojarad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) develops through a multistep process originating from precancerous polyps. Elucidating the mechanisms that regulate immune homeostasis in precancerous lesions and in the systemic circulation prior to malignant transformation is of critical importance. This study aimed to evaluate alterations in the expression levels of the CTLA-4 and CD28 genes in patients with hyperplastic and adenomatous colorectal polyps.MethodsPeripheral blood samples were collected from 40 patients diagnosed with adenomatous or hyperplastic colorectal polyps and 30 healthy controls. The relative expression levels of CTLA-4 and CD28 were quantified using real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR). Transcriptomic data for microRNAs (miRNAs) and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) were retrieved from the TCGA-COAD database. Differential expression analysis was performed using the edgeR package, with thresholds set at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1.5. Subsequently, miRNA–mRNA and lncRNA–miRNA interactions were integrated to construct a lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory network. Immune cell infiltration analysis was conducted using the TIMER platform to evaluate the association between gene expression levels and immune cell infiltration in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD).ResultsThe results demonstrated that CTLA-4 and CD28 expression levels were upregulated in patients with colorectal polyps, although the magnitude of upregulation varied between polyp subtypes. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis yielded area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.66 and 0.54 for CTLA-4 and CD28, respectively. A significant positive correlation was observed between CTLA-4 and CD28 expression levels. In COAD tissues, 245 differentially expressed miRNAs and 868 differentially expressed lncRNAs were identified from the TCGA dataset. Construction of the lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA network identified KCNQ1OT1 as a hub lncRNA, potentially regulating CD28 and CTLA-4 indirectly through interactions with multiple miRNAs. Furthermore, TIMER analysis revealed significant associations between CD28 and CTLA-4 expression and the infiltration of immune cells, including neutrophils, macrophages, CD8⁺ T cells, and CD4⁺ T cells, within the COAD cohort.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that CTLA-4 and CD28 are upregulated in patients with colorectal polyps and exhibit a positive correlation in expression. The regulatory network analysis highlights KCNQ1OT1 as a potential upstream modulator of these genes via miRNA-mediated mechanisms. Moreover, the strong association between CTLA-4 and CD28 expression and immune cell infiltration underscores their potential role in immune dysregulation during the early stages of colorectal tumorigenesis. Collectively, these findings suggest that CTLA-4 and CD28 may contribute to polyp progression through modulation of the immune microenvironment.

Background

Genetic or epigenetic alterations in somatic cells can lead to cancer [1]. These alterations result in uncontrolled cell proliferation and ultimately tumor formation [2]. During this process, tumor cells acquire distinct molecular features that promote growth, survival, and evasion of the host immune system [3].Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second most common cancer in women and the third most common in men worldwide. It is estimated that by 2030, the global burden of CRC will exceed 2.2 million new cases and 1.1 million deaths [4]. Most CRCs develop from small clusters of cells known as polyps, which gradually progress to neoplastic lesions and eventually invasive carcinoma [5–7]. In new studies, CRC has been considered beyond being a genetic disease [8, 9], with its molecular changes reflected humoral in patient serum, providing potential non-invasive biomarkers, and these circulating changes may influence the immune system during disease progression. Colorectal polyps are broadly classified into neoplastic and non-neoplastic types. Neoplastic polyps include conventional adenomatous polyps (CADs), sessile serrated adenomas (SSAs), and traditional serrated adenomas (TSAs). Conventional adenomas are further categorized as tubular, tubulovillous, or villous [7]. Non-neoplastic polyps include hyperplastic, hamartomatous, and inflammatory polyps [10]. Additionally, the current updated guidelines of the United States Multi-Society Task Force (USMSTF) classify colorectal adenoma polyps into two main groups: high-risk adenomas (defined as an advanced adenomas (villous histology, high-grade dysplasia, or ≥ 10 mm) or ≥ 3 adenomas) and low-risk adenomas (defined as 1 to 2 tubular adenomas < 10 mm with low-grade dysplasia) [11]. Notably, proximal (right colon) and ≥ 5 mm hyperplastic polyps may be high-risk polyps [12]. The immune system plays a dual role in cancer development and progression [13, 14]. Based on available studies, a higher abundance of CD8⁺ T cells is associated with improved survival in CRC [15, 16]. However, their impact on tumor immunity is heterogeneous, and the interactions between different immune cells and CRC have been widely investigated [17]. Therefore, the type and function of immune cells in CRC have been studied, and the concept of immunoscores in CRC has been proposed [18, 19]. Several studies in various cancers, including CRC, have shown that the immune system can recognize tumor cells at early stages, while immune evasion mechanisms may already be established in precancerous conditions [20, 21].The immune tumor microenvironment (ITME) in CRC significantly influences tumor progression and is associated with clinical outcomes [22].

However, information regarding the immune status of precancerous colorectal polyps remains limited [7]. Additionally, the mechanisms that occur in the immune balance stage of neoplastic lesions are particularly important, and in some cases, they can prevent colorectal neoplasia from becoming malignant for decades [23, 24]. However, few studies have investigated immune editing in precancerous colorectal polyps [24]. Key components of the immune system include cytotoxic T lymphocytes, helper T lymphocytes, and regulatory T cells, which play distinct roles in controlling tumor growth [25]. T-cell immune responses are guided by a set of co-inhibitory and co-stimulatory molecules [26]. The main molecules that direct T-cell responses, cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and cluster of differentiation 28 (CD28), play crucial roles in regulating immune activity both in health and across various disease conditions, including cancer. CD28 and CTLA-4 are homologous molecules that interact with the shared ligands (CD80 and CD86) expressed on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) but transmit opposing stimulatory and inhibitory signals [27]. Therefore, their concurrent evaluation provides a more accurate insight into the mechanisms maintaining immune homeostasis and the balance between T-cell activation and immune suppression.

CD28 plays a role in the activation of T cells, the proliferation of cells, the expression of antiapoptotic genes, and inflammatory responses [28].It also exerts anti-inflammatory effects in regulatory T cells (Tregs) [29]. Kucukhuseyin et al. reported higher serum levels of CD28 in CRC patients compared with controls [30]. CD28 expression in breast cancer [31], lung adenocarcinoma [32] and ovarian cancer [33] indicates the role of CD28 in tumorigenesis or tumor suppression. The CTLA-4 molecule is introduced as a generator of inhibitory signals, which are necessary to terminate immune responses, maintain immune tolerance, and regulate cell cycle progression and apoptosis [34, 35]. A study by Kamal et al. [36] revealed a significant increase in the level of CTLA-4 gene expression in the peripheral blood of patients with CRC and investigated the diagnostic biomarker potential of CTLA-4. Zhang et al. also associated changes in the expression of several co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory immune molecules with the progression of colorectal polyps to CRC [26].

Considering the limited research on immune interactions in colorectal polyps, accumulating evidence indicates that immune checkpoint molecules, including CTLA-4 and CD28, are subject to extensive post-transcriptional regulation. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) can directly bind to the 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs) of CTLA-4 and CD28 mRNAs, thereby modulating their expression at the translational level [37–39]. In parallel, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as key regulators of immune gene expression, acting both as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) that sponge miRNAs and as transcriptional co-regulators [40–42]. Therefore, we aimed to characterize the expression profiles of miRNAs and lncRNAs and to explore their regulatory relationships with CTLA-4 and CD28 in this setting. To achieve this goal, we measured the expression of the CTLA-4 and CD28 genes in the whole blood of patients with conventional adenoma and hyperplastic colorectal polyps. The expression of these genes was also evaluated as potential diagnostic and minimally invasive biomarkers. In addition, we integrated differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) and differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database to construct a lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory network. We further used the TIMER database to assess the associations between CD28 and CTLA-4 expression levels and tumor-infiltrating immune cells in the TCGA-COAD cohort. This comprehensive approach provides deeper insight into the regulatory mechanisms of CTLA-4 and CD28 and their potential role as biomarkers in colorectal cancer and polyps.

The number, location, and histological type of the polyps were confirmed by pathological examination. Additionally, 30 volunteers were included in the study as healthy controls. The inclusion criteria comprised patients diagnosed with colorectal adenomatous or hyperplastic polyps and the availability of complete clinicopathological data. The exclusion criteria included a history of cancer, autoimmune diseases, viral infections such as hepatitis or acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), use of immunomodulatory drugs, incomplete clinicopathological data, and refusal to participate.

Peripheral blood samples (5 mL) were collected from all participants and transported to the laboratory in tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for RNA extraction.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from whole blood using the FavourPrep™ Total RNA Extraction Kit (FAVORGEN, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration (ng/µL) was measured using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). RNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The extracted RNA samples were stored at − 70 °C until complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis.

For cDNA synthesis, 300 ng of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the AddScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (AddBio, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Primer design

Sequences of the genes CTLA-4 (AF414120.1), CD28 (AH002636.2), and GAPDH (housekeeping genes) (DQ403057.1) were extracted from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Then, primers were designed for these genes via Gene Runner software (version 6.5). In the next step, the features and specificity of the primers were evaluated via the http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nihdatabase. Additionally, primers were synthesized by Pishgam, Iran. Real-time PCR

To quantitatively measure the expression levels of CTLA-4, CD28, and GAPDH, real-time PCR was performed using a Rotor-Gene Q real-time PCR cycler (Qiagen, Germany). The reactions were carried out in 40 cycles using RealQ Plus 2× Master Mix Green (Ampliqon, Denmark).

The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 62 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 20 s.

The expression levels of CTLA-4 and CD28 in each sample were normalized to GAPDH by calculating the ΔCt values. Relative gene expression was determined using the 2^−ΔΔCt method (log2 fold change) [43, 44].

Statistical analysis

The real-time PCR results were analyzed, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, California, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in gene expression between groups were compared using unpaired parametric tests (Student’s t-test and ordinary one-way ANOVA) or unpaired nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test), as appropriate.

Correlation analysis and simple linear regression were performed to evaluate gene-level associations. Prior to selecting the appropriate statistical test, the distribution of each variable was assessed. Pearson’s correlation was applied for variables with a normal distribution, whereas Spearman’s rank correlation was used for variables that were not normally distributed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess the diagnostic value of gene expression levels. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

TCGA data retrieval

To find differentially expressed miRNAs and lncRNAs we use TCGA-COAD dataset (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Expression data was gathered for 524 colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) patients from the TCGA-COAD database. Utilizing TCGAbiolinks [45–47]. package in R software (version 4.2.2), we accessed lncRNA expression profiles for 482 COAD samples and 42 normal adjacent colon tissues, along with miRNA sequencing data for 457 COAD samples and 8 normal adjacent colon tissues. We also acquired clinical information from the TCGA-COAD dataset.

Identification of differentially expressed RNAs (DERNAs)

To identify lncRNAs and miRNAs that are differentially expressed in COAD relative to normal tissue, we employed the edgeR package in R (https://www.r-project.org/) to examine the expression profiles. We used a false discovery rate (FDR) approach to pinpoint significant discrepancies in RNA expression. Results indicating an FDR < 0.05 and fold changes |log2 FC| > 1.5 are considered statistically significant.

Construction of a lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA Network

Initially, we used the Mirnet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/) [48] to predict interactions between miRNA and mRNA. We then employed the Venn package in R to assess the similarity between the expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) from the TCGA database and the predicted miRNAs. Subsequently, interactions between these shared miRNAs and lncRNAs were predicted using the minet tool. Common lncRNAs were identified by comparing DElncRNA and predicted lncRNAs using the Venn package. Finally, the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network was constructed by integrating the lncRNA-miRNA and miRNA-mRNA interaction networks.

Tumor infiltration analysis

We employed the TIMER [49] web tool (http://timer.cistrome.org/) to explore the association between tumor-infiltrating immune cells and the expression of prognostic genes in COAD patients. Using Spearman’s test, we analyzed the correlation and set a p-value < 0.05 as the criterion for statistical significance.

The expression levels of these genes were subsequently measured and analyzed separately among patients with different types of polyps and healthy controls. Finally, CTLA-4 and CD28 expression levels in patients were also evaluated according to demographic and clinicopathological characteristics.

Expression of CTLA-4 and CD28 in the whole blood of patients with colorectal polyps

We examined the expression levels of CTLA-4 and CD28 in the whole blood of patients with colorectal polyps and healthy controls. The results showed that CTLA-4 expression was significantly higher in patients than in healthy controls (P = 0.0089) (Fig. 1a). Additionally, CD28 expression was higher in patients than in healthy individuals; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.1931) (Fig. 1b).

Comparative investigation of CTLA-4 and CD28 expression in patients with different types of colorectal polyps and healthy controls

The expression levels of CTLA-4 and CD28 were also investigated and analyzed in patients with adenomatous and hyperplastic polyps. The findings revealed that CTLA-4 expression was significantly upregulated in patients with adenomatous polyps compared with healthy controls (P = 0.0162), while CD28 expression was also increased in this group, although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.3762). In addition, CTLA-4 and CD28 expression levels in patients with hyperplastic polyps were higher than those in healthy controls; however, these differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.0674 and P = 0.9476, respectively).

Moreover, no significant differences in CTLA-4 or CD28 expression were observed between patients with adenomatous polyps and those with hyperplastic polyps (Fig. 3a, b). As shown in Fig. 3c, d, we further compared CTLA-4 and CD28 expression in patients with adenomatous polyps (tubular and tubulovillous/villous) and hyperplastic polyps with that in the control group. The results demonstrated that CTLA-4 expression in patients with tubular adenomas was significantly higher than that in healthy controls (P = 0.0077).

Evaluation of CTLA-4 and CD28 gene expression in patients with colorectal polyps according to demographic and clinicopathological characteristics

We measured CTLA-4 and CD28 expression levels in the whole blood of patients with colorectal polyps according to age (> 50 years/<50 years), sex (male/female), polyp location (left/right/bilateral), number of polyps (> 1/1), and family history of cancer (positive/negative).

Based on the classification of the left and right colon, the left colon includes the splenic flexure, descending colon, and sigmoid colon, whereas the right colon includes the cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and transverse colon. Our results showed that CTLA-4 expression levels in patients with polyps located in the left colon and in both the left and right colon were significantly higher than those in patients with right-sided polyps (P = 0.0431 and P = 0.0296, respectively). However, no significant difference was observed between patients with left-sided polyps and those with polyps on both sides of the colon. CD28 expression levels were also compared among patient groups based on polyp location, and no significant differences were found (Fig. 4a). As shown in Fig. 4b, CTLA-4 expression levels in patients with a positive family history of cancer were significantly higher than those in patients with a negative family history (P = 0.0148). Similarly, CD28 expression levels were higher in patients with a positive family history than in those with a negative family history; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.0854). Finally, analysis of CTLA-4 and CD28 expression according to age, number of polyps, and sex revealed no significant differences among patients with colorectal polyps (Fig. 4c–e).

Evaluation of the potential expression levels of the CTLA-4 and CD28 genes for the diagnosis of patients with colorectal polyps

To investigate the diagnostic value of CTLA-4 and CD28 gene expression, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. The optimal cut-off value was determined using Youden’s index, and the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. ROC analysis for CTLA-4 yielded an AUC of 0.6671, with a sensitivity of 60% and a specificity of 70%, indicating statistical significance (P = 0.0173). In contrast, the AUC for CD28 was 0.5471 and did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.5025). The sensitivity and specificity of CD28 were 92.5% and 30%, respectively.

In addition, a combined ROC analysis of CTLA-4 and CD28 was performed using a binary logistic regression model to expand the dataset. The combined model (CTLA-4 + CD28) demonstrated an AUC of 0.6750, with a sensitivity of 60% and a specificity of 73.3%, indicating a moderate diagnostic performance (P value = 0.0127) (Fig. 5).

DERNAs in COAD

The TCGA-COAD dataset discovered 868 differentially expressed long non-coding RNAs (DElncRNAs) and 245 differentially expressed microRNAs (DEmiRNAs). Construction of a lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA Network in COAD

Having identified differentially expressed lncRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs (including CTLA-4 and CD28) in COAD, and having observed significant correlations between the expression levels of these immune checkpoint genes and non-coding RNAs, we sought to integrate these findings into a regulatory framework. Accumulating evidence supports the competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) hypothesis, in which lncRNAs act as molecular sponges for miRNAs, thereby derepressing miRNA-targeted mRNAs. We therefore constructed a lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA network to visualize potential ceRNA interactions involving CTLA-4 and CD28. This network integrates only those RNAs that were both differentially expressed and significantly correlated in our dataset, providing a focused map of putative regulatory axes that may influence CTLA-4 and CD28 expression in colon cancer.

The regulatory network comprised 44 lncRNAs, 15 miRNAs, and 2 mRNAs. The lncRNA KCNQ1OT1 may act as a hub lncRNA because it directly interacted with 13 miRNAs and then indirectly interacted with all miRNA-targeted mRNAs (CD28, CTLA4) in this lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network (Fig. 7a). Moreover, the mRNA CD28 may act as a hub gene for that it directly interacted with 3 miRNAs (miR-17, miR-20a, miR-106a) and then indirectly interacted with 3 lncRNA (HAGLR, LINC00689 and PVT1) (Fig. 7b) and the mRNA CTLA-4 may act as a hub gene for that it directly interacted with 2 miRNAs (miR-92a, miR-1277) and then indirectly interacted with 3 lncRNA (PURPL, KCNQ1OT1 and HOTAIR) in this lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network (Fig. 7c).

Discussion

CRC is the second most common cancer worldwide and accounts for approximately 10% of all cancer diagnoses. Conventional CRC treatment methods, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, have not yielded satisfactory results [8, 50, 51], and recent findings have clarified the key role of the immune system in the development of cancer [52, 53]. As a result, immunotherapy benefits only a subset of colorectal cancer patients, highlighting the need for predictive immune biomarkers [50]. The study of precancerous colorectal lesions can aid the development of immunotherapies and prevent progression to [20]. Although immune biomarkers outperform histopathological features in predicting recurrence and survival, few studies have examined immune interactions in precancerous colorectal polyps [7]. Furthermore, recent reviews have shown that the density of various immune cell populations, cytokine expression levels, and immune receptor profiles change along the adenoma–carcinoma sequence, highlighting the importance of investigating immune-related biomarkers in precancerous colorectal polyps [54]. The immune surveillance system is not always effective, and tumor-induced immunosuppression often involves functional impairment and exhaustion in effector lymphocytes, including T cells and sometimes NK cells. This dysfunction is not limited to the tumor microenvironment and has also been observed in circulating lymphocytes of patients with melanoma and head and neck cancers [55–57]. In this study, we measured the expression levels of two important molecules involved in the response of T cells (CTLA-4 and CD28) in the whole blood of patients with colorectal polyps. The present study revealed that the expression levels of CTLA-4 and CD28 in the whole blood of patients with colorectal polyps were higher than those in the control group. The expression of co-inhibitory molecules, such as CTLA-4, in immune cells is crucial for their suppressive [58]. Elevated CTLA-4 expression on CD8 + T cells indicates a state of cellular exhaustion [25]. The CTLA-4 pathway mediates immunosuppression via Tregs, impaired APCs function, reduced T cell proliferation, and lower cytokine production [59, 60]. Moreover, bioinformatics analysis revealed that CD28 is a hub gene that interacts directly with three oncogenic miRNAs: miR-17, miR-20a, and miR-106a. These miRNAs are well documented for their involvement in regulating cancer cell proliferation, immune evasion, and apoptosis suppression. Their interactions with CD28 may downregulate immune responses, allowing CRC cells to escape immune surveillance, a common hallmark of advanced-stage CRC [45, 46, 61]. miR-17, which belongs to the miR-17‒92 cluster, has been reported to play an oncogenic role in various cancers by promoting cell proliferation and decreasing apoptosis. The suppression of CD28 by miR-17 likely inhibits immune cell activation, impairing the body’s ability to target cancer cells [48, 49, 62]. miR-20a and miR-106a, both of which are part of the miR-17‒92 cluster, exhibit similar characteristics.

The overexpression of these genes has been linked to enhanced cell migration, invasion, and metastasis in CRC. The regulation of CD28 expression by these miRNAs indicates a potential pathway through which cancer cells evade immune detection [63, 64]. The indirect regulation of CD28 through the interaction of these miRNAs with lncRNAs such as HAGLR, LINC00689, and PVT1 adds complexity to the lncRNA‒miRNA‒mRNA network. These lncRNAs are involved in various cancer-related processes, acting as sponges for miRNAs and influencing immune responses [65–70]. HAGLR plays a role in cancer cell proliferation and migration [65–67]. In CRC, its interaction with miR-17-5p, miR-20a, and miR-106a suggests that it could regulate CD28 by sequestering these miRNAs, thereby promoting T-cell activation and anti-tumor immunity. LINC00689 and PVT1 are also involved in cancer biology. PVT1 is particularly known for its role in cancer progression and drug resistance. By interacting with these miRNAs, LINC00689 and PVT1 may influence immune system regulation in CRC by affecting the expression of CD28 [68–70]. CTLA-4 is a well-established immune checkpoint molecule that suppresses T-cell activation and is essential for immune homeostasis. In addition to its immunoregulatory role, CTLA-4 signaling has been implicated in the indirect regulation of cell-cycle progression and apoptosis through modulation of intracellular pathways, including RAS-related signaling. Accordingly, CTLA-4 is a major target of cancer immunotherapy, as its inhibition can restore antitumor immune responses [35, 71, 72].In this lncRNA‒miRNA‒mRNA network, CTLA-4 is another hub gene that interacts directly with miR-92a and miR-1277. MiR-92a, another member of the miR-17‒92 cluster, has been linked to oncogenesis in various cancers, including CRC [73, 74]. However, miR-92a also plays a role in promoting cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, suggesting that its overall impact may depend on its expression and interaction with other molecules [73, 74]. miR-1277 is less well characterized in CRC but has potential regulatory effects on immune checkpoint molecules such as CTLA-4. The lncRNA‒miRNA‒mRNA network also involves the lncRNAs PURPL, KCNQ1OT1, and HOTAIR, which indirectly regulate CTLA-4 by interacting with miR-92a and miR-1277. PURPL, an oncogenic lncRNA, stabilizes p53 and has been implicated in regulating the immune response [75]. Its interaction with miRNAs that target CTLA-4 suggests that it may influence immune evasion mechanisms by modulating CTLA-4 expression.

KCNQ1OT1 and HOTAIR are well known for their roles in epigenetic regulation and cancer metastasis [76–78]. Both lncRNAs likely modulate CTLA-4 expression by competing with miRNAs that target CTLA-4, contributing to immune suppression and CRC progression. Recent studies show that high levels of soluble CTLA-4 (sCTLA-4) in the serum of CRC patients are significantly associated with lymphatic invasion. Furthermore, patients with elevated CTLA-4 expression on tumor cells and sCTLA-4 exhibit significantly poorer disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) [79]. CRC patients show high CTLA-4 expression on CD4 + tumor-infiltrating T cells, particularly FOXP3 + Helios+ regulatory T cells, highlighting the key suppressive role of Tregs in [80]. In a 2023 study by Dutta et al., high CTLA-4 expression was reported in 71.8% of SCLC tumor tissues, along with increased sCTLA-4 in patients’ blood. Elevated IDO activity, which induces apoptosis in effector T cells and Treg differentiation, and reduced IL-2 levels indicate an immunosuppressive environment, suggesting CTLA-4 may predict immunotherapy [81]. Similarly, Saleh et al. found that increased CTLA-4 expression in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and tissues of CRC patients is associated with suppressed anti-tumor immunity and treatment resistance, highlighting its potential role in CRC progression [82]. Researchers reported upregulation of CTLA-4 in the peripheral blood of 60 CRC patients, highlighting its diagnostic and prognostic [36]. CTLA-4 is highly and stably expressed on Tregs [83] ), and increased Treg numbers in the blood and precancerous polyp tissue may enhance immunosuppression [84, 85]. Although CD28 is essential for T cell function and immune stimulation, it can exert anti-inflammatory effects on Tregs and, depending on its expression, indirectly inhibit the immune system. Furthermore, CD28 signals are required for maximal IL-10 production by Tregs [80, 86]. In addition, increased IL-17a levels have been reported in the tissues and Vδ2 T cells of CRC patients [87, 88], and its expression in colorectal adenoma polyps rises with dysplasia severity [89, 90]. Finally, a positive correlation between CD28 expression and IL-17a levels in Vδ2 T cells of CRC patients has also been suggested [88]. In 2021, Li et al. reported increased CD28 and CTLA-4 in the sentinel lymph node of CRC patients compared with nonsentinel nodes, indicating active immunity despite T-cell exhaustion [91]. However, Lee et al. observed downregulation of these markers in PBMCs of CRC patients and those with adenoma polyps, showing altered immune profiles in precancerous lesions [92].Similarly, Gruber et al. linked reduced CD28 in cytotoxic T cells from breast cancer patients to immunosuppression and tumor spread [31]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), high CD28 in tumor tissues correlated with longer survival and more CD8 + and CD4 + T cells, highlighting its anti-tumor role [93].

Also, In 2022, a study by Swiderska et al. compared the serum of patients with epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) with that of patients with benign lesions. They reported that the concentration of CD28 in the serum of EOC patients is significantly increased. It is also possible that the CD28 level can serve as a diagnostic biomarker [33]. Furthermore, Sun et al. showed that in young lung adenocarcinoma patients, CD28 expression was higher in the progression group, and high CD28 was associated with shorter DFS and longer OS, indicating its tumorigenic role in early lung [32]. Overall, CD28 may play different roles in the prognosis of various cancers [93]. A 2023 study by Zhang et al. reported that increased expression of co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory receptors in colorectal precancerous lesions and CRC tissues is linked to polyp progression and an immunosuppressive microenvironment [26].

Our study, among the first to examine these gene changes in colorectal polyps, supports systemic immunosuppression in patients, given the upregulation of CTLA-4 and CD28. Additionally, The positive correlation between CTLA-4 and CD28 expression observed in both patients and controls reflects coordinated immune regulation under normal conditions. This correlation is attenuated in patients, suggesting a disruption in immune regulation and altered immune homeostasis in patients with colorectal polyps.

We compared CTLA-4 and CD28 expression in patients with adenoma and hyperplastic polyps versus controls. Both genes were upregulated in adenoma patients, and hyperplastic polyps showed similar regulation. Interestingly, the expression levels were comparable between adenoma and hyperplastic polyps, suggesting that even polyps with lower malignant potential may contribute to systemic immune suppression. When looking at polyp subtypes, CTLA-4 was significantly increased in tubular polyps compared to controls, while tubulovillous/villous polyps showed a non-significant increase. CD28 expression was slightly elevated across all polyp types. These findings highlight the role of tubular polyps in immune suppression, and although their malignant potential is lower than that of villous or tubulovillous polyps [94], the upregulation of immune checkpoint genes in tubular polyps may indicate an early mechanism of immune escape before histological progression. In line with previous findings, a study in 2020 reported that CTLA-4 expression in the circulation of early-stage CRC patients was higher than in advanced-stage patients, suggesting that CTLA-4 expressing T cells accumulate in peripheral blood during disease onset [82].

Our results report a significant increase in the expression of CTLA-4 in patients with polyps in the left colon compared to the right colon. Also, patients with polyps on both sides of the colon showed higher expression compared to the right colon. The expression level of CD28 was almost similar across groups. CTLA-4 and CD28 expression increased in patients with a positive family history of cancer. Finally, expression levels were almost similar regarding sex, age, and number of polyps.

ROC curve analysis of the CTLA-4 expression level revealed an AUC of 0.66. Additionally, with a specificity of 70% and sensitivity of 60%, it is statistically significant (P value = 0.0173) and has moderate biomarker properties. However, more studies are needed to prove this. On the other hand, the CD28 expression level had an AUC of 0.54. The specificity and sensitivity of CD28 were 30% and 92.5%, respectively, but these differences did not reach statistical significance (P value = 0.5025). In addition, a combined ROC analysis of CTLA-4 and CD28 was performed. The combined model showed an AUC of 0.67, with a sensitivity of 60% and a specificity of 73.3% (P value = 0.0127), indicating moderate diagnostic performance. Compared with the separate analyses, the combined model showed a slight improvement over CTLA-4 alone and a more significant improvement over CD28 alone; however, the overall discriminatory power remained moderate.

The lncRNA‒miRNA‒mRNA network involving CD28, CTLA-4, miRNAs, and lncRNAs plays a critical role in immune regulation in colorectal cancer.By acting as hub genes; CD28 and CTLA-4 modulate key immune checkpoints that influence T-cell activation and immune evasion in CRC. miRNAs such as miR-17, miR-20a, miR-106a, miR-92a, and miR-1277 regulate the expression of these hub genes, while lncRNAs like HAGLR, PVT1, PURPL, and HOTAIR provide additional layers of regulation. These findings open new possibilities for therapeutic strategies targeting lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA networks to modulate immune responses and improve CRC treatment outcomes. From this perspective, the network derived from CRC datasets was not intended to represent a polyp-specific model, but rather to serve as a mechanistic and hypothesis-generating framework that may reflect regulatory programs initiated in the early stages of colorectal tumorigenesis and progressively strengthened during malignant transformation. This biological continuity can provide a logical bridge between our clinical findings in polyp samples and the network-based observations derived from CRC datasets. Accordingly, the components of this network may serve as potential candidate biomarkers for future studies evaluating their diagnostic or prognostic value in colorectal polyps. However, given the limited availability of omics data in colorectal polyps, these proposed regulatory interactions should be interpreted with caution and require further validation in precancerous tissues and independent future studies to confirm their functional relevance and clinical utility.

One of the main strengths of this study is that it is among the first to evaluate systemic immune changes in patients with colorectal polyps by examining both inhibitory and stimulatory checkpoint genes (CTLA-4 and CD28). The use of minimally invasive biomarkers and the analysis based on polyp types and clinicopathological features further add value. These findings suggest that future screening protocols should consider not only the histopathological characteristics of polyps but also the patients’ immune responses.

The study’s limitations include the small sample size, lack of protein-level analysis, and absence of long-term patient follow-up. Nevertheless, it provides important preliminary evidence for the role of immune checkpoint gene expression in the early assessment of colorectal polyps.

Conclusions

The present study revealed that upregulation of CTLA-4 and CD28 may contribute to the suppression of systemic immunity in patients with conventional adenomatous and hyperplastic colorectal precancerous polyps and, by disrupting immune surveillance, potentially increase the risk of malignant transformation. Given the importance of colorectal polyps in the development of CRC and the lack of reliable biomarkers for early diagnosis, evaluating the diagnostic value of co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory immune molecules in blood samples as a minimally invasive approach warrants further comprehensive investigation.
