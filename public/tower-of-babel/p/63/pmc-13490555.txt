Plantaris tendon morphology in Papio anubis: a comparative anatomical study
Authors: Ingrid C Landfald, Rui Diogo, George Triantafyllou, Maria Piagkou, Robert Haładaj, Łukasz Olewnik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe plantaris muscle shows marked variability in humans, yet comparable data from non-human primates remain limited. This study examined plantaris tendon morphology in 46 lower limbs from 23 adult Anubis baboons (Papio anubis) using standardized dissection, morphometry and a human-derived insertional classification. A plantaris muscle was present in all specimens, and tendon morphology was limited to two recurrent patterns: a narrower band-like configuration corresponding to human Type II and a broader sheet-like configuration corresponding to human Type V. Type II predominated (73.9%), whereas Type V occurred in 26.1% of limbs. Between-type differences involved tendon width, thickness and the level of the extension point relative to the calcaneus, while overall limb dimensions did not differ significantly. These findings indicate that, within the examined sample, plantaris tendon morphology in Papio anubis was restricted to two recurrent configurations rather than displaying the broader insertional spectrum reported in humans. The results provide a preliminary comparative anatomical baseline for future studies of plantaris tendon variation across primates, while broader taxonomic, geographic and population-level sampling is required before broader evolutionary or species-wide interpretations can be drawn.Subject terms: Anatomy, Medical research, Zoology

Introduction

The plantaris muscle is a small fusiform component of the superficial posterior compartment of the leg. It is classically described as originating from the lateral supracondylar line of the femur and the capsule of the knee joint, and giving rise to a long, slender tendon that descends obliquely between the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles before coursing to insert on or near the calcaneal tuberosity, usually medial to the Achilles tendon1,2. In humans, its direct contribution to plantarflexion and knee flexion is generally considered modest compared with the gastrocnemius–soleus complex; however, its long tendon, close relationship to the Achilles tendon and reported intramuscular organisation have led to renewed interest in its potential proprioceptive and sensorimotor role within the posterior crural compartment2–6. Although often labelled a vestigial structure with limited functional importance in humans, anatomical series have demonstrated striking variability in its presence, architecture and attachment, including unilateral or bilateral agenesis, multi-headed or duplicated bellies, and a wide spectrum of insertion patterns that have been organised into reproducible types based on their relationship to the Achilles tendon and deep crural fascia5,7–19. Several of these morphotypes, particularly broad sheet-like expansions enveloping the medial aspect of the Achilles tendon and ligamentous or blended insertions into the calcaneus, have been implicated in mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy, acute musculotendinous injury patterns such as so-called “tennis leg”, and technical challenges during tendon graft harvesting or reconstructive procedures around the ankle3,20–31.

Building on this general variability, several classification systems have formalised the spectrum of plantaris tendon insertions in humans, ranging from early schemes based on topographical relationships to the calcaneus and Achilles tendon22,30,32 to the six-type framework proposed in adult cadaveric series by Olewnik and colleagues, which was subsequently expanded to an eight-type system in foetal and adult material15,19,33. Within this latter framework, Type II denotes a band-shaped insertion directly into the medial aspect of the Achilles tendon, sharing a common paratenon, whereas Type V describes a broad, sheet-like expansion that wraps around the posterior–medial surface of the Achilles tendon, maximising contact between the two structures15,19,33. These two morphotypes are of particular biomechanical and clinical interest, as they have been repeatedly implicated in mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy and related pain syndromes in both imaging and surgical series, and are frequently targeted in surgical excision or debridement procedures aimed at reducing mechanical conflict along the medial border of the Achilles tendon3,20,27–31. In parallel, intramuscular innervation mapping using modified Sihler’s staining, together with histological and experimental work on muscle spindle distribution and tendon architecture in muscles acting in parallel combinations, has been interpreted as supporting a specialised sensorimotor role for the plantaris muscle within the Achilles complex, challenging the traditional notion of a purely vestigial muscle2–6,34. The recent description of a robust plantaris ligamentous tendon, forming a consistent tendinous–ligamentous bridge between the plantaris muscle, the posterior femur and the iliotibial band, further suggests that in humans the plantaris apparatus may be undergoing functional reorganisation rather than simple evolutionary regression11.

Across mammals, the plantaris muscle is commonly described as a distinct component of the posterior crural musculature, although its size, tendon architecture and functional integration vary among taxa35,38. In non-human primates, classical and modern anatomical surveys consistently report a plantaris muscle within the triceps surae complex, but typically provide only succinct descriptions of its origin, course and general insertion, without detailed analysis of plantaris tendon morphology or its relationship to the Achilles tendon39,40. From a comparative and evolutionary perspective, non-human primate data are important because they may help distinguish features that are broadly shared within primates from those that are more specific to the human lineage. However, existing comparative data do not allow human-derived insertion schemes to be directly mapped onto non-human primate species and, to our knowledge, no study has systematically applied a contemporary human classification of plantaris tendon types to baboons or quantitatively compared these configurations in Papio using standardised geometric measurements.

Anubis baboons (Papio anubis) provide an attractive model in which to explore these questions. As large, semi-terrestrial cercopithecids they possess a robust triceps surae complex and well-developed distal limb musculature, making them suitable for comparative work on calf muscle–tendon architecture39,40. In the present cadaveric study, we examined 46 lower limbs from adult Papio anubis and focused on two clinically relevant patterns of plantaris tendon morphology, Types II and V, defined according to their relationship to the Achilles tendon and deep crural fascia in human-based classifications14,19,33. Using a standardised protocol, we recorded leg length, muscle-belly and tendinous lengths, the distance from the extension point of the plantaris tendon to the calcaneal tuberosity, and tendon width and thickness at both the divergence from the soleus and the extension point, with selected variables normalised to leg length. Our aims were to (1) document the prevalence and side/sex distribution of Types II and V in Papio, (2) quantify their geometry and spatial relationship to the Achilles tendon, and (3) compare these patterns qualitatively with the range of plantaris tendon configurations described in humans without pooling morphometric datasets across species3,20,27–30. Because Type II is a frequent and reproducible band-like configuration in human anatomical series14,15, we hypothesised that a Type II-like pattern would predominate if the human-derived classification captured recurrent configurations in baboons. We further hypothesised that Type V-like configurations, if present, would show a broader distal expansion positioned closer to the calcaneal insertion, and that the two morphotypes would differ primarily in tendon geometry rather than in overall limb dimensions. These hypotheses were framed as comparative expectations rather than as assumptions of direct equivalence between human and baboon plantaris morphology.

Materials and methods

Specimens

We examined 46 lower limbs (23 left, 23 right) from 23 adult Anubis baboons (Papio anubis; 13 males, 10 females), obtained post-mortem from zoological facilities and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. No animals were culled specifically for this study. Under Directive 2010/63/EU and the Polish Act of 15 January 2015, research conducted exclusively on dead archival specimens falls outside the scope of animal experimentation requiring ethical approval; formal approval by an institutional animal ethics committee was therefore not required. Provenance was verified from collection records, and limb-level metadata included side (left/right; L = 23, R = 23).

Dissection

Standard posterior leg dissection was performed to expose the triceps surae complex and the plantaris muscle. The skin, subcutaneous tissue and crural fascia were removed to reveal the gastrocnemius, soleus and plantaris muscle. The plantaris muscle was identified from its origin on the lateral supracondylar region of the femur, tracked through the musculotendinous junction and followed distally to its insertion in the calcaneal region. For each limb, we documented (1) the course and configuration of the plantaris tendon, (2) its relationship to the Achilles tendon, and (3) the point at which the plantaris tendon emerged from the deep crural fascia and diverged from the soleus/Achilles tendon complex.

All plantaris tendons were assessed macroscopically against the complete six-type human classification of plantaris tendon insertion described by Olewnik et al.14,15. No prior decision was made to restrict the assessment to Type II or Type V configurations. For each specimen, the distal course, insertion pattern and relationship of the plantaris tendon to the Achilles tendon, calcaneal tuberosity and deep crural fascia were evaluated. For reference, Olewnik et al.14 distinguish six patterns of plantaris tendon insertion in humans: Type I, a wide, fan-shaped insertion on the medial side of the calcaneal tuberosity; Type II, a band-shaped insertion along the medial calcaneal tuberosity sharing a paratenon with the Achilles tendon; Type III, insertion on the calcaneal bone anterior to the Achilles tendon; Type IV, insertion into the deep crural fascia without direct contact with the calcaneus or Achilles tendon; Type V, a very broad tendon expansion encircling the posterior and medial surfaces of the Achilles tendon; and Type VI, insertion near the flexor retinaculum of the tarsal canal. After assessment of all specimens, only configurations corresponding to human Type II and Type V were identified in the present Papio anubis sample. Type II was defined as a band-shaped plantaris tendon inserting into the medial calcaneal region in close continuity with the Achilles tendon, whereas Type V was defined as a broad, sheet-like expansion enveloping the posterior–medial surface of the Achilles tendon and blending with the deep crural fascia.

Measurements

Linear dimensions were obtained with digital callipers (resolution 0.01 mm). During morphometric assessment, each hindlimb was placed in a consistent posterior-facing orientation, with the posterior crural compartment fully exposed. Measurements were taken without applying traction to the plantaris tendon or Achilles tendon, and joint position was kept non-forced as far as permitted by fixation and specimen preservation. For each limb, we recorded: (1) leg length, defined as the distance from the lateral femoral condyle to the calcaneal tuberosity; (2) plantaris muscle belly length, measured from the femoral origin to the musculotendinous junction; (3) plantaris tendon length, measured from the musculotendinous junction to the calcaneal insertion; (4) distance from the extension point of the plantaris tendon to the calcaneal tuberosity, with the extension point defined as the point at which the plantaris tendon emerged from the deep crural fascia and began its distal expansion toward the calcaneal region; (5) tendon width at the divergence point, where the plantaris tendon separated from the soleus/Achilles tendon complex; (6) tendon thickness at the divergence point; (7) tendon width at the extension point; and (8) tendon thickness at the extension point.

Where appropriate, selected variables were additionally normalised to leg length to account for inter-individual size differences. Each variable was measured at least twice using the same standardized protocol, and the mean of repeated readings was used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Depending on distributional assumptions, between-type comparisons (Type II vs. Type V) used Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. To aid interpretation of between-type differences, effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d for continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Because comparisons were limited and hypothesis-driven, p-values are presented descriptively and interpreted alongside effect-size estimates. Analyses were performed in Python using pandas and SciPy.

Ethics statement

This study used archival post-mortem hindlimb specimens of Papio anubis obtained from zoological facilities. Under Directive 2010/63/EU and the Polish Act of 15 January 2015, research performed on dead archival specimens does not constitute an animal experiment and therefore does not require formal ethical committee approval. Provenance and handling of all specimens were documented in institutional collection records. The study did not involve living animals or human participants.

Results

Prevalence and gross architecture

A plantaris muscle was identified in all 46 lower limbs; no unilateral or bilateral agenesis was observed. The plantaris tendon descended along the posteromedial aspect of the leg, superficial to the soleus and deep fascia, towards the calcaneal region without aberrant or intramuscular courses. In all cases, both the extension point, where the plantaris tendon emerged from the deep crural fascia, and a divergence point from the soleus/Achilles tendon complex were clearly identifiable. Descriptive morphometric data for all limbs are summarised in Table 1. Mean leg length was 220.96 ± 15.04 mm. The mean plantaris tendon length was 176.72 ± 12.80 mm, and the mean plantaris muscle belly length was 77.38 ± 6.27 mm. The mean extension point–calcaneal distance was 26.89 ± 2.13 mm.

Discussion

In this cadaveric series of 46 lower limbs from adult Papio anubis, the plantaris muscle was universally present and the tendon conformed to only two recurrent morphotypes that correspond to human Types II and V. This restricted spectrum contrasts with the broader variability described in human anatomical series and indicates that, within the examined sample, plantaris tendon morphology in Papio anubis was limited to two recurrent distal configurations14,15,19,33. The predominance of Type II and the absence of additional insertional variants, accessory expansions or complete agenesis indicate a relatively consistent organisational pattern in the examined specimens, although broader sampling is required before this pattern can be interpreted as species-wide.

When placed in a broader comparative framework, the Papio plantaris muscle conforms to a long-tendon architecture that is broadly comparable to that described in humans and other mammals. In our material, the tendon was disproportionately long relative to leg length and to the relatively small muscle belly, with the extension point lying only a short distance proximal to the calcaneal tuberosity. Human cadaveric and surgical series similarly emphasise a long, slender plantaris tendon that is often sufficient for graft harvesting, contrasted with a compact muscle belly3,22,24,41,42,]. At the same time, humans exhibit substantial variability in overall architecture, including unilateral or bilateral agenesis, multi-headed or duplicated muscles, atypical fusions and a wide spectrum of insertion patterns1,4,7,8,12,13,17,18,43–48. Comparative anatomical syntheses indicate that a well-developed plantaris muscle with a long tendon contributing to ankle control, propulsion and elastic energy storage is characteristic of many primates and carnivores, whereas in several ungulate taxa the muscle is reduced or functionally replaced by other components of the caudal thigh and crural musculature35–40. Within this continuum, baboons in the examined sample showed a relatively consistent plantaris muscle–tendon arrangement: they retain a consistently present plantaris muscle with a long tendon closely integrated with the Achilles tendon, but pattern this architecture into only two distal morphotypes, providing a preliminary comparative reference point against which the greater structural lability observed in humans can be interpreted.

In humans, the plantaris muscle encompasses not only a clinically relevant proportion of unilateral or bilateral agenesis but also multi-headed configurations, duplicated muscles and a wide array of insertion patterns beyond Types II and V. Cadaveric and imaging series have documented double and even three-headed plantaris muscles, highly complex variants with bipartite or multiple tendinous insertions, ligamentous or blended plantaris-calcaneal continuations, and occasional atypical relationships to the popliteal neurovascular bundle1,7–9,15,17,18,34. Several of these configurations, particularly broad sheet-like or ligamentous expansions closely apposed to the medial border of the Achilles tendon, have been implicated among the structures involved in mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy and in the pathoanatomy of so-called tennis leg, and they can complicate or constrain the use of the plantaris tendon as a graft in reconstructive procedures involving the hand, knee or Achilles tendon itself3,20–22,24–31,41,49. Against this background, the Papio pattern documented here - consistent presence of the plantaris muscle, restriction to two distal morphotypes and absence of extreme multi-headed or aberrant neurovascular variants -provides a preliminary reference point for comparison with the more variable human condition.

Within Papio, the contrast between Types II and V defines a clear geometric dichotomy in the way the plantaris tendon interfaces with the Achilles tendon. Type II consistently presented as a slender, cord-like tendon along the medial border of the Achilles tendon, inserting into the medial region of the calcaneal tuberosity in close continuity with the tendon, with a relatively narrow but thick cross-section at the extension point. Type V, in turn, formed a broad, sheet-like distal expansion that wrapped around and closely invested the posteromedial aspect of the Achilles tendon, with a wider but thinner tendon at the extension point. Morphometric comparisons showed a greater extension point–calcaneal distance in Type II than in Type V, indicating a more proximally situated extension point in Type II and a more distal, calcaneus-adjacent extension point in Type V, together with higher distal thickness in Type II and greater distal width in Type V. These between-type differences remained after normalising tendon measures to leg length, suggesting that they reflect true geometric variation rather than simple differences in overall limb size. The right-sided predominance of Type V in our material should be interpreted cautiously given the sample size and may reflect stochastic variation rather than true functional lateralisation, but it underlines the value of recording side-specific data. Taken together, these data reinforce a dichotomy between a cord-like Type II configuration and a sheet-like, wrap-around Type V configuration, conceptually analogous to the band-shaped and broad-encircling insertions described in human material14,33. In humans, Types II and V occur against a broader spectrum of insertion morphologies but are repeatedly singled out as clinically important because their close, often extensive contact with the medial Achilles tendon is associated with mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy and targeted in operative debridement or plantaris excision3,20,27–30. In this context, the recurrent geometry of Types II and V in the examined Papio anubis sample supports the interpretation that the plantaris tendon maintained a consistent anatomical relationship with the Achilles tendon. Developmental and neuroanatomical observations from human studies provide useful context but should be interpreted cautiously in relation to the present Papio anubis material. Developmental presence alone cannot exclude vestigial status, as structures with reduced adult function may still form during embryonic or foetal development. However, human foetal studies show that the plantaris muscle may already be present as a discrete muscle belly and tendon, with proximal and distal attachments that can be classified using adult-derived schemes19,47. In addition, intramuscular innervation mapping using modified Sihler’s staining in adult human material has revealed an organised intramuscular nerve tree rather than a rudimentary pattern, with segmental branches distributed along the muscle belly and musculotendinous junction5. Together with histological and experimental work on muscle spindle distribution in small muscles acting in parallel with larger synergists6, these observations support a cautious interpretation of the plantaris muscle as a structured and potentially functional component of the posterior compartment. The present findings in Papio anubisshould therefore be viewed as comparative anatomical context rather than as direct developmental or functional evidence.

Across mammals, the plantaris appears to follow a broad phylogenetic gradient in which many cursorial and scansorial species, including numerous non-human primates, retain a well-developed and consistently present muscle-tendon unit, whereas humans and some African apes occupy a more labile end of the spectrum. Classical veterinary syntheses describe a robust plantaris with a long tendon in several carnivores and primates, in contrast to partial reduction or loss in various ungulate lineages, where its functions are partly assumed by the flexor digitorum superficialis and gastrocnemius muscles35–38. Within Primates, comparative accounts indicate that cercopithecoids and many hominoids, including macaques and chimpanzees, typically possess a distinct plantaris closely integrated with the calf muscle complex, while humans are characterised by inconsistent presence of the muscle and by the widest range of insertion patterns described to date3,13,40,50. Against this background, the Papio configuration documented here - universal expression of the plantaris muscle, restriction to two distal morphotypes and absence of extreme variants in the examined material suggests a more restricted range of plantaris tendon configurations than that reported in humans. This interpretation remains preliminary and requires confirmation in broader taxonomic, geographic and population-level samples.

The geometric differences between Types II and V in Papio may be relevant to how the plantaris tendon relates to neighbouring structures. Type II follows a narrower, thicker course with a more proximal ExP, whereas Type V forms a broader and thinner configuration situated closer to the calcaneus and in more extensive contact with the Achilles tendon. These features are consistent with differences in local tissue apposition and gliding relationships, although direct biomechanical testing was not performed in the present study. The findings should therefore be interpreted as an anatomical framework for future functional studies rather than as direct evidence of mechanical behaviour.

From an evolutionary perspective, the contrast between the examined Papio sample and humans may reflect a more restricted plantaris tendon pattern in the present material compared with the broader variability documented in human anatomical series. This interpretation should be viewed cautiously, but it is consistent with the possibility that the plantaris apparatus in humans has undergone substantial reorganisation rather than simple reduction11,13. In that context, the Papio pattern may serve as a useful comparative reference for interpreting human plantaris tendon variation within a broader comparative anatomical framework.

In human orthopaedics and sports medicine, the plantaris is increasingly recognised as a potential co-contributor to mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy, local pain syndromes and difficulties during tendon-harvesting procedures3,20,23,27–31. Although the present study was not designed to model disease directly, the close relationship between the plantaris tendon and the Achilles tendon in both Papio morphotypes supports the value of comparative anatomy in framing future translational questions. In particular, the Type V configuration, with its broad apposition to the Achilles tendon, may provide a useful anatomical reference for imaging, histological or biomechanical studies aimed at clarifying plantaris-Achilles interactions.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although the sample of 46 lower limbs from 23 adult Papio anubis individuals is valuable for a non-human primate cadaveric study, it remains limited in size, particularly when compared with the larger human anatomical series and the extensive historical record of human cadaveric dissection. Second, all specimens were derived from a single zoo-based archival population. Therefore, potential population-level, geographic and genetic variation could not be assessed, and close relatedness among some individuals cannot be excluded. This limitation is important because reduced genetic and population diversity may contribute to the relatively restricted range of morphologies observed in the present material. Third, zoo conditions do not fully reproduce the locomotor demands, ecological pressures and selective environments experienced by wild olive baboons, which may also influence musculoskeletal variation. Fourth, the material consisted of formalin-fixed cadavers without behavioural, dynamic or functional data, which precludes direct linkage between tendon architecture and in vivo locomotor performance. The effects of fixation and post-mortem handling on absolute morphometric values, although likely modest, also cannot be entirely excluded. Fifth, we did not perform histological analyses, muscle spindle counts, intramuscular innervation mapping, developmental assessment or in vivo imaging such as ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging. As a result, the proposed functional and biomechanical interpretations remain inferential. Finally, although each measurement was repeated and averaged, formal intraobserver and interobserver reliability testing was not performed. These limitations do not diminish the descriptive value of this rare anatomical sample, but they require caution when extrapolating the findings beyond the examined specimens.

Looking ahead, several lines of enquiry could build on the present work. First, applying the same dissection protocol, distal classification criteria and morphometric approach to additional cercopithecoid and hominoid species would help to determine whether a bimodal Type II/Type V pattern is a general primate feature or a Papio-specific configuration, and whether other taxa introduce additional morphotypes or different frequency balances39,40. Second, ontogenetic series of Papio, spanning late foetal to juvenile stages, would allow testing whether a broader spectrum of plantaris muscle architectures is present early in development and subsequently canalised by growth and mechanical loading, in analogy to the human foetal data indicating an early establishment of adult-like insertion patterns19,47,51. Third, functional studies combining electromyography, controlled ex vivo loading, ultrasound or MRI-based motion analysis and finite element modelling of the Achilles–plantaris complex in limbs with Type II versus Type V tendons could begin to quantify how these morphotypes differ in load transfer, contact mechanics and proprioceptive signalling. Finally, comparative developmental and genetic work, including three-dimensional imaging of muscle–tendon patterning and analyses of candidate regulatory pathways in humans and Papio, may help clarify why plantaris tendon morphology is highly polymorphic in humans and whether comparable or distinct patterns occur across non-human primate lineages13.

Conclusion

In this cadaveric series of 46 lower limbs from adult Papio anubis, the plantaris muscle was present in all specimens, and plantaris tendon morphology was restricted to two recurrent distal configurations corresponding to human Types II and V. These morphotypes differed primarily in tendon geometry and in their relationship to the Achilles tendon, whereas overall limb dimensions remained comparable. The findings provide a preliminary comparative anatomical reference for future studies of plantaris tendon variation across primates and for cautious interpretation of human plantaris-related clinical observations.
