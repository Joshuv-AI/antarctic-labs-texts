H3K36 methyltransferase SDG102 enhances salt tolerance by altering the methylation level of genes in maize (Zea mays L.)
Authors: Xinyang Liu, Yuhong Zheng, Yang Liu, Han Wang, Xiaoshuang Wei, Hongshuang Liu, Huai Tian, Xinyi Zhao, Zhenhui Wang, Xin Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChromatin modification governs plant abiotic stress responses, yet the regulatory interplay between H3K36 methylation-mediated transcriptome reprogramming and DNA methylation dynamics in maize salt tolerance remains elusive. This study identifies the maize-specific H3K36 methyltransferase SDG102 as a positive contributor of salt tolerance. Under salt stress, SDG102-overexpressing plants showed 10–21.3% higher antioxidant enzyme activities and reduced malondialdehyde content, confirming enhanced ROS scavenging and membrane integrity. Transcriptome sequencing of wild-type and SDG102-overexpressing plants revealed 614 differentially expressed genes predominantly enriched in ion homeostasis, antioxidant defense, and hormone signaling pathways. Genome-wide bisulfite sequencing detected about 45,817 differentially methylated regions, of which 5,896 DMRs located in the promoter region were anchored to 4,195 genes, and the DMRs with CHH background were anchored to the most genes CHH hypomethylation specifically overlaps with CHH hypomethylation specifically overlapped promoter regions of salt-tolerance genes ZmNHX16 and ZmNHX17. Integrated analysis supports a model wherein SDG102 orchestrates salt tolerance by modulating DNA methylation, specifically coregulating CHH methylation. Six key salt-tolerance genes were identified, advancing mechanistic understanding of salt adaptation and providing vital targets for crop improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08447-4.Keywords: DNA methylation, Molecular evolution, Salt stress

Background

Soil salinization has emerged as a major environmental threat to global food security [1]. Recent data indicate that approximately 833 million hectares of land worldwide are affected by salinity, leading to reduced productivity in nearly 20% of irrigated agricultural zones [2]. The dual mechanisms of salt stress damage in plants are well established: ionic toxicity caused by Na⁺ and Cl⁻ accumulation disrupts cellular membrane integrity and enzyme activity [3], while high pH conditions exacerbate osmotic stress and nutrient imbalances [4]. Gramineous crops, particularly maize with its fibrous root system, exhibit heightened sensitivity to salinity, which impairs relative growth rates, osmotic homeostasis, transpiration, ion transport, photosynthetic efficiency, and senescence [5–8]. In addition, an excessive accumulation of salt in a maize plant’s cells can produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), which inhibit photosynthetic activity [9, 10]. As the world's most cultivated cereal crop, maize suffers significant yield losses under saline conditions [11]. Improvement of maize salt stress tolerance is therefore of paramount importance for global food security.

In maize plant cells, salt-induced osmotic leading to an excessive accumulation of ROS, which in turn gives rise to oxidative stress [12, 13]. To mitigate oxidative stress caused by ROS accumulation under high salinity conditions, plants have evolved ROS scavenging mechanisms, including non-enzymatic antioxidant metabolites and enzymes [14]. The enzyme systems in plants include catalase (CAT) and other enzymes [15–17], showed that in rice roots exposed to saline-alkali stress, the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, POD, and CAT was significantly elevated, which ultimately mitigated cell damage and improved its chances of survival. In soybean roots, usually accompanied by an increase in POD activity and a significant decrease in H2O2 content under salt stress [18]. In order to survive to respond by deploying various strategies, the establishment of an enhanced level of biosynthesis of osmolytes, and the activation of ROS scavenging systems [19, 20].

Chromatin-based epigenetic modifications play an essential role in regulation of gene expression in eukaryotes. At the epigenetic level, three histone modifications, acetylation, methylation and phosphorylation have been reported to influence salt-induced stress responses [21]. High salt-induced response triggers the activation of cell wall related genes, the transcriptional up regulation of these genes has been correlated with increased H3K9ac in the promoter and the open reading frames [22]. H3K27me3 play crucial roles in generating somatic stress memory induced by salt preexposure [21, 23]. Histone modifications further fine-tune chromatin dynamics to regulate stress-responsive transcription. SET-domain proteins, conserved from yeast Set1 to plant orthologs, catalyze methylation at H3K4, H3K9, H3K27, H3K36, and H4K20 [24]. H3K4me3, a transcriptional activation mark, is established by TrxG proteins to activate stress-response genes in Arabidopsis [25]. Methylation of histone H3 lysine 36 (H3K36) and H3 lysine 4 (H3K4) are most likely associated with the transcription of active euchromatin, while the methylation of histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9) and H3 lysine 27 (H3K27) are associated with silent chromatin [26]. H3K36 methylation is involved in a variety of processes such as DNA repair, flowering, pollen development, response to stress, and disease resistance in plants [27].

Histone methylation is mediated by histone lysine methyltransferases (HKMTases) and HKMTases are called the Set Domain Group (SDG) proteins. At least five Arabidopsis SDGs, six maize (Zea mays) proteins, and five rice (Oryza sativa) proteins belong to Ash1 SET proteins that may be involved in H3K36 methylation [28, 29]. A total of eight of these have been found to demonstrate HKMTase activity, which plays a crucial role in flowering time and floral organ development. In Arabidopsis, H3K36 methylation is catalyzed by the histone methyltransferases SDG8 and SDG26, which are involved in regulating many plants growth and developmental processes [30, 31]. Furthermore, H3K36 methylation contributes to the regulation of light and carbon response [32], photoperiod-dependent flowering time control [33], temperature response [34], and nitrate signaling [35]; Thus, indicating that H3K36 methylation functions in response to abiotic stress in plants. The histone methyltransferases SDG708, SDG724 and SDG725 are responsible for H3K36 methylation in rice, and loss of each protein causes a late-flowering phenotype [31, 36]. SDG725 is also involved in brassinosteroid-regulated pathways and position-specific intron retention [37, 38]. Transcriptome analysis indicates that numerous genes are abnormally expressed in SDG708-knockdown mutants, including many stress-responsive genes [39], which suggests that SDG708 may be involved in stress responses. Despite these advances, the role of H3K36 methylation in maize salt tolerance remains unexplored, warranting investigation into its species-specific regulatory mechanisms. Plants activate dynamic epigenetic reprogramming as a core adaptive strategy under salt stress. H3K36-specific histone methyltransferases regulate stress-responsive gene expression by modulating chromatin states; specifically, rice SDG708 enhances drought resistance via ABA biosynthesis [31], while tomato SDG mutants exhibit elevated multi-stress tolerance [40]. SDG102, the closest maize homolog of rice SDG708 and Arabidopsis SDG26 [41]. Notably, SDG102-deficient plants exhibit increased DNA damage following UVB irradiation [42], yet its functions in maize salt tolerance and development remain unreported due to the absence of mutants with discernible phenotypes.

Critically, given the robust crosstalk between histone modifications and DNA methylation—where H3K36me2/3 recruits DNMT3A or directs the RdDM pathway in plants [43, 44], and H3K36me2 deficiency causes genome-wide DNA hypomethylation in Arabidopsis [45]—this knowledge gap motivated our investigation. We therefore employed previously established transgenic lines overexpressing ZmSDG102. Physiological assays were conducted at the V3 stage under 150 mM NaCl treatment at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Omics profiling was conducted at the V7 stage under normal conditions. Data integration links physiological and molecular responses to assess constitutive priming and enhanced salt stress tolerance (Fig. 1 provides a schematic representation of the experimental design). Through integrated RNA-seq and whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) analyses, we demonstrate that ZmSDG102 modulates CHH methylation patterns, thereby altering transcriptional activity of key salt-responsive genes to enhance antioxidant defense and ion homeostasis. Consistent with these molecular findings, physiological assays revealed improved ROS scavenging capacity and enhanced membrane stability in overexpression lines, confirming its role in salt tolerance. Collectively, this study unveils a coordinated regulatory mechanism linking H3K36 methylation with DNA methylation reprogramming, identifies critical salt-responsive genes, and provides novel theoretical insights and molecular targets for improving maize salt tolerance.

Methods

Plant material

The seeds of the maize inbred line B73 used in this study were preserved in the Maize Seed Resource Library at Jilin Agricultural University. Wild-type B73 (WT) served as the control, and the experimental materials included the SDG102 overexpression line OE3 (T2 generation plant). Transgenic maize plants overexpressing SDG102 under the control of the constitutive 35S promoter were generated via pollen-mediated transformation, from which the OE3 line was selected for further investigation (Supplementary Material 3). Southern blot analysis confirmed that both overexpression lines contained only a single independent insertion of the transgene in the genome (Fig. S1). Detailed methods for the generation of the overexpression maize lines are provided in a prior publication [41].

Salt treatment and sampling at seedling stage

Maize plants were hydroponically cultivated in controlled growth chambers using 96-cell hydroponic boxes (127mm × 87 mm × 114 mm) containing half-strength Hoagland's nutrient solution. The growth conditions were set to: 28°C/22°C (day/night), a 14-h photoperiod with a light intensity of 600 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1, and 60% relative humidity. Salt stress treatment was initiated at the three-leaf (V3) stage. The treatment was applied by replacing the nutrient solution in the hydroponic system with 1 liter of half-strength Hoagland's solution containing 150 mM NaCl. Control plants received an equal volume of fresh half-strength Hoagland's solution without NaCl. Healthy leaf samples were collected at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours after treatment initiation for subsequent physiological measurements. The initial sampling (0 h) was conducted on 19 November 2024 at 10:30 (ZT4). The sampling site was the leaf tissue of the 2nd and 3rd healthy leaves from top to bottom of the plant (about 10 cm long, without midvein) leaf segments approximately 10 cm in length (excluding the midvein) were collected from the 2nd to 3rd fully expanded healthy leaves from the top of the plants. The fresh samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further analysis for salt tolerance traits.

Physiological indicator measurement

The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and the content of malondialdehyde (MDA) were determined in all 24 samples (covering four time points with three biological replicates each) using established methods. SOD activity was assayed by the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction method [46]. The reaction mixture consisted of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 130 mM methionine, 750 μM NBT, 100 μM EDTA-Na₂, and 20 μM riboflavin. After incubation under 4000 lux light for 30 min, the absorbance was measured at 560 nm. One unit (U) of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit 50% of NBT photoreduction. POD activity was determined according to the guaiacol method [47] by monitoring the increase in absorbance at 470 nm in a reaction system containing 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), 20 mM guaiacol, and 40 mM H₂O₂. CAT activity was measured by the ultraviolet absorption method [48], based on the decomposition of H₂O₂ at 240 nm in a mixture of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 15 mM H₂O₂. The MDA content, as an indicator of lipid peroxidation, was quantified using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction [49]. Briefly, the enzyme extract was mixed with a solution containing 0.6% TBA and 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), heated in a boiling water bath for 20 min, and then cooled and centrifuged. The absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 532 nm, 600 nm, and 450 nm. The MDA concentration was calculated using the formula: 6.45 × (A532 – A600) – 0.56 × A450. All assays were performed with three biological and three technical replicates. Enzyme activities were expressed in units per gram of fresh weight (U/g FW), and MDA content was expressed in μmol per gram of fresh weight (μmol/g FW).The dried and weighed samples (approximately 0.2 g) were digested with 5 mL of a mixed-acid solution (HNO₃:HClO₄ = 4:1, v/v, both GR grade) in PTFE digestion vessels at 175 °C for 3 hours until the solutions became clear. After cooling, the digests were diluted to 50 mL with ultrapure water and further diluted if necessary. The concentrations of Na⁺ and K⁺ were determined using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer with an air-acetylene flame [50, 51]. Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE) and were statistically analyzed by two-way ANOVA using R software.

RNA isolation and RNA-seq libraries preparation

To further investigate the function of SDG102, overexpression (OE) and wild-type (WT) plants at the V7 stage were selected for transcriptome analysis. Leaf samples were collected from V7-stage OE and WT plants under normal (non-stressed) growth conditions. Genetically modified materials for corn are provided by the laboratory [41]. The library was constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer's recommendations. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) with the S4 flow cel.

RNA-seq preprocessing

Following quality control and the removal of poor-quality reads, approximately 6 Gb of high-quality, 150-bp paired-end reads were generated per library. These clean reads were then aligned to the maize genome. The RNA-Seq clean reads were aligned to the maize genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCF_000005005.2/). A high degree of correlation between replicates (R > 0.95) suggested that high quality RNA-Seq data were obtained. In addition, data quality analysis was performed by visualizing the results of the principal component analysis (Fig. S2). In this study, we conducted alignment analysis of RNA-seq data from WT and OE maize samples, with three biological replicates set for each sample. Statistical results revealed that the total read counts for each sample ranged between 47.1 million and 52.6 million (Table S1), indicating a sufficient data volume to meet the requirements for subsequent analyses. The alignment rates of the wild-type samples exhibited high consistency, with WT-3 achieving the highest alignment rate of 96.95%. In contrast, the alignment rates of the overexpression samples were slightly lower, ranging from 93.98% to 94.73% (Table S2), yet remained within an acceptable range. Furthermore, we documented the total aligned read counts for each sample and provided the alignment rates for both Read1 and Read2. The results demonstrated that the paired-end read alignment consistency across all samples was high, ensuring the reliability of the data. To enhance the accuracy of the analytical outcomes, we excluded genes with excessively low expression levels in subsequent analyses to mitigate potential noise interference (Table S2).

RNA-Seq data analysis

Specifically, QuantSeq 3' mRNA-Seq reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.7.10a) [52]. Read counts for each gene were generated using featureCounts (v2.0.6) from the Subread package [53] based on the reference annotation file. Differential expression analysis was performed using the edgeR package (v3.40.2) [54] within the R environment (v4.3.2) using Bioconductor [55]. Genes with an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses were conducted using TBtools (v2.0) [56], and the results were visualized using R. The significance threshold for enrichment was set at FDR < 0.05.

Gene expression analysis

Fresh leaves from maize plants at the V3 stagewere harvested for total RNA extraction using an Easy Pure Plant RNA Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Transgen Biotech, Beijing, China). A total of 2 ug RNA was prepared for first-strand cDNA synthesis using Easy Script One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Transgen- Biotech). RT-qPCR was performed using the gene-specific primers listed in Supplementary Table 3. The housekeeping gene ZmACT1 (GRMZM2G126010) was used as an internal control to normalize the data. The data shown in the bar charts represent the mean values of three replicates.

WGBS sequencing

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) was performed using genomic DNA extracted from V7-stage OE and WT maize plants. Leaf samples were collected from V7-stage OE and WT plants under normal (non-stressed) growth conditions. Genomic DNA was isolated from frozen leaf tissues using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. DNA integrity and purity were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop spectrophotometry, respectively, and DNA concentration was accurately quantified using a Qubit fluorometer. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. DNA samples were fragmented by sonication to an insert size of 200–300 bp, followed by end repair, A-tailing, and ligation of methylated adapters. Bisulfite conversion was then carried out using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold™ Kit (Zymo Research, USA), during which unmethylated cytosines were converted to uracils, while methylated cytosines (5mC) remained protected. The resulting libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) to generate 150-bp paired-end reads, with a minimum sequencing depth of 15× per sample.

WGBS data analysis

The quality of the raw sequencing data was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.9), and Trim Galore (v0.6.7) was employed to remove low-quality reads and adapter sequences. High-quality reads were then aligned to the maize reference genome (B73_RefGen_v4; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCF_000005005.2/) using Bismark software (v0.23.0) [57]. Alignment parameters were set to allow a maximum of 2 mismatches, retaining only uniquely mapped reads with Q20 quality scores or higher (Table S4). Methylation sites (5mC) in CG, CHG, and CHH contexts were identified.

Differential methylation regions (DMRs) between OE and WT groups were detected using DSS software (v2.44.0) [58] with a smoothed functional approach (smoothing.span = 200 bp). The analysis was performed under the null hypothesis (μi1 = μi2, delta = 0) using a per-CpG significance threshold of p < 1e-05. A contiguous region was defined as a DMR if it met the following criteria: 1) contained a minimum of 3 significant CpG sites; 2) had a physical length ≥ 50 bp; and 3) contained more than 50% of CpG sites with p < 1e-05 within the region. Adjacent DMRs located within 100 bp (dis.merge = 100) were merged. The final set of high-confidence DMRs was further filtered using stringent statistical criteria: an effect size of |ΔmC| ≥ 0.20 and a significance threshold of FDR (q-value) < 0.05, and were required to be consistently detected in at least two biological replicates with an average sequencing coverage > 15×.

The identified DMRs were annotated using ANNOVAR [59], and their enrichment in promoter regions (defined as ±2 kb from transcription start sites) and gene body regions was analyzed. Functional enrichment analysis of DMR-related genes was performed using the GO [60] and KEGG [61] databases. All samples met strict quality control standards, including an alignment rate > 70% and coverage > 15×, ensuring data reliability and reproducibility (Fig. S3). This high-quality dataset provides a foundation for investigating how SDG102 overexpression influences salt tolerance-associated DNA methylation patterns (CG/CHG/CHH contexts) and their regulatory mechanisms in maize (Table S5).

Results

SDG102 overexpression enhances maize salt tolerance by activating antioxidant systems and reducing membrane damage

This study elucidates the dual physiological mechanisms by which SDG102 overexpression enhances maize salt tolerance through coordinated reinforcement of antioxidant defense and ion homeostasis. Under 150 mM NaCl stress for 72 hours, the OE exhibited significantly improved stress adaptation: reduced leaf wilting and higher maintained plant height and biomass compared to the wild type (WT) (Fig. 2a). Physiological assays revealed a comprehensive enhancement of antioxidant capacity in OE plants. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity reached 132.1 U/g FW at 72 h, representing a 21.3% increase over WT (108.8 U/g FW) (Fig. 2b). Meanwhile, peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) activities showed sustained increases, reaching 368.4 U/g FW and 763.1 ×103 U/g at 72 h, which were 14.3% and 10.0% higher than WT, respectively (Fig. 2c-d). This synergistic activation of antioxidant enzymes effectively scavenged salt-induced reactive oxygen species, thereby mitigating oxidative damage. Consistent with this, the malondialdehyde (MDA) content in OE plants was 44.6% lower than in WT at 72 h (15.3 vs. 27.6 μmol/g FW) (Fig. 2d), indicating significantly reduced membrane lipid peroxidation and better preservation of membrane integrity. Regarding ion homeostasis, OE plants demonstrated superior selective ion regulation, accumulating significantly less Na⁺ while maintaining higher K⁺ levels in leaves compared to WT throughout the stress period (Fig. 2f and g), thus maintaining a more favorable K⁺/Na⁺ ratio. In summary, SDG102 overexpression collectively confers enhanced salinity tolerance in maize by simultaneously strengthening the antioxidant system, alleviating membrane damage, and improving ion homeostasis.

Transcriptome analysis reveals key DEGs and pathways regulated by SDG102 in maize

To profile the constitutive transcriptional changes induced by SDG102 overexpression, we performed RNA-seq analysis on leaf tissues from V7-stage WT and OE plants grown under normal (non-stressed) conditions. RNA-Seq analysis revealed 614 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the overexpression (OE) and wild-type (WT) lines (Supplementary Material 3). Among these, 285 genes were significantly upregulated and 329 were significantly downregulated in the OEs (Fig. 3a-b). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed for enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) terms, including the terms from biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. The analysis indicated that the DEGs were significantly enriched in biological processes such as stress response, ion homeostasis, transporter activity, and response to oxidative stress (Fig. 3c). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated that the DEGs were notably enriched in pathways related to plant hormone signal transduction and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (Fig. 3d). The plant hormone signal transduction pathway plays a critical role in plant responses to abiotic stresses, such as salt stress, while the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites is closely associated with antioxidant defense and stress adaptation. These results suggest that SDG102 overexpression may influence key pathways by regulating genes involved in cellular ion balance and stress signaling.

To further elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of SDG102 overexpression on maize salt tolerance, we screened 30 genes with the highest expression levels related to salt stress from DEGs (Fig. 3e and Table S6). Cluster analysis showed that 13 of these genes were significantly upregulated, while 17 were significantly downregulated. The upregulated genes included several involved in antioxidant defense, whereas the downregulated genes encompassed some related to ion transport. These genes may serve as key candidates influencing maize salt tolerance under SDG102 overexpression. In summary, SDG102 overexpression significantly altered the gene expression profile of maize, particularly exhibiting notable regulatory effects on pathways related to stress response and ion homeostasis. These findings provide a crucial theoretical foundation for further deciphering the molecular mechanisms of SDG102 mediated salt tolerance in maize.

Epigenetic regulation of SDG102: genome-wide DNA methylation and pathway enrichment analysis

To determine the intrinsic impact of SDG102 on the methylome, we conducted whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) on V7-stage plants under control conditions. We identified 45,817 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) between OE and WT maize plants, using a threshold of p* < 1 × 10⁻5 and requiring a minimum of 3 significant cytosines over a 50-bp length. Genome-wide methylation analysis identified a total of 45,817 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) distributed across various genomic functional domains (Fig. 4a). Although a substantial number of DMRs were also located in repetitive sequences and gene bodies, we focused subsequent analysis on the 5,896 DMRs residing in promoter regions (defined as ±2 kb from transcription start sites). This focus was guided by the established biological principle that promoter methylation exerts the most direct and pronounced impact on transcriptional regulation [62, 63]. These promoter-associated DMRs anchored 4,195 genes (Fig. 4c), demonstrating that SDG102 overexpression significantly reshaped the epigenetic landscape within gene promoters. Among these, 5,896 DMRs were situated in promoter regions (defined as ±2 kb from transcription start sites, TSSs) (Fig. 4a, Table S7). This set included 3,608 hypermethylated DMRs (1,476 in CG, 1,582 in CHG, 550 in CHH contexts) and 2,280 hypomethylated DMRs (733 CG, 475 CHG, 1,080 CHH). Adjacent DMRs within 100 bp were merged, and only regions with >50% of cytosines meeting the significance threshold were retained. These promoter-associated DMRs anchored a total of 4,195 genes (Fig. 4c), demonstrating that SDG102 overexpression significantly altered the methylation landscape within gene promoter regions. Although there are no significant differences between the three methylation contexts on different genomic features (Fig. 4b), the three sequence contexts (CG, CHG, CHH), DMRs that only occur in the CHH state have the most genes anchored (Fig. 4c), indicating a potentially key role for CHH methylation in SDG102-mediated epigenetic regulation. Furthermore, the number of transposable elements (TEs) associated with DMRs substantially exceeded the number of genes (Fig. 4d), suggesting that SDG102 overexpression might enhance maize salt tolerance, at least in part, by modulating TE activity, thereby influencing genome stability and gene expression.

Functional enrichment analyses of promoter CHH-DMR-associated genes revealed their central roles in salt stress adaptation (Fig. 4e-f). Genes modulated by SDG102 overexpression are enriched in ion transport, glycosylation processes, DNA/RNA-related functions, and key cellular structures—collectively supporting cellular integrity and stress adaptation under salinity. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that SDG102-regulated genes are significantly involved in biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (including flavonoids), starch and sucrose metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, and lipid-related pathways (such as linoleic and α-linolenic acid metabolism). These pathways collectively contribute to osmotic adjustment, antioxidant capacity, and stress signaling under salt stress.

The correlation between DMRs and DEGs reveals the salt tolerance mechanism mediated by SDG102

Genome-wide DNA methylation analysis revealed that the DMR-associated genes were significantly enriched within interconnected pathways that have established roles in salt stress response (Fig. 4e-f). Integrated analysis of OE versus WT groups identified 279 genes exhibiting concurrent promoter DMRs and DEGs (Fig. 5a). Among the anchored hypermethylated genes, 144 were low expressed and 49 were highly expressed. Conversely, among the hypomethylated genes, 79 showed low expression while 69 exhibited high expression (Fig. S4), suggesting that epigenetic reprogramming may play a role in the SDG102-mediated salt tolerance mechanism. By integrating methylome and transcriptome data, CHH methylation (non-CG sites) exhibited the strongest association with DEGs (Fig. 5b-d and Table S8).

Validation of salt stress-responsive candidate genes activated by SDG102 overexpression

This study revealed through RT-qPCR analysis that multiple salt stress-responsive genes were significantly upregulated in OE (Fig. 7). Six newly identified core genes include: ZmUFG27 (Zm00001d014792), encoding an ethylene signaling core regulator; vacuolar membrane-localized Na+/H+ antiporter genes ZmNHX16 (Zm00001d026118) and ZmNHX17 (Zm00001d028330); potassium transport-related genes ZmHAK24 (Zm00001d037305) and ZmHAK8 (Zm00001d029072); and molecular chaperone-encoding gene ZmHSP28 (Zm00001d018298) (Fig. 7a-f). Functional annotation indicated these genes participate in ethylene signal transduction, ion compartmentalization, potassium homeostasis maintenance, and protein folding protection, likely enhancing salt tolerance by synergistically regulating redox balance, ion homeostasis, and signaling pathways.

Notably, four previously reported salt stress-positive regulatory genes also showed significant upregulation in OE lines (p < 0.05). The auxin signaling component ZmIAA9(Zm00001d013399) exhibited a 7.6-fold increase (Fig. 7g), potentially mediating cell elongation under salt stress; the bHLH transcription factor ZmbHLH32(Zm00001d043515) was upregulated 3.6-fold (Fig. 7h), likely activating downstream stress-responsive genes; the antioxidant enzyme ZmCAT1(Zm00001d033241) displayed a 5.5-fold enhancement (Fig. 7i), confirming its pivotal role in ROS scavenging; and the vacuolar chloride channel ZmCLCg(Zm00001d002091) increased 5.3-fold (Fig. 7j), suggesting its critical function in ion balance. These expression patterns align with prior studies [64].

Molecular findings corroborated phenotypic observations: OE lines exhibited superior salt tolerance under 150 mM NaCl treatment, with a 43% reduction in MDA content at 72 hours (Fig. 2k), directly linked to enhanced ion transporter gene expression and reinforced antioxidant systems. The coordinated upregulation of ZmNHX16/17 and ZmCLCg likely improved vacuolar Na+ and Cl- sequestration, maintaining cytosolic ion homeostasis. These results systematically elucidate the molecular mechanism by which SDG102 overexpression enhances salt tolerance through reconstructing gene regulatory networks, providing novel targets for crop stress resistance improvement.

Discussion

An important aspect of our experimental design warrants discussion. While the physiological characterization of salt tolerance was conducted at the V3 seedling stage under acute salt stress, the transcriptomic and methylomic profiling were performed on V7-stage plants under non-stressed conditions. This design precludes a direct, stage-matched correlation between the omics changes and the immediate stress response. Instead, our data reveal the pre-existing, constitutive molecular landscape shaped by SDG102 overexpression. The significant alterations in gene expression and, most notably, in CHH methylation observed in OE plants even in the absence of stress, suggest that SDG102 acts as an epigenetic primer, establishing a genomic environment that is predisposed for a more robust activation of stress-responsive pathways, such as antioxidant defense and ion homeostasis, when challenge arises. This concept of 'epigenetic priming' for abiotic stress tolerance is emerging in plant biology, and our findings position SDG102 as a key regulator in this process in maize.

ZmSDG102 overexpression enhances maize salt tolerance by epigenetically activating a coordinated defense network that integrates antioxidant response and ion homeostasis. This multifaceted enhancement is evidenced not only by the synergistically elevated activities of SOD (21.3%), POD (14.3%), and CAT (10.0%)—which collectively enhance ROS scavenging—and a marked 44.6% reduction in MDA content to mitigate oxidative damage, but also by a significant improvement in ion selectivity. The OE plants exhibited a superior ability to restrict toxic Na⁺ accumulation while maintaining higher essential K⁺ levels in leaves, thereby preserving a more favorable K⁺/Na⁺ ratio, a hallmark of cellular ionic balance under salinity. The temporal shift in antioxidant enzyme activities—where SOD activity declined after an initial peak while POD and CAT activities continued to rise—reflects a sophisticated, coordinated reprogramming of the defense system under prolonged stress, likely facilitated by SDG102. This dynamic transition from superoxide dismutation (SOD) to hydrogen peroxide detoxification (POD/CAT) is crucial for preventing cytotoxic ROS accumulation [65, 66]. Notably, the concurrent improvement in ion homeostasis and antioxidant capacity suggests a potential link: reduced Na⁺ influx and better K⁺ retention may alleviate ion-induced oxidative stress, while a robust antioxidant system may protect ion transporters from oxidative inactivation, creating a positive feedback loop that reinforces overall tolerance. This conserved, multi-layered regulatory mechanism, observed also in wheat [67] and grapevine [68], underscores the cross-species potential of epigenetic regulators like SDG102 in enhancing stress resilience through the integrated modulation of metabolic and transport pathways.

This study proposes that the H3K36 methyltransferase SDG102 may enhance maize salt tolerance through synergistic interactions between epigenetic and transcriptional regulation, further advancing our understanding of plant stress-responsive gene regulation. The results validate the evolutionary conservation of H3K36 methylation in plant stress adaptation and reveal its species-specific roles in maize salt tolerance. Specifically, H3K36 methylation exhibits high evolutionary conservation across plants, particularly in species such as rice and tomato, where methylation status directly influences stress resistance. For instance, rice H3K36 methylation regulates ABA biosynthesis to improve drought tolerance [31], while imbalance of H3K4/H3K36 methylation in tomato compromises stress resistance [40], collectively indicating the pivotal regulatory role of H3K36 methylation in plant stress responses.

In this study, overexpression SDG102 significantly upregulated the expression of ion transporter genes ZmNHX16/17 and molecular chaperone gene ZmHSP28 in maize, suggesting that H3K36 methylation may enhance salt tolerance through stabilizing protein folding and ion compartmentalization. Furthermore, dynamic CHH methylation changes played critical roles in SDG102-mediated regulation, mirroring the salt stress-induced CHH hypomethylation observed in rice [69]. This plasticity of CHH methylation implies its universal importance in crop stress adaptation, reinforcing the central role of epigenetics in plant stress resistance.

A notable finding lies in elucidating CHH methylation's dual functions in chromatin remodeling and genome stability maintenance. Unlike the CG methylation-dominated genic regulation in Arabidopsis [31, 70, 71], we hypothesize that SDG102 in maize might balance these epigenetic modalities. It is important to note, however, a limitation of our experimental design. The physiological data from the V3 stage primarily reflect the short-term response to salt stress, and this study does not fully elucidate the role of SDG102 in mediating longer-term adaptation mechanisms at the V7 stage under sustained salinity.

Additional investigations reveal cross-regulation between epigenetic modifications and hormone signaling pathways. Under SDG102 overexpression conditions, potential interactions between hormone signaling and epigenetic regulation appear to significantly amplify maize salt stress responses, particularly evidenced by the coordinated upregulation of ZmbHLH32 and ZmIAA9 [64].

This study elucidates the potential molecular mechanisms by which SDG102 regulates maize salt tolerance through H3K36me and CHH methylation, providing novel perspectives for crop improvement. The findings indicate that SDG102 could be applied in crop engineering through epigenetic editing and multi-gene coordination strategies, particularly showing significant potential in maize salt-tolerant breeding due to its close association with salt tolerance-related gene expression and functional regulation. Furthermore, SDG102-mediated salt tolerance mechanisms might exhibit applicability to other crops, offering new directions for stress tolerance enhancement. However, further exploration is required to clarify how SDG102 specifically regulates stress-related gene expression and interacts with transcription factors. Future studies should employ diverse experimental approaches to validate these interactions and decipher their synergistic mechanisms. Collectively, this work not only enriches the theoretical framework of plant salt tolerance regulation but also proposes innovative strategies and targets for crop salt-tolerant breeding. Subsequent research will focus on unraveling the SDG102-transcription factor interaction network to comprehensively elucidate its regulatory mechanisms in plant salt tolerance.

Conclusions

This study establishes ZmSDG102 as a pivotal epigenetic regulator enhancing maize salt tolerance through multi-layered molecular reprogramming. Key findings demonstrate that SDG102 overexpression: (1) bolsters integrated antioxidant defenses, elevating SOD, POD, and CAT activities by 21.3%, 14.3%, and 10.0% respectively while reducing MDA accumulation by 44.6%, thereby mitigating oxidative damage; and concurrently improves ion homeostasis by significantly restricting Na⁺ accumulation and maintaining higher leaf K⁺ levels under salt stress, resulting in a more favorable K⁺/Na⁺ ratio.(2) rewires transcriptional networks, with 614 DEGs enriched in ion homeostasis (ZmNHX16/17, ZmHAK8/24), stress signaling (ZmIAA9, ZmbHLH32), and ROS detoxification (ZmCAT1, ZmHSP28); (3) orchestrates epigenetic remodeling, driving 45,817 DMRs—notably promoter-associated CHH hypomethylation. Reveals a DNA methylation where CHH methylation serves as the core layer governing chromatin accessibility and salt-responsive gene expression.

These mechanisms collectively enhance membrane integrity, ion sequestration, and ROS scavenging under salt stress. Our integrated epigenomic-transcriptomic analysis identifies six core genes (ZmUFG27, ZmNHX16/17, ZmHAK8/24, ZmHSP28) as prime targets for breeding salt-resilient maize. Future work should prioritize in planta validation of CHH methylation-engineered lines to advance epigenetic crop improvement.
