Clinical subtyping of severe acute pancreatitis reveals heterogeneous associations with early management strategies: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jianhua Wan, Yaoyu Zou, Maobin Kuang, Shixuan Xiong, Qiaofeng Chen, Huajing Ke, Wenhua He, Yin Zhu, Nonghua Lu, Liang Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground The clinical prognosis of severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) varies significantly, and traditional scoring systems fail to reveal its intrinsic heterogeneity.Methods This multicenter retrospective cohort study enrolled SAP patients from a Chinese Jiangxi cohort (n = 3,022) and the US MIMIC-IV cohort (n = 840). Consensus K-means clustering was applied to the derivation cohort to identify clinical subphenotypes, which were externally validated in the independent cohort. An XGBoost classifier was constructed based on selected variables and its performance was evaluated. Multivariable Cox regression models were used to analyze the association of each subphenotype with mortality and to assess heterogeneous associations with three key early interventions: early fluid intake, low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) anticoagulation, and early enteral nutrition.Results Cluster analysis consistently identified three stable and reproducible SAP clinical subphenotypes: K1 (Stable type, n = 1,446), characterized by younger age, stable hemodynamics and renal function, and good nutritional status; K2 (Renal impairment-dominant type, n = 317), defined by severe renal insufficiency, hypoalbuminemia, and lower mean arterial pressure; and K3 (Elderly/Hepatic impairment type, n = 1,259), featuring the oldest age and significantly elevated liver injury markers. The K2 subphenotype had the highest in-hospital mortality (44.8%), significantly greater than that of K1 (8.3%) and K3 (14.9%) (p < 0.001). Multivariable Cox regression showed that, using K1 as reference, the K2 subphenotype was independently associated with an increased risk of 28-day mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 6.70, 95% CI 4.94–9.08). The constructed XGBoost subphenotype classifier demonstrated excellent discriminative performance in the training, test, and external validation sets (macro-average AUCs: 0.997, 0.994, and 0.958, respectively). Analysis of heterogeneous associations revealed that fluid overload (total intake ≥ 2.5 L within the first days) was significantly associated with increased mortality specifically in the K3 subphenotype. LMWH therapy showed a clear survival benefit only in the K2 subphenotype (aHR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.45–0.96, p = 0.031).Conclusion This study identified three SAP subphenotypes with significantly heterogeneous clinical features and prognoses based on early routine indicators and developed a high‑performance classifier for bedside use. The distinct associations with fluid administration and anticoagulation across subphenotypes suggest that phenotype‑based strategies may optimize the clinical management of SAP, although these findings are hypothesis‑generating and require prospective validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-05976-0.

Background

The clinical management of severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) remains a formidable challenge in critical care and gastroenterology. Despite advancements in supportive care, its in-hospital mortality rate remains as high as 30%, and survivors often face a protracted recovery, imposing a heavy burden on healthcare systems [1–4]. Traditional risk assessment tools like the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) score, while effective for population-level prediction, compress a series of physiological abnormalities into a single state, failing to reveal the underlying heterogeneous pathophysiology [2, 5]. This simplified model may obscure significant differences in treatment associations among patients with different intrinsic phenotypes, limiting the precise optimization of therapy. In practice, patients with similar initial severity scores often exhibit vastly divergent clinical courses and outcomes, strongly suggesting the existence of unrecognized patient subgroups with distinct biological and clinical characteristics.

Driven by the precision medicine concept, disease subtype analysis using large-scale clinical data has opened new avenues for understanding complex disease heterogeneity [6]. This unsupervised learning approach can identify homogeneous patient clusters from real-world data without pre-set assumptions and has achieved breakthroughs in fields like sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome, revealing biological subphenotypes and distinct treatment association patterns [7–11]. However, such in-depth phenotypic studies in SAP remain scarce [12, 13]. Current guidelines primarily offer broad therapeutic frameworks based on etiology and organ failure, lacking individualized recommendations tailored to different phenotypes [2, 3]. Key treatment measures—such as early fluid resuscitation, the timing and beneficiaries of anticoagulant therapy, and nutritional support strategies—remain controversial, which may partly stem from insufficient understanding of heterogeneity across subgroups [14–18].

Therefore, this study integrated data from a large Chinese single-center cohort and an international U.S. critical care database. Using a consensus clustering algorithm, we identified and validated data-driven clinical subphenotypes in an unsupervised manner. We then constructed and validated a classifier that may facilitate rapid subtype identification. Finally, we thoroughly explored differences in the associations of key interventions—including fluid intake, anticoagulant therapy, and early enteral nutrition—among subphenotypes, aiming to provide hypothesis‑generating evidence for phenotype-based individualized treatment strategies. By integrating two independent cohorts, this study hopes to reveal the intrinsic heterogeneous characteristics of SAP and explore their application in clinical management.

Methods

Data sources and study population

This study analyzed data from two independent cohorts: a Chinese cohort from Jiangxi Province and the United States-based MIMIC-IV cohort. Data for the Jiangxi cohort were extracted from the electronic medical records of SAP patients admitted to the Department of Gastroenterology at The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between 2012 and 2024. Data for the U.S. cohort were sourced from this publicly available critical care database (MIMIC-IV) [19]. The authors completed the required training (Cooperative Institution Training Program No. 12601935) and signed data use agreements for restricted database access. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No.: 2011001) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and STROBE reporting guidelines. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective use of anonymized data [20, 21].

The study population comprised adult patients with SAP admitted to the intensive care unit, diagnosed according to the 2012 Revised Atlanta Classification [22]. Operational definitions: Acute pancreatitis was defined by the presence of at least two of the following: abdominal pain consistent with AP, serum amylase/lipase ≥ 3 times the upper limit of normal, and/or imaging findings characteristic of AP. Severity was classified as severe if persistent organ failure (> 48 h) was present. Organ failure was defined as: Respiratory failure: PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio < 300 mmHg or the use of mechanical ventilation. Cardiovascular failure: Systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg despite adequate fluid resuscitation, or the need for vasopressor support. Renal failure: Serum creatinine ≥ 171 μmol/L or the initiation of renal replacement therapy. These definitions were extracted from structured EHR data (laboratory results, vital signs, ventilation/flowsheet records, medication administration). Exclusion criteria were age under 18 years, pregnancy, pre-existing end-stage liver or kidney disease, and cases with extensive missing data that precluded analysis. Extensive missing data was defined a priori as cases where more than 30% of the key variables selected for analysis (including demographics, vital signs, and the six core laboratory variables for clustering) were missing, or where the primary outcome (in-hospital mortality) was not recorded. The patient screening process is detailed in Figure S1.

Data collection and preprocessing

Baseline data were collected from the first complete 24‑hour period following ICU admission. For each variable, the first recorded value within this window was used. Collected variables included demographic information, vital signs, laboratory test results (complete blood count, liver and kidney function, electrolytes, etc.), and comorbidities. Variables with over 30% missing data were excluded from analysis. For remaining variables, missing values were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations. The proportion of missing data for each variable and comparisons of distributions before and after imputation are provided in Tables S1–2 and Figures S2–3. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plots (Tables S1–2, Figures S4–5). Early fluid administration was quantified using the total volume of intravenous fluids (including crystalloids, colloids, and blood products) recorded in the electronic health record during the first 3 ICU days. Fluid intake was defined as the sum of all intravenous fluids administered. Urine output was recorded daily. Fluid balance was calculated as total intake minus total output.

Feature selection for subtyping

To identify key features for constructing subphenotypes, we first applied the Boruta algorithm [23]—a random forest-based feature selection method—to each cohort independently to identify all features relevant to patient status (Figure S6A). We then selected the top 10 most important features from each cohort. To assess multicollinearity, we calculated the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Spearman’s correlation coefficients. After removing variables with high multicollinearity or high correlation, six representative variables were retained for subsequent analysis: Creatinine (Cr), Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN), Age, Hemoglobin (Hb), Mean Blood Pressure (MBP), and Hematocrit (HCT) (Figure S6B–D).

Identification and validation of clinical subphenotypes

We applied the consensus K-means clustering algorithm to the derivation (Jiangxi) cohort using the six selected variables to identify clinical subphenotypes. The optimal number of clusters was determined by comprehensively evaluating the silhouette coefficient, the elbow method (within-cluster sum of squares), and cluster stability (Tables S3–4, Figures S6E, S7–8). To validate the robustness and generalizability of the subphenotypes, we applied the same variables and clustering method to the external U.S. validation cohort (Figures S6–7, 9). Additionally, we conducted sensitivity analysis using Gaussian Mixture Models, a probabilistic clustering approach, on both cohorts to assess the stability of the subtype structure (Figures S10–11). Clinical characteristics are presented as median (interquartile range) or frequency (%). Group comparisons employed the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Construction and evaluation of the subtype classifier

To develop a clinically applicable subtype identification tool, we trained an eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) classifier using 6 selected variables [24]. The XGBoost classifier was developed as a clinically applicable tool to reliably reproduce the previously defined k-means subphenotype labels. Its value lies in robustly being easily integrated into EHR systems. The XGBoost classifier was developed exclusively in the Jiangxi cohort. This cohort was randomly split into a training set (70%) and an internal test set (30%). Nested cross‑validation was employed: an outer fivefold loop for unbiased performance evaluation and an inner fivefold loop for hyperparameter tuning. Model performance was evaluated in the test set and independently validated in the US cohort. We assessed performance using accuracy, precision, recall, F1‑score, area under the ROC curve (AUC), calibration curves, and Brier score. The contribution of individual features to model predictions was interpreted using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) value analysis. To enhance the usability and accessibility of the model, the final version was deployed as a web-based interactive Shiny application [25].

Outcome assessment and sensitivity analyses

The primary outcome was all-cause in-hospital mortality. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the Log-rank test. When the proportional hazards assumption was violated, landmark analysis compared differences in all-cause mortality within 28 and 56 days of admission. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to calculate adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) with 95% confidence intervals, evaluating the independent association of each subtype with mortality risk. Models were adjusted for smoking, alcohol use, history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, pancreatitis etiology, and the use of albumin, low molecular weight heparin (LMWH), and insulin therapy. To assess incremental prognostic value, we also adjusted for APACHE II score (calculated within 24 h) and compared the prognostic performance of the subphenotypes with that of APACHE II. Subgroup analyses stratified by APACHE II score (< 8, ≥ 8; < 16, ≥ 16) were performed. To verify robustness, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses: restricting to patients enrolled in recent years; including only patients admitted within 3 days of symptom onset; excluding patients with recurrent pancreatitis; excluding patients with comorbid hypertension and diabetes; and reassessing subtype-prognosis associations after applying propensity score weighting methods (inverse probability, overlap, matching, and entropy weighting) to balance baseline differences.

Analysis of heterogeneous associations with early management strategies

We analyzed heterogeneous associations with three key interventions: early fluid intake, LMWH anticoagulation, and early enteral nutrition (EEN, started within one week). Time zero was defined as the time of ICU admission. Early Fluid Intake was the total volume of all intravenous fluids (crystalloids, colloids, and blood products) administered within the first 24 h of ICU admission. Patients were then categorized into two groups based on this 24-h volume: ≥ 2.5 L and < 2.5 L. This 2.5L threshold was empirically derived from the restricted cubic spline analysis. LMWH Anticoagulation was defined as the administration of any prophylactic or therapeutic dose of LMWH within the first 72 h of ICU admission. Patients who received LMWH for the first time after this 72-h window were classified in the non-exposed group for the primary analysis. Patients with clear contraindications to anticoagulation (active bleeding, coagulopathy with INR > 2.0, platelet count < 50 × 10⁹/L, or recent major surgery) documented within the first 24 h were excluded from this specific analysis to mitigate confounding by indication. EEN was defined as the initiation of enteral nutrition (via nasogastric or nasojejunal tube) within 7 days of ICU admission. Patients who never received EN or received it after 7 days served as the comparator group. Patients with absolute contraindications to enteral feeding, such as mechanical intestinal obstruction, active gastrointestinal bleeding, mesenteric ischemia, or those requiring emergency abdominal surgery within the first 24 h, were excluded from this specific analysis.

For each subtype, we used multivariable-adjusted Cox models to assess the association of each intervention on in-hospital mortality. To address confounding by indication, we performed propensity score matching and weighting within each subphenotype separately. 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching (caliper = 0.2 SD of logit PS, without replacement) was used. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used as a sensitivity analysis. Balance diagnostics, including standardized mean differences (SMD, with < 0.1 as balance threshold), and Love plots, were assessed and are reported in the Supplementary Materials. Heterogeneity was formally tested by including a subphenotype‑by‑exposure interaction term in Cox models. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for admission body weight were performed to account for body size. For continuous variables (e.g., fluid intake), we employed restricted cubic splines to model nonlinear relationships with mortality risk and visualize risk curves across subphenotypes. To mitigate reverse causality, we conducted a landmark analysis.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2) and Free Statistics software (version 2.3). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Full reproducible code is available at GitHub (https://github.com/xiongsx123-art/AP_subphenotypes_R). The overall analytical workflow is shown in Fig. 1.

Results

Patients and study cohorts

This study included 3,862 patients with SAP: 3,022 from the Jiangxi cohort (China) and 840 from the MIMIC-IV cohort (U.S. cohort) (Fig. 1, Figure S12). Baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The median age was 51.0 years in the Jiangxi cohort (61.3% male), compared to 57.0 years in the older MIMIC cohort, which had significantly higher rates of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes. In-hospital mortality was 14.9% and 10.6%, respectively.

Characteristics of SAP subphenotypes

Subtype K1 (Stable type, n = 1,446): The youngest patients (median age 40.0 years), with relatively stable hemodynamics (higher mean arterial pressure), preserved renal function (lowest creatinine and blood urea nitrogen), and the best nutritional and hematological markers (highest hemoglobin, hematocrit, and albumin).

Subtype K2 (Renal impairment-dominant type, n = 317): Characterized predominantly by severe renal insufficiency, with markedly elevated creatinine (median 354.5 μmol/L) and blood urea nitrogen (median 20.4 mmol/L). This group also exhibited hypoalbuminemia (median albumin 30.8 g/L) and lower mean arterial pressure (median 94.0 mmHg).

Subtype K3 (Elderly, Hepatic impairment type, n = 1,259): The oldest patients (median age 66.0 years), with more pronounced hepatic injury (highest alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and total bilirubin levels).

Applying the same clustering variables to the MIMIC validation cohort reproduced three subphenotypes with highly consistent characteristic patterns (Fig. 2, Table S6). Sensitivity analysis using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) yielded corresponding subphenotypes (G1, G2, G3), demonstrating robustness through strong agreement in patient distribution, core features (e.g., high creatinine/blood urea nitrogen in K2/G2), and prognostic trends (Figure S14, Tables S7–8). Robustness across alternative feature sets was assessed by repeating clustering with expanded variable sets; results showed moderate to high agreement with the original subphenotypes (Table S9, Figure S15) and consistent mortality gradients in landmark analyses (Figure S16).

Clinical outcomes of different subphenotypes

In the Jiangxi cohort, the K2 subtype had the worst prognosis, with a significantly higher in-hospital mortality rate (44.8%) than the K1 (8.3%) and K3 subphenotypes (14.9%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A, Table S5). Established high-risk factors such as advanced age and poor renal function were reflected in each subtype’s characteristics (Fig. 3B, C). Landmark analysis at 28 and 56 days confirmed the K2 subtype had the lowest survival probability (p < 0.05). In multivariate Cox regression, using K1 as reference, the K2 subtype was independently associated with increased risk of 28-day mortality (adjusted Hazard Ratio [aHR] 6.70, 95% CI 4.94–9.08) and 56-day mortality (aHR 5.70, 95% CI 3.88–6.57). The K3 subtype also carried a higher mortality risk than K1 (28-day aHR 2.08, 56-day aHR 1.72) (Fig. 3D). When directly compared with the APACHE II score, the K‑means‑derived subphenotypes exhibited stronger associations with 28‑day and 56‑day mortality, as reflected by higher adjusted hazard ratios (Table S10). Subgroup analyses stratified by APACHE II (< 8, ≥ 8; < 16, ≥ 16) showed that the increased mortality risk of K2 and K3 persisted across severity strata, with no significant interaction (p for interaction > 0.1; Tables S11–12).These prognostic patterns were replicated in the MIMIC validation cohort and using GMM clustering (Fig. 3, Figures S17–19). A series of sensitivity analyses—restricting by enrollment year, time from onset, excluding recurrent cases or patients with hypertension/diabetes, adjustment for APACHE II score and applying multiple propensity score methods—did not alter the core association between subphenotypes and adverse prognosis (all p < 0.01) (Figures S20–22, Table S13).

To explore whether the subphenotypes reflect dynamic physiological changes, we performed latent class trajectory analysis of serum creatinine levels during the first three ICU days. Three distinct creatinine trajectories were identified (Figure S15F, Table S14): a persistently low trajectory (57.3% of patients), a moderately increasing trajectory (25.6%), and a persistently high trajectory (17.1%). Patients in the persistently high trajectory had the highest in-hospital mortality (43.8%), followed by the moderately increasing (17.9%) and persistently low (4.9%) trajectories (log-rank p < 0.001). The trajectory assignments showed moderate agreement with the baseline subphenotypes: 87% of patients in the K2 (renal impairment) subphenotype belonged to the persistently high creatinine trajectory (Figure S16F, Table S15).

Construction and evaluation of the SAP subtype classifier

Using 6 selected variables, we constructed an XGBoost model for rapid SAP subtype identification. The model demonstrated excellent performance in the training set, test set, and independent MIMIC validation set (Tables S9–10), with macro-average AUCs of 0.997 (training), 0.994 (test), and 0.958 (U.S. cohort) (Fig. 4, Tables S16–17). Brier scores of 0.05, 0.08, and 0.33, respectively, and well‑calibrated predictions (Figure S23). SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis identified key classification drivers: creatinine and blood urea nitrogen were most important for distinguishing the K2 subtype; age, hemoglobin, and hematocrit were core features differentiating K1 and K3 (Fig. 4A–E). Heterogeneous associations with key treatment measures across subphenotypes

Subphenotypes exhibited distinct differences in urine output and fluid infusion volume during the first 3 days after admission. A key observation was that urine output plateaued once the infusion volume exceeded approximately 3000 ml (Fig. 5A–E). Restricted cubic spline analysis identified a non‑linear increase in mortality risk when first day fluid intake exceeded approximately 2.5 L (Figure S24). Using a threshold of 2.5 L, adjusted Cox regression indicated that high fluid intake was associated with increased mortality only in the K3 subtype (aHR 1.73, 95% CI 1.29–2.33, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5G). After propensity score weighting within each subphenotype, the association remained significant in K3 (aHR 1.65, 95% CI 1.23–2.21) but not in K1 or K2 (Figure S25, Table S18). Adjusted Cox regression indicated that high fluid overload was most harmful in the K3 subtype (Fig. 5G). Sensitivity analyses adjusting for admission body weight and landmark analysis within the first 48 h yielded consistent results (Figure S26, Table S19). Regarding anticoagulation, the use of LMWH was associated with a significantly reduced risk of in-hospital mortality specifically in the K2 subtype (aHR 0.66, 95% CI 0.45–0.96, p = 0.031), but not in the K1 or K3 subphenotypes (Fig. 5H). In contrast, early enteral nutrition (EEN) showed a consistent trend toward improved survival across all three subphenotypes (all p < 0.001) (Fig. 5I). These heterogeneous association patterns were also observed in the GMM classification (Figure S27).

Discussion

In this study, we employed two unsupervised methods across two independent cohorts to identify and validate three highly heterogeneous, data-driven clinical subphenotypes of SAP using routine early-admission indicators. These subphenotypes differ markedly in baseline characteristics and prognosis. We developed an easy-to-use classifier to facilitate their timely clinical application. Crucially, they exhibit significantly different association with key treatment strategies.

The identified subphenotypes represent three distinct clinical profiles within the SAP spectrum: a stable type, a renal impairment-driven type, and an elderly/hepatic impairment type. Their significance lies in how they each correspond to a dominant aspect of SAP’s complex pathophysiology. The K2 subtype, characterized by severe early renal insufficiency, hypoalbuminemia, and lower mean arterial pressure, had an in-hospital mortality rate of 44.8%, far exceeding the others. This profile reflects a critical state of severe systemic inflammation, capillary leakage, and potential renal hypoperfusion, which may explain its relative insensitivity to fluid intake and potential benefit from interventions targeting microcirculatory dysfunction and hypercoagulability [13, 26, 27]. In contrast, The K3 subtype, defined by advanced age and, as a post-hoc descriptive enrichment, was also characterized by significantly elevated liver injury markers, suggesting a higher prevalence of biliary etiology. Its higher prevalence of biliary etiology suggests an important role for biliary obstruction and secondary infection. This group’s vulnerability to fluid overload underscores the challenge of maintaining homeostasis amidst declining organ reserve and hepatic dysfunction [28, 29]. The K1 subtype comprises younger patients with better organ reserve, whose favorable prognosis suggests treatment could avoid excessive aggressiveness, potentially reducing iatrogenic complications.

To facilitate rapid, objective subtype identification in clinical practice, we developed and externally validated an XGBoost-based classifier [24, 30]. This tool translates our machine learning framework into a potential bedside decision-support system. Its preserved discriminative performance in the independent validation set supports the technical feasibility of future real-time patient classification, moving the concept closer to clinical implementation. This represents a key paradigmatic shift from traditional scoring systems: while tools like APACHE II estimate a probability of risk, our classification distinguishes disease phenotypes, which are directly tied to distinct pathological mechanisms and treatment associations.

The clinical significance of these subphenotypes is underscored by their heterogeneous associations with core therapies. For the K3 subtype, overly aggressive fluid resuscitation was significantly associated with increased mortality [29, 31, 32]. This aligns with the group’s characteristics—older age, hepatic insufficiency, and likely diminished cardiorenal reserve—arguing for more refined, conservative fluid intake to balance resuscitation against volume overload [33]. In contrast, for the high-mortality K2 (renal impairment) subtype, LMWH use was associated with a significant survival advantage. This suggests underrecognized microcirculatory thrombosis or hypercoagulability may be a key driver of organ injury in these patients, and that suggesting a potential role for microcirculatory improvement, though this is hypothesis-generating [34, 35]. This finding offers a targeted rationale for anticoagulant therapy in specific SAP subpopulations. Finally, early enteral nutrition demonstrated a consistent survival benefit across all subphenotypes, reaffirming its role as a cornerstone therapy for severe pancreatitis [17, 36]. It is crucial to reiterate that the following findings regarding fluid, LMWH, and EEN represent exploratory observational associations.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective observational study, we cannot establish causality. Despite using multiple statistical methods to control for confounding and conducting extensive sensitivity analyses, residual confounding, reverse causation, and time‑related biases may persist. The observed heterogeneous associations should be considered hypothesis‑generating and require validation by prospective studies or randomized controlled trials. Second, the current classification relies on clinical and routine laboratory data. Future integration of genomics, metabolomics, or detailed inflammatory mediator profiles could refine these subphenotypes and elucidate underlying molecular drivers. Third, the classifier requires prospective validation in real‑world clinical settings. Fourth, the lack of certain variables (lactate, detailed ventilation parameters) in both cohorts limited our ability to explore more granular physiological domains. Fifth, while we performed trajectory analysis for creatinine, comprehensive time‑varying analyses using methods like marginal structural models were not feasible due to data limitations; this remains an important direction for future research.

In summary, this study systematically defines the clinical heterogeneity of SAP and provides a practical tool for its classification. We demonstrate that early phenotypic stratification can improve prognostic assessment and identify subgroups with differential associations to key treatments, such as fluid intake and anticoagulation. Future research should focus on prospectively validating the clinical utility of this classification system and its bedside tool, while investigating the distinct biological mechanisms of each subtype. The goal is to achieve phenotype-guided individualized therapy and improve outcomes in severe pancreatitis.
