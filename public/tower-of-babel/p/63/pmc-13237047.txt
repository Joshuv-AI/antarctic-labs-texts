A Community Dataset for Large-Scale River Nitrogen Modeling in the United States
Authors: Shuyu Y. Chang, Doaa Aboelyazeed, Kamlesh Sawadekar, Digant Chavda, Chaopeng Shen, Kimberly J. Van Meter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water quantity predictions have advanced rapidly, driven by ready-to-use benchmarks such as CAMELS (Catchment Attributes and Meteorology for Large-Sample Studies). In contrast, large-scale water quality predictions, especially for nutrients, lag behind due to a lack of comparable datasets. Existing water quality datasets face four major limitations: (1) underrepresentation of human-impacted systems, (2) absence of nutrient inputs, (3) incomplete watershed metadata, and (4) sparse monitoring coverage. To address these gaps, we developed IWAND-Nitrogen (Integrated Watershed Attributes and Nutrient Data for Nitrogen) for the contiguous United States. IWAND-Nitrogen integrates 574,767 nitrate records from 1,877 catchments (median 272 samples per gauge; IQR = 231–346) with at least 200 measurements each from 1980–2023, linked with 93 watershed attributes, eight nitrogen input forcings (both basin-averaged and gridded), and eleven climate forcings. Compared to existing benchmarks such as CAMELS-Chem, IWAND-Nitrogen complements prior efforts by extending spatial/temporal coverage and enhancing representation across anthropogenic gradients. IWAND-Nitrogen aims to serve as a nutrient community benchmark, advancing from model development to new insights from catchment to national scales.

Background & Summary

Human activities have overloaded Earth’s nitrogen (N), unleashing nutrient pollution that degrades freshwater and marine ecosystems worldwide1–6. In the U.S., 41% of surveyed rivers and streams are in poor condition for nitrogen, with the Plains and Lowlands most affected, followed by the Eastern Highlands and the West7. In terrestrial ecosystems, excess agricultural runoff and waste effluents have expanded drinking water quality concerns3,8,9 and elevated water treatment costs10,11. Concurrently, algal blooms, declining oxygen levels, widespread hypoxia, and their harmful impacts on fish and other aquatic aerobic life have been extensively documented since the mid-20th century in coastal waters12,13, rivers14, and lakes15,16.

Despite decades of regulation efforts, progress in reducing riverine N loads remains inconsistent. Since 1972, the U.S. has invested approximately $1 trillion through the Clean Water Act17, yet protected rivers declined from 60% in 2006 to under 50% by 202018. While some watersheds show improvement, many continue to struggle with persistent or even increasing nutrient loads19–21. The Mississippi River Basin, for instance, may require decades to achieve needed 60% N reductions20,22, while Chesapeake Bay’s 2025 goals for 25% reductions in N relative to 2009 remain only partially met23,24.

To effectively evaluate past efforts and protect water resources moving forward, it’s critical to improve our understanding of how and why river N dynamics have changed both temporally and spatially. Achieving this requires: (1) Comprehensive monitoring to capture variability in N fluxes, (2) Precise quantification of anthropogenic, reactive N inputs, and (3) Robust modeling frameworks to extrapolate insights beyond monitored sites.

Growing efforts have been made in monitoring and quantifying nutrient inputs. Riverine nutrients are among the most frequently measured water quality parameters in the U.S., supported by long-term sampling programs25,26, expanded monitoring networks25, high-frequency sensors27,28, and open-data repositories. By 2013, 25 million riverine nutrient records had been collected from 321,927 sites by 488 organizations29, though nearly half suffered from missing or ambiguous metadata, limiting their usability. Parallel research has also improved landscape-scale nutrient flux estimates. At the national scale, the NANI/NAPI30,31 toolbox enables extraction of net nutrient inputs for any U.S. region (e.g., watershed or county). While the Trajectories Nutrient Dataset for nitrogen (TREND-nitrogen)32 provides extended temporal coverage with 88 years (1930–2017) of county-scale nutrient mass balance estimates, its spatial resolution remains limited. To overcome this limitation, Chang and Byrnes (2026) developed gTREND-Nitrogen33, a 250 m gridded dataset that quantifies landscape nitrogen inputs considering land use change and population distribution.

With data as a foundation, water quality modeling has progressively shifted from traditional process-based approaches and simple regressions toward large-scale predictions. Machine Learning (ML) models are flexible, adaptable, integrative, scalable, and fast34,35 at learning complex relationships by strategically leveraging diverse data inputs. Recent advances have shown that ML can successfully predict and understand key water quality parameters, including nutrients in both surface water and groundwater cross scales36–41.

However, large-scale water quality predictions still significantly lag behind those in streamflow and flood forecasting34, where research has advanced from pure data-driven to hybrid models42,43, from temporal gap-filling to ungauged basin prediction, from trend analysis to extreme events detection44–46, and from bettering predictions to understanding human impacts and management strategies47. One key reason for the slower progress is that the development of standardized and comprehensive benchmarking datasets has lagged by several years34. The earliest benchmark for large-scale hydrology emerged in 2017 with the release of CAMELS (Catchment Attributes and Meteorology for Large-sample Studies) for the contiguous United States (CONUS)48, which catalyzed a rapid expansion in large-scale hydrologic modeling, and was later expanded into the global community dataset, Caravan49.

Despite this lag, there are emerging efforts to develop larger-scale water quality databases. In the following discussion, we focus specifically on datasets including nitrogen. Globally, the GLObal RIver CHemistry database (GLORICH)50 collects 24 water chemistry parameters from 1.27 million samples, with a focus on large, human-impacted rivers. The Global River Water Quality Archive (GRQA)51, which incorporates GLORICH, extends to 42 parameters and over 17 million measurements. The Surface Water Chemistry dataset (SWatCH)52 expands water chemistry data coverage to include canals, ponds, lakes, and reservoirs across seven continents, with 24 variables from over 5 million samples. The OLIGOTREND dataset synthesizes 4.3 million chlorophyll-a and water quality observations from rivers, lakes, and estuaries worldwide53. The most recent ChemlotUS collected 35 million water quality records from 290 thousand locations in rivers across the CONUS54. An important regional contribution is the55 QUADICA (water QUAlity, DIscharge and Catchment Attributes for large-sample studies in Germany), which provides the first large-sample water quality data for 1,300 German catchments and notably includes nutrient input data and streamflow.

While these datasets represent substantial collections of water chemistry data, only GLORICH and QUADICA (Germany) include associated catchment boundaries and paired catchment properties. More importantly, only QUADICA (Germany) provides time-series input variables needed to quantify climate impacts and human activities - the dominant sources for river nutrients. The absence of delineated watersheds and essential modeling inputs has particularly hindered streamlined workflows for researchers directly focused on model development and testing.

In the US, the most recently published CAMELS-Chem56, extending the established CAMELS dataset, incorporates 18 water chemistry parameters, discharge, and atmospheric deposition across 516 minimally disturbed catchments. This dataset serves as an important data platform to fundamentally understand climate-water quality relationships in near-pristine conditions and isolate natural biogeochemical processes from anthropogenic influences. However, because it focuses exclusively on minimally disturbed catchments, models trained on CAMELS-Chem may face challenges when applied to more human-impacted river systems, which dominate much of the US landscape, as data-driven approaches fundamentally cannot generate knowledge beyond their training domain. While CAMELS-Chem is considered one of the most comprehensive water chemistry datasets available, its spatial and temporal coverage remains relatively limited. As a result, it may not fully capture the complexity and variability of river systems at a continental scale. Additionally, it has yet to incorporate data from the rapidly expanding nutrient monitoring networks.

Here we note four major limitations of many of the existing water quality benchmarks, focusing on nutrients: (1) Underrepresentation of human-impacted systems: Current benchmarks disproportionately focus on minimally disturbed watersheds, limiting model transferability to agricultural and urban systems where nutrient pollution is the most severe. (2) Missing anthropogenic nutrient inputs: Most datasets lack long-term, spatially explicit nutrients application information (e.g., fertilizer and manure), which are the dominant nutrient sources and critical model inputs. (3) Incomplete watershed metadata: Many datasets omit essential geospatial descriptors (e.g., watershed boundaries and flow networks), which hinder research streamline. (4) Sparse monitoring coverage: Temporal and spatial sampling resolutions are often insufficient to capture nutrient dynamics.

To address these gaps, we have developed the Integrated Watershed Attributes, and Nutrient Data for Nitrogen (IWAND-Nitrogen) for the CONUS (Fig. 1)—a ready-to-use, community benchmark dataset designed to advance large-scale nutrient research. IWAND-Nitrogen brings together nitrogen inputs (available in both gridded and basin-averaged formats), hydrometeorological forcings, catchment attributes, and riverine nitrogen responses from over 1,877 river systems across the CONUS. Riverine nitrate concentration data

National-wide daily river nitrogen concentration records were collected and processed from the Water Quality Portal (WQP)57, which integrates publicly available water quality data from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) National Water Information System (NWIS)58 and the EPA Water Quality Exchange (WQX) Data Warehouse59. The “wqTools” package60, an R tool developed by the Utah Division of Water Quality, was utilized to download gauge metadata and associated measurements. We selected gauges labeled as “Stream” with at least 200 sampling activities per site between January 1, 1980, and August 7, 2023, as the start of the project. We filtered records where the “characteristic type” was classified as “Nutrient”—a standardized metadata field adopted from the EPA’s Substance Registry System (SRS). Although the USGS system traditionally uses parameter codes, most have been mapped to corresponding SRS names, enabling consistent filtering across both data sources using the “characteristic type” field from the WQP. Nitrate (NO₃) was selected as the primary nitrogen species reported in this dataset, as it is the most commonly monitored, modeled, and targeted in nitrogen reduction strategies. Both NO₃ and combined NO₃ + NO₂ measurements were harmonized and reported as NO₃ to improve spatial and temporal coverage.

While the WQP provides quality-assured records, the raw downloaded data still required extensive preprocessing to address inconsistent formats, unit discrepancies, missing metadata, outlier values, and duplicate measurements29. To address these challenges, we employed a multi-step data cleaning procedure: 1) Filtering nitrate species: we retained entries with the characteristic type labeled as “Nitrate”, “Inorganic nitrogen (nitrate and nitrite)”, “Inorganic nitrogen (nitrate and nitrite) retired use Nitrate + Nitrite,” and “Nitrate + Nitrite”54; (2) Unit unification: concentration data reported as nitrate (NO₃) were converted to nitrate-N (NO₃-N) values; (3) Daily aggregation by calculating mean values for days with multiple records; (4) Incomplete and duplicate records were removed, including those lacking location or date information, missing nutrient concentration values, or containing duplicate measurements.

After data cleaning, harmonization, and removing sites with insufficient measurements, the IWAND-Nitrogen dataset comprises 574,767 in-situ NO₃ concentration records, across 1,877 catchments, each with at least 200 observations collected between 1980 and 2023. The median number of annual samples is approximately 15,500 (IQR = 6,600–19,400) across the CONUS, with a mean of 13,200 samples per year. The data collection effort intensified over time, with the number of samples increasing to a maximum of 22,790 records in 2016, after which a decrease is observed. The dataset represents a diverse range of landscapes, human impacts, and climate regimes, with drainage areas varying from ~10 to over 100,000 km².

Mapping gauges to stream flowlines

We first mapped/snapped the 1,877 WQP gauges to their nearest river segments in the NHDPlus V2 flowline61 network and recorded the associated Common Identifier (COMID), which is a unique numerical identifier assigned to each stream or river segment (flowline) in the dataset. This spatial join was essential for subsequent data preparations, including extracting watershed boundaries, extracting basin attributes, and retrieving time-series forcing information.

NHDPlus V261 is a comprehensive geospatial dataset that integrates high-resolution (1:10,000-scale or better) National Hydrography Dataset (NHD) flowlines with terrain information from the 10-meter National Elevation Dataset (NED). It defines flowlines and catchments (subbasins), each indexed by a unique COMID. These catchments are nested within the hierarchical hydrologic units of the Watershed Boundary Dataset (WBD), allowing upstream tracing from any pour point to delineate the full contributing drainage area.

To snap stream gauges to the nearest NHDPlus flowline, we followed a systematic approach to ensure accuracy and computational efficiency. (1) We first generated a layer of hub points every 75 meters along the NHDPlus flowlines using the “Points Along Geometry”62 in QGIS, minimizing snapping errors. (2) Each stream gauge was then snapped to its nearest hub point, with the assigned COMID and snapping distance recorded using the “Distance to Nearest Hub”63 tool in QGIS. Due to the large dataset size, these steps were performed separately for each Hydrologic Unit Codes (HUC2) watershed to improve computational efficiency.

Watershed boundaries

Watershed boundary provides fundamental geospatial information and is essential for extracting forcing information at the catchment level. Our approach to defining watershed boundaries relied on two key strategies: (1) pairing gauges with watershed data from existing datasets via names/COMIDs, and (2) developing an automatic watershed extraction algorithm for the rest to effectively handle complexities in manual watershed delineation. We primarily leveraged two datasets: the GAGES II dataset (Geospatial Attributes of gauges for Evaluating Streamflow64 and the WaterTrends dataset21. GAGES II, provides geospatial data for over 9,000 stream gauges maintained by the USGS. The WaterTrends dataset includes 1,300 USGS and non-USGS gauges, covering various water quality study sites.

Gauges matching those in the GAGES II (Group I) and WaterTrends (Group II) datasets were identified through a name-based query, allowing direct use of their watershed boundary shapefiles. This approach provided watershed boundaries for 188 and 45 gauges, respectively. To further leverage these datasets, we identified gauges in our dataset with common COMIDs in GAGES II (Group III) and WaterTrends (Group IV). For WaterTrends, COMIDs were readily available, allowing direct matching. For GAGES II, we first snapped its gauges to the nearest flowlines to assign COMIDs to all of them, then identified those with common COMIDs in our dataset. This COMID-based query identified 249 and 303 common gauges in GAGES II and WaterTrends, respectively.

1,092 gauges (Group V) did not match any previously identified groups based on name or COMID queries. To generate watershed boundary shapefiles for these gauges, we designed a customized algorithm leveraging the features of the NHDplus V2 dataset. This process was initiated by snapping as detailed in the Mapping Gauges to Stream Flowlines section, and then employed the “nhdplusTools”61 R package to map all upstream COMIDs relative to a specified COMID. This step was essential for mapping all flowlines (COMIDs) converging into the flowline (COMID) linked to each gauge. Subsequently, we combined all sub-catchments that feed into each gauge based on the identified upstream COMIDs, creating an aggregated watershed boundary shapefile for each gauge (Fig. 3c). Collectively, these Group V watersheds, which are predominantly small-to-medium in size, cover approximately 24.9% of the CONUS. In total, the 1,877 watersheds in the IWAND dataset represent approximately 70.4% of the CONUS area.

Watershed attributes

A total of 93 static attributes for streams, catchments, and subcatchments were extracted from the NHDPlus V261 and the Select Attributes for NHDPlus V2.165 (hereafter referred to as the NHDPlus SAttributes dataset), by linking the COMID. In addition to these NHDPlus-derived attributes, we incorporated 2023 crop coverage data for five major crop types—corn, soybeans, alfalfa, winter wheat, and non-alfalfa hay—from the gridded United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Cropland Data Layer (CDL)66. Notably, attribute prefixes indicate different spatial scales: “TOT_” represents attributes aggregated across the total upstream area, “CAT_” represents attributes of the subcatchment directly adjacent to the sampling gauge, and “STR_” denotes attributes associated with the river flowline.

The extracted attributes describe a wide range of stream and watershed characteristics, including stream metadata (e.g., stream order); catchment metadata (e.g., catchment area, slope, and elevation); surface variables (e.g., cropland land use percentages, seasonal vegetation indices, and corn coverage); geological properties (e.g., baseflow index, topographic index, and permeability); hydrological attributes (e.g., annual streamflow and actual evapotranspiration, AET); and anthropogenic influences (e.g., population density, and the number and storage capacity of reservoirs). While several datasets of catchment attributes for large-sample hydrology now exist, we emphasize that IWAND-Nitrogen is the first to provide information characterizing not only the lumped catchment, but also the river and subcatchment, offering a more detailed and accurate representation of the hydrological and biogeochemical dynamics observed at the gauge. For example, if a subcatchment is dominated by agricultural land use, agricultural runoff originating from the subcatchment—bypassing extensive transport and biogeochemical processes—can directly influence nutrient levels at the gauged river. In addition, each river was linked with a COMID, allowing users to flexibly extract further NHDPlus catchment attributes as needed.

Forcing time-series

Quantifying the changes in hydrometeorological cycles and anthropogenic N inputs over time is critical for understanding and modeling river N dynamics. IWAND-Nitrogen therefore provides two types of time-series data including: 1) eleven basin-averaged daily meteorological forcings from the North American Land Data Assimilation System (NLDAS)67, and 2) eight annual nitrogen input forcings in both basin-averaged and gridded format obtained from the gTREND-Nitrogen33 dataset.

Meteorological forcings were derived from NLDAS-267, an offline data assimilation system featuring uncoupled land surface models which are driven by observation-based atmospheric forcings. Widely used in meteorological and hydrological research, NLDAS-2 has also served as the forcing dataset for prominent large-sample hydrology datasets, such as CAMELS-US48, CAMELS-Chem56, and Caravan49. The eleven forcings in IWAND-Nitrogen, include wind components, temperature, humidity, radiation, pressure, and precipitation (Table 3). These were downloaded from the official portal of the Goddard Earth Science Data and Information Services Center (GES DISC)68 in raster format at an hourly temporal resolution and a ⅛ ° spatial resolution across the CONUS. To align with the daily time step of stream nutrient measurements, hourly values were aggregated daily by averaging for most variables, and summing for daily precipitation and potential evapotranspiration. We then computed basin-averaged values for each forcing using zonal statistics69 in Python. Meteorological forcings: variable names, processing methods, and units.

Eight nutrient forcings (Table 4) were extracted from the gTREND-Nitrogen (1930–2017)33 dataset. gTREND-Nitrogen33 is the most comprehensive, long-term, high-resolution nitrogen (N) mass balance dataset for the CONUS. It was developed by downscaling county-scale TREND-N32 according to gridded land use/land cover change information, and population distribution, to generate 250-meter grid-scale estimates of both surface N fluxes (e.g., fertilizer application, atmospheric deposition, manure inputs, biological N fixation, and crop N uptake) and population-based fluxes of human waste. IWAND-Nitrogen provides these forcings from 1980–2023 in two formats: 1) annual basin-averaged time series as comma-separated values (.csv) files, and 2) the original 250-meter gridded data as CF-compliant NetCDF (.nc) files for each of the 1,877 watersheds. The gridded data, produced by clipping CONUS-wide rasters to individual watersheds and converting them to xarray format70, preserve the high spatial heterogeneity of nitrogen inputs, enabling flexible aggregation and direct use in distributed models. Annual basin-averaged eight N input forcings from 1980 to 2017 were extracted using zonal statistics69 in Python. For both data formats, N input forcings were assumed constant from 2017–2023, as this period extends beyond the final year of the gTREND-Nitrogen source data. Technical Validation

We performed technical validation to ensure the quality of the IWAND-Nitrogen dataset by: (1) validating the mapping of WQP gauges to NHDPlus V2 flowlines; (2) assessing the accuracy of watershed boundary extraction; and (3) benchmarking IWAND-Nitrogen against established water quality datasets like CAMELS-Chem.

COMID Validation

We linked WQP gauge locations to NHDPlus V2 flowlines (Section Mapping Gauges to Stream Flowlines) to enable the extraction of catchment attributes and watershed boundaries using the NHDPlus V2 dataset. Given the importance of accurate gauge-to-flowline mapping, we implemented a two-step validation process based on either the reported watershed area or the gauge-watershed location.

For gauges with WQP-reported watershed areas, we validated the mapping by comparing the watershed area to the reported value. For gauges without WQP-reported watershed areas but leveraging the GAGES II dataset, we compared the cumulative watershed area derived from the assigned COMID to the watershed area reported in GAGES II. Gauges with more than a 15% discrepancy between delineated and reported watershed areas were manually reviewed using high-resolution imagery, and COMIDs were reassigned when necessary to ensure mapping accuracy. Note that for gauges derived from WaterTrends, we used the COMID reported in their dataset, with no further validation required. Of the 354 rivers with ground truth drainage area data available for COMID validation, 309 rivers were successfully snapped using our methodology (drainage area agreement within ± 15%), demonstrating the feasibility of our approach. As the 45 rivers from WaterTrends were provided with pre-validated COMIDs, they required no further validation.

To further assess the accuracy of the COMID assignment, we conducted an intersection analysis between gauge locations and watershed boundaries. We found that 80% of gauges were located within their respective watershed boundaries, supporting the reliability of the snapping process. For the remaining ~400 gauges outside the watershed polygon, manual inspection using high-resolution basemap revealed that only 17 had been incorrectly snapped. Most of these mismatches were concentrated in central-south Florida, where the combination of dense waterways (including wetlands, lakes, and artificial channels) and the extremely flat topography of southern Florida complicated NHDPlus subcatchment delineation and our watershed extraction. The 17 incorrectly snapped gauges were subsequently excluded from the final dataset.

Overall, the majority of NHDPlus flowlines (93%, or 1,605 in total) contained a single monitoring gauge. A smaller fraction of flowlines had multiple gauges—6% with two gauges and < 1% with three to four. To support flexible data usage, we provide the snapped flowline identifier (COMID), enabling users to determine whether to group WQP monitoring data when analyzing nearby gauges. The median snapping distance was 42.6 m (IQR = 22.0 m–76.7 m). Since the relevance of snapping distance may vary depending on river size54, we include the snapping distance (HubDist) in the dataset to enable filtering or selection based on user-specific analytical needs.

Watershed boundaries validation

Watershed boundaries were extracted either by matching names with the GAGES II or WaterTrends datasets (Groups I and II) or using COMID information (Groups III, IV, and V). For the latter groups, even with corrected snapped COMID, a potential discrepancy arises when gauges are not precisely located at the outlet of the subcatchment in the NHDPlus dataset (Fig. 4). In such cases, the entire subcatchment area was used without excluding the region downstream of the target gauge, which can lead to overestimations in catchment areas. Enhancing the water quality database: CAMELS-Chem vs. IWAND-Nitrogen

Our vision for IWAND is to advance large-scale water quality predictions and data-driven decision making by developing a broader community data resource that extends beyond existing water quality model benchmarks such as CAMELS-Chem56. While CAMELS-Chem represents the state-of-the-art water chemistry dataset at the CONUS scale, it is primarily designed to support fundamental process understanding and trend detection within minimally human-impacted watersheds under pristine conditions. Expanding upon CAMELS-Chem, IWAND aims to: 1) extend monitoring efforts spatially and temporally by providing a more inclusive dataset, and 2) improve the representation of watersheds across a gradient of anthropogenic influence from minimally to significantly contaminated.

Usage Notes

The current version of the IWAND-Nitrogen dataset is available at (10.5281/zenodo.17677638)71, and a project homepage is available at https://github.com/schanghydro/IWAND-Nitrogen. Users are encouraged to take the following considerations into account when using the dataset:Gauge selection and spatial distributionWe conducted an exhaustive search and selected stream gauges with long-term records ( ≥ 200 observations over 38 years). Due to the natural distribution of rivers, gauge locations, selection criteria, and the filtering of gauges with incorrect watershed boundaries, gauge density is higher in the eastern CONUS compared to the western CONUS, with the highest densities located in HUCs 2, 3, and 7 (see Table 1). We acknowledge the spatial imbalance and users conducting large-scale analyses or region-specific studies may apply additional selection criteria to tailor the dataset to their research objectives.Co-sharing COMIDApproximately 8% of NHDPlus flowlines contain more than one WQP monitoring gauge. These cases were retained in the dataset; however, users are encouraged to evaluate whether to group gauges for their specific analyses.Temporal inconsistencies across forcing variablesWe acknowledge the temporal inconsistencies among the different forcing variables in IWAND-Nitrogen. The nutrient input forcing data from gTREND-Nitrogen are provided at an annual resolution for the period 1930–2017. For years beyond 2017, we maintained the same nutrient input forcing values as 2017, as the current gTREND-Nitrogen does not extend beyond this timeframe. The climate forcing from NLDAS-2 is available daily. In-situ river nutrient concentrations from the WQP consist of discrete samples, which may include single measurements or multiple samples on the same day; these were subsequently aggregated to a daily scale. To preserve the integrity of the original datasets, we deliberately avoided imposing additional modeling layers to reconcile these temporal differences. This approach allows users the flexibility to either upscale the daily climate and nutrient concentration data to an annual resolution or downscale the annual nutrient inputs to a daily timestep, based on the specific requirements of their research.IWAND as a flexible and expandable platformIWAND serves as a flexible platform that allows users to incorporate additional attributes and new forcing variables, and even extend to new gauges seamlessly. Each gauge is associated with a COMID identifier and watershed boundary, enabling users to integrate supplementary watershed characteristics and forcing data. Furthermore, users can extend the dataset by incorporating new basins following our established methodology, see our project github page https://github.com/schanghydro/IWAND-Nitrogen. The dataset and all associated code are openly available, making it easy to customize and expand IWAND for specific research applications.

Temporal inconsistencies across forcing variablesWe acknowledge the temporal inconsistencies among the different forcing variables in IWAND-Nitrogen. The nutrient input forcing data from gTREND-Nitrogen are provided at an annual resolution for the period 1930–2017. For years beyond 2017, we maintained the same nutrient input forcing values as 2017, as the current gTREND-Nitrogen does not extend beyond this timeframe. The climate forcing from NLDAS-2 is available daily. In-situ river nutrient concentrations from the WQP consist of discrete samples, which may include single measurements or multiple samples on the same day; these were subsequently aggregated to a daily scale. To preserve the integrity of the original datasets, we deliberately avoided imposing additional modeling layers to reconcile these temporal differences. This approach allows users the flexibility to either upscale the daily climate and nutrient concentration data to an annual resolution or downscale the annual nutrient inputs to a daily timestep, based on the specific requirements of their research.
