No Difference in Clinical Outcomes Between Concomitant Anterior and Posterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction With and Without Independent Suture Tape Augmentation
Authors: Adam J. Tagliero, Brandon Cabarcas, Sanathan Iyer, Adam V. Daniel, John J. Kelly, Aaron J. Krych, Bruce A. Levy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare clinical and functional outcomes (including range of motion, clinical laxity, reoperation/revision rates, and patient-reported outcome measures) of combined anterior cruciate ligament/posterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR/PCLR) with and without suture tape (ST) augmentation via a propensity-matched cohort analysis.MethodsA retrospective review was performed to identify all patients who underwent simultaneous primary ACLR/PCLR between 2001 and 2022. A 1:1 propensity match was performed using patient sex, Schenck classification grade, age ±8 years, and body mass index ±10 to produce 2 cohorts: ACLR/PCLR with and without (control) ST augmentation. Clinical outcomes (visual analog scale [VAS], range of motion, Tegner activity scale, Lysholm score, and International Knee Documentation Committee subjective score) were analyzed.ResultsThe propensity match produced a final cohort of 50 ACLR/PCLR patients: 25 with ST augmentation and 25 nonaugmented controls. Most patients had a KD3M injury (12 in each cohort), and no significant differences were noted in concomitant neurovascular, meniscal, or chondral injuries between the groups. Forty patients (80%) completed follow-up at a mean of 4.7 years for the ST cohort and 8.5 years for the control cohort (P > .05). Postoperative ST versus control values for outcome measures including VAS at rest (0.9 vs 1.5; P = .71), VAS with use (3.0 vs 2.2; P = .24), International Knee Documentation Committee (75.7 vs 76.3; P = .37), Lysholm (81.8 vs 77.6; P = .91), and Tegner activity (4.8 vs 5.4; P = .37) scores showed no differences (P > .05 for all). There were no differences in excessive postoperative clinical laxity between ST and control cohorts (ST n = 1 vs control n = 2, Lachman 2+, P = .140; ST n = 3 vs control n = 1, posterior drawer 2+, P = .122). The total number of patients who underwent reoperation was 7 of 25 (28%) for the ST cohort and 8 of 25 (32%) for the control cohort (P = .76).ConclusionsConcomitant ACLR/PCLR utilizing ST augmentation showed similarly satisfactory postoperative clinical outcomes at a minimum 2-year follow-up compared to a matched cohort without ST. The use of an independent ST did not limit motion or increase the incidence of complications or revision surgery.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective comparative case series.

Managing multiligament knee injuries (MLKIs) can prove extremely challenging for orthopaedic surgeons despite major technological advances and modern surgical techniques. Prior investigations have shown improved clinical and functional outcomes with surgical intervention compared to nonoperative treatment of MLKIs.1,2 However, relatively high rates of perioperative complications, including arthrofibrosis, residual laxity, graft failure, and the need for revision surgery, have been reported.3, 4, 5

Suture tape (ST) augmentation is a form of intra-articular stabilization that is designed to provide additional biomechanical strength and support that acts as a checkrein if the reconstructed graft experiences supraphysiologic load, thus reducing the risk of plastic deformity and rerupture.6,7 Biomechanical investigations have demonstrated that ST augmentation can increase ultimate tensile strength and reduce graft elongation after posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) reconstruction (PCLR), as well as significantly increase graft stiffness and load to failure after anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction (ACLR).8, 9, 10 Early clinical results of isolated ACLR or PCLR with ST augmentation have shown promise, with low rates of graft failure, complications, and reoperations, as well as a faster time to return to preoperative activity levels and improved patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) at 2 years.11,12 Additionally, some early clinical evidence suggests that ST augmentation in the setting of combined ACL/PCL injuries and multiligament knee reconstruction can provide successful patient outcomes with failure and complication rates equivalent to traditional reconstruction techniques.13,14 However, there remains a paucity of literature specifically regarding the postoperative outcomes following combined ACLR/PCLR with ST augmentation.

The purpose of this study was to compare clinical and functional outcomes (including range of motion, clinical laxity, reoperation/revision rates, and PROMs) of combined ACLR/PCLR with and without ST augmentation via a propensity-matched cohort analysis. We hypothesized that ACLR/PCLR with ST augmentation would produce similar outcomes compared to nonaugmented ACLR/PCLR and would have no impact on perioperative complications or reoperation rates.

Methods

Following institutional review board approval (IRB: 07-004018), a retrospective review of a prospectively collected clinical database from a single institution was conducted to identify all patients who underwent simultaneous primary ACLR/PCLR between 2001 and 2022. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients who underwent primary concomitant ACLR/PCLR with or without independent ST reinforcement. Patients were excluded if they underwent revision ACLR/PCLR, were polytraumatized patients, had isolated ST application to either the ACL or PCL, or were deceased at follow-up. Patients who underwent a total knee arthroplasty (TKA) prior to final follow-up were included for all analyses except comparisons of final PROM scores.

All patients meeting initial inclusion and exclusion criteria were divided into 2 groups: (1) ACLR/PCLR with suture tape augmentation (ST group) and (2) nonaugmented ACLR/PCLR (control group). Patients in the ST cohort were matched 1:1 with patients in the control group of primary ACLR/PCLR without ST reinforcement. Matching was performed according to age at time of ACLR/PCLR (within 8 years), sex, body mass index (BMI; within 10 kg/m2), and exact KD grade (according to the Schenck classification of knee dislocations).15 These matching criteria were chosen to facilitate direct comparisons of patients with the same knee injuries (exact KD grade) while also sharing similar demographic factors in accordance with prior studies evaluating cruciate reconstruction with suture tape augmentation.11,16

Patient medical records were individually reviewed to obtain patient demographics, preoperative and postoperative physical examination findings, concomitant procedures, reoperations, and complications. All patients were followed up clinically up to 2 postoperative years with an examination and radiographs per standard practice at our institution. Physical examinations were performed by the operating surgeon using a goniometer for range of motion. Patients beyond 2 postoperative years were contacted for follow-up via telephone or e-mail, when necessary, to collect postoperative PROM scores. Postoperative PROMs (including the visual analog scale [VAS], Tegner activity score, Lysholm score, and International Knee Documentation Committee [IKDC] subjective score) were obtained.16,17

Surgical Technique

The authors’ preference for ACLR with ST augmentation utilizes an all-inside technique with quadrupled semitendinosus-gracilis autograft, or 4-stranded allograft.17 The grafts were prepared with the GraftLink (Arthrex) technique incorporating an adjustable loop device (ALD) (TightRope; Arthrex) for suspensory fixation on both the femur and tibia. A suture tape (FiberTape; Arthrex) was passed through the femoral ALD’s cortical button and incorporated into the construct.17 The ACL femoral sockets were drilled antegrade via a low anteromedial portal to a minimum intraosseous depth of approximately 20 to 25 mm. A FlipCutter (Arthrex) was utilized to drill the tibia socket retrograde with a minimum intraosseous depth of approximately 30 mm. The graft was first passed into the knee joint and secured to the femur using the femoral ALD’s button against the cortex of the lateral femur. The tibial ALD was then passed into the knee joint and through the tibial socket using a passing suture. The external Attachable Button System (ABS; Arthrex) was utilized to pass the graft suspensory sutures and suture tape through the button and obtain fixation on the tibia. The graft was preliminarily cycled through flexion and extension to remove any potential creep. Prior to final graft tensioning, the suture tape was fixed distally with a suture anchor (SwiveLock; Arthrex) on the anteromedial tibial cortex approximately 1 cm distal to the tunnel in full extension. Final retensioning of the graft was then performed on both the femoral and tibial ALDs with the knee in full extension.

For PCLR and ST augmentation, the authors’ preference is an all-inside technique with either a quadrupled peroneus longus or tibialis anterior allograft (Fig 1). The PCL GraftLink (Arthrex) technique was used to create a double suspensory fixation construct using ALD fixation on both the femur and tibia.16 The independent suture tape was passed through the femoral ALD’s cortical button to incorporate it into the construct. An accessory posteromedial portal and a 70° arthroscope were utilized to optimize arthroscopic visualization. The Anatomic Contour PCL Tibial Guide (Arthrex) was inserted and positioned at the base of the PCL facet between the mammillary bodies. A FlipCutter was used to drill a 40-mm tibial socket in retrograde fashion. On the femur, the native PCL anterolateral bundle footprint was identified, an accessory inferolateral portal was established, and a low-profile cannulated reamer was used to drill a 25- to 30-mm femoral socket. The tibial end of the PCL graft was inserted into the joint first using passing sutures, followed by the femoral end. The femoral ALD’s cortical button was flipped against the medial femoral cortex, and the graft was advanced into the femoral socket. The external ABS was attached to the tibial ALD and suture tape. The graft was appropriately tensioned in 90° of knee flexion, and the TightRope strands were tied over the ABS button and secured into a swivel lock anchor for backup fixation. The suture tape was fixed to the tibia via an additional swivel lock anchor in 90° of knee flexion. Afterward, final PCL graft tensioning was performed. Note that the authors performed tensioning and fixation of the PCL graft as described prior to final tensioning and fixation of the ACL graft. However, the femoral ALD cortical buttons on both the ACL and PCL grafts allowed for retensioning if needed, as determined by the operating surgeon. Statistical Analysis

Data were collected and stored utilizing Excel (Microsoft) and REDCap (REDCap Consortium) software. Data were analyzed using BlueSky Statistics (BlueSky) and Prism GraphPad (GraphPad Software). Baseline patient characteristics were presented as means and percentages, with standard error of mean or 95% confidence intervals when appropriate. Physical examination findings and PROMs were analyzed for parametric/nonparametric assumptions utilizing Shapiro-Wilk normality tests. Continuous variables were then analyzed using Student t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 analysis or Fisher exact tests when appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Results

A total of 137 patients underwent ACLR/PCLR with or without suture tape reinforcement between 2001 and 2022 at a single institution. Of these patients, 21 were excluded (12 revision ACLR or PCLR cases, 1 polytraumatized, 3 ST applied to only 1 ligament, 1 osteosarcoma, and 4 deceased at final follow-up). As a result, 116 patients (27 ST and 89 control) were eligible for a 2-year follow-up and able to participate in the propensity match. Discussion

The major findings of this study are that ST augmentation during concomitant ACLR/PCLR produced similar clinical outcomes, PROM scores, and postoperative clinical laxity compared to without ST augmentation. There were no significant differences in the number of revisions or reoperations between groups and no evidence of increased implant-related perioperative complications.

Several options exist when considering technique and graft choice for combined ACLR/PCLR, including both autograft and allograft bone–patellar tendon–bone, quadriceps and hamstring tendons, Achilles tendon allografts, and single- or double-bundle techniques.18, 19, 20 No single graft or technique choice has shown superior clinical outcomes in the literature. While technically challenging to perform, several investigations have demonstrated that MLKI reconstructions, including combined ACL/PCL procedures, can successfully address clinical instability, with some studies reporting satisfactory outcomes and low revision rates up to 15 years postoperatively.21, 22, 23 Notably, patients obtain good overall function and return to activity despite incomplete restoration of normal knee kinematics and some degree of residual laxity.22, 23, 24 The primary role of the PCL is to resist posterior translation of the tibia relative to the femur, with both anterolateral and posteromedial bundles contributing throughout the range of motion of the knee.25 In our analysis, postoperative posterior drawer testing results did not differ among cohorts. However, residual PCL laxity after PCLR can be a difficult outcome to quantify postoperatively.26 Clinical examination maneuvers, such as the posterior drawer test, have shown sensitivity and specificity of greater than 90% in detecting PCL insufficiency.27 That being said, more subtle laxity may be more difficult to detect, as examination accuracy increases with higher-grade injuries.27 Particularly in the setting of bicruciate injuries or other MLKIs, accurately detecting and quantifying pathologic laxity can pose a significant clinical challenge and requires substantial experience managing these complex injuries.

To our knowledge, this investigation is the first to report the clinical results of a direct comparison of patients undergoing combined ACL/PCL reconstruction with ST augmentation versus a matched control cohort without ST augmentation. Prior available studies have evaluated single case series of multiligamentous knee reconstructions augmented with ST, in the absence of matched controls, or performed systematic reviews of the literature where any study that utilized suture tape augmentation during multiligamentous knee reconstruction was included.13,14 In our analysis, patients in the ST augmentation group reported similar postoperative VAS, Tegner activity scale, Lysholm, and IKDC scores compared to patients without ST augmentation, with PROM values on par with prior studies.19,20,28 Implementing a 1:1 propensity matching methodology by age, sex, BMI, and exact KD classification minimizes the potential impact of demographic differences while comparing similar injuries of the same severity. Additionally, no significant differences in concomitant preoperative neurovascular, meniscal, or chondral injuries were noted between the groups. Younger patients under 30 years of age have been reported to experience increased functional outcome scores compared to older patients at short- to mid-term follow-up after MLKI reconstruction, but these differences have not been proven to be maintained at a minimum 10-year long-term follow-up.23,29 When considering the presence of a preoperative neurovascular injury, patients who sustained an associated popliteal artery injury requiring bypass grafting produced significantly lower clinical knee outcome scores at a mean of 6 years postoperatively versus patients without vascular injury.30 Interestingly, some evidence has shown that although patients with a partial common peroneal nerve palsy are more likely to recover antigravity strength than patients with a complete nerve injury, overall postoperative clinical knee function after MLKI reconstruction is similar between those with a nerve injury and those without.31

Safety concerns and the potential for implant-related perioperative complications must be considered when contemplating ST augmentation during ACL or PCL reconstruction. Since the 1980s, synthetic devices designed to augment or replace ACL grafts during reconstruction have appeared on the market.7 However, these implants were associated with unacceptably high failure rates.32, 33, 34, 35 Although the independent suture tape is designed to protect the graft from supraphysiologic load, concern exists regarding the potential for suture tape overtensioning or graft stress-shielding.6,36 These issues can theoretically lead to overconstraint of the graft, decreased stability, hindrance of the graft ligamentization process, and increased risk of graft failure.37 However, a biomechanical study by Bachmaier et al.36 showed that the suture tape acted as a “load-sharing” device, as loads within the graft were noted even with peel forces. In the present analysis, both the ST augmentation and nonaugmentation cohorts experienced similar rates of reoperation. Additionally, there were no differences in the number of postoperative complications or any evidence of implant-related complications among the ST augmentation cohort. These findings support that although theoretical safety concerns over ST augmentation are plausible, clinical impact remains to be realized. The results of the present investigation align well with the available, albeit limited, body of clinical evidence regarding ACL or PCL reconstruction augmented with independent ST fixation that shows no increases in graft failure or implant-related perioperative complications.16,17,38,39

Recent biomechanical and clinical investigations have shown that ST augmentation in PCL reconstruction can produce superior clinical outcome scores, return to activity, and postoperative clinical laxity compared to traditional techniques, as well as improved postoperative clinical laxity.11,12,16 However, the potential impact of ST augmentation on graft failure rate remains less clear. In our analysis, we found no differences in terms of clinical outcome scores, clinical laxity, or reoperation rates in patients who underwent combined ACLR/PCLR with versus without ST. This potentially could have been influenced by the severity of injury since combined cruciate reconstruction may not necessarily produce equivalent results as isolated PCL reconstruction. Additionally, while there were no differences found in postoperative clinical laxity among ST and control patients, objective data from stress radiographs could have allowed for a more precise evaluation of posterior tibial translation and potentially different results. Cost-effectiveness of ST augmentation is another clinically relevant issue that must be considered. While efforts to augment the biological environment and mechanical strength of cruciate ligament reconstruction have been described, some techniques, such as the addition of bone marrow aspirate concentrate and demineralized bone matrix, can add approximately $2,000 to the cost of each procedure.40 Some authors argue that the cost of ST augmentation alone is minimal compared to other reconstruction augmentation methods via orthobiologics, particularly if the addition of the ST leads to fewer graft failures.41 In our analysis, there were no differences in graft failure when evaluating for excessive postoperative clinical laxity or revision rate when comparing combined ACLR/PCLR with or without ST augmentation. While the final cost is multifactorial, depending on individual factors such as institution setting and geographic location, it is important to consider potential increases in surgical cost and operative time when contemplating ST augmentation. While ST augmentation may increase the biomechanical strength of cruciate reconstruction, larger randomized clinical trials with long-term follow-up are warranted to assess whether ST augmentation influences postoperative graft failure rate.

Limitations

Our study is not without limitations. Heterogeneity of graft choices could not be accounted for and was left to surgeon preference. The retrospective nature of the study design also inherently limited the level of analysis. The matching criteria used in this investigation were implemented to allow for direct comparisons of knee injuries by matching by exact KD grade among 2 patient cohorts of similar demographics. However, this subsequently limited the sample size of our final cohorts. A larger sample of patients may potentially have allowed for greater detection of differing outcomes between cohorts or the occurrence of additional complications. The PROMs utilized in this study may possess a ceiling effect, whereby smaller, yet clinically impactful, differences in outcomes may not be captured once a certain threshold score is achieved. Furthermore, contacting patients via telephone for final follow-up has the potential for recall bias. Additionally, the follow-up times between the ST cohort and the control cohort varied substantially because ST augmentation was a newer adopted practice. Even though follow-up time was not statistically significant between the 2 cohorts, the difference may have impacted PROMs. Finally, the risk of inter- and intrarater variation in clinical performance of examination maneuvers cannot be eliminated. While objective data from stress radiographs or other methods, such as KT-1000 testing, would have significantly contributed to the evaluation of postoperative clinical laxity, these were not readily available to provide a statistically sound comparison between matched patients.

Conclusions

Concomitant ACLR/PCLR utilizing ST augmentation showed similarly satisfactory postoperative clinical outcomes at a minimum 2-year follow-up compared to a matched cohort without ST. The use of an independent ST did not limit motion or increase the incidence of complications or revision surgery.
