Dexamethasone conjugation to an Avidin-Nucleic-Acid-NanoASsembly eliminates the steroid plasma absorption, enhancing selective lung tropism in a murine model of pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Annalisa Morelli, Elisa Schiavon, Martina Bruna Violatto, Giulia Yuri Moscatiello, Anita Salmaso, Alice Passoni, Alessia Lanno, Simone Bernardotto, Martina Stocco, Andrea Mattarei, Renzo Bagnati, Federica Meloni, Paolo Bigini, Margherita Morpurgo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite their anti-inflammatory activity, corticosteroids are limited in clinic due to poor selectivity and their side effects. The ability to cross biological barriers makes them powerful yet unspecific, leading to toxicity and a low therapeutic index that limits their chronic use in autoimmune, inflammatory, and infectious diseases. It is needed another approachfor innovative targeted delivery strategies. This study aimed at investigating if the dexamethasone conjugation to Avidin-Nucleic-Acid-NanoASsembly (ANANAS) could allow its selective lung release in the bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model. Since recent evidence showed a selective ANANAS accumulation in macrophage lysosomes in a liver fibrosis model, an acid-sensitive hydrazone linker was used to facilitate dexamethasone release into pulmonary macrophages, key players in lung fibrosis. Systemic ANANAS-Dex administration in healthy mice showed no dexamethasone release in plasma or peripheral organs, with delivery exclusively targeting the liver, independent of the health status. While this confirmed the nanocarrier safety, it underscored the influence of the administration route, rather than the disease state, on ANANAS-Dex tropism. The study on intranasal administration highlighted that: 1) free Dex circulates in the bloodstream, while ANANAS keeps the drug confined in the lungs; 2) ANANAS-Dex results in sustained drug release in the lungs, enhancing the lungs/plasma-peripheral organs ratio; 3) fibrotic mice exhibited prolonged kinetics and macrophage targeting. Based on the biodistribution and pharmacokinetics studies, it is possible to achieve controlled and safe steroid release in lung disorders, reducing systemic toxicity and potentially enhancing clinical compliance.Keywords: Lung fibrosis, nanoparticles, intranasal administration, inflammation, corticosteroids, drug delivery

Introduction

Corticosteroids are synthetic drugs that mimic cortisol, a hormone produced by the adrenal glands. In the case of glucocorticoids, their primary actions include the regulation of immune responses, inflammation, and metabolism, while mineralocorticoids play a crucial role in electrolyte balance and blood pressure regulation (Williams 2018). Corticosteroids inhibit genes responsible for the expression of cyclooxygenase-2, inducible nitric oxide synthase, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and various interleukins (IL). They are widely used for their potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties, making them effective in treating conditions such as asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and autoimmune disorders. Corticosteroids are also utilized to manage acute conditions, including severe allergic reactions and exacerbations of chronic illnesses (Vandewalle et al. 2018). Although steroids are very effective, their wide-body distribution upon administration, aided by their ability to readily cross biological barriers, is a limitation for patients, who are often forced to discontinue therapy due to the onset of serious complications, such as osteopenia and vertebral collapse, decreased bone density, diabetes, hypertension, mood, and cognitive disorders, increased susceptibility to infections and obesity (Schacke 2002; Weinstein 2011).

One solution to this problem could be the strategic accumulation of the active drug specifically within the target organ, thereby reducing the total dosage and avoiding undesirable systemic side effects.

Over the past decade, an increasing number of trials have demonstrated the ability of nanoscale carriers to improve selective transport and targeted delivery to specific organs (B. Y. S. Kim et al. 2010; Yetisgin et al. 2020; Mitchell et al. 2021). Most types of nanoparticles (NPs) show natural tropism for macrophages (both resident and recruited), which play a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of inflammatory diseases. Macrophages possess a robust capacity to identify, internalize, and degrade various types of NPs, making these systems ideal drug carriers for inflammation-related diseases (Byrne et al. 2016; Murray and Wynn 2011).

Among them, a novel nanocarrier called Avidin-Nucleic-Acid-Nano-Assemblies (ANANAS), has been thoroughly characterized and validated by in vitro and in vivo studies. ANANAS are biodegradable, biocompatible, and non-immunogenic poly-avidin-based NPs obtained from the high-affinity interaction between the egg white protein avidin and a non-coding nucleic acid strand (Morpurgo et al. 2004; Pignatto et al. 2010). Due to the strong affinity (Kd ∼1 0 −15 M) between avidin and biotin, these NPs can be easily loaded, with stoichiometric control, with biotin-linked bioactive or contrast agents. In recent years, ANANAS-based formulations have shown potential in various biomedical applications, including diagnostics and drug delivery (Bigini et al. 2014).

In particular, dexamethasone (Dex) carrying ANANAS formulations in which the drug was tethered via an acid-reversible hydrazone (Hz) linker (ANANAS-Dex) have been investigated for their potential in treating liver autoimmune diseases, showing efficacy and lack of off-target release of the drug when administered parenterally (Violatto et al. 2019; Ongaro et al. 2022). In ANANAS-Dex formulations, to prevent the release of the free drug in plasma and permit drug release within the acidic environment of target tissue endosomes/lysosomes, the drug is loaded onto the carrier by means of a biotin conjugate and an acid-reversible hydrazone linker. In these studies, a marked tropism of ANANAS for the target tissue (liver) macrophages was observed in both healthy and pathological conditions.

This work aimed to extend our investigation on ANANAS-Dex and explore its potential for the treatment of inflammatory-driven fibrotic lung disorders, in which macrophages have a pivotal role. Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) are a group of heterogeneous diseases characterized by various degrees of lung inflammation and diffuse fibrosis. Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a condition marked by a chronic and progressive tissue repair response that leads to irreversible scar formation, lung remodeling, and impaired gas exchange. Excessive deposition of Extracellular Matrix (ECM) proteins induces fibrotic tissue accumulation, alveolar destruction, and irreversible loss of lung function (Wijsenbeek and Cottin 2020; Wijsenbeek et al. 2022). The pathogenesis of ILDs involves several cellular compartments, including the epithelium, lung fibroblasts, and the innate and adaptive immune system. However, despite numerous research efforts that clarified several key passages in the development of progressive and irreversible fibrosis, the contribution of each of these factors, particularly of innate/inflammatory response, to disease development, remains unclear (Martinez et al. 2017; Richeldi et al. 2017). Current clinical treatments for lung inflammation that may lead to fibrosis in the context of immune allo- or auto-reactivity include corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents to mitigate inflammation and anti-fibrotic drugs (nintedanib and pirfenidone) to slow down the progression of fibrosis (Lederer and Martinez 2018; Johannson et al. 2021; Wijsenbeek et al. 2022). Steroids have the sole anti-inflammatory function and are critical in the early stages of damage. Indeed, extinguishing the inflammatory focus is an important requirement in many forms of chronic alveolar damage, since it limits the macrophage activation and phenotype shift, the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and the related transformation of epithelial cells into fibroblasts. Earlier studies on ANANAS-Dex have shown that (Violatto et al. 2019; Ongaro et al. 2022) the chemistry of the hydrazone bond and the nature of the spacer between biotin and the hydrazone impact the NP behavior in vitro and in vivo (Ongaro et al. 2022). Following the composition/functionality relationships identified in these earlier studies, a new ANANAS-Dex formulation (third generation) was optimized for this work to further improve the carrier drug loading, its colloidal stability, and its tropism for tissue-resident macrophages.

Following in vitro pre-formulation studies, the improved properties of the novel ANANAS-Dex were confirmed by performing a pharmacokinetics and biodistribution study in healthy animals following parenteral administration. Finally, the formulation was evaluated in the bleomycin (BLM) on the PF mouse model, which reflects several key aspects of human lung fibrosis [19].

To this end, the biodistribution and pharmacokinetics of Dex upon parenteral administration and nasal instillation to PF mice, either as a free drug or when nanoformulated were measured. In addition, the biodistribution of the nanoformulation was followed by in vivo optical imaging and its tissue localization in lung sections was investigated by confocal microscopy. Finally, the clinical manifestations of PF in mice were evaluated by histological assays. Overall, this study explores the therapeutic potential of ANANAS-Dex in the context of lung disorders, providing a promising approach for targeted treatment with controlled drug delivery and potential clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Hexafluorophosphate Azabenzotriazole Tetramethyl Uronium (HATU), N,N-Diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA), triethylamine (TEA), Piperidine, and HCl 4 M in dioxane sand all solvents were purchased from Merck Sigma-Aldrich. Biotin-NHS was purchased from Byosynth; dexamethasone was purchased from Metapharmaceutical. Biotin-Poly-(Ethylene Glycol) (PEG)5kDa-SVA and biotin-PEG5kDa-methoxy (biotin-mPEG) were purchased from Laysan Bio (Huntsville, AL, USA). Avidin was from E.protein (Belgium). NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker Avance III 400 spectrometer (frequencies: 400.13 and 100.62 MHz for 1H, and 13C nuclei, respectively) equipped with a multinuclear inverse z-field gradient probe head (5 mm). For data processing, TopSpin 4.0.8 software was used, and the spectra were calibrated using solvent signal (DMSO 1H 2.50 ppm 13C 39.52, MeOD 1H 4.78 ppm 13C 49.15 ppm). Multiplicities are reported as follows: s, singlet; d, doublet; t, triplet; q, quartet; m, multiplet; b, broad; dd, doublet of doublets. Mass spectra were recorded by direct infusion ESI on a Thermo Fisher Scientific LCQ Fleet ion trap mass spectrometer. Dynamic light scattering measurements were performed with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZS, and UV-Vis spectra were recorded with an Agilent Cary 60 UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Gel permeation chromatography was carried out on a Cytiva Akta purifier Fast Protein Liquid Chromatography (FPLC) system.

Synthesis

The newly synthesized biotin hydrazone drug conjugates (compounds 6 and 7, Figure 1) were obtained through classic synthetic chemistry techniques as detailed in the Supporting Information (S.I.) file. In these conjugates the drug was linked selectively at the carbonyl C3 (see also S.I. file) via a hydrazo-hydrazone (Hz-Hz) linker to a short amino-terminating di-ethylenoxide EO2 spacer (which is more water-soluble than the 6-carbon atom as previously described (Ongaro et al. 2022)). The EO2 conjugate was linked via an amide bond either directly to biotin (compound 6) or to a ω-carboxy, α-biotin-5kDa PEG (leading to compound 7). All newly synthesized compounds were analyzed by High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry (HR-MS), NMR, Reverse Phase Chromatography (RP-HPLC), and UV-Vis spectroscopy. The identity of Compound 7 was verified by a combination of assays, namely by UV-Vis spectroscopy analysis to confirm the presence of the Dex C3-Hz bond (which displays a typical chromophore at 300 nm) and by quantification of biotin (Green 1975), PEG (Sims and Snape 1980) and Dex content. The latter was quantified by reverse phase chromatography (RP-HPLC, Phenomenex Kinetex C18 column, eluent A, H2O + TFA 0.1%; eluent B, 5% A in ACN; gradient from 10% to 95% B in 10 min) following acid-triggered (HCl 0.1 M) release from the conjugate. Biotinamidohexylamido-alexa633 (Biotin-C6-Alexa633) was obtained in DMSO by direct conjugation of Alexa Fluor™633NHS Ester (Invitrogen, 1 equiv.) with biotinamidohexylamine (1 equiv) in the presence of triethylamine (2 equiv) (Bigini et al. 2014).

Schematic representation of the formulations tested and the assembly process. (A) Assembly of core NPs; (B) the biotin-dexamethasone synthesized for this work. The PEG linker in compound 7 has 5 kDa MW (n = 112); (C) functional assembly and generation of ANANAS-Dex; (D) acid-triggered release of the free drug.

Nanoparticle assembly

Core ANANAS containing the minimum amount of 5 kDa methoxy-PEG (12.5% of available biotin binding sites – BBS) to guarantee buffer solubility were prepared according to Pignatto (Pignatto et al. 2010) and freeze-dried after purification. Core NPs were reconstituted in 10 mM phosphate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4 (PBS) buffer, and added the biotinylated elements (compounds 6, 7, or biotin-mPEG) to the desired biotin/BBS molar ratios. When needed, biotin-C6-alexa633 was also added at predefined biotin/BBS molar ratios (ANANAS-Dex-Alexa) (Bigini et al. 2014).

Dexamethasone release

The release of Dex from the EO2-Hz-Hz linker was measured in vitro, at three pH values (100 mM phosphate pH 7.4, 100 mM Na acetate, pH 5.0, and 100 mM Na acetate, pH 4.0). Compound 6 (B-EO2-Hz-Dex, low MW conjugate) was tested both as a free molecule and when linked to the NPs, whereas compound 7 was tested only as a free molecule. When tested as free molecules, the release rate was assessed by UV-Vis spectrophotometry (see also S.I. for method validation). Samples were incubated (at 3·1 0 −5 M) in the selected buffer at the temperature of choice (50 °C, 60 °C and 70 °C) and the disappearance of the Hz-related chromophore was quantified from the intensity of its specific absorption band at 300 nm. The release rate (% Dex release/hour) at 37 °C was then extrapolated from the Arrhenius plot generated with the kinetic constants obtained at the three temperatures. When tethered to the NPs, the release was monitored at 37 °C only, by measuring via RP-HPLC the concentration of the free drug liberated. Before HPLC analysis, the protein fraction was removed from the solution by cold acetone precipitation.

In vitro studies

Human lung adenocarcinoma (A549, cod. ATCC-CCL-185, LGC Standards S.r.L.) cells and human non-tumorigenic bronchial epithelium (BEAS-2B, cod. ATCC-CRL-9609, LGC Standards S.r.L.) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Milan, Italy) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), L-glutamine (Gibco), penicillin/streptomycin (Corning, New York, USA). Cells were maintained in T25 flasks at 37 °C in humidified 5% CO2 and routinely split every 3–4 days.

Cytotoxicity

To evaluate the cytotoxic effect of ANANAS-Dex, A549 cells and BEAS-2B cells were seeded on 96-well plates (2,5 x 103 cells/well) in a complete DMEM medium with 10% FBS. After 24 hours, ANANAS-Dex was added to the cells at final concentrations of 100, 30, 15, 6, and 1 µg/ml. Control cells were treated with the corresponding volume of growth medium. As a positive control, cells were treated with 2% Triton X-100 solution. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere, and their viability was determined 48 hours later. The viability assay was carried out with 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cell viability was determined by measuring the absorbance at 590 nm using Infinite M200 (Tecan).

ROS production

To better understand if the treatment with ANANAS-Dex could alter ROS levels, the level of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a reactive oxygen species (ROS), has been measured through the ROS-Glo™ H2O2 Assay (Promega, Milan, Italy). A549 cells and BEAS-2B cells were seeded on 96-well plates (5 x 103 cells/well) in a complete DMEM medium with 10% FBS. After 24 hours, ANANAS-Dex was added to the cells at final concentrations of 30 and 6 µg/ml. Benserazide 100 µM was added as a positive control. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere for 2 hours, and ROS levels were measured following the manufacturer’s instructions. Luminescence signal corresponding to ROS production was detected using GloMax® Discover Microplate Reader (Promega, Milan, Italy).

Imaging studies

A549 cells and BEAS-2B cells were seeded on 12-well plates (Corning Costar, Arizona, USA) with a ø19 mm cover glass (VWR, Avantor, Pennsylvania, USA) for each well (1 × 104 cells per 1 ml per well) in a complete DMEM medium with 10% FBS. After 24 hours, cells were treated with 30 and 6 µg/ml ANANAS-Dex NPs (1 mg/ml stock solution). Control cells were treated with the corresponding volume of growth medium. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere for 2, 24, and 48 hours. At the different timepoints, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PAF) solution for 10 min at room temperature, and membrane glycoproteins were stained with Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA) 488 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific W11261) diluted 1:200 for 10 min at room temperature. Cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33258 (Sigma-Aldrich 23491–45-4) at 2 μg/ml concentration for 10 min at room temperature. Fluoromount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Merck, Sigma-Aldrich F4680) was used to mount glasses. Images were acquired at the different time points using Confocal microscopy (Nikon A1 Confocal, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification of NPs uptake

The so acquired images were analyzed to quantify and measure the rate of NPs uptake into cells expressed as the ratio of the area occupied by the signal of the NPs and the number of nuclei in that region of interest (ROI). Five randomly selected ROIs for each condition were analyzed through ImageJ software (v.1.53). The extracted ROIs were split into three fluorescence channels. The first channel corresponding to the Hoechst stain was processed through a manual thresholding method to generate an instance segmentation mask for each nucleus. Clustered objects were separated into single nuclei through a shape-based algorithm (Watershed). The resulting multi-label nuclear mask served as a reference for single-cell segmentation. The same threshold procedure was applied to the third channel, related to the Cy5 fluorescence signal of NPs, to obtain a binary mask. Multiple parameters were estimated for each ROI, including the number of nuclei and the area occupied by the Cy5 fluorescence, so that the rate of internalization over time was measured as previously described.

In vivo studies

Twelve-week-old C57BL/6J male mice (25 grams) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Mario Negri Institute for Pharmacological Research IRCCS; they received food and water ad libitum and were regularly checked by a certified veterinarian, responsible for animal welfare supervision and experimental protocol review. All experiments were approved by the IRCCS-IRFMN Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and subsequently approved by the Italian ‘Istituto Superiore di Sanità’ (ID: 558/2021-PR).

Justification for the use of animals: some in vitro studies and in murine models of lung fibrosis associated with inflammation have already provided encouraging indications on the capacity for internalization in macrophages and the anti-inflammatory effect produced by nanocarriers. However, their biodistribution within the cells of the lung parenchyma and/or inflammatory infiltrate cannot be studied using in vitro systems, because they depend on mechanisms such as penetration into the organ, internalization in different cells, organ clearance and systemic absorption. All these considerations make the use of animals necessary. Among the species listed in Annex 1 of D.Lgs no. 26 of 4 March 2014, the mouse is the mammalian species with the least neurological development, in which it is possible to reproduce a model of pulmonary fibrosis, comparable to human IPF. At present, no other inflammation-associated lung fibrosis models are available from species with lower neurodevelopment. Consequently, it is impossible to use another scientifically valid and practically applicable method that does not involve the use of mice. The authors have adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Animal model of pulmonary fibrosis

Fibrosis was induced with a single IN administration of Bleomycin sulfate (TCI, Tokyo − 5 mg/kg in saline 0.9% – BLM). Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 0.4 mg/kg medetomidine and 36 mg/kg ketamine. The wetting gel was applied to the eyes of the animals to prevent ocular damage during IN administration. Mice were subsequently treated with BLM dissolved in saline. The treatment was instilled via a micropipette, 25 µl per nostril, with a total volume of 50 µl. Anesthesia was reversed immediately after administration with atipamezole (0.8 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), and animals were allowed to recover under the heating lamp to minimize distress before being returned to their cages. Body weight and survival were monitored. In this model, the inflammatory phase occurs 7 days post-induction.

In vivo treatments

For the IV biodistribution study in healthy animals, a total of 35 male animals were used. Each experimental group consisted of 16 mice, which received an intravenous injection of either 0.167 mg/kg of Dex in free solution or linked to ANANAS-Dex-Alexa (11.4 mg/kg – ANANAS) dissolved in 200 µl of saline. 3 saline-treated mice were included as an inner control. Animals were euthanized by decapitation, using inhalation anesthesia (5% isoflurane), 30 min, 4, 24, and 48 hours after treatment for ex vivo biodistribution study, histological analysis, and pharmacokinetics.

For the IN biodistribution study in healthy animals, a total of 43 males were used. Each experimental group consisted of 20 mice, which received an intranasal administration of either 0.167 mg/kg of Dex in free solution or linked to ANANAS-Dex-Alexa (11.4 mg/kg – ANANAS) dissolved in 50 µl of saline. The treatment solution was applied in the mice’s nostrils (25 µl per nostril) using a micropipette following anesthesia with medetomidine (0.4 mg/kg) and ketamine (36 mg/kg) injected intraperitoneally. Anesthesia was reversed immediately after administration with atipamezole (0.8 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). 3 saline-treated mice were included as inner controls. Animals were euthanized by decapitation, using inhalation anesthesia (5% isoflurane), at 15 min, 30 min, 2, 24, and 48 hours for ex vivo biodistribution study, histological evaluation, and pharmacokinetics (given the direct delivery of the IN route to the lungs, for this study earlier time points were analyzed compared to those considered for the systemic administration). For the study in fibrotic animals, a total of 43 male animals were used. The experimental design, including IN administration, dosing, and sample collection, was identical to that used for healthy animals. This study started 7 days after fibrosis induction via BLM treatment as detailed in paragraph 2.5.1. For the safety evaluation and biochemical and histopathological analyses methods, see S.I.

Ex vivo fluorescence imaging

Fluorescence images were acquired with an IVIS Lumina III imaging system (PerkinElmer). The following acquisition parameters were used: excitation filter range 680 to 740 nm, emission filter (790 nm, exposure time 2 s), binning factor 4, and f/Stop 2. The liver, kidneys, spleen, lung, and brain were removed and scanned for ex vivo optical imaging. Organs were collected without perfusing the animal, so the signal also refers to their blood vessels. Spectral unmixing, image processing, and analysis were done using Living Image 4.3.1 software (PerkinElmer).

Tissue collection and histological analysis

At the moment of sacrifice, tissues were collected and directly frozen in dry ice. Cryostat sections were cut at 20 μm and mounted on glass slides. Slides were post-fixed in 10% formalin (BioOptica, Italy) for 20 minutes, washed three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min, and incubated for 1 hour with a blocking solution (PBS-Normal Goat Serum, NGS, 10%-Triton X-100 0.1%), then washed again with PBS. For subcellular localization, the antibody anti-CD68 (specific for lysosome and endosome membranes of macrophages) was used: primary rat anti-CD68 monoclonal antibody (1:200, MCA1957, Lot. 155083, Bio-Rad) + Triton X −100 0.1% + NGS 3% in 1X PBS overnight at 4 °C. After washing with 1X PBS, the slides were incubated with the Alexa488 conjugated secondary antibody (1:500, IS20023, Lot. 17C0803, Immunological Science) for 1 hour at room temperature in 1% 1X-NGS PBS solution. After this step, the slides were incubated with the Hoechst nuclear marker 33258 (2 µg/mL in 1X PBS, Sigma Aldrich) for 10 minutes. Fluoromount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Sigma Aldrich) was used for mounting with a cover glass. The slides were then observed using the Virtual Slide Microscopy (Olympus, Japan). For confocal microscopy analysis, samples were acquired using Nikon A1 Confocal microscopes and pseudocolored (blue for Hoechst 33258, green for Alexa Fluor 488, and red for Alexa Fluor 633).

Image processing and quantification

Images were acquired by the Olympus Virtual Slide Microscope VS120-S6-W and were saved in the Olympus-CellSense proprietary file format (.vsi). The so acquired images were analyzed to quantify and measure Pearson’s correlation coefficient ‘r’ between the green signal of the macrophages and the red signal of the NPs. Three 3000 × 3000 pixels (∼1000 µm2) random regions of interest (ROIs) were selected for each time point and experimental condition. These ROIs were exported from QuPath software (Bankhead et al. 2017). Then, ROIs were analyzed through the JaCoP plugin on Fiji ImageJ, an object-based method exploited to assess the co-localization between macrophages and ANANAS NPs. First, the extracted ROIs were imported into ImageJ and split into three fluorescence channels. A manual thresholding procedure was applied to the second channel, related to the Alexa Fluor 488-labeled macrophages, and to the third channel, related to the Cy5 fluorescence signal of ANANAS NPs. The so obtained two images were exported as TIFF files and re-opened in ImageJ to obtain two 8-bit images. We finally exploited the JaCoP plugin and input the two images to measure co-localization and correlation between intensities on a pixel-by-pixel basis. After the first co-localization analysis, we performed a Costes’s randomization to verify the reliability of the data obtained. Multiple parameters were estimated for each ROI, including Person’s coefficient, M1 & M2 coefficients, and Costes’s automatic threshold analysis.

Pharmacokinetics

The levels of Dex were quantified from the lungs, plasma, and liver of both IV- and IN-treated mice were analyzed. Lung and liver tissues were digested with methanol (1:2 w/v) and acetonitrile (1:1 w/v), stirred, and sonicated for 20 min. Then water (1:10 w/v) was added, and stirring and sonication were repeated. The resulting samples were centrifuged at 7000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was further cleaned up with solid-phase extraction using Sep-Pak C18 1 cc Vac Cartridges, conditioned before use with 1 mL of methanol, followed by 1 mL of water. Samples were loaded on the SPE columns and passed through dropwise. Then cartridges were rinsed with 1 mL of water/acetone (80:20) and then with 1 mL of water before drying the columns under vacuum for 5 min. Samples were eluted with 1.8 mL of acetonitrile into glass receiving tubes. Plasma aliquots were directly eluted with acetonitrile (1:4 v/v) and centrifuged at 7000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. All samples were evaporated to remove the organic phase. Just before analysis, they were suspended in 100 μL of 0.05% acetic acid/acetonitrile (80:20) in autosampler vials. The internal standards to measure the Dex in organs utilized and Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) and Tandem Mass Spectrometry (MS/MS) analyses were performed as previously described (Violatto et al. 2019; Ongaro et al. 2022).

Data calculation & statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were done using GraphPad Prism version 10 for Windows (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SE). The differences between groups were compared using one-way ANOVA analysis, followed by Sidak’s image processing and quantification, Tukey’s for pharmacokinetics study, or Dunnet’s post hoc test analysis. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant and reported on graphs. Grubb’s test was used to detect and remove outliers. For in vivo experiments, the number of animals sacrificed at each data point was minimized according to the 3Rs principle.

Results

Design of the biotin dexamethasone conjugates for ANANAS decoration

ANANAS drug loading relies on reversible biotinylated drug conjugates that bind to avidin-biotin binding sites (BBS), positioning the drug on the nanoparticle surface (Ongaro et al. 2022). The drug’s distance from the protein core depends on the spacer length between biotin and the drug moiety (Figure 1). A short-spaced biotin linker places the drug near the protein surface (inner layer), while a long-spaced linker (e.g. 5 kDa PEG) positions it on the outer layer.

Results generated with first and second generation ANANAS-Dex formulations administered parenterally (Violatto et al. 2019; Ongaro et al. 2022) showed that that (a) only hydrazide-hydrazone (Hz-Hz) but not carbamate hydrazide-hydrazone (Cb-Hz) bonds allow acid-selective release of the drug; (b) the chemical environment surrounding the Dex-hydrazone link, which depends also on its distance from the NP protein core, affects the conjugate hydrolytic stability and shorter spacers display longer lasting drug release profiles; (c) the presence of a 6-carbon atom spacer at the ω end of the 5 kDa PEG reduces the conjugate solubility and has a negative impact on the polymer ability to colloid protection; (d) the ratio between the number of methoxy- or Dex ω-ending 5 kDa biotin-PEGs at the NP surface affects the NP biodistribution pattern and the interaction with macrophages: a higher percentage of methoxy-ending PEGs make the NPs more stealth, while higher amounts of ω-Dex-ending PEGs favor the interaction with tissue resident macrophages, positive for CD68 staining.

Keeping the above information in mind, a novel set of short- and long-spaced biotin-hydrazo-hydrazone (Hz-Hz)-Dex conjugates was designed and synthesized to generate third-generation ANANAS-Dex (Figure 1) with optimized features. Selective linkage of the drug at the carbonyl C3 was confirmed by NMR 1H-d6DMSO spectroscopy (Violatto et al. 2019). The identity and purity (94%) of the products were confirmed by 1H-NMR and Mass Spectrometry (S.I. file). The UV-Vis spectra show the formation of an absorption band with a maximum at 300 nm (Figure 2), typical of the Dex-Hz conjugates at the drug C3 carbonyl (Ongaro et al. 2022), with a molar extinction value of about 20.000 M − 1 cm − 1 at neutral pH. When purified by flash chromatography (Silica gel F60), compound 6 elutes as two distinct products, which were initially isolated and identified as the hydrazone cis/trans isomers (Figure S1 in S.I.). Following characterization, it was demonstrated that the two isomers have superimposable behavior in aqueous media (Figures S1 and S3 in S.I.), therefore, they were used in a mixture in all experiments.

pH-dependent release of dexamethasone

The pH-dependent hydrolytic stability of the EO2-Hz-Dex linker was verified by testing compounds 6 and 7 at pH 7.4, 5.0, and 4.0 (Table 1 and Figure S4 in S.I.). We observed high stability at neutral or slightly acidic pH (pH 5.0) and slow but sustained drug release at pH 4.0 (6% hydrolysis/hour), with no difference between the Cis and Trans isomers (Figure S3 in S.I.). Tethering the short-spaced biotin conjugate to the ANANAS core improved the hydrolytic stability (about 10 times higher with respect to the free molecule). The results are almost superimposable to those obtained with a biotin-Hz-Hz-dexamethasone conjugate having a 5-carbon atom aliphatic spacer instead of the EO2 (Ongaro et al. 2022). In that work, it was demonstrated that the presence of the 5kDaPEG between biotin and the C5-Hz-Hz-Dex bond does not affect the hydrolysis pattern and that the buffering effect exerted by attachment to core NPs occurs in short-spaced but not in PEG-spaced conjugates. Therefore, compound 7 was not tested when tethered to the NPs, and it is expected to behave similarly when free in solution.

Pre-formulation studies

The ideal formulation should ensure high Dex loading, controlled release, and colloidal stability. Based on these criteria, we conducted pre-formulation experiments to determine the best composition for in vivo testing (Ongaro et al. 2022). For maximum drug loading, all NP BBS should bind to a Dex-biotin conjugate. In core NPs, 12.5% of BBS are occupied by biotin-PEG5kDa-methoxy for colloidal stability, leaving 87.5% available for other biotinylated elements (Pignatto et al. 2010). However, considering that steric factors may hamper the access of large MW biotin conjugates (as the PEG derivatives) (Morpurgo et al. 2012), and the potential negative effects on colloidal stability exerted by hydrophobic moieties at the NP surface (Ongaro et al. 2022), it is fundamental to experimentally assess both the loading capability for the different biotin conjugates and the colloidal stability of the functionalized NPs.

To optimize the NP Dex loading, we initially quantified the maximal capability of core NPs for each of the two biotin-Dex conjugates. To this end, core NPs were mixed with different amounts of either compounds 6 or 7, and the number of unbound conjugates was quantified by gel permeation chromatography (Figure 3). Concurrently, the colloidal stability of the mixtures was assessed by performing dynamic light scattering measurements one hour after mixing and 24 and 48 hours later (Figure 3D and Table S1). The maximum capability for the high MW and low MW conjugates was reached at about 27% or 60% of the total BBS, respectively, confirming that steric hindrance plays a significant role in dictating NP loading when using bulky biotin conjugates. The addition of the large PEG conjugate did not affect colloidal stability, while the presence of the low MW conjugate alone made the system colloidally unstable.

A second set of experiments was carried out by mixing core NPs with both compounds 6 and 7 at 57.5% and 30% of total BBS, respectively. In these conditions, a fraction of both conjugates remained free in solution (data not shown). Notably, no aggregation was observed in the mixture, indicating that the large PEG conjugate is capable of conferring colloidal stabilization also in the presence of the partially destabilizing compound 6.

Finally, a formulation loaded at 25% BBS with compound 7 and 40% BBS with compound 6 was tested, showing full retention of the two biotin conjugates (Figure 3C). This formulation (ANANAS-Dex) was selected for the in vivo experiments. In this formulation, each NP carries about 870 molecules of Dex, which corresponds to 15.8 µg of Dex/mg of carrier. A second formulation was also optimized for the biodistribution studies in which fluorescent biotin-C6-alexa633, necessary for tracking, was added at 5% BBS coverage to the detriment of compound 6, which was added at 35% BBS coverage. In this case, each NP carries about 800 Dex moieties, corresponding to 14.6 µg Dex/mg of the carrier. Based on the composition and the hydrolytic stability of the two components, we calculated the drug release rate at pH 4.0 from these formulations to be approximately 2.7% and 2.5%/h, respectively.

In vitro preliminary studies

To ensure the preliminary safety of the novel ANANAS-Dex formulation, a series of in vitro assays on both A549 and BEAS-2B cells were conducted to evaluate their cytotoxicity and biocompatibility, through the evaluation of viability, cell health, and internalization of the NPs.

Cells were treated with different concentrations (1–100 μg/ml) of NPs, and their viability was monitored for 48 hours. Treatment with the different doses did not cause a significant reduction in the cell viability in both A549 and BEAS-2B cells (Figure 4A), indicating that the nanoformulation did not affect the cell growth and metabolic activity of these cells.

To better understand not only the viability, but also the effective health of the cells, ROS levels were measured in cells treated with two selected concentrations of ANANAS-Dex: 6 and 30 μg/ml. ROS are indeed beneficial to the cell as natural byproducts of metabolism and have roles in cell signaling, but they can also lead to cellular damage and oxidative stress (Liguori et al. 2018). Through the analysis of the production of ROS, we observed no alteration in the oxidative stress of the cells treated compared to the control ones, at any of the concentrations tested, suggesting that the treatment does not affect the levels of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and consequently the health of the cells (Figure 4B).

Subsequently, we selected the concentration of 6 μg/ml for the study of cellular internalization. Representative images of the uptake of fluorescent ANANAS-Dex in both cell lines are reported in Figure 4C,E. In this condition, at a concentration not inducing cytotoxicity, the incubation time affects the amount of signal inside the cells. We indeed observed no internalization at the earlier timepoint of 2 hours of incubation, and an initial uptake at 24 hours that increased rapidly at 48 hours. In both cell lines, the red signal of the NPs after 24 hours of treatment is very low and spread in the whole cytoplasm of the cells, but at 48 hours a higher and perinuclear red signal is observed, thus indicating an efficient retrograde intracellular transport of the NPs from the cell membranes (green signal) toward the nuclei (blue signal) (Shafaq-Zadah et al. 2020). Although the progressive increase of fluorescence inside the cells, furthermore confirmed by the quantitative analysis (Figure 4D,F), the staining remained confined to the cytoplasmic region and did not overlap with the nuclear area, clearly suggesting that the NPs are not able to cross the nuclear membrane. As shown in the quantitative analysis in Figure 4D,F, A549 cells had a slightly lower internalization rate than BEAS-2B cells, especially at 48 hours of incubation.

ANANAS and dex distribution in healthy mice

Before proceeding with the biodistribution study, a series of safety evaluations were performed to assess the potential toxic effects of prolonged systemic administration in mice of the novel ANANAS-Dex. Throughout the treatment period, the animals were closely monitored for any signs of adverse effects, including changes in behavior, bodyweight, and overall health status (Figure S5). In addition, comprehensive hematological analyses, including total blood cell counts, were conducted to detect possible alterations. Serum biochemical parameters were measured to evaluate liver and kidney function, as well as general metabolic status (Figures S6 and S7). Finally, histopathological examinations of major organs were carried out to identify any microscopic tissue alterations (Figure S8). No toxic effects were observed in any of the parameters assessed, indicating that the compound was well tolerated under the experimental conditions.

The biodistribution of the novel ANANAS-Dex upon IV or IN administration was determined in healthy mice. Similar to what was observed with earlier ANANAS-Dex generations (Bigini et al. 2014; Violatto et al. 2019; Ongaro et al. 2022), upon IV administration (Figure 5) this formulation also displayed rapid and marked hepatic accumulation and reduced tropism toward other filter organs. The IVIS images showed an intense signal in the liver, which decayed slowly over time, while the signal in other organs was manifold lower and decreased more rapidly. The pattern observed in the brain, spleen, and lungs likely accounts for the NPs present in the plasma and does not suggest any accumulation in these organs. The longer-lasting signal in the kidneys was in line with the progressive clearance and excretion via urine (Figure 5A) of either the entire NPs or their degradation products (Figure 5A). The longitudinal observation was confirmed by the group-averaged signals of individual organs (Figure 5C–G). The liver displayed the highest signal intensities at all timepoints, confirming that this tissue is the main target for the NPs when administered parenterally. Thirty minutes after administration, the organ-to-liver signal intensity ratios were approximately 0.5 for the lungs, 0.7 for the kidneys, and 0.2 for the spleen and brain. Four hours after administration, the liver had retained 58% of the 30-minute signal, the brain and spleen (53–54%), the kidneys 35%, and the lungs 31%. The rapid decrease of the NP-related signal in the lungs excludes the possibility of their deep penetration into this organ parenchyma and suggests their location within the lung vasculature (the capillaries that promote gas exchange and the venules that transport the circulating elements).

When ANANAS-Dex was administered intranasally, a totally different biodistribution pattern was observed. The IVIS images showed that in this case (Figure 5B), the NP-associated signal concentrated almost exclusively in the lungs, with an average intensity (Figure 5I) that is markedly higher than that registered in the same tissue upon IV treatment (1010 vs.108). Unlike the systemic administration route, interaction with this organ is strong and persistent. The NP-related signal in the lungs decayed very slowly, with 35% and 23% of the maximum signal retained 24 and 48 hours after administration, respectively. The group-averaged signal intensities (Figure 5H–L) showed transient but significant penetration in the brain and a very low but longer-lasting presence in the kidneys and liver. At the liver, a NP-related signal was observed already at the first time point (15 minutes after administration) and remained steady for up to 24 hours. Its intensity was about 200-fold lower than in the lungs, and about 10-fold less compared to the parenteral administration. Interestingly, the NP-related signal in the brain followed a similar pattern to that in the lungs, albeit with a 100-fold lower intensity. Following the IN route, brain penetration can occur via two main routes distinct from the blood-brain barrier passage. The first is through the nasal epithelium of olfactory mucosa and subsequent retrieval transport by vomeronasal organs; the second is by direct uptake of nanometric particles by the synaptic end-plate of olfactory nerve terminals.

As expected, the biodistribution data show that the NP tropism is strongly influenced by the route of administration. The parenteral route led to an almost selective liver accumulation, whereas the IN one led to lung penetration. We examined the histological tissue sections to elucidate the NP localization within these organs. In line with the IVIS data (Figure 5E) and with earlier studies with first and second generation ANANAS-Dex administered parenterally (Violatto et al. 2019), this new formulation penetrated the liver parenchyma rapidly (Figure S9), where it was captured by Kupffer cells. In line with the IVIS signal, the NPs displayed a long persistence at this organ as their signal was still present 24 hours after administration. Similarly, the lung histology of mice sacrificed at different time points after IN administration confirmed the rapid accumulation of ANANAS-Dex in the pulmonary parenchyma. The time-resolved analysis showed that initially (between 15 minutes and 2 hours post-administration), the NPs diffused within the lung parenchyma and later migrated toward the alveoli. Although imaging cannot quantitatively determine the fraction of NPs that remain in the airways and penetrate the lung parenchyma, reaching the alveoli, it is arguable that this kind of tropism could play a key role in several therapeutic strategies. Co-localization studies revealed that the presence of ANANAS in lung macrophages progressively increased over time until the end of our observations (48 hours after administration). The higher-magnified confocal images (Figure 6) confirmed the uptake by resident macrophages over time. Notably, the NP-related staining decreased with time, in line with ex vivo fluorescent signal quantification (Figure 5I).

The biodistribution study offers insights into the carrier’s ability to penetrate and accumulate in specific organs or cells and the kinetics of removal. However, this behavior does not necessarily mirror the fate of the cargo drug. The pharmacokinetic profile of the steroid in the lungs, liver, and plasma of animals treated (both IV and IN) with ANANAS-Dex was thus measured and compared to that of the unformulated drug by quantifying free Dex in these tissues using reverse phase chromatography (Figure 7).

As regards the drug administered in the free form, the pharmacokinetic profile following IV administration showed a rapid diffusion of the drug in all body compartments, as directly observed in the liver (Figure 7C) and lungs (Figure 7A), and a drop over time, which was faster in plasma than in the other organs (plasma and tissue levels dropped below the limit of detection 4 and 24 hours after administration, respectively).

Also, the IN administration resulted in widespread distribution of the drug throughout the body, with levels comparable to those achieved after IV injection, even at early timepoints. This rapid absorption can occur directly during instillation, by penetrating the nasal capillaries, or after reaching the lungs, likely from the alveolar structures. The high Dex levels in the lungs (Figure 7D – grey line) indicate an efficient passage through the respiratory tree and penetration across the alveolo-capillary barrier. An increase in drug levels between 30 minutes and 2 hours after treatment was observed, as well as an overall greater drug persistence compared to the IV administration. This difference is compatible with some ‘depot’ effect at the inhalation site. Overall, the results obtained with the free drug demonstrate that both administration routes induce rapid blood absorption and off-target biodistribution.

A totally different Dex kinetics was observed when the drug was administered within the ANANAS-Dex formulation. Upon IV administration, the free drug was found only in the liver but not in the lungs or plasma. This finding is consistent with the IVIS organ scanning, which showed the NP-associated fluorescent signal almost exclusively in the hepatic tissue.

Interestingly, the presence of the drug in the liver was long-lasting, with 30% (9 ng/kg) and 13% (4 ng/kg) of the 4 hours peak (30 ng/kg) concentration still present 24 and 48 hours after administration, respectively. In addition, the NP-related signal peaked earlier (30 minutes, Figure 5D) than the free Dex levels (4 hours, Figure 7D). This discrepancy is in line with the mechanism of drug release from the NPs, which requires an acidic environment and therefore their penetration into lysosomes. Indeed, as observed by histology (Figure S9), there was a latency between the penetration of ANANAS into liver parenchyma and its internalization into the phagocytic lysosomes. Moreover, no free drug was detected in plasma (Figure 7B,E), independently of the route of administration, thus confirming the stability of the hydrazone bonds when the particles circulate in this neutral pH compartment.

Dex levels in the liver of mice treated IN with ANANAS-Dex showed a slow but progressive increase over time, reaching a peak value of about 20 ng/g 24 hours after administration and then declining to zero at 48 hours (Figure 7F – green line). Drug levels in the lungs displayed a more rapid kinetic profile but were lower than those in the liver, peaking at just over 3 ng/g at 30 minutes post-treatment (Figure 7D – green line).

The evidence of a delay in drug accumulation kinetics between the lung and the liver suggests that ANANAS-Dex may not penetrate lung macrophages but could enter the circulation and be taken up by the liver. However, the absence of the NP-related fluorescent signal suggests a rapid accumulation of free drug that, due to its lipophilic nature, enters the bloodstream, from where it freely diffuses to other organs, including the liver. The extensive penetration of ANANAS into the lysosomes of perialveolar macrophages (Figure 6, right panel) further supports this hypothesis, reinforcing previous findings of ANANAS-Dex efficient internalization into the target tissue macrophages (Violatto et al. 2019). The anatomical localization of lung macrophages in healthy mice, positioned in close contact between alveolar and capillary surfaces, combined with the endothelial permeability of the gas exchange structures, facilitates the rapid absorption of Dex into the bloodstream. Therefore, the higher levels of the steroid in the liver compared to the lungs likely depend on this organ’s greater capacity to retain and metabolize the drug. However, it is not possible to exclude that lung-infiltrated macrophages migrate from the lungs into the bloodstream toward the liver, transporting previously ingested NPs and releasing Dex once they reach hepatic tissue for metabolism. Another hypothesis is that small amounts of NPs, once instilled in the nose, bypass the trachea and bronchial tree and reach the gastrointestinal tract. This would allow direct passage from the intestine to the liver through the portal system, avoiding systemic circulation and the detection of the drug in plasma (Figure 7E). Anyway, the IN administration of the drug, loaded in ANANAS, led to a massive penetration of both the nanocarrier and payload into the target organ.

ANANAS and dex distribution in fibrotic mice

The experiments on healthy animals clearly showed the superiority of the IN route when the lungs are the targets for pharmacological action. This further study was relevant for understanding how pathological conditions can modify the kinetics of drugs and nano-drugs and, above all, for assessing the possible exploitation of IN administration of ANANAS-Dex for future translational research (Moeller et al. 2008; B. Moore et al. 2013; Della Latta et al. 2015; Li et al. 2022).

Figure 8 shows the results of the IVIS scanning of the organs of fibrotic animals, at 7 days post BLM administration, treated intranasally (Figure 8A) with ANANAS-Dex. The pattern was similar to that observed in healthy littermates (Figure 5B,H–L), with the nanoassemblies rapidly penetrating the lungs and declining slowly over time. Notably, the NP-related signal intensity was similar to that detected in healthy animals (Figure 8B). This efficient pulmonary entry confirms the ability of the NPs to move along the airways and to penetrate the lung barrier despite the morpho-functional modifications occurring in fibrotic mice (S.-N. Kim et al. 2010). The NP signal in the lungs diminished to some extent faster when compared to healthy mice. This may be related to a more efficient removal by activated macrophages (Libório-Ramos et al. 2023). In the lungs, the NP signal reached its maximum 15 minutes after treatment, slightly earlier than in healthy animals, and then it decreased slowly and was still present at the end of the observation. The measurements at 24 and 48 hours after administration decayed by 32% and 15% of the maximum signal retained, respectively.

Similar to what was observed in healthy animals, the ANANAS-related signal was found in the brain of BLM-treated mice, following a similar pattern to that in the lungs. However, it was about 100-fold lower compared to healthy littermates (Figure 8C). No NP-related signal was found in the liver (Figure 8D) or spleen (Figure 8E). The lack of fluorescence in the kidneys of diseased mice (Figure 8F) slightly differs from what was observed in healthy mice, in which a low but detectable signal was found (Figure 5L). This little difference could be due to a more efficient degradation of nanoassemblies in the lung parenchyma.

The co-localization study between ANANAS (red signal) and macrophages (CD68 – green signal) showed progressive penetration into the lungs (Figure 9A) and interaction at the single-cell level (Figure 9B). Overall, the staining pattern was similar to that observed in healthy mice (Figure 6). However, while no significant difference between healthy and BLM-treated animals could be visualized 24 hours after administration, the higher magnification images showed the presence of NP-related red signal in macrophages of fibrotic mice at earlier time points (30 minutes after administration) compared to healthy animals (Figure 9B), suggesting a faster interaction with these cells in the inflamed condition. In Figure S10 (S.I. file), it is evident that Iba1-positive cells in the lungs of BLM-treated mice exhibited marked hypertrophy. This morphological characteristic is strongly linked to enhanced uptake ability in response to an inflammatory state. Additionally, the significant alteration of lung parenchyma observed in Figure S10 (S.I. file) through Haematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) staining further supports the hypothesis of a pro-inflammatory induction in treated mice. The passage from the airways to the lung macrophages involves different cell populations, such as epithelial and endothelial cells. As previously stated, the marked alteration of pulmonary cytoarchitecture in fibrotic mice may influence both the kinetics and the rate of internalization. Figure S10 shows that in mice treated with BLM, there is an initial inflammatory and immunomodulatory process followed by progressive fibrotic changes in the lung tissue. The timing of the start of treatment with ANANAS-Dex is crucial for its effectiveness. It is indeed important to reach the target in an M1-like phase where the septa and alveoli present an important inflammatory infiltrate attracted by the macrophages. However, starting treatment at a more advanced stage of the pathogenesis, where macrophages become pro-fibrotic, could reduce the effectiveness of alveolar penetration and anti-inflammatory effects.

To quantify these two processes, the co-localization of macrophage- and NP-related signals was measured by Pearson’s coefficient (Figure 9C). Progressive co-localization occurred in both healthy and inflamed tissues. Fibrotic mice showed a greater macrophage penetration at the earlier time points (up to 2 hours), while, later on, a marked accumulation of NP in healthy mice led to a greater co-localization index in this group compared to the pathological one. The lower NP:macrophage co-localization in the lungs of BLM-treated mice 24 and 48 hours, compared to the healthy group, may be due to the massive cell infiltrates, hyperproliferation of fibroblasts, and ECM deposition occurring in pathological conditions.

The different stability and persistence of ANANAS in the lungs, as well as the presence of pathological tissue, could profoundly alter the drug release from the carrier and its availability in the lungs, bloodstream, and other organs of fibrotic mice. The levels of the free steroid measured in lungs (A), plasma (B), and liver (C) of the fibrotic mice treated IN with either Dex alone (grey lines) or combined with ANANAS (blue lines) are shown in Figure 10. After administration of the free drug, fibrotic mice had similar plasma levels to those observed in healthy mice, but markedly lower amounts in both lungs and liver, which also diminished more rapidly than in the healthy group (Figure 10B). The reason why diseased mice showed lower and less prolonged accumulation of Dex in the lungs with respect to the healthy siblings cannot be ascribed to a higher penetration into the bloodstream, since the plasmatic levels of the two groups were similar (Figures 7D and 10A - grey lines). Therefore, changes in the lung parenchyma associated with fibrosis may affect the path of drug absorption when in the free form, in the end reducing its residence time in this organ.

A different situation occurred when administering the drug within the nanoformulations. In this case, the levels of free drug in the lungs were much higher than in the healthy group. In particular, 15 minutes after IN administration, the free drug level in the lungs was 40 ng/ml compared to 0.5 ng/ml in healthy mice (80-fold higher), and also at the subsequent timepoints, it was significantly (5–15-fold) higher than in healthy mice.

In healthy animals, no free Dex was found in the plasma after receiving ANANAS-Dex. However, a small amount of free drug (4.5 ng/mL) was briefly detected in the plasma of diseased animals, but this was only observed at 30 minutes. This may indicate a faster degradation of ANANAS-Dex in the inflamed pulmonary lysosomes with the consequent release of small amounts of the drug into the bloodstream. This transient level correlates well with the decrease in the NP-related fluorescence signal observed between 30 minutes and 2 hours after instillation (Figure 8A,B). The presence of the steroid in the plasma of ANANAS-Dex-treated mice is followed by its later increase in the liver (Figure 10C). However, even if more persistent, Dex levels in this organ were not significantly higher than those measured in healthy mice (Figure 7F).

Notably, the pharmacokinetics of Dex administered in solution showed an opposite trend; particularly in the inflamed model, the drug levels in all analyzed tissues were lower than those registered in healthy animals and diminished more quickly. Overall, these data indicate that the inflamed/fibrotic condition reduces Dex bioavailability when administered in solution but increases when administered using the NP formulation. This nanoassembly can release higher and longer-lasting levels of the drug at the targeted tissue. This could potentially improve therapeutic effectiveness and/or extend the intervals between repeated administrations.

Discussion

The general objective of this study was to evaluate the potential of ANANAS-Dex for the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis, with the main hypothesis that if the carrier can concentrate in the inflamed lung, it could release the drug locally. This goal was addressed by a multi-fold strategy: on one hand, we evaluated if and how ANANAS-Dex can reach the lungs following administration via either the parenteral or IN route and how the disease state of the lung affects this parameter. At the same time, by capitalizing the structure/functionality relationships obtained in previous studies, we aimed at optimizing a third generation formulation which, while maintaining strong tropism for tissue macrophages, could release the active payload more slowly than previous generations and thus fully exploit the long-lasting tissue residence of the carrier.

In vitro and in vivo studies carried out on A549 and BEAS-2B cells and on healthy animals confirmed the goodness of the design and the safety of the new formulation. Similar to the previous generations of ANANAS-Dex, this new formulation also concentrates the NPs in the macrophages of the target tissues. When comparing the levels of the drug in the liver obtained here upon IV administration with those of earlier generations (Violatto et al. 2019; Ongaro et al. 2022) administered via the same route, the new formulation results in higher and longer-lasting (up to at least 48 hours) amounts of free drug. Although qualitatively this new formulation does not differ from the previous ones, from a quantitative point of view, higher and even longer-lasting liver levels of the drug have been achieved. This is in line with the greater hydrolytic stability of the new biotin-Hz-Hz-Dex conjugates observed in the in vitro drug release assays and with a potentially higher tropism for the liver macrophages promoted by the Dex moieties exposed at the outer surface of the NPs (Ongaro et al. 2022).

The results also clearly showed that only the use of the nanocarrier is capable of preventing off-target drug release, while the IN – but not the parenteral – route is capable of bringing the NPs to the lung.

Notably, unformulated Dex, even when administered by the most direct IN route, was unable to localize at the lungs as, indeed, its levels in plasma and liver were similar to those generated by parenteral administration. On the contrary, IN administration of the nanoassembly led to almost exclusive localization in the lungs of both the carrier and the drug. It is widely known that inhalation therapy with steroids is currently used in pathologies such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, and therefore, this approach could be extended to a broader range of pulmonary disorders (Raissy et al. 2013).

The high potential of this nanoformulation further stemmed when comparing the biodistribution and drug kinetics obtained in healthy and inflamed animals treated intranasally. While the BLM-induced mouse model is widely used in PF research, it does not fully replicate the chronic, progressive pathology characteristic of human PF and often self-resolving course. Nevertheless, for this study in diseased animals, the BLM model was chosen due to its ability to induce reproducible fibrotic changes within a defined timeframe, its capacity to mimic key inflammatory features of early-stage fibrotic disease, and its suitability for evaluating therapeutic interventions under controlled experimental conditions (Moore and Hogaboam 2008).

Unformulated Dex led to lung drug levels that were lower in diseased animals than in healthy ones, suggesting the access to the lung parenchyma is impaired in the inflamed situation; on the contrary, administration of the nanoformulated drug led to significantly greater drug levels in diseased lungs compared to healthy ones. Notably, this result correlates with a more rapid uptake of NPs by the pulmonary macrophages in inflamed mice and a more rapid decrease of carrier-related signals (IVIS data) from this tissue. Scarring tissue might represent thus a barrier to the diffusion of NP and their uptake by resident alveolar macrophages. It is arguable whether steroids could have a negative impact on resident cells such as fibroblasts. There is evidence suggesting that steroids, particularly Dex, affect fibroblast proliferation both in vitro and in vivo (Kraft et al. 2001). While we cannot exclude the possibility that ANANAS penetration is specific to macrophages, the time course demonstrated in Figure 9 clearly indicates a predominant interaction with these cells. In several processes of fibrosis associated with inflammation, including the model of PF induced by BLM (Libório-Ramos et al. 2023), resting macrophages undergo a marked phenotypical change. Among these changes, it is well-described that hypertrophic-activated lysosomes fuze with phagosomes and other structures dedicated to efficient digestion of their contents, typical of M1-like Cd68+ cells (Sica and Mantovani 2012; Sica et al. 2014). Therefore, we cannot exclude that the lower NP:macrophage co-localization could be due to a more intense and rapid lysosomal activity in the inflamed and fibrotic pathological tissue (Bernardotto et al. 2023).

We thus hypothesize that the transition from resting to activated macrophages may enhance their interaction and internalization of ANANAS-Dex, leading to the subsequent release of the drug in the inflamed tissue (Figure 11). Overall, this result strongly supports the advantage of using this nanoformulation in the pathological context, whereas it is markedly detrimental in mice treated with the free drug.

Conclusions

In summary, we showed that ANANAS-Dex, when administered intranasally is a promising drug delivery system for pulmonary fibrosis. While the present study focused on a single dose (acute) treatment, several endpoints could be explored in future studies to test the efficacy of a chronic therapy. In principle, the results obtained here could be translated into an inhalation therapy for treating diseases that require direct lung delivery with the advantage of reduced drug dosage and dosing frequency, reducing the risk of steroid-associated systemic side effects and improving patient compliance. Notably, earlier studies showed that this class of biodegradable, biocompatible NPs exhibit low immunogenicity and no toxicity upon repeated parenteral administration (Violatto et al. 2019). The precise control of physicochemical parameters achieved through high-affinity interactions that drive functional assembly formation holds promise for future translation from preclinical to clinical applications.

To minimize animal distress, the wetting gel was applied to the eyes of the animals to prevent ocular damage during instillation, and they were allowed to recover under the heating lamp at the end of the procedures before being returned to their cages.

Justification for the use of animals: some in vitro studies and in murine models of lung fibrosis associated with inflammation have already provided encouraging indications on the capacity for internalization in macrophages and the anti-inflammatory effect produced by nanocarriers. However, their biodistribution within the cells of the lung parenchyma and/or inflammatory infiltrate cannot be studied using in vitro systems, because they depend on mechanisms such as penetration into the organ, internalization in different cells, organ clearance and systemic absorption. All these considerations make the use of animals necessary. Among the species listed in Annex 1 of D.Lgs no. 26 of 4 March 2014, the mouse is the mammalian species with the least neurological development, in which it is possible to reproduce a model of pulmonary fibrosis, comparable to human IPF. At present, no other inflammation-associated lung fibrosis models are available from species with lower neurodevelopment. Consequently, it is impossible to use another scientifically valid and practically applicable method that does not involve the use of mice.

121 twelve-week-old C57BL/6J male mice (25 grams) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and acclimatized for seven days before the experiments, maintained in conditions of 22 °C–25 °C and 50% relative humidity, and 12 h day/night cycles. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Mario Negri Institute for Pharmacological Research IRCCS; they received food and water ad libitum and were regularly checked by a certified veterinarian, responsible for animal welfare supervision and experimental protocol review.

The mice were anesthetized with medetomidine-ketamine (intraperitoneal injection of 0.4 mg/kg medetomidine and 36 mg/kg ketamine) for the IN treatments. Anesthesia was reversed immediately after administration with atipamezole (0.8 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). At the end of the study, all mice were euthanized using inhalation anesthesia (5% isoflurane) followed by decapitation.
