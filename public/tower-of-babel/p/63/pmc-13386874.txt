Domestic pig skin peptides: targeted starvation therapy for gram-positive bacteria MRSA through ABC transporters
Authors: Qing Zhang, Shuteng Huang, Kairu Che, Zhicheng Yin, Xiaohan Wang, Lei Zhang, Yongqiang Lin, Desheng Cai, Jiayu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), which were conserved constituents of the innate immune system, exhibit antimicrobial properties similar to those of traditional antibiotics and have a low potential for the development of resistance. Thus, they were showing a great capacity for application. This study performed screening of twelve candidate antimicrobial peptides by means of pig skin enzymatic hydrolysis and activity prediction. The synthesis of these peptides was undertaken using Fmoc solid phase synthesis and purified via HPLC than 95%. The Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), Staphylococcus aureus (SA) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) were selected to evaluate antibacterial activity in vitro. The results indicated that peptide P5 exhibited the most significant inhibitory effect on MRSA and SA, with an inhibition rate of 91.83%. Furthermore, its antibacterial activity against MRSA and SA was found to be stronger than that against E. coli, demonstrating a dose-dependent relationship. Growth curve analysis revealed that P5 completely inhibited the growth of MRSA within 16 h at 3 mg/mL. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the CCK-8 assay and live/dead cell staining. The survival rates of both L929 and MDCK cells exceeded 87%, indicating good biocompatibility. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analyses demonstrated that treatment with the P5 was associated with significant transcriptional changes in MRSA. The differentially expressed genes were enriched in the ABC transporter pathway, as well as pathways related to arginine biosynthesis and metabolism, histidine metabolism, and quorum sensing. These findings suggest a potential link between P5 and these pathways, thereby providing a foundation for future mechanistic studies. In conclusion, the porcine skin-derived antimicrobial peptide P5 exhibited significant antimicrobial activity, low cytotoxicity, and a mechanism of action that was distinct from traditional antibiotics, thereby demonstrating promising potential for clinical translation in the fight against drug-resistant bacterial infections. Introduction

The escalating issue of Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) represented one of the most formidable challenges to global public health in the 21st century [1, 2]. MRSA was a significant concern due to its high prevalence in both hospital and community settings, coupled with its strong pathogenicity, which resulted in increasingly limited options for effective treatment [3, 4]. This highlighted the urgent need to develop novel antimicrobial agents with unique mechanisms of action to circumvent existing resistance pathways.

For an extended period, the discovery of new antibiotics has predominantly depended on synthetic chemistry and the screening of soil microorganisms [5]; however, this methodology had gradually become exhausted. In this context, AMPs have emerged as a highly promising class of therapeutic candidates. Also referred to as host defense peptides, AMPs represent a highly conserved component of the innate immune system across animals, plants, and insects [6, 7]. AMPs typically exhibited broad-spectrum activity against bacteria [8, 9]. Some AMPs could kill bacteria by affecting intracellular metabolic activities without compromising membrane integrity [10]. Unlike traditional antibiotics that target specific proteins or biochemical pathways, this mechanism made it difficult for bacteria to develop resistance. Research indicates that AMPs exert their antibacterial effects through dual modes of action: membrane-mediated and non-membrane-mediated pathways [11]. Certain AMPs form transient pores in the membrane, enabling them to enter the cell and act intracellularly [12]. Others induce bacterial death by binding to the membrane without causing structural disruption, ultimately leading to cell demise [10]. Their antimicrobial activity is largely determined by net positive charge and hydrophobic amino acids, enabling selective binding to negatively charged bacterial membranes [13, 14]. T phenylalanine (F) and tryptophan (W), which enhance membrane interaction through hydrophobic side chains [15, 16]. Proline (P), which can be imported into the cytoplasm via the SbmA transporter to inhibit protein synthesis without compromising membrane integrity [17].

Research on novel antimicrobial peptides was increasingly focusing on natural sources, particularly those derived from animals adapted to pathogen-rich environments [18–20]. The skin of amphibians, fish, and mammals served as a rich reservoir for these compounds, functioning as the first line of defense against infections [21]. Pig skin had garnered significant attention due to its unique translational medical value [22, 23]. Domestic pigs exhibited a closer evolutionary and physiological relationship with humans, and their skin was consistently exposed to a substantial microbial load [24]. Therefore, we hypothesized that their skin, as an integral component of the innate immune defense system, might harbor a rich reservoir of potent antimicrobial peptides.

This study synthesized twelve antimicrobial peptides through enzymatic hydrolysis and subsequent screening. Following preliminary screening for anti-MRSA activity, peptide P5, which demonstrated the strongest antimicrobial efficacy, was selected. Gram-positive bacteria (MRSA and SA) and Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli) were selected as the in vitro activity evaluation models. Additionally, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) technology was employed to analyze their antibacterial mechanisms. The in vitro biosafety of P5 was evaluated using CCK8 and live/dead cell staining assays. In conclusion, P5 was an antimicrobial peptide that hold promising prospects for clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Domestic pigs skin was purchased from Yantai Xiwang Meat Food Co. Pepsin, trypsin, sodium hydroxide, and hydrochloric acid were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Science and Technology Co. MRSA (ATCC 43300) strains were prepared by Ningbo Mingzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Ningbo, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) were obtained from Gibco Life Sciences (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Live/dead BacLight bacterial viability kit was from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). L929 cells (NCTC clone 929) were purchased from Mingzhou Biotech, and MDCK cells (NBL-2) were obtained from Sevier.

Extraction and isolation of pig skin peptides

Bionic peptides were extracted from domestic pig skin using previously established methods [25, 26]. In brief, skimmed pig skin was mixed with deionized water, boiled for 15 min, and then cooled to 37 °C. The pH was adjusted to 1.5 using 10% HCl, followed by the addition of pepsin for 1 h. Subsequently, the pH was adjusted to 8.0 with 1% NaOH, and trypsin was added for 3 h. The mixture was boiled for 10 min to inactivate the proteases. After reduced-pressure filtration, the filtrate was stored at 4 °C for 12 h, after which the surface oil was removed. The solution was then centrifuged at 5,000 ×g for 10 min, repeated three times. The resulting digest was filtered through a 3 kDa ultrafiltration membrane, concentrated using a rotary evaporator, and lyophilized to obtain the crude peptide extract.

Analyzing peptide sequences of house pig skin

The complete amino acid sequence of porcine skin peptides was determined using the EASY-nLC1200-Q Exactive Plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), employing a C18 reversed-phase analytical column (75 μm×20 cm, 3 μm) as the separation medium. The mobile phase A consisted of a mixture of 99.9% water and 0.1% formic acid, while mobile phase B comprised 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. The liquid phase gradient was as follows: 0–3 min, 2–6% B; 3–42 min, 6–20% B; 42–47 min, 20%-32% B; 47–48 min, 32–100% B;48–60 min, 100% B. The mobile phase flow rate was maintained at 300 NL/min. In ESI+ mode, a data-dependent scanning approach was utilized, with full scan acquisition (m/z 200–2000) conducted in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 70,000 (AGC 3e6). The top 20 peptide signals (charge state ≥ + 1) were isolated and fragmented using high-energy collision dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 28.0. The capillary temperature was set to 275℃, and the spray voltage was maintained at 1800 V. Fragment ions were measured in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 17,500 (AGC 1e5). The maximum fill times for the full scan and MS/MS scan were set to 50 ms and 45 ms, respectively, with a dynamic exclusion time of 30 s.

Screening antimicrobial peptides

The peptides obtained from domestic pig skin were screened for activity using Peptide Ranker, with a probability value threshold set at > 0.7 [27]. Subsequently, toxicity screening was performed using ToxinPred (SVM-based model), retaining only peptides predicted as “Non-Toxin”. Subsequently, the screening of potential antimicrobial peptides was subsequently performed by evaluating their capacity to interact with bacterial membranes, a key mechanism of their antibacterial action. Potential AMPs were identified through this screening process. The candidate peptides were then synthesized using an Fmoc solid phase peptide synthesizer and purified to over 95% purity via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The molecular weight of the synthesized peptides was verified using mass spectrometry (MS). The processes of peptide synthesis, liquid chromatography, and mass spectrometry analyses were conducted by Hefei Hesheng Biotechnology Co.

Antimicrobial activity evaluation

To evaluate the antimicrobial activity of peptides P1-P12, peptide solutions were prepared in 96-well plates using two-fold dilution method. A total of 10 µL of MRSA suspension was added to each well and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. To further evaluate the bacterial activity of the P5, representative bacteria were selected for experimentation: the Gram-positive bacterium MRSA, SA and the Gram-negative bacterium E.coli. P5 was diluted into five concentrations 5, 4, 3, 2, and 1 mg/mL using broth. Subsequently, 10µL (1 × 106 CFU/mL) of each bacterial suspension was added followed by incubation at 37 °C for 16 h. Morphological effects of P5 on MRSA

The effect of P5 was assessed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). In brief, a bacterial suspension of MRSA at a concentration of 106 CFU/mL was treated with the peptide at final concentrations of 1, 3, and 5 mg/mL for 2 h at 37 °C, with a blank control group established for comparison. The bacteria were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation and rinsing three times with saline. Subsequently, the cells were dehydrated using 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, and 95% ethanol solutions, each for 15 min, and the ethanol was replaced with anhydrous methanol three times. Finally, the dried samples were coated with gold and examined under a scanning electron microscope.

Biosafety evaluation of P5

Biosafety evaluation includes cytotoxicity and hemolysis tests. The cytotoxicity of P5 on L929 and MDCK cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and co-cultured with varying concentrations of P5 (up to 3 mg/mL) for 16 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and cytotoxicity was assessed by calculating the ratio of absorbance between treated groups and the control group. Additionally, live/dead cell staining was employed for visual evaluation of P5 cytotoxicity. Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated from fresh anticoagulated whole blood of rats by centrifugation (4 °C, 3000 rpm, 15 min). The RBC pellet was washed three times with PBS and then resuspended in PBS to prepare a 40% (v/v) stock suspension. For the assay, 100 µL of the stock suspension was mixed with 900 µL of peptide solutions at different concentrations (or control solutions) to achieve a final RBC concentration of 4% (v/v). Deionized water-treated samples served as the positive control, while PBS-treated samples were used as the negative control. After incubation for 1 h and centrifugation, the supernatant was collected to measure the absorbance at 540 nm. Statistical analysis

In this study, all experiments were conducted with three independent biological replicates, each consisting of three technical replicates. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed with the t-test in GraphPad. In all cases, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001 were considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Screening of antimicrobial peptides

From an initial pool of 3,282 peptides derived from porcine skin, a systematic screening strategy was employed to identify potential antimicrobial peptides. Bioactivity prediction using Peptide Ranker, with a threshold of > 0.7, yielded 343 candidates. Subsequent toxicity screening via Toxin Pred retained 288 peptides predicted to be non-toxic. Based on established structure–activity relationships of antimicrobial peptides, the 288 peptides were further filtered to retain those containing cationic lysine (K) [14] residues and hydrophobic phenylalanine (F) [15] or tryptophan (W) [16] residues, resulting in a final selection of 12 candidate peptides. Based on the aforementioned research, this screening process culminated in the identification of 12 peptides (Table 1), which were synthesized using Fmoc solid-phase synthesis and purified to over 95% purity via HPLC. Antimicrobial efficacy of 12 antimicrobial peptides against MRSA

The 12 peptides were dissolved at a concentration of 3 mg/mL and subsequently screened for their inhibitory effects against MRSA bacteria. As illustrated in the Fig. 1A, all peptides, with the exception of P3, exhibited some degree of inhibitory effect on MRSA. Notably, the P5 demonstrated a significantly stronger inhibitory effect, achieving an inhibition rate of 91.83%. In contrast, the inhibition rates of the other peptides were below 80%, and P3 exhibited a pro-fertilization effect on the bacteria. Subsequently, we further validated the in vitro antimicrobial activity of the twelve peptides using an agar plate coating assay, as depicted in the Fig. 1B. The results were consistent with those obtained from the initial screening. Therefore, it was concluded that the P5 was the most effective among the 12 peptides in terms of antimicrobial activity, leading to its selection for further antimicrobial exploration.

Exploring the antimicrobial effects of P5

The bactericidal efficacy of P5 against Gram-positive bacteria (MRSA) and Gram-negative bacteria (E.coli) was evaluated, with testing conducted at five different concentrations (1 mg/mL, 2 mg/mL, 3 mg/mL, 4 mg/mL, and 5 mg/mL). The peptide exhibited a dose-dependent inhibitory effect, with the most pronounced activity observed against MRSA in comparison to E.coli, as illustrated in the accompanying Fig. 2A. P5 exhibited potent antibacterial activity against Gram-positive. Furthermore, we evaluated the inhibitory effect of P5 against standard Staphylococcus aureus (SA). As shown in Fig. S4, at concentrations 3 mg/mL, P5 exhibited antibacterial rates of 97.21% against SA. These results further confirm the potent antimicrobial activity of P5 against Gram-positive bacteria, including drug-resistant MRSA and standard S. aureus strains. However, compared to its antibacterial effect against E.coli, P5 exhibits distinct species selectivity, a phenomenon that warrants in-depth investigation. While the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria may indeed constitute a permeability barrier, we speculate that this selectivity is more likely associated with the specificity of a certain ABC transporter in MRSA serving as the target. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of peptide P5 were determined using the standard broth microdilution method. As presented in Fig. S3, P5 demonstrated an MIC of 2 mg/mL and an MBC of 4 mg/mL against MRSA, indicating its potent antimicrobial activity.

The relative rate and extent of the bactericidal activity of the P5 were further assessed through growth curve analysis. As depicted in the Fig. 2B, at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, the P5 demonstrated a modest bactericidal inhibition against MRSA; however, the effect was limited. Notably, at a concentration of 3 mg/mL, the P5 completely inhibited MRSA growth within 16 h, although bacterial growth resumed over time. At a concentration of 5 mg/mL, the P5 successfully inhibited MRSA growth entirely within 24 h. These findings reaffirm the potent antibacterial activity of the P5 against MRSA, with its inhibitory effect exhibiting a clear dose-dependent relationship.

Biosafety evaluation of P5

To confirm its biosafety, we conducted cytotoxicity tests and hemolysis experiments. The toxicity of P5 on L929 and MDCK cells was assessed through the use of a CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 3A, the survival rate of L929 cells reached 89.09% after treatment with 3 mg/mL of P5. At 1 mg/mL, the peptide also promoted growth in MDCK cells. Furthermore, high biocompatibility was observed across elevated concentrations, with cell survival rates remaining at 92.2% and 87.3% at 2 mg/mL and 3 mg/mL, respectively (Fig. S2). These results collectively indicate that the P5 exhibits good biocompatibility. Additionally, in the Fig. 3B live and dead cell staining was performed using an inverted microscope for intuitive visualization. The morphology of L929 cells in the P5 group post-administration of P5 remained unchanged compared to the blank group. The high survival rate of L929 cells in the P5 group further corroborates the biocompatibility of P5 with L929 cells in the blank group. As shown in Fig. 3C, P5 exhibits excellent biocompatibility. Even at the 5 mg/mL tested concentration, P5-treated rat erythrocytes did not show significant hemolysis during the observation period, with the hemolysis rate consistently remaining below the 5% safety threshold.

Analysis of the antibacterial mechanism using RNA-seq and qRT-PCR

To explore the antimicrobial mechanism in greater depth, an RNA-seq study was conducted, and a total of 2,892 genes were identified. Subsequently, a significant number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the control group and the P5 administration group (*P < 0.05, critical value of fold change > 2). Among these, 283 genes were up-regulated and 241 were down-regulated. From the DEGs, we selected nine significant differential genes: artR, artQ, hutU, hutI, hutG, argG, argH, rocF, and rot [28–30], which primarily regulate arginine biosynthesis and histidine metabolism (Table S1). Their gene expression levels are presented in Table S1. Heatmap analysis revealed significant differences in the mRNA expression levels of DEGs between the control and treated groups, with the nine key DEGs identified in the volcano plot (Fig. 5A). To further explain the pathways of the genes affected by P5, we performed an enrichment analysis on the KEGG database and the Gene Ontology (GO) database. The enrichment analysis of KEGG signaling pathways revealed that gene expression in P5-treated bacteria was primarily associated with membrane transport, amino acid metabolism, energy metabolism, and so on. (Fig. 5B). Meanwhile, the GO database was utilized to analyze the 20 biological processes associated with the differentially expressed genes, encompassing biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions (Fig. 5C). The expression levels of DEGs were assessed using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). The results indicated that the mRNA expression of DEGs in the treatment group was downregulated (Fig. 5E), which aligns with the findings presented in the volcano plot. In summary, these experimental results suggested that P5 could influence the ABC transporter protein pathway, arginine metabolism and biosynthesis, histidine metabolism and population sensing (Fig. 6). Based on these findings and the established role of ABC transporters in bacterial physiology—where they facilitate nutrient uptake and are localized to the inner membrane—we propose the following working hypothesis for future investigation: P5 may disrupt ABC transporter function, potentially affecting nutrient import and energy homeostasis, which could subsequently impact cell wall integrity and bacterial viability. However, we emphasize that this hypothetical model requires experimental validation, as transcriptomic data alone cannot establish causality [31]. Thus, the inhibition mechanism of P5 differs from that of traditional antibiotics, and this approach might be widely applicable in mitigating the long-term misuse of antibiotics.

Conclusion

This study isolated a series of peptides from domestic pig skin through enzymatic extraction and identified 12 predicted antimicrobial peptides through a simple screening process. These target peptides were synthesized using the Fmoc solid-phase method. The antimicrobial activity of the 12 peptides was initially assessed against MRSA, confirmed through agar plate coating experiments, which showed that P5 exhibited significant antimicrobial activity. Subsequently, the antimicrobial efficacy of P5 against MRSA, SA, and E. coli was evaluated in conjunction with MRSA growth curve analysis, demonstrating that P5 effectively inhibited MRSA and SA. Additionally, the safety evaluation of P5, including CCK8 tests, hemolysis experiments, and staining of live and dead cells, indicated its excellent biocompatibility. The antimicrobial mechanism was further explored through transcriptome sequencing and qPCR experiments, revealing its inhibition of amino acid biosynthesis and metabolic relationships. Notably, the stability of P5 under physiological conditions still requires improvement, as its antimicrobial activity in serum environments necessitates higher concentrations to be maintained, suggesting a certain metabolic instability of the natural peptide sequence. Therefore, subsequent strategies such as truncation optimization, D-amino acid substitution, or cyclization modifications can be employed to enhance its protease resistance and in vivo stability while retaining the core active fragments. In conclusion, P5 has been identified as a safe, non-toxic peptide with significant antimicrobial activity, potentially reducing the risk of resistance due to the frequent use of broad-spectrum biocides, thereby providing new insights for the exploration of natural antimicrobial peptides and laying a foundation for subsequent structural optimization and clinical translation.
