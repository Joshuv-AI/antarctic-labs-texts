Comparison of monthly bleeding rates after direct oral anticoagulant initiation for venous thromboembolism and atrial fibrillation
Authors: Dominic Awuah, Sofia Zestos, Josh Errickson, Xiaowen Kong, Mona Ali, Christopher Giuliano, Brian Haymart, Scott Kaatz, Deborah DeCamillo, Noelle Ryan, Suman Sood, James B. Froehlich, Geoffrey D. Barnes, Jordan K. Schaefer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDirect oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are commonly used for the treatment of atrial fibrillation (AF) and venous thromboembolism (VTE) but increase the risk of bleeding.ObjectivesWe aimed to evaluate the distribution of bleeding events after anticoagulant initiation.MethodsAdult patients in the Michigan Anticoagulation Quality Improvement Initiative registry on DOAC for AF or VTE were followed from anticoagulant start to identify any bleeding event within 12 months. The primary outcome was any bleeding; secondary outcomes were bleeding subtypes. Monthly event rates were compared by Poisson and proportion tests.ResultsA total of 2663 patients (mean age 66.9 years, 49% male sex [as recorded in the electronic health record], 72.1% AF) who were starting DOACs were followed. Bleeding event rates were highest after anticoagulant initiation. For VTE, patients experienced 110, 55, and 36 bleeding events per 100 patient-years the first 3 months of anticoagulation, respectively. For AF, patients experienced 66, 43, and 33 bleeding events per 100 patient-years the first 3 months of anticoagulation, respectively. After adjustment for covariates, bleeding was higher for patients with VTE compared with AF, with 51 vs 34 bleeds per 100 patient-years (rate ratio, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.58-0.80; P < .001), largely driven by differences in the first month of anticoagulation (141 vs 75 bleeds per 100 patient-years; rate ratio, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.38-0.74; P < .001).ConclusionBleeding rates were highest during the first 3 months of oral anticoagulation; bleeding was higher for patients with VTE compared with AF. These findings emphasize the need for careful management and monitoring after DOAC initiation.

Introduction

The direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), including apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban, are commonly used for the prevention of stroke in most patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) and for the management of venous thromboembolic disease (VTE), which includes deep vein thrombosis and/or pulmonary embolism [1]. Appropriately selected patients are often initiated on a DOAC after a diagnosis of AF and/or VTE or they are transitioned to a DOAC from another anticoagulant, such as a vitamin K antagonist [2].

Higher bleeding rates have been observed for patients newly initiating oral anticoagulation as compared with those previously maintained on anticoagulation [3,4]. For example, patients starting warfarin have a higher bleeding risk during the first few months, which decreases over time. However, the temporal change in bleeding risk associated with the initiation of DOACs has not been well defined. Furthermore, it is not clear whether monthly bleeding event rates after DOAC initiation differ by indication for anticoagulation (eg, AF vs VTE). Given differences in baseline bleeding and thrombotic risk by indication [[5], [6], [7]], it could be anticipated that initial bleeding event rates may differ by indication. This may be further compounded by the higher intensity doses of anticoagulation used for apixaban and rivaroxaban for acute VTE management. Therefore, we sought to determine how bleeding event rates varied over time after DOAC initiation in patients with AF and VTE.

Methods

Study design and participants

The Michigan Anticoagulation Quality Improvement Initiative is a collaborative of 6 anticoagulation management services in Michigan. These centers include both urban and rural practices serving a diverse group of patients, with hundreds to tens of thousands of patients being followed at each site. Both academic and community practices are included. Four of these 6 sites collect data on patients in a structured random sample of DOAC-treated patients, as previously described [8].

Data on patient demographics, indications for anticoagulation, dosing, concomitant medications, comorbidities, bleeding and thrombotic history, and laboratory values, among others, are collected at enrollment by chart abstraction. A Charlson Comorbidity Index score and Hypertension, Abnormal renal/liver function, Stroke, Bleeding history, Labile international normalized ratio, Elderly (age > 65 years), and Drugs/alcohol (HAS-BLED) score was calculated for each patient at registry enrollment, with the HAS-BLED score modified to exclude labile international normalized ratio [9,10].

For this analysis, we used data from November 2015 to November 2023. Included patients were aged > 18 years, newly started on DOACs for AF or VTE, with at least 3 months of follow-up data. We excluded patients treated with other anticoagulants, on anticoagulants immediately prior to the study period, anticoagulated for other indications, or patients anticoagulated for both AF and VTE.

Patients were followed from the time of study enrollment until stopping anticoagulation, the end of the study period, death, or loss to follow-up. Data were collected by trained data abstractors, using predefined forms, with random chart audits by the coordinating center to verify accuracy and completeness.

Outcome measures and analysis

We analyzed monthly bleeding rates for the first 12 months of DOAC treatment. The primary outcome was any bleeding, which is a composite outcome of major bleeding as defined by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis [11], nonmajor bleeding, and a small number of events that could not be classified as either major or nonmajor. Secondary outcomes included major bleeding and nonmajor bleeding. Multiple events were allowed in the primary analysis and a sensitivity analysis until first bleeding event.

Descriptive statistics are presented along with event rates as the number of events per 100 patient-years. Comparison between groups was done using chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Event rates were compared by Poisson regression using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) and Poisson test using R 64-4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Patient demographics, medical history, and comorbidities were included in regression model. The study was approved by the institutional review board at each center before data collection was started. Given that the study was part of a quality improvement project, a waiver of informed consent was granted.

Results and Discussion

This observational cohort study followed 2663 patients, consisting of 1919 AF and 744 VTE patients. The cohort had an average age of 69.3 years, 48.4% were female sex, 82.8% identified as White race, and 48.6% had a body mass index > 30 kg/m2 (Table 1). Aspirin use at the time of DOAC initiation was common, occurring in 30.3% of patients, primarily ≤ 100 mg daily. Unadjusted, patients with VTE experienced 109.7 bleeds per 100 patient-years versus 54.8 bleeds per 100 patient-years in month 1 compared to month 2. There was a significant decline in event rates over the 12-month study period (P < .001 for trend; Figure 1). Major bleeding event rates also decreased for patients with VTE over time (P = .008 for trend; Figure 2). After adjustment for significant covariates, the greatest decline in bleeding event rates for VTE occurred between months 1 and 2 (132.0 vs 66.0 bleeds per 100 patient-years), with a rate ratio of 2.0 (95% CI 1.3-3.0; P < .001, Supplementary Table 1). Adjusted major bleeding event rates were higher in month 1 than in month 2, (2.9 vs 1.3 per 100 patient-years), but the difference was not statistically significant (rate ratio, 2.3; 95% CI, 0.7-7.6; P = 0.19). The adjusted rates of bleeding requiring an emergency department (ED) visit, and nonmajor bleeding events were significantly higher in the first month of anticoagulation compared with the second month (89.2 vs 20.3 per 100 patient-years; rate ratio, 4.4; 95% CI, 1.7-11.4; P = .002 and 119.5 vs 58.7 100 patient-years; rate ratio, 2.0; 95% CI, 1.3-3.2; P = .002, Supplementary Table 1). Unadjusted, patients with AF experienced 65.7 bleeds per 100 patient-years vs 43.1 bleeds per 100 patient-years in month 1 compared with month 2. There was a significant decline in event rates over the 12-month study period (P < .001 for trend; Figure 1). Major bleed rates did not significantly change over time for patients with AF (Figure 2). After adjustment for significant covariates, the greatest decline in bleeding event rates for AF occurred between months 1 and 2 (86.6 vs 56.9 bleeds per 100 patient-years), with a rate ratio of 1.5 (95% CI, 1.1-2.1, P = .02; Supplementary Table 1). Adjusted major bleeding event rates were higher in month 1 than in month 2, (3.7 vs 2.8 per 100 patient-years), but the difference was not statistically significant (rate ratio, 1.3; 95% CI, 0.5-3.5; P = .62). The adjusted rates of bleeding requiring an ED visit were also not significantly different between months 1 and 2 (43.0 vs 29.2 per 100 patient-years; rate ratio, 1.5; 95% CI, 0.8-2.8; P = .23). The absolute number of bleeds decreased with time, with patients with AF having 105, 69, and 53 bleeds over the first 3 months of anticoagulation and patients with VTE having 68, 34, and 22 bleeds (Supplementary Table 6). Patients with VTE had higher bleeding event rates compared with AF, with unadjusted event rates highest in the first month (109.7 vs 65.7 events per 100 patient-years for VTE and AF in month 1, P = .0018) and then declining over time (26.8 vs 17.9 events per 100 patient-years for VTE and AF over months 1-12, P = .60; Table 2, Figure 1). After adjustment for significant variables, bleeding remained higher for patients with VTE vs AF, with 50.5 versus 34.3 bleeds per 100 patient-years (rate ratio, 0.7; 95% CI, 0.6-0.8; P < .001) over the course of the study period. This was especially true for the first month, with the VTE group having 140.8 vs 74.7 bleeds per 100 patient-years with AF (rate ratio, 0.5; 95% CI, 0.4-0.7; P < .001), but these differences were not observed in months 2 or 3. Patients with VTE also had significantly higher rates of major bleeding, bleeding requiring ED visits, and nonmajor bleeding during month 1 that was not observed in months 2 or 3. Overall, first year nonmajor bleeding was significantly higher for patients with VTE compared with AF, but not for major bleeding or bleeding requiring an ED visit (Table 2). In our sensitivity analysis, limiting patients to their first bleeding event, results were similar to the primary analysis. However, the unadjusted event rates were slightly lower. Patients with VTE experienced 107.3 vs 44.5 bleeds per 100 patient-years in month 1 compared with month 2, with a significant decline in event rates over time (P < .001 for trend; Supplementary Table 8). Major bleeding, nonmajor bleeding, and ED visits for bleeding significantly declined over time (Supplementary Table 8). Patients with AF experienced 60.4 vs 31.1 bleeds per 100 patient-years in month 1 compared with month 2, with a significant decline in event rates over time (P < .001 for trend; Table 2). Major bleeding, nonmajor bleeding, and ED visits for bleeding did not decline over time (Supplementary Table 8).

In this analysis of >2500 patients initiating DOAC therapy for treatment of AF or VTE, significantly higher bleeding rates occurred during DOAC initiation as compared with maintenance. This emphasizes the importance of close patient follow-up during the first several weeks of therapy to improve recognition of bleeding events that could be mitigated with early intervention. Furthermore, it is important to consider the difference in bleeding risk between the initiation and maintenance phase of DOAC therapy when evaluating clinical trials and observational anticoagulant studies. While there is evidence that bleeding is higher during the initial phase of anticoagulation compared with extended treatment [12,13], this study adds more detail on the timing of excess early bleeding risk in this population, which has both clinical and research implications.

In our cohort, we observed differences in unadjusted bleeding rates among patients with AF and VTE in the first month of anticoagulation, likely partially as a result of different bleeding risk factors in the 2 populations [14]. However, these differences persisted even with adjustment for covariates. Apixaban and rivaroxaban use a higher initial dose for treatment of acute VTE compared with stroke prevention in AF, and it is possible that this dosing variation could at least partially explain our findings. This is supported by the COmparison of Bleeding Risk Between Rivaroxaban and Apixaban trial results that showed that 15 mg twice daily of rivaroxaban for 3 weeks was associated with higher early bleeding compared with 10 mg twice daily for 7 days with apixaban [15]. Dabigatran and edoxaban use 5 days of parenteral before initiation for VTE treatment, but we had very few patients treated with these drugs. Dosing and bleeding risk for VTE has been of particular interest recently with the publication of the REduced Dose Versus Full-dose of Direct Oral Anticoagulant After Unprovoked Venous Thromboembolism [16] and APIxaban Cancer Associated Thromboembolism trials [17] that evaluated DOAC dose reduction for extended-phase VTE management, suggesting that lower doses may lower bleeding risk. In our population, 27.4% of the patients with VTE population was on reduced-dose DOACs, with the timing of the dose reduction variable, often before 6 months. For patients with AF, 3.7% of patients were on reduced dosing, and this dose reduction would be “inappropriate” based on the package insert for most of these patients. Dose intensity should be considered when interpreting our study results.

Strengths of this study include a diverse patient population, manual abstracted data, and robust follow-up. The sensitivity analysis accounts for potential survivor bias and supports the primary analysis findings. Limitations include those inherent to the observational study design and a lack of data on medication adherence, and the study population was limited to 4 anticoagulation management service sites in the state of Michigan, United States. We were not able to fully adjust for dose intensity or dose reductions, as the potential reason for dose reduction could be variable and potentially dynamic. We only studied patients newly starting anticoagulation and did not compare bleeding to patients switching to DOACs from an alternative anticoagulant. Patients were all followed by a quality improvement collaborative that regularly engages in quality improvement initiatives, potentially limiting generalizability.

In summary, bleeding rates are highest in the initial month of anticoagulation with a DOAC, and bleeding is higher for patients with VTE compared with AF. Accordingly, close clinical follow-up with attention to bleeding risk reduction and prompt bleed management may be especially important after anticoagulant initiation. Duration of anticoagulant use should be considered when evaluating studies on bleeding outcomes, given the higher bleeding risk with anticoagulant initiation compared with maintenance. Further research could confirm if the higher initial doses of DOACs used with apixaban and rivaroxaban for VTE could account for the higher initial bleed rates seen with VTE compared with AF.
