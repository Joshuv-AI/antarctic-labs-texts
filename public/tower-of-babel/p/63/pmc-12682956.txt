Real-time margin assessment for video-assisted thoracic surgery: A pilot clinical trial
Authors: Jayender Jagadeesan, Sarah Lobo, Alan Lucas, Julie Barlow, Ahmed Sadek, Zahra Sarawat Alam, Rajat Mukherjee, Eric J Schmidlin, Michael T Jaklitsch, Lynette Marie Sholl, Raphael Bueno, Scott J Swanson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo accurately provide real-time intraoperative guidance on margins despite significant lung deformation. With lung cancer screening programs, smaller, early-stage lung nodules are being detected. Sublobar resection of peripheral small nodules is noinferior to lobar resection in terms of disease-free recurrence and long-term survival while preserving healthy lung function. However, accurately ensuring sufficient resection margin is challenging.MethodsA novel navigation system with a tumor marker (J-bar), surgical stapler instrumented with a position sensor, and software to compute the distances in real time has been developed and utilized during surgery to resect lung cancer. The software measurement of the J-bar to the tip of the stapler cutline (J-bar-tip) was compared with the distance measured from the resected sample in pathology. The aim was to establish the safety and feasibility of intraoperative use.ResultsTwenty-five patients were enrolled in this study; 3 procedures were not included. For the 22 procedures: average nodule size = 17.5 mm, pleural surface distance = 8.0 mm with 5 nodules >20 mm from the surface. All patients underwent complete nodule resection. The median time to place the J-bar was 2.8 minutes and to clamp the sensorized surgical stapler at the first staple line was 1.8 minutes. There was a significant correlation between the software readings and the pathological J-bar-tip readings. There were no study-related anticipated or unanticipated adverse events related to the device.ConclusionsThe safety and feasibility of using this novel navigation system to provide intraoperative distance from the J-bar to the stapler has been demonstrated.Key Words: navigation system, lung surgery, real-time margin assessment, early-stage nodule detection and resection

Central Message

A novel navigation system with a tumor marker, sensorized stapler, and real-time software enables accurate intraoperative margin assessment, ensuring total lung nodule resection despite deformation.

Perspective

This novel navigation system enhances intraoperative margin assessment in lung cancer localization and resection, addressing key challenge in ensuring adequate margins despite lung deformation. By improving surgical precision, it may optimize oncologic outcomes and lung function. Its integration into the surgical workflow and strong correlation with pathology suggest potential for widespread adoption.

Lung cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide, with an estimated 2.4 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths annually.1 In the United States, lung cancer accounts for 27% of all cancer-related deaths with 5-year survival of only 16% to 19%,2 which is far lower than the survival for colon, breast, and prostate cancers. These poor survival rates are partly explained by late-stage presentation. In 2011, The National Lung Cancer Screening Trial demonstrated a detection and survival benefit for lung cancer screening using low-dose computed tomography (CT).3 Consequently, the incidence of small, early-stage pulmonary nodule detection has increased since their implementation.4

Minimally invasive surgical approaches, such as video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) have mostly replaced open thoracotomy as the standard of care for the surgical resection of early-stage lung cancer and have shown improved perioperative outcomes with equivalent oncologic outcomes when compared with the open approach.5 Recent data from the CALGB 140503 trial has shown that sublobar resection was oncologically noninferior when compared with lobar resection.6 These findings, in combination with the aforementioned increased detection of small nodules on screening low-dose CT, have increased the utilization of minimally invasive thoracic surgical techniques for the treatment of early-stage non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).5,7,8 However, the recent screening, clinical, and technical advances have presented new challenges in the treatment of NSCLC, including accurate nodule localization and surgeon-measured intraoperative margin assessment.

Nodule Localization

Currently, surgeons rely on preoperative CT imaging to localize nodules. However, these images are acquired with the lung in an inflated state, and therefore reflect the nodule size, location, and distance from the chest wall under nonoperative conditions. However, during surgical resection, the surgeon manipulates the parenchyma and attempts to locate the nodule with the lung collapsed and in a deflated state.9 The significant changes in lung shape, size, and position between the inflated and deflated states result in increased difficulty in precisely mapping the nodule's location based on preoperative CT imaging, which can, in turn, result in missed nodules or insufficient resection margins. For these smaller, deeper, or nonpalpable nodules, minimally invasive surgical techniques lack the necessary haptic and tactile feedback needed for accurate localization.4,7,10

Margin Assessment

It is oncologically imperative to achieve adequate resection margins during surgery for NSCLC. For small (≤2 cm) nodules, we have previously shown that resection margins of 10 mm had a 45% lower local recurrence rate than 5 mm margins. Furthermore, patients with a 15-mm resection margin had 59% lower local recurrence rate compared with those with 5 mm margins.11 The current National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend resection margins ≥2 cm or greater than or equal to the diameter of the nodule.12 The current standard for intraoperative margin estimation by a surgeon is via careful visual inspection and palpation.13 As mentioned, these strategies become less reliable with smaller and deeper nodules.10,14 The gold standard for margin assessment utilizes fresh frozen section evaluation by a pathologist without any real-time intraoperative surgical guidance.13 Thus, there remains a clinical need for accurate, reliable, and reproducible margin assessment intraoperatively by the surgeon.

In summary, the current surgical landscape of lung cancer management has led to earlier and smaller nodule detection, as well as the increased implementation of minimally invasive and sublobar surgical approaches. These advances have elicited several novel surgical challenges, including intraoperative nodule localization and margin assessment. In this article, we describe a novel electromagnetic navigation system with the potential to assess margins intraoperatively in real-time, as shown in Figure 1. As a first step, the clinical trial described in this article demonstrates the ability of the navigation system to accurately measure intraoperative distances from a tumor marker, called J-bar, placed close the nodule, to the surgical margin, a surrogate distance measurement of margins.

Methods

With approval from the institutional review board (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04702165; approval No.: 2020P002692; approval date: January 19, 2021) and Food and Drug Administration (Investigational Device Exemption G200178), we prospectively enrolled subjects with small pulmonary nodules suspicious for malignancy who were referred for surgery at a single academic medical center. Informed consent for publication was obtained.

Patient Selection

For this study, patients suitable for VATS lung wedge resection surgery due to comorbidities, limited lung function, small nodules, multiple distant nodules, and/or patient preference were recruited. Inclusion criteria included patients with lesions that are nodules/ground-glass opacity or other abnormal opacity that can be accurately measured in at least 1 dimension using conventional techniques, located in the outer half portion of the lung. The patients selected were older than age 21 years, and female patients who were pregnant or breastfeeding or with a pacemaker were excluded from the study.

Navigation System

The navigation system for localizing and resecting lung nodules consists of hardware and software components. The hardware component consists of an actively tracked fiducial marker, called the J-bar, with an embedded electromagnetic (EM) position sensor, anchored in the lung close to the nodule and an EM-sensorized clip attachment for the surgical stapler. The software component of the navigation system reads the position measurements from the J-bar and sensorized surgical stapler to compute the distances and provide real-time display to the surgeon. The distance of the J-bar, which is anchored close to the nodule, to the surgical stapler provides a surrogate measurement of the margin distances. The details of the navigation system are provided in Appendix E1.

Ex Vivo Porcine Lung Testing

Before evaluating the navigation system in human beings, extensive tests were done in ex vivo porcine lungs (Figure 2, A-C) and 1 cadaver (Figure 2, D-G) to evaluate the clinical workflow, accuracy, electrical isolation, and mechanical strength of the J-bar. To evaluate the accuracy of the navigation system, freshly excised porcine lungs were used to place the J-bar at specific locations in the lung. The EM-sensorized surgical stapler was used to cut the lung tissue and excise a wedge-like segment of the lung within which the J-bar had been placed, as shown in Figure 2, A-C. Before making the cut, the stapler was clamped, and the software J-bar tip, J-bar mid, and J-bar base measurements presented by the navigation system were recorded. Once the wedge resection was completed, the resected lung sample was analyzed. The J-bar within the lung sample was exposed, and the physical distance of the J-bar to the stapler margin was measured using a calibrated caliper.

Navigated Surgery: Placement of the J-bar

Upon arrival to the operating room, patients underwent routine induction of general anesthesia and endotracheal intubation. Patients were then placed in the lateral decubitus position to allow for routine thoracoscopy. The nodule was localized by 2 methods: by palpation or localization using intraoperative C-arm CT imaging.

Localization by Palpation

With the lung collapsed, the lung parenchyma was palpated based on the diagnostic CT images. Following thoracoscopic localization of the nodule, the lung surface nearest to the lesion was marked. A needle with the J-bar was then inserted through 1 of the ports into the lung parenchyma, targeting a position close to the nodule at the most critical margin. After the needle was inserted, the stylet lock was removed, and the J-bar was deployed at this location.

Localization by Intraoperative C-arm CT Imaging

With the lung inflated and the patient in lateral decubitus position, the chest was imaged using intraoperative C-arm CT. The C-arm CT image was acquired during the end-inspiratory phase of respiration using a 5-second protocol consisting of 248 projections acquired over 200°. Using the Syngo iGuide needle guidance software (Siemens Healthcare AG), the J-bar was placed at a predetermined location close to the nodule. In all instances, the J-bar was placed at the critical margin between the nodule and the staple line.

Navigated Surgery: Lung Resection

Once the J-bar was placed, a standard video-assisted lung surgery commenced utilizing 3 to 4 chest port incisions. The lung adjacent to the target nodule was bluntly grasped and the EM-sensorized surgical stapler was introduced via a 12-mm chest port guided to the resection margin under direct thoracoscopic visualization. Once satisfied with the location of the stapler in relation to the target nodule, the stapler was clamped. Software measurements of the J-bar tip, J-bar mid, and J-bar base distances were recorded. The lung sample was then resected and the distal end of the first staple line was either marked with surgical clips or a suture. Because distance measurements require 2 precise points to be defined, for this study, the distance from the tip of the first staple line to the J-bar (J-bar tip) was analyzed to evaluate the accuracy of the navigation software. The lung nodule was then resected with the J-bars still secured in the parenchyma.

Pathological Analysis

The specimen was first examined to confirm that both the target lung nodule and the J-bar were within the resected specimen. The J-bar was located by palpation and careful cross-sectioning of the surrounding lung tissue was performed to expose it without dislodging it. The pathologist used a calibrated caliper to measure the distance from the tip of the staple line to the center of the J-bar (J-bar tip). Pathologists were blinded to the intraoperative software measurements.

Study Objective

The primary objective of this proof-of-concept study was to establish a safe and optimal procedural workflow for placing the J-bar and resecting the nodule using the navigation system. The secondary objectives were to determine the accuracy of the navigation system by comparing the software-measured J–bar-to-stapler distances to the distances measured using a caliper from pathology as a first step toward evaluating margin distances.

Statistical Analysis

To assess the degree of association between the software-measured and the caliper-measured J-bar cutline distances, we employ several descriptive methods as well as plots. A standard scatterplot of the 2 measurements is presented, together with both a linear and a locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (nonparametric) approximation, with corresponding 95% CI. The Spearman correlation coefficient is also reported as an index of general agreement. Further, a direct implication of the estimated regression model is the bias estimate with the corresponding 95% limits of agreement, shown as a Bland-Altman plot. As a cross-check, we also estimate the intraclass correlation and the concordance correlation coefficient, with their own 95% CI. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess whether the average measurements from the 2 methods differed significantly. All the analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Safety Analysis

VATS resection of lung lesions were considered to have acceptable morbidity if major complications, which qualify per the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grade of 4 or 5, were observed among 3 or fewer of the 25-subject total.

Efficacy Analysis

The secondary objective was to compare the J-bar tip measurements obtained by the navigation system in vivo and from pathology. A distance measurement is considered clinically acceptable if it is no less than the value obtained by the pathologist and within 0.5 cm larger; that is, within pathologist's measurement +0.5 cm because the navigation system will be used in vivo with the tissue stretched in contrast to the specimen conditions in the frozen section room while the tissue is fully relaxed. The end points were compared between the navigation system and the pathologist using the paired t test. Linear regression was used to determine whether the difference may be represented by the estimated slope coefficient.

Results

Twenty-five procedures on 24 eligible patients were performed in this study under an institutional review board-approved protocol after obtaining Investigational Device Exemption approval from the Food and Drug Administration. Two procedures were aborted due to significant chest wall adhesions and initial pathological analysis showing metastasis to the lymph nodes. For 1 of the patients, pathologist-measured distance was missing and 1 of the patients was enrolled twice in this study for 2 separate procedures. Study Outcomes

Preclinical studies

Sixty J-bar units were tested to confirm that the anchor had adequate retention strength within a porcine lung of 1.1 N to withstand the manipulation of the lung during surgery without being dislodged. Detailed biocompatibility and electrical testing were also conducted in conformance with the ISO 10993-1 guidelines and IEC 60601-1 guidelines, respectively. Twenty-nine ex vivo porcine experiments were first performed to validate the performance of the navigation system, as shown in Figure 2, A-C. The software recorded J-bar tip measurements were compared with the corresponding measurements from pathology. The average error between software and pathology-measured J-bar tip reading was 2.1 ± 1.6 mm. The clinical workflow for placement of the J-bar and using the navigation system to guide the surgical stapler was also validated in 1 cadaver, as shown in Figure 2, D-G.

Clinical Study

Of the 22 human clinical procedures analyzed as part of this work, 21 procedures had the J-bar placed after localization by palpation. For 1 procedure, the J-bar was placed after localization by intraoperative C-arm CT imaging guidance in the inflated lung. In all procedures, the J-bar was successfully anchored close to the nodule without any complications, as shown in Figure 3, F. Figure 3, G, shows the EM-sensorized surgical stapler being clamped before resection, whereas Figure 3, E, shows the real-time software display. The median time to place the J-bar was 2.8 minutes (range, 1.3-12.7 minutes), and the median length of time for placing the first staple line using the EM-sensorized surgical stapler after placement of the J-bar was 1.8 minutes (range, 0.6-5.0 minutes). During pathological analysis, the resected sample was sectioned to expose the J-bar to obtain the J-bar tip pathological distance, as shown in Figure 3, G. The statistical analysis conducted resulted in a significant correlation between the software readings and the pathology readings (correlation coefficient = 0.56; 95% CI, 0.18-0.80; P = .0065). Also, a paired t test for the software and pathology readings showed no significant difference between readings (CI for the difference in means: −4.39 to 6.07; P = .74). To visualize the actual differences between the software and the pathology readings, the datasets were processed and represented as box plots. Using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, we obtained a P value of .840, indicating no statistically significant difference between the 2 measurement methods. The chart shown in Figure 4, C, provides the visual representation of the software readings box plot and the pathology readings box plot, and the paired differences between the 2 groups of readings. The mean, median, SD, minimum, and maximum values for both groups are reported together with the values of the differences. The interquartile range of the software measurements was slightly wider than the interquartile range of the pathology measurements at the lower quartile (2 vs 2.6 cm), indicating that the distribution of the software reading data was a little more dispersed than the pathology readings. However, differences in the means and medians between the software and the pathology readings are close to null (0.1 and 0.1). Further analysis of the paired differences demonstrates that there is almost no difference between the software and the pathology readings.

A linear regression analysis model between the software and pathology measurements showed a moderate correlation and an estimated an intercept of 1.443 and a slope of 0.522, (95% CI, 0.208-2.678 for the intercept, slope: 0.163-0.881; both estimates P < .01). The intercept estimate suggests that the software measurements tend to overestimate the pathology measurements, mostly for small values of the latter. The graph shown in Figure 4, D, provides a representation of the scatterplot with software readings on the y-axis and the pathology reading on the x-axis. A Bland-Altman plot was then used to assess potential biases or variability between the 2 measurements (software and pathology) and evaluate their agreement (refer to the graph in Figure 4, D). The estimated value of the difference of the 2 measurements (software – pathology) as a function of their average, suggests a slight overestimation of the software measurement compared with the pathology measurement. The value of the mean difference between the 2 groups suggests that there is no systematic bias and that the 2 methods agree and can be used interchangeably.

There were no study-related anticipated or unanticipated adverse events related to the device. In all cases, the fiducial was included in the resected lung. There was 1 unexpected grade 5 serious adverse event due to postoperative dislodgement of a hemostatic clot that required operative re-exploration complicated by cardiorespiratory collapse and death, but unrelated to the research trial in any way. Therefore, the study reported no deaths related to the device, and all patients were discharged from the hospital with a median length of stay of 2 days, (range, 0.5-8 days). None of the patients had a Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grade of 4 or 5 complications.

Discussion

We have described a novel navigation system integrated with the J-bar and an EM-sensorized stapler to precisely measure the distance from the J-bar to stapler margin of the lung resection. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first navigation system that has been clinically validated in humans that can ensure sufficient resection margins despite significant manipulation, deformation and collapsing of the lung. By placing the J-bar on the critical margin between the nodule and the stapler, the navigation system can ensure that the resected lung sample has sufficient margin, which in turn, has the potential to lead to improved long-term prognosis for the patient. As demonstrated in the article, the J-bar can be placed close to the nodule under direct thoracoscopy or intraoperative C-arm CT guidance to further guide the nodule resection.

Several research groups have investigated methods of improving the localization of lung nodules. These methods can be broadly categorized into 3 groups: localization using hardware markers (eg, Kopan's needle,15 platinum coils,16 Miller corkscrew wire,17 and T-bars18), localization using intraoperative imaging (eg, thoracoscopic ultrasound19,20 and near infrared imaging21), localization using marking agents (eg, methylene blue,22 contrast media,23 India ink,24 and colored collagen25). None of the methods developed thus far can accurately localize the lung nodule and guide the surgeon to the optimal resection margin.

Near infrared imaging21-based methods have proven to be of great interest in recent years in ensuring sufficient margins due to the avoidance of hardware fiducials.21 One of the problems with this approach is the high false-positive rates of detecting nonmalignant lung nodules and spillage of the dye, which makes it difficult to localize the correct nodule and ensure margins. A newer reagent that overcomes these problems is the tissue-specific agent Cytalux (pafolacianine/OTL-38; On Target Laboratories), which binds to the folate receptor expressed in pulmonary adenocarcinomas.26 Although the initial Phase 2 clinical trial shows immense promise and provides beneficial information concerning the primary location of the disease, the fluorescence imaging provides only qualitative data. This is due to a variety of uncontrolled environmental factors that interrupt the fluorescence signal, such as ambient light from operating room overhead/laparoscope lights, camera-to-target distance, and camera angle. Further, the method is restricted to penetration depths of up to 1 cm, which makes it difficult to detect deeper tumors and further guide resection.27 Further, even if the nodule is accurately localized using the near-infrared imaging-based method, the surgeon has to guide the surgical stapler without any feedback on the quantitative distance of the unseen deeper nodule to the surgical stapler. On the other hand, the navigation technology proposed in this article has the ability to track deeper nodules and ensure margins. The average distance from the pleural surface was 8.0 mm, and there were 5 nodules >20 mm from the pleural surface that were targeted using the navigation system.

This clinical trial shows the flexibility and potential utility of J-bar localization of target nodules in a controlled clinical setting, wherein most nodules in this study were palpable, a reflection of the inclusion criteria in this initial first-in-human trial to establish safety and workflow reliability. The J-bars were successfully deployed via 2 unique surgical approaches: via intraoperative C-arm CT guidance in a hybrid operating room and via direct thoracoscopic visualization. This flexibility provides an ability to adjust surgical planning depending on the nodule characteristics such as size, type of nodule, depth from the pleural surface, and ability to palpate the nodule. Additionally, the median thoracoscopic J-bar deployment time in addition to the time required to guide the EM-sensorized surgical stapler to the optimal margin was <5 minutes, highlighting the simplicity of the J-bar navigation system setup. The present study measures the distance from the surgical stapler to the intraoperatively placed J-bar tip at the critical margin, which serves as a surrogate for the margin distance. As part of our ongoing work, we are focused on providing surgeons with the true margin distance; that is, the distance of the stapler to the nodule, using the information on the nodule shape from preoperative CT imaging and the location of the J-bar with respect to the lung nodule. We will further evaluate the navigation system in a follow-up study that includes a comparator arm using the standard-of-care procedure to better assess its applicability and effectiveness in early-stage lung cancer, particularly for nonpalpable ground-glass opacity and deeply located nodules.

One challenge we faced in this study was validating the navigation system by comparing software measurements of J-bar location in vivo with ex vivo distance measurements taken by the pathologist. Lung parenchyma's viscoelastic nature makes accurately measuring margin distance prone to discrepancies caused by forces acting on the tissue. There exist unique mechanical, volumetric, and elastic properties of lung tissue under normal physiologic conditions, under mechanical ventilation, when collapsed and retracted intraoperatively, and when excised from the chest.28 Before nodule resection, the surgeon must create tension on the lung parenchyma by bluntly grasping and extending the tissue to position and clamp the surgical stapler at the resection margin. At this point, the software measures the distance from the J-bar to the surgical stapler with the lung retracted due to the grasping and clamping forces. However, during gross dissection of the resected specimen in pathology, the lung tissue is relaxed with no external forces acting on the tissue when measuring the distance from the J-bar to the tip of the first stapler line. This difference in external forces acting on the tissue can result in significant discrepancies observed between software and pathological distance measurements. Additionally, gross and microscopic pathological examination of resected specimens routinely utilizes sharp dissection of the resected sample to expose the lung nodule for margin analysis. This sharp dissection can further deform the resected specimen, which can lead to additional variability in ex vivo distance measurements from the J-bar to the stapler margin in pathology. For these reasons, ex vivo margin distance measurements from pathology do not represent an ideal 1-to-1 correlative validation metric for the in vivo measurements acquired by the J-bar navigation system. In contrast, the navigation software's accuracy was much higher when validated on ex vivo porcine lungs due to controlled experimental conditions, allowing for comparison of software and pathological readings under similar external forces and minimal tissue and J-bar deformations before obtaining the true distance measurements.

To verify the measurements, we must accurately define 2 points in the 3-dimensional space, which here were defined at the distal end of the first staple line and the midpoint of the J-bar. The end of the first staple line was marked using either a suture or clip once the sample was removed from the thoracic cavity. However, per standard procedure, multiple consecutive stapler lines are placed to fully remove the nodule. Alternatively, the removal of some wedges can require staple lines fired at different angles, which can make it challenging to pinpoint the end of the first stapler line or, worse, result in cutting through the first staple line. Consequently, this can lead to inaccuracies in defining precisely where the first stapler line ends on the resected lung sample when measuring the distance to the J-bar tip on the resected sample, further resulting in errors in obtaining accurate distance measurements.

From a statistical perspective, although a key limitation of this study is the small sample size, it is sufficient to detect a correlation of 0.75 using Pearson test with 90% power at a 10% significance level. Although formal inferential conclusions are limited by the number of data points, the dataset provides meaningful descriptive insights and demonstrates consistent trends that support the feasibility of the approach. We see this as a valuable foundation for future studies with larger cohorts that will allow for more robust estimation of variability and effect sizes.

Despite the challenges posed by lung deformation and the difficulty of obtaining precise distance measurements for validating software outputs, we have demonstrated that, as an initial step toward accurate margin assessment, the navigation system can consistently deliver reliable, safe, and accurate real-time measurements of the distance between the J-bar and the surgical stapler. As part of our future studies, we are developing a navigation system that can place the J-bar at a specific location with respect to the nodule segmented from the diagnostic CT imaging in 3 dimensions. Combining the J-bar location with respect to the nodule, and the real-time J-bar to stapler distances, an accurate estimate of the true margin distances can be computed. This, in turn, can allow for a targeted resection of small, nonpalpable, lung nodules while achieving sufficient margins to ensure an adequate oncologic resection needed for optimal long-term outcome for the patients. With continued development and operator experience, we anticipate that the discrepancy between intraoperative software-based measurements and pathological margin assessments will decrease. A major part of this variability appears to be related to specimen handling during gross and frozen section analysis. As workflows become more standardized and refined through repeated clinical use, we expect greater consistency and alignment between intraoperative and ex vivo measurements, further enhancing the reliability of this technology in surgical decision making.

The Details of the Different Components of the Navigation System.

J-bar Tumor Fiducial and Electromagnetic-sensorized Surgical Stapler

The design of the J-bar is shown in Figure 3, B. The J-bar is manufactured using nitinol, which is superelastic, biocompatible, and corrosion-free. A Nitinol tube with an outer diameter of 0.72 mm and an inner diameter of 0.55 mm has been laser machined. The proximal hollow cylindrical tube houses an electromagnetic (EM) position sensor (Northern Digital Inc), whereas the distal end of the anchor has been machined into prongs that form the anchoring part of the J-bar. Further, minute serrations are created on the prongs of the J-bar that allow the J-bar to firmly anchor into the lung tissue and prevent migration when forces are applied to the J-bar. The J-bar is loaded into the distal end of an 18-gage needle, prongs first (Figure 3, A). The needle has a slot laser-machined to ensure that the J-bar can be loaded and deployed through the needle. The needle also consists of a metallic stylet within the lumen that is locked in place to prevent accidental deployment of the J-bar (Figure 3, C). Once at the target, the locking clip is removed and the J-bar is deployed using a metal stylet that is inserted through the lumen of the needle. On being completely deployed, the prongs regain their original curved shape and open to firmly anchor the J-bar into the lung tissue close to the nodule. The real-time position and orientation measurements from the EM position sensor within the J-bar allow for the accurate tracking of a single point close to the nodule, thereby constantly providing positional feedback despite significant tissue deformation. The wires extending from the EM sensor of the J-bar also allow for a secondary confirmation of the nodule location within the lung.

To track the surgical stapler in real time, we have developed a 3-dimensional (3D) printed attachment integrated with the EM sensor that can be attached to the surgical stapler (Medtronic) at a particular position on the stapler, as shown in Figure 3, D. Because multiple stapler reloads are used for lung cancer resection, we have designed the attachment to be easily attached and detached from the stapler reloads. Detailed experiments were performed to evaluate the effect of the metal of the surgical reloads on the sensor measurements, based on which the optimal location of the sensor was determined on the nonbending section of the surgical stapler. The EM sensor was affixed to this 3D printed attachment such that the axis of the sensor passed through the central axis of the surgical stapler.

Software

The software of the lung navigation system was developed as a module in the open-source image-processing software, 3D Slicer. The software is based on a workflow that mimics the clinical workflow and consists of the following steps: preoperative planning to create the 3D models of the anatomy, setting up of the EM tracking system and instrument displays, calibration of the instruments to evaluate the stapler tip positions from the EM sensor measurements, setting up of the displays for the surgeon, and logging real-time data from the J-bar and EM-sensorized surgical stapler. The real-time position and orientation data from the 2 EM sensors attached to the J-bar and surgical stapler are acquired and sent to the navigation system at an update rate of 40 Hz with a display update frequency of 25 fps. At every time step, the software computes 5 distances between the J-bar and the surgical stapler – distance of the J-bar to the tip of the cutline (J-bar tip), midpoint of the cutline (J-bar mid), base of the cutline (J-bar base), tip of the surgical stapler, and tangential distance to the stapler. These distances are displayed in real time on the software to provide intraoperative guidance on the surrogate margin distances, as shown in Figure 3, E. For this study, only the distance of the J-bar to the tip of the stapler cutline (J-bar tip) was compared with the distance measured in pathology.
