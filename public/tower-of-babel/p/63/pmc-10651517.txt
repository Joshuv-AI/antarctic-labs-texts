Oxidation mechanism of pyrite in a thiosulfate solution: electrochemical impedance (EIS) and in situ Raman spectroscopy
Authors: Jose A. Tamayo, Ferley A. Vásquez, Franky Bedoya, Jorge A. Calderón
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Ammoniacal thiosulfate has been used lately as an alternative lixiviant for leaching gold from sulfides ores which are not amenable for cyanidation. However, the oxidation of the sulfide minerals generates products that inhibit the dissolution of gold and can promote the degradation of the leaching solution. The complexity of the ammoniacal thiosulfate leaching system has prevented the unification and clarification of the mechanisms of oxidation of sulfide ores used for gold extraction.In this study, a method combining polarization curves, Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and in situ Raman spectroscopy was implemented to investigate the oxidation process of high-purity pyrite. Pyrite samples were dispersed in carbon paste electrode (CPE-Py). The polarization curves of CPE-Py exhibited an increase in current values for overpotentials greater than 0.1 V, indicating the initiation of mineral oxidation processes. Subsequently, a maximum current was observed initially, followed by subsequent decrease, indicating the occurrence of passivation processes on the electrode surface. Hydrodynamic polarization curves demonstrated that the overpotential at which the passivation process occurs is independent of mass transport, suggesting that the passivation products were formed through solid-state transformation.Impedance spectra revealed that at overpotentials below 0.1 V, a partially resolved capacitive semicircle was observed, which was associated with the resistance encountered when charge was transferred between the solution and the surface layer interface. This resistance decreased as the polarization overpotential increased, implying a decrease in charge transfer kinetics. At higher overpotentials (0.3 V–0.4 V), a second capacitive semicircle appeared, linked to the oxidation of one or several species present in the mineral.In situ Raman spectroscopy was utilized to identify the oxidation species of pyrite in ammonia-thiosulfate ((NH4)2S2O3) leaching solution at a pH = 10.2. The composition of the species varied depending on the applied anodic potential. At low anodic potentials (0.1 V), Fe(OH)2 and thiosulfate (S2O32−) were formed, while at high anodic potentials (0.4 V), iron products such as Fe3O4 and γ-FeOOH, as well as sulfide species including thiosulfate, tetrathionates and sulfates (S2O32−, S4O6−2 and SO42−) were formed.

Introduction

In recent years, the utilization of thiosulfate solutions (S2O32−) for gold leaching has been extensively researched as a substitute for the traditional cyanide leaching process [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Thiosulfate solutions have a lower environmental impact, and they have been found to be effective in extracting gold, especially from refractory minerals with gold concentrations greater than 60 wt% [10,11]. Refractory minerals, such as pyrite, pyrrhotite, chalcopyrite, sphalerite, and galena [12,13], are known to exhibit poor gold recovery through cyanidation due to the “preg-robbing” effect [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Among these minerals (such as pyrrhotite, chalcopyrite, sphalerite), pyrite is one of the main host minerals for gold [20]. However, the leaching of gold from sulfide minerals, including these phases, can lead to undesirable reactions or destabilization of the leaching solution [21]. Numerous studies have focused on understanding the chemistry of the reactions involved and have proposed different mechanisms for the oxidation and dissolution of gold in thiosulfate solutions. Hitherto, a consensus on the oxidation mechanism has not yet been proposed.

Feng and van Deventer (2006) investigated the effect of pyrite in leaching assays via thiosulfate solutions with gold foil, and they observed the generation of a passive layer formed on the surface of the foil which decreased the dissolution of gold. This layer was associated with the possible formation of iron oxides, although the specific type of iron oxide was not identified [22]. Leaching assays in alkaline conditions using pyrite have revealed the production of different species of sulfides, oxides and oxidized iron resulting from the dissolution and oxidation of the mineral. Accordingly, Feng and van Deventer (2006) proposed a catalytic effect of pyrite on the degradation of thiosulfate through the formation of tetrationates, polyithionate, tritionate. Hamilton and Woods [23] also found that the dominant reaction in pyrite dissolution in alkaline solutions involved the formation of Fe(OH)3 and sulfate, as depicted in reaction (1).(1)FeS2 + 11H2O → Fe(OH)3 + 2SO42− + 19H+ + 15e−

For the sulfur species, the formation pathway occurs through metastable sulfur species, such as polysulfides, thiosulfates, tetrationates, tritionates, sulfites, and finally sulfates [24,25]:(2)S2− → S2−n → S0 → S2O32− → S4O62− → S2O42− → S3O62− → SO32- → S2O62− → SO42-

Similarly, Feng and van Deventer (2010) reported the generation of ferric oxide and sulfate from the oxidation of pyrite in thiosulfate (S2O32−) solutions and under oxidative conditions (reaction 2). They proposed that sulfate ions could be formed through the degradation of thiosulfate or by the oxidation of pyrite. Other studies have identified the formation of sulfur species, including adsorbed monoatomic sulfide, polysulfides (Sn−) and polyithionates during the oxidation of pyrite in thiosulfate solutions [[26], [27], [28]].(3)4FeS2 + 15O2 + 16NH3 + 12H2O → 2Fe2O3.2H2O + 8(NH4)2SO4

Other studies have suggested the formation of oxyhydroxides and iron oxides from the dissolution and oxidation of pyrite under alkaline conditions (reactions 1, 3, and 4) [[28], [29], [30]]; however, the identification of these species was not clear.(4)FeS2 + 1.5O2 + 4OH− → 2FeOOH + 2HS- + S2O32−

Carbon paste electrodes (CPE) are useful for characterizing the dissolution of sulfide minerals and have been employed in conjunction with in situ Raman spectroscopy to study the oxidation of sulfide ores [31]. Ammoniacal thiosulfate alkaline solutions have been used to evaluate the anodic dissolution of a pure pyrite (FeS2) sample dispersed in carbon paste electrode (CPE).

The objective of this work is to provide insights of the oxidation process of pyrite through the combination of polarization curves, in situ Raman spectroscopy, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. The analysis of the anodic dissolution process of sulfide ores and the characterization of the chemical species formed on the pyrite surface during the electro-dissolution process in an ammoniacal thiosulfate solution allows to propose a reactional mechanism that explains the active-passive transition of pyrite under these conditions.

Experimental conditions

Mineral samples

The Center for Mining and Agricultural Industry Management at the regional level (CPGMAE) supplied a specimen of high purity pyrite, which was extracted from a typical gold ore sample obtained from a town Remedios mining area in the Antioquia region of Colombia. The single crystals underwent grinding and were then sieved using a Tyler 200 mesh screen (75 μm opening). The purity of the samples was confirmed using a JEOL 6490LV scanning electron microscope equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) for imaging and elemental analysis.

Preparation of electrodes

A description of the setup of carbon paste electrodes (CPE) can be found elsewhere [[32], [33], [34]]. To summary, the mixture was prepared by combining the components in the following proportions: 23.2 wt% sampled mineral (pyrite), 54.4 wt % graphite (99.9995 %, Sigma-Aldrich), and silicone (or nujol) oil 22.4 wt % (C7H8OSi, d25°C 0.963 g cm−3, Sigma-Aldrich). A blank (baseline) was disposed using the same procedure, excluding the addition of the mineral sample to the mixture. A rotating disk electrode (RDE) custom-made was filled with carbon paste (referred to as CPE-Py). The electrode had a geometric (surface area) of 0.071 cm2 and was laterally insulated with PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene). Before each measurement, the electrode surface was refreshed and smoothed using plain paper. Electrical contact was established by a attaching a Cu-wire to a Pt-tip, which was entered into the paste at the electrodes [35].

Chemicals and solutions

The chemical and solutions employed in the study were of high-quality chemicals quality and demineralized water with a conductivity of 18.2 MΩcm. The chemicals utilized included anhydrous ammonium-thiosulfate ((NH4)2S2O3) and 25 % ammonia (NH3) solution. The composition of the ammoniacal thiosulfate solution consisted of 0.2 mol L−1 (NH4)2S2O3 and 0.6 mol L−1 NH3, with a pH range between 10.3 and 10.5.

Electrochemical analyses

Polarization curves and the application of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analyses were conducted in a configuration of three (3) electrode immersed in a glass beaker with a volume of 100 ml. The cell consisted of a graphite rod counter-electrode with a surface area of 510 mm2, and a Ag/AgCl (3.0 M KCl) reference electrode. The rotating carbon paste disk electrodes (RDE – CPE) acted as the working electrode. The potentials in the study were referenced to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) scale, with the assumption that the Ag/AgCl electrode had a potential of 0.209 V vs. SHE. All the electrode potentials in this study are reported vs. SHE. The measurements were conducted using a 302 N Autolab potentiostat/galvanostat (Mark Metrohm-Autolab), performed using the NOVA 1.11 software. Polarization curves were first obtained in potentiostatic mode, followed by measurements at various rotation speeds.

Potentiostatic polarization curves were acquired by using the open circuit potential (OCP) as a reference and applying overpotentials ranging from −90 mV to 500 mV in steps of 30 mV. Each overpotential value was kept constant for a period of 600 s. Prior to each test, the system was allowed to stabilize at the OCP for 1.0 h (see OCP reported in Fig. S2 of the supporting information). Additionally, a polarization curve was recorded using a pyrite-free carbon paste electrode (CPE - baseline) for comparison.

Similarly, polarization curves under hydrodynamic conditions were measured by changing the rotation speed from 100 rpm to 4225 rpm, while the potential was maintained at the OCP.

Following each polarization measurement, EIS measurements were conducted. To avoid interference from passivation phenomena that may occur in previous polarizations, each EIS measurement was performed on a newly prepared surface of the CPE with pyrite (CPE-Py).

In situ Raman spectroscopy

The experiments were conducted in a custom-made electrochemical cell equipped with a 302 N Metrohm Autolab instrument, which was controlled using the NOVA 1.11 software. The working electrode (WE) used in the measurement was a CPE with dispersed pyrite (FeS2) grains, referred to as CPE-Py. The counter-electrode used in the setup was a platinum Pt-wire with an area of 2.0 cm2. The reference electrode was an Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl). The working electrode, with an exposing an area of 0.071 cm2, was enclosed in a polyethylene holder and placed at the uppermost central portion of a transparent cylindrical acrylic container. The counter-electrode was arranged concentrically and in the same plane with the working electrode.

The laser-beam was perpendicularly directed towards the upper surface of the working electrode, which was electrically connected to a Cu-wire located at the lower part of the cell. The LabRAm microprobe confocal system was employed to capture the Raman scattering patterns. A 50× objective with a large working distance was utilized, employing an excitation line at 632.8 nm and a laser power of 1.6 mW. A slit width was configured to 200 μm, and the pinhole diameter was set at 600 μm. Scattering patterns were recorded within the frequency interval covered by 100–1200 cm⁻1, employing an exposure time of 15 s and performing 3 accumulations for each measurement. Initially, the focus was adjusted on the FeS2 grains to capture the Raman scattering patterns at 0 min. Afterward, the (NH4)2S2O3 (ammoniacal thiosulfate) was introduced into the cell, and a period of 60 min was allowed to reach stability. After the period of stabilization, the system was subjected to potentials of 0.1 V and 0.4 V vs. SHE. Raman experiments were executed in situ at various time intervals, spanning from 5 to 180 min. All experiments were performed under a temperature of 20 ± 2 °C, and a freshly prepared solution was utilized for each measurement.

Results and discussion

Potentiostatic polarization curve and EIS

Fig. 1 shows the polarization curves of two carbon paste electrodes: one without mineral (CPE - baseline) and with pyrite up to 300 and 500 mV (CPE-Py, represented by blue and red markers, respectively). The CPE baseline exhibited negligible current changes within the investigated potential range. On the other hand, when exploring anodic potentials up to 300 mV (Fig. 1, CPE-Py in blue) the pyrite CPE displayed a substantial increase in potential current values between 100 and 300 mV vs. SHE, indicating potential oxidation processes of the ore particles. Upon extending the anodic scanning potential range to a maximum of 500 mV (Fig. 1, CPE-Py in red), a pronounced peak of maximum current intensity was observed at around 350 mV vs. SHE, followed by a sharp decline and subsequent reduction in current. This behavior is characteristic of an active-passive response, with oxidation occurring at the maximum current of 350 mV, followed by passivation at the electrode surface which leads to decreased currents. Fig. 2 depicts the impedance spectra of CPE-Py electrodes under quiescent conditions (open circuit potential) and various anodic polarization potentials as shown in Fig. 1. All Nyquist plots display a capacitive half-circle at high frequencies, representing the resistance to charge transfer in parallel with the capacitance of the electrical double layer [37,38]. The impedance spectra obtained at low overpotentials, ranging from OCP to 90 mV vs. OCP (Fig. 2a), display a capacitive semicircle associated with the resistance to charge transfer in parallel with the electrical double layer capacitance on the electrode surface. As the polarization overpotential increases, the impedance of the semicircle decreases, indicating a decrease in charge transfer kinetics. At intermediate anodic overpotentials exceeding 200 mV vs. OCP (Fig. 2b), a second capacitive semicircle emerges at low frequencies, with characteristic frequencies of 0.005 Hz. The appearance of this second semicircle coincides with a change in the slope (dE/dI) of the polarization curve, transitioning to a lower Tafel slope value (from 0.014 to 0.007 V dec−1). This slope change signifies a shift in the dissolution path of the mineral from low to high anodic overpotentials. The presence of the second capacitive semicircle at low frequencies suggests its association with the oxidation of the mineral, possibly involving the formation of at least one intermediate species. Electrochemical study under hydrodynamic conditions

Hydrodynamic polarization curves and EIS

Fig. 4 illustrates the polarization curves obtained for a CPE-Py at varying rotation velocities, as specified in Fig. 4 legend. It is evident that the anodic current increases with the rotation velocities of the electrode. These results indicate that the oxidation process of the mineral is governed by mass transport at all the studied rotation speeds. Moreover, it is noteworthy that the passivation peak potential remains constant regardless of the rotation speed, suggesting that the observed passivation process is unaffected by mass transport. The peak current, characteristic of the oxidation process, occurs at the same potential value and increases with the enhancement of the hydrodynamic conditions in the system. This observation implies that the species responsible for material passivation is formed through a solid-state transformation process from a species previously generated during anodic dissolution. The formation of the pre-passive species is facilitated by mass transport [37].Fig. 4Potentiodynamic polarization curves for carbon paste electrodes with pyrite (CPE-Py), in a solution containing 0.2 mol L−1 ammonium thiosulfate [(NH4)2S2O3] and 0.6 mol L−1 ammonia solution at 25 % (NH3), with a pH range of 10.3–10.5. For polarization values ranging from the OCP to 250 mV vs. OCP (Fig. 5a–f), a decrease in impedance is observed, transitioning from kilohms to a few hundred ohms, as the applied potential increases. This indicates an enhancement in the dissolution kinetics of the material with increasing anodic polarization and hydrodynamic conditions, which is consistent with the observations in the polarization curves (Fig. 4). At an anodic polarization of 350 mV vs. OCP, where the current peak occurs (Fig. 5h), the impedance of the system increases again to the kilohms range compared to that obtained at 250 mV (Fig. 5f). This phenomenon is associated with the passivation of the mineral. No negative resistance was observed at the zero-frequency limit in this impedance diagram (Fig. 5g), as expected during the passivation process. However, an increase in the impedance of the second capacitive arc and a tendency to remain open were observed, indicating an extremely slow passivation process with a characteristic frequency potentially in the microohm range, which is significantly lower than the frequencies analyzed in this study.

Fig. 6a–b presents the electrical parameters calculated from the EIS data. The results indicate a slight decrease in film resistance (Rfilm) and charge transfer resistance (Rct) from 0 to 250 mV vs. OCP overpotential, which aligns with the trends observed in the polarization plots (Fig. 4), where the current density decreases beyond 400 mV vs. OCP. However, beyond 250 mV vs. OCP, both Rfilm and Rct start to increase, suggesting the occurrence of a passivation process or changes in the resistive layer on the electrode surface. Similarly, the characteristic time of the passive film (Tfilm) exhibits an inflection point at 200 mV vs. OCP, indicating possible changes in the passive film. Spectroscopic study the surface oxidation of pyrite in a thiosulfate solution

In situ Raman spectroscopy analysis was employed to carry out a spectroscopic study on the surface oxidation of pyrite in a thiosulfate solution (Fig. 8). The measurements were performed at two anodic potentials: 0.10 V and 0.40 V vs. OCP, representing the initial and final potentials within the range of the hydrodynamic electrochemical study (Fig. 4), Raman spectra at additional potentials are included in supporting information (Fig. S1). The Raman spectrum obtained at 0 min of anodic polarization exhibited characteristic vibrations corresponding to FeS2 pyrite at 344 cm−1, 381 cm−1, and 431 cm−1 [41] (Table 1). After 30 min of polarization at 0.1 V, two new bands were observed in the Raman spectrum (Fig. 8, grey line), which were different from those observed in the initial pyrite spectrum. These peaks were identified at approximately 444 cm−1 and 1000 cm−1 (Fig. 8 - grey line). The peak at 444 cm−1 corresponds to the vibration of ferrous hydroxide Fe(OH)2 vibration, as previously demonstrated in a separate study [31] and reported by other authors [42]. Although other vibrations associated with ferrous hydroxide Fe(OH)2 were not observed, this band was consistently present in the Raman spectra obtained at low anodic overpotentials (0.1 V vs. OCP) - Fig. 8, grey line, between 30 and 180 min). These findings support the results of the previous electrochemical study, which indicated that ferrous hydroxide Fe(OH)2 is a product of the initial stages of pyrite dissolution and oxidation [31]. The peak centered around 1000 cm−1 is associated with the symmetric stretching vibration (SO3) of thiosulfate (S2O32−), as previously identified and reported [[43], [44], [45]]. For the in situ Raman spectrum obtained after applying an anodic overpotential of 0.4 V vs. OCP for 30 min (Fig. 8 - black line, Table 1), two bands associated with symmetric vibrations were observed. One band corresponds to the SO3 symmetric vibrations of sulfate of sulfate (SO42−) at 982 cm−1, and the other band corresponds to tetrathionate (S4O62−) at 1050 cm−1 [43,44]. Additionally, the spectroscopic analysis revealed that the symmetric stretching vibrations of thiosulfate (SO3) were represented by a broad and less defined band, specifically at 1000 cm−1 [43,44].

Afterward, with the increase in polarization time from 30 to 180 min at 0.4 V vs. OCP, additional peaks were observed in the in situ Raman spectra (Fig. 8 - black line). The band associated with la vibration νsym - SO3 of thiosulfate (1000 cm−1) becomes more defined and intense. Furthermore, new bands associated with the formation of sulfur species appeared with increased time. These include three bands for the tetrathionate at 260 cm−1 (vibration δSSinternal), 532 cm−1 (vibration δasym - SO3) and 1040 cm−1 (vibration νsym - SO3). Similarly, three bands for the thiosulfate at 1122, 1129, and 1155 cm−1 (vibration νasym - SO3) and two bands associated with the sulfate at 1110 and 1129 cm−1 (vibration νsym - SO) were observed [43,44].

In addition to the peaks related to sulfur species, the Raman spectrum obtained after 180 min of polarization at 0.4 V vs. OCP (Fig. 8 - black line) reveals bands at 554 and 670 cm−1 associated with the formation of magnetite (Fe3O4) [[46], [47], [48]]. This result has also been previously confirmed and identified through electrochemical measurement [31]. Similarly, bands at 252, 526, and 650 cm−1 appeared, allowing the identification of the Lepidrocrocite (γ-FeOOH) formation [49].

Several studies have proposed that the oxidation of pyrite(F2S) in alkaline condition or ammoniacal thiosulfate ((NH4)2S2O3) solutions results in the formation of hydroxide (Fe(OH)2), oxyhydroxide (FeOOH), and iron oxides (Fe2O3) [[28], [29], [30],50,51]. However, the definitive identification of these compounds has not been achieved. Conversely, other research suggests that the oxidation mechanism of pyrite in alkaline conditions may involve the formation of elemental sulfur (S0) [23,51,52]. In the proposed reactions, sulfide (S2−) is transformed from the inner-surface of pyrite to S0 at the outer surface, which then diffuses into the solution and is oxidized to form metastable sulfur species. The pathway for the formation of metastable sulfur species can be presented based on the standard free energy differences as follows: S2− → S2−n → S0 → S2O32− → S4O62− → S2O42− → S3O62− → SO3 2- → S2O62− → SO4 2-. Peters [25] proposed an oxidation states diagram of sulfur species at pH 10 versus standard free energy. The species above the bridged lines are more unstable compare to sulfide (S2−) and sulfate (SO4 2-), which are the most stable species. Therefore, metastable species produced during sulfide oxidation can form on the pyrite surface as products of its oxidation.

The obtained results, using a solution of 0.20 mol L−1 (NH4)2S2O3 and 0.60 mol L−1_NH3 at pH 10.3, provide confirmation of the benefits of employing in situ experiments. These measurements allowed for the identification of the species formed during pyrite oxidation in ammoniacal thiosulfate solution, to formulate an appropriate oxidation mechanism.

According to the above findings, the anodic dissolution of pyrite initiates with the formation of sulfur and iron (II) hydroxide (Fe(OH)2), as depicted in the first part of the mechanism, reaction (1) (Fig. 9). Subsequently, the anodic oxidation proceeds through two different paths. One path involved the formation of γ-FeOOH/Fe3O4, confirming the proposed in reaction (2).

According to the reaction scheme presented in Fig. 9, the oxidation of pyrite occurs through several pathways. Initially, pyrite experiences oxidation, leading to the formation of an adsorbed Fe(II) species and elemental sulfur (pathway 1). The Fe(II) is oxidized to Fe(II-III) species, which can either form Fe3O4 oxide (magnetite) or an oxyhydroxide FeOOH (lepidocrocite). The formation of these species leads to the passivation of the material (pathway 2). The adsorbed elemental sulfur is oxidized to S(II) species, such as S2O32− ion (thiosulfate), which diffuses into solution (pathway 3). Thiosulfate species then undergo transformation to more oxidized species like tetrathionate and ultimately sulfate [24]. The proposed mechanism in Fig. 9 establishes the equations for mass and charge balance for mathematical validation. Thiosulfate formed in pathway 3 is responsible for mass transport in the system, while Fe(III) species contribute to the passivation of the mineral at anodic overpotentials around 350 mV vs. OCP.

Conclusions

Spectroelectrochemical measurements coupled with in situ Raman spectroscopy were conducted to elucidate the oxidation mechanism of pyrite under alkaline conditions using an ammoniacal thiosulfate solution. This study involved the utilization of high-purity pyrite dispersed in carbon paste (CPE) on rotating disk electrodes. The oxidation tests were performed within a favorable potential range for pyrite dissolution and oxidation. The obtained results demonstrated the effectiveness of these methods in identifying the species formed during anodic dissolution of sulfide ores, such pyrite.

By employing these techniques, the oxidation mechanism of pyrite was successfully elucidated. Previous studies had proposed several potential mechanisms without definitive verification. However, the methodology applied in this research allowed for the identification and proposal of an appropriate oxidation mechanism for pyrite.

The primary discoveries arising from anodic process oxidation of pyrite in the ammoniacal thiosulfate leaching system can be succinctly outlined as follows:1)The anodic polarization currents of the carbon paste electrode with pyrite (CPE-Py) are directly related to the oxidation processes of pyrite.2)Passivation processes are independent of hydrodynamics, indicating that passivation occurs in the solid state.3)During the passivation process, various species are formed, which accumulate on the electrode surface and lead to a decrease in the polarization current.4)Electrochemical impedance and in situ Raman spectroscopy assays allowed for the identification of the products formed throughout the oxidation of pyrite in the ammoniacal thiosulfate leaching system (pH = 10.2) as a function of the applied anodic potential5)At low anodic potentials (0.100 V vs. OCP), species such as Fe(OH)2 and polysulfides were identified. At high anodic potentials (0.400 V vs. OCP), the formation of iron products (Fe3O4 and γ-FeOOH) and soluble sulfide species, including thiosulfate, tetrathionates, and sulfate (S2O32−, S4O6−2 and SO42−).
