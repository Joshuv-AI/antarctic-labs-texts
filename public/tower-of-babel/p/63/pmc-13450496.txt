Establishment of the first nationwide registry for inherited hemoglobin and blood-related disorders (IHBD) in Saudi Arabia: design and clinical evaluation
Authors: Hatoon Ezzat, Ahmed Khalifa, Linah Tasji, Mohammed Al-Abdulali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInherited hemoglobinopathies, particularly sickle cell disease (SCD) and thalassemia, represent a major public health burden in Saudi Arabia. However, comprehensive nationwide real-world data have historically been fragmented across healthcare facilities. This study describes the development, governance framework, and epidemiological characterization of the Saudi National Inherited Hemoglobin and Blood Disorders (IHBD) Registry and reports population-level patterns derived from unified Ministry of Health (MOH) data integration.MethodsA nationwide registry infrastructure was established under the MOH using phased implementation: retrospective manual digitization of historical records (2016–2018), followed by phased automated electronic medical record (EMR) integration implemented between 2019 and 2025. Between 2019 and 2025, 166,727 IHBD-related diagnostic entries were extracted from 31 MOH-affiliated hospitals. Two different data sources were leveraged. For all records, 3-year retrospective data were entered, and prospective data were utilized moving forward in the IHBD registry. Records were consolidated using national identification numbers to generate a de-duplicated patient-level dataset. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and geographic distributions. IHBD Registry coverage per 100,000 population was calculated using the 2024 Saudi census estimate. Associations between disease groups and geographic regions were evaluated using chi-square testing.ResultsAfter deduplication, 105,008 unique patients with inherited blood disorders were identified, corresponding to approximately 298 patients per 100,000 population in the IHBD Registry. The registry captured 105,008 unique diagnosed patients (surveillance coverage), of whom 65,679 (62.6%) had fully structured, longitudinally validated records within the registry module. SCD represented the predominant subgroup. Geographic clustering was observed, with the Eastern (32.0%), Makkah (28.1%), and Riyadh (19.1%) regions accounting for the majority of registered cases. Approximately 32.6% of patients had multiple IHBD-related diagnoses, reflecting overlapping hemoglobinopathy and bleeding disorder classifications within the healthcare system.ConclusionsThe IHBD Registry provides the first unified, nationwide MOH-based dataset for inherited hemoglobinopathies, enabling population-level surveillance and regional burden assessment. By integrating phased data consolidation and EMR interoperability, the registry establishes a scalable national infrastructure for rare hematologic disease monitoring. These findings support evidence-based resource allocation, regional health planning, and future expansion toward integrated screening, longitudinal outcome tracking, and advanced analytic applications.Keywords: Sickle cell disease (SCD), Thalassemia, National registry, Vaso-occlusive crisis, Real-world evidence, Hemoglobinopathies, Healthcare utilization

Introduction

National patient registries for inherited hematologic disorders, such as sickle cell disease (SCD) and thalassemia, are essential for advancing epidemiological surveillance, optimizing disease management, and informing healthcare policy. These registries facilitate the collection of longitudinal real-world data that improve disease characterization and provide insight into treatment outcomes and healthcare utilization [1, 2]. Inherited hemoglobinopathies, including SCD and thalassemia, remain among the most prevalent genetic disorders worldwide, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, the Indian subcontinent, and Mediterranean regions [2, 3]. According to World Health Organization (WHO) estimates, SCD carrier rates in parts of Africa range from 10 to 40%, with approximately 453,000–515,000 neonates affected annually [3]. Thalassemia also represents a major global health burden, with an estimated 80 million β-thalassemia carriers worldwide [2].

In Saudi Arabia, SCD demonstrates marked geographic clustering, with the highest prevalence reported in the Eastern Province (145 cases per 10,000 population), followed by the Southern (24/10,000), Western (12/10,000), and Central regions (6/10,000) [4]. The national Premarital Screening (PMS) program further estimates that 0.29% of the adult population is affected by SCD mutations, with a carrier rate of 4.36%, while thalassemia carrier and affected rates are estimated at 1.4% and 0.05%, respectively [4]. Despite this substantial disease burden, real-world epidemiological data in Saudi Arabia remain fragmented across healthcare institutions, limiting comprehensive surveillance, longitudinal monitoring, and evidence-based healthcare planning.

International registry initiatives have demonstrated significant improvements in disease surveillance, care coordination, and quality monitoring for inherited blood disorders [5]. However, most existing registries are region-specific, disease-specific, or limited in electronic integration. To date, no unified nationwide MOH-based longitudinal hemoglobinopathy registry integrating electronic medical record (EMR) data across Saudi Arabia has been systematically described.

This study presents the development and implementation of the Saudi National Inherited Hemoglobin and Blood Disorders (IHBD) Registry, an executive project managed by the Blood Disorders Administration (BDA) at the Saudi MOH. The registry integrates retrospective and real-time clinical data for SCD, thalassemia, hemophilia, and other inherited blood disorders through connected dashboards and centralized data systems to support clinical decision-making, healthcare planning, and research advancement.

Several international initiatives, including the CDC Sickle Cell Data Collection (SCDC) program in the United States, the UK National Haemoglobinopathy Registry (NHR), and the Indian Sickle Cell Disease Registry, have demonstrated the value of registry-based surveillance systems for inherited blood disorders [6–8]. These registries support longitudinal monitoring, healthcare planning, and quality improvement activities; however, many remain region-specific, disease-specific, or limited in real-time electronic medical record (EMR) integration and nationwide interoperability [6–8].

In Saudi Arabia and the broader Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, comprehensive nationwide longitudinal registries integrating centralized governance, standardized data harmonization, real-time dashboards, and unified EMR-based surveillance remain limited [5, 9–13]. The Saudi National Inherited Hemoglobin and Blood Disorders (IHBD) Registry was therefore developed to address these gaps through nationwide EMR integration, centralized patient identification, standardized governance, and real-time dashboard connectivity across Ministry of Health (MOH)-affiliated hospitals.

Methods

This study represents a nationwide retrospective registry-based observational study using routinely collected administrative and clinical data from MOH hospitals.

Registry establishment and objectives

The Saudi National Inherited Hemoglobin and Blood Disorders (IHBD) Registry was established by the MOH to provide a centralized, longitudinal surveillance platform for inherited blood disorders, including sickle cell disease (SCD), thalassemia, hemophilia, and other rare bleeding disorders. The registry was designed to support epidemiological research, optimize disease management, enable healthcare utilization monitoring, and inform national health planning and policy development.

The registry integrates multi-source clinical data into a unified digital infrastructure, enabling structured data analysis and real-time decision support through interactive dashboards.

Governance structure and regulatory framework

To oversee registry development and ensure compliance with national regulations, the MOH convened a multidisciplinary Inherited Blood Disorders Committee. The committee included hematologists, epidemiologists, health informaticians, registry coordinators, and data scientists. The governance framework focused on standardized International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD-10) case definitions, data harmonization across participating hospitals, quality assurance and validation protocols, and alignment with Saudi health information governance regulations. International best practices from the International Society of Pharmacoepidemiology (ISPE) and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) were incorporated to enhance methodological rigor and sustainability.

Ethical approval and data protection

Ethical approval was obtained from the Central MOH Institutional Review Board (IRB) and the Saudi National Committee for Bioethics.

All registry data are stored on MOH-secured servers. Patient confidentiality is maintained through strict de-identification protocols, encrypted storage environments, and restricted role-based access controls. The registry complies fully with Saudi national data protection standards.

Study population and geographic coverage

The registry includes all patients diagnosed with inherited blood disorders who received care at 31 MOH-affiliated hospitals across eight administrative regions of Saudi Arabia.

Participating institutions include tertiary referral centers, suburban hospitals, and rural healthcare facilities, ensuring comprehensive national representation and minimizing geographic bias.

Inclusion criteria comprised all patients with at least one documented ICD-10 code corresponding to inherited hemoglobinopathies or inherited bleeding disorders within participating MOH facilities during the study period. Patients without valid national identification numbers were excluded from patient-level analyses.

Data extraction, consolidation, and validation

Initial data extraction

Between 2019 and 2025, MOH hospital information systems recorded 166,727 IHBD-related diagnostic entries based on standardized ICD-10 codes covering hemoglobinopathies, hemophilia, and rare bleeding disorders.

Patient-level consolidation

Using national identification numbers as unique patient identifiers, duplicate records were consolidated, resulting in 105,008 unique IHBD patients. This confirms that individual patients may carry multiple IHBD-related diagnoses within the healthcare system.

Data validation

Data cleaning procedures included duplicate detection and removal, cross-hospital record reconciliation, diagnostic consistency checks, and verification of missing values. Records with missing key identifiers were excluded from patient-level consolidation. Missing demographic variables were reported, but not imputed, and analyses were conducted on an available-case basis.

Multi-phase registry implementation

To ensure longitudinal completeness and historical depth, registry implementation was conducted in two structured phases:

Phase I – retrospective manual digitization (2019–2023)

This phase included manual abstraction of paper-based medical records and digitization of historical legacy files covering retrospective patient data from 2016–2018, prior to full EMR adoption. Data validation and harmonization were performed by trained registry coordinators.

Phase II – automated EMR integration (2023–2025)

This phase introduced automated near real-time EMR ingestion, standardized data exchange protocols, continuous updating of patient records, and integration of structured laboratory and treatment data.

The 166,727 diagnostic entries reported in the current study represent the unified operational registry dataset extracted between 2019 and 2025. Historical retrospective records from 2016–2018 were incorporated during Phase I to support longitudinal enrichment and retrospective validation.

Data elements and clinical variables

Across both phases, collected variables included demographic characteristics, genotypic and phenotypic classifications, disease complications, laboratory findings, pharmacological treatments, transfusion history, vaso-occlusive crises (VOCs), and healthcare utilization and hospitalization metrics.

Current registry scope

The IHBD registry currently maintains over 65,000 fully structured patient records with validated longitudinal profiles. The system integrates Newborn Screening (NBS) and PMS data spanning 2004–2025. Registry expansion and system optimization remain ongoing, with progressive integration of additional healthcare facilities and analytic modules.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.13) with the Pandas, NumPy, and SciPy libraries. Data preprocessing and validation were performed prior to analysis to ensure accuracy and internal consistency.

Epidemiological analyses were conducted on the de-duplicated patient-level dataset (N = 105,008 unique patients), whereas registry operational metrics reflect the actively structured cohort. Diagnosis-level analyses were performed separately when appropriate to account for total recorded diagnostic events (N = 166,727).

Continuous variables (e.g., age) were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median with interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Age was calculated using a fixed reference date (January 1, 2025) to ensure reproducibility. Patients were categorized into predefined age groups (0–5, 6–12, 13–18, 19–40, 41–60, and > 60 years).

Associations between categorical variables (e.g., disease group and geographic region) were evaluated using the chi-square (χ2) test. Effect size was quantified using Cramér’s V to assess the strength of association. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated for national coverage estimates. All analyses were conducted using the finalized cleaned dataset extracted from the MOH Data Center and were fully reproducible.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate predictors of SCD diagnosis. SCD status was modeled as a binary dependent variable, with age, sex, and province entered as independent variables. Patients with missing demographic or regional covariates were excluded from the regression analysis. Riyadh Province was used as the reference category for province-level comparisons. Model fit was assessed using the likelihood ratio chi-square test and pseudo R-squared, and results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs and p-values.

Results

Based on the 2024 Saudi population estimate (35,300,280) [14], the national IHBD registry corresponds to approximately 298 registered patients per 100,000 population (105,008 unique patients).

Between 2019 and 2025, a total of 166,727 IHBD-related diagnostic records were identified. After consolidation using national identification numbers, 105,008 unique patients were confirmed.

Among these, 70,748 patients (67.4%) had a single diagnosis, while 34,260 (32.6%) had multiple IHBD-related diagnoses.

Statistical regional and demographic associations

Associations between primary disease group and geographic region were evaluated using the chi-square (χ2) test on the patient-level dataset (N = 105,008). A highly significant association was observed between disease group and health region (χ2 = 22,099.30; df = 60; p < 0.001), indicating non-random geographic distribution of inherited blood disorders across Saudi Arabia. Effect size estimation using Cramér’s V demonstrated a small-to-moderate association (V = 0.195).

Similarly, age group distribution differed significantly across disease categories (χ2 = 2,801.47; df = 25; p < 0.001), although the effect size was small (Cramér’s V = 0.069). The mean age of the cohort was 31.46 years (SD = 18.62), with a median age of 30.19 years. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the majority of SCD cases were concentrated in the 15–49 year age group, with a gradual decline in older age categories.

IHBD registry focused insights

Of 105,008 patients, 65,679 (62.6%) were fully enrolled in the IHBD Registry, including 36,565 individuals with SCD (55.7%) and 2,461 with thalassemia (3.8%). The remaining cases consisted of inherited bleeding disorders, as well as newborns who were incorporated during the later phases of the IHBD project to ensure comprehensive monitoring, as shown in Fig. 6.

Distribution of fully structured IHBD Registry records, including SCD, thalassemia, inherited bleeding disorders, and NBS-linked registry entries

The NBS/NB Cases subgroup included 23,732 registry-linked neonatal screening records incorporated during later implementation phases to support early surveillance and longitudinal monitoring. Among these, 8,040 cases were related to glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, while 15,541 cases were categorized under hemoglobin electrophoresis/HPLC-based HGBE classifications. The HGBE category included screening patterns such as FAB, FAC, FAD, FAE, FAS, FS, FSC, FSD, FSE, and related hemoglobin variants, excluding thalassemia classifications. Within the HGBE subgroup, 568 cases represented confirmed disease patterns, whereas 14,973 represented carrier or trait-related findings. An additional 151 entries remained temporarily unclassified pending confirmatory validation.

Disease phenotypic and genotypic characteristics

Among the SCD patients, the most prevalent phenotype was homozygous sickle cell anemia (HbSS), representing 75.9% of the SCD cohort. This finding aligns with global data from regions with high SCD prevalence, particularly in areas with historical gene flow from Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. The HbSC phenotype accounted for 9.7% of SCD cases. The remaining 14.4% comprised other, less common variants of SCD, indicating the genetic heterogeneity within the Saudi population.

In the thalassemia cohort, β-thalassemia major was the predominant genotype, affecting 50.5% of patients. This severe form of thalassemia often requires regular blood transfusions and chelation therapy as well as endocrinopathies such as Diabetes Mellitus, significantly impacting healthcare resources. β-thalassemia intermedia, a less severe form, was identified in 34.2% of patients, often characterized by moderate anemia and less frequent transfusion dependency. Other thalassemia variants made up the remaining 15.3%, highlighting the need for comprehensive genetic screening to accurately identify the spectrum of hemoglobinopathies present in the population.

Treatment patterns and healthcare utilization

The therapeutic management of SCD and thalassemia within the registry reflects real-world treatment practices. Although hydroxyurea is guideline-recommended, utilization remained relatively limited 15.2% in SCD. Hydroxyurea is widely recognized for its role in reducing the frequency of VOCs and improving hematological outcomes by inducing fetal hemoglobin (HbF) production. Potential contributors to the relatively low hydroxyurea utilization may include treatment adherence challenges, variability in prescribing practices, limited follow-up, and patient-related factors. Blood transfusions were administered via simple exchange in 81.5% of cases. Despite the benefits of both therapies, the relatively low usage rate indicates potential barriers such as patient compliance, side effect profiles, or availability issues, warranting further investigation into healthcare delivery practices.

The registry data revealed a high incidence of VOCs among SCD patients, with 66.3% reporting at least one VOC episode during the study period. These crises, often precipitated by infection, dehydration, or stress, result in significant morbidity and frequently necessitate acute medical intervention. The registry currently reports cumulative VOC occurrence during the observation period; future longitudinal analyses will incorporate annualized VOC rates and person-year follow-up metrics to provide more robust estimates of disease severity and healthcare utilization over time. The high rate of Emergency Department (ED) visits further underscores the acute care needs of these patients, with 43% of SCD and 49% of thalassemia patients requiring ED services at least once for acute complications or blood transfusion.

Among thalassemia patients, emergency department utilization was primarily associated with transfusion-related care, symptomatic anemia, infection-related complications, and acute pain or cardiopulmonary symptoms requiring urgent evaluation.

Because follow-up duration varied across patients and hospitals during phased registry implementation, cumulative VOC occurrence was reported in the present analysis to avoid biased annualized estimations.

Hospital admission rates were also notably high, with 34.8% of SCD patients and 43% of thalassemia patients needing inpatient care during the observation period. The requirement for hospital-based interventions highlights the chronic and recurrent nature of disease exacerbations. Additionally, Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admissions were documented for 4% of SCD patients, indicating severe complications such as acute chest syndrome or multi-organ failure. These findings reflect the substantial healthcare burden posed by these disorders and underscore the need for integrated care models and enhanced preventive strategies.

Although the registry contains genotype/phenotype fields and healthcare utilization variables, direct linkage between specific genotypes (e.g., HbSS or HbSC) and ICU admission frequency was not analyzed in the present descriptive phase because genotype completeness and longitudinal follow-up time varied across hospitals. Phase III will prioritize genotype-linked outcome analyses, including ICU admissions, VOC frequency, transfusion burden, and hospitalization rates.

Among thalassemia patients, iron chelation therapy was a critical component of management, particularly for those undergoing regular blood transfusions. A total of 1,165 patients received chelation to mitigate iron overload, which, if left unmanaged, can lead to severe complications such as hepatic cirrhosis and cardiac dysfunction. The administration of chelation therapy reflects proactive management to prevent long-term morbidity in transfusion-dependent patients.

Economic burden and financial impact

In Saudi Arabia, the total SCD burden from a societal perspective was 3.6 times higher than the payer’s perspective across all age groups. Acute complications represented 91% and 86% of the total economic burden of SCD from the payer’s and societal perspectives, respectively. Among acute complications, the cost of VOC management was the main driver of SCD burden, accounting for 82% of costs from the payers’ perspective and 76% from the societal perspective. Among ‘VOC management’, hospital visits accounted for 60% of the main cost driver from a payer’s perspective, whereas productivity loss and out-of-pocket (OOP) expenses accounted for 36% and 32%, respectively, from a societal perspective. Patients with ≥ 5 VOC per year incurred the highest annual cost across all age groups. These estimates were derived from previously published national economic evaluations [11, 13].

Summary of key findings

The IHBD Registry successfully captured over 65,000 detailed patient records, representing the largest and first national-level longitudinal dataset for inherited blood disorders in Saudi Arabia. SCD remains the predominant disorder nationwide, accounting for approximately 62% of all registered cases under primary classification. Regional and city-level distributions indicate highly localized disease clusters, particularly in the Eastern, Makkah, and Southern regions. These findings confirm long-recognized epidemiological patterns and reinforce the value of a unified IHBD Registry for monitoring disease burden, tracking outcomes, and planning targeted health interventions.

Future work (phase III)

The planned Phase III expansion of the IHBD Registry aims to transition the system from a descriptive national repository into a patient-centered digital platform incorporating predictive analytics and enhanced clinical decision support.

Future development will focus on expanding data integration beyond hospital-based encounters to include structured linkage with the NBS and PMS programs. This integration is expected to facilitate longitudinal monitoring and earlier identification of individuals at risk for inherited blood disorders. Available data for NBS date back to 2023, while PMS is an older screening program in Saudi Arabia, dating back to 2004.

As a quality check of patient records, all diagnostic ICD-10 codes will be validated using the diagnostic laboratory method (Hemoglobin electrophoresis and/or HPLC).

Because the current registry infrastructure is primarily integrated across MOH-affiliated hospitals, patients managed exclusively in private, military, or specialized academic healthcare systems may be underrepresented. This may introduce selection bias and affect the generalizability of prevalence and severity estimates at the national level. Future Phase III expansion aims to improve representativeness through integration with additional healthcare sectors, including tertiary referral and academic institutions such as university hospitals, King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center, National Guard hospitals, and military healthcare systems. Integration with national genomic and digital health initiatives—including genomic sequencing platforms and population health programs—will further enable comprehensive linkage of clinical, phenotypic, and genetic data.

Phase III also envisions incorporation of patient-reported outcomes (PROs), including quality of life measures, treatment adherence indicators, pain burden, and functional status. These enhancements may support the development of AI-assisted predictive models for complication risk stratification and clinical pathway optimization.

Future dashboard enhancements will include role-based access control and advanced analytics modules to support clinicians, researchers, and policymakers while ensuring compliance with MOH data governance regulations.

Moreover, future expansions of the registry may incorporate artificial intelligence–based analytic frameworks to support early detection, risk stratification, and clinical decision support. Controlled evaluations of AI-assisted screening approaches in hemoglobinopathies have demonstrated promising diagnostic performance, suggesting potential integration pathways within national registry infrastructures [16].

Through these phased enhancements, the IHBD Registry has the potential to evolve into a national decision-support infrastructure aligned with Saudi Arabia’s digital health transformation strategy.

Discussion

The IHBD Registry provides the first comprehensive national overview of inherited blood disorders in Saudi Arabia based on real-world clinical data. A total of 105,008 unique patients were identified, of whom 65,679 were fully enrolled within the structured registry module. Under primary disease classification, sickle cell disease (SCD) represented the largest subgroup (65,287 patients), followed by sickle cell trait and thalassemia-related disorders.

The registry demonstrated marked geographic clustering. Province-level analysis showed concentration of cases in the Eastern, Makkah, and Southern regions, with multivariable regression indicating substantially higher adjusted odds of SCD in Jazan and Aseer provinces compared to Riyadh. These findings align with historically reported regional hemoglobinopathy patterns while providing updated population-level quantification.

Although age distribution patterns were evaluated, the current registry design and follow-up duration do not yet allow robust survival or life expectancy estimation analyses.

These results align with global experiences demonstrating that national registries and digital dashboards are essential tools for improving the management of complex chronic disorders. For example, the Indian SCD Registry (ISCDR) demonstrated the feasibility of electronic data capture in geographically concentrated populations by integrating mobile technology for SCD surveillance [17]. Similarly, the National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission Dashboard in India emphasized screening and prevention at a national scale to reduce disease prevalence [18]. While these systems share conceptual similarities with the IHBD Registry, their scope is predominantly limited to SCD and carrier detection, whereas the Saudi model incorporates a wider spectrum of inherited blood disorders—including thalassemia and hemophilia—making it more comprehensive in terms of disease representation.

The IHBD Registry also differs in its architectural complexity and degree of integration. Dashboards such as the Indiana SCD Data Collection (IN-SCDC) leverage multi-source datasets to inform local policies and targeted community interventions; however, their coverage remains state-based and fragmented rather than nationally unified. In contrast, the IHBD Registry draws data from 31 MOH-affiliated hospitals, integrates NBS and PMS programs, and uses the national ID system to consolidate patient encounters, thereby achieving a level of continuity and interoperability unmatched by U.S. or Indian systems [6, 7, 17, 18]. This national integration allows the IHBD Registry to overcome limitations reported in prior registries, such as incomplete demographic capture, duplicated patient entries, and disconnected clinical records [6, 7].

Furthermore, while Electronic Health Records (EHR)-based registries at centers such as the University of California, San Diego, and the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia have demonstrated measurable improvements in preventive care and hydroxyurea utilization through targeted metrics [6, 7], these remain institutional implementations. The IHBD Registry extends this approach to a nationwide platform, offering stakeholders aggregated, real-time dashboards for policy formulation, resource allocation, and regional prioritization, particularly in high-burden provinces such as Jazan, Aseer, and the Eastern region. Such system-level decision support positions the IHBD Registry as more than a clinical database—it functions as a digital health infrastructure aligned with Saudi Arabia’s national healthcare transformation vision.

Implications for health policy and strategic planning

The data generated by the IHBD Registry are not only valuable for clinical management but also serve as a cornerstone for health policy formulation. Key policy implications include:

Resource Allocation and Regional Health Planning: The registry’s real-time epidemiological data allow the MOH to prioritize healthcare resources efficiently. Regions with high disease burdens, such as the Eastern and Southern provinces, can be allocated more specialized care units, hydroxyurea supply, transfusion facilities, and iron chelation therapies.

Resource Allocation and Regional Health Planning: The registry’s real-time epidemiological data allow the MOH to prioritize healthcare resources efficiently. Comparison with international registry models

The management of SCD and other inherited hemoglobinopathies increasingly relies on structured registries and digital dashboards to enhance surveillance, care coordination, and policy development. These platforms enable systematic data collection, longitudinal monitoring, and the evaluation of clinical quality indicators, thereby improving healthcare planning and patient outcomes.

Several national and institutional initiatives have demonstrated the value of registry-based approaches.

The Indian Sickle Cell Disease Registry (ISCDR) utilizes an Android-based application integrated with a centralized dashboard to collect comprehensive patient data, including clinical history and treatment records. Within one year of implementation, the system registered 324 SCD patients and 1,771 carriers, demonstrating feasibility in resource-constrained settings [17]. Complementing this effort, the National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission Dashboard in India supports nationwide screening and management strategies aimed at eliminating SCD as a public health concern by 2047. The initiative integrates awareness programs, universal screening, genetic counseling, and multi-level clinical management across 17 high-prevalence states [18].

In the United States, the Indiana Sickle Cell Data Collection (IN-SCDC) Dashboard integrates multi-source clinical and administrative data across the Indiana healthcare ecosystem, enabling longitudinal surveillance from 2015 onward. Supported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), this system facilitates epidemiological analysis, health disparity assessment, and policy planning [20]. Similarly, EHR-based registries developed at the University of California San Diego demonstrate high accuracy in identifying SCD patients 92.6% positive predictive value), improving preventive care outreach and monitoring quality metrics [6]. At the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, the Comprehensive Sickle Cell Center (CSCC) implemented an EHR-integrated registry that significantly improved adherence to clinical performance indicators, including transcranial Doppler screening and hydroxyurea utilization [7].

For rare diseases more broadly, the OSSE dashboard framework provides structured visualization of registry metrics, including patient counts and biospecimen availability, offering a scalable model for data-driven rare disease management [8]. At the national level, the UK National Haemoglobinopathy Registry (NHR) supports longitudinal outcome tracking and therapeutic monitoring across England [10], while the Sickle Cell Data Collection (SCDC) program in the United States conducts multi-state surveillance of healthcare utilization, survival, and treatment trends [9]. Additionally, the Thalassemia International Federation (TIF) registry aggregates international data from 62 member countries, strengthening global advocacy and policy reform initiatives [12].

Despite these advancements, common challenges persist across registry systems, including ensuring data quality, long-term sustainability, cybersecurity, interoperability with national health information systems, and adaptability to evolving clinical requirements. Many registries rely on periodic data uploads or partial integration with EMR, which may introduce latency and manual abstraction errors.

When compared with these international models, the Saudi National Inherited Hemoglobin and Blood Disorders (IHBD) Registry distinguishes itself through comprehensive EMR integration, real-time data capture, and nationwide coverage across 31 MOH–affiliated hospitals. This centralized integration reduces data fragmentation, minimizes manual errors, and enables near real-time surveillance. Furthermore, regional disease mapping within the registry has identified the Eastern Province as the primary epicenter of SCD prevalence (31.6%), generating actionable insights for targeted intervention strategies and resource allocation at the national level.

Despite its achievements, the IHBD Registry faces challenges similar to those noted in other registries, including phased enrollment, variability in coding practices, and the need for standardized phenotyping and treatment pathway documentation. However, unlike systems described in Related Work, the Saudi model has already incorporated plans for Phase III expansion, including AI-driven analytics, integration with additional ecosystem platforms, and the capture of patient-reported outcomes, features that could bridge many of the gaps observed in earlier international efforts.

In summary, while several international registries focus primarily on SCD or carrier detection, the IHBD Registry incorporates multiple inherited blood disorders within a unified national framework. Its integration with national identification systems and centralized MOH hospital networks provides a level of continuity that addresses limitations commonly reported in fragmented registry systems, such as duplicated records and incomplete demographic capture.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, although the registry is longitudinal in design, the current analysis is primarily descriptive and cross-sectional because follow-up duration varied across hospitals and implementation phases. Therefore, cumulative outcomes such as VOC occurrence, emergency department utilization, and hospital admission should not be interpreted as annual incidence rates. Second, the present dataset is based on MOH-affiliated hospitals; patients managed exclusively in private, military, or specialized academic centers may be underrepresented, which may affect the generalizability of prevalence and severity estimates. Third, patient identification relied initially on ICD-10 coding, and although diagnostic consistency checks were performed, complete phenotypic confirmation using hemoglobin electrophoresis, HPLC, or genetic testing was not available for all patients at this stage. Future Phase III expansion will strengthen laboratory-based validation, cross-sector integration, and person-year longitudinal analyses.

Conclusion

The Saudi national IHBD Registry marks a major milestone in improving disease surveillance and management across the Kingdom. Through standardized data collection, stakeholder collaboration, and integration with EMR, the registry has generated valuable insights into disease prevalence, treatment patterns, and healthcare burden. Looking ahead, expanding registry coverage to more healthcare institutions, incorporating patient-reported outcomes, and leveraging artificial intelligence–driven analytics will further strengthen its role in enhancing research, clinical decision-making, and national health planning.
