Variations in energy and nutrient intake across competitive-season phases in relation to athletes’ competitive schedule: the case of university male track and field jumpers by competitive level
Authors: Taketo Yamaguchi, Ryoma Fujii, Rikako Yoshitake, Yuki Aikawa, Naomi Omi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Purpose]Appropriate dietary intake across different training phases is crucial for improving the performance of track and field jumpers. However, few studies have investigated dietary intake based on the training phase and competitive level. The purpose of this study was to evaluate dietary intake in track and field jumpers across different training phases and competitive levels.[Methods]A total of 28 male collegiate jumpers participated. A food frequency questionnaire assessing 1-month dietary intake was administered four times: transition phase (TP), preparatory phase (PP), early competitive phase (ECP), and late competitive phase (LCP). Participants were divided into the Exceeded Group (EG) and the Nonexceeded Group (NEG) based on competition standards.[Results]Of the 28 participants, 20 were included in the final analysis. Energy, protein, fat, vitamin B1, and vitamin B6 intake decreased during the ECP and LCP compared with intake during the TP. Intake of these nutrients was higher in the EG than in the NEG, with the EG significantly showing higher intake during the TP. However, these group differences were not observed during the LCP.[Conclusion]These results indicate the necessity of appropriate dietary strategies for each training phase.Keywords: macronutrients, micronutrients, periodization, athletic performance, dietary intake

INTRODUCTION

In track and field, jump events include high jump, pole vault, long jump, and triple jump. Speed and explosive movements are the major determinants of athletic performance in these events. Therefore, optimizing body weight and composition through appropriate dietary intake is necessary for improving these factors [1]. Furthermore, proper energy and nutrient intake have been reported to reduce the risk of injury and illness and enhance recovery ability [2]. Therefore, ensuring appropriate nutrition throughout the year is crucial.

In track and field, a one-year season is divided into three phases: transition phase (TP), preparatory phase (PP), and competitive phase (CP) [3]. This periodization allows athletes to improve their competitive performance. In jump events, the TP is a period for physical and mental recovery. Training during the PP focuses on increasing muscle mass and acquiring technical skills to enhance performance during the CP, whereas training during the CP focuses on adjusting body weight and fat mass to optimize the power-to-weight ratio [4]. Mujika et al. [5] reported that dietary intake should be planned and flexibly adjusted according to individual goals, volume, and training intensity, which change throughout the season and training phases. However, dietary intake during different training phases of jump events has not been investigated. Additionally, how dietary intake varies across the season remains unclear. Clarifying this can provide fundamental data on appropriate energy and nutrient intake for each season.

A previous study involving baseball players reported that dietary intake differed according to competitive level [6]. Similarly, dietary intake among jumpers differs according to competitive level. Clarifying these differences can provide valuable insights into improving competitive performance.

Therefore, this study investigated the actual variations in dietary intake according to the training phase and competitive level in male track and field jumpers.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

A total of 28 male collegiate track and field jumpers participated in this study. Among them, six were high jumpers, five were pole vaulters, eight were long jumpers, and nine were triple jumpers. Participants who could not complete all assessments due to injury or health issues were excluded. The study’s purpose and procedures were explained to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained. This study was conducted in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Participants and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Institute of Health and Sport Sciences, University of Tsukuba (No. 024-42).

Dietary Assessment

A food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was administered four times to assess dietary intake over the preceding 1-month period for each phase: December 2023 (TP), March 2024 (PP), June (early CP: early competitive phase (ECP)), and August (late CP: late competitive phase (LCP)). A shortened version of the FFQ NEXT (Kenpakusha) with 66 items was used to account for the possibility that participants, as collegiate students, frequently consumed meals outside of home or used takeout options and to reduce the burden of the survey [7]. The questionnaire has been validated in collegiate athletes [8]. Energy and nutrient intake were assessed using the FFQ NEXT, a specialized software developed based on the Standard Tables of Food Composition in Japan, 2020 (Eighth Revised Edition).

Grouping by Competitive Level

Participants were categorized into two groups based on the entry standard for the 2024 Kanto Inter-University Track and Field Championships (high jump: 2.03 m, pole vault: 4.90 m, long jump: 7.40 m, and triple jump: 14.90 m). Participants who exceeded the entry standard were assigned to the Exceeded Group (EG), whereas those who did not meet the standards were assigned to the Nonexceeded Group (NEG). This competition is an event in which all universities across the Kanto region compete to their fullest potential. With live internet broadcasting, it holds considerable importance for collegiate athletes in the Kanto region. The World Athletics scores for the EG and NEG were 1,034 ± 35 and 888 ± 65, respectively, with a significant difference observed between the two groups (Table 1).

Body Composition Measurements

Body composition was measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (Horizon, Hologic, QDR System Software Version 12.6). Body weight was measured using an electronic scale (TANITA, MC-190), and height was measured using a stadiometer (NAVIS, YS-OS). All measurements were performed early in the morning under a fasting condition to avoid the influence of food intake.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0). Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparisons of participant characteristics between groups were made using an independent t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed variables. For the analysis of dietary intake, normality was confirmed for all variables. Therefore, a two-way mixed analysis of covariance was conducted to examine the main effects of phase (within-subject factor) and group (between-subject factor) and their interaction. Jump-event type was incorporated into the model as an adjustment factor to statistically control for the influence of event-specific differences. The assumption of sphericity was evaluated using Mauchly’s test. When violated, degrees of freedom were adjusted using the Greenhouse–Geisser correction. Post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Bonferroni’s adjustment to maintain the family-wise error rate when significant main effects or interactions were observed. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η2p). The 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) of the mean difference were presented for the primary outcomes. For all analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

Of the 28 male collegiate track and field jumpers, 8 were excluded for not completing the assessments across all four phases due to injuries or health issues. Consequently, the final analysis included 20 male collegiate, comprising 5 high jumpers (EG, n = 3; NEG, n = 2), 3 pole vaulters (EG, n = 3; NEG, n = 0), 5 long jumpers (EG, n = 3; NEG, n = 2), and 7 triple jumpers (EG, n = 3; NEG, n = 4). Table 1 shows the participants’ age, physical characteristics (height, body mass, and body mass index), and body composition (fat-free mass and percentage body fat) at the beginning of the TP. No significant differences in participant characteristics were observed between the EG and the NEG.

Energy and Nutrient Intake

Figure 1 illustrates variations in energy and nutrient intake according to the training phase and competitive level. A significant interaction between phase and group was observed for vitamin B1 (η2p = 0.184), indicating that the pattern of reduction across phases differed between the EG and NEG. No significant interactions were observed for all other variables, indicating similar variation patterns regardless of the competitive level. However, significant main effects of the training phase were observed for energy (η2p = 0.315), protein (η2p = 0.225), fat (η2p = 0.317), vitamin B1 (η2p = 0.299), and vitamin B6 (η2p = 0.177), with values decreasing as phases progressed. Post hoc comparisons revealed that energy (95% CI: TP vs. ECP [40–343], TP vs. LCP [44–367]), fat (95% CI: TP vs. ECP [0.7–17.7], TP vs. LCP [1.7–16.5]), and vitamin B1 (95% CI: TP vs. ECP [0.02–0.38], TP vs. LCP [0.02–0.38]) intake were significantly higher during the TP than during the ECP and LCP. Significant main effects of competitive level were observed for energy (η2p = 0.336), protein (η2p = 0.295), fat (η2p = 0.399), vitamin B1 (η2p = 0.590), and vitamin B6 (η2p = 0.371), with higher values observed in the EG than in the NEG. Vitamin B1 intake was significantly higher in the EG than in the NEG during the TP (95% CI: 0.19–0.62), PP (95% CI: 0.05–0.34), and ECP (95% CI: 0.01–0.24). Energy and vitamin B6 intake were significantly higher in the EG than in the NEG during the TP (energy [95% CI: 121–516] and vitamin B6 [95% CI: 0.27–0.99]) and ECP (energy [95% CI: 25–312] and vitamin B6 [95% CI: 0.02–0.61]). Protein intake was significantly higher in the EG than in the NEG during the TP (95% CI: 2.3–17.4). Fat intake was significantly higher in the EG than in the NEG during the TP (95% CI: 4.8–27.1) and PP (95% CI: 0.7–15.0).

DISCUSSION

The observed relative decreases in energy intake during the ECP and LCP may be due to attempts to optimize the power-to-weight ratio. In track and field jumpers, muscle mass increases during the PP, and body weight and fat mass are adjusted during the ECP and LCP to optimize the power-to-weight ratio and enhance performance [4]. Moreover, gradual weight reduction, rather than rapid weight loss, has been reported to have a greater positive effect on jumping performance [9]. Accordingly, the relative reduction in energy intake during the ECP and LCP may reflect efforts to achieve appropriate body weight and composition in accordance with seasonal training goals. Among the energy-producing nutrients, fat intake showed a marked decrease during the ECP and LCP, indicating that adjustments in fat intake may have been implemented to achieve these goals. The relatively higher intake of energy and certain nutrients observed in the EG during the TP, PP, and ECP disappeared during the LCP. This convergence may have been associated with a reduction in appetite caused by the summer heat [10]. Indeed, regional meteorological data for the study period showed that the maximum Wet Bulb Globe Temperature during the LCP reached a level categorized as “danger” for physical activity (Supplemental Table S1). Beyond this environmental stress, the accumulation of fatigue throughout the competitive season should be considered a critical factor. These variables were not directly evaluated in this study; however, factors such as seasonal fatigue and summer heat may have contributed to the difficulty for athletes to maintain adequate food intake regardless of their competition level.

However, prolonged reduction in energy intake for weight loss may increase the risk of low energy availability and associated injuries and health issues [11,12]. Minimizing training time lost to injury or illness is crucial for achieving performance goals [13]. Therefore, maintaining appropriate energy intake throughout the season is essential. Maintaining appropriate energy availability during the TP and PP has been demonstrated to reduce these risks [14]. In this study, the relatively higher energy intake observed in the EG compared with the NEG during the TP may be associated with the higher competitive level in the EG.

Furthermore, our results revealed that vitamin B1 and vitamin B6 intake, which are related to energy metabolism [15], demonstrated relative variations across the training phases. This might be due to a decrease in energy intake. This suggests that even in ECP and LCP, where energy intake is reduced, it is important for jumpers to maintain a diet that does not reduce vitamin and mineral intake.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size (n = 20) and uneven distribution across jump-event types may have affected its statistical robustness. Event-specific influences were statistically adjusted by including jump-event type as an adjustment factor; however, it cannot be ruled out that the results were influenced by specific event characteristics due to this sampling imbalance. Consequently, this imbalance may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtler effects. While these findings provide valuable fundamental data, they may not be fully representative of all track and field jumping events. Therefore, caution is required when generalizing these results to the broader jumping population, as they may primarily reflect the characteristics of the over-represented events. Second, this binary classification based on a single threshold may not fully capture the continuous variability of individual performance, despite using the entry standards for the Kanto Inter-University Track and Field Championships as a clear performance indicator for collegiate athletes. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted as a comparison between competitive levels. Additionally, we used the FFQ to evaluate variations in energy and nutrient intake. The FFQ is useful for evaluating habitual dietary intake in athletes; however, it may underestimate actual energy intake [7]. In addition, the FFQ NEXT used in this study may not reflect supplementation, such as protein supplements. Therefore, it may not be ideal for evaluating absolute intake values. However, dietary intake in this study was analyzed within the same cohort based on the training phase and competitive level, focusing on phase-specific variations as relative values rather than absolute values, thus maintaining the value of this study.

In conclusion, this study investigated relative variations in energy and nutrient intake across different training phases and competitive levels among male collegiate track and field jumpers. The results revealed that energy, protein, fat, vitamin B1, and vitamin B6 intake relatively decreased during the ECP and LCP compared with the TP. Additionally, the EG showed relatively higher intake of these nutrients than the NEG, with the EG demonstrating significantly higher intake during the TP. However, these differences disappeared during the LCP. Our findings underscore the practical necessity of monitoring energy and nutrient intake throughout the competitive season. Such regular assessment is essential for optimizing athletic performance and proactively managing injury and illness risks. These findings reflect relative phase-specific variations rather than precise absolute intake levels due to the nature of the assessment method; however, they provide practically relevant information regarding seasonal nutritional trends for track and field jumpers.
