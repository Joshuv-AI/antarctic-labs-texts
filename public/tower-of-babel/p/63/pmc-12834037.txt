Retention of lysosomal acid sphingomyelinase protects from Niemann-Pick Disease
Authors: Cameron A Beard, Kyra N Hermanson, Justin M Snider, Aki Hara, Brandon K Dahl, Janet J Allopenna, Marilyn T Marron, Benjamin Newcomb, Benjamin E Low, Michael V Wiles, Russell W Jenkins, Lina M Obeid, Yusuf A Hannun, Ashley J Snider
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNiemann-Pick Disease (NPD) types A and B are lysosomal storage disorders resulting from dysfunction or loss of acid sphingomyelinase (aSMase), which hydrolyzes sphingomyelin (SM) to ceramide and phosphocholine. Patients with NPD-A develop severe neurologic and visceral pathology and rarely live beyond the age of 3 years, while patients with NPD-B typically live to adolescence/early adulthood without neurologic involvement. There are currently no therapies for NPD-A. SMPD1, the gene that encodes aSMase, gives rise to two distinct enzymes - lysosomal sphingomyelinase (L-SMase) and secretory sphingomyelinase (S-SMase), with the latter being associated with inflammation and chemokine amplification. This study sought to define the role of secretory-deficient aSMase variants in NPD. Human NPD fibroblasts transfected with wildtype or two aSMase variants demonstrated that serine residues at either position 507 or 508 in human aSMase retained L-SMase yet lacked S-SMase activity, basally and in response to inflammatory stimuli. We next generated a novel mouse model harboring the S505A variant (aSMaseS505A), corresponding to S507A in humans, in the endogenous Smpd1 gene. Mice expressing the S505A variant of aSMase retained L-SMase activity and tissue SM levels in the brain but lacked S-SMase activity in serum. ASMaseS505A mice were protected from NPD physiology and pathophysiology including accumulation of foam cells in the spleen, liver and cerebellum, as well as loss of Purkinje cells. Moreover, aSMase−/− mice demonstrated significantly decreased locomotor control and this was prevented in aSMaseS505A mice. Together, these studies suggest a retention of L-SMase may serve to pave the way for ERT in NPD.Keywords: Nieman-pick disease, Acid sphingomyelinase, Lysosomal storage disorder, Ceramide, Sphingomyelin

Introduction

Acid sphingomyelinase (aSMase) catalyzes the hydrolysis of sphingomyelin (SM) to ceramide and phosphocholine. Deficiency of aSMase results in Niemann-Pick Disease (NPD) types A and B (Schuchman, 2007), lysosomal storage disorders characterized by accumulation of SM within the endo-lysosomal compartment. NPD-A patients exhibit splenomegaly and hepatomegaly within the first six months of life. This rapidly progressing form of NPD results in severe neurologic and visceral pathology leading to death typically before the age of three (McGovern et al., 2006; Graber et al., 1994). Similar to NPD-A, NPD-B patients typically present with splenomegaly and hepatomegaly, as well as respiratory complications. However, NPD-B patients generally live into adolescence or early adulthood and commonly do not manifest neurological symptoms (Graber et al., 1994; McGovern et al., 2008). NPD patients lack both lysosomal (L-SMase) and secretory (S-SMase) (Schissel et al., 1998; Schissel et al., 1996), as do aSMase knockout (aSMase−/− ) mice which have been used as a model for NPD. Homozygous aSMase−/− mice develop progressive lipid storage disease in the lungs, liver, spleen and brain. Loss of aSMase activity also leads to loss of Purkinje cells in the cerebellum and altered locomotor function (Horinouchi et al., 1995; Macauley et al., 2008).

There is evidence showing that restoration of aSMase activity may protect against symptoms of NPD (Garnacho et al., 2017; Passini et al., 2005). Restoration of aSMase activity in mice with either fusion or recombinant proteins partially protected from neurologic deterioration but did not completely blunt accumulation of SM in tissues (Marathe et al., 2000). Generation of transgenic mice to restore aSMase activity (Jones et al., 2008) or delivery of recombinant aSMase via nanocarriers (Garnacho et al., 2017), infusion (Miranda et al., 2000a), or adeno-associated virus (AAV) (Bu et al., 2012; Samaranch et al., 2019; Passini et al., 2007; Dodge et al., 2005) have demonstrated partial recovery from specific aspects of NPD; however, none have completely restored aSMase activity or alleviated all symptoms of NPD. Moreover, adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivery of recombinant aSMase in non-human primates resulted in significant neural inflammation and expression of the chemokine CCL5 (Salegio et al., 2012). Until recently, therapeutic options have demonstrated limited success. Olipudase alfa (Xenpozyme™), a recent FDA-approved aSMase enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), has shown reduction in SM accumulation, liver and spleen volume and improved lung function in adult and pediatric patients with non-CNS manifestations of NPD. However, there are currently no therapies for NPD-A.

ASMase activity, specifically S-SMase has been shown to increase in mouse models of inflammation (Wong et al., 2000), as well as in human diseases of chronic inflammation (Doehner et al., 2007; Hanaoka et al., 2018; Jenkins et al., 2013; Kiprianos et al., 2012; Rodriguez-Sureda et al., 2016; Claus et al., 2005). Thus, some of the key deleterious effects of ASM replacement therapies may be due to the pro-inflammatory action of S-SMase. This association of S-SMase activity with inflammation generated interest in expression of mutant forms of aSMase that retain L-SMase activity but lack S-SMase activity. Marathe et al. demonstrated that expression of a SMase-Lamp1 fusion protein protected from neurologic disease in aSMase−/− mice by restoring L-SMase activity (Marathe et al., 2000). Jenkins et al. demonstrated that a serine to alanine mutation at position 508 resulted in retention of L-SMase activity but loss of S-SMase activity upon stimulation with proinflammatory cytokines (Jenkins et al., 2010). In addition, S-SMase activity was both necessary and sufficient for the induction of CCL5 (Jenkins et al., 2011), the latter being a key manifestation of the untoward effects of AAV-mediated restoration of aSMase. Therefore, we set out to generate a novel genetically engineered mouse model (GEMM) containing a secretory-deficient aSMase to examine NPD-associated pathology and alteration in locomotor function. Using a secretory-deficient mouse model, we demonstrate that loss of S-SMase but not L-SMase activity results in protection from NPD, suggesting that secretory-deficient aSMase may serve as a viable therapeutic for NPD-A.

Methods and materials

Cell culture and generation of stable transfectants

Niemann-Pick type A dermal fibroblasts (NPD-1, GM13205) were obtained from the Coriell Cell Repository. Second-generation self-inactivating lentivirus expressing wild-type (WT) human aSMase, S507A, S508A and S507/S508A mutant aSMase, or lacZ (control) were produced using triple plasmid co-transfection of 293FT cells. 293FT cells were transfected using 1 μg of pCMV-dR8.2 dvpr, 1 μg of pCMV-VSV-G, and 1 μg of the transfer plasmid and Lipofectamine 2000 in a 3:1 ratio. Medium was replaced after 16 h transfection with complete medium (DMEM, high glucose, GlutaMAX™ supplement, with pyruvate, supplemented with 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum) and conditioned medium was collected at 48 h. Viral supernatants were cleared of cells by filtering through a 0.45 μm filter, TU was quantified with Lenti Rapid Titer sticks (Origene EB200001), and virus was applied to GM13205 cells at an MOI of 3. Cells were maintained in DMEM+10 % FBS + 15 μg/mL Blasticidin (Invitrogen Cat# A1113903), under standard cell culture conditions (5 % CO2 37 ◦C).

Generation of aSMaseS505A mice

To generate the aSMaseS505A mice (C57BL/6 J-Smpd1em2Mvw/MvwJ), C57BL/6 J (JAX stock#000664) zygotes were microinjected with ~2–5 picoliters of a mixture as previously described (Low et al., 2016). The microinjection mixture contained: Cas9 mRNA (30 ng/μl), two single-guide RNA’s (sgRNA#1603, sgRNA#1604; each 15 ng/μL) and a 200 nt oligonucleotide donor (Integrated DNA Technologies, oligo#1549, 1 ng/μl). Both sgRNAs were designed to target the codon corresponding to Ser505 of the mouse Smpd1 gene (NCBI 20597), which corresponds to the Ser507 in humans. To reduce off-target potential, sgRNA#1603: 5’-GGACCACATGAGAGCTTC, and sgRNA#1604: 5′-GGACCACATGAGAGCTTCC, were both designed as truncated guides (Fu et al., 2014). The 200 nt oligonucleotide donor (oligo#1549: 5′-ACAGAAAGGGATGGAAGCCAAAGCAGG(21)TCCCTTCCTTACCACTCAACCCTCCCTTCTCGCAGGCTACCGAGTTTACCAAATAGATGGAAACTACCCCGGAGCCTCTCATGTGGTCCTGGACCACGAGACCTACATCTTGAATCTGACCCAGGCCAACGCAGCAGGAGGCA-CACCAAGCTGGAAGCGCCTCTACAGGGCTC), was designed to generate a serine to alanine mutation via homology-directed repair. Putative founders were backcrossed to wild-type C57BL/6 J mice, and the desired allele was confirmed in the heterozygous (aSMaseS505A) offspring. Wildtype (aSMase+/+) and knockout (aSMase−/− ) genotypes were confirmed using routine PCR and gel electrophoresis, and aSMaseS505A genotypes were confirmed via genetic sequencing. Male and female mice were used for experiments. All animal experiments were approved by the University of Arizona Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and followed US National Institutes of Health “Using Animals in Intramural Research” and American Veterinary Medical Association guidelines.

SMase activity assays

The assays for L-SMase and S-SMase activities were adapted from Jenkins et al. (Jenkins et al., 2010) with SM (18:1/6:0) substituted for radioactive substrate and detection accomplished via LC/MS/MS. NPD fibroblasts expressing WT human aSMase, S507A, S508A and S507/S508A mutant aSMase, or lacZ (control) were serum starved in Opti-MEM (Lifetechnologies Cat# 31985062) for 4 h prior to stimulation with 20 ng/mL TNF-α (R&D Systems Cat# 210-TA-100) for 24 h. Media was snap frozen for S-SMase activity (see below) and cell pellets were snap frozen in L-SMase activity buffer containing 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 0.0004 % Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, and protease inhibitors (ThermoFisher Cat#78429). Lysates were then sonicated briefly, and cellular debris and unbroken cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 ×g for 5 min at 4 ◦C. Similarly, brain tissues were homogenized with glass beads using MP Biomedicals FastPrep-24 in L-SMase activity buffer and stored at − 20 ◦C. 40–50 μg of protein lysate (determined by BCA assay) was brought to 100 Ll total volume in water and added to 100 Ll reaction buffer containing 50 μL C6 SM (substrate) and 10 Ll C6 (ceramide internal standard) presented in micelles (produced through sonication) containing 0.2 % Triton X-100 in sodium acetate buffer (250 mm, pH 5.0) and 1.0 mm EDTA. For both S-SMase and L-SMase assays the reactions were conducted for 4 h at 37 ◦C. S-SMase activity was performed with the indicated volume of cell culture media or serum from mice in a final volume of 100 μL and assayed with a similar reaction buffer, with the addition of 0.1 mm ZnCl2 in place of 1.0 mm EDTA. Additional samples were tested independently and SMase activity remained constant through at least 2 freeze-thaw cycles. Mass transitions for the substrate [SM (18:1/6:0)], the product [ceramide (18:1/4:0)], and internal standard [ceramide (18:1/6:0)] were optimized as follows; Substrate-563.4 m/z_184.1 m/z_22Ce, 398.4 m/z_264.3 m/z_19Ce, 370.4 m/z_264.3 m/z_18Ce, respectively. Additional LC/MS/MS parameters including source conditions and chromatography were adapted from Bielawski et al. (Bielawski et al., 2009) analysis for common sphingolipids.

Mass spectrometry for quantitation of sphingolipids

Sphingolipid analyses were conducted as done previously (Choi et al., 2018). Briefly, tissues were homogenized in 2 mL extraction solvent (2:3, 70 % isopropanol/ethyl acetate) with glass beads using MP Biomedicals FastPrep-24. Samples and internal standard were resuspended in mobile phase B (1 μM ammonium formate, 2 % formic acid in MEOH) after being dried under nitrogen. Analyses were conducted by the University of Arizona Cancer Center Analytical Chemistry Shared Resource Core on Agilent 1290 uPLC tandem Thermo Vantage Triple Quadrupole. Data were normalized to volume for serum and protein for tissues. Data were normalized to volume for serum and total lipid phosphate (Pi) for tissues and data are represented as pmol/nmol Pi (Bligh and Dyer, 1959; Van Veldhoven and Bell, 1988).

Histology

Mice were anesthetized under isoflurane anesthesia and perfused with 10 % formalin (Masterflex Pump Cole-Parmer® Instruments, Vernon Hills, IL, USA). Brain, liver, spleen, and lung tissues were harvested, kept in 10 % formalin overnight, then transferred into 70 % ETOH. Samples were processed, embedded, sectioned and stained for H&E by the University of Arizona Cancer Center (UACC) Tissue Acquisition & Cellular/Molecular Analysis Shared Resource (TACMASR). Images were captured on EVOS M500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Grip strength testing

Grip strength was measured using a Grip Strength Meter (Columbus Instruments, Columbus, Ohio, USA). Fore paw and all paw grip strength were measured using a metal grid attached to the meter. Mice were allowed to grip the grid and then consistently and gently pulled until they released. Five recordings were averaged and grip strength recorded in grams force.

Rotarod testing

Rotarod Rotamex 5 (Columbus Instruments, Columbus, Ohio, USA) was used to assess locomotor coordination. Mice were individually placed in one of four lanes on the instrument and measurements collected simultaneously. Rotation speed began at 2 RPM and increased 0.5 RPM every 5 s. Run time and maximum speed were recorded for three separate tests and averaged.

Statistical analysis

All grouped data are presented as the mean ± SD. Numbers of animals and experimental replicates are indicated in the figure legends. Statistical differences between groups were evaluated via one-or two-way ANOVA (Tukey’s multiple comparisons). Normally distributed data were analyzed using a one-way or two-way ANOVA with a Tukey posttest to correct for multiple comparisons, otherwise Kruskal-Wallis tests and Dunn’s posttests were performed. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.5.0 software. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Serine to alanine mutations in aSMase exhibit L-SMase but not S-SMase activity in NPD fibroblasts

Proinflammatory cytokines have been shown to promote S-SMase activity in MCF7 cells (Jenkins et al., 2010), and in turn, S-SMase was shown to induce the production and secretion of CCL5 (Jenkins et al., 2011). Our previous work investigated the role of S-SMase using the S508A mutation in cells; however, the human aSMase protein has vicinal serine residues at the 507 and 508 positions. Therefore, in order to begin addressing the potential use of a secretory-deficient form of aSMase as a potential ERT, NPD-A fibroblasts were stably transfected with WT, S507A, S508A or the combination S507A/S508A aSMase and stimulated with either vehicle or 20 ng/mL TNFα for 24 h. Cells expressing WT aSMase exhibited a significant increase in L-SMase activity, in both vehicle- and TNFα-treated cells (Fig. 1A). Expression of S507A resulted in a ~ 2.7-fold and ~ 5.9-fold increase in L-SMase activity basally and upon TNFα stimulation, respectively, as compared to empty vector (EV). Expression of S508A resulted in a ~ 20.2-fold and ~ 44.5-fold increase L-SMase activity basally and upon TNFα stimulation, respectively, as compared to EV. The combination of S507A/S508A resulted in ~8.6-fold and ~ 22.8-fold increase L-SMase activity basally and upon TNF stimulation, respectively, as compared to EV (Fig. 1B. As demonstrated previously (Jenkins et al., 2010), S-SMase activity was significantly increased in cells expressing WT aSMase following TNFα treatment (Fig. 1C). However, cells expressing S507A, S508A, or the S507A/S508A did not exhibit increased S-SMase activity basally (over the EV expressing cells) or in response to TNFα. These data demonstrate that mutation of the serine residues at either the S507 or S508 positions in human aSMase retain L-SMase activity and ablate S-SMase activity, basally and following pro-inflammatory stimuli.

S505A mutation in aSMase results in loss of S-SMase activity in vivo

In order to investigate the potential for a secretory-deficient aSMase as ERT for NPD, in collaboration with The Jackson Laboratory, CRISPR-Cas9 was used to generate a novel mouse model containing a S505A mutation, corresponding to S507 in human aSMase, in the endogenous mouse Smpd1 gene (Fig. S1). We elected to generate the mouse S505A mutant, as the human S507A mutation resulted in loss of S-SMase but retention of residual L-SMase activity (Fig. 1B).

To establish the effects of the S505A mutation on SMase activity and sphingolipid levels, serum and cerebellar samples were collected from aSMase+/+, aSMase−/−, and aSMaseS505A mice at 4 months of age. Serum S-SMase activity was significantly decreased in both aSMase−/− and aSMaseS505A mice compared to aSMase+/+ mice (~62 % and ~ 48 % respectively, Fig. 2A). In addition, serum SM levels were analyzed using LC-MS/MS (Fig. 2B), as well as several species of SM. Loss of aSMase, but not the S505A mutation, resulted in significantly increased total SM, long-chain SMs (C14-C18; Fig. 2C-E), and select very long-chain SMs (C20, C24:1; Fig. 2G-H) in serum. Several species of SM were not altered among genotypes (C20, C22, and C26; data not shown). Together, these data demonstrate that the S505A mutation in Smpd1 resulted in a loss of S-SMase activity with minimal alterations in circulating SM levels.

L-SMase activity is retained in cerebellar tissues of mice with S505A mutation in aSMase

Lack of aSMase activity results in accumulation of SM in numerous tissues, especially the brain (Otterbach and Stoffel, 1995). Therefore, the effects of aSMaseS505A on L-SMase activity and sphingolipid levels were assessed. Mice expressing the S505A variant or deficient in aSMase exhibited significantly reduced L-SMase activity as compared to aSMase+/+ mice (~5-fold, Fig. 3A). However, aSMaseS505A mice, which lacked S-SMase activity (Fig. 2A), exhibited significantly more L-SMase activity than aSMase−/− mice (1.18 ± 0.36 vs 0.017 ± 0.04, corresponding to 11.6 % and 0.17 %, respectively). Mice deficient in aSMase, but not aSMaseS505A mice, exhibited significant increases in total cerebellar SM (Fig. 3B), as well as several species of SM. Specifically long-chain SMs (C14, C16, and C18 species; Fig. 3C-F) as well as very long-chain SMs (C20, C22, and C24 species, Fig. 3G-N) were increased in brain tissues from aSMase−/− mice but not aSMaseS505A mice. Additionally, aSMase, but not aSMaseS505A mice, exhibited increases in C18:1, and C24:1 SM (Fig. 3 E and K) though these changes were not significant. These data suggest that, similar to circulation, aSMase regulates most SM species in cerebellar tissues. Importantly, these data demonstrate that mice expressing the S505A mutation in aSMase retain sufficient L-SMase activity in the brain to maintain SM levels similar to aSMase+/+ mice.

S505A mutation in aSMase does not result in NPD pathology in lung, liver, spleen, or cerebellar tissues

ASMase deficiency results in a progressive lysosomal storage disorder causing accumulation of lipids in several visceral tissues. In the brain lipid accumulation results in ataxia, cerebellar disfunction and loss of the Purkinje cell layer beginning at 6–8 weeks of age (Horinouchi et al., 1995; Macauley et al., 2008). Therefore, to determine the effects of the S505A mutation on overt NPD pathology over time, spleen, liver, lung and brain tissues were collected from aSMase+/+, aSMase−/−, and aSMaseS505A mice at 2, 4 and 6 months of age representing early-, mid- and late-stage disease. Examination of the spleen, liver, and lungs at 4 months of age demonstrated normal histology in both the aSMase+/+ and aSMaseS505A mice (Fig. 4, left and right panels). ASMase−/− mice exhibited significant accumulation of foam cells (lipid-filled macrophages, indicated by arrows) in spleen, liver, and lung tissues (Fig. 4, center panels). Mice deficient in aSMase exhibited an increase in the foam cells (Fig. 5, center panels, red arrows) and loss of the Purkinje cell layer at 4 months of age (Fig. 5, center panels). However, mice with either wild-type aSMase or the S505A mutation demonstrated no loss of Purkinje cells (Fig. 5, top and bottom panels, black arrows). Examination of liver, lung tissues, and cerebellar tissues revealed overt pathology in aSMase−/− mice, but not aSMase+/+ or aSMaseS505A mice at 2 and 6 months of age (Fig. S2 and S3). These data suggest that mice harboring the S505A mutation are protected from pathology associated with loss of aSMase activity.

Mice harboring S505A mutation retain locomotor coordination

Locomotor decline has previously been documented in aSMase−/− mice (Miranda et al., 2000a). Locomotor coordination could be altered by either decreased muscle strength and function or loss of balance. Therefore, to evaluate the effects of the S505A mutation on coordination, mice were assessed at 4 months of age. Muscular strength, which has not previously been assessed in these mice, was measured using a grip-strength meter. Forepaw grip strength was significantly decreased in aSMase−/− mice as compared to both aSMase+/+ and aSMaseS505A mice (Table S1). All paw grip strength was also decreased in aSMase−/− mice as compared to aSMase+/+ mice. Locomotor coordination was measured using a Rotarod Max for total run time and maximum tolerated speed. Loss of aSMase, but not the S505 mutation, significantly decreased both total run time (Fig. 6A) and maximum speed (Fig. 6B). Coordination was also significantly decreased in aSMase−/−, but not aSMaseS505A mice at 2- and 6-months of age (Table S2). These data demonstrate preservation of modest L-SMase activity is adequate to prevent neurodegeneration and motor impairments.

Discussion

Loss of aSMase activity results in NPD-A and NPD-B, and reconstitution of aSMase activity has demonstrated improvement in visceral accumulation of SM but has limited efficacy in CNS tissues. In addition, in studies using non-human primates AAV administration of aSMase resulted in neural inflammation, especially the production of CCL5, a chemokine that has been shown to be a effector of S-SMase (Salegio et al., 2012). Therefore, this study set out to determine if deficiency in S-SMase but not L-SMase activity would prevent NPD-like symptoms. NPD fibroblasts transfected with either WT or S507A mutant aSMase exhibited L-SMase activity; however, only NPD fibroblasts expressing WT aSMase exhibited increased S-SMase activity basally and in response to TNFα. These data demonstrated that the S507A mutation prevents basal and TNFα-stimulated S-SMase activity. Mice harboring the S505A mutation in aSMase were also deficient in S-SMase activity demonstrated by decreased SMase activity in serum. Conversely, aSMaseS505A mice retained sufficient L-SMase activity in cerebellar tissues to maintain SM levels similar to aSMase+/+ mice. This retention of L-SMase activity prevented both visceral and CNS lipid accumulation. Moreover, aSMaseS505A mice exhibited significantly improved locomotor control in comparison to aSMase−/− mice. Together these data suggest that the S505A mutation in aSMase restores sufficient L-SMase activity to abrograte symptoms associated with NPD.

S-SMase activity has been implicated in several inflammatory diseases and to be activated by proinflammatory cytokines. In mouse models using LPS or cytokine injections, S-SMase activity has been shown to increase in serum (Wong et al., 2000; Claus et al., 2005; Lightle et al., 2003). Similarly, LPS injections in healthy volunteers significantly increased serum ceramides, and this was attributed to activities of both serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT) and S-SMase activities (Lightle et al., 2003). Patients with sepsis have also been shown to have significantly increased S-SMase activity, demonstrating a role for S-SMase under pathophysiologic conditions of severe inflammation. Additional studies have demonstrated increased S-SMase activity in chronically ill patients with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (Jenkins et al., 2013), heart failure (Doehner et al., 2007), rheumatoid arthritis (Hanaoka et al., 2018), and vasculitis (Kiprianos et al., 2012). In cell culture, S-SMase, but not L-SMase activity, was significantly increased in human vascular endothelial cells (HUVECs) treated with IL1β, IFNγ, or IFNβ, but not IL4 (Marathe et al., 1998). Similarly, S-SMase activity was significantly increased in MCF7 cells transfected with WT aSMase and stimulated with either TNFα or IL1β (Jenkins et al., 2010). In addition, expression of the S508A mutant aSMase in MCF7 cells resulted in retention of L-SMase activity but loss of S-SMase activity upon stimulation with proinflammatory cytokines (Jenkins et al., 2011). This increase in S-SMase was also required for activation of downstream inflammatory responses. Here we demonstrate that the expression of the S507A mutant aSMase in NPD fibroblasts prevented S-SMase activity upon treatment with TNFα. Together these studies demonstrate that S-SMase activity is regulated by, and/or plays a role in, inflammation. Moreover, the S507A mutation prevents S-SMase activity while retaining residual L-SMase activity and may have potential as an ERT in NPD.

There are discrepancies in the literature as to the absolute amount of residual aSMase activity that delineates between NPD-A and NPD-B; however, residual L-SMase activity specifically in CNS tissues has been shown to protect from neurologic disease. Expression of a transgenic aSMase-Lamp1 fusion protein in aSMase−/− mice, prevented NPD pathology (Marathe et al., 2000). These mice lacked S-SMase activity yet retained ~20 % of L-SMase activity, while the S505A mutant mice exhibited ~12 % retention of L-SMase activity as compared to aSMase+/+ mice. This residual L-SMase activity prevented the accumulation of SM in CNS and visceral tissues, further suggesting that minimal L-SMase activity is required to maintain physiologic SM levels in these tissues.

ASMase−/− mice lack both L-SMase and S-SMase activity and exhibit progressive accumulation of SM in visceral organs and the brain (Horinouchi et al., 1995). SM accumulation in liver, spleen, and lungs from patients with NPD-A and NPD-B results in hepatosplenomegaly, as well as reduced liver and lung function (Horinouchi et al., 1995; Schuchman and Desnick, 2017). Accumulation of large lipid-laden macrophages, namely foam cells, in visceral organs is also associated with NPD patients and in aSMase−/− mice (Schuchman and Desnick, 2017). Though these foam cells produced less superoxide (Dhami et al., 2001), they have been associated with a pro-inflammatory environment in the lung (Ikegami et al., 2003; Brinkman et al., 2005), and fibrosis in the liver (Moles et al., 2012; Thurberg et al., 2012). Foam cells associated with NPD have also been well documented in visceral tissue in aSMase−/− mice (Horinouchi et al., 1995; Otterbach and Stoffel, 1995). In this study foam cells were present in liver, brain, and lungs as early as two-months of age in aSMase−/− mice and increased progressively with age. However, foam cells were absent in these tissues, up to six months of age in both aSMase+/+ and aSMaseS505A mice, suggesting that retention of residual L-SMase in the S505A mutant protects from NPD-associated pathology in visceral organs. Future studies will be geared toward expression of the S505A mutant aSMase in visceral and CNS tissues to determine if this expression can prevent and/or rescue from the NPD phenotype in aSMase−/− mice.

NPD-A patients are typically diagnosed early in life due to hepatosplenomegaly (McGovern et al., 2006), and display progressive motor deterioration due to CNS involvement. The aSMase−/− mouse model has been used to demonstrate increased SM in the brain (Ledesma et al., 2011; Scandroglio et al., 2008). Additional gangliosides, GM2 and GM3, were also elevated in the brains of aged aSMase−/− mice (Scandroglio et al., 2008). In the brain these lipid accumulations lead to progressive decline in locomotor function, primarily due to the loss of Purkinje cells (Macauley et al., 2008; Sarna et al., 2001). Residual L-SMase, via expression of the LAMP-1/aSMase fusion protein, was also able to prevent accumulation of SM in the brain, as well as the associated neurologic phenotype (Marathe et al., 2000). The S505A mutant mice also retained ~12 % L-SMase activity, which was sufficient to prevent accumulation of SM in the brain, retain locomotor function, and prevent loss of Purkinje cells. Together these studies further demonstrate the ability of L-SMase activity to prevent the neurologic phenotype and pathology associated with NPD-A.

Based on the necessity for only residual aSMase activity to prevent NPD-B, studies have focused on the potential for recombinant proteins, AAV expression vectors and nanocarrier delivery systems for their potential as ERT in NPD patients. ICAM-1/aSMase nanocarriers decreased SM accumulation and foam cell infiltration in lungs of treated aSMase−/− mice (Garnacho et al., 2017). Infusion with recombinant human aSMase has been shown to reduce accumulation of SM and prevent/reverse pathology in visceral organs in aSMase−/− mice; however, this did not correct neurologic deficits or prolong lifespan (Miranda et al., 2000b). Several AAVs have been utilized to increase aSMase activity in aSMase-deficient mouse models. Delivery of human aSMase via AAV under a liver specific promotor decreased SM accumulation and pathology in the liver, spleen and kidneys (Barbon et al., 2005). This restoration of aSMase activity in visceral organs also decreased inflammation in the lungs. Studies examining AAV delivery of aSMase to the cortical, subcortical and cerebellar regions in aSMase−/− mouse brains have also demonstrated success in increasing lifespan in aSMase−/− mice (Bu et al., 2012). Additional studies in aSMase−/− mice showed that lifespan was most significantly extended when treated with combined systemic and CNS delivery of AAV-aSMase, compared to either approach alone (Passini et al., 2007). Though our study did not directly examine lifespan, our data in aSMaseS505A mice demonstrate an absence of NPD at 6 months of age, while the aSMase−/− mice have significant lipid accumulation in CNS and visceral tissues as well as significantly diminished locomotor capability. Additional studies using AAV have demonstrated that specific AAV serotypes led to improved locomotor function in aSMase−/− mice (Dodge et al., 2005). Recent studies administering aSMase to the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using the AAV9 serotype in aSMase−/− mice improved locomotor control and NPD pathology (Samaranch et al., 2019). This study also examined AAV9-aSMase delivery in non-human primates. In contrast to studies using AAV2-aSMase, delivery of AAV9-aSMase to the CSF resulted in no overt toxicity or inflammation at one or three months. These studies and others led to the successful development of, and recent FDA approval for, Olipudase alfa (Xenpozyme™) as an ERT for patients with non-CNS manifestations of NPD. Though there are still no approved ERTs for the NPD-A patients, our studies suggest that expression of a secretory-deficient mutant aSMase has the potential to reverse or prevent disease.

Reversing or preventing the CNS manifestations of NPD has also been attempted in rodent models using direct infusion and AAV vectors. Repeated intracerebroventricular (ICV) delivery of recombinant human aSMase was able to decrease the accumulation of SM in CNS tissues and partially restore locomotor function in aSMase−/− mice (Dodge et al., 2009). AAV delivery of aSMase via direct injection in the CNS has also been shown to decrease accumulation of SM, prevent loss of Purkinje cells and locomotor decline (Passini et al., 2005; Bu et al., 2012; Samaranch et al., 2019; Dodge et al., 2005). However, direct infusion of AAV-human aSMase into non-human primates resulted in significant neural inflammation and specific upregulation of CCL5 (Salegio et al., 2012). This may be due in part to interactions between the AAV vector and the host immune system. Our future studies plan to determine if the use of the secretory-deficient mutant form of aSMase presents a novel approach for potential recombinant studies geared at restoring L-SMase activity and potentially avoiding the amplified inflammatory response attributed to S-SMase activity.

In conclusion, this study further demonstrates that the S507A mutation in human aSMase prevents secretion of aSMase, and specifically increased S-SMase activity associated with inflammation. Mice harboring the S505A mutation in aSMase lack S-SMase activity and retain L-SMase activity. This residual L-SMase activity is sufficient to prevent accumulation of foam cells in visceral tissues and accumulation of SM in the brain. The S505A mutation also prevents loss of Purkinje cells and locomotor decline associated with loss of aSMase activity. These studies highlight the need for alternative strategies for ERT treatment for NPD-A that factor in delivery to the CNS and prevention of inflammation.
