Consultative General Internal Medicine for Complex Care: The Model, Outcomes, and Lessons Learned
Authors: Aditya Kumar Ghosh, Joshua D Overgaard, Ibrahim Abubeker, Elizabeth M Rice, Amy M Larson, Erin M Pagel, Ryan T Hurt, Elizabeth A Gilman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the landscape of modern medicine evolves, new health care delivery models are needed to meet the growing complexity of medical care. Primary care clinicians are increasingly managing patients with multiple chronic conditions, a challenge that will intensify as the population ages. Complex medical care involves multiple factors that contribute to difficulties in providing optimal quality of care for patients facing these types of medical problems. To address this need, models of consultative general internal medicine have emerged at various tertiary care institutions across the United States to synthesize prior evaluations, recommend appropriate testing and specialty consultations, and integrate findings into a cohesive care plan for implementation by patients’ local care teams. Variability in expertise, patient volume, and structure exists across institutions, but the expansion of multidisciplinary care capabilities during the COVID-19 pandemic has created new opportunities for destination-type practices. This narrative review explores how consultative medicine will be crucial to providing integrative, patient-centric care by enhancing diagnostic accuracy, coordinating multidisciplinary input, and communicating actionable care plans to patients.Keywords: Consultative Medicine, complex medical care, General Internal Medicine, Primary Care, High Quality Care

Introduction

Medicine’s complexity grows with each generation of scientific discovery and diagnostic expansion. 1 William J Mayo recognized this in his 1910 Rush Medical College address: “The best interest of the patient is the only interest to be considered, and in order that the sick may have the benefit of advancing knowledge, union of forces is necessary.” 2 He foresaw the challenges of modern medicine, noting that no single practitioner can master all medical knowledge and evidence. Thus, collaborative and coordinated patient care is needed. This has guided the Mayo Clinic Model of Care, a collaborative multispecialty framework built upon the value: “the needs of the patient come first.” 3 However, when multiple specialized physicians share a patient, complexity introduces new failure points. Gaps can appear in maintaining a broad differential diagnosis, synthesizing large volumes of prior data, coordinating evaluations across specialties, and managing differing expert opinions. Traditionally, primary care alone has been relied upon to coordinate the workup and recommendations from multiple teams.

Definitions of primary care vary: the American Academy of Family Physicians emphasizes first-contact and comprehensive care for patients with undifferentiated symptoms, and the National Institutes of Health defines it more broadly as integrated, accessible care that addresses most health needs and fosters sustained patient partnerships. 4,5 Although still relevant, these roles are rapidly evolving in the postpandemic era, with consolidation of practices and expanded use of virtual care. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated this shift, leaving primary care teams to manage with increasingly complex patients, often with limited specialty access, greater reliance on advanced practice providers, and growing adoption of telehealth. These challenges are further exacerbated in underserved areas.

Physician shortages are projected to exceed 80,000 in the United States by 2036 with over 30,000 in primary care. 6 Demand is rising, and specialty access is more limited. This means primary care clinicians face broader responsibilities but still lack the resources to manage complex, undifferentiated conditions. The result is diagnostic delays, complications, and higher health care costs. 7

Consultative medicine is emerging to address health care gaps for patients with complex and undiagnosed conditions. A recent review says consultative medicine is a modern diagnostic specialty rooted in generalism. It combines Oslerian focus on careful observation with multidisciplinary collaboration, as outlined in the Mayo Model of Care. 8 These ideas match the advances in diagnostic capabilities that bring individualized medicine to the bedside (for example, advanced imaging and DNA-based tests). Patients who benefit have multisystem symptoms, atypical presentations, medically unexplained complaints, and long diagnostic journeys.

Although consultative medicine is gaining recognition at several tertiary referral centers, its practice model is still little described in literature. There is limited information on how these programs are organized, coordination between specialties, or adaptation to changes in digital medicine, artificial intelligence, and new diagnostics such as multicancer detection (MCD) tests. In this narrative review, the authors examine the Mayo Clinic model of consultative general internal medicine. The authors look at its structure, novel services, and outcome data. This narrative review aims to develop a framework for integrating this approach into modern health care delivery.

What Is Complex Care?

The practice of managing complex medical conditions has received increased attention recently, as patients find it difficult to have their conditions appropriately managed by their local primary care physicians. 8 Issues that patients with complex medical conditions face include long wait times from initial request to seeing their physicians, short visit times leading to inadequate time for the clinicians to get effective understandings of their patients’ problems, and overall difficulty with generating effective dialogues with their doctors about the nature and complexity of their presentations. By understanding this patient population and what comprises complex care, steps can be taken to ensure that new models of medical care are developed to effectively address their needs. Complex medical cases can present in many ways, each creating challenges for effective, comprehensive care. Presentations may include rare diseases, atypical forms of well-known conditions, new or unexpected conditions in otherwise stable patients, or multisystem problems requiring multispecialty care from a generalist. 8 Some patients with complex cases wait years for an accurate diagnosis. 9 Others have “medically unexplained” symptoms, with no clear diagnosis. These patients are often misdiagnosed and untreated. 10,11 Complex care models must consider all of these patients.

Providing specific diagnoses with a detailed care plan is crucial. Diagnostic uncertainty has been linked to delayed and suboptimal care. 12 Additionally, errors in diagnosis can cause substantial patient harm. The National Academy of Science reports diagnostic errors result in about 10% of patient deaths and account for 6% to 17% of adverse hospital events. 13 These issues affect patients’ ability to manage their conditions, as they are directed toward ineffective treatments and medications. The potential for uncertainty and errors in complex cases highlights the need for specialized internists who can handle such challenges efficiently and effectively.

Consultative General Internal Medicine: A Model for Managing Complex Cases

Consultative medicine leverages detailed history-taking, thorough and prompt diagnostic evaluation, and a concise summary of medical conditions relayed to the primary care team. These practices operate outside patients’ primary care teams, providing expertise to synthesize care for complex patients. Physicians in consultative medicine are internal medicine specialists who join to apply their knowledge and utilize tertiary referral center resources to manage complex conditions. At Mayo Clinic, there are no specific additional training or certification requirements to join; the program recruits board-certified internal medicine physicians with an interest and aptitude for complex or undiagnosed cases. These workups account for patients’ prior extensive evaluations and medical complexity. 8

A key benefit of consultative medicine models is its capacity to address complex cases and optimize management plans for patients to share with their primary care teams. A retrospective study at an academic medical center assessed initial and final diagnoses in its consultative medicine clinic from 2009 to 2010. 8 The study characterized diagnostic outcomes as: 1) same referral and final diagnosis; 2) final diagnosis further defined/refined compared to the referral diagnosis; and 3) distinctly different referral and final diagnosis. 8 Results showed that 66% of final diagnoses were better defined/refined, 21% of final diagnoses were distinctly different from their initial diagnoses, and only 13% of these diagnoses remained unchanged after patients completed their workup through the consultative medicine clinic. This study supports the pivotal role that consultative medicine clinics can play in optimizing the care of patients, if implemented at more institutions across the nation. 8

Consultative medicine practices can also serve as a critical lifeline for patients seeking second opinions on conditions that have gone unanswered due to their complexity. 8 Many primary care practitioners welcome second opinions, as they have been found to lead to changes in diagnosis and/or treatments, an overall positive clinical impact in patients’ care, and improvements in patient satisfaction. 14,15 As the need for high-quality second medical opinions arises for patients with complex medical questions, consultative medicine practices are well-positioned to meet this need.

The term “diagnostic odyssey” is commonly used to describe patient journeys in consultative medicine practices. It is important to understand that a diagnostic odyssey workup, however, indicates a specific workup that utilizes genetic evaluations to obtain precise diagnoses for rare and undiagnosed diseases, and it is not an essential aspect of every consultative medicine workup. In contrast to a diagnostic odyssey evaluation, most consultative medicine clinics focus on comprehensive care: thorough history-taking, physical examinations, advanced diagnostics, and timely, coordinated specialist consultations to create a comprehensive treatment plan. 8 Some institutions use a tumor-board-like approach, such as the Socrates Project at Northwestern University. 16 In this model, a general internist presents complex cases to a multidisciplinary team (including other internists, subspecialists, surgeons, radiologists, and pathologists) that collaborates on making diagnostic recommendations for the referring provider. Whether using focused evaluation or collaborative decision-making, institutions have developed strategies to meet the growing complexity of patient care.

In the consultative medicine model at the Mayo Clinic, the patient experience is focused on episodic, complex care evaluations. Patients may self-refer or be referred by their local care team to the consultative medicine clinic, often after initial referrals to other specialties are redirected due to multisystem concerns or undiagnosed conditions. After requesting an appointment, patients complete a patient visit questionnaire (PVQ) outlining their concerns, priorities, and prior diagnostic testing. A general internist reviews each PVQ to triage anticipated testing and specialty consultations, enabling development of a tailored, multidisciplinary itinerary.

Each patient’s episode of care focuses on diagnosing challenging medical conditions and creating targeted treatment plans using multidisciplinary assessments. The episode begins with a 90-minute evaluation by a consultative medicine physician, allowing comprehensive history-taking, physical examination, record review, documentation, and care coordination. Following this assessment, patients undergo targeted testing and consultations, with the itinerary adjusted as needed based on clinical findings. Results from ongoing evaluations are continuously integrated by the consultative physician to refine the diagnostic approach.

Once final diagnoses and recommendations are made, the internal medicine physician conducts a summary visit. This summary visit is an important aspect of the episode of care. The internist finalizes the patient’s diagnoses and recommendations, providing a comprehensive, integrated plan. This summary visit lends itself well to a virtual option for patients who are unable to attend a follow-up visit. Upon completion of the comprehensive evaluation, a summary document, like a hospital discharge summary, is generated for each patient. This document outlines essential findings and specific management recommendations for patients to share with their local care teams. The outpatient summaries are designed to help busy local clinicians quickly identify the final care recommendations without wading through multiple documents. This step ensures patients are safely transitioned back to their local care teams and helps prevent communication lapses between health systems. These recommendations typically include several tiers of actionable recommendations, supporting ongoing local care (Figure 1).

The internists involved in the consultative medicine model at Mayo Clinic do not provide longitudinal care. Instead, they emphasize that long-term care is provided by patients’ primary care clinicians back home. Patients who want to return to the clinic must submit another PVQ for consideration. This allows Mayo Clinic consultative medicine to serve more patients who may benefit from this care model. As the clinic focuses on complex and undiagnosed cases, resources and workflows are not designed to provide longitudinal care for chronic and preventive health care needs.

Analysis of Internal Data Outcomes at the Mayo Clinic Consultative Medicine Clinic

The Mayo Clinic Center for the Science of Health Care Delivery evaluated deidentified outcomes data from 573 patients seen in the consultative medicine practice with complex medical conditions. Use of this data did not require institutional review board approval due to minimal risk. To assess diagnostic and treatment outcomes, chart reviews of clinical notes were conducted across visits, with repeat visits consolidated as part of a continuous care episode. As a result, 55% of patients had a change or clarification in diagnosis, and 82% experienced a change in treatment plan, including 65.6% with substantial changes following their workup. Specifically, among 49 patients seeking surgical second opinions, 17 avoided surgery by pursuing comprehensive medical management. Furthermore, many of these complex patients, who were high utilizers of health care, also experienced reduced health care usage and costs. These outcomes are shown in Figure 2. Extrapolating to the 5000 patients seen in the consultative medicine clinic in 2024, approximately 2750 patients had a change in diagnosis during their episode of care, and over 4000 experienced treatment modifications. Patient-reported experiences were highly favorable, with 98% rating the practice 4 or 5 on a 5-point Likert scale and expressing strong confidence in the recommendations.

It is important to acknowledge the possibility of selection bias, given the patients seen in the tertiary care clinic can be self-referred individuals who are seeking second opinions. As the data available for this review are confined to the patients’ time in the clinic, long-term longitudinal outcome tracking was not performed. Further studies are warranted to follow these patients and assess their outcomes in the time after completion of their consultative medicine visits.

Focused Clinical Practices Within Consultative Medicine

This consultative internal medicine model has also allowed the authors to develop focused clinical practices that address specific patient needs, which are nuanced but not addressed by other specialties. The authors have developed 2 such specific areas with acute access for timely diagnosis, including the Undiagnosed Mass Clinic and the Multicancer Detection Clinic.

The Undiagnosed Mass Clinic emerged from an unmet need within the institution for patients seen in the emergency department who were found to have a new, suspicious mass on imaging, regardless of whether it was related to their presenting concern or not. The consultative general internists who participate in this clinic have developed and continue to hone skills related to utilizing the advanced diagnostic modalities and interventional radiology expertise to obtain tissue diagnoses in an expedited manner. 17

Another unmet need identified was the need for a clinic where patients who have obtained MCD tests elsewhere could undergo evaluation. As these tests are new, the emerging science is not widely known, particularly because they lack Food and Drug Administration approval for use. Furthermore, many clinicians are unfamiliar with how to order an MCD test for their patients. The authors have gathered general internists who have a special interest in evaluating patients with these positive test results to help provide answers for patients and, once again, decrease the burden on primary care clinicians. 18

Part of the benefit of allowing these specialty clinics to develop within the consultative medicine model is that the authors have adequate visit length to be able to take a thorough history, evaluate outside medical records and imaging, perform a thorough physical examination, and consult with radiology, pathology, and other specialists across the institution to develop a diagnostic plan.

Conclusion

As medicine evolves, new health care delivery models are needed for a changing patient population. When deciding whether a patient should have a workup in a consultative medicine practice outside their local primary care practice, patients and providers should discuss whether this type of evaluation may be indicated based on the complexity of the evaluations required. Consultative medicine evaluations can seem costly upfront, due to travel, lodging, and the often-required multisystem workups. However, these evaluations often reduce total health care spending by shortening diagnostic journeys, avoiding unnecessary testing, and improving diagnostic accuracy. 8

Primary care shortages are deepening, and care is shifting toward the patient-centered medical home. The growing burden of chronic disease will intensify pressure on frontline clinicians. This drives the need for integrated, multidisciplinary consultative models. In this setting, consultative general internists can play a critical role, synthesizing complex data, guiding resource-efficient care, and delivering coordinated high-quality diagnostic and treatment plans to be executed by local care teams.

Consultative medicine programs remain limited nationwide. Only 3 major medical systems offer such practices. 8 The scarcity and inversely proportional demand for these practices can create equity and accessibility issues. Therefore, more systems should create models across the nation, so patients with complex medical conditions can receive these evaluations.
