Rent as a toxic exposure: The Bronx and Manhattan
Authors: Deborah Wallace
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesNew York City, like many global cities, suffers from an ever-intensifying housing famine, especially of low-cost rental units, which has led to steeply rising rents and housing code violations. The purpose of this study is to explore whether rent affects public health patterns over the Bronx and Manhattan.Study designMedian rent was regressed against public health indicators by borough 2005–2021.Data and methodsBronx and Manhattan annual birth rates, rates of low-weight births, obesity prevalence, and mortality rates from drug overdose, cerebrovascular disease, heart disease, and diabetes were acquired from the NYC Department of Health. Annual median rent for each borough was acquired from the American Community Survey. Bivariate and multivariate regressions revealed the associations.ResultsBoth boroughs’ birth rates and obesity prevalence associated strongly with median rent, negatively for birth rate, positively for obesity prevalence. Rates of Bronx low-weight births, of Bronx and Manhattan drug deaths, and of Bronx cerebrovascular deaths declined in the early years but came to a threshold and rose. The threshold year-2021 rates associated strongly with annual median rents for those years. Rates of heart disease and diabetes mortalities showed a threshold effect, but plateaued, not rising.ConclusionMedian rent behaved like a toxic chemical, eliciting defined patterns of public health degradation. The mix of responses to median rent mimic public health responses to the heavy metal lead: some without and some with a threshold. Toxic chemicals are regulated. Rent should be also.

Introduction

Background on toxic exposures

The heavy metal lead triggers different reactions at different doses. It has no threshold for disrupting development in fetuses and young children [1]. In adults, blood lead levels of 25–40 μg per deciliter trigger health effects, some without overt symptoms. Levels of 40–80 seriously damage health. Levels over 80 leave serious permanent health damage. The nervous and renal systems show reactions before most others [2]. The scientific literature has described lead's dose/response system. Lead is one of the well-studied toxins. Its pattern of inducing different symptoms at different levels of exposure holds true for several other toxins.

In this study of rent and housing violations during the housing famine, rising rent shows dose/response patterns like a toxic chemical.

Background of the housing famine

The roots of the current housing famine lie in public policies generated by the FIRE (Finance, Insurance, and Real Estate) industries: redlining, urban renewal, and planned shrinkage. Redlining implemented Homer Hoyt's ethnic grading system with Northern Europeans at the top, Negroes and “Mexicans” at the bottom, and Jews only slightly above the bottom. He defined property values along this gradient. Property owners in redlined areas could not receive federally-backed mortgages, home-improvement loans, or insurance. The disinvestment eroded building and neighborhood conditions. For detailed description of 1938 redlining and its origins, see [3 and references therein].

In the post-war era, Congress passed acts enabling urban renewal, also a policy from the FIRE industries. The “slums” generated by redlining would be razed and redeveloped with massive housing projects. Communities of color again became the targets and suffered destabilization [4]. More housing units were destroyed than built [4]. Governmental managers of urban renewal forced the migration of whole populations such as the removal of the Puerto Rican residents of “San Juan Hill” into the South Bronx. Lincoln Center stands on the site of “San Juan Hill” [5].

Roger Starr, the intellectual of the real estate industry, created the policy of planned shrinkage which dictated governmental disinvestment in poor neighborhoods of color, labeled “dying”. It paraded as a triage system, accelerating the so-called inevitable death of these neighborhoods and syphoning resources into “healthy” neighborhoods to keep them healthy [6]. First implemented in New York City during the Lindsay Administration and heavily during the 1975 fiscal crisis, the brunt fell on the Fire Department. Poor communities, especially communities of color, lost fire companies, and all neighborhoods lost adequate staffing of the remaining companies. The initial 1972 cuts raised fire deaths in 1973 and ignited the famous 1970's fire epidemic, especially concentrated in the South and Central Bronx. Further rounds of cuts in 1974, 1975, and 1976 burned large portions of housing in whole neighborhoods [3]. Between 1970 and 1980, New York City lost 300–350,000 housing units, most of them low-cost housing [7]. The imbalance between demand and supply of fire service continues to erode housing stock in the Bronx and Manhattan [8].

Roger Starr's two main books exhibit his virulent, irrational racism behind planned shrinkage: Urban Choices and The Rise and Fall of New York City [9,10]. Three of his major New York City governmental implementers (Donna Shalala, John Zuccotti, and Victor Marrero) during the fiscal crisis slipped into the Carter Administration spread the pseudoscience of planned shrinkage nationally [6].

Although redlining and urban renewal had planted the seeds of the current housing famine, planned shrinkage germinated those seeds and fertilized them into full bloom. In New York City and many others, the discriminatory structure of housing preservation services such as fire prevention and control remain in their planned shrinkage configuration and continue to eat away at the existing housing [8] while the mass media report on real estate developers' campaign for new tax incentives and subsidies to build new housing.

The Roosevelt Administration structured the New Deal, under which redlining originated, to thwart the spread of socialism [11]. The field notes of the Federal Housing Administration's surveyors on which the redlining scheme depended include comments on ethnicities, immigrant status, and political leanings of inspected communities [3]. The detailed machinations of urban renewal in many cities reveal intertwining of political and economic interests. Developers grew fat with undervaluation of the “slum” properties they could purchase and tax abatements and subsidies [4]. The marriage of the real estate industry and New York City government briefly came into the open when Beame named Starr as Commissioner of the Department of Housing Preservation and Development a brief appointment because of the outcry against planned shrinkage [6].

Rents locally in New York City and nationally have sky-rocketed in recent decades. Another outfall of the housing famine is the impunity with which landlords leave housing code violations unrepaired. Revenue increases and costs decrease. In the Bronx, the 2005–2024 annual median rent positively associates with the annual number of housing violations with an R-square of 0.80: the more rent, the more violations. Epidemiologists have long identified housing as a major determinant of population health [12]. Dynamics of rent and violations deserve examination as potential forces in that determination.

Methods

Data sources

County and community district (CD) data on rates of birth and of low-weight births came from the table “Live Births by Selected Characteristics and Community District of Residence, New York City” in the 2005–2021 Annual Summary of Vital Statistics at the NYC Department of Health (DoH) website. Annual rates of specific cause mortality in the Bronx and Manhattan were acquired for 2005–2021 from a table in the Annual Summary titled “Deaths and Death Rates per 100,000 Population for Selected Underlying Causes by Community District, New York City.” The DoH's home page has a button “data’ which leads to another button “publications” which leads to “periodic publications” button. One types in “Annual Summary of Vital Statistics”. Annual rates of obesity came from DoH also: https://a816-dohbesp.nyc.gov/indicatorPublic/data-explorer/overweight/?id=2063#dusplay=summary.

County-level median gross rents 2005–2019 from the American Community Surveys came from website Department of Numbers [13]. The 2020 and 2021 median rents were estimated from changes 2019–2020 and 2020–2021 in “real median gross rent” furnished by the Furman Center [14]. The median gross rents 2005–2019 reported by Department of Numbers associated with those reported by the Furman Center with an R-square of 0.97. Thus, the calculated 2020 and 2021 median rents are valid. Data USA supplied CD socioeconomic Census data for 2020 [15].

Bronx housing violations data were downloaded from the open-access violations database of the Department of Housing Preservation and Development.

Analytical methods

Only very simple analytical methods were applied to the data: graphing rates against year, graphing rates against median rent, bivariate regression, multivariate regression, and comparisons of means, medians, and standard deviations. Excel spreadsheets organized the data and created the graphs. Statgraphics for Windows version 5.1 was the statistics program.

Results

Linear dose/response relations with median rent

Median rent over the Bronx increased by 30 % between 2005 and 2022; over Manhattan by 41 %. In 2005, the Bronx-wide percent of income going to rent was 31.94; in 2022, 37.21. In 2005, the Manhattan-wide percent of income going to rent was 22.05; in 2022, 24.63. Even in 2005, Bronx tenants spent a median percent of their incomes on rent well beyond the 25 % level recommended in the 1970s and beyond the presently recommended 30 %. In Manhattan, even the 2022 median percent of income spent on rent remained a bit below the 25 % level and well below 30 %. However, rents in Manhattan were much higher than in the Bronx. In 2005, the difference was 32 %; in 2022, 43 %.

Dose/response relations with a threshold

Several markers of public health improved in both boroughs in the early years of the study period: percent low-weight births and rates of drug deaths, heart disease mortality, diabetes mortality, cerebrovascular mortality. The 2011–2021 rates of low-weight births in the Bronx associated positively with median rents with R-square 0.71. The 2010–2021 rates of drug deaths in the Bronx associated positively with median rents with R-square 0.86; those of Manhattan, 0.70. The 2009–2021 Bronx cerebrovascular mortality rate associated with median rent with R-square 0.98.

The median rents of the inflexion points were: Bronx low-weight birth rate $1093, Bronx drug death rates $1088, Manhattan drug death rates $1458, and Bronx cerebrovascular death rates $1050. Because the Bronx rates of low-weight births, drug deaths, and cerebrovascular deaths associated with median rent and not with income percent devoted to rent, we can hypothesize that the driving force for these public health patterns was rent increase and that these inflexion points showed a threshold effect of rent.

If Manhattan had maintained its early rate of decline in drug death rate through the study period, it would have had a drug death rate of zero by 2015 instead of the 29.2 per 100,000 in 2021. If the Bronx had maintained its early rate of decline in drug death rate through the study period, its 2021 drug death rate would have been 5.2 per 100,000 instead of the registered 56.6.

A second temporal pattern of borough-level public health indicator has a threshold with cessation of decline followed by a plateau. The indicator declined markedly in the early years, reached an inflexion, and then showed a bumpy, wavy plateau, usually with a 2020 spike. Heart mortality rate provides an example (Fig. 4a and b). In the Bronx, the year of heart mortality inflexion point 2011; in Manhattan, 2013/2014. The declines in both boroughs from 2005 to inflexion were steady and large.Fig. 4Rate of heart mortality by year 2005–2021 a. Bronx b. Manhattan.Fig. 4

In the Bronx, heart mortality rate declined an average of 9.17 deaths/100000 per year 2005–2011. In Manhattan, the average annual decline was 6.81/100000 per year 2005–2014. If those rates had continued through 2021, the Bronx would have had a 2021 heart mortality rate of only 42.6, instead of 189; Manhattan, 57.6 instead of 166. With every dollar of rent rise, the Bronx suffered a rise of 0.52 heart deaths per 100000 population and Manhattan, a rise of 0.22. The Bronx median rent at its 2011 inflexion point was $1093; the Manhattan at its 2014 inflexion point, $1616.

Diabetes mortality rate also showed an inflexion point in both boroughs, followed by a plateau. In the Bronx, the inflexion occurred in 2010. The plateau ended in 2015 with instability through 2021. In Manhattan, the inflexion occurred in 2009, with the following plateau also ending in 2015, a year that began a period of instability through 2021. Once these boroughs entered the plateau, no reliable declining trend recurred.

The strongest socioeconomic (SE) associations with declines in birth rate over the Bronx CDs percent households with rent burden (R-square 0.79) and housing violations/1000 units (R-square 0.61). Housing violations in the Bronx

During the study period, housing violations increased spectacularly in the Bronx (Fig. 5). The only major dip occurred in 2020 when the strictures to limit transmission of COVID-19 disrupted documenting violations. In 2005, about 19,000 Bronx housing violations were registered at HPD. By 2024, that number had ballooned to over 330,000. Furthermore, 2018–2024, the nature of these violations had changed: a higher percent fell into the “C” class, immediately dangerous or an immediate threat to health, the most serious class.Fig. 5Bronx housing violations by year 2005–2024.Fig. 5

When annual median rents and annual housing violations were regressed against annual birth rates in the Bronx in multivariate regression, the combination of these factors associated with birth rates negatively (R-square 0.92). This tiny increase in R-square over that of the bivariate regression of only median rents and birth rates (R-square 0.90) shows the deep influence of median rent.

Discussion

The Bronx attained the dubious distinction of unhealthiest county in New York State [16]. Both public health professionals and local elected politicians named an array of socioeconomic factors: extremely low household incomes, low educational attainment, high unemployment rate, high percent of non-European Americans, and high percent of low-wage workers [3]. Housing factors in this pattern of health deficits escaped mention.

A few studies on other geographic areas found that rent associated negatively with birth rate [17,18]. Another few studies found that rent and rent burden associated positively with obesity rate [19,20]. This study found that both the Bronx and Manhattan suffered declining rates of birth and increasing rates of obesity that strongly associated with median rent. In contrast to the Bronx, Manhattan had community districts with high percent of adults with college or higher degrees, median household incomes over $100,000, high percent of white population, low unemployment rates, and low percent of extremely overcrowded housing units [21]. Like the effect of lead on fetal and early childhood development, the effect of rent showed no threshold for either birth rate or obesity rate in either the Bronx or Manhattan.

In the Bronx, median rent showed evidence of thresholds in its associations with rates of low-weight births ($1093), of drug deaths ($1088), and of cerebrovascular deaths ($1050). Once median rent reached these levels, the decline in these rates ceased, followed by rates that strongly associated with median rent. Indeed, the R-square for rates of cerebrovascular mortality regressed against median rent for 2009–2021 was 0.98; for percent low-weight births 2011–2021, 0.71; for rate of drug deaths 2011–2021, 0.86.

The third class of dose/response behavior supported the results for the two described above, the class without a threshold and the class with the threshold followed by a rent-associated rise: a threshold followed by a plateau. In other words, the early decline in mortality rate ceased at a defined point which leads to a wavering plateau. Examples of this third class are heart mortality and diabetes mortality rates. The years of inflexion points for these mortality rates are: Bx heart 2011, Mn heart 2013, Bx diabetes 2010, Mn diabetes 2009.

Three criteria dictate assignment of causality to an independent variable: strength of association, consistency in association, and plausible mechanisms for association. The large R-squares for the first two classes of dose/response behavior fulfill the criterion for strength of association. The associations for decline in birth rate and increase in obesity rate arose in two different boroughs. The association for drug death rate also arose in two different boroughs. The Bronx associations for percent low-weight births and cerebrovascular mortality rate closely show consistency with that for drug death rate. Both boroughs suffered cessation of declines in heart disease and diabetes mortality rates, revealing consistency.

Are there mechanisms for these associations and consistent process patterns? The force of rent and rent rise can channel through both direct material deprivation and sense of threat to security.

In 2022, median household income in Manhattan was $99530; in the Bronx, $47036. The “real” median gross rents (rent plus utilities) were $2100 and $1400 [14]. Thus, this median rent burden in Manhattan was 25.3 % and that in the Bronx, 35.7 %. If this median rent is subtracted from the median income, the median Manhattan household has $74330 left and the Bronx, $30236. The US Census [22] reported that in 2019–2023, Manhattan had 2.01 persons per household; the Bronx, 2.57. Per person resource for the median Manhattan household, after rent payment, is $36980; that for the Bronx, $11765. The differences between the boroughs with respect to household income and household size may explain differences with respect to relationships between rent and health outcomes. Some outcomes strongly associated with rent in the Bronx show little or no association in Manhattan.

The range of 2022 median household income by CD is $32310-$79190 for the Bronx and $36930-$165380 for Manhattan. East Harlem is the sole Manhattan CD with a median household income of less than $50000: $36930. Seven of the Manhattan twelve CDs had median household incomes over $100000. None of the Bronx CDs had a median household income over $100000. Eight of the twelve Bronx CD had median household incomes of less than $50000.

Both material deprivation and structural stress pervade the Bronx. Families must choose between paying for electricity and paying for the son's eye glasses; between paying for fresh produce and paying for prescription medicines; between paying for winter coats and paying for required school supplies. These decisions directly affect health.

Families feel the threat of yet another rent rise, of possible eviction, of the precarity of being the doubled-up people in an apartment without their name on the lease.

Many rent-associated health indicators have been linked to structural stress in decades of public health literature. The work of Bjorntorp [23] linked stress to metabolic syndrome, obesity, and diabetes. Spiegel et al. [24] linked stress to sleep dysfunctions and obesity. Geronimus [25] linked structural racism and its stresses to accelerated aging in Black women and higher rates of low-weight births than to white women of the same age. Many other authors linked stress with low-weight births (Nkansah-Amankra et al. [26]; Borders et al. [27]; Dallo et al. [28]. Nkansah-Amankra et al. [26] demonstrated linkages between family and neighborhood levels of poverty and educational attainment, linkages that determined risk of low-weight birth.

Case and Denton [29] termed drug-overdose deaths, “deaths of despair”. In Reckoning, Currie [30] conveyed the sociology of drug use and its roots in unemployment, poverty, and precarity. The association in the Bronx and Manhattan of drug mortality rates with rent pinpoint rent as a major source of despair and precarity.

Stroke also associated in public health literature with psychosocial stress (Kronenber et al. [31]; Behymer et al. [32]). Kronenberg et al. also linked psychosocial stress with such cardiovascular conditions as myocardial infarction. Thus, stroke and heart disease share a determinant.

All criteria for concluding that rent behaves as a toxic exposure in the Bronx and Manhattan have been met: strength of associations, consistency of associations, and likely mechanisms and mediators between cause and outcomes. Rent behaving as a toxic exposure in 21st century America necessitates a regulatory response akin to regulations on lead, mercury, and arsenic exposures. Although this geographically limited study is the first to examine the effect of rent on rates of several health indicators, the previous studies by other authors on rent and birth rates and on rent and obesity point to a general phenomenon.

Rent as a toxic exposure also opens the discussion to issues of environmental justice. Poor women have higher risk of low-weight births. Poor women in poor neighborhoods have additional risk of low-weight births. The dynamics of rates of low-weight births in the Bronx reflect the environmental injustice of the inequality of neighborhood rent and socioeconomic resources over the CDs. Birth rates also showed neighborhood effects with poor CDs showing greater declines in birth rate between 2005 and 2021. Drug death rates showed a similar SE gradation. One determinant of the vast differences between the Bronx and Manhattan in rates of low-weight births, drug deaths, obesity, cerebrovascular deaths, and heart disease deaths may well be the differences in rent burden and in the remaining household income per person after rent payment.

In most industrial and post-industrial countries, environmental and occupational health regulations stipulate exposure concentrations and durations for toxic chemicals. These regulations often result from conflicts between the regulating agencies, affected corporations, and organizations such as labor unions and environmental groups. However, the regulations limit exposures. Rent has no health-based regulation. If further research supports this study's results and conclusions, public policy requires regulation.
