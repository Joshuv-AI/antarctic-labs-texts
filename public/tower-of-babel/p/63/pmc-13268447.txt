The J-shaped association between the ZJU index and depression: A cross-sectional study from NHANES
Authors: Huasheng Zhang, Wanyi Liu, Yuliang Chen, Yang Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have confirmed that the ZJU index is effective in capturing trends in metabolic disorders and disease risk. However, no study has statistically analyzed the relationship between the ZJU Index and depression. This study included data from 16,008 participants sourced from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2005–2018. Weighted multivariable logistic regression and generalized additive models were used to explore the independent and nonlinear relationships between the ZJU index and depression. A 2-piece linear regression model was used to estimate threshold effects, along with subgroup analyses and interaction tests. To account for multiple testing in subgroup analyses, P values for interaction were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. A total of 16,008 participants were included, with 7.42% classified as having depression. The prevalence of depression initially decreased and then increased across the ZJU index quartiles (Q1: 6.35%; Q2: 5.38%; Q3: 6.83%; Q4: 11.32%; P < .0001). Both unadjusted and adjusted models demonstrated that higher ZJU index levels were associated with increased depression risk. In Model III, each unit increase in the ZJU index was associated with a 1.4% higher likelihood of depression (Model III: odds ratio (OR) = 1.014, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.006, 1.022). Curve fitting indicated a J-shaped association between the ZJU index and depression, with a breakpoint (K) of 35.386. To the left of this breakpoint, the ZJU index showed a negative association with depression (OR = 0.948, 95% CI: 0.917–0.980), while to the right, a positive association was observed (OR = 1.022, 95% CI: 1.013–1.032). Subgroup analyses and interaction tests revealed that the association between the ZJU index and depression was dependent on drinking status and diabetes presence (P < .05). Among individuals who consumed alcohol or had diabetes, a positive association between the ZJU index and depression was observed, whereas no significant association was found among nondrinkers or those without diabetes, where the ZJU index and depression showed a nonlinear relationship. There is a J-shaped association between the ZJU index and depression, suggesting that a higher ZJU index may be linked to an increased risk of depression.

1. Introduction

Depression is a mental disorder characterized by a persistent and pervasive low mood, accompanied by symptoms including anhedonia, feelings of guilt, impaired concentration, decreased appetite, suicidal ideation, and other disturbances in cognitive, behavioral, and social functioning.[1] According to the Global Burden of Disease, the global prevalence of depression rose from 172 million cases in 1990 to 258 million in 2017, an increase of 49.86%.[2] In 2017, nearly 17.3 million adults in the United States experienced at least one major depressive episode, with a prevalence of about 7.1%, the highest among individuals identifying as 2 or more races.[3] Depression contributes substantially to the global burden of disease. In 2008, the World Health Organization ranked major depressive disorder as the third leading cause of global disease burden and projected it to become the leading cause by 2030.[4] While antidepressants are widely used and relatively effective in clinical settings, they frequently cause side effects and can take weeks to show benefits.[5] Identifying new biomarkers for estimating depression risk is therefore crucial for improving prevention and treatment strategies.

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome, which includes obesity, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, glucose intolerance, and type 2 diabetes.[6,7] Depression often co-occurs with numerous chronic conditions, many of which are also associated with NAFLD.[8] The ZJU index, first proposed by Wang et al in a cross-sectional study involving 9602 Chinese individuals who underwent liver ultrasonography, has been recognized as an effective screening tool for NAFLD in Chinese populations (AUC = 0.822). This novel metabolic index integrates aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), triglycerides (TG), body mass index (BMI), and glucose levels, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment than single biochemical markers.[9]

Although the ZJU Index is primarily used to evaluate parameters related to metabolic syndrome, accumulating evidence has demonstrated a bidirectional association between metabolic dysfunction and depression. The interplay between metabolic syndrome and depression may influence depressive symptoms not only through the direct effects of metabolic abnormalities but also indirectly via mechanisms such as hepatic dysfunction and systemic inflammation. Previous studies have shown that NAFLD is closely associated with the onset of depression, potentially affecting emotional regulation through pathways involving insulin resistance and inflammatory responses. By integrating multiple metabolic markers, including the ALT/AST ratio, TGs, BMI, and blood glucose levels, the ZJU Index comprehensively reflects early metabolic disturbances, thereby providing a novel perspective for assessing depression risk. Elevated ZJU Index values may indicate increased systemic inflammation and impaired liver function, which could exacerbate the onset or severity of depression. Emerging evidence further suggests complex interactions among metabolic disorders, liver dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and depression, potentially mediated through dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Collectively, as a comprehensive metabolic biomarker, the ZJU Index offers novel insights into the potential link between metabolic disorders and depression.[10–13]

To date, only limited studies have investigated the association between the ZJU index and depression. A recent cross-sectional study based on NHANES data has reported a significant association between the ZJU index and depression in U.S. adults. However, further validation and exploration of nonlinear relationships and subgroup heterogeneity remain necessary. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the potential relationship between the ZJU Index and depression using data from the NHANES, thereby elucidating the link between the ZJU Index and depression in the U.S. population.

2. Subject and methods

2.1. Data and sample source

This cross-sectional study used data from the NHANES from 2005 to 2018. NHANES employs a complex, stratified, multistage probability cluster sampling design to recruit a representative sample of the U.S. population.[14] The NHANES study protocol received approval from the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, with written informed consent obtained from all participants or the parents and/or legal guardians of participants under 16 years of age. Detailed NHANES study design and data are publicly accessible at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.Participants underwent standardized home interviews and health examinations at mobile examination centers (MECs) to assess medical and physiological conditions, alongside laboratory testing to gather lab data. We selected 7 NHANES cycles from 2005 to 2018 to evaluate the association between the ZJU index and depression. Exclusion criteria for this analysis included age <18 years, pregnancy, and missing data for depression diagnosis or ZJU index calculation. A flowchart of the study process is shown in Figure 1.

2.2. Definition of depression

Depression symptoms were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) based on DSM-IV criteria. Each item on the questionnaire is scored from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with a total score ranging from 0 to 27. A PHQ-9 score of ≥ 10 is used to diagnose depression, providing a sensitivity and specificity of 88%.[15]

2.3. Covariates

Based on previous research and biological considerations, we collected as many known confounding covariates related to depression as possible. Demographic characteristics included age, gender (female and male), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other Hispanic, Mexican American, and other races), education level (less than high school, high school, more than high school), poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), marital status (married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, never married), smoking status, and drinking status. These data were obtained through standardized questionnaires and face-to-face interviews. Physical examinations and laboratory tests were conducted by trained healthcare professionals at MECs. Smoking status was categorized as “never” (smoked <100 cigarettes in a lifetime) or “ever” (smoked at least 100 cigarettes in a lifetime). Drinking status was classified as “no” (consumed <12 alcoholic beverages in the past 12 months) or “yes” (consumed at least 12 alcoholic beverages in the past 12 months).[16] BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2) and was widely used to estimate overweight and obesity status.[17] Participants were classified as hypertensive if they met any of the following criteria: average systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or were informed by a healthcare professional that they had hypertension or were taking antihypertensive medication.[18] Participants were considered diabetic if they met any of the following criteria: informed by a healthcare professional that they had diabetes, currently using insulin, or taking oral medications to lower blood sugar.[19] Diagnoses of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease (CHD), angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, and stroke were ascertained based on self-reported information obtained from standardized questionnaires. Laboratory measurements included serum TG, total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), albumin, ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), hemoglobin, red blood cell (RBC) count, white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, percentage of segmented neutrophils, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, absolute segmented neutrophil count, and fasting glucose levels.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Given the complex, multistage sampling design of the NHANES survey, appropriate sample weights corresponding to different survey cycles were incorporated into all analyses to obtain nationally representative estimates of health-related parameters.[20,21] Continuous variables were expressed as weighted means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. We employed weighted Student’s t tests (for continuous variables) and weighted chi-square tests (for categorical variables) to assess differences among participants grouped by ZJU quartiles. To estimate the association between ZJU and depression, we used multivariable logistic regression models, examining the relationship across 3 different models. In Model 1, no covariates were adjusted. Model 2 adjusted for gender, age, race, education level, PIR, and marital status. Model 3 adjusted for gender, age, race, education level, PIR, marital status, smoking, drinking, (HDL-c) (mg/dL), LDL-c (mg/dL), TC (mg/dL), albumin (g/dL), ALP (U/L), BUN (mg/dL), Scr (mg/dL), WBC (109/L), segmented neutrophils percent, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, segmented neutrophils count, RBC (109/L), hemoglobin (g/dL), platelet count (PLT) (109/L), hypertension, diabetes, congestive heart failure, CHD, angina pectoris, heart attack, and stroke. Additionally, we utilized generalized additive models (GAM) and smooth curve fitting to analyze the nonlinear relationship between ZJU and depression. If a nonlinear correlation was observed, we employed piecewise linear regression models to fit each interval and calculate threshold or saturation effects. A likelihood ratio test was conducted to compare the single linear model (non-piecewise) with the piecewise linear regression model to determine the presence of a threshold. Finally, we conducted subgroup analyses on the correlation between ZJU and depression, stratifying by factors including gender (male/female), race (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other Hispanic, Mexican American, and other races), education level (less than high school, high school, more than high school), marital status (married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, never married), drinking (yes/no), smoking (yes/no), hypertension (yes/no), diabetes (yes/no), CHD (yes/no), and stroke (yes/no). These stratification factors were also considered as prespecified potential effect modifiers, and interaction terms were added using likelihood ratio tests to assess the heterogeneity of associations between subgroups.

Missing values were imputed using the median for continuous variables or the mode for categorical variables based on the existing cases for these variables. All analyses were performed using the R Package (http://www.r-project.org) and Empower Stats (http://www.empowerstats.com), with a significance level set at P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 16,008 participants were included in the study, with males accounting for 50.01% and females for 49.99%. The average age was 47.16 ± 17.36 years, and 7.42% of participants were classified as having depression. The prevalence of depression varied across ZJU quartiles, decreasing initially and then increasing (Q1: 6.35%; Q2: 5.38%; Q3: 6.83%; Q4: 11.32%; P < .0001). Statistically significant differences (P < .05) were observed among the 4 ZJU quartiles regarding various demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, race, education level, PIR, marital status, drinking status, BMI, waist circumference, HDL-c (mg/dL), TG (mmol/L), LDL-c (mg/dL), TC (mg/dL), albumin (g/dL), ALT (U/L), AST (U/L), ALP (U/L), BUN (mg/dL), Scr (mg/dL), WBC (10^9/L), segmented neutrophils percent, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, segmented neutrophils count, RBC (109/L), hemoglobin (g/dL), PLT (109/L), as well as the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, congestive heart failure, CHD, angina pectoris, heart attack, and stroke. 3.2. Association between ZJU and depression

Table 2 presents the relationship between ZJU and depression. Our findings indicate that higher ZJU levels are associated with an increased likelihood of depression. This correlation was observed in both unadjusted and adjusted models. In Model III, for each one-unit increase in ZJU, the odds of depression among participants increased by 1.4% (Model III: odds ratio (OR) = 1.014, 95% CI: 1.006, 1.022). Furthermore, when the total ZJU was categorized into quartiles, this association remained statistically significant. In Model III, participants in the highest ZJU quartile had a 27.2% greater likelihood of having depression compared to those in the lowest ZJU quartile (OR = 1.272; 95% CI: 1.046–1.549, P = .016).

Conversely, to the right of the breakpoint, ZJU showed a positive correlation with depression (OR = 1.022, 95% CI: 1.013–1.032) (Table 3).

3.4. Subgroup analysis

To verify the homogeneity of the association between ZJU and depression in the general population and to identify population-specific characteristics, our study conducted subgroup analyses and interaction tests based on factors such as gender, race, education level, marital status, drinking habits, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, CHD, and stroke. However, the results were not consistent across these factors. Significant interactions were detected with drinking habits and diabetes (P < .05), while no statistically significant interactions were observed for gender, race, education level, marital status, smoking status, hypertension, CHD, or stroke (all interaction P > .05). Our findings indicate that the association between ZJU and depression is dependent on drinking habits and diabetes status (P < .05). 3.5. Association of the ZJU index with depression in non-drinking patients

The assessment of potential nonlinear relationships using GAM and smooth curve fitting further enhanced our analysis. When stratifying by drinking status, the resulting curves indicated a nonlinear relationship within the non-drinking cohort (Fig. 4). Applying a piecewise linear regression model revealed a positive correlation between the ZJU index and depression in the drinking population (OR = 1.023, 95% CI: 1.012–1.034). Conversely, in the non-drinking population, the relationship between the ZJU index and depression was nonlinear. The threshold effect analysis identified a breakpoint (K) at 29.877. On the left side of this breakpoint, there was a negative correlation between the ZJU index and depression (OR = 0.806, 95% CI: 0.695–0.935), while no such negative correlation was detected on the right side (OR = 1.009, 95% CI: 0.997–1.022) (Table 4).

4. Discussion

This cross-sectional study investigated the association between the ZJU Index and depression using pooled data from the NHANES 2005–2018. A total of 16,008 eligible participants were included in the analysis. The results demonstrated a significant positive association between the ZJU Index and the risk of depression, which remained robust after full adjustment in Model 3. Smoothing curve fitting and threshold effect analyses revealed a J-shaped relationship between the ZJU Index and depression, with an inflection point at 35.386. Below this threshold, the ZJU Index was inversely associated with depression, whereas a positive association was observed above the threshold. Interaction analyses indicated that sex, race, education level, marital status, smoking status, hypertension, CHD, and stroke did not significantly modify this association. Although drinking status and diabetes showed nominally significant interactions, none of the interaction terms remained statistically significant after false discovery rate correction, suggesting that subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive.

A recent study by Cheng et al. (2026) also investigated the association between the ZJU index and depression using NHANES data. Consistent with our findings, they reported that higher ZJU index levels were associated with an increased risk of depression and identified a nonlinear, J-shaped relationship. However, several differences should be noted. First, the identified threshold values differed (31.39 in their study vs 35.386 in ours), which may be attributed to differences in sample size, covariate adjustment, and statistical modeling strategies. Second, while their subgroup analysis suggested effect modification by race, our findings indicated potential interactions with drinking status and diabetes. These similarities and differences collectively strengthen the evidence supporting a nonlinear association between the ZJU index and depression while highlighting the need for further studies to clarify population-specific effects.

The observed J-shaped association suggests that depression risk may increase simultaneously at both the lower and upper ends of the ZJU index distribution through distinct mechanisms. Elevated ZJU index values may reflect metabolic dysfunction, hepatic stress, and heightened systemic inflammation: factors consistently associated with depression. Conversely, lower ZJU index levels may correlate with malnutrition, chronic disease burden, sarcopenia, or socioeconomic disadvantage—conditions independently linked to depression. Individuals with low BMI, reduced TG levels, or abnormal liver enzyme markers may remain vulnerable to depression despite seemingly low metabolic risk scores, potentially due to factors such as frailty, insufficient metabolic reserve, or psychosocial stress.[22]

Notably, large-scale population-based studies have reported similar J-shaped or nonlinear associations between depression and body mass index, dietary factors, and inflammatory markers. These findings suggest that the relationship between metabolic status and depression is not strictly linear, potentially reflecting heterogeneous biological and psychosocial pathways operating at opposite ends of the metabolic spectrum. The present study extends this concept to the ZJU Index, a composite metabolic biomarker integrating body mass index, blood glucose, TGs, and the ALT/AST ratio. Compared with the previous study, our analysis incorporated a broader range of covariates, including detailed laboratory indicators and comorbid conditions, which may provide a more comprehensive assessment of the association. Previously, the ZJU Index has primarily been applied to the assessment of NAFLD and has shown positive correlations with hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in U.S. adults.[12] Li et al.’s meta-analysis, which included 6 studies involving 167,554 cases of depression, found a significant association between depression and increased NAFLD risk (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: [1.05, 1.24], P = .002).[23] Insulin resistance, a common feature of NAFLD, may explain the link between NAFLD and depression, given its known associations with depressive brain mechanisms.[8] Additionally, impaired liver function may trigger systemic inflammation, and heightened inflammatory markers could exacerbate depressive symptoms.[24] Metabolic disruptions and hepatic abnormalities may also affect mood regulation through the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, potentially raising depression risk.[25] Depression can itself lead to poor health behaviors, such as unhealthy eating, sleep disturbances, and reduced physical activity.[26] Unlike isolated biochemical markers, the ZJU index integrates metabolic and hepatic biomarkers, offering insights into metabolic dysregulation and disease risk. A higher ZJU index may indicate increased systemic inflammation and a higher prevalence of NAFLD, potentially exacerbating depression risk or severity.[27] In this study, we observed that the relationship between the ZJU index and depression varied by drinking and diabetes status (P for interaction < 0.05). Specifically, among individuals who drank alcohol or had diabetes, the ZJU index was positively associated with depression risk, suggesting that a high ZJU index could indicate a greater depression risk in these populations. In contrast, among those who did not drink or have diabetes, this association was not significant, and the ZJU-depression relationship was nonlinear. Drinking and diabetes may increase liver strain, leading to NAFLD, insulin resistance, and other metabolic abnormalities.[28] A higher ZJU index may indicate greater hepatic damage or metabolic dysregulation, both of which are linked to depression through multiple mechanisms. Additionally, diabetes is often accompanied by systemic inflammation, characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory markers such as TNF-α and IL-6, which can disrupt the blood-brain barrier and affect neurotransmitter metabolism, leading to depressive symptoms.[29,30] Among diabetic individuals, a higher ZJU index may reflect increased inflammatory responses, thus elevating depression risk. Both alcohol consumption and diabetes can also impact HPA axis function, triggering chronic stress responses, and HPA axis dysfunction has been linked to depression through feedback disruptions, elevated cortisol levels, and insulin resistance.[31,32] In drinkers and diabetic patients, a high ZJU index may thus reflect HPA axis dysregulation, further increasing depression risk. The ZJU Index provides a comprehensive perspective on early markers of metabolic syndrome by integrating multiple metabolic biomarkers, including BMI, blood glucose, TG, and the ALT/AST ratio. Compared to using any single biomarker alone, the ZJU Index more accurately reflects the complexity of metabolic disorders, thereby revealing potential associations between metabolic dysfunction and depression. Metabolic disorders—particularly nonalcoholic fatty liver disease and diabetes: may induce depression by amplifying systemic inflammatory responses, disrupting neurotransmitter metabolism in the brain, or impairing hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis function.

Our study has several strengths. The analysis is based on a nationally representative dataset with sample weights, making the findings generalizable to the U.S. adult population. We controlled for multiple confounding variables, and the large sample size allowed us to conduct robust subgroup analyses. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, the diagnosis of depression relies on the PHQ-9 questionnaire, which may introduce recall bias and affect the reporting of symptom severity. Some physical symptoms in the PHQ-9 (such as fatigue and changes in appetite) overlap with metabolic diseases (such as obesity, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [NAFLD], and insulin resistance), which may lead to an overestimation of the association between metabolic markers such as the ZJU index and depression. Nevertheless, the PHQ-9 is still widely used in various studies for the assessment of depression.[33,34] Second, the small effect size (odds ratio) suggests that the clinical and public health significance of this association may be limited. Given the large sample size of NHANES, even weak associations can achieve statistical significance; thus, distinguishing statistical significance from clinical impact is crucial. Third, the findings of this study are based solely on a single country and specific population, necessitating further research to validate its applicability to other populations. Most importantly, the cross-sectional study design cannot establish a causal relationship between the ZJU index and depression. Further validation through randomized controlled trials is required to confirm this association and elucidate potential mechanisms. Therefore, while this index provides important clues regarding metabolic factors associated with depression, its clinical utility must be interpreted with caution, particularly when developing prevention or treatment interventions. Additionally, although a recent study has reported similar findings, differences in analytical strategies and covariate adjustments may limit direct comparability.

5. Conclusion

We identified a J-shaped association between the ZJU index and depression, which integrates key biological markers such as body mass index, blood glucose, TGs, and the ALT/AST ratio. A higher ZJU index may reflect more severe systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, suggesting a potential link between elevated ZJU levels and depression. Further longitudinal and interventional research is warranted to verify our findings and to explore whether management of the metabolic factors captured by the ZJU index (e.g., through dietary adjustments and weight reduction) is associated with a lower risk or severity of depression.
