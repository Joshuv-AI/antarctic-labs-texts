Genome‐Wide Translatome Analysis Following Low‐Dose Ketamine to Reveal Novel Targets for Antidepressant Treatment
Authors: Oliver H. Miller, Nils Grabole, Isabelle Wells, Ludovic Bellier, Jonathan J. Nassi, Benjamin J. Hall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLow‐dose ketamine is an efficacious antidepressant for treatment‐resistant unipolar and bipolar depressed patients. Major depressive disorder patients receiving a single infusion report elevated mood within 2 h, and ketamine's antidepressant effects have been observed as long as 7 days posttreatment. In light of this remarkable observation, efforts have been undertaken to “reverse‐translate” ketamine's effects to understand its mechanism of action. Major advances have been achieved in understanding the molecular, cellular, and circuit‐level changes that are initiated by low‐dose ketamine. Although enhancement of protein synthesis clearly plays a role, the field lacks a comprehensive understanding of the protein synthesis program initiated after ketamine treatment. Here, using ribosome‐bound mRNA footprinting and deep sequencing (RiboSeq), we uncovered a genome‐wide set of actively translated mRNAs (the translatome) in medial prefrontal cortex after an acute antidepressant‐like dose of ketamine. Gene Ontology analysis confirmed that initiation of protein synthesis is a defining feature of antidepressant‐dose ketamine in mice, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis pointed to a role for GPCR signaling, metabolism, vascularization, and structural plasticity in ketamine's effects. One gene, VIPR2, whose protein product VPAC2 acts as a GPCR for the neuropeptide vasoactive intestinal peptide, was characterized in the cortex and identified as a potential novel target for antidepressant action. We demonstrate that VPAC2's functional expression in medial prefrontal cortex is limited to somatostatin‐positive neurons and that in vivo dosing of a VPAC2 agonist elicits complex effects on prefrontal cortical pyramidal neurons, bidirectionally modulating their activity and disrupting the structure of coordinated neural activity. Finally, we show that VPAC2 agonism is sufficient to drive an antidepressant response, confirming the validity of our approach to targeted drug development.

Introduction

Depression is the leading psychiatric cause of disability globally (Murray 2012), and first‐line therapies targeting the monoamine system are ineffective in a significant population of depressed patients (Mrazek et al. 2014; Rush 2006; Trivedi 2006). In patients who do respond to first‐line therapies, weeks to months of continuous treatment are required to reach peak therapeutic efficacy. A single infusion of the N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate (NMDA) glutamate receptor antagonist ketamine elicits an antidepressant response within 110 min of administration (Zarate et al. 2006). Despite ketamine's promise, its clinical use is limited by significant drawbacks. Patients commonly report dissociative and psychotomimetic side effects necessitating administration in a clinical setting, which restricts its accessibility to many patients. This underscores the urgent need to identify the molecular cascades downstream of NMDA receptor antagonism that mediate ketamine's therapeutic actions. By targeting downstream effectors, it may be possible to develop novel antidepressants that reproduce ketamine's efficacy while avoiding adverse effects. While ketamine has a serum half‐life on the order of hours, the antidepressant effects of a single low dose can persist for as long as a week in treatment‐resistant MDD patients (Zarate et al. 2006). What then is the basis for this long‐lasting effect? Several groups have reported that ketamine and specific antagonists of GluN2B‐containing NMDA receptors rapidly induce protein synthesis: in these studies, differential regulation of a handpicked subset of candidate proteins was observed (Autry 2011; Li 2010; Miller et al. 2014). Targeted Western blot experiments, while undoubtedly informative, provide insight only given an a priori hypothesis. Other groups have sought out an unbiased understanding of ketamine's effects on the transcriptional level (Bagot 2016a, 2016b). The results of these studies provide valuable insight into the differential regulation of mRNA expression and gene networks; yet, because transcriptional and translational processes are not perfectly coupled, there remains potential for false positives and false negatives when trying to define the mechanism of ketamine's actions. For example, posttranscriptional regulation of mRNA by RNA‐binding proteins such as FMRP and ZPB1 acts as a checkpoint between mRNA expression and protein synthesis and can be dynamically directed by intrinsic and extrinsic cues to suppress, or permit, translation of a given transcript (Bramham and Wells 2007; Glisovic et al. 2008; Holt and Schuman 2013).

For these reasons, we sought to assess the full complement of mRNAs that are actively translated in response to ketamine, in order to identify novel pathways underlying its long‐lasting antidepressant effect. Ribosomal mRNA profiling involves isolation and deep sequencing of mRNAs being translated at the ribosome at a given snapshot in time (Ingolia et al. 2009) and provides a quantitative and genome‐wide assessment of genes differentially regulated in a given condition. Here, we used ribosomal mRNA profiling to compare translational regulation in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) of mice injected with saline, an antidepressant (low) dose of ketamine, or a non‐antidepressant (high) dose of ketamine. Gene set analysis demonstrates that behaviorally effective, low‐dose, but not high‐dose, ketamine strongly upregulates pathways involved in the initiation and regulation of protein synthesis, and reveals significant enrichment of 17 diverse gene sets and downregulation of seven. We selected VPAC2, a druggable GPCR with an unexplored role in antidepressant behavior and a receptor for endogenous vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), for further investigation based on its large fold change, high significance, and tractability. We demonstrate that VPAC2 is functionally expressed specifically in somatostatin‐positive (SOM+) inhibitory interneurons and that activation of VPAC2 enhances SOM+ excitability in mPFC. Finally, we demonstrate that agonizing VPAC2 in vivo has significant impacts on broader pyramidal (PYR) neuron activity in the prefrontal cortex. VPAC2 might serve as a target for future antidepressant interventions.

Results

Calibration of Ketamine's Antidepressant Effects

Although ketamine's long‐lasting antidepressant‐like effect in rodents is robust and replicable, the exact dosage necessary to elicit such an effect varies depending on species, strain, age, sex, and even animal housing conditions (Bechtholt‐Gompf 2011; Carrier and Kabbaj 2013; Franceschelli et al. 2015; Nosyreva et al. 2014). Therefore, we first calibrated our in‐house antidepressant dose before undertaking genomic analysis (Figure 1A). Compared to saline‐injected controls, mice injected with 3 mg/kg ketamine displayed antidepressant‐like behavior in the forced swim test (FST), 24 h after a single treatment (Figure 1B). No other dose (10, 50, or 100 mg/kg) proved to drive antidepressant‐like behavior under these conditions, and locomotion rates were normalized before test time (Figure 1B,C). Based on these observations, we selected our experimental groups as saline (control), 3 mg/kg ketamine (antidepressant‐like), and 100 mg/kg ketamine (non‐antidepressant‐like) for ribosomal profiling (Figure 2).

Ribosomal profiling workflow. After 7 days of handling, mice were injected with saline, low‐dose ketamine (3 mg/kg), or high‐dose ketamine (100 mg/kg) and sacrificed 30 min later. mPFC tissue was collected and rapidly frozen. Tissue was processed to obtain total mRNA (for RNA‐seq) and ribosome‐protected mRNA (for ribosomal profiling), and cDNA libraries were produced and sequenced. cDNA reads were mapped to the mouse genome to assess differentially expressed genes and for gene set pathway analysis. Finally, we manually selected candidate genes based on high fold change, tractability, presumed safety, and novelty.

Isolation of Actively Translated mRNAs and Quality Control

Animals from all groups were handled for 5 min per day for 7 days before treatment in order to habituate the mice and reduce experimentally irrelevant changes in translation due to handling. Thirty minutes after saline or ketamine treatment, mice were rapidly sacrificed, and tissue from mPFC was isolated. Samples were processed to obtain both cytosolic mRNAs (for RNA‐Seq) and ribosome‐bound mRNA fragments (for ribosomal mRNA profiling). Quality control of the data confirms the prefrontal–cortical identity of the mRNA samples (Figure S1). Bioinformatic analysis revealed the set of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially translated genes (DTGs) in response to low‐ and high‐dose ketamine treatment.

Profiling the Translatome After Antidepressant Ketamine

Low‐dose ketamine is thought to exert its antidepressant effects in part by blocking NMDARs on inhibitory interneurons in mPFC and causing cortical disinhibition (Duman et al. 2016; Homayoun and Moghaddam 2007; Miller et al. 2016). Based upon this, we hypothesized that RNA‐Seq analysis would show increases in early immediate gene (IEG) expression in the 3‐mg/kg ketamine‐treated mPFC samples. To the contrary, analysis of mRNA abundance showed that 100‐mg/kg‐treated mice had increased expression of IEGs Jun, Klf2, Fos, and Klf4, and no significant increases in expression of IEGs in the low‐dose group were observed (Figure S2). Dysregulation of biological pathways and functional gene sets contributes to the manifestation of depression‐related behaviors (Bagot 2016a). Gene Ontology (GO) gene sets are curated based on the functional roles of the contained genes (Ashburner 2000). Analysis of RiboSeq data showed that low‐dose ketamine treatment drove a robust enhancement of several GO classes relating to the initiation and regulation of protein synthesis, including Eukaryotic Translation Elongation, Translation Initiation Complex Formation, and Peptide Chain Elongation (Figure 3A). These results fit well into the existing body of literature, in that initiation of mTOR‐dependent protein synthesis is an essential component of ketamine's long‐lasting antidepressant effects (Li 2010).

(A) Gene Ontology (GO) gene set analysis reveals that low‐dose, but not high‐dose, ketamine treatment results in upregulation of pathways involving translational regulation. (B) Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrates that low‐dose ketamine enhances 17 diverse gene sets involved in GPCR signaling, neuronal metabolism, vascularization, and structural plasticity. (C) GSEA gene set “GPCR” enhancement is driven by Vipr2 (encoding the VPAC2 receptor). (D) Six GSEA gene sets are significantly decreased following low‐dose ketamine.

In contrast to GO, classification of Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) gene sets is designed to detect changes in groups of functionally related genes whose expression is coordinately regulated (Mootha 2003). GSEA analysis revealed that low‐dose ketamine alters the expression of diverse gene sets involved in GPCR signaling, neuronal metabolism, vascularization, and structural plasticity (Figure 3B). Of particular interest is the robust 1.83‐fold increase in enrichment for the GPCR gene set. This gene set's enrichment is driven by core enrichment of G‐protein GNG4, serotonin receptor HTR1D, and VIP receptor VIPR2, among others (Figure 3C).

Interestingly, the GSEA gene sets that were downregulated after low‐dose ketamine primarily involve the replication of mitotic cells (Figure 3D). Together, these data suggest that an antidepressant dose of ketamine increases GPCR signaling, metabolism, and structural reorganization of existing cells, while potentially limiting the genesis of new glia or other mitotic cell types.

Identification of VPAC2 as a Relevant DTG After Acute Ketamine

Since enrichment analysis pointed to a significant change in genes related to neuronal function, such as GPCRs, we set out to identify candidate genes that, when differentially translated after low‐dose ketamine treatment, could drive the antidepressant phenotype. The low‐dose ketamine samples contained actively translating mRNAs representing 13,003 total genes (Figure 4A, top). Compared to saline‐treated control, 3‐mg/kg ketamine‐treated mice displayed a significant increase in the translation of 61 genes and a decrease in the translation of 22 genes (Figure 4A, bottom).

To ensure that the genes being translated are related to antidepressant‐like‐dose ketamine, and not due to nonspecific NMDAR antagonism‐related processes, we subtracted out any genes that were also significantly differentially regulated in mice injected with 100 mg/kg ketamine (Figure 4B,E), leaving 49 significantly increased DTGs and 21 significantly decreased DTGs (Figure 4C,F). With this increased confidence in the relevance of the remaining DTGs, we manually selected targets to study further based on the following criteria: The target (1) is pharmacologically tractable, (2) has a favorable prediction for side effects based on expression pattern and known phenotypes from knockout or knock‐in models and any other published data, and (3) has no established role in depression. Examples of significantly altered DTGs that were not investigated further include Ms4a15 (not selectively targetable), Aqp1 (nonspecific, body‐wide expression), and Th (established role in depression).

Based on the GSEA data pointing to the relevance of GPCRs in antidepressant‐dose ketamine's effects, and the fact that it is one of the most differentially increased DTGs, a prime candidate emerged in VIPR2. The VIPR2 mRNA codes for VPAC2 protein, a membrane‐bound, Gs‐coupled GPCR. Its primary endogenous ligand is VIP, a 28‐amino‐acid neuropeptide synthesized by VIP+ inhibitory interneurons. In the suprachiasmatic nucleus, ultrastructural data suggest that VIP peptide is co‐released with GABA from VIP+ interneuron synapses (Buijs et al. 1995; Castel and Morris 2000), and high‐frequency stimulation of VIP+ interneurons in the myenteric plexus elicits VIP peptide release and a slow depolarization of connected cells (Willard 1990). If this mechanism is conserved in mPFC, the upregulation in expression of VPAC2 that we observed after low‐dose ketamine treatment would promote a delayed, prolonged excitability in the postsynaptic partners of VIP+ interneurons.

Functional Expression of VPAC2 Is Confined to SOM+ Interneurons in mPFC, and Activation of VPAC2 Drives Excitability of SOM+ Interneurons in mPFC

First, we sought to determine which cell types in mPFC express VPAC2. To accomplish this, we crossed a Cre‐dependent tdTomato reporter mouse line (Ai14) (Madisen et al. 2010) to lines expressing Cre in subpopulations of inhibitory interneurons and patched fluorescent neurons in mPFC. Analysis of spiking parameters in SOM+, parvalbumin positive (PV+), and PYR neurons (Figure S3) shows expected characteristics: PV+ interneurons displayed a short latency to spike (Figure S3B,D) and a shorter action potential (AP) half‐width (Figure S3C,D) compared to SOM+ and PYR neurons. Both PV+ and SOM+ inhibitory interneurons displayed lower capacitance compared to PYR neurons (Figure S3F), and SOM+ cells showed higher membrane resistance relative to PV+ or PYR neurons (Figure S3G).

VIP interneurons in the cortex have recently become the focus of an intense effort of study (Fu 2014; Fu et al. 2015; Gentet 2012; Joo 2004; Karnani 2016). In the cortical microcircuit, VIP+ interneurons preferentially inhibit SOM interneurons via fast GABAA neurotransmission. Given the established cytoarchitecture and the published expression patterns in cortical neurons (Joo 2004), we hypothesized that SOM neurons would express VPAC2 and be VIP peptide‐sensitive.

To test this hypothesis, we assessed the effects of VPAC2 agonism on the excitability of these three mPFC neuronal cells. Under conditions of synaptic blockade (NBQX, APV, Picrotoxin), we injected depolarizing currents sufficient to elicit 5 ± 1 APs for SOM+ and PYR neurons, and the minimum amount of current to elicit consistent firing in PV+ neurons. After 5 min of stable AP responses, we washed in the selective VPAC2 agonist Bay55‐9837 (Bay55) and observed the effects on spiking in these neurons (Figure 5). When patching SOM+ neurons, perfusion of Bay55 increased the number of APs elicited by a maximal 260% (p < 0.01) (Figure 5A). Inclusion of GDPβs in the patch pipette prevented the excitatory effects of Bay55 (Figure S4), suggesting that VPAC2 agonism activates SOM+ interneurons by activating G‐protein‐coupled signaling pathways, such as activating adenylyl cyclase and elevating intracellular cAMP. Although VIP+ interneurons preferentially innervate SOM+ interneurons, they also form synaptic contacts with PV+ and PYR neurons (Dávid et al. 2007; Hajós et al. 1998). In contrast to SOM+ neurons, when recording PV+ or PYR neurons, perfusion of VPAC2 agonist had no significant effect on excitability (Figure 5B,C). These results suggest a cell‐type‐specific expression of VPAC2, which, when activated, leads to preferential excitation of SOM+ interneurons in mPFC.

VPAC2 Reshapes Pyramidal Activity and Prefrontal Network Dynamics In Vivo

While acute brain slice experiments provide mechanistic insight into the direct pharmacological actions of VPAC2 agonism on identified neurons, the emergent impact of such manipulations within intact neural circuits remains difficult to predict. To address this, we next examined how systemic VPAC2 activation shapes activity in mPFC microcircuits in vivo.

Given the cell‐type specificity observed ex vivo—that VPAC2 selectively increases excitability in SOM+ interneurons—we hypothesized that in vivo administration of the VPAC2 agonist Bay55 would lead to enhanced inhibition of PYR neurons and a corresponding decrease in their activity. To test this, we used cellular‐resolution calcium imaging in freely behaving mice. Pyramidal neurons in the mPFC were labeled via stereotaxic injection of AAV1‐CaMKII‐GCaMP6f (Figure 6A), and a GRIN lens was implanted to allow visualization of activity using a head‐mounted miniscope (Figure 6B,C). After recovery and habituation, mice were recorded during a 10‐min baseline session in the open field, injected with vehicle or Bay55 (0.2 mg/kg), and recorded again for 20 min posttreatment (Figure 6D). Calcium traces were extracted and deconvolved into inferred spike activity (Figure 6E), allowing us to compare within‐cell modulation across the session.

Contrary to our hypothesis, VPAC2 activation did not lead to a unidirectional suppression of PYR neuron activity. Instead, it induced bidirectional modulation: a significantly greater proportion of PYR neurons were either activated or inhibited following Bay55 administration, compared to vehicle (Figure 6F–I). Moreover, Bay55 increased the strength of modulation in both directions: the magnitude of inhibition in inhibited neurons was significantly greater than in vehicle‐treated controls, and the magnitude of activation in activated neurons increased, though to a lesser degree (Figure 6J–K). These findings suggest that VPAC2 agonism does not globally silence PYR neurons via interneuron‐mediated inhibition but instead shifts population dynamics by driving divergent activity profiles across subpopulations.

To probe whether this reorganization extended beyond single‐cell modulation to network‐level structure, we examined cell–cell correlations in activity before and after treatment. Correlation matrices of Z‐scored Spearman's rho values were generated from deconvolved calcium traces during baseline (Figure 7A,D) and posttreatment (Figure 7B,E) recordings. Bay55 treatment significantly reduced overall pairwise correlations among PYR neurons, compared to vehicle (Figure 7G), suggesting that VPAC2 activation disrupts coordinated activity across the population.

To assess how the structure of network relationships changed, we computed difference matrices using the absolute change in Z‐scored Spearman's rho between baseline and posttreatment sessions. Bay55 produced greater disruption in the correlation structure than vehicle (Figure 7C,F,H). Together, these results indicate that VPAC2 activation reduces the coherence of prefrontal network dynamics and reorganizes the structure of functional connectivity.

Activation of VPAC2 Drives Motivated Escape Behavior

Having established that ketamine treatment selectively increases VPAC2 expression and that VPAC2 agonism specifically enhances excitability of SOM+ interneurons, resulting in complex modulation of PYR neuron dynamics in the mPFC, we next sought to determine if VPAC2 activation could elicit antidepressant‐like behavior. To test this, we subchronically treated mice with the VPAC2 agonist Bay55 (0.2 mg/kg) and evaluated its effects on motivated escape behavior in the FST. Treatment with Bay55 significantly reduced immobility time compared to vehicle‐treated controls, consistent with an antidepressant‐like effect (Figure 8A). To ensure this behavioral effect was not confounded by changes in general activity levels, we measured basal locomotion using the open‐field test and confirmed that Bay55 treatment did not significantly alter locomotor activity at this dose (Figure 8B). Taken together, these results support VPAC2 activation as a novel mechanism for producing antidepressant‐like effects.

(B) The same treatment has no significant impact on total distance traveled in the open‐field test, suggesting that the observed behavioral effects are not confounded by changes in general locomotor activity (Control: 599.6 ± 41.8 cm; Bay55: 573.1 ± 45.2 cm; ns, p = 0.57, unpaired t‐test). *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Discussion

Our GO analysis reveals that robust translation initiation is a hallmark of ketamine's antidepressant effect, rather than a nonspecific effect of NMDAR antagonism. Pathways associated with translational regulation were upregulated following low‐dose ketamine, whereas they were downregulated at supra‐antidepressant doses. GSEA highlights roles for GPCR signaling, neuronal metabolism, vascularization, and structural plasticity in mPFC following antidepressant ketamine treatment. These findings support existing literature (Alva et al. 2006; Banasr et al. 2011; Duman and Li 2012; Fu 2014; Kang 2012; Lorrain et al. 2003; Nagele 2015; Romero 2011; Witkin et al. 2007) while providing new avenues for understanding ketamine's sustained antidepressant effects.

Among the pathways of interest, the GPCR gene set and specifically GPCR VPAC2 emerged due to its high fold change, pharmacological tractability, and an unexamined role in depression. Our findings demonstrate that VPAC2 agonism selectively activates SOM+ interneurons in the mPFC, and in vivo activation of VPAC2 produces an antidepressant‐like response. This selectivity is intriguing, particularly in light of recent cell‐type‐specific RNA‐seq data showing that Vipr2 mRNA is present in not only SOM+ but also PV+ and Reelin/HTR3A+ interneurons (Zeisel 2015). As VPAC2 is primarily a Gs‐coupled GPCR, it is possible that in PV interneurons, Gs signaling does not link to pathways that regulate cAMP or, alternatively, that cAMP activation in PV cells does not couple to intracellular pathways that influence firing probability.

Based on our acute brain slice electrophysiology results demonstrating that VPAC2 agonism specifically activated SOM+ interneurons, we hypothesized that treatment with a VPAC2 agonist, in vivo, would inhibit PYR neuron function. Instead, in vivo single‐cell calcium imaging revealed that VPAC2 agonism results in complex, multidimensional changes to prefrontal cortical function: treatment with the VPAC2 agonist BAY55 increased the proportion and magnitude of neurons that were both activated and inhibited, compared to vehicle. This complex response may reflect the recurrent nature of intact cortical microcircuits and the influence of afferent and efferent connections in this region. Rather than unilaterally inhibiting PYR neurons, VPAC2 activation modulates a broader range of prefrontal cortical activity, suggesting that ketamine's antidepressant effects may result from a nuanced balance of excitation and inhibition in specific cell types.

Bay55 was engineered through site‐directed mutagenesis with the aim of optimizing VPAC2 selectivity while minimizing VPAC1 and PAC1 activation. These modifications yielded a peptide with a sub‐nanomolar dissociation constant at VPAC2 (Kd = 0.65 nM) and greater than 100‐fold selectivity for VPAC2 over VPAC1 and PAC1 (Tsutsumi et al. 2002). Because Bay55 is structurally analogous to native VIP (a peptide known to cross the blood–brain barrier via passive diffusion) (Dogrukol‐Ak et al. 2003; Dufes et al. 2003), it is expected to similarly penetrate the CNS. Indeed, peripheral administration of Bay55 has been previously demonstrated to engage its target to elicit biochemical effects in the CNS (Hadwen et al. 2014). In our study, subjects were dosed with Bay55 at 0.2 mg/kg. Based on previous studies demonstrating that between 0.11% and 0.15% of VIP enters the brain after peripheral treatment (Dogrukol‐Ak et al. 2003; Dufes et al. 2003), this 0.2 mg/kg dose of Bay55 would result in a total brain concentration in the range of approximately 58–80 nM. Assuming a 5% unbound fraction, the free brain concentration would range from about 2.9 to 4 nM. At these free concentrations, Bay55 would achieve approximately 82%–86% occupancy at VPAC2, while occupancy at VPAC1 would be much lower (4%–6%) and binding to PAC1 negligible, suggesting that the observed behavioral and prefrontal cortical neuronal effects are attributable to the selective activation of VPAC2 on SOM interneurons. Although the precise free fraction in vivo requires confirmation, even conservative estimates suggest high VPAC2 receptor occupancy and specificity.

Despite evidence to the contrary, one prominent hypothesis for ketamine's antidepressant effects is that it preferentially antagonizes NMDARs on inhibitory interneurons, leading to disinhibition of PYR neurons. This disinhibition is thought to promote tonic firing, resulting in cortical hyperactivation, glutamate release, and synaptic plasticity similar to long‐term potentiation (LTP). According to this disinhibition hypothesis, LTP‐like plasticity supports synaptogenesis in mPFC PYR neurons, potentially yielding an antidepressant effect. Previous studies show that NMDAR antagonist MK801 induces disinhibition in the deep‐layer prelimbic mPFC (Homayoun and Moghaddam 2007) and elevates PFC glutamate levels as measured by microdialysis (Moghaddam et al. 1997), supporting this theory. However, GluN2B‐specific antagonists have been shown to produce antidepressant‐like effects without causing cortical disinhibition (Jiménez‐Sánchez et al. 2014), suggesting that disinhibition may be more correlative than causative for ketamine's antidepressant action. Consistent with this, our findings reveal that supra‐antidepressant doses, but not effective antidepressant doses, of ketamine stimulate cortical activation, as shown by increased translation of IEGs within 30 min of treatment. These results, along with others in the literature, suggest that ketamine's antidepressant effects do not rely on prefrontal disinhibition.

Although a role for VPAC2 in major depression has not, to our knowledge, been previously identified, there is precedence for a role in neuropsychiatric disease. Copy number variations in the gene coding for VPAC2, Vipr2, have been identified to confer a significant risk for schizophrenia (Vacic 2011), pointing to the importance of this gene for the maintenance of normal brain function. The association of Vipr2 copy number variations with schizophrenia suggests a broader role for VPAC2 signaling in maintaining cortical circuit stability and healthy cognitive function, further highlighting the potential relevance of VPAC2 in depression and related neuropsychiatric disorders. Our data support a model in which ketamine increases VPAC2 expression in SOM+ interneurons, thereby modifying their sensitivity to VIP/ PACAP and shaping future network dynamics. This delayed enhancement in signaling may represent one mechanism by which ketamine's molecular effects are translated into sustained changes in prefrontal circuit function.

This study demonstrates the utility of unbiased translational profiling of an established antidepressant to discover and validate novel therapeutic targets. By leveraging cell‐ or circuit‐specific molecular handles such as VPAC2, it may be possible to develop targeted antidepressant therapies that enhance efficacy while minimizing side effects. Fully realizing this therapeutic potential will require further exploration of VPAC2 function at cellular, circuit, and behavioral levels.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, all experiments were conducted in naïve mice, which allowed us to identify primary molecular and circuit‐level effects of ketamine without the confounding influence of chronic stress. However, it remains to be determined whether the same pathways, including VPAC2 upregulation, are engaged in models that more closely reflect the pathophysiology of depression. Future studies in chronic stress or corticosterone exposure paradigms will be essential to validate the translational relevance of these findings.

Second, while we demonstrate that VPAC2 activation is sufficient to produce antidepressant‐like behavioral effects, we did not test whether VPAC2 is necessary for ketamine's therapeutic action. Direct evidence linking VPAC2 to ketamine's mechanism of action will require pharmacological blockade or genetic loss‐of‐function approaches.

Third, we did not evaluate the effects of individual ketamine enantiomers. Given evidence that (R)‐ketamine may yield superior antidepressant efficacy with fewer side effects than (S)‐ketamine, it will be important to determine whether VPAC2 upregulation is preferentially driven by one enantiomer over the other.

Finally, our behavioral analysis was limited to the FST in naïve animals. Although the FST is widely used as a screen for antidepressant‐like activity, further studies are needed to assess the effects of VPAC2 agonism across a broader range of behavioral paradigms and in stress‐based models of depression.

Experimental Procedures

Animal Experimentation

All studies at Roche were performed with strict adherence to the Swiss federal ordinance on animal protection and welfare as well as according to the rules of the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC), and with the explicit approval of the local veterinary authorities. Mice were housed on a 12/12‐h light–dark cycle, at ∼20°C and ∼55% humidity. Regular rodent chow and tap water were available ad libitum. Mice were weaned at ∼P30 by gender. Juveniles were group‐housed to not more than three mice per cage. Male mice 2–3 months of age were used at the time of analysis for all physiology, behavior, and histology studies.

All studies at Inscopix were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of LifeSource Biomedical Services at the NASA Ames Research Center, an AAALAC‐accredited organization. Male mice 4–6 months of age were used for all in vivo imaging studies. Mice were maintained in a reversed 12/12‐h light/dark cycle with food and water ad libitum. Each mouse was co‐housed with a conspecific and was semi‐separated from the conspecific by a porous divider in the cage throughout the study, in order to maintain the integrity of the GRIN lens implant. All experiments were performed during the dark cycle of the animals.

Mice

GluN2B‐Floxed Mice

Mice with exon 5 of the GRIN2B gene flanked by loxP sites were used for all experiments at Roche to maintain the same background as experiments from previous studies (Miller et al. 2014; Brigman et al. 2010). At no point in this study were GluN2B‐floxed mice exposed to Cre, and therefore, these mice are wild‐type at the protein level.

Ai14 Mice

The Ai14 mouse (Rosa‐CAG‐LSL‐tdTomato‐WPRE: ΔNeo) has a LoxP‐flanked STOP cassette preventing transcription of downstream tdTomato cDNA incorporated into the Rosa locus (Madisen et al. 2010). Expression of Cre‐recombinase results in Cre‐LoxP recombination and deletion of the STOP cassette, allowing for expression of tdTomato in cells of interest. Ai14 mice were crossed with SOM‐cre and PV‐cre mice for cell identification.

Somatostatin Cre Mice

Sst‐IRES‐Cre (Jax stock: 013044) mice have a SOM‐IRES‐Cre knock‐in allele with an internal ribosome entry site and Cre recombinase in the 3ʹ UTR of the somatostatin locus. When SOM‐IRES‐Cre mice are bred with mice containing loxP‐flanked sequences, Cre‐mediated recombination will result in the deletion of the floxed sequences in the SOM‐expressing cells in the offspring.

Parvalbumin Cre Mice

PVcre (Jax stock: 008069) mice have an IRES‐cre‐pA knock‐in allele with an internal ribosome entry site and Cre recombinase in the 3ʹ UTR of exon 5 of the parvalbumin locus. When PV Cre mice are bred with mice containing loxP‐flanked sequences, Cre‐mediated recombination will result in the deletion of the floxed sequences in the PV‐expressing cells in the offspring.

C57BL/6 Mice

C57BL/6 (Jax Stock: 000664) mice were used for all in vivo imaging (Inscopix) studies.

Library Preparation and Sequencing

Preparation of ribosome profiling and RNA sequencing libraries using the TruSeq Ribo Profile kit (Illumina, #RPHMR12126) was performed as detailed in the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, frozen brain tissue samples were ground with a pestle on dry ice and thawed in the presence of lysis buffer containing 1% TritonX‐100, 0.1% NP40, 1 mM DTT, DNase (0.01 U/µL), and 100 µg/mL cycloheximide. Three quarters of the debris‐cleared lysate was used for ribosome footprinting by digestion with RNase for 45 min at room temperature. The remaining quarter of the sample was used for RNAseq library prep workflow, followed by isolation of the ribosome‐protected mRNA fragments by phenol–chloroform extraction.

For ribosomal RNA depletion, the RiboZero magnetic Gold kit (Illumina) was used. The following steps—PAGE purification, end repair, adapter ligation, reverse transcription, and circularization—were performed as described in the manufacturer's protocol, and final libraries were purified once more on a PAGE gel to remove residual primers.

Quality of amplified libraries was assessed by capillary electrophoresis with a high‐sensitivity DNA chip on a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and quantified by quantitative PCR with a sequencing library quantification kit (KAPA Biosystems) on a Roche Light Cycler 480. Multiplexed libraries, with 1% PhiX control spiked in, were sequenced on a HiSeq2500 instrument for 50 cycles using version 4 chemistry reagents (Illumina).

Bioinformatic Analysis

Data processing and bioinformatics analysis were performed as previously described (Grabole et al. 2016). In short, linker tags were removed from RNA sequencing and ribosome profiling reads by the FASTX Toolkit; all reads that mapped to rRNAs, tRNAs, or mitochondrial rRNAs were removed; and the remaining reads were mapped to RefSeq (v38) by TopHat. Finally, all read counts that mapped uniquely to genes were extracted for expression analysis. We applied the edgeR algorithm for differential gene expression analysis and identified genes with changes larger than 150% or less than 70% of saline control levels, with a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p‐value less than 0.01, as significantly changed. We applied the camera algorithm for GSEA with gene sets collected in the Roche internal database RONET. GO enrichment analysis was performed using Fisher's exact test.

Electrophysiology

Slice Preparation

As previously described (Miller et al. 2014; Miller et al. 2017), for acute slice recordings, adult 2‐ to 3‐month‐old mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and decapitated. The brain was rapidly removed and placed into ice‐cold‐modified NMDG solution (composition in mM: 110 NMDG, 110 HCl, 3 KCl, 10 MgCl2 6H2O, 1.1 NaH2PO4·H2O, 0.5 CaCl2 dihydrate, 25 glucose, 3 pyruvic acid, 10 ascorbic acid, 25 NaHCO3, 305–310 mOsm). A vibratome (Leica VT1200S) was used to generate 350‐µm PFC coronal sections containing the mPFC. Slices were allowed to recover in oxygenated (95% oxygen, 5% CO2) NMDG for 30 min at 34°C, then transferred to a holding chamber where they were incubated in bicarbonate‐buffered ACSF (composition in mM: 124 NaCl, 4 KCl, 26 NaHCO3, 1.26 NaH2PO4, 3 MgSO4, 2 CaCl2) at room temperature for at least another 30 min before whole‐cell recordings. ACSF solutions were bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2 at all times to maintain consistent oxygenation and pH.

Whole‐Cell Patch‐Clamp Recordings

For current clamp recordings, borosilicate glass pipettes were filled with a potassium‐gluconate intracellular solution (120 K Gluconate, 10 Hepes, 10 D‐Sorbitol, 1 MgCl2 6H2O, 1 CaCl2, 1 EGTA, 5 phosphocreatine disodium salt hydrate, 2 ATP disodium salt hydrate, 0.3 GTP sodium salt, pH adjusted to 7.3, 290 mOsm). Whole‐cell patch‐clamp recordings were acquired (MultiClamp 700B, Axon Instruments), digitized at 20 kHz (Digidata 1550, Axon Instruments), filtered at 2 kHz, and recorded using pClamp software. Clampfit (Axon Instruments), MiniAnalysis (Synaptosoft), and custom‐written macros in IgorPRO (Wavemetrics) were used to analyze raw data, and GraphPad Prism was used to perform statistical analyses, as noted in the text. Pipette resistances ranged from 2 to 5 MΩ. Series access resistance ranged from 4 to 20 MΩ and was monitored for consistency. Cells were discarded if leak current rose above 200 pA, access resistance was greater than 20 MΩ, or if it changed by more than 15% during recording. Layer III neurons confined to prelimbic and infralimbic mPFC were targeted for recording, observable with epifluorescent imaging.

At baseline for SOM and PYR neurons, APs were evoked by small current injections, sufficient to elicit four to six APs. For PV neurons, a baseline of four to six APs was unattainable, so the minimum stimulus necessary to evoke tonic firing was used. After a minimum of 5 min of baseline recording, Bay55 was perfused into the bath for 5 min, and the recordings continued unadjusted for the entirety of the recording (up to 1 h). Graphs are displayed as running averages of three sequential sweeps.

Mouse Behavior

Open‐Field Test

The open‐field test was conducted using the Accuscan open‐field system. Mice were introduced into the arena at a fixed corner of an acrylic (42 × 42 × 30.5 cm) field arena and allowed to explore the arena freely. The arena was cleaned in between subjects with 70% ethanol.

FST

The FST was carried out as previously described (El Yacoubi 2003; Porsolt et al. 1977). Briefly, mice were introduced to a cylinder filled with room‐temperature water. The cylinder was 15 cm in diameter and 20 cm in height, and it was filled to 15 cm (sufficient to prevent mice from using tails to support themselves) with room‐temperature water (23°C–26°C). Two to four mice were tested simultaneously with a screen to separate the cylinders. Videos were recorded from a top‐mounted camera for 6 min. After the testing, mice were rubbed dry and placed under an infrared lamp for about 20 min. Immobility in the last 4 min of the 6‐min test session was scored blind to genotype and treatment. The cylinders were cleaned in between subjects with 70% ethanol. For VPAC2 agonist behavioral experiments, mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of Bay55 (0.2 mg/kg) for 4 days. The FST was conducted on day 5 to assess behavioral effects following subchronic VPAC2 activation.

In Vivo Calcium Imaging

Surgeries

We anesthetized 10‐ to 15‐week‐old C57BL/6 male mice (Jackson Laboratory, Inc.) with isoflurane (2% in O2) and prepared each animal for virus injection by making a midline incision in the scalp to expose the skull, followed by a craniotomy positioned over PFC. AAV1.CaMK2.GCaMP6f (Inscopix Ready‐to‐Image Virus mouse Neocortex; titer: 3.35 vG/mL) was injected unilaterally in the right hemisphere (AP 1.9 mm; ML 0.4 mm relative to Bregma). A total of 600 nL of virus was injected at depths of 1.75 and 1.65 mm below the cortical surface (300 nL injected at each depth) at a rate of 50 nL/min with a 33‐gauge beveled needle. One week following virus injection, each mouse was again anesthetized and implanted with a 1‐mm‐diameter, ∼4‐mm‐length Proview integrated lens probe (Inscopix) at the following brain coordinates: AP 1.9 mm, ML 0.40 mm relative to Bregma, and DV 1.62 mm below the cortical surface. Cortical tissue was aspirated above the site prior to implantation. Mice were ready for the first imaging sessions 3 weeks following the lens implantation procedure.

Data Collection

Prior to imaging, mice were handled and habituated to wear a miniature microscope (Inscopix nVista 3.0 miniscope; dimensions: 8.8 mm × 15 mm × 22 mm, weight 2.2 g) for 20 min per day for a duration of 4 days. On the last 2 days of habituation, mice were placed in the open‐field chamber for 20 min per day. All mice received two IP doses of saline to habituate them to the route of administration in advance of the study. The miniscope was mounted to the baseplate for imaging and behavioral experiments. On experimental days, mice were placed for 5 min in the OFC for habituation before recording. Treatments were randomized and counterbalanced across 2 days of experimentation. Prior to vehicle or Bay55 administration, a 10‐min baseline session was recorded. Vehicle or Bay55 was then administered IP; animals were placed in an OFC and were immediately recorded in a 10‐min posttreatment session.

Behavioral videos were collected at a sampling rate of 30 Hz with the Inscopix nVision behavioral camera focused bottom‐up on a transparent‐bottomed, 42‐cm‐long square open‐field apparatus. Behavioral videos were temporally synchronized with miniscope recordings. The experimental room remained dark except for the IR lighting surrounding the open field.

Data Analysis

4.7.3.1 Average Speed

Animal Center of Mass (COM) in the arena was computed by averaging coordinates of seven body parts obtained from processing the behavioral videos through an internally developed behavior‐tracking model based on DeepLabCut (Mathis et al. 2018; Nath et al. 2019). Body part estimates were smoothed using a 0.5‐s‐wide median filter. Average speed (cm/s) was computed as the sum of animal COM changes across consecutive frames (distance traveled) divided by the number of frames and multiplied by the sampling rate.

All neural processing was performed on concatenated baseline and posttreatment recordings from each session. Raw calcium imaging movies were motion‐corrected using NoRMCorre (Pnevmatikakis and Giovannucci 2017; reference frame computed from frames of the first recording; with the following parameters: gSig_filt = (6, 6); max_shifts = (12, 12); pw_rigid = False), and cells were extracted using CaImAn CNMF‐E (Zhou et al. 2018; with the following parameters: p = 1; gSig = (3, 3); gSiz = (10, 10); merge_thr = 0.7; min_corr = 0.8; min_pnr = 10). Spike events were then deconvoluted from the fluorescence traces of extracted cells using the OASIS algorithm (Zhou et al. 2018), and spike events below a threshold of 3 standard deviations of the neural noise were rejected.

CNMF‐E‐extracted cells were curated by a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier, developed using a training dataset based on 17 spatial and functional features and made of high‐quality, manually labeled traces belonging to both cells and non‐cells. CNMF‐E‐extracted cells classified as cells by the SVM were included in all following analyses. Motion‐corrected movies and extracted cells were quality‐controlled through visual and quantitative validation.

4.7.3.2 Average Spike Rate

For each recording, we divided the number of deconvolved spike events by the number of time samples (movie frames) and multiplied by the sampling rate to obtain the average spike rate, in Hz.

4.7.3.3 Treatment Modulation Index (TMI)

We quantified the magnitude and statistical significance of treatment‐induced spike rate modulation for each cell by comparing the average spike rate from the baseline (mu_baseline) and treatment (mu_treatment) recordings. Specifically, we defined the TMI as (mu_treatment − mu_baseline) / (mu_treatment + mu_baseline). TMI is bounded between [−1 1], with −1 for a fully treatment‐silenced cell (only has spikes during baseline) and 1 for a fully treatment‐awakened cell (only has spikes during posttreatment). Negative TMIs indicate cells being down‐modulated by the treatment, while positive TMIs indicate up‐modulation. To assess the significance of the TMI values, for each cell, we performed a permutation test by creating a null distribution of 1000 TMI values computed on stationary bootstrapped spike trains (block resampling spike trains with replacement) and assessing whether the observed TMI was outside [2.5, 97.5] percentiles of the null distribution. In TMI figures, cells with positive and significant observed TMI were plotted as “Activated”; cells with negative and significant observed TMI were plotted as “Inhibited.” The magnitude of treatment modulation for significantly up‐ or down‐modulated cells was quantified as “Activation Strength” and “Inhibition Strength,” respectively.

4.7.3.4 Cell–Cell Pairwise Correlations

For each recording, we quantified the magnitude and statistical significance of all cell–cell pairwise correlations to assess the population‐level correlation, an indicator of neuronal network function. We computed the correlation matrix from the raw CNMF‐E traces, resulting in an n × n matrix (with n being the number of cells) when Rij represents the Pearson's r correlation coefficient between cells i and j (for i and j between 1 and n). We applied the Fisher transformation (inverse hyperbolic tangent, or arctanh) to the matrix, in order to unbound Pearson's r from [−1, 1] to [−inf, inf] and enable further statistical analysis. To assess the significance of the Fisher‐transformed Pearson's r values, we standardized (Z‐scored) the matrix using the mean and standard deviation of a null distribution obtained by computing Fisher‐transformed correlation matrices from 1000 sets of circularly permuted cell traces (each cell being independently shifted, effectively breaking any temporal relationship between cells while conserving each cell's time structure). We finally averaged the magnitude (absolute value) of the Z‐valued correlation coefficients to obtain a metric capturing the population‐wide strength of overall correlation (both anticorrelated and correlated).

4.7.3.5 Correlation Matrix Structure

We assessed potential changes in the correlational structure across consecutive recordings by computing the cell–cell pairwise correlation matrix similarity. For each pair of baseline and posttreatment recordings (sharing the same set of accepted cells, having been processed by CaImAn CNMF‐E as concatenated recordings), we computed the Pearson's r correlation coefficient between their respective cell–cell pairwise correlation matrices. Values closer to 1 showed a preserved correlational structure, that is, the same groups of cells across recordings exhibiting coordinated activity, while values closer to 0 showed a disrupted correlational structure, independent of the degree of coordinated activity in the cell population.

Statistics

Linear Mixed‐Effects Model

For each dependent variable (e.g., average spike rate), we fitted a linear mixed‐effects model with three fixed effects (condition [baseline or posttreatment], treatment [vehicle or Bay55], and treatment × condition interaction) and one random intercept (animal, to account for interindividual variability). We reported the p‐values for each significant effect by level of significance, starting at the trend level: ∼ <0.1, * <0.05, ** <0.01, and *** <0.001.
