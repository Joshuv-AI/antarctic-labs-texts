Efficacy and safety of 2–4 mg brexpiprazole in the management of schizophrenia: A systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Sher Bano, Aashan Ahmad, Abdul Haseeb Mohammed, Ismailova Rushana Nurbekovna, Saja Saad, Noor Fatima, Muhammad Bilal Akbar, Zainab Salahuddin, Sadikun Nabi, Mohammad Mustafa Ababneh, Laiba Ajmal, Sidra Hariss, Hiba Manaf, Farah Saleh, Raouf Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrexpiprazole 2–4 mg daily is a novel dopamine D2 partial agonist approved for schizophrenia and improves effectiveness and tolerance by balancing D2, 5‐HT1A, and 5‐HT2A effects. This review assesses current evidence on its efficacy and safety within this dose range. PubMed, ScienceDirect, and The Cochrane Library were searched for trials published between 2011 and 2025 using specific keywords. Eligibility criteria included schizophrenia patients diagnosed using DSM‐IV, DSM‐IV‐TR, DSM‐5, or ICD‐10, with intervention being 2–4 mg of brexpiprazole. Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) score and Clinical Global Impression ‐ Severity scale (CGI‐S) were the primary efficacy endpoints, and treatment‐related adverse events were used to assess efficacy. Subgroup analysis was planned between the two dosage groups. The quality of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was evaluated using the ROB2 tool, and the protocol registration was completed in PROSPERO. Among 464 retrieved articles, five trials with 2182 participants were found to be eligible. Brexpiprazole significantly improved total PANSS scores (mean difference [MD] −5.76; 95% confidence interval [CI] −7.69 to −3.83; p < 0.00001), positive subscale scores, negative subscale scores, and CGI‐S scores (MD −1.35; 95% CI −1.87 to −0.84; p < 0.00001). The treatment‐emergent adverse events were similar to placebo, and the risk of discontinuation was less with brexpiprazole (risk ratio 0.58; 95% CI 0.430.77; p = 0.0002). The most usual extrapyramidal symptom was akathisia. Brexpiprazole 2–4 mg showed significant improvements in PANSS and CGI‐S scores, with a lower risk of treatment discontinuation. These findings support its efficacy and tolerability in managing schizophrenia.Keywords: brexpiprazole, CGI‐S, PANSS, randomized controlled trials, schizophrenia

INTRODUCTION

Schizophrenia is a mental disorder characterized by various psychotic episodes that are mainly marked by episodes of delusions and hallucinations, along with periods of complete social withdrawal, apathy, anhedonia, and impairment of the neurocognitive functional status of the patient. 1 When talking about the incidence, globally, schizophrenia affects approximately 0.75% of the world population, accounting for a major disease burden and millions of dollars of financial burden each year. 2 It is considered to be one of the most disabling disorders and is ranked 12th on the list alongside stroke and myocardial infarction. The estimated burden of the disease can be known by the fact that from the year 1990 to 2019, the prevalence of the disease increased by over 65%, 37%, and 65%, respectively, further signifying its morbidity burden. 3

Considering the massiveness of the public health strain caused by schizophrenia, new research is being directed to come up with novel treatment plans for the disorder. The first line treatment for schizophrenia is antipsychotics. First‐generation antipsychotics have been known to improve significantly the depressive symptoms of schizophrenia but have major side effects including tardive dyskinesia and prolongation of the QT interval resulting in heart blocks. 4 As these side effects greatly impacted the patient's quality of life, newer generation antipsychotics were later introduced. These include clozapine, olanzapine, and risperidone, to name a few. Although these drugs have better tolerability and reduce the side effects, particularly concerning the motor regions, they bring forth other abnormalities such as metabolic disturbances, excessive sedation, and excessive weight gain. Among major trials conducted, olanzapine and risperidone have been reported to excessively increase the patient's weight and body mass index (BMI) along with deranging the lipid profile. 5 Thus, the need for novel agents that offer both efficacy with a good safety profile remains a clinical dilemma in psychiatry that still needs to be addressed properly.

Brexpiprazole, a serotonin–dopamine activity enhancer and quinol derivative, is a novel atypical antipsychotic newly introduced into the market with partial agonist activity at dopamine receptors, particularly the D2 ones, greatly reducing extrapyramidal symptoms. Brexpiprazole's affinity for D2, 5‐HT1A, and 5‐HT2A receptors is balanced. While 5‐HT2A antagonism adds to antipsychotic effectiveness and may ameliorate extrapyramidal symptoms, offering a more acceptable treatment profile in schizophrenia, 5‐HT1A partial agonism may lessen anxiety and depressed symptoms and perhaps promote cognitive performance. 6 Even though brexpiprazole has the same functional structure as aripiprazole, it has a major effect on the D2 receptors as explained previously making it an ideal candidate for lowering akinetic effects of previous antipsychotic medications. Clinical trials conducted on the effectiveness of this drug have shown that brexpiprazole at doses from 2 to 4 mg/day is effective in reducing symptoms of acute schizophrenia while also being well tolerated by a wide variety of patients. 7 Reduction in akathisia was noticed to be one of the most clinically significant effects of brexpiprazole as compared to other antipsychotic agents. When talking about the pharmacokinetic properties, which are essential to reveal the mechanism and duration of action of the drug, it was revealed that the drug reaches maximum concentration after 4 h and has a half‐life of 91 h. 8

It must still be kept in view that the effectiveness of certain approaches differs significantly among antipsychotics depending upon the patient's condition and the overall psychological as well as racial and ethnic factors. 9 While a few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted on the subject matter have explored the efficacy of brexpiprazole in acute schizophrenia, variability in study design, patient populations, and outcomes reveals some complexities that must be adequately addressed. A meta‐analysis comparing the dosages of drugs for maximal alleviation of symptoms suggested that the negative symptoms showed significant reduction on doses of 2 mg per day, while the positive symptoms showed significant improvement on doses of 4 mg per day. 10

The conclusions presented in the summary above strongly support the need for further analytical evidence obtained through a methodical, systematic review and meta‐analysis of the efficacy of 2–4 mg/day of brexpiprazole in alleviating the symptoms of acute schizophrenia. The main goal of this systematic review and meta‐analysis was to evaluate and put into context the efficacy of brexpiprazole in the range of 2–4 mg/day in acute schizophrenia patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted according to guidelines set out by PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses), and the protocol for this review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251126121). 11

Search strategy

We searched three databases, namely Science Direct, The Cochrane Library, and PubMed, to retrieve relevant articles. The search was restricted to trials published between 2011 and 2025. The keywords used to build the search strategy were the same for all databases. For PubMed and Science Direct, the search looked like “((brexpiprazole) OR (OPC‐34712)) AND (schizophrenia),” while for Cochrane, the search looked like “(Brexpiprazole):ti,ab,kw OR (OPC‐34712):ti,ab,kw AND (Schizophrenia):ti,ab,kw.” The articles found were exported to EndNote for further screening and selection.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility was defined using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Participants above 13 years of age. (2) Participants with schizophrenia meeting the criteria as mentioned in Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)‐IV, DSM‐IV‐TR, DSM‐5, or ICD‐10. (3) Study design is RCT and published in English. (4) Studies reporting on at least one of the selected outcomes, that is, Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) score, Clinical Global Impression ‐ Severity scale (CGI‐S) score, and adverse events. (5) Studies using 2–4 mg brexpiprazole and having a comparator.

However, we excluded studies having schizophrenia patients not diagnosed using DSM‐IV, DSM‐IV‐TR, DSM‐5, or ICD‐10 and studies not using 2–4 mg brexpiprazole. Studies not published in English and study designs other than RCTs, such as cohorts, cross‐sectional studies, case reports, letters, correspondences, reviews, and conference abstracts were also excluded. Studies not reporting on the prespecified outcomes were also excluded along with studies not having a comparator.

Our primary outcomes included PANSS score and CGI‐S score. The secondary outcomes included any treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs), discontinuation of treatment, akathisia, sedation, and weight gain.

Screening, selection, and data extraction

Following the retrieval of articles, they underwent different stages of screening and selection. Initially, duplicates were searched and removed. The remaining studies were screened based on their titles and abstracts, and only relevant articles were included; their full texts were searched. Articles with available full text were screened against our selection criteria, and eligible articles were included in our analysis. This whole process was carried out independently by two reviewers, and any conflicts were resolved with the help of a third reviewer.

The included articles underwent data extraction in an Excel sheet where author id, location, study design, sample size, sample age, intervention and comparator details, male‐to‐female ratio, and outcomes analyzed were collected. Similarly, continuous and dichotomous data for our specified outcomes were also collected.

Quality assessment and data synthesis

Quality of the included RCTs was assessed using the ROB2 tool by Cochrane. 12 This tool consists of five domains and an overall bias domain. These areas assess bias caused by randomization, variation from the intervention, outcome measurement, missing outcome data, and result reporting. An overall high risk of bias is indicated by a high risk in one domain or some concerns in more than one domain. A study was assigned an overall bias grade of some concerns if one domain exhibited some problems, while the remaining domains had minimal risk. A study was classified as having a low risk of bias if all domains were low risk.

The statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4. Risk ratios were calculated for binary (dichotomous) outcomes, and mean differences were estimated for continuous outcomes using a random‐effects Mantel–Haenszel method. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. I², where a value below 25% is considered low, 26%–50% is considered mild, 51%–75% is considered moderate, and >75% is considered high. Forest plots were used to present the results of our analysis, and subgroup analyses were also conducted by dosage of brexpiprazole. A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was also carried out to enhance the robustness of our findings. Funnel plot or Egger's test to assess publication bias could not be performed due to the limited studies available. Additionally, subgroup analysis based on age, as there is a mixed population in our analysis, could not be conducted as well due to limited data. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The search for articles on databases like PubMed, The Cochrane Library, and Science Direct yielded 464 articles. Search for duplicates removed 23 articles, and 441 articles underwent title and abstract screening leaving 42 articles behind. The full text of all 42 articles was searched, and only the full text of 31 articles could be retrieved. These remaining articles underwent full text screening against our specified selection criteria, and only five articles 7 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were included, as shown in Figure 1.

Characteristics of studies

We included five trials that fulfilled our criteria. They included 2182 participants, but our analysis included 1755 (80.43%), of whom 1134 (64.6%) were in the 2–4 mg brexpiprazole group and 621 (35.4%) in the placebo group, with details summarized in Table 1. Males were more in number compared to females, and the age of the participants ranged from 13 to 56 years. Four of the studies used DSM‐IV‐TR as a standard for schizophrenia diagnosis, while one study used DSM‐5. Baseline PANSS and CGI‐S were calculated, and the changes from baseline were analyzed. Benzodiazepines were the most frequently reported class of concomitant medications in the brexpiprazole group, that is, 689/1134 (60.8%).

Quality of RCTs

Of all included trials, only two demonstrated a low risk of bias, while two studies, Correll et al., 2015, and Ishigooka et al., 2018, showed some concerns due to deviations from the intended intervention. 7 , 13 The study by Citrome et al., 2016, was considered high risk due to some concerns in Domains 2 and 3 because the study was open‐label, so participants were aware of the intervention used leading to D2 some concerns, and similarly, the high dropout rate, which was not assessed for cause, led to some concerns in D3, 16 as shown in Figure 2.

Risk of bias assessment of included randomized controlled trials.

Effect on PANSS score

All five included trials reported the effect of 2–4 mg brexipiprazole on PANSS scores. The analysis showed that the mean PANSS improved significantly from baseline with brexipiprazole compared to placebo (mean difference [MD] −5.76; 95% confidence interval [CI] [−7.69, −3.83]; p < 0.00001) with zero statistical heterogeneity. For individual subscales of PANSS, positive subscale score improved significantly from baseline with the use of brexipiprazole (MD −1.30; 95% CI [−2.17, −0.43]; p = 0.003), but mild statistical heterogeneity was observed. Similarly, brexipiprazole also improved negative subscale scores significantly, too, with no heterogeneity (MD −1.35; 95% CI [−1.87, −0.84]; p < 0.00001). The results of the total PANSS and its subscales are presented using a forest plot in Figure 3.

Effect of brexpiprazole on Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) scores.

Effect on CGI‐S

The CGI‐S was given in all of the 5 trials. The mean difference between brexipiprazole and placebo showed an improvement in baseline CGI‐S scores with brexipiprazole, and the results were significant (MD −0.34; 95% CI [−0.54, −0.14]; p = 0.0007), but moderate statistical heterogeneity was observed, that is, 63%. A forest plot was used to represent the results, as shown in Figure 4.

Effect of brexpiprazole on Clinical Global Impression ‐ Severity scale (CGI‐S) scores.

Adverse events

The adverse event profile used in our analysis consisted of any TEAE and discontinuation of treatment. For any TEAE, almost no difference was observed between brexipiprazole and placebo, and the results were not significant with low heterogeneity of 23% (risk ratio [RR] 0.95; 95% CI [0.87, 1.05]; p = 0.31).

The risk of discontinuation of treatment was low in the experimental group compared to placebo with 42% lower risk of discontinuing the therapy, and the results were significant with no statistical heterogeneity (RR 0.58; 95% CI [0.43, 0.77]; p = 0.0002). The forest plot showing the results is given in Figure 5.

Forest plot for adverse events and treatment discontinuation.

A total of 69 extrapyramidal symptoms‐related adverse events were observed in the brexpiprazole group. Of 69 events, akathisia was the most common extrapyramidal symptom observed, that is, 56/69 (81.1%) with brexpiprazole. The pooled analysis showed that the risk of akathisia was reduced with brexpiprazole compared to placebo (RR 0.89; 95% CI [0.50, 1.58]; p = 0.70) with mild heterogeneity. Similarly, two studies reported on weight gain, and four studies reported on sedation. Risk of sedation was reduced with brexpiprazole compared to placebo (RR 0.76; 95% CI [0.27, 2.16]; p = 0.67) with no heterogeneity, and risk of sedation was increased with brexpiprazole compared to placebo with mild heterogeneity (RR 1.68; 95% CI [0.43, 6.55]; p = 0.45). None of these results were significant as shown in Figure 6.

Risk of extrapyramidal symptoms, sedation, and weight gain with brexpiprazole compared to placebo.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed according to the dosage of brexpiprazole. Only three studies reported results of both dosages separately. Improvement in PANSS score was compared between 2 and 4 mg of brexipiprazole. The results showed that no significant difference was observed in the mean change of PANSS in both groups, thus the dose did not affect the results (MD −0.06; 95% CI [−3.96, 3.84]; p = 0.97, see Figure 7).

Subgroup analysis of Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) score changes by brexpiprazole dose (2 mg vs. 4 mg).

Sensitivity analysis

A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was performed to identify sources of heterogeneity and assess the robustness of the results. The change from baseline in total PANSS was not significantly negative upon sensitivity analysis. However, by removing the study Ishigooka J et al., 2018, the results of the positive subscale improved significantly and heterogeneity decreased to zero (MD −1.62; 95% CI [−2.34, −0.90]; p < 0.0001). 13

The results for CGI‐S did not change much either; however, upon removal of Correll CU et al., 2015, the heterogeneity improved from moderate to mild, that is, 43%, and the mean difference was still in favor of brexipiprazole (MD −0.27; 95% CI [−0.46, −0.08]; p = 0.005). 7 For any TEAEs, the results significantly became in favor of brexipiprazole after removal of Kane JM et al., 2015, resulting in 10% lower risk of any TEAE compared to placebo with no heterogeneity (RR 0.90; 95% CI [0.82, 0.99]; p = 0.03). 15

DISCUSSION

The results of this meta‐analysis provide a much‐needed insight on the efficacy and safety of 2–4 mg brexpiprazole in schizophrenia. The mean PANSS score showed a significant improvement in the brexpiprazole group, with scores in both subscales improving. Brexipiprazole had a significant positive effect on CGI‐S compared to placebo, with a 42% lower risk of treatment discontinuation. However, there was no difference in adverse events associated with brexpiprazole and placebo, although, after removal of Kane JM et al.'s study in the sensitivity analysis, the results were in favor of brexpiprazole with 10% lower risk of treatment‐related adverse events. 15

First‐generation antipsychotics are known to cause severe motor adverse effects. Second‐generation antipsychotics such as brexpiprazole not only reduce the positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia but also lack side effects on the motor system. However, a side effect unique to these antipsychotics is cognitive impairment. 17 Cognitive function in schizophrenia studies is often assessed by the cognitive component of the PANSS. 18 Our analysis revealed a significant improvement in PANSS and CGS‐S scores from baseline in brexpiprazole patients compared with placebo, an outcome reinforced by previous meta‐analyses. 19 , 20

Compared to aripiprazole, brexpiprazole has higher affinity for 5‐HT1A (as a partial agonist) and 5‐HT2A receptors (as an antagonist), which is similar to its affinity at D2 receptors. This binding at 5HT2A has clinical benefits such as contribution to antipsychotic activity, while activity at 5HT1A has shown to reduce anxiety, depression, and improve cognitive function. This binding activity and hence improvement in symptoms, possibly translates to a higher improvement in PANSS score in the brexpiprazole group than placebo and a much better safety profile and favorable side effect profile. 7 , 21 Treatment‐related adverse events did not differ significantly between the brexpiprazole and placebo groups in our analysis. However, when Kane JM et al.'s 15 study was removed in the sensitivity analysis, the brexpiprazole group had 10% lower risk.

A similar outcome regarding adverse events was observed in Zhao et al.'s study; however, there was an increase in body weight in brexpiprazole patients, which was attributed to higher blood glucose levels and a change in lipid fractions. 22 A post hoc analysis of 5 placebo‐controlled trials by Meade et al. had the same incidence of TEAEs in both groups. Brexipiprazole had no indication of sedating side effects, as seen with olanzapine, quetiapine XR, and risperidone, nor activating side effects, as seen with lurasidone, cariprazine, and risperidone. 23 Switching to brexpiprazole is beneficial for those who do not respond well to aripiprazole or bupropion. 24 Our study revealed a 42% lower risk of therapy discontinuation among brexpiprazole patients. Kishi et al.'s analysis also revealed higher discontinuation rates with placebo, an outcome mainly owed to adverse events and schizophrenia. 25

This meta‐analysis had several limitations. First, the analysis was conducted on only five RCTs with a limited number of participants (2182). Second, only participants with an acute exacerbation of schizophrenia were included. Limited data prohibited subgroup analysis by age. As this study had both adults and adolescents, the results should be interpreted with caution. Thirdly, the short treatment duration (6 weeks) of brexpiprazole in these trials requires that the results be interpreted accordingly, as it does not ensure sustained efficacy and relapse prevention. Similarly, the full text of 11 studies could not be retrieved, which could introduce bias due to missing relevant studies. Lastly, due to participants being of different geographical areas, trials may differ in baseline characteristics and hence placebo response.

CONCLUSION

Brexpiprazole 2–4 mg significantly improved PANSS and CGI‐S scores compared to placebo.

The probability of treatment termination was consistently reduced with brexpiprazole, implying improved adherence. Overall, brexpiprazole was effective in treating schizophrenia's basic symptoms. Its safety profile was satisfactory supporting brexpiprazole as a viable treatment choice for schizophrenia patients.

This meta‐analysis provides a pathway for researches to assess the optimal dose of brexpiprazole further and conduct long‐term follow‐up trials to better evaluate its sustained efficacy, relapse prevention, and treatment‐related adverse effects. It is the need of the hour to explore whether baseline severity, duration of illness, or other comorbidities affect brexpiprazole's efficacy and safety.
