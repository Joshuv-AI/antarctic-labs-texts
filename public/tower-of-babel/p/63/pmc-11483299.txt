Autologous ADSCs with exogenous NPY promotes fracture healing in ovariectomized rats
Authors: Hong Liu, Jiaoyang Wu, Huixuan Wu, Ting Wang, Houde Zhou, Min Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the role of autologous adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) with exogenous neuropeptide Y (NPY) on osteoporotic fracture healing.MethodsWe established a fracture healing model in ovariectomized rats of osteoporosis. Autologous ADSCs, isolated from rats' subcutaneous adipose tissue, with exogenous NPY were transplanted into the fracture site four times a week. The fracture healing was observiked with X-ray diagnostic techniques, and the rats’ tibias were taken out for section-staining and micro-CT scanning 3 and 6 weeks after fracture. Next, we explored the underlying mechanism by which the transplantation contributed to fracture healing.ResultsCo-culture and co-transplantation of ADSCs with NPY could stimulate the osteoporotic fracture healing. NPY may promote the osteogenic differentiation of ADSCs through Y1 receptor.ConclusionThis result provides a platform for the development of novel therapies for bone regeneration with endogenous ADSCs.

Introduction

In recent years, the view of bone remodeling as primarily controlled by endocrine, paracrine and mechanical processes has expanded to incorporate the importance of central and peripheral neuronal signals [1]. Neuropeptide Y (NPY) is an important signaling pathway that is widely expressed in the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS) [1]. NPY regulates appetite and energy balance in the central nervous system [2] and plays a protective role against chronic bone loss-induced stress [3]. It has been reported that NPY is expressed in several cell types, including osteoblasts and adipocytes [4], as well as being important in regulating bone mass throughout the body [1,5].

As compared to other sources, adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) are one of the most promising cells for regenerative medicine, due to they possess a higher differentiation potential and are easily propagated [6]. As mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), ADSCs are capable of maintaining their undifferentiated state, controlled by the stemness related genes, as well as differentiating [6]. ADSCs can differentiate into osteogenic cells given the appropriate conditions [7]. This process is regulated by numerous osteogenesis factors including runt-related transcription factor 2 (runx2), osterix, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), osteocalcin (OC), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) [7]. As the master transcriptional factor of osteogenesis, runx2 plays an essential role in osteogenic differentiation and normal skeletal development [7].

Patients with osteoporosis are more likely to suffer from fractures, which can cause significant morbidity and mortality. Moreover, osteoporotic fractures are difficult to treat because of their decreased bone strength and suboptimal healing capacity, leading to both fixation failures and non-unions [8]. By combining synthetic bone graft substitutes with bioactive molecules, such as growth factors, peptides, and small molecules, bone precursor cells can be delivered more efficiently, resulting in better bone formation and metabolism [8]. It has been demonstrated in murine studies that both NPY ligands and receptors are active regulators of bone mass and osteoblast activity as well as integrating with mechanical signals during local loading [1]. As fracture healing progressed, serum levels of NPY increased significantly, reaching a peak in the 8th week [9]. In our previous study, we found that NPY could regulate the differentiation of primary cultured human ADSCs [10]. In the present study, we hypothesize that NPY combined with ADSCs may facilitate clinical translation and provide novel insights into bone transplantation in the future.

Materials and methods

Isolation and culture of ADSCs

ADSCs of rats were isolated and cultured as previously described [11]. Briefly, female Sprague Dawley rats' inguinal subcutaneous white adipose tissues were obtained before fracture. The ADSCs were cultured in modified Eagle's media (DMEM, Gibco) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1 % penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in 5 % CO2. The following experiments were conducted using the third passage of cells grown to 80 %–90 % confluency using 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA (Gibco). Experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

Identification of ADSCs

The morphology of ADSCs was observed via microscopy (Leica, DMI 4000, Germany) and the immunophenotypes of ADSCs (CD44/CD45-) were characterized by LSR II flow cytometry (BD Bioscience). The data were analyzed by Flowjo X 10.0 (Tree Star Inc.). All experiments were conducted in triplicate. ADSCs’ osteogenic differentiation were measured via Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) staining and Alizarin Red staining (Solarbio, China) [12]. For identification of ADSCs differentiation, cells were induced for 14 days and 21 days with different treatment. Then cells were fixed in 0.4 % formaldehyde (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China) for 5 min, washed with PBS and deionized water, and stained with 2 % Alizarin Red solution for 10 min, then rinsed with distilled water. ALP activity was evaluated using an ALP staining kit. The osteogenic differentiation and mineralization level of ADSCs was evaluated according to the intensity of ALP and Alizarin Red staining.

Osteogenic differentiation of ADSCs

To determine the effects of NPY (10−4 M, 10−6 M, 10−8 M, 10−10 M and 10−12 M) on osteogenic differentiation, ADSCs were cultured in osteogenic media (growth culture media supplemented with 20 mM β-glycerophosphate, Sigma, 50 mM L-ascorbic acid-2-phosphate, Sigma, and 1 nM dexamethasone, Sigma) for 14–21 days. The media were refreshed three times a week during osteogenic differentiation.

Reagents

Y1 receptor antagonist (BMS 193885, R&D, 1 × 10−9 M), Y1 receptor agonist (Leu31, Pro34, R&D, 2 × 10−9 M). Modified ferro O- solid green cartilage staining was operated using the Staining kit (Solarbio, China).

Experimental animals

Fifty-seven female Sprague Dawley rats, aged 6 months with the body weight of 270 ± 30 g, were included in this study. Fifteen rats were used to study the effect and mechanism of NPY on the osteogenic differentiation of rats' ADSCs in vitro. Forty-two rats were used for operation. Four green fluorescent proteins (GFP) mouses (Jackson lab, stock number 006567) aged 2 months, two female and two male, were included in this study. Every four animals were kept in one cage with climate-controlled conditions (25 °C, 55 % humidity, 12 h of light alternating with 12 h of darkness). Free access to tap water and standard laboratory diet were permitted [13]. All the animal experiments were conducted in accordance with international standards on animal welfare as well as being compliant with the Animal Research Committee of the university. For animal welfare, animals were monitored on a daily basis and examined for following indications: body temperature, body weight changes, clinical signs, water and food consumption, and behavior.

Ovariectomized rat model of osteoporosis

After bilateral ovariectomy (n = 39) or sham operation (n = 3) according to a previous report [14], 3 months were allowed to pass before fracture surgery for the establishment of standard osteoporotic animal models [15]. Then three randomly selected ovariectomized (OVX) rats and the three sham-operated ones were euthanized for bone mineral density (BMD) and body weight, and the uterus was harvested for comparison of size and uterine weight to confirm the establishment of osteoporotic animal model. Next, the remaining 36 OVX rats were then used for the tibial fracture model.

Fracture model experiments

Briefly, rats were generally anesthetized before fracture. To obtain a reproducible fracture in the rats' tibia, we used our own design of fracture modeling support, which had been granted utility model patent. A 1-mm-diameter Kirschner's wire was inserted into the tibia through the patellar tendon, then the bottom third of tibial fracture was conducted using the fracture modeling support. For the first 3 days post-operatively, pain levels of the rats were controlled by injecting buprenorphine every day. All efforts were made to help rats suffer the least and all surgical procedures above-mentioned were performed in a sterile environment. All rats received a postoperative X-ray on the following day to confirm the success of the transverse tibial fractures. Three rats were excluded due to comminuted fracture patterns, leaving 33 rats in the modeling group.

Infusions

After fracture operation, all animals were randomly divided into four groups, and the pharmaceutical interventions commenced from the second postoperative day through the following injections at fracture sites four times a week: vehicle (osteogenic differentiation fluid) (group ODF-vehicle, n = 10); ADSCs plus osteogenic differentiation fluid (ADSCs derived from the osteoporotic fracture rats, 2 × 1010 cells/ml, group ADSCs, n = 10); NPY (10−8 M) plus ADSCs (derived from the osteoporotic fracture rats, 2 × 1010 cells/ml) and osteogenic differentiation fluid (group.

NPY and ADSCs, n = 10); ADSCs derived from GFP mouse plus osteogenic differentiation fluid (group GFP, n = 3). All rats received a volume of 20 μl/side at an infusion rate of 5 μl/min. During treatment, the body weight of the animals was measured weekly. All experimental rats survived without any complications, including infections.

Microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) imaging

Five rats from the three groups (group ODF-vehicle, group ADSCs, group NPY and ADSCs) were randomly sacrificed at three weeks and six weeks after the surgery. Tibias were removed and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Fracture callus was evaluated using a micro-CT system (Quantum GX, PerkinElmer, America) at a resolution of 72 μm with 90 kV and 80 μA. We constructed 3-dimensional structures.

Histology

After micro-CT scanning, three rats’ tibias from the three groups (group ODF-vehicle, group ADSCs, group NPY and ADSCs) were randomly decalcified with 10 % EDTA solution for 3 weeks and were subsequently embedded in paraffin. Longitudinal, sagittal thin (7 μm) sections were sliced by a Leica RM2235 microtome (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Eisfeld, Germany). Sections were then mounted on glass slides, and stained with Safarin-O/Fast green method to allow identification of bone and cartilage.

Laser scanning confocal microscope

ADSCs, derived from GFP mouse plus osteogenic differentiation fluid (group GFP), were injected at rats' fracture sites four times a week for one week. The three rats’ tibias were then sectioned longitudinally through the fracture sites using a hard tissue saw microtome (Buehler 699-ISF-6531, China) to produce specimens that were ∼100–120 mm thick. Sections were mounted on glass slides, and observed using the laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM, German Zeiss lsm 780) to allow identification if the ADSCs migrated to fracture were involved in fracture healing.

Western blotting analysis

The cultured cells were lysed with RIPA lysis and extraction buffer (KeyGEN Biotechnology, China) to acquire bone-related proteins such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), runt-related transcription factor 2 (runx2), osterix, bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) and osteocalcin (OC). The concentration of these proteins was determined via the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein quantification kit (Beyotime, China). And Western blot was conducted as previously described [16].

The β-catenin was used as an internal control. The primary antibodies used in the experiments were anti-ALP (1:1000; Abcam, USA), anti-runx2 (1:1000; Abcam, USA), anti-BMP2 (1:1000; Abcam, USA), anti-OC (1:1000; Abcam, USA) and anti-β-catenin (1:1000; Abcam, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cells induced for 14 and 21 days using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A Reverse Transcription System (TaKaRa, Japan) was used to reverse-transcribe messenger RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) and qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green PCR master mix (TaKaRa, Japan) on an ABI 7900 fast real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, USA). β-catenin was used as an internal control to normalize the expression levels of mRNA. The relative expression levels were then calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primers for β-catenin, runx2, osterix and OC were purchased from Sangon (Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

All studies were randomized to reduce the influence of confounding factors and the randomization sequence was generated by a random number table. All independent experiments were performed in triplicate and all quantitative data were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad software 7.0 and SPSS 20.0. Two-tailed Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups and One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc test were used to calculate the P-values for multiple comparisons. A value of P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant and represented as the symbols “∗“. P < 0.01 was represented as the symbols “∗∗“. P < 0.001 was represented as the symbols “∗∗∗“.

Discussion

Bone grafting is widely used in hospitals to repair damaged, aging, or diseased bone tissue. Grafting of bone facilitates bone healing by stimulating osteogenesis (i.e., bone generation) at the site of injury, but this can only be achieved when augmentation includes cells capable of forming bone [17]. This procedure is the safest and most effective since it uses the patient's own cells (to facilitate osteogenesis) and proteins (to facilitate osteoinduction), as well as providing a framework for the formation of new bone (osteoconduction) [17,18]. However, bone autograft is limited in quantity (about 20 cm3) and its harvesting (e.g., from the iliac crest) requires an additional surgery, which is often painful and causes complications [17,18]. The next solution is allograft bone directly obtained from tissue banks (fresh-frozen) or prepared to be conserved (dried or lyophilized). This solution does not contain viable cells and some matrix proteins are disrupted by virus-inactivation treatments and the freezing procedure; thus, it only ensures osteogenesis induction properties. Moreover, allograft bone may transfer disease or lead to immunological rejections [17,19].

In addition to bone grafts, substitutes and their reinforcer with growth factors and synthesis strategies, cell therapies have been gradually proposed to evolved into new osteoinductive and osteogenic medium that can be used safely and effectively competing with currently available criteria. MSC-based cell therapy is an interesting alternative. MSCs are non-haematopoietic stromal stem cells that can be derived from a variety of tissues, including periosteum, fat, bone marrow, muscle, umbilical cord blood, peripheral circulation, skin and dental tissues. In addition to self-replication, they are able to differentiate into and contribute to mesenchymal tissues, including bone, ligaments, cartilage, muscles, tendons, and fat tissue. Bone marrow MSCs are currently the most appropriate cells for inducing bone repair, because they have strong osteogenic potential and can be obtained by culturing iliac crest aspirates. However, the number of bone marrow MSC is limited and the collection requires additional surgical intervention and often results in pain and complications [17]. Patients with fracture healing received bone marrow MSC cells at an average concentration of 1500 per mL, with 54, 000 in a total volume of 20 mL [20].

Adipose derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) have osteogenic potential and can be derived from a wide range of sources to meet the sufficient concentration of mesenchymal stem cells required for osteoporotic fracture healing. ADSCs could be obtained from autologous subcutaneous adipose tissue. In the course of MSC-based cell therapy, how to enhance osteogenic differentiation of ADSCs is the most important problem to be solved. Bone remodeling is mainly regulated by endocrine and paracrine, and bone homeostasis is also controlled by central and peripheral nerves, the most important of which is the NPY system. NPY is known to be expressed in bone and adipose tissue and acts as a neurotransmitter to regulate bone metabolism [4,[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. Our study shows that NPY promotes osteogenic differentiation of ADSCs in rats. This was based on the observation that NPY treatment led to upregulation of osteogenic genes and typical morphological changes after 14 and 21 days of osteogenic differentiation.

The effect of NPY on osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs has been supported by previous studies. Liu et al. found that NPY had a concentration-dependent positive effect on osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, while Ma et al. found that NPY and BMP2 increased osteoblast activity by increasing inter-cellular communication in the gap junction [26,27]. In our study, ADSCs were obtained from rats’ autologous adipose tissue. We added the NPY into the osteoblastic differentiation medium and injected the infusions at fracture sites four times a week. Our results showed that autologous ADSCs with exogenous NPY transplantation could stimulate osteoporotic fracture healing in vivo. We further used GFP-tagged murine ADSCs transplantation and found the participation of exogenous ADSCs during fracture healing. Previous studies have shown that serum NPY in SD rats increased significantly after fracture healing and reached a peak at the 8th week of fracture healing [9]. NPY inhibitors can inhibit fracture healing [9]. The possible function of the peripheral NPY system in local bone remodeling was also highlighted in another study, which showed regeneration of NPY fibers in callus and braided bone on day 7 of tibial fracture in SD rats [28]. Our findings add to a growing body of evidence that exogenous NPY combined with autologous ADSCs could promotes osteoporotic fracture healing. The study holds potential significance for understanding and developing fracture treatment methods for osteoporosis patients, particularly postmenopausal women.

NPY acts through several different receptor subtypes (Y1, Y2, Y4, Y5, and Y6) [29]. NPY receptor subtypes are limited to tissue-specific expression and explain the different roles of NPY in different tissues [29]. Our study showed that only Y1R was expressed on the surface of ADSCs. Furthermore, these cells exhibit a reduction in calcium deposition in extracellular matrix mediated via Y1R agonist in the presence or absence of NPY. In contrasts, it was found that mineralized nodules were more obvious after the addition of Y1R antagonist. Another previous study showed that NPY was a negative regulator of Y1 receptor expression, and exogenous NPY down-regulates Y1R expression on the surface of MC3T3 cells [30]. Functional analysis also revealed the osteogenic potential of NPY, and osteoblast phenotypic markers were significantly enhanced in osteoblasts stimulated by NPY, which may be related to the down-regulation of Y1 receptor [30]. NPY receptor function is regulated by multiple mechanisms, including receptor internalization/desensitization and recycling/re-sensitization [31]. The C-terminal of the Y1 receptor plays a role in desensitization and internalization [32]. Previous studies had pointed out that Y1 receptor was easily desensitized after prolonged exposure to agonists, and the response was rapidly weakened [[32], [33], [34]]. Studies using radio-labeled agonists [35] or GFP-labeled Y1 receptors [36] had revealed rapid cycling of the receptors [32,36,37]. In contrast, the Y2 receptor also binds NPY and PYY with high affinity, with less desensitization and endocytosis [36,37].

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, we recognized the limitations of simulating human clinical disease using animal models and our current model also has limitations and requires validation in a large animal model. Second, we only studied the pro-osteogenic effects of NPY on ADSCs, and the effects of NPY on bone-resorbing osteoclasts and chondrogenic differentiation of ADSCs should be explored in the future. Third, our findings provide some tentative initial evidence that exogenous NPY promotes osteogenic differentiation via down-regulating Y1R expression on the surface of ADSCs. We acknowledge that future work about additional in-depth mechanism study is required, particularly on the unique signaling pathway and molecular mechanism of NPY.

Conclusion

The findings of this study combined with those in previous reports suggest that NPY may play a role in the regulation of skeletal homeostasis, probably through Y1R. This knowledge provides a platform for the development of novel therapies for bone regeneration with endogenous ADSCs.
