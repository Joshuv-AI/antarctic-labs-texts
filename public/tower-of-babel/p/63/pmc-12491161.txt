Protocol to record single-unit sensory neurons in an intact mouse ex vivo muscle-nerve-DRG-spinal cord preparation
Authors: Adam J Dourson, Michael P Jankowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryDorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons innervate peripheral tissues to relay tactile, thermal, and chemical information to the central nervous system (CNS). Here, we present a protocol to dissect an ex vivo preparation that retains anatomical innervation patterns to the hind paw muscles of the mouse. We also describe steps for directly recording single DRG neurons while applying physiologically relevant stimuli to their receptive fields. This protocol provides important information for studying sensory input from primary afferents.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Dourson et al.1Subject areas: Developmental biology, Neuroscience, Systems biology

Before you begin

The protocol below describes the specific steps to dissect and record from dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons while stimulating their receptive fields (RFs) in mouse hind-paw muscle. Primary afferent neurons in the DRG relay numerous types of sensory information from peripheral structures, such as skin/muscle/viscera, to the central nervous system by firing action potentials (APs) which originate due to a stimulus at the peripheral RF of a given neuron1,2,3,4 Nociceptors are a particular type of sensory neuron that convey signals to the central nervous system (CNS) which are perceived as painful and are most often thinly myelinated (Group III/A delta) or unmyelinated (Group IV/C fibers).

Innovation

Electrophysiological recordings from DRG neurons have been completed for decades, most commonly by patch clamp electrophysiology from dissociated cells. The alternative intact preparation discussed here differs because it spares neurons from axotomy and dissociation, while maintaining some native anatomy and cellular environment.5,6 In addition to the preparation from fully developed mice, we also discuss the adaptations necessary to perform the experiments in neonates as young as seven days old (P7), providing use for hypotheses targeting the developing nervous system as well.

Setup of dissection and recording areas

These depictions are mimics of the actual setup used here. Modifications to suit individual working areas may be necessary. These should be located nearby one another for transfer steps.

Recording chamber

A small Petri dish (30 mm) with access from the bottom is placed towards the lower portion of a larger Petri dish (100 mm). Make a custom inflow opening in the center of the small dish and the opposite end of the large dish. Add a base layer of sylgard leaving the inflow openings free. Finally, create a small opening at the back of the small dish to allow the nerve to span the inner chamber to the outer chamber. Place metal mesh above inflows as a filter.

Step-by-step method details

Dissection

Timing: 1–3 h, optimal <2 h

The first step of this protocol is to perfuse the animal, isolate the target limb, and dissect the hind paw muscle-tibial nerve-sciatic nerve-DRG-SC in continuity. The purpose of this is to isolate an intact system to record from single DRG neurons while recording from their receptive field in the muscle.

Perfusion dish: 10 min.

Dissection dish: 1–3 h.

Note: This protocol is written for adolescent/adult C57Bl/6 or Swiss Webster mice. Adaptations for neonatal mice (P7-P14) are noted throughout the protocol. If a different strain of mice is used, or a different species, variations in anatomy may require alterations to the protocol.

CRITICAL: During all dissection steps, keep solutions at 4°C and with oxygenation.

Expected outcomes

In this ex vivo intact electrophysiology preparation, it is expected to record from a ∼3–12 neurons of interest/preparation. Neurons recorded by these protocols will mainly be type III or type IV (thinly myelinated or unmyelinated, respectively) sensory neurons which correspond to A delta and C fibers that innervate the skin as these are the largest population of muscle afferents. Functional characterization of these neurons reveal that individual neurons can respond to mechanical force (low threshold mechanoreceptors (LTMRs) or high threshold mechanoreceptors (HTMRs)), variable temperatures, or directly by molecules/chemicals/metabolites at their receptive field, such as those released during exercise or injury.2,3 We found that about 1/3 to 1/2 of these cells will be mechanically sensitive, a quarter will be thermally sensitive and a 1/3 to 1/2 will be sensitive to metabolite mixtures (depending on age).1,4,9 There is also a “silent” population of sensory neurons that do not respond to any of the standard physiological stimuli applied. Mechanical sensitivity will range between 0.07 grams (LTMRs) of force up to 10 grams (HTMRs) of force to evoke a series of APs but will be an average of about 4 grams in an uninjured/naive animal. We found that one day following a muscle incision there is a decrease in threshold to about 1 gram of force necessary to evoke APs.4 Spontaneous activity is rare but does occur.

Limitations

While there are several benefits of this preparation such as an intact system and knowledge of the innervation location, there are also limitations. This is a specialized preparation that has multiple failure points and steep learning curve. In addition, sharp electrode recordings from tissue are less stable than other forms of electrophysiology and impaled cells are sometimes lost. Typically, an impaled cell can be recorded for ∼10 min. Longer protocols (30+ min) will not work well with this preparation. Finally, since these recordings are not in vivo, contribution to the signals recorded from dissected systems and the tissue response to dissection can be confounding factors for recording neuronal activity.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

During the preparation checks, the muscle does not respond to direct stimulation (step 28).

Potential solution

First, it is important to determine the site of the problem’s occurrence. To do this, first stimulate the tibial nerve. Is there a response? If there is, then stimulate the sciatic nerve. If there is no response at this level, then the nerve could have been damaged during the dissection or there is a conduction block between the inner and outer chambers. If there is not a response to the tibial nerve stimulation, then it is likely that the muscle was either injured during dissection or was removed from oxygenated aCSF for too long. Both outcomes will require a new preparation.

Problem 2

The recording electrode will not enter the DRG (step 30).

Potential solution

If the DRGs are well located in the spinal column but recording electrodes continue to break upon approach, it is likely that the sheath may not have been effectively cleaned off of the top of the DRG. Repeat step 24 to clean the DRGs better. Sections of translucent tissue should be visualized when opening a pocket to record from.

Problem 3

The animal is no longer being perfused after flipping from supine to prone (steps 2 and 3).

Potential solution

If you do not see oxygenated aCSF perfusion while performing the laminectomy then it is likely that the butterfly needle was dislodged from the heart. Be certain before turning the animal that the needle is fixed in place. When turning the animal, it is best to do it in one smooth motion. Place the perfusion line in a spot it will not be tangled, lift and flip the animal. If perfusion is lost while turning the animal, it is possible that the preparation can still be recorded from, but it is likely that the quality will drop. Proceed with haste to the dissection dish if this is the case.

Problem 4

The recordings are unstable/there is a lot of noise (Step 34).

Potential solution

There are several potential causes of this problem. First, ensure that the headstage and outflow tubes are grounded properly. One common source of this problem is that the nerve was not cleaned sufficiently. Non-neuronal tissue attached to the nerve will cause it to float and the constant flow of the recording dish will cause movement. If the nerve is floating it is possible to clean it further in the recording dish, but it is best practice to perform this step effectively in the dissection dish.

Problem 5

Inner or outer bath of the recording dish is not circulating correctly (Step 19).

Potential solution

There are two 500 mL flasks during recording, one for the inner bath and one for the outer bath. If it is found that one of these flasks is reducing in volume, while the other is increasing, then there is a leak in the system causing a mixing of the inner and outer baths. Typically, then, only one outflow pump will be drawing up oxygenated aCSF leading to the discrepancy between flasks. If this is observed, stop recording, and find the leak. This will be occurring either at the petroleum jelly dam that the nerve passes through, or it is overflowing from the inner chamber to/from the outer chamber.
