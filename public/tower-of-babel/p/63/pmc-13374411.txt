Trends in physical fitness performance and body mass index of Swiss military conscripts, 2006–2024: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Kübra Stoican, Alain Dössegger, Zeno Giovanni Stanga, Kaspar Staub, Rahel Gilgen-Ammann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study examined temporal trends in physical fitness performance, body mass index (BMI) and physical activity behaviour among Swiss military conscripts from 2006 to 2024, including male conscripts and female volunteers, and explored differences by age, language region and urbanicity.DesignCross-sectional analysis of nationwide conscription data between 2006 and 2024.SettingSwiss Armed Forces conscription centres.ParticipantsRecords from 706 994 individuals (99.1% male conscripts and 0.9% female volunteers) were analysed. Introduction

Physical fitness is a key indicator of health and functional capacity.1,4 The key components of physical fitness include cardiorespiratory endurance, muscle strength, balance and body composition.5 Among these, body composition is often assessed using indices such as the body mass index (BMI), which, although widely used, provides only a crude and indirect estimate of adiposity. The waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) is an adequate indicator of central adiposity and has been shown to better reflect body fat distribution than BMI.6 Examining trends in both BMI and WHtR may help distinguish whether changes in body mass reflect overall weight gain or shifts in central adiposity. Monitoring long-term changes in physical fitness performance, physical activity behaviour and excess weight is essential for gaining a deeper understanding of broader public health trends at the population level. Over the past few decades, cardiorespiratory endurance and muscle strength have declined among children and adults across Europe, North America and Asia, while the global average BMI has increased substantially since 1990.7,11

Military service is mandatory for young men in several countries. Temporal trends in physical fitness of the civilian population may therefore be reflected among conscripts. Several studies have shown that similar trends in physical fitness performance and BMI have been observed among military conscripts over time. A study in Finland found that the average BMI of male conscripts rose steadily from the early 1990s, although this increase slowed between 2005 and 2015. Despite this slowdown, the proportion of conscripts with poor aerobic capacity and muscle fitness has continued to grow.12 13 Likewise, a study of US Army conscripts from 1975 to 2013 found higher BMI values alongside a decrease in endurance performance over time.14 On the other hand, a recent study showed that the cardiorespiratory endurance and muscle strength of Norwegian conscripts improved between 2006 and 2020.15

Earlier research in Switzerland indicated that BMI started to rise toward the end of the 1980s and stabilised after 2009.16 A subsequent analysis of male conscripts’ data from 2006 to 2015 found no clear positive or negative trends in aerobic endurance or muscular strength, while performance in core stability tests and self-reported physical activity behaviour improved.17 In contrast, balance performance declined marginally and mean BMI increased slightly over the same period. Evidence regarding women remains limited, since military service is voluntary for women in many countries. One study reported relatively stable BMI values among female volunteers in Germany between 2000 and 2004, alongside a decline in physical fitness.18 Another study analysing data from female volunteers in Finland between 2005 and 2015 found an increasing trend in BMI but stable cardiorespiratory endurance and muscle strength over time.19

Building on earlier work,17 the present study addresses the research question of how physical fitness performance and BMI among Swiss conscripts have developed over a more recent and extended period, and whether these trends differ across demographic subgroups. We extend the analysis to 2006–2024 and broaden it to include female volunteers. Specifically, we examine temporal trends in the Fitness Test for the Army (FTA) score, its individual components and BMI in male conscripts and female volunteers; assess whether these trends differ by age, language region and urbanicity; and provide a descriptive overview of self-reported physical activity behaviour and WHtR.

Methods

Data source

This is a cross-sectional analysis of routinely collected administrative data from the Swiss Armed Forces (SAF) conscription process. In Switzerland, conscription into the SAF is mandatory for all males aged 18 years and older. Although military service is voluntary for women, female volunteers participate in the conscription process, and their data were included in the present study. Data were collected annually during routine conscription between 2006 and 2024. The conscription process is conducted over 2 days at one of six centres across the country, during which candidates undergo medical, physical and psychological assessments. Basic sociodemographic information is collected during conscription, including age, place of residence, language and physical activity behaviour. Information on language region and urbanicity was supplemented with ecological data from the Swiss Federal Statistical Office.20 The SAF is authorised to provide anonymised datasets for academic purposes (MIG, BG 510.91).

Measurement procedures and instruments

Body height (cm) and weight (kg) using stadiometers and scales were assessed by medically trained personnel with conscripts in underwear and without shoes. Since 2018, waist circumference (cm) has also been measured using a tape at the midpoint between the lower end of the rib cage and the upper end of the pelvis.21 Self-reported physical activity behaviour was assessed using the validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short-Form,22 which captures the frequency and duration of vigorous, moderate and low intensity activities. Physical fitness was assessed using the FTA,23 a five-test battery: standing long jump (SLJ), one-leg standing balance (OLS), seated shot put (SSP), trunk muscle strength test (TMST) and progressive endurance run (PER). Each test and its protocol are summarised in online supplemental table S1. Raw values were converted to standardised point scores (0–25 per test; online supplemental table S2), summed to the FTA score (0–125). The PER, referred to as the ‘Conconi test’ in earlier Swiss conscription literature,17 is a paced incremental running test based on the Conconi protocol, evaluated by final running time and validated for conscription use with good reliability and concurrent validity against treadmill-derived peak oxygen uptake.17 23 Estimated maximum oxygen uptake (VO2max) was calculated using the formula of Wyss et al,23 with indoor times harmonised to outdoor-equivalent times before calculation. The FTA protocol, including equipment and scoring tables, remained consistent throughout the study period, with centrally trained staff and standardised procedures ensuring comparability over time. Participation required prior medical clearance; missing FTA data therefore predominantly represent conscripts medically exempted from physical testing.

Data preparation

BMI (kg/m²) was calculated from body weight and height and categorised according to WHO guidelines as follows: underweight (BMI<18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²) and obese (≥30.0 kg/m²).24 The WHtR (cm/cm) was calculated from waist circumference and body height, and categorised according to the Ashwell Shape Chart6 as follows: healthy (0.40–0.49), increased risk (0.50–0.59) and high risk (≥0.60). Physical activity behaviour was stratified into four categories (trained, sufficiently active, partially active, inactive) based on the Swiss physical activity guidelines25; full category definitions are provided in online supplemental table S3. Language regions were classified as German-speaking, French-speaking or Italian-speaking, with Romansh-speakers merged with the German-speakers due to the very low number of individuals. Urbanicity was categorised as urban, intermediate or rural based on conscripts’ municipality of residence, using FSO urban–rural typology, which combines morphological (population density), functional (commuter flows) and accessibility criteria.20

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included all individuals attending Swiss conscription between 1 January 2006 and 31 December 2024. No a priori sample size calculation was performed, as the analysis used the entire eligible population. Anthropometric measurements were excluded as biologically implausible if body weight fell outside 30–200 kg, body height outside 130–220 cm or waist circumference outside 30–200 cm.26 27 This was labelled as the BMI dataset.

For physical fitness analyses, per-test plausibility thresholds were determined empirically based on visual inspection of the full distributions across all years and informed by the FTA scoring boundaries (online supplemental table S2). Thresholds were chosen to retain biologically plausible measurements while excluding values likely to reflect measurement or transcription errors. The applied thresholds were: SLJ 1.0–3.5 m; SSP 3.55–12.0 m; TMST 5–350 s; OLS 10–200 s; PER 59–1310 s. These thresholds were applied uniformly across all years. Conscripts without complete FTA results, predominantly individuals medically exempted from physical testing, were excluded from FTA analyses. This was labelled as the FTA dataset.

Statistical analysis

Prior to the main analyses, the BMI and FTA datasets were compared across all study variables (age, BMI, language region and urbanicity) to assess whether the subset with complete fitness test data differed from the full anthropometric sample; differences were tested using χ2 tests, with Cramer’s V as the effect size measure.

Generalised additive models

Generalised additive models (GAMs) were employed to flexibly model potential non-linear relationships between conscription time and the two main outcome variables, the FTA score and BMI. Separate models were fitted for male conscripts and female volunteers. The analysis was performed in two steps: First, univariable GAMs were fitted to describe temporal trends in the FTA score and BMI. In these models, the month and year of conscription were encoded as a continuous numeric variable spanning from January 2006 to December 2024, corresponding to 228 consecutive months. Subsequently, the univariable models were stratified by age, language region and urbanicity to explore whether temporal trends differed across these subgroups. Age was categorised into four groups for the univariable models: 18, 19, 20 and >20 years. Second, multivariable GAMs were fitted to estimate the adjusted temporal trends in the outcome variables (FTA score and BMI) while accounting for relevant covariates. The categorical covariates included urbanicity and language region for both outcomes. Age at conscription was included as a continuous variable in the multivariable models to capture non-linear associations. As the analysis was based on an existing administrative dataset, adjustment variables were limited to those collected at conscription and were included to reduce potential bias in the estimated temporal associations. Seasonality was incorporated as a cyclic spline to capture recurring fluctuations across the calendar year. For the FTA score model, BMI was additionally included as a continuous covariate to examine whether the temporal trend in FTA scores is associated with concurrent changes in BMI. All continuous covariates (age, BMI and conscription time) were modelled using thin plate regression splines.28 For categorical covariates, regression coefficients, effect sizes (Cohen’s d), 95% CIs and corresponding p values were reported. For continuous variables results were presented as curves with 95% CIs and interpreted visually. These curves represent predicted means, with other covariates held at reference values or sample means; y-axis ranges reflect variation in adjusted means, not the range of individual values. All analyses were performed in Python (V.3.11.7) using the pyGAM library.29 The number of basis functions for conscription time, age and BMI was set to 7, 5 and 5, respectively, while seasonality was modelled using a cyclic cubic spline with 12 basis functions.

Additionally, the same GAM framework was applied to the raw data of each of the five individual physical fitness performance tests SLJ, OLS, SSP, TMST and estimated VO2max to examine their specific temporal patterns and to assess whether certain components contributed more strongly to the overall FTA score trend. GAMs were fitted using WHtR as the outcome to assess whether central adiposity followed patterns similar to BMI. As a sensitivity analysis, all male-conscript GAMs (FTA score and each fitness test) were rerun without BMI as a covariate.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in any stage of the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Results

Data characteristics

Between 2006 and 2024, a total of 706 994 Swiss conscripts were recorded in the dataset. After cleaning, 52 023 cases (7.4%) were excluded due to missing or erroneous anthropometric values, leaving 654 971 conscripts (92.6%) available for the BMI dataset, including 99.1% men and 0.9% women. Among these, 96 747 (14.7%) individuals were excluded from FTA analyses due to missing or erroneous data, leaving 558 224 individuals (78.9%) for the final data analysis, including 99.2% men and 0.8% women. The FTA dataset was a subset of the BMI dataset, comprising conscripts with complete physical fitness test data. Although differences in age and BMI between the two groups reached statistical significance (p<0.001), effect sizes were negligible (Cramer’s V=0.02–0.03). The descriptive characteristics of these two datasets are presented separately in online supplemental tables S4 and S5, and their yearly distributions are provided in online supplemental tables S6 and S7. A total of 552 205 (78.1%) conscripts completed the physical activity behaviour questionnaire, including 99.1% men and 0.9% women. For the WHtR analysis, data were available for 186 416 conscripts assessed between 2018 and 2024, 98.2% men and 1.8% women.

The proportion of those aged over 20 years increased from 19% to 34%, with the sharpest increase occurring after 2018 (online supplemental table S6). A post-2018 increase among conscripts aged over 20 years was observed across all language regions but was most pronounced in the French-speaking region (online supplemental table S8). The proportion with normal weight declined from 74% to 64%. The proportion of overweight individuals rose from 17% to 23%. Obesity increased from 5% to 9% among all conscripts between 2006 and 2024.

FTA scores and performance tests for male conscripts

The univariable GAM for male conscripts shows that there was a non-linear decline in FTA scores over time, with the steepest decrease occurring between 2020 and 2024 (figure 1a). When the data were stratified, there were subgroup differences (figure 1b–d). 18-year-old male conscripts achieved the highest FTA scores, whereas conscripts aged over 20 years showed the lowest (figure 1b). Higher mean FTA scores were observed among Italian-speaking conscripts, while mean FTA scores among French-speaking conscripts declined after 2018 (figure 1c). Results for the smooth terms are presented in online supplemental figure S1. In the adjusted multivariable GAM, the predicted mean FTA score in male conscripts declined from 74.2 points (95% CI 73.9 to 74.4) in 2006 to 70.4 (95% CI 70.1 to 70.6) in 2024. Participants from the Italian-speaking region achieved higher FTA scores than those from the German-speaking region (1.94 points; 95% CI 1.76 to 2.12). However, the Italian-speaking group represented only 4.1% of the total sample (online supplemental table S5), and the corresponding effect size was small (d=0.13) (online supplemental table S9). FTA scores decreased steadily with age for the male conscripts (online supplemental figure S1c), and both being underweight and overweight were associated with lower FTA scores compared with those of normal weight (online supplemental figure S1d).

Sensitivity analysis

When BMI was removed from the models, the estimated effects of year and age changed noticeably in the GAM models for FTA, SSP and TMST for male conscripts; these patterns can be seen in online supplemental figure S23–25 and online supplemental table S25–27.

BMI for male conscripts

In the univariable GAM, BMI showed a small increase from 2006 to 2024, rising from 23.0 kg/m² to 23.9 kg/m² (figure 3). This pattern was observed across all subgroups (figure 3b–d). Older conscripts consistently had higher BMI than younger ones. In contrast, 18 and 19 year-olds showed little or no increase in BMI across the study period (figure 3b). In the adjusted multivariable GAM, the predicted mean BMI in male conscripts rose from 23.2 kg/m² (95% CI 23.1 to 23.2) in 2006 to 24.1 kg/m² (95% CI 24.0 to 24.2) in 2024 (online supplemental figure S19). The smooth for age revealed a positive, approximately linear association with BMI (online supplemental figure S19c). Differences between language regions and urbanicity groups were minimal and not statistically meaningful after adjustment (online supplemental table S21).

BMI for female volunteers

The univariable GAM showed a marginal increase in BMI between 2006 and 2024, from 23.0 kg/m² to 23.4 kg/m² (figure 4). BMI values were consistently higher among older female volunteers (figure 4b). In the adjusted multivariable GAM, the predicted mean BMI rose from 23.0 kg/m² (95% CI 22.7 to 23.3) in 2006 to 23.4 kg/m² (95% CI 23.2 to 23.6) in 2024 (online supplemental figure S20). The smooth term for age showed a positive association with BMI (online supplemental figure S20c). There were no statistically significant differences by language region or urbanicity after adjustment (online supplemental table S22).

Waist to height ratio

In the adjusted multivariable GAM, the predicted mean WHtR was 0.47 (95% CI 0.46 to 0.47) in 2018 and 0.47 (95% CI 0.47 to 0.47) in 2024 for male conscripts, and 0.46 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.46) in 2018 and 0.46 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.46) in 2024 for female volunteers (online supplemental figure S21a–22a). The age smooth showed a positive association with WHtR (online supplemental figure S21c–22c). No meaningful differences were observed between language regions or urbanicity categories (online supplemental table S23–24).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined temporal trends in physical fitness, BMI (and WHtR from 2018 onwards) and physical activity behaviour among Swiss conscripts from 2006 to 2024. Physical fitness declined among male conscripts but improved among female volunteers, while BMI increased modestly in both sexes, and WHtR remained stable in the years available. In contrast, self-reported physical activity increased across the study period for both men and women.

Physical fitness performance trends

Several countries have reported reductions in the aerobic capacity of conscripts, with multiple studies documenting a consistent decline in running performance or VO2max from the 1980s to around 2015.12,1430 In Switzerland, however, aerobic capacity (VO2max) remained stable until 2020, after which a slight decrease occurred. The small or no changes in muscle strength reported in these international studies are also consistent with our findings. In contrast, Norwegian conscripts improved in cardiorespiratory endurance and muscle strength between 2006 and 202015; this likely reflects the Norwegian model’s selection of fitter individuals into the assessment process.31

In 2018, the further development of the SAF was implemented. This reform expanded the possibility of postponing conscription, and the effect of this is visible from 2020 onwards (online supplemental table S6), when the proportion of older conscripts increases sharply. As younger conscripts performed better than older ones, adjusting for age flattened the yearly trend, indicating that the rise in conscription age partly explains the observed decline in FTA over time. This pattern also explains the pronounced decline in FTA observed in the French-speaking region in recent years, given that this region exhibited the greatest increase in the proportion of conscripts aged over 20 years (online supplemental table S8). However, a residual decline persists after age adjustment and may be related to disruptions associated with the COVID-19 pandemic period, which should be examined further.

For female volunteers, FTA scores increased over time. More specifically, TMST and OLS performance improved across the study period, while the other test components remained relatively stable. However, the female data represent a volunteer cohort, which may not be fully representative of the general Swiss female population within this age range. There is limited evidence on female volunteers, but a study from Finland similarly reported no meaningful change in running performance and muscle strength among volunteer females during conscription fitness testing between 2005 and 2015.19

BMI trends

Although mean BMI increased only modestly (0.9 kg/m2 in men and 0.4 kg/m2 in women) over the study period, the proportion of conscripts classified as overweight and obese rose by 6 and 4 percentage points, respectively. WHtR remained stable for both sexes during the years for which WHtR data were available (2018–2024). This suggests that, although overall body mass increased, indicators of central adiposity did not change. One explanation is that increased muscle mass contributed to higher BMI without affecting WHtR, which is a more accurate indicator of fat mass.32 As expected, lower physical fitness performance values were associated with higher BMI values, which is consistent with the literature.7,1433

Physical activity behaviour

Despite the decrease in physical fitness performance, the self-reported physical activity behaviour data showed an increase in the proportion of conscripts who classified themselves as trained over the study period. This increase was observed in both men and women, by 16 and 14 percentage points, respectively, and observed consistently across all age groups. In Switzerland, a 2024 report indicates that many adolescents aged between 11 and 15 are insufficiently active34; however, specific data for young adults aged between 18 and 25 are lacking. At the European level, repeated cross-sectional surveys indicate that sedentary behaviour increased between 2002 and 2017 across adult age groups, including young adults aged 18–24 years.35 This discrepancy may be explained by several factors. First, it is well established that the retrospectively self-reported physical activity behaviour reflects habitual or perceived activity levels rather than actual activity level, and tends to be overestimated.36 37 Another possible explanation is that the types of physical activity behaviour reported by conscripts may not be adequate to develop the specific fitness levels required for military performance tests.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, women participate voluntarily, meaning that the results may not represent the wider female population. Second, BMI cannot distinguish between fat mass and muscle mass, and more accurate indicators such as WHtR have only been available in recent years. Third, physical activity behaviour was assessed using a self-report questionnaire, which shows only moderate correlation with objective accelerometer-based measures.38 Fourth, the study population consisted of young people with Swiss citizenship, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations. Finally, although we adjusted for age, language region, urbanicity and seasonality, several relevant variables were not available in the dataset, including socioeconomic status, migration background and lifestyle factors beyond self-reported physical activity. Residual bias from these unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. We also cannot fully exclude that the observed trends are partly influenced by methodological factors.

Conclusion

Physical fitness declined among male conscripts but improved among female volunteers, while BMI increased modestly in both sexes, and WHtR remained stable in the years available. In contrast, self-reported physical activity rose substantially, indicating a mismatch between perceived activity and objectively measured fitness, and suggesting that current activity patterns may be insufficient to maintain physical fitness. By extending national surveillance to 2024 and including female volunteers, this study provides important new insights into evolving fitness and health-related indicators in young adults. The findings underscore the need for continued objective monitoring and for targeted strategies aimed at improving physical fitness, particularly among young men, and at better understanding the behavioural and environmental drivers of these trends.
