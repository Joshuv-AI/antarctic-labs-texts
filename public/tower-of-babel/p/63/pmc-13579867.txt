Platelet-derived biomaterials in osteoporosis: mechanisms, evidence and translational prospects
Authors: Samson Prince Hiruthyaswamy, Dinesh R Rao, Priyadharshini Velavan, Thamizharasi Veeran, Yukesh Kumar Sakthivel, Kanagavel Deepankumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by impaired bone microarchitecture and reduced bone strength arising from dysregulated coupling between osteoclast-mediated resorption and osteoblast-driven formation. Estrogen deficiency, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered RANKL/OPG signaling amplify osteoclastogenesis while suppressing Wnt/β-catenin–dependent osteoblast differentiation, resulting in progressive trabecular thinning and cortical porosity. Although antiresorptive agents and anabolic therapies reduce fracture risk, they do not fully restore bone quality, microvascular integrity, or osteoimmune balance. Platelet-derived biomaterials (PDBs), including platelet-rich plasma, platelet-rich fibrin, platelet lysates, and platelet-based hydrogels, represent autologous bioactive systems enriched with platelet-derived growth factor, transforming growth factor-β, vascular endothelial growth factor, insulin-like growth factor-1, and bioactive lipid mediators. Upon activation, PDBs provide an early burst and sustained release of growth factors within a fibrin matrix that supports mesenchymal stem cell recruitment, RUNX2- and Osterix-mediated osteogenic differentiation, extracellular matrix deposition, and angiogenic coupling through VEGF-driven neovascularization. Concurrently, PDBs modulate osteoclast activity by influencing the RANKL/OPG axis, promoting macrophage polarization toward an M2 reparative phenotype, and attenuating inflammatory cytokine signaling. Integration of PDBs with osteoconductive scaffolds and nanostructured delivery platforms further enhances mechanical stability, local retention, and spatiotemporal biofactor presentation in osteoporotic bone defects. Preclinical evidence demonstrates improvements in bone mineral density, trabecular architecture, and fracture repair; emerging clinical applications in spinal fusion and fracture management suggest translational promise. However, variability in platelet concentration, activation protocols, and dosing standardization remains a barrier to widespread clinical adoption. PDB-based regenerative strategies therefore offer a mechanistically targeted and clinically adaptable approach to restoring skeletal integrity in osteoporosis.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Platelet-derived biomaterials, Bone remodeling, Osteoimmunomodulation, Regenerative orthopedics

Introduction

Osteoporosis is the prominent clinical circumstance typified with alleviated bone tissue density and strength that eventually opens the doors to fragility. These fragile bones are more vulnerable to cracking of bones [54, 74]. The micro structural decline of bone tissue organisation leads to the development of this silent disease as it remains asymptomatic unless there is a bone split [12]. Due to the discoordination in the bone assimilation and bone configuration there will be the development of weakened bone which is more prone to fractures at the different sites such as hip, spine and wrists. Osteoporosis can be classified into primary osteoporosis and secondary osteoporosis based on the developed symptoms and risks. Generally, primary osteoporosis occurs with age-related attributes including postmenopausal, senile and idiopathic osteoporosis, whereas secondary osteoporosis results from abnormal bone metabolic activity and non-specific medical conditions. Apart from age the other attributes such as gender, lifestyle aspects such as alcohol consumption, exaggerated smoking, dietary deficits, hormonal changes and overdose of medication will favour the development of osteoporosis in people across the globe [4]. The silenced disease proliferation makes the clinical diagnosis very challenging and crucial. As of now some of the diagnostic methods such as bone mineral density testing such as dual energy, X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans and Quantitative Computed Tomography (QCT), Fracture Risk Assessment (FRAX) Score. Other biochemical tests and imaging methods help to detect the biomarkers that are disturbing the bone metabolism and to evaluate bone quality thereby aids for the successful pre diagnosis of osteoporosis.

The promising advancements in osteobiology and pathogenic mechanisms led to the development of a variety of treatments towards osteoporosis. Among all those, application of anti-catabolic agents, selective oestrogen receptor modulators, RANK ligand antagonists, cathepsin K blockers, anabolic agents, parathyroid hormone (PTH) analogues and sclerostin inhibitors are most effective therapeutic approaches for osteoporosis [20]. Some of the important catabolic agents belongs to bisphosphonates which includes zoledronic acid, alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate these drugs block the osteoclast enzyme Farnesyl diphosphate synthase and eventually leads to the osteoclast apoptosis and decreased prenylation of proteins [77]. Hormonal therapy was also found to be promising in the females which aims at infusion of oestrogen or in combination with progesterone. Another prominent biological interferer is the Selective Oestrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs) which attach to oestrogen assimilators such as ER-alpha and ER-beta to mimic the estrogen analogous actions. Different types of SERMs are available for different clinical conditions but raloxifene is the ideal SERMs for osteoporosis as it behaves as an estrogen activator in the bone tissue and thereby reduces the cracking of bone and increases bone density [47]. Denosumab is another ideal monoclonal antidote effective against the RANKL found in the osteoblast and thereby reduces the osteoclast count [39]. The serine peptidase Cathepsin K of the osteoclasts holds the exceptional mechanism of deterioration of type 1 collagen of the bone tissue matrix to lower the assimilation ratio and thereby aids for increase in bone cell density. Along with these, the human parathyroid hormone analogues are also promising therapeutics against osteoporosis and more advanced research is required to understand the exact mechanism of other analogues such as PTHrp, c-src suppressors, Intergin and Chloride Channel detractors [14].

The platelet derived biomaterials (PDBs) serve as the exceptional therapeutic bone materials for multifactorial osteoporosis. These platelet derived concentrates hold high potential towards bone repair due to its various biochemical properties. Broad spectra of growth factors including TGF- β, VEGF etc. and various cytokines are present in the platelets which depicts a crucial part in regeneration of bone tissues. It is known that platelets rich plasma (PRP) or the Platelet Rich Fibrin (PRF) helps in the process of osteogenesis by splashing the growth factors at the injury site and thereby differentiates the osteoblasts. The presence of various anti-inflammatory cytokines in the biomaterials decreases the local inflammation and fosters the microenvironment for tissue regeneration and these platelet derived concentrates can be integrated with various scaffolds. Due to its efficacy, easy preparation and uncomplicated escape mechanism from the immune system of the host as it is prepared from their own blood cells these PDBs are widely used for the treatment of osteoporosis [26, 72].

The present study aims at exploring various cutting edge therapeutic approaches for osteoporosis and mainly on the PDBs to enhance bone health. The PDBs have already shown promising results to improve bone remodelling to elevate the bone density without the secondary infections and reduced inflammatory responses. This review highlights the recent advancements in bone medicine and biomaterial science to develop a novel, user-friendly bone scaffold to treat osteoporosis and its high potential in bone regeneration.

Platelet derived biomaterials

The bone is the complex rigid connective tissues present in the body of the human which provides the structural support and protects the integrity of various organs from the external mechanical stress. Osteoporosis, Due to the various internal and external variables the bone loses its density to form weak and brittle which is more susceptible to mild fractures. Osteoporosis occurs when there is regeneration of new bones without the breaking down of old bones. Nowadays various biomaterial-based treatments are available for this abnormal bone condition to prevent the various complications such as bone breaks in spine and hip. Among all such treatments, the PDBs found to be promising biomaterial based therapeutic approaches in bone medicine. The PDBs can be defined as the biomaterials where scaffolds are incorporated with various rejuvenating properties of platelets including growth factors that enhance the healing and bone regeneration capacity. These growth factors make PDBs an ideal therapeutic candidate for tissue repair responses in orthology and dentistry to reduce the bone graft rejection. As of now, wide range of biomaterials are efficient towards the treatment of osteoporosis including PRP, PRF, Leukocyte and Platelet-Rich Plasma (L-PRP), Leukocyte and Platelet Rich fibrin (L-PRF) and Plasma Rich Growth Factors (PRGF).

The PDBs is a multi-faceted therapeutic approach for osteoporosis that procures the reinvigorating capacity of thrombocytes to repair the bone damage to improve remodelling ability of bone tissue through various physio-biological processes also plays a commendable role in supplementation of growth factors to damaged osteocytes [5, 9]. The PDBs in the form of PRP and PRF delivered to promote propagation of osteoblast receptors thereby enhances the rate of differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) into osteoblast for bone remodelling also a study conducted by Dohle et al., looks at the ability of PRF to affect primary human osteoblasts (pOBs) and co-cultures of pOBs with human dermal microvascular endothelial cells (HDMECs) both directly and indirectly through the use of two different speeds of centrifugation (low speed at 44 g or high speed at 710 g) by assessing how PRF affects the viability of both types of cells, osteogenic gene expression of the pOBs (e.g [24]., ALP, Col-1, BMP-1/2, SPARC), the amount of growth factors released (TGF-β1, PDGF-BB) and the formation of vascular structures (via indirect effect) by the HDMECs through the trans-well system. Other growth factor associated with PDBs are β-Transforming growth factors, Insulin like growth factors and Vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGFs) which stimulates the bone matrix synthesis, bone cell proliferation and angiogenesis which is distributes the oxygen to the bone healing site. These growth factors aid to balance the bone resorption and bone formation through improved osteogenic activity and hence, reduces the osteoclast-based bone tissue loss.

Another important mechanism by which PDBs heals the bone damage is through osteoinduction and Osteogenesis by harbouring stem cells from bone marrow and other neighbouring tissues. These recruited stem cells help in the differentiation of MSCs into osteoblasts. To some extent some PDBs such as TGF-β can also inhibit the process of bone resorption from osteoclasts and aids for the ideal bone remodelling process. Other growth factors like VEGFs promote angiogenesis at the site of bone damage or injury as blood flow is compromised in the patients having osteoporosis. Hence, the excess amount of nutrients and oxygen is supplemented through enhanced vascularization in the osteoblast at the time of healing or remodelling of damaged bone tissues with the help of PDBs.

Along with these the PDBs can be incorporated with various bone graft materials like autografts, allografts and synthetic scaffolds to fabricate solid physical matrix which is crucial in bone remodelling process and it acts as ideal scaffold to support cell adhesion, cell migration and bone mineralization. Cell adhesion can be improved through the osteoblast cells and other regenerative cells including stem cells where it attaches to scaffold and helps in the formation of new bone. This macro matrix facilitates the deposition of wide range of minerals to strengthen the newly fabricated bone in osteoporotic patients. The PDBs are rich in anti-inflammatory cytokines and other protein molecules that can lower the local inflammation at the site of injury and post-surgical inflammatory response to provide the ideal atmosphere for the bone healing in osteoporotic patients. It is evident that PDBs accelerates the process of fracture healing by enhanced callus formation and bone remodelling. PDBs aid the rapid formation of callus to bridge the fracture rapidly and also PDBs play a vital role in the remodelling process by remodelling of callus into efficient and non-brittle mature bone.

Preparation of PDBs for the treatment of bone remodelling in osteoporotic patients involves complex procedures. It starts with the basic step of procurement of the patient’s own blood and concentrating the blood plasma to isolate platelets rich in growth factors. With the help of sterile needles and techniques a blood around 8-65mL is withdrawn from the patient it is mixed with anticoagulant such as sodium citrate and EDTA to prevent coagulation during centrifugation [89, 97]. After the addition of anticoagulant, centrifugation holds as the crucial step in the preparation of PDBs as it helps to separate the blood components and to isolate platelet rich plasma. During centrifugation two spins (soft spin and hard spin) will be carried out for the proper separation of blood components. During the first/ soft spin the blood components get separated into three layers where Red Blood Cells (RBCs) forms the bottom layer, White Blood Cells (WBCs) forms as Buffy coat in the middle layer and Plasma having platelets forms as the topmost layer. During the soft spin the blood sample is spun at 1000–1500 RCF for 10–15 min. During the second/ Hard spin the plasma layer of soft spin is collected and centrifuged again at a higher speed of 2000–4000 RCF for 15 min. This will help the plasma to separate into two layers as platelet- poor plasma (PPP) at the top and platelet pellet at the bottom layer. After all, the pellet is resuspended to fabricate PRPs [15, 27].

Above steps as explained in the Fig. 2 is followed by the activation of PRPs through Calcium Chloride or thrombin. The CaCl2 or the thrombin is used to activate PRPs procured in the hard spin step of centrifugation [48]. Activation is marked by the development of fibrin clot and thereby it releases a wide range of growth factors and inflammatory cytokines. In some cases, the activated PRPs form a gel which can be further used as scaffold for bone remodelling. Upon successful activation the PRPs can be blended with bone grafts materials to improve the process of osteogenesis through supplementation of scaffold and growth factors for development of new bone and promotion of bone healing respectively. The graft materials can be autografts, allografts and synthetic bone scaffolds for bone regeneration. PRFs are another type of PDBs where it is synthesised without the addition of anticoagulant for the high healing capacity by forming highly dense fibrin networks over the damaged bone tissues.

Preparation of platelet derived biomaterials

Administration of PRPs and PRFs as ideal PDBs to osteoporotic patients can be done through direct injection to the osteoporotic bone, sites of bone breaks or to the sites which require the bone remodelling. The dose of PDBs can be injected to patients during surgeries including spinal fusions and fractures restorations to speed up the bone remodelling and topical administration is another way of supplementing PDBs where PRPs or PRFs is directly applied topically to injured bone tissues or by incorporating it with bone scaffolds.

Thus, PDBs have emerged as a potential tool in enhancement of osteoporosis therapy and thereby it offers trailblazing solutions which aim at improving bone tissue health and regeneration capacity. The PDBs can be synthesised using the user-friendly procedure and can be mixed with different kinds of bone scaffolds to improve the healing abilities of damaged bone.

Physiochemical properties of PDBs

Platelet derived biomaterials are evolving as cutting-edge tool in the regenerative medicine especially in the treatment of osteoporosis due to their exceptional physicochemical properties aiding for rapid tissue repair, wound healing and therapeutic interventions. PDBs are composed of wide range of bioactive substances and structural components that owes them unique physiochemical properties and diverse role in the regenerative biology and these PDBS are procured form the platelets mainly Platelet Rich Plasma, Platelet Rich Fibrin or lyophilized platelet derivatives [8]. The physical appearance of these different kinds of PDBs are depends upon the method of synthesis as PRP appears as pale-yellow fluid. In contrast to leukocyte poor PRP, leukocyte rich PRP consists of higher level of growth factors and interleukin. PRF appears to be a fibrin clot or gel and lyophilized preparation products as off white to pale yellow powder [99]. The purity of the prepared products can be analysed through colour as reddish hue indicates the presence of residual blood cells [8]. The activated platelets undergo morphological alterations and with larger diameter of more than 20 μm where the resting platelet has discoid shape with the smaller diameter of 2–4 μm [18]. On other hand, the activated fibrin fibres have the smaller size of 50–200 nm which further assembles to form three-dimensional matrix which provides the great mechanical stability for the fibrin fibres. The particle size mainly influences surface interaction and matrix formation [13]. The viscosity of PDBs is low in liquid preparations nearly close to that of plasma (1.5-2 cP) but due to the fibrin polymerization the viscosity gradually increases and plays a very important role in injectability and retention at defect site. Since it has density slightly higher than that of plasma, on and around 1.04 g/mL it aids for the optimization in centrifuge protocols. The pH is maintained around 7.2–7.4 to preserve the protein stability and non-invasive quality check can be done by estimating the platelet concentration can be measured at 560–595 nm.

Chemically, the PDBs are enriched with adhesive proteins including fibrin, fibronectin, vitronectin and laminin which acts as a scaffold for the cell attachment along with these it also consists of albumin and globulin. Being the reservoir of growth factors, it is concentrated to higher value compared to baseline plasma levels [13, 18]. Platelet Derived growth factor (PDGF) can be 4–6 times higher, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) 2–4 times higher, and VEGF 2–3 times higher, along with significant levels of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) [3, 90].

PDBs display their therapeutic properties in bone regeneration by a complex mixture of growth factors, cytokines, and bioactive lipid mediators. Among these, prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂), a major lipid mediator of bone metabolism, acts as a dual regulator of osteoblast and osteoclast functions in a concentration-dependent manner. The controlled release of PGE₂ and other bioactive lipid mediators from activated platelets may also play a role in the anabolic properties of PDBs in osteoblast differentiation and, at the same time, modulating osteoclastogenesis by regulating the RANKL/OPG balance. Therefore, modulating the physiological balance of bone tissue through lipid-driven pathways may be one mechanism by which PDBs exert their regenerative effects on bone structures affected by osteoporosis [41, 85].

The carbohydrate moieties mainly membrane glycoproteins like GPIIb/IIIa and the GP Ib-IX-V complexes are critical for platelet adhesion and aggregation [85]. Calcium (Ca²⁺) is a key player in bringing together platelets to form a structure called a fibrin web within PDBs. The other two common types of ions, which are divalent cations (the most common being magnesium, Mg²⁺), are required for enzymes responsible for the decomposition of proteoglycan and/or glycol-protein structures that comprise the non-cellular components of new bones (extracellular matrix) to work. Overall, these ions are responsible for creating a stable structure to use when the new bone is formed PDB and dynamically reconstructing that structure from bones after initial formation [95].

Platelet-derived biomaterials, when activated, initially have an instantaneous release of the soluble growth factors contained in platelets around 24–48 h. This is followed by a prolonged release phase where the fibrin matrix and any bioactive molecules entrapped within the matrix, mediate the delivery of those molecules over time. The sustained release profile of the bioactive molecules from the platelet-derived biomaterials is one of the main ways that the bioactive materials stimulate osteogenesis and ultimately repair bone defects [51].Platelet-derived biomaterials have proteolytic enzymes including matrix metalloproteinases including MMP-2 and MMP-9) which impact remodeling of extracellular matrix. The activated fibrin network provides structural stability, while both the scaffolding’s density and its degree of crosslinking determine the mechanophysical characteristics of the scaffold, and the biochemical microenvironment including pH conditions, and endogenous antioxidant systems may impact the stability and functionality of growth factors located in the matrix [31].

Structure of fibrin clots determines mechanical characteristics of PRF and. When factor XIIIa crosslinks fibrin fibers, it stiffens the fibrin network and increases its elastic modulus. This enhancement improves the structural integrity of platelet-derived biomaterials and supports their use for bone regeneration [37]. The shear modulus can be varied from 10 to 1000 Pa where stiffer gels offer greater support in load bearing bio mechanical applications with the pore sizes within the fibrin network typically range form 10–200 μm with higher porosity promoting the vascular ingrowth and cell migration [100]. Fibrinogen-derived gels mechanical strength responds very sensitively to protein levels and the way in which the fibrin is arranged in the gel. Rheological tests show that the shear modulus (G) increases as the fibrinogen levels rise. This change in value indicates that the density and number of connections in the fibrinogen fibres are increased due to increased concentration of fibrinogen in the gel. Thus, mechanical properties of fibrin-based biomaterials can be controlled by changing the amount of the fibrinogen and how the gel is prepared [37]. The degradation rate varies from days to week depending upon the fibrinolytic activity which determines the duration of growth factor availability at active target site. It is also prominent that PDB gels also exhibit the strong adhesiveness to moist tissue surfaces which further aids localized application without the need for sutures [64].

Considerations of stability and storage are pragmatic in terms of the feasibility of applications. For fresh PRP, greatest bioactivity is preserved if used within 4–6 h of preparation. Storage of lyophilized PDBs can extend 6–12 months when maintained between − 20 °C and − 80 °C, while retaining biological potency, temperature fluctuations and repeated freeze-thaw cycles should be avoided, when possible, to reduce denaturation of proteins and activity of growth factors, especially when trehalose or other type of cryoprotective agent is included in their preparation [49, 98]. On the premise of biological safety, any PDB to be administered in clinical settings must be shown to be sterile and free of bacterial and fungal contaminants, as determined by aerobic and anaerobic cultures [6]. Endotoxin levels should also be < 0.5 EU/mL using the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay [42]. Autologous will confer negligible risk of immunogenicity, with allogeneic PDBs requiring some type of testing for compatibility to minimize unwanted immune responses [32].

To be able to assess these physicochemical aspects of PDBs, a series of analytical approaches are used. A range of methods can be used to look at platelet count and platelet size including using a standard haematology analyser, flow cytometry or dynamic light scattering. To measure protein concentration, common assays are the Bradford or BCA assays. Specific growth factor concentrations can be measured with either ELISA or multiplex bead assays. Fibrin architecture is assessed qualitatively using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), viscosity is measured using rheometry and mechanical strength or other scaling measures are assessed using oscillatory rheology or compression.

Types of platelet derived biomaterials for the treatment of osteoporosis

Platelet rich plasma (PRP)

PRP is an autologous biological therapy used for biomedical application which is based on the basic principle is to procure the small part of blood plasma that contains higher concentration of platelets compared to its concentrations in the blood. In 1970s it was first used as biomaterials in surgery, and they termed it as glue which is similar to fibrin glue which we are using in now in the surgical operations. At present the fibrin glue is synthesised from the PPP. PRP is a liquid concentrate of platelets suspended in the plasma containing growth factors including PDGF, VEGF, TGF-β, IGF-1 and EGF. Some of the cytokines found in the PRPs are IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α. The PRPs known to have various biological role in the regenerations mainly by platelet degranulation which leads to the release of growth factors for tissue repair, Stimulations of fibroblasts, osteoblasts and stem cells for cell proliferation and migration, it increases the matrix remodelling by the synthesis of collagen and also it modulates inflammation by reducing pro inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α. Thus, the PRP, a cocktail of plasma and platelet biomolecules plays major role in the regulation of hemostasis, coagulation, inflammation, tissue repair and regeneration.

The synthesis of PRP is three step procedure which involves collection of blood, fractionation of blood and activation of PRF. Generally, the small volume of peripheral blood is collected in tubes from the patient which is having high concentration of platelets. After collection of the blood it has to treated with anticoagulants to prevent coagulation which will be achieved by the supplementation of Sodium citrate and EDTA which blocks the coagulation cascade by chelating calcium or by using heparin that blocks thrombin. The high preservation capacity and low secretion of microvesicles due to platelet activation makes sodium citrate a best anticoagulant. Collection of blood is followed by blood fractionation where blood samples are centrifuged to obtain the PRP formulation. In the first centrifugation blood is softly spinned at the rate of 1200-1500 rpm for 5–10 min to separate the Red Blood Cells without damaging its components. The blood in this centrifugation is divided into lower layer of RBCs, a thin middle layer of WBCs and topmost plasma layer with high load of platelets. The first soft spin is followed by the second hard spin at the rate of 2000–2500 rpm for 10 min at 4℃ to concentrate the platelets. It is also evident that we can add the buffy coat of leukocyte to PRP (LR-PRP) for further regenerative applications and the erythrocyte layer should be discarded as it causes hemolysis and eryptosis and make the PRP unfit for medicinal biomaterials. Once the blood is fractioned, the platelets need to be activated to obtain the PRPs. In this step the polymerization of fibrinogen takes place where network of fibrin entraps the molecules and activates the platelets. This process can be exogenous where they will freeze and thaw alternatively or by some chemical substances such as calcium chloride (10%) and thrombin (500 Units/mL). When fibrinogen reacts with thrombin it forms fibrin clots which is used as scaffold for the tissue regeneration. In endogenous method of activation, the PRPs is supplemented to the body directly without activating them [82].

The study conducted by Qiao et al., showed that 3D printed porous titanium (pTi6A14V) scaffolds coated with freeze dried PRP promoted the attachment, proliferation, migration and osteogenic differentiation of Osteoporotic Bone Marrow Mesenchymal Stem Cells (OP-BMSC) in the rabbit model of osteoporosis which was established by bilateral ovariectomy (OVX). The Bone Volume/Tissue Volume ratio of pTi, TrdPRP (Traditional PRP coated pTi scaffolds) and FDrPRP (Freeze dried PRP coated pTi scaffolds) were 8.69 ± 1.10%, 13.53 ± 2.24% and 19.08 ± 2.31% at 6 weeks and 12.19 ± 1.46%, 17.04 ± 1.31%, and 23.80 ± 3.03% at 12 weeks. FDrPRP proved to have highest Trabecular Thickness (Tb.Th) and Trabecular Number, Tb.N (p < 0.05) and the lowest Trabecular Separation (Tb.Sp) among the three groups (p < 0.05) at 6 and 12 weeks and Micro-CT results showed PRP coated scaffolds promoted bone formation compared to pTi group [73]. Another study conducted by Malhotra et al., showcases the maximum efficacy of PRP in the bone tissue healing in combination with osteoconductive scaffold without allogenic products and autologous harvest of various tissues [61]. As investigated by Liu et al., in their previous study they showed that intraosseous transplantation of PRF into osteoblast cells into ovariectomized senescence accelerated mice, OVX-SAMP8 inhibited osteoporosis. Further, they studied the pathogenesis of osteoporosis and focused on the interlink between bone marrow adipogenesis and osteogenesis as there was no proper research on the same. In their study they used the PRP to prevent the adipocyte differentiation (3T3-L1) and supressed the BMPR-IA molecular Pathway as it has PGDF, TGF- β1, IGF which makes PRP a better molecular modulator and promoted osteogenesis with the help of upregulation of RUNX2, OPN, OCN and downregulation of PPAR-γ2 and Leptin [57].

Platelet rich fibrin (PRF)

PRF is a predominant autologous second-generation platelet concentrate obtained from the patient blood which has got a pivotal role in regenerative medicine, dentistry and orthopaedics [23]. Due its high wound healing, tissue regeneration and bone grafting properties PRP makes the best magic biomaterial for the treatment of osteoporosis. It is a solid matrix formed due to the natural agglutination of platelets and fibrin polymerization which helps for the regulated release of growth factors required for the bone regeneration with the help of growth factors namely PDGF, TGF-β, VEGF and EGF. Another most prominent factor about the PRPs is that it doesn’t require any anticoagulants and fibrin clots will be formed within 2–3 min. Currently three types of PRF available namely L-PRF which contains the WBCs and Fibrin, Advanced Platelet Rich Fibrin (A-PRF) which is obtained from lower speed centrifugation to get more number of viable cells, Injectable Platelet Rich Fibrin (i-PRF) which is basically an injectable liquid form of PRF which makes the fibroblast to move faster and it contains more amount of PDGF and Collagen which is used in combination of various biomaterials to enhance regeneration, Advanced Platelet Rich Fibrin Plus (A-PRF+) which is has the low centrifugation rate as compare to A-PRF.

The process of production of PRFs are mostly similar to PRPs where ample amount of blood is collected from the patient and then the blood components are separated using centrifuge at 2000 rpm to 3000 rpm f0r 10–12 min at 37℃. The blood must collected form the patient or concerned body without adding the anticoagulants as we require natural fibrin clot within 2–5 min. The fibrin clot is generated as platelets reacts with fibrinogen and fibrin crosslinking reaction carried out between fibrin and factor 13 to form stable PRF Matrix. The pH needs to be maintained at 7.2 for the matrix stability and amidst of all advantages the PRF cannot be stored which is major drawback for using it as the biomaterials in regenerative medicine.

As concluded by Le et al., PRF is a promising host-derived scaffold for regenerative endodontic treatment (RET). The study investigated the effects of A-PRF + and i-PRF on the proliferation, migration, and differentiation of SCAPs. The results showed that both A-PRF + and i-PRF can induce SCAPs proliferation, migration, and differentiation, but A-PRF + was superior in supporting the proliferation and migration of SCAPs [53]. As investigated by Can et al., Human-derived platelet-rich fibrin (HPRF) differs from conventional limbal epithelial stem cell (LESC) culture systems primarily in its completely xeno- and chemical-free composition, using autologous human blood-derived fibrin matrix as both substrate and conditioned medium, while conventional systems often rely on human amniotic membrane (HAM), animal-derived components like fetal bovine serum (FBS) or murine feeders, and additives such as cholera toxin or DMSO. The study highlights the advantages of HPRF as a biosafe, cost-effective alternative to conventional culture systems, free from xenobiotics, animal-derived components, or harsh chemicals, providing a biomimetic limbal niche that may improve clinical outcomes in LESC transplantation for limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD) [22].

Platelet lysates (PL)

Platelet lysates are the liquid formulations procured by breaking platelets to release intracellular growth factors into bioavailable solutions. Synthesis of platelet lysates are the two-step process which involves Platelet Lysis after separating from whole blood followed by the filtration and purification. Due to freeze thaw cycle at -80℃ and 37℃, the α granules ruptures and it releases the various growth factors including VEGF, PDGF and IGF-1 which then centrifuged at 4600 rpm for 20 min to get rid of cell debris. After the centrifugation, supernatant was filtered through 70 μm, 0.22 μm, 0.45 μm. Further it is purified and stored at -80℃ for further studies including stem cell culture, wound healing and tissue engineering [70].

The study conducted by Chayatup et al., showed that injectable PL-TSF (Platelet Lysate-Silk Fibroin) hydrogel which was designed for disc repair has improved the chondrocyte proliferation and anabolic gene expression (SOX9, ACAN, COL2A1) and supressed the catabolic (MMP13) and inflammatory responses (IL 1B) and thus its potential as less invasive regenerative therapy for Canine Intervertebral Disc Degeneration (IVDD) is highlighted [16]. Another study conducted by Pirsavabi et al., discusses that effect of human platelet lysate (HPL) on the ex vivo expansion and lineage commitment of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) derived from the peripheral blood where HPL significantly enhanced the HSPC proliferation resulting in a 1.96 fold increase in cell numbers compared to the control group by day 7 of culture and with reduced population doubling time. Along with these, the HPL also maintained the clonogenic capacity of CD34+ HPSCc figured out by colony forming units (CFU) between HPL treated and control groups. HPL effectively enhanced HSPC expansion while preserving their multilineage differentiation potential, making it a promising xenogeneic-free supplement for HSPC culture protocols [70].

Platelet derived hydrogels

Platelet derived hydrogels are the specialized hydrogels formed by the cross-linkage between platelet derived protein and biocompatible polymer which aids the slower release of therapeutics. It is composed of platelet lysates, biopolymers including alginate, chitosan, hyaluronic acid and crosslinking agents. PRP or the PL is formulated as discussed in 4.1 and 4.3 to which biopolymer(1–5%) of interest is added which then crosslinked through UV light, enzymes or chemical agents such as glutaraldehyde(0.01–0.5%), Calcium Chloride (5-10mM) which forms the hydrogel matrix/3d Scaffold which will trap the platelets inside and can be stored at 4℃ for short term and long term storage can be achieved through − 20℃.

As developed by Lin et al., an injectable, homogenous co network hydrogel composed of methacrylated platelet lysate (PLMA) and poly ethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) functionalized with stem cell forming peptide E7. The hydrogel PP7 demonstrated optical porosity, mechanical properties and degradation kinetics for osteochondral regeneration. Also, 10% of PP7 enhanced proliferation, migration, and penetration of both synovial fluid-derived MSCs (SFMSCs) and bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs). PP7 promoted chondrogenic differentiation of SFMSCs in cartilage-mimetic conditions and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in bone-mimetic microenvironments [55]. Another study conducted by Salsabil et al., describes the development of platelet lysate (PL) incorporated sodium alginate polymannuronate nanogel modified with mussel inspired PEGylation and embedded in carbopol matrix designed for scar-minimized healing of diabetic wounds. Nanogels showed superior blood clotting (BCI 14 ± 0.2) and spreadability (6–7 cm diameter). In streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice, Alg-PL-PEG-C achieved 99% wound closure by day 10, outperforming PL alone and controls, with rapid re-epithelialization, reduced inflammation, neoangiogenesis, collagen remodeling (basket-weave pattern), basement membrane regeneration, skin appendages, and minimal scarring (scar index < 10) [81].

Mechanism of bone remodelling and repair of osteoporosis

Osseous remodelling is a highly regulated dynamic process which includes the sequential action of osteoclasts and osteoblasts where the ossification is carried out by osteoblasts by breaking down the damaged bone through the process of resorption with the help of osteoclasts. Bone remodelling is a very crucial repair mechanism of the skeletal system of the human body which affects the bone strength, helps to adapt to high mechanical load and maintains the calcium and phosphate balance. This vigorous biological process involves various phases such as Activation Phase, Resorption Phase, Reversal Phase, Formation Phase and Resting Phase [78, 86].

Bone regeneration starts with the activation phase where the inactive osteoblasts or bone lining cells recognize the various signals of bone damage including mechanical stress, micro injury or other hormonal signals, calcium requirement and micro fractures in the osteoid. The osteoblasts after sensing the signals it recruits the osteoclast precursors such as Monocyte / Macrophage from the bloodstream to the surface of bone tissue. Under the influence of Macrophage Colony- Stimulating Factor (M-CSF) and Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor Kappa-Beta Ligand (RANKL) the precursor molecules differentiate into pre- osteoclasts where RANKL which is secreted by Osteoblasts will bind to RANK receptors on the pre osteoclasts to differentiate into mature osteoclasts [21, 46, 86]. Once the inactive pre osteoclasts differentiate into mature osteoclasts the phase of resorption takes place on the surface of bone tissue where the mature osteoclasts form a unique structure called sealing zone by attaching onto the surface of bone. This zone aids for isolation of specific areas of bone to be remodelled from the neighbouring tissues. Within the sealed zone the osteoclasts form the ruffled membrane which helps to increase the surface area of resorption. In this particular stage the osteoclasts secrete hydrogen and chloride ions into the sealing zone to dissolve the inorganic mineral component of bone and to reduce the pH for the degradation. Furthermore, the osteoclasts secrete cathepsin K, a lysosomal enzyme and metalloproteins (MMPs) which helps in the breakdown of organic matrix made of type 1 collagen. As this degradation proceeds there will be a formation of a resorption pit called Howship’s Lacuna and osteoid fragments and minerals are released to bloodstream through osteoclasts which eventually increase the calcium and phosphate levels [46].

Once the osteoclasts finish their work, they undergo programmed cell death and clears the way for bone regeneration to begin. In this reversal phase there is transition from resorption to formation where a transitional group of cells called reversal cells helps to clean up the debris in the howship’s lacuna and precondition the site for osseous development. The reversal cells guide osteoblast precursor cells to translocate into the resorption site and it differentiates into mature osteoblasts which are swayed by various factors, Bone Morphogenic Proteins (BMPs) and Wnt signalling pathways [21].

The formation phase is marked by the pause of reversal phase where osteoblast releases the organic components of newly fabricated unmineralized bone matrix called osteoid which is made of type 1 collagen, osteocalcin and osteonectin. Once the osteoid is formulated, mineralization process is carried out by osteoblasts by secreting vesicles filled enzymes such as alkaline phosphatase which aids for the breakdown of phosphate group and allowing to form hydroxyapatite through crystallisation of calcium and phosphate. The formed crystals start to become hard and eventually it develops as mature bone tissue. Osteocyte formation is another important event in the phase of formation in which some of the osteoblasts become embedded in the matrix and differentiate into osteocytes. Osteocytes are the persistent cells that help to maintain the bone tissues and which are connected to each other through a complex network of canaliculi by which it senses the various physico mechanical stress and communicate with neighbouring bone cells [78, 86]. Resting phase is the last event in the process of bone remodelling which is marked by the surface covering of bone through inactive osteoblast called bone lining cells which safeguard the bone surface and help for the future bone regeneration cycles. This quiescent stage can last up to several weeks to months depending upon the external shear stress and systemic requirement of calcium [78].

Platelets play a vital role in bone tissue regeneration mainly through their participation in hemostasis, inflammation and tissue regeneration under the influence of various growth factors and cytokines. Hemostatic process begins with the damaged blood vessels due to the fractures and platelets rush to the vascular injury site and initiates the process by attaching onto collagen and other matrix protein at the injury site and forms a platelet plug to stop the bleeding. These accumulated platelets aggregates and releases granules composed of clotting factors, fibrinogen and von Willebrand factors which leads to the development of fibrin clot. Fibrin clot stabilises the platelet plug and provides a scaffold for the repairing of damaged tissues [5, 19, 41]. Activated platelets secretes various bioactive molecules such as PDGF, TGF-β, VEGF, bFGF and Insulin like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) which contributes to the repair the damage and to recruit the immune cells to the injury site. Released cytokines and chemokines triggers the activation of local inflammatory response required for the healing and neutrophils and macrophages in the injury site eliminates all the debris and secretes more cytokines. Secreted growth factors recruit the MSCs from neighbouring tissue and bloodstream where the MSCs differentiate into osteoblasts and chondroblasts which are important for the bone tissue remodelling. It is also prominent that the platelets aid the conversion of pro-inflammatory (M1) macrophages to anti and regenerative state (M2) macrophages and thus enhances the rate of bone healing. The VEGF released by the platelets helps in the formation of new blood vessels and doubles the speed of the regeneration process by supplying the ample amount of oxygen and nutrients to the injury site [5, 19, 41].

Interaction of PDBs with bone tissue plays a crucial part in the process of bone healing and regeneration. The PDBs interact with osteoblasts, osteoclasts and osteocytes in a very peculiar manner. The PDBs interact with bone forming cells, Osteoblasts and enhance the remodelling process through various processes including the proliferation of osteoblasts, differentiation of osteoblasts and osteoid synthesis and mineralization. The proliferation of osteoblasts is carried by the secreted wide range of cytokines and growth factors. The Bone Morphogenic Proteins (BMPs) induces the differentiation of MSCs into mature osteoblasts by the upregulation of transcription factors Runx2 and Osterix. Also, Wnt/ β- catenin pathways are also activated through TGF-β and IGF-1 thus both these mechanisms help the osteoblast to differentiate. PDBs stimulates the synthesis of collagen type 1 which acts as scaffold for the accumulation of minerals and also IGF-1 and PDGF enhances the activity of alkaline phosphatase and thereby improves the mineralization capacity of bone matrix by forming hydroxyapatite crystals [41, 45].

Interaction of PDBs with the osteoclasts to enhance the bone resorption is a very challenging step in the bone remodelling procedure. The PDBs maintain the osteoclast activity through balancing the bone formation to bone resorption. The osteoclast differentiation is controlled by the Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor Kappa-β Ligand (RANKL) and Osteoprotegerin (OPG). Also, PDBs are known to be involved in the coupling process between osteoclasts and osteoblasts. The growth factors including IGF-1 and TGF-β protect the osteocytes by inhibiting the apoptosis or by activating the PI3K/Akt pathway so that the bone tissue integrity can be maintained. Sclerostin production by the osteocytes inhibits bone formation by blocking the Wnt signalling pathway in osteoblasts. Downregulation of the protein sclerostin enhances the bone remodelling process [5, 19, 41]. So PDBs interact with bone cells through a wide range of mechanisms to enhance the bone remodelling process. Additionally, PDBs are involved in the angiogenesis which is critical for delivering the nutrients and cells to the bone repairing site. Thus, through these biochemical interactions PDBs play an exceptional role in bone repair and remodelling.

Platelet-derived chemokines and neutrophil-driven osteoclastogenesis

Upon activation, platelet α-granules release a diverse repertoire of chemokines that regulate leukocyte trafficking, immune activation, and remodelling of the bone microenvironment. Key platelet-derived chemokines include CCL5 (RANTES), CXCL4/platelet factor 4 (PF4), CXCL7/NAP-2, and CXCL1/GRO-α, along with adhesion molecules such as P-selectin, which mediate platelet–leukocyte interactions. These mediators establish chemotactic gradients and adhesive interfaces that initiate recruitment of neutrophils, monocytes, and other immune cells into the local inflammatory niche [63, 83]. As illustrated in Fig. 4 (Step 1), platelet activation therefore represents an early immune-priming event rather than a purely hemostatic process. Among platelet-derived chemokines, CXCL4/PF4 is particularly important because it binds extracellular matrix glycosaminoglycans and promotes leukocyte adhesion within vascular and inflammatory microenvironments. Gray et al. [34] demonstrated that CXCL4 interacts with endothelial extracellular matrix proteoglycans, increasing leukocyte adhesion, vascular permeability, and inflammatory recruitment. This supports the concept that platelet-derived CXCL4 can stabilize chemokine gradients and prolong immune-cell recruitment at injury sites. Within osteoporotic bone, such chemokine retention may enhance the persistence of inflammatory leukocytes and thereby sustain osteoclastogenic signalling. CCL5/RANTES further contributes to platelet–leukocyte aggregate formation by interacting with chemokine receptors such as CCR1 and CCR5 on neutrophils and monocytes. In inflammatory bone conditions, this mechanism is important because recruited monocytes and neutrophils can supply osteoclast precursors or amplify the cytokine milieu required for osteoclast differentiation. Recent discussions on spontaneous osteoclastogenesis in osteoporosis highlight that platelet-derived CCL5 and CXCL4 may contribute to recruitment and activation of monocyte-lineage osteoclast precursors, thereby connecting platelet activation with pathological bone resorption [80].

Platelet–chemokine–immune axis regulating osteoclastogenesis in osteoporosis

Platelet-derived chemokines can also influence neutrophil recruitment through CXCL7/NAP-2-dependent mechanisms. Although this axis has been most clearly demonstrated in tissue repair models, it provides useful mechanistic insight into platelet-driven neutrophil recruitment. Graca et al. [33] showed that platelet-secreted CXCL7 promoted tissue regeneration by recruiting neutrophils to injured muscle, indicating that platelet chemokines can directly regulate neutrophil entry into damaged tissues. In bone remodelling, a similar platelet-driven recruitment process may influence the early inflammatory phase following microdamage, fracture, or osteoporotic bone injury. The recruitment cascade is further reinforced by P-selectin–PSGL-1 interactions, which facilitate platelet–neutrophil tethering, rolling, adhesion, and aggregate formation. These platelet–neutrophil aggregates are increasingly recognized as drivers of thromboinflammatory signalling, where neutrophils are not merely recruited but become activated through direct platelet contact [63]. This step is represented in Fig. 4 (Step 2).

Once recruited, neutrophils can regulate osteoclastogenesis through several interconnected mechanisms. Activated neutrophils release TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, reactive oxygen species (ROS), elastase, myeloperoxidase, MMP-8, and MMP-9. These mediators activate NF-κB, MAPK, and JAK/STAT-related inflammatory signalling, which increase the responsiveness of osteoclast precursors to RANKL and promote downstream osteoclastogenic transcriptional programs such as NFATc1. Recent osteoimmunology studies emphasize that inflammatory cytokines and immune-cell-derived signals strongly influence the RANKL/RANK/OPG axis and regulate the balance between osteoclast-mediated resorption and osteoblast-mediated formation [88, 96]. These mechanisms are depicted in Fig. 4 (Step 3–4).

A key mechanism by which neutrophils modulate osteoclast activity is through NETosis. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) consist of DNA scaffolds decorated with histones, elastase, myeloperoxidase, and other granule proteins. NETs can intensify inflammation by activating pattern-recognition receptors and promoting downstream NF-κB-dependent cytokine production. Importantly, NETs have now been directly linked to bone erosion and osteoclastogenesis. Schneider et al. [84] showed that NETs mediate bone erosion in rheumatoid arthritis by enhancing RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis, while Guo et al. [36] demonstrated that activated NETosis of bone marrow neutrophils upregulated osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption in postmenopausal osteoporosis models. These studies provide direct experimental support for the reviewer’s concern that recruited neutrophils can modulate osteoclast differentiation and activity. NET-associated signalling may also sustain a chronic osteoclastogenic environment through oxidative stress and inflammasome activation. Guo et al. [36] reported that NETosis in bone marrow neutrophils promoted osteoclastogenesis through activation of the cGAS–STING/AKT2 axis, and that inhibition of NETosis using the PAD4 inhibitor GSK484 attenuated osteoporosis in ovariectomized mice. This is highly relevant to osteoporosis because it shows that neutrophil activation is not only a marker of inflammation but can functionally drive osteoclast-mediated bone loss. Therefore, platelet-driven neutrophil recruitment may become pathological when the inflammatory response is prolonged or unresolved.

The CXCL12/CXCR4 and CX3CL1/CX3CR1 axes provide additional mechanisms through which chemokines regulate osteoclast precursor recruitment and retention. CXCL12 is known to attract hematopoietic progenitors and osteoclast precursors, while CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signalling contributes to monocyte-lineage cell adhesion and localization at bone surfaces. Salamanna et al. [80] emphasized that platelet activation may support spontaneous osteoclastogenesis in osteoporosis through chemokine-mediated recruitment and differentiation of osteoclast precursors. These pathways are summarized in Fig. 4 (Step 5). Taken together, platelet-derived chemokines establish an osteoimmune cascade in which platelets initiate immune recruitment, neutrophils amplify inflammatory signalling, and osteoclast precursors become more responsive to RANKL-dependent differentiation. This pathway expands the role of platelet-derived biomaterials beyond growth factor-mediated osteogenesis and highlights their immunological influence on bone remodelling in osteoporosis.

Macrophage polarization as a regulatory axis of osteoclastogenesis

In parallel with neutrophil-driven inflammatory signalling, macrophage polarization represents a critical regulatory axis within the osteoimmune network. Macrophages exhibit high phenotypic plasticity and can shift between a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and an anti-inflammatory, reparative M2 phenotype. This transition is important because macrophages and osteoclasts share a monocyte/macrophage lineage, and the inflammatory state of macrophages directly influences whether the bone microenvironment favors osteoclastogenesis or bone regeneration [40, 101]. Platelet-derived biomaterials, especially platelet-rich plasma (PRP), can modulate macrophage phenotype. Uchiyama et al. [94] demonstrated that PRP affects M1/M2 macrophage polarization and promotes an anti-inflammatory reparative phenotype characterized by increased expression of M2-associated mediators. This provides a direct link between platelet-derived biomaterials and macrophage-mediated immunoregulation. As integrated in Fig. 5, this macrophage axis acts as a counter-regulatory pathway against neutrophil-driven inflammatory osteoclastogenesis. M2 macrophages secrete IL-10, TGF-β, VEGF, arginase-1, and other pro-resolution mediators, which suppress excessive inflammation and support tissue repair. In the context of bone remodelling, these cytokines reduce osteoclast differentiation by interfering with the major osteoclastogenic pathways. IL-10 suppresses osteoclast formation by reducing inflammatory signalling and limiting NFATc1-dependent transcriptional activation, while also favoring an increase in OPG relative to RANKL. TGF-β contributes to immune resolution and can suppress osteoclastogenic NF-κB signalling while supporting osteoblast differentiation and extracellular matrix production [40, 101].

Macrophage polarization-mediated regulation of osteoclastogenesis and bone regeneration in osteoporosis

The connection between macrophage polarization and osteoclast formation is particularly important in osteoporosis because chronic low-grade inflammation increases osteoclast activity through the RANKL/RANK/OPG axis. M1 macrophages release TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, which intensify NF-κB signalling and increase osteoclast precursor sensitivity to RANKL. By contrast, M2 macrophages shift the microenvironment toward resolution, reducing the availability of pro-osteoclastogenic cytokines and promoting osteoblast-supportive signalling. Thus, PDB-induced M2 polarization can indirectly suppress osteoclastogenesis by reducing inflammatory pressure and restoring the RANKL/OPG balance. Macrophage-mediated regulation also connects to the NET-driven pathway described above. NETs and neutrophil-derived mediators can activate macrophage inflammatory signalling and inflammasome responses, thereby sustaining IL-1β production and promoting osteoclast differentiation. However, when PDBs promote M2 polarization, macrophages may counteract this feed-forward inflammatory loop by secreting IL-10 and TGF-β, dampening NF-κB/NLRP3 signalling and supporting tissue repair. This creates a mechanistic balance between neutrophil-driven osteoclast activation and macrophage-mediated osteoclast suppression. Macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype functions as a counter-regulatory mechanism within the platelet-mediated osteoimmune network. By suppressing inflammatory signalling, modulating the RANKL/OPG axis, and promoting osteoblast-supportive repair pathways, M2 macrophages reduce osteoclast differentiation while favoring bone regeneration. This axis therefore directly addresses how PDB-induced macrophage polarization influences osteoclast formation and provides a more complete mechanistic explanation of PDB-mediated bone remodelling in osteoporosis.

Denosumab

Denosumab a human monoclonal antibody (IgG2) widely used to neutralize the RANKL (Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor- κB ligand) which is a crucial cytokine essential for the formation, activation and survival of osteoclasts. As denosumab focuses on potential inhibition of osteoclast mediated bone resorption, it blocks RANKL directly which interfere with the primary mechanism underlaying the pathological bone loss. RANK, the receptor widely expressed on the osteoclast precursors and mature osteoclasts to which denosumab binds with high affinity and prevents the interaction between RANK and RANKL thus it results in the induction of apoptosis in mature osteoclasts as denosumab supresses the osteoclastogenesis.

As studied by Jin et al., where they studied the role of denosumab in combination with metformin which has showed synergistic effect on bone resorption and improved the bone loss and glucose metabolism in patients associated with osteoporosis and diabetes. Denosumab alone (DEN) improved bone loss but had only a mild impact on the progression of glucose metabolism. Conversely, metformin alone (MET) significantly enhanced blood sugar control but showed minimal effect on preventing bone loss. Importantly, the combination of metformin and denosumab worked synergistically to effectively reduce bone loss in diabetic postmenopausal osteoporosis patients [43]. As Denosumab Metformin reduces oxidative stress and encourages osteogenic differentiation of Tr-BMMSCs, aiding mandible bone defect healing in T2DM rats via the NRF2-GPX7 pathway [25]. It also activates the AMPK/Nrf2 pathway to inhibit ferroptosis, offering protection against diabetic osteoporosis [59]. Additionally, metformin shows potential as a treatment for osteoporosis by promoting type H vessel formation through suppression of the YAP1/TAZ-HIF1α axis [103]. The addition of teriparatide to ongoing denosumab therapy significantly improves lumbar spine bone mineral density in patients with severe osteoporosis on established denosumab. Sequential therapy, where denosumab is withheld during teriparatide, yields inferior spinal gains. Both approaches maintain stable bone mineral density at hip and femoral neck sites, with no clinical fractures or serious adverse events reported [52].

Selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM)

Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs) selectively bind to estrogen receptors in multiple locations throughout the body. Depending on the site of action in the body, SERMs can therefore act like an estrogen in some tissues and like an antiestrogen in others which makes a dual action like an estrogen-like action on bone, but an antagonistic effect in breast and endometrial tissue [28]. This ability to provide an estrogen like skeletal benefit has opened a new avenue of treatment for osteoporosis. When SERMs bind with estrogen receptors on the surface of bone cells, they cause changes in the expression of the genes controlled by estrogens. The direct effect of SERMs binding to bone cell estrogen receptors is to reduce RANKL, a physiological substance that stimulates the formation and function of osteoclasts and increase the levels of osteoprotegerin (OPG), the protein which inhibits RANKL. Some of the SERM widely used in the treatment of the osteoporosis includes Raloxifene, Tamoxifen, Toremifene, Bazedoxifene and Ospemifene respectively. The net result of these biochemical changes is a reduced number and function of osteoclasts, and decreased bone resorption. This reduction in osteoclast activity results in decreased frequency of bone turnover and increased/the maintenance of peak BMD, thereby reducing the risk for vertebral fractures, particularly in postmenopausal women. The use of either raloxifene or bazedoxifene has also demonstrated a positive effect on maintaining BMD in postmenopausal women, thus significantly reducing the risk of experiencing a vertebral fracture. However, the benefits of SERMs on the risk for non-vertebral fractures have not yet been established. Moreover, SERMs promote growth of hormone-dependent malignancies in breast and uterine tissue, thereby further reducing the risks of acquiring hormone-dependent malignancies beyond that seen with estrogen alone. SERMs are a safe alternative to conventional therapy for osteoporosis, because they exhibit the same benefits for bone as does estrogen, and because they have fewer negative effects when compared with traditional HRTs.

As studied by Gennari et al., Osteoporosis develops when the balance between bone formation and resorption is disrupted, most commonly due to estrogen deficiency after menopause. This study shows that estrogen normally suppresses RANKL expression in bone lining cells cells that regulate when and where bone resorption begins. When estrogen levels fall, bone lining cells produce excess RANKL, which activates osteoclasts and accelerates bone breakdown, leading to the characteristic bone loss seen in osteoporosis. Thus, their study provides strong mechanistic evidence that estrogen deficiency–induced osteoporosis is driven primarily by uncontrolled RANKL signaling in bone lining cells [30]. A study conducted by the Guo et al., demonstrated that the SERM raloxifene loaded polymeric nanoparticles (RLX- PNPs) remains one of the safest long-term therapies for postmenopausal osteoporosis, exerting estrogen-agonistic effects on bone that help suppress bone resorption and preserve bone density. The study highlights that raloxifene’s mechanism involves modulation of estrogen receptors to maintain skeletal homeostasis, although its clinical effectiveness is limited by extensive first-pass metabolism, inadequate absorption and low bioavailability when administered orally. The study also reports that sequential use of raloxifene after teriparatide produced only modest improvements in femoral neck BMD and minimal change in lumbar spine BMD, indicating its comparatively weaker anabolic support relative to other antiresorptive agents. Despite these limitations, the study emphasizes that raloxifene remains a valuable option for fracture-risk reduction in postmenopausal women due to its sustained release and potential bioavailability of the incorporated drug [35].

Anabolic treatments

Osteoporosis anabolic medication stimulates osteoblast activity to promote bone growth and increase the rate at which bones are remodelled, resulting in improved bone mineral density and improved strength. This method is in contrast to anti-resorptive medications, which primarily work by preventing the breakdown of bone tissue. The anabolic agents used to treat osteoporosis include teriparatide and abaloparatide, both of which are PTH receptor agonists that stimulate the production of new bone formation rather than resorbing existing bone. Romosozumab is a monoclonal antibody that also promotes bone formation, while, at the same time, it inhibits the reabsorption of existing bone tissue by blocking sclerostin, a protein that inhibits bone formation [10].

Parathyroid hormone analogue

Teriparatide is an anabolic (bone-building) agent used in the treatment of osteoporosis. It is a recombinant form of human parathyroid hormone consisting of the first 34 amino acids, which, when administered intermittently, stimulates new bone formation by activating osteoblasts more than promoting bone resorption. This results in increased BMD, particularly in the spine and hip, and significantly reduces the risk of vertebral and nonvertebral fractures in patients with osteoporosis Studies conducted on teriparatide have demonstrated its efficacy as an osteoanabolic agent through daily subcutaneous administration, leading to increased bone formation and BMD in the sequential treatment of osteoporosis in a patient with spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) type 3c. In a randomized, double-blind controlled trial, postmenopausal women with osteoporosis received once-daily injections of teriparatide (20 µg) for up to 24 months along with calcium and vitamin D supplementation, resulting in significant reductions in vertebral and non-vertebral fracture incidence compared to placebo. The drug stimulates osteoblast formation while inhibiting apoptosis, promoting net bone gain rather than resorption. Moreover, sequential therapy with bisphosphonates following teriparatide maintains the achieved BMD improvements and further reduces fracture risk. These clinical studies provide a robust framework supporting teriparatide’s role in managing osteoporosis, particularly in patients with high fracture risk or those who have failed antiresorptive therapies [75].

Teriparatide has demonstrated proven efficacy in the treatment of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women, men, and patients with glucocorticoid induced osteoporosis. Clinical trials have consistently shown that teriparatide increases bone mineral density and significantly reduces the incidence of both vertebral and non-vertebral fractures. However, its use carries potential complications, most notably the risk of hypercalcemia, which occurs in approximately 5% of patients and is typically transient and intermittent. This hypercalcemia may lead to secondary hypercalciuria and, in rare cases, nephrolithiasis. These adverse effects are generally monitored and managed during therapy to ensure patient safety while optimizing the anabolic benefits of teriparatide treatment [56].

Abaloparatide serves as an anabolic agent for treating osteoporosis in postmenopausal women at high fracture risk, particularly that intolerant to or unresponsive to other therapies. It functions as a synthetic analogue of parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP 1–34), selectively activating the parathyroid hormone receptor type 1 (PTH1R) with preference for the G-protein-dependent RG conformation. This binding triggers a transient cyclic AMP (cAMP) signalling pathway in osteoblasts, promoting bone formation on periosteal, trabecular, and cortical surfaces while minimizing osteoclast-mediated resorption [2]. Clinical evidence demonstrates abaloparatide increases BMD at the lumbar spine, total hip, and femoral neck, with reductions in vertebral fractures by approximately 86% and nonvertebral fractures by 43%. Compared to teriparatide, abaloparatide yields similar or superior BMD gains at femoral sites, lower hypercalcemia risk, and mild adverse effects such as nausea, dizziness, and headache. Daily subcutaneous administration at 80 micrograms for up to 2 years supports its role in severe osteoporosis management [2, 87].

This study conducted by Makino et al., demonstrates that abaloparatide(ABL), a bone anabolic agent, promotes vertebral bone repair in ovariectomized (OVX) rats with a drill-hole defect, increasing bone formation and bone mineral content, improving trabecular microarchitecture, and enhancing failure load; histology showed elevated osteoblast activity (ALPase, PHOSPHO1) with reduced osteoclast activity (CatK), suggesting a favourable formation versus resorption balance and potential modeling based bone formation in later repair stages, though chondrogenesis was not observed, indicating repair proceeds via membranous ossification in this vertebral defect model; limitations include the young age of the OVX rats, the defect model versus human fracture, the high ABL dose used, and the lack of data on cessation effects, but overall findings imply ABL could aid vertebral fracture healing and reduce nonunion and secondary fracture risk in osteoporotic vertebral fracture patients, pending further dosing, long-term, and translational studies [60].

Romosozumab

Romosozumab is a monoclonal antibody drug that falls under the class of osteoanabolic agents and works to treat patients who have osteoporosis by inhibiting the action of the protein Sclerostin (SOST), which is responsible for the suppression of Wnt pathway activity; therefore, stimulating the formation of new bone through Wnt signalling pathway and preventing the process of bone resorption. Romosozumab will stimulate Wnt signaling and, as a result, lead to an increase in bone formation and a decrease in the amount of bone that will be lost from the individual. Romosozumab causes rapid increases in the BMD of most skeletal sites, including the spine, the total hip, and the femoral neck, compared to bisphosphonates, denosumab, or parathyroid hormone analogues. Romosozumab is given as a subcutaneous injection every month at a dose of 210 mg. Additionally, romosozumab greatly lowers the risk of hip or vertebral fractures in individuals with osteoporosis both post-menopausal women and men with a high risk of a fracture through its significant effect on increasing the BMD. In general, romosozumab is a powerful drug that can promote the development of new bone and inhibit the loss of older bone, thus making the bones stronger and decreasing the risk of fractures in people with osteoporosis. Romosozumab will provide a viable alternative to current osteoporosis drugs based on its ability to increase BMD.

The study conducted by Tominaga et al., found that rates of major osteoporotic fractures were lower among romosozumab users compared to teriparatide user The 1-year incidence of major osteoporotic fractures was 10.14 per 100 person-years for teriparatide and 7.01 per 100 person-years for romosozumab (HR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.71, 0.89). The 2-year incidence of composite fractures was 9.16 per 100 person-years for teriparatide and 6.79 per 100 person-years for romosozumab (HR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.72, 0.90). Romosozumab use was also associated with lower rates of vertebral, proximal humeral, distal forearm, and proximal femoral fractures compared to teriparatide over 1 and 2 years of follow-up [93].

In the VICTOR (Verification of efficacy and safety of ibandronate or denosumab for postmenopausal Osteoporosis after 12-month treatment with Romosozumab as sequential therapy) study conducted by Kobayakawa et al., treatment-naive patients showed larger increases in lumbar spine BMD after romosozumab, while those who had prior anti-resorptive therapy experienced lower but still significant BMD gains. Treatment naive patients had the greatest lumbar spine BMD increase (up to 17.4%-21.4% at 24 months). Patients previously treated with anti-resorptive agents had smaller BMD gains (approximately 9.6%-14.0%), but the differences in BMD increase between prior treatment groups and treatment-naïve groups were not statistically significant at 24 months. Despite prior anti-resorptive therapy, bone turnover markers Procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide 1 (P1NP) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase isoform 5b (TRACP-5b) normalized within reference ranges after 12 months of romosozumab, indicating restored balanced bone remodeling. Thus, prior anti-resorptive therapy might blunt but does not prevent a meaningful BMD increase with romosozumab in postmenopausal osteoporosis patients. The study suggests that romosozumab remains effective irrespective of prior treatment status, but treatment-naive patients tend to have a higher BMD response [50].

Romosozumab outperforms traditional bisphosphonates and anabolics in BMD gains and fracture reduction, remaining effective even after prior antiresorptive therapy. Sequential therapy with agents like denosumab or ibandronate optimizes long-term outcomes, positioning romosozumab as a key advancement in personalized osteoporosis management.

Challenges associated with platelet derived biomaterials

Patient compliance

The way patients comply with treatments using Platelet Derived Biologic Material will not be adequately researched until clinical trials of PDB and PRP Treatment for Osteoporosis can produce quantifiable numbers for Patient Compliance. Currently, the only information is available from other biological therapies, Wound Healing and Osteoarthritis treatments using PRP Injections, in which most patients ≥ 65 years of age reported adherence rates of 50–70% at 6–12 months due to the demands of the procedures, such as repeated autologous blood draws of 20–60 mL, centrifugation on-site, and local and systemic injections every 4–8 weeks, to deliver growth factors essential for Bone Remodeling. Osteoporosis patients experience compounded barriers to compliance by being frail with limited mobility, as well as experiencing discomfort at the injection sites when suffering from an inflammatory response similar to the 40–60% compliance rates seen in Osteoporosis patients taking oral Antiresorptives like Bisphosphonates. There is controversy over optimal methods of administration of PRP for Osteoporosis, including Local versus Systemic administration, Single administrations versus Multiple administrations of a given dose, and the impact of inconsistent protocols and non-standardised patient education on non-adherence [5].

Poor compliance disrupts sustained bioactive release (PDGF/TGF-β for RANKL/OPG modulation), leading to suboptimal bone mineral density gains and microstructural restoration in preclinical osteoporosis models, though human data is sparse. Preclinical evidence suggests combining PDBs with scaffolds or stem cells enhances outcomes, but clinical translation hinges on adherence to mitigate risks like transient efficacy or dropout. Aspects of improving adherence to treatment include simplified Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) kits, ultrasound-guided minimally invasive delivery systems, and telemedicine support, which can be learned from general regenerative medicine’s focus on the importance of physician trust and decreased frequency of visits [5, 67].

Lack of standardised animal models

Lack of standardized animal models constitutes a major obstacle in biomedical and pharmaceutical research, particularly for PDB therapies in osteoporosis. These models are indispensable for exploring disease mechanisms, assessing therapeutic interventions, and delineating biochemical pathways, yet discrepancies in genetic profiles, housing environments, and dietary regimens across studies generate variable results [5, 71].

The absence of standardized animal models presents a challenge to biomedical and pharmaceutical development for PDBs in osteoporosis research. Animal models allow for the study of disease processes, therapeutic modalities, and biochemical pathways to be developed and studied. Due to the differences among various studies between genetics, environmental housing conditions, and food diet of the animals, this has resulted in great variability in the results that have been reported [5, 71] Currently, preclinical (research done prior to clinical studies) studies have utilized predominantly ovariectomized (OVX) models of mice, rats, and rabbits to simulate bone loss experienced by menopausal women however, these studies have shown much variability in many different parameters that limit the use of these models.

Specific examples include OVX mice, which show that PRF and adipose-derived stem cells augment bone consolidation as seen through micro-CT, while rat models do not demonstrate an effective method of healing fractures since they have different leukocyte to platelet ratios and different expressions of sclerostin when compared to OVX mice. The difference in models used makes replication difficult and less likely to succeed in humans because there are no standardized protocols that allow a laboratory to replicate what occurred in the original laboratory. Thus, the clinical development of PDBs intended to regenerate bone will take longer [69].

Regulatory issues and laws

Their vagueness and inconsistency create problems for rapid growth and market accessibility for PDB-based treatments; they could limit the recognition of their full therapeutic effect and result in delays in access to these new products causing patient noncompliance. To gain regulatory clearance, a thorough body of safety, toxicity and efficacy data must be generated. However, the cost and time involved to generate the required data is related directly to the variations in production processes, surface modification processes, and particle sizes. Therefore, establishing a set of standardised protocols for testing PDBs should be the primary goal for streamlining regulatory review and minimising variability from production batch to production batch. Additionally, ethical factors are of utmost importance in all stages of research and development, requiring open and transparent practices, as well as obtaining informed consent of study participants to ensure patient safety and promote patient trust. To successfully support the clinical translation and ease of availability of PDB therapies for the treatment of osteoporosis, all regulatory and ethical issues must be addressed appropriately.

There are numerous difficulties associated with PDBs under the FDA regulations for human cells/tissues and cellular and tissue-based products. These regulations are detailed in 21 CFR 1271, and consequently, all PDBs must adhere to a risk based regulatory framework approved by the FDA’s CBER. Preparation devices undergo 510(k) clearance only for homologous uses such as improving bone graft handling. Therefore, they do not receive any specific endorsement for treating osteoporosis. Off-label use of preparation devices for promoting bone healing implicates the clinician for legal liability. To avoid regulatory pre-market approval requirements, clinicians must also ensure that PDBs are minimally manipulated and implanted surgically in conjunction with other surgical procedures. Each country operates under its own unique regulatory framework. In the U.S., the regulatory environment for PDBs is stringent. In contrast, countries like Japan and South Korea have developed a much more efficient process to regulate regenerative therapies (e.g., preparing PDBs for a particular disease). The European Union also has its own regulatory environment regarding PDBs. To receive approval, Ortho biologics must comply with Medical Device Standards (MDR) and have sufficient data to demonstrate clinical efficacy prior to approving PDBs for treating osteoporosis. Due to their multifaceted nature, complex regulations slow down the speed of clinical introduction for osteoporosis-related PDBs and ultimately increase the costs associated with developing these products by necessitating greater effort towards producing comprehensive preclinical safety, toxicity and efficacy data prior to granting regulatory approval.

Pharmacological and toxicological barrier

Using PDBs, such as PRP and PRF, to treat osteoporosis with pharmacological approaches has several challenges, including differences in dosages, ways of preparing the products, and ways of administering the products. There are also limited data on how effective PDBs are for regenerating bone based on how they release growth factors, as this varies based on centrifugation speed, number of leukocytes present, and activation agents in the preparation. In osteoporotic bones where the microstructure of the bone is compromised, the use of PDBs may provide unpredictable osteogenic stimulatory effects. Debate continues as to whether systemic or local administration is the most effective method, as local application provides limited immediate efficacy because of the rapid degradation of the material and therefore no continued effect on the healing process of bone fractures or implant-osseointegration without the use of adjunctive therapy, including stem cell therapy or scaffold use [7].

Toxicological concerns with using PDBs for orthopaedic injuries include the potential for post-operative infections due to microbials being introduced during blood collection, centrifugation, injection, etc., with reports of Staphylococcus aureus, Cutibacterium acnes, and Streptococcus mitis being noted. Inflammatory reactions, such as excessive synovitis or local swelling, may occur from an overactivation of cytokines or due to patient specific factors, including diabetes. Rarely, patients have suffered severe adverse reactions from inadvertent vascular injection, including blindness. Other factors in addition to the risk of infection that may lead to an adverse event with PDBs includes allergic reactions to additives in PDBs such as calcium citrate, patients with nodular formation associated with sarcoidosis, and patients with gout who have experienced gout flares as a result of the lysis of platelet uric acid. Therefore, it is critical to develop standardized protocols for using PDBs in treating orthopaedic injuries, and thorough patient screening and counselling will be necessary [5, 7].

Biological barriers

Biomaterials from platelets, e.g. PRP and PRF are subject to biological limitations in osteoporosis treatments resulting from variabilities in how they are being prepared, including variances in centrifugation parameters and activation agents, all of which lead to variable concentrations and release of the requisite osteogenic growth factors and subsequently their kinetics. The use of platelets concentrations greater than 60% and or 8.21 × 109 /mL has been shown to inhibit osteoblast proliferation, viability, and migration, while concentrations of 1–5% will support these processes, which has led to a narrow therapeutic window complicated by patient-specific (age-related) deficiencies in bone microstructures. Unstable osteogenic effects of growth factors deteriorate quickly without a scaffold, whereas dysregulation occurs from TGF-β1, impairing the recruitment of mesenchymal stem cells and their subsequent differentiation and No Standardization yields various preclinical outcomes related to healing [9, 58, 106].

PRF’s fibrin matrix provides sustained growth factor release through protection of growth factors from degradation by proteolytic enzymes. However, when utilized alone, it does not give reliable results regarding induced bone regeneration in Non-Osteoporotic Models, therefore requiring the use of combinations of graft materials or implants to assist osseointegration [41]. Variability of published studies concerning the predicted biomechanical properties and histopathologic changes resulting from the use of PDBs as a therapy for improving bone density in the osteoporotic population are defined by factors such as differences in leukocyte content and absent predictive biomarkers, thus limiting their use as a reliable method of translating to osteoporosis treatment [19]. These barriers highlight the need to optimize protocols for the use of PDBs to influence RUNX2, OPN, and OCN expression while minimizing the inhibitory effects [19].

Synthesis of PDBs

There are many systemic barriers to the synthesis of platelet-derived biomaterials for treating Osteoporosis via Autologous PRP and PRF due to the inconsistent nature of the manufacturing protocols being utilized across the several studies evaluating PRP or PRF treatments for Osteoporosis. For instance, individual laboratories are performing blood processing differently by perpetrating conflicting methodology (from the 150 g initial low-speed centrifugation to the 2700 g high-speed centrifugation for PRF) which leads to variable yields of platelets (1-10x baseline), yields of leukocytes and concentrates of growth factors such as PDGF BB, TGF- β1, VEGF which are important for Osteogenesis. Concentrations of these growth factors above 60% or 8.21 × 109/mL can inhibit osteoblast proliferation and migration while lower concentrations will not stimulate the upregulation of RUNX2, OPN or OCN or support mineralization in animals with Osteoporosis. Individual variability, such as age, antiplatelet medication, and comorbidities can all negatively impact the amount of bioavailable growth factors. Further, both PRP and PRF degrade rapidly, within hours and days respectively, and will require modifications (e.g., scaffolds or gels) to control the release kinetics of the growth factors. Finally, while it is possible to produce PRP and PRF using an allogeneic donor to generate greater quantities, the inability of the industry to find a suitable solution for immunogenicity, limited shelf life and a lack of usable biomarkers are creating financial challenges and limiting the ability to conduct clinical trials without appropriate automated closed systems.

Translational obstacles

Delays in the use of PDBs for treatment of osteoporosis result from translational obstacles, such as lack of consistency in animal models used for pre-clinical testing of these materials that replicate the deficits experienced in the osteoporotic patient population. Regulatory hurdles created by the FDA’s requirements under 21 CFR 1271, which require extensive safety and effectiveness data to be generated, contribute to the delay in the pathway to the clinic. Generally, a lack of planning for funding short-term projects means that there is less incentive to conduct long-term studies to validate the efficacy of these materials [11, 38].

Culture differences between basic scientists and physicians, a limited capacity for conducting large-scale trials, the complexity of ethics approval processes, and issues related to intellectual property have made it difficult to translate the use of PRP and PRF into clinical practice. These issues lead to high rates of failure during phase transitions, which increase costs and make it difficult for new therapies to be accepted clinically, despite having a strong profile as a source of osteogenic growth factors [62, 79].

Stability, leukocyte and activation barrier

The stability of PDB is relatively low, with the growth factor in the autologous preparation of PRP usually being degraded within hours, and growth factor in the PRF usually being degraded within days due to cell mediated degradation, this leads to the necessity to either use the material immediately after preparation, or tissue as a modifier of the properties of the PRF/PRP such as crosslinking with carbodiimides or by using albumin gel to give higher sustained release of osteogenic proteins. The major limitation for storage of autologous tissue is point-of-care preparation since any means of refrigeration and or freezing cause changes in the overall structure of the platelets. Therefore, the ability to develop, scale and produce an allogenic source of these products will be limited by the short shelf-life of these products and possible immunogenicity. To produce the quantity of product needed for widespread treatment of osteoporosis, there must be the development of automated processing systems, including methods of lyophilization that will retain the bioactivity of growth factors (PDGF-BB, TGF-β1, VEGF) [5, 19].

Leukocyte-rich PDB are known to stimulate inflammatory pathways through the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-1β, TNF-α), and in osteoporotic conditions, this activation may lead to excessive stimulation of osteoclasts, causing catabolism of bone matrix. Leukocyte-poor variants of PDB promote differentiation of osteoblasts as opposed to provoking inflammation. Any cellular contaminants introduced during centrifugation (erythrocytes, neutrophils) can subsequently reduce the purity and bioactivity of the growth factors contained within the PDB, creating a dose-dependent inhibition of osteoblast proliferation, especially at concentrations of > 60% platelets. Standardization of the leukocyte content of the PDB is critical for the successful restoration of trabecular bone [5].

PRP uses anticoagulants such as citrate-phosphate-dextrose to chelate calcium and delay the clotting process; however, they also inhibit the natural activation and releasing of growth factors. Calcium chloride and thrombin provide a rapid burst of activity, which may limit their use in the long-term treatment of osteoporosis. There are many ways to activate platelets depending on the agent used. If too much protease is present, there will be a premature degradation of the fibrin matrix. Additionally, some medications used by patients (aspirin) may also impair the response of the platelets and cause variability in the release of VEGF and PDGF. The wide variation in release of these factors makes it necessary to develop a standard protocol to achieve a proper balance between early and late osteogenic signalling while minimizing potential patient risks [5].

Conclusion

As the field of osteoporosis treatment grows, so will the role that the synergy between PDBs and sophisticated drug delivery systems will play in providing care. Currently, there is ongoing exploration of how to encapsulate growth factors and bioactive molecules within PDBs as a means of improving their targeting capabilities toward bone and their controlled release of therapeutic agents. These systems will allow prolonged and sustained release of osteoinductive factors, which would lead to less frequent treatment and better patient compliance. Furthermore, the combination of PDBs with small molecule inhibitors or biologics that target bone resorption could offer a variety of their own benefits such as synergies yielding more holistic management of diseases like osteoporosis, particularly when faced with resistance from patients to conventional therapies. Another attractive area for the use of PDBs is the application of these biomaterials in combination with nanotechnology. Nanoparticles loaded with platelet-derived factors or other osteogenic agents may be engineered that will increase their bioavailability, with targeted delivery to bone sites, maximizing therapeutic outcomes. This design will allow for highly specific and localized treatment with a minimal in the way of side effects while enhancing efficacy in the treatment of osteoporosis.

Additionally, the use of nanostructured PDBs could give a mechanical advantage in terms of providing structural support to compromised bones, potentially reducing the likelihood of fracture with time. The development of such nanomedicines may confer major elevations in terms of quality of life on patients suffering from osteoporosis. The future vision for the clinical parade of PDB based treatment of osteoporosis would take place within the ambit of personalized medicine, whereby treatment modalities are predicated upon the genetic, biochemical, and physiological profile of the individual patient. Such expertise in addressing the various myriad mechanisms through which bone loss may occur within each individual offers an increased opportunity for the clinician to refine the application of PDBs for maximal impact. Also, with the expanding evidence supporting the regenerative potential of PDBs, such treatments are likely to form an integral part of the treatment and management of osteoporosis and restoration of bone density in severe bone degeneration. With ongoing research and further clinical trials, there is a general expectation that PDB based therapies will be widely adopted, providing a more efficient, regenerative, and sustainable alternative for current osteoporosis treatments.

Future Perspectives

Various studies being done to improve the therapy of osteoporosis with the use of platelet-derived biomaterials and it focuses on the quality control of the various forms of PRP and PRF by developing optimal centrifugation processes and removing the white blood cells (leukocytes) in order to provide a consistent level of growth factors such as PDGF-BB, TGF-β1 and VEGF, which are responsible for facilitating the formation of osteoblasts through RUNX2 activation and the elevation of the levels of OCN in ovariectomized experimental models. Another area of investigation involves the use of PDBs in conjunction with mesenchymal stromal cells, bioengineered hydroxyapatite scaffolds, or sequentially with anabolic agents including teriparatide for the delivery of PDBs to enhance the restoration of trabecular microarchitecture by means of the sustained bioactive release of PDBs when combined with carbodiimide crosslinked hydrogels, thereby allowing PDBs to circumvent the transient limitations of native PDB formulations [44] and the introduction of a new PRF system consisting of an albumin gel and PRF (Alb-PRF) offer novel means of enhancing the functional life of these biological composites [29]. Further pivotal Phase III randomized controlled trials, such as NCT07078006, an ongoing prospective study on ClinicalTrials.gov investigating the efficacy of injectable-PRF compared to PRP for the treatment of vertebral compression fractures in patients with osteoporosis with a focus on reducing pain, improving scores on the Oswestry Disability Index, and restoring vertebral height in postmenopausal women, will help to establish the efficacy, safety and adherence of PDB treatment in geriatric populations through evidence from real-world pharmacovigilance registries to support these studies.

Innovations that translate to healthcare practices encompass all fields relating to advanced PRF matrix technology. These advanced PRF matrix technologies include using a variant of human serum albumin (HSA) to enhance the albumin component of the PRF matrix, as well as creating concentrated growth factors (CGFs) with the help of genipin cross-linking for increased stability over time. They also include developing nanostructures that allow for intraosseous delivery or targeting, developing and utilizing AI-driven scaffolds to tailor treatment to each patient, and incorporating them into bisphosphonate therapy protocols to better accommodate the wide variability of synthetic procedures due to factors such as donor variability, regulatory restrictions on HCT/P development, and finding a scalable model for producing such tissue grafts. They provide mechanisms for rapidly delivering FDA/EMA-approved protocols for managing high-risk conditions associated with osteoporosis by encouraging patients to utilize these cutting-edge technologies as an affordable option for developing their autologous radiolucent Tissue Culture grafts, as concluded by Yu et al., that autologous PRP is better option for the Osteoinduction and improvement of bone regeneration [104]. Followed by the future investigations into the use of I-PRF and Alb-PRF in both in vitro as well as in vivo models for the treatment of osteoporosis will be essential.
