The impact of non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) on the development of prediabetes in middle-aged and older Chinese adults: a national cohort study
Authors: Jintao Chen, Liying Yan, Qingan Fu, Miao Yu, Yue Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) has demonstrated significant clinical application potential in the evaluation of vascular-related diseases. Our objective was to explore the impact of NHHR on the progression from prediabetes to diabetes or the regression to normal fasting glucose (NFG).MethodThis study utilized data from the first and third waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, involving 2922 participants. Based on the changes in glycemic status during the follow-up period, the outcome variables were categorized into three categories: progression to diabetes, persistence in prediabetes status, or return to NFG. Multivariable logistic regression analyses, restricted cubic spline regression analyses, stratification analyses, and sensitivity analyses were used to assess the effect of NHHR on the development of prediabetes.ResultsIn a follow-up study spanning approximately three years, we observed that 558 participants (19.1%) returned to normal blood glucose levels, while 514 participants (17.6%) progressed to diabetes. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, we found a linear negative relationship between NHHR and the reversion from prediabetes to NFG [(odds ratio (OR) 0.88, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.80–0.98)]. Additionally, NHHR exhibited a linear positive association with the progression from prediabetes to diabetes (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.15–1.41). Stratified analyses revealed no significant interactions between NHHR and the subgroup variables.ConclusionNHHR is significantly associated with prediabetes development in middle-aged and older adults. Lower NHHR levels increase the likelihood of prediabetes regression, whereas higher NHHR levels elevate the risk of prediabetes progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01064-1.Keywords: Prediabetes, NHHR, Normal fasting glucose, Middle-aged and older Chinese adults

Introduction

Prediabetes is a state that lies between normal blood glucose levels and diabetes [1, 2]. Although the pathological changes in prediabetes are not as severe as in diabetes, it remains a common and significant health issue, affecting approximately 720 million people worldwide [3]. In China, around 50% of individuals aged 50 and above are diagnosed with prediabetes [4]. Research has shown that the occurrence of prediabetes often signals adverse health outcomes, with patients having a higher risk of developing diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, kidney diseases, and even death [5, 6]. Previous studies have shown that the cumulative incidence of progression to diabetes in individuals with prediabetes can be as high as 39% within five years [7]. However, it is noteworthy that 33–59% of individuals with prediabetes can revert to normal fasting glucose (NFG) levels [7]. Therefore, targeted research on the factors driving prediabetes and the implementation of early intervention strategies hold significant potential for preventing the progression to diabetes.

Non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) is an important non-traditional lipid parameter that has garnered significant attention in recent years due to its inclusion of both anti-atherogenic and atherogenic lipid components. Research has demonstrated that NHHR not only holds substantial value in assessing atherosclerotic diseases and their negative consequences but also plays a significant role in the reflection of insulin resistance, which is strongly associated with glucose metabolic disorders [8–11]. Overall, NHHR has shown considerable clinical application potential in evaluating vascular-related diseases. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential value of NHHR in predicting the onset of diabetes in healthy adults [12–14]. However, the association between NHHR and changes in blood glucose levels (progression or regression) in individuals with prediabetes remains underexplored in previous studies. Given the clinical significance of prediabetes—where progression is linked to an elevated risk of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, while regression is associated with improved outcomes—identifying factors correlated with these transitions may help inform early risk stratification and intervention strategies [1, 15].

This study utilizes nationally representative data to explore the role of NHHR in the progression of prediabetes to diabetes or its regression to NFG. Through this research, we aim to uncover the relationship between NHHR and changes in glucose status, providing new insights and strategies for the management and intervention of prediabetes.

Methods

Study population and design

China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is an ongoing longitudinal study focusing on middle-aged and older adults residing in Chinese communities [16]. The project employs rigorous random sampling methods, with baseline data collected from 450 villages and households across 150 counties and districts nationwide. All participants are interviewed face-to-face by professionally trained staff, using standardized questionnaires to gather sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related data. The primary goal of CHARLS is to collect high-quality, representative data to analyze China's aging population and promote interdisciplinary research on aging-related issues.

Since 2011, CHARLS has completed five waves of surveys: 2011 (Wave 1), 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4), and 2020 (Wave 5). Only Wave 1 and Wave 3 provided participants'blood test data, so our study population was selected from the 7,648 individuals who participated in the 2011 and 2015 surveys and had data on their blood tests. The exclusion criteria included: 1) Participants who were under 45 years old at Wave 1 (n = 154); 2) Participants lacking data necessary for calculating NHHR (such as HDL and total cholesterol) at Wave 1 (n = 132); 3) Participants whose blood samples were taken in a non-fasting state (n = 572); 4) Participants without baseline data for prediabetes evaluation (such as self-reported diabetes history, HbA1c, and fasting glucose) at Wave 1 (n = 50); and 5) Participants who had normal or low blood glucose levels or were already diagnosed with diabetes at Wave 1 (n = 3661); and 6) Participants lacking data for prediabetes progression evaluation (such as self-reported diabetes history, HbA1c, and fasting glucose) at Wave 3 (n = 157). Ultimately, 2922 participants were included in this study (Fig. 1).

Data collection and definition

As previously mentioned, data were collected by face-to-face interviews using standardized questionnaires. The questionnaires captured sociodemographic characteristics, including gender, age, educational level, marital status, and place of residence. Lifestyle information, such as alcohol consumption and smoking status, was also recorded. Additionally, participants provided self-reported health data, covering conditions such as hyperlipidemia, hypertension, chronic lung disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, liver disease, heart disease, cancer, and stroke. Biochemical markers, including blood uric acid, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), total cholesterol (TC), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), were measured. These biochemical indicators were assessed using blood samples collected after participants had fasted overnight. The samples were processed and analyzed at a central laboratory in Beijing following standardized procedures. Blood pressure (BP) measurements were obtained by averaging three readings taken while the participant was seated, after a five-minute rest in a quiet environment [17]. Hypertension was identified if the participant had a BP of 140/90 mmHg or higher, self-reported a history of hypertension, or was currently using antihypertensive medication [18]. Obesity was determined by a body mass index of 28 kg/m2 or higher [17].

Calculation of NHHR

Based on the data from Wave 1, the NHHR was calculated using the following formula, which was calculated as NHHR = (TC (mmol/L)-HDL-C (mmol/L))/HDL-C (mmol/L) [9].

Determination of prediabetes and its development

Based on the standards set by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and previous research, this study categorized the development of prediabetes into two outcomes: regression to NFG and progression to diabetes [19–21]. In this study, prediabetes was defined as an HbA1c level of ≥ 5.7 and < 6.5%, or an FBG level of ≥ 5.6 and < 7.0 mmol/L [21]. NFG was defined as an HbA1c level < 5.7% and an FBG level < 5.6 mmol/L [21]. Diabetes was defined as a self-reported history of diabetes, an HbA1c level of ≥ 6.5%, or an FBG level of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L [21].

Statistical analysis

To analyze the development of prediabetes, a one-to-one matching method was used to divide the data for each category into binary datasets [22, 23]. Each dataset categorized participants into five groups based on the quintiles of NHHR values at baseline.

For descriptive statistics, continuous variables are expressed as medians (interquartile range) or means ± standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, while continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

To analyze the association between NHHR and the development of prediabetes, we fitted three logistic regression models to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Specifically: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for gender, age, place of residence, smoking, educational level, marital status, drinking, and obesity; Model 3 further adjusted for hyperlipidemia, stroke, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, heart disease, cancer, and liver disease, as well as diastolic blood pressure, systolic blood pressure, serum uric acid, and hs-CRP. Trend tests were conducted using the median values of each group. To examine the dose–response association between NHHR and the development of prediabetes, we constructed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models with 5 knots. Additionally, we performed stratified analyses to investigate whether the relationship between NHHR and prediabetes development varied by age, gender, obesity, heart disease, or hypertension. We also performed three sensitivity analyses to validate the robustness of our results. First, considering the effect of missing values, we repeated the main analysis after excluding missing values. Second, considering the possible impact of cardiovascular disease and tumors on the results, we repeated the primary analysis after excluding subjects with heart disease, stroke, and cancer at baseline. Third, we repeated the primary analysis after further adjusting for the use of antihypertensive and antihyperlipidemic drugs. Finally, we repeated the primary analysis after further adjusting for HbA1c and FBG.

All statistical analyses were done using R software 4.2.0 and SPSS 26.0. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For missing covariates, we dealt with them employing multiple imputation methods.

Results

Overall study population baseline characteristics by glycemic status during follow-up

During a follow-up period of approximately 3 years, 19.1% of the participants regressed to NFG, while 17.6% progressed to diabetes. Table 2 shows the study population baseline characteristics stratified by glucose status at follow-up. We observed that participants who regressed to NFG were more likely to be male, younger, and have a higher proportion of higher education, with a lower prevalence of heart disease, stroke, obesity, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. In contrast, participants who progressed to diabetes were generally older and more likely to have stroke, hypertension, and heart disease. They also had higher baseline levels of TC, HDL, NHHR, uric acid, hs-CRP, and BP.

The odds ratio for the development of prediabetes

Table 3 presents the results of the logistic regression analysis examining the association between NHHR and the progression of prediabetes. Consistent results were observed across three different models with varying adjustments for covariates. We found that NHHR was negatively associated with the regression of prediabetes to NFG and positively associated with the progression to diabetes. When NHHR was treated as a continuous variable, after adjusting for all confounders, the odds ratio (OR) for regression to NFG was 0.88 (95% CI 0.80–0.98), while the OR for progression to diabetes was 1.28 (95% CI 1.15–1.41). In the analysis grouped by NHHR quintiles, after adjusting for all confounders, the ORs (95% CI) for regression to NFG for Q2, Q3, Q4, and Q5 compared to Q1 were 0.89 (0.67–1.18), 0.77 (0.58–1.03), 0.66 (0.49–0.89), and 0.64 (0.47–0.87), respectively. Similarly, the ORs (95% CI) for progression to diabetes were 1.11 (0.78–1.58), 1.43 (1.02–2.02), 1.70 (1.21–2.39), and 2.01 (1.44–2.83), respectively. RCS regression analyses, conducted both before and after adjusting for covariates, indicated a linear negative association between NHHR and the regression of prediabetes to NFG (Fig. 2). In the unadjusted RCS regression analysis, NHHR shows a nonlinear positive association with the progression of prediabetes to diabetes (Fig. 2). However, after adjusting for covariates, the RCS regression analysis reveals a linear positive association between NHHR and prediabetes developing into diabetes (Fig. 2).

Stratified analysis and sensitivity analyses

The results of the stratified analysis, shown in Fig. 3, indicate no significant interactions between NHHR and the subgroups defined by gender, age, obesity, hypertension, and heart disease. The relationship between NHHR and prediabetes development remained consistent across these subgroups.

Stratified analysis between NHHR and the progression of prediabetes

In the sensitivity analysis, when participants with a history of heart disease, stroke, or cancer at baseline were excluded, 2016 participants (prediabetes and diabetes populations) and 2127 participants (prediabetes and NFG populations) were enrolled in the analyses. The relationship between NHHR and the progression of prediabetes was consistent with the primary analysis after adjusting for all confounders (Table S1). After excluding participants with missing covariate data at baseline, 1980 participants (prediabetes and diabetes populations) and 2020 participants (prediabetes and NFG populations) were enrolled in the analyses. The relationship between NHHR and the progression of prediabetes remained stable after adjusting for all confounders (Table S2). Furthermore, the results remained consistent with the primary analysis after further adjusting for the use of antihypertensive and antihyperlipidemic medications (Table S3). Similarly, further adjustment for HbA1c and FBG levels did not substantially alter the observed associations (Table S4).

Discussion

In this cohort study, we have, for the first time, identified that NHHR significantly influences the development of prediabetes. Specifically, a lower NHHR is associated with a higher likelihood of regression from prediabetes, while a higher NHHR is linked to a greater risk of progression to diabetes. Additionally, subgroup analyses showed that the impact of NHHR on prediabetes development was consistent across different subgroups defined by gender, age, obesity, hypertension, and heart disease. To further validate the robustness of our findings, we conducted three sensitivity analyses, all of which yielded results consistent with the primary analysis. These findings suggest NHHR may serve as a useful clinical indicator, and that monitoring NHHR levels could potentially contribute to diabetes prevention strategies aimed at supporting the transition from prediabetes to normal glucose regulation.

Prediabetes is a state that lies between normal blood glucose levels and diabetes [1]. Most previous studies have concentrated on the progression of prediabetes to diabetes [24, 25]. However, the reversal of prediabetes—i.e., the return to NFG—is equally important, as it is strongly correlated with decreased risk of diabetes and its complications [15, 26]. According to relevant studies, approximately 31% of individuals with prediabetes may revert to NFG within 1.6 years [15]. Notably, the rates of prediabetes reversal and progression are largely dependent on the diagnostic criteria used. Currently, there are five different definitions of prediabetes in clinical practice, and the lack of clear guidelines makes the selection of a preferred definition a challenge in the field [27]. This study adopted the diagnostic criteria set by the ADA and found that, over a follow-up period of about three years, 19.1% of participants reverted to NFG, while 17.6% progressed to diabetes. Additionally, we observed that the subjects who recovered to NFG had a lower prevalence of heart disease, stroke, obesity, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. At baseline, these individuals also had lower levels of HDL-C, TC, uric acid, hs-CRP, and blood pressure. In contrast, participants who progressed to diabetes had higher levels of these health indicators. Therefore, the early identification and timely intervention of risk factors that may lead to disease progression in individuals with prediabetes is of significant clinical importance for promoting the reversal of prediabetes.

NHHR is an emerging lipid marker that has gained recognition for its clinical potential in vascular diseases [9, 28, 29]. For instance, a study involving 930 patients hospitalized for chest pain found that higher baseline NHHR levels were associated with an increased risk of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and coronary artery disease (CAD) [29]. Similarly, a retrospective cohort study of 10,183 Chinese community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults revealed a positive correlation between NHHR and stroke risk [30]. Additionally, the relationship between NHHR and diabetes has been extensively studied [12, 13, 31, 32]. A study of 4882 healthy participants aged 40 and older without diabetes identified NHHR as an independent risk factor for diabetes [32]. Another study involving 15,464 Japanese adults with normal baseline glucose levels indicated that NHHR was superior to traditional lipid markers in predicting diabetes risk [14]. While these studies demonstrate that NHHR, as a marker of metabolic abnormalities, is strongly correlated with the risk of new-onset diabetes, they primarily focus on the general population and overlook the specific subgroup of individuals with prediabetes. These studies have mainly verified that higher NHHR levels may increase the risk of diabetes but have not explored the possibility that lower NHHR levels might reduce this risk.

In this study, we divided the total population into two cohorts based on changes in glucose status during the follow-up period and explored the association between NHHR and the regression of prediabetes to NFG and the relationship between NHHR and the progression of prediabetes to diabetes, respectively. The significance of this study is to further elucidate the effect of NHHR on the development of prediabetes based on existing studies. We further explain that higher NHHR levels may serve as an indicator of unfavorable metabolic conditions associated with the transition from prediabetes to diabetes, while lower NHHR levels may reflect metabolic states more conducive to the return to normal glucose regulation. By comparing the effects of high and low NHHR on individuals with prediabetes, our study offers more comprehensive and robust evidence, enhancing the reliability of the conclusions, which previous studies have not fully addressed.

With the rapid global economic growth and the increasing challenge of an aging population, the rapid rise in prediabetes has become a significant burden for families and societies worldwide [1, 4]. Currently, regulatory authorities have not approved drugs for the treatment of prediabetes [15]. Therefore, lifestyle modifications and risk factor interventions remain the primary means of managing prediabetes [21, 33]. Lipid profiling provides crucial information about lipid metabolism and the health status of individuals, playing a vital role in predicting the risk of various diseases [9, 34]. Given that NHHR is a cost-effective and readily obtainable lipid marker in clinical practice, enhancing health education for middle-aged and older adults, particularly focusing on lipid management in individuals with prediabetes, is of paramount importance. By increasing public awareness of prediabetes and promoting effective lipid management strategies, we can significantly reduce the health and economic burdens associated with prediabetes, thereby bringing long-term benefits to both individuals and society. Improving understanding and adopting proactive measures to manage lipid profiles can help prevent the progression of prediabetes to diabetes, ultimately leading to better health outcomes and a reduced healthcare burden.

Strengths and limitations

The highlights of this study include its status as the first cohort study to investigate the impact of NHHR on the development of prediabetes. Our systematic comparison of prediabetes trajectories across NHHR levels provides robust epidemiological evidence supporting the clinical value of NHHR monitoring. These findings highlight the potential importance of considering NHHR levels when assessing metabolic risk and developing strategies to support glucose normalization in prediabetic individuals. However, several limitations must be considered when interpreting our findings: 1) This study did not collect data from oral glucose tolerance tests, which could have missed some individuals with impaired glucose tolerance [35]. This could potentially underestimate the regression rate to NFG and the incidence of diabetes [35]; 2) The lack of specific timing data for outcomes during follow-up prevented the use of Cox proportional hazards models and Kaplan–Meier (K-M) statistical methods for more in-depth analysis; 3) The study did not collect data on physical activity, which could be an unmeasured confounding factor influencing the results [36]; 4) Due to missing HbA1c and fasting blood glucose data, a large number of participants were excluded, which may have resulted in sampling bias; 5) The low prevalence of subjects when stratified by obesity and heart disease presents a challenge for subgroup analyses and may make it difficult to produce meaningful results; 6) While we adjusted for multiple established confounders, the potential influence of unmeasured or residual confounding (including physical activity patterns, medication history, dietary habits, and socioeconomic status) should be considered when interpreting our findings, as these factors might affect the precision of our effect size estimates. 7) The subjects in this study were mainly community-dwelling middle-aged and elderly individuals in China, so caution is needed in generalizing these findings to other ethnic populations. In summary, while this study has made significant progress in understanding the relationship between NHHR and the development of prediabetes, the above limitations need to be considered when interpreting the results to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the findings.

Conclusions

This study has revealed the significant impact of NHHR on the development of prediabetes in middle-aged and older adults. The findings indicate that lower NHHR levels are associated with a higher likelihood of reversing prediabetes, while higher NHHR levels may accelerate its progression. These results provide novel clinical insights, suggesting that incorporating NHHR into routine monitoring could help identify high-risk individuals earlier and guide personalized intervention strategies, thereby potentially delaying or preventing the onset of diabetes.
