Brain–computer interface–based neurofeedback training enables transferable control of cortical state switching in humans
Authors: Seitaro Iwama, Atsuya Matsuoka, Junichi Ushiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHumans can flexibly control actions. Although closed-loop regulation of sensorimotor activity has been demonstrated since the 1960s, whether these volitional transitions can be rapidly controlled to improve behavior was unknown. Here, we demonstrate that humans can learn to self-regulate sensorimotor rhythms (SMR) using a brain–computer interface to master volitional switching between distinct cortical states defined by SMR amplitude. This learned control was associated with modulated neural dynamics in the sensorimotor network and characterized by rapid, large-scale shifts in periodic and aperiodic brain activity. Critically, the acquired skill generalized to overt motor tasks, significantly shortening reaction times for both muscle contraction and relaxation, suggesting that both reward-based and brain-state–contingent learning mechanisms contribute to intercontext generalization and enable mental practice gains.

Humans possess the remarkable ability to flexibly initiate (1–3), sustain (4, 5), and switch between actions (6–10) in accordance with internal goals or external cues. This behavioral flexibility is thought to rely on neural mechanisms that mediate transitions between distinct cortical states (11). Although such state transitions are thought to mediate everyday motor behavior, evidence has primarily supported the reverse relationship. Thus, it remains unknown whether humans can acquire volitional top-down control over neural dynamics and whether such an ability contributes to behavioral flexibility.

A brain–computer interface (BCI) (12–14) that couples endogenous neural activity with external feedback allows us to test the effects of volitional manipulation of intrinsic neural dynamics on behavior. Because BCI operation can reinforce specific aspects of neural activity, such as firing rates (15), local patterns of population-level activity (16) or interregional coupling (17, 18), it can enhance behavioral performance in tasks that specifically engage these reinforced neural states. For instance, BCI operation modulates local population activity associated with motor cortical excitability, shortens movement reaction time (19, 20) and improves arm control under perturbation (21). The closed-loop experimental paradigms, also referred to as neurofeedback (22), train participants to self-regulate specific neural activity with the aim of improving motor performance (23–25). However, it remains unclear whether participants can learn to rapidly switch motor cortical activity as during overt movement, and how such trained dynamics contribute to behavior. We hypothesized self-regulation of neural dynamics during BCI use can generalize during movement execution and BCI operation engages neural populations that are also recruited during natural behavior at the whole-brain level.

Here, we asked participants to alternate between motor imagery and relaxation states during BCI control and regulate sensorimotor rhythm (SMR) derived from the primary sensorimotor cortex (SM1) (26) based on real-time feedback from BCI (27). We found that participants modulate targeted neural dynamics through training and increase state transitions characterized by distributed whole-brain phase coupling. Furthermore, these neural adaptations generalized to open-loop and overt motor tasks, demonstrating that the dynamic coordination of neural states underlying volitional control is both trainable and transferable.

Results

Volitional Modulation of Cortical States Using BCI Control.

Forty-one participants completed a 2-d BCI-based neurofeedback training (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). During the training, we extracted relative spectral power of scalp electroencephalogram (EEG) derived from SM1 and visualized an on-screen bar whose vertical position on screen showed the modulation magnitude, with the top indicating power decrease and the bottom indicating power increase. Since SMR derived from SM1 has been thought to reflect the corticothalamic activity (28) and its magnitude is associated with increased SM1 excitability (29), we instructed participants to attempt to move the bar to a target zone, top during motor imagery, bottom during relaxation without overt movement (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). The task alternated when participants successfully reached the required brain state, as determined by the online calculated EEG spectral power at the individual alpha frequency (IAF), a prominent spectral peak around 8 to 13 Hz derived from SM1 (30).

The participants were allocated to one of two groups: the experimental group (Real, N = 19), in which participants observed the bar height linked with their own scalp EEG, and the yoked-sham group (Yoked, N = 22), in which the bar with a height derived from the prerecorded data acquired was shown during the same task. Group allocation was randomized and double-blinded throughout the study.

The participants from both groups exhibited typical EEG spectral power profiles throughout the experiment (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C–G). We then tested whether its volitional controllability changed through training (Fig. 1A). Specifically, we quantified the total number of instruction changes within a block, which indicates the overall success rate of prosthetic control (16). These counts were subjected to three-way rmANOVA with two within-participant factors (block and day) and a between-participant factor (group). The real group data represented actual experimental events, whereas the yoked-sham group data were recalculated with recorded EEG in a pseudo-online manner. We found a significant interaction between day and group, with a significant increase in the number of instruction changes on day 2 compared with that on day 1 in the real group indicating that veritable closed-loop training improved prosthetic control (Fig. 1B; rmANOVA, F(5,195) = 4.96, P = 0.032, ηp2 = 0.11; post hoc analysis, pairwise t test with Bonferroni correction, t(18) = −3.41, P = 0.009, d = −0.60, see SI Appendix, Table S1 for the full statistical results).

Since switching events occur when the spectral power is modulated as intended, we examined event-related spectral power (ERSP) modulation at the IAF and in the beta band (14 to 30 Hz). In the real group, significant spectral power changes were localized around the targeted primary sensorimotor areas (Corrected-P < 0.05, Fig. 1C), indicating that instruction switching primarily involves neural populations targeted by the BCI paradigm. Time–frequency representations derived from the contralateral sensorimotor regions showed rapid spectral power attenuation at the IAF in the motor imagery state (Fig. 1D), whereas the modulation relative to the instruction alternation was delayed in the relaxation state, as suggested by the significant power attenuation at the onset of relaxation (Fig. 1E).

Learning-related increases in acquired scores between days 1 and 2 were mainly associated with successful performance in the motor imagery task in the real group (Fig. 1F, F (1,39) = 37.93, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.5; t(18) = −6.6, P < 0.001, d = −3.15). However, smaller but significant point increases between days were also observed in the relaxation state in the real group (Fig. 2G, F (1,39) = 23.7, p< 0.001, ηp2 = 0.42, t(18) = −5.6, P < 0.001, d = −2.52). Collectively, these results suggest that state transitions between desynchronized and synchronized states in SM1, associated with the motor imagery and relaxation are induced by volitional switching of the target during BCI control.

High-Amplitude Sensorimotor Oscillation Drives Improvement in BCI Control.

Learning of spectral power modulation during BCI-based neurofeedback indicates that the oscillatory dynamics in the sensorimotor network were reorganized to produce the required neural dynamics. We then focused on transient sensorimotor oscillatory activity in the alpha-band, that was used for real-time feedback (Fig. 2A). Since such bursty events contribute to the BCI control during the relaxation period, where higher power is required, three burst metrics, burst probability, duration, and intensity were calculated. These metrics reflect independent aspects of burst events contributing to the BCI task; probability is related to the frequency of bursty events (31, 32), duration represents the prolonged or shortened events (33, 34), and intensity indicates instantaneous synchronization of the neural population (35, 36). Between-group differences in those derived from the targeted left M1 indicate the increased bursty activity in the real group (P < 0.05, Fig. 2B). Further, whole-brain analyses of burst characteristics were exploratorily conducted using signals derived from 27 cortical parcels covering both hemispheres using cortical source estimation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Regions exhibiting significant interaction effects between day and group were visualized via linear mixed-effects models (P < 0.01) for the three metrics (Fig. 2C). The significant interaction effects indicating the training-induced increase in the burst probability and duration in the real group were localized around sensorimotor areas, including the primary sensorimotor cortices, premotor cortex, and medial frontal regions labeled as supplementary motor areas (SMA).

Since BCI control required participants to self-modulate neural signals originating from their left SM1 region, we hypothesized that changes in burst profiles in these regions would lead to performance improvements. Three-way mixed rmANOVA for the data in the left M1 considering block, day and group effects (Fig. 2D) revealed significant interactions among these three factors for burst duration but not burst probability or intensity (Probability: F(5,195) = 2.66, P = 0.051, ηp2 = 0.07; Duration: F(5,195) = 2.99, P = 0.029, ηp2 = 0.07; Intensity: F(5,195) = 1.19, P = 0.32, ηp2 = 0.03). Post hoc analysis revealed significant increases in burst duration in block 6 compared with that in block 1 on day 1 in only the real group in the relaxation period (t(18) = −4.68, P < 0.001, d = 0.94). The results indicate volitionally increased IAF power during relaxation period was achieved by the self-induction of sustained alpha oscillation indexed by burst duration rather than the increase in its instantaneous amplitude.

To investigate the BCI-based neurofeedback training effects on the sensorimotor network, we tested if the learning modulates the burst metrics in the primary sensory cortex (S1) and SMA thought to be related to BCI control (37, 38). In the left S1, the burst probability and duration in the relaxation state significantly increased between days 1 and 2 in the real group (Probability: t(18) = −2.83, P = 0.044, d = −0.69; Duration: t(18) = −2.78, P = 0.05, d = −0.67). In the SMA, the burst probability in the relaxation state significantly increased in block 6 compared with that in block 1 in the real group (t(18) = −4.53, P < 0.001, d = −0.59; t(18) = −4.37, P = 0.001, d = −0.57). Additionally, the point acquisition during each relaxation block was significantly correlated with the burst characteristics across all three regions only in the real group (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

Together, these findings suggest that BCI control training involves the self-modulation of oscillatory activity distributed in sensorimotor areas. Moreover, systematic learning-related changes in oscillatory burst rates were only observed in the real group, indicating that active modulation of neural activity through repetitive BCI control is required for these effects to emerge.

Instruction Switching Engages Large-Scale, Spectrally Structured State Transitions.

Having established that targeted neural dynamics were achieved by the regional oscillatory activity, we next sought event-related whole-brain activity patterns associated with volitional state control of SMR. To do this, we used a hidden Markov model (HMM)-based approach to identify state transitions in an unsupervised manner (39) (Fig. 3A). This approach enables the data-driven identification of switching-related neural dynamics given a predetermined number of states and multiple independent HMM runs robustly converged to a stable and reproducible result (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). We set the number as 12 in the present study while the HMM parameter selection does not impact the key findings (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The identified states were consistently observed across participants in both groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A), indicating that brain state transition dynamics found by HMM are shared across individuals.

First, we identified states that were significantly modulated by instruction switching to visualize the event-related responses associated with whole-brain activity. To achieve this, we segmented the temporal sequences of the inferred states and analyzed event-related state changes. Our analysis revealed that the probability of visiting certain states in the HMM was high only for a subset of states with the large effect sizes, irrespective of the direction of instruction switching (Fig. 3B). The specificity, the likelihood of finding each HMM state active during 500 ms before and after the transition was compared (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). A permutation test confirmed significant differences in specificity before and after the transition for states 6 and 12 (P < 0.001), and other significant states were less visited compared to the state 6 and 12 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C), indicating that these states were specifically engaged during instruction switching in both directions. Moreover, the identified neural dynamics were strengthened through training (Fig. 3C, Mixed rmANOVA, interactions of day and group: F(1,38) = 4.10, P = 0.05, ηp2 = 0.10; Post hoc t test, Day 1 vs. Day 2 in real group: t(17) = 3.24, P = 0.01, d = 0.67) while baseline was comparable between groups (t(38) = −1.8, P = 0.46). These results suggest that state transition dynamics related to instruction switching are shared between the two transition directions and are enhanced through real BCI training.

Next, we investigated the spectral characteristics of key states during instruction changes. For each HMM state, the spectral density of the source signals from each cortical region defined in the atlas space was computed (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). The obtained power spectra were then parameterized by the spectral slope and IAF peak (Fig. 3D). The two parameters represent the broadband and frequency-specific spectral characteristics. The aperiodic slope is thought to be associated with balance between excitatory and inhibitory neural activity, based on their differential contributions to high and low frequency power (40). Meanwhile the periodic peak modulation reflects neural synchronization within specific frequency, thus the operation enables comparison of the spectrally resolved brain states.

We found that the two states whose probability prominently decreased and increased during instruction changes (i.e., states 6 and 12) were characterized by significant changes in both periodic components in the sensorimotor areas and broadband aperiodic slopes in the frontal areas. In state 6, which occurred less frequently after instruction change, significantly greater spectral power at the IAF and a larger aperiodic exponent were observed compared with those in state 12, which occurred more frequently after instruction change (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A–C). This indicates that instruction change leads to attenuation of the periodic component at the IAF and a steeper spectral slope (i.e., an increase in the aperiodic exponent). These findings suggest that switching mental tasks for BCI control involves rapid modulation of both broadband and periodic spectral power at the whole-brain level, leading to transient attenuation in the target frequency band.

Furthermore, to investigate interregional communication during oscillatory spectral power control throughout training, we examined whole-brain phase coupling (i.e., coherence) during each HMM state as demonstrated in the resting-state data (39). In addition to states 6 and 12 being identified key states during instruction switching, statistically significant phase coupling at the IAF was observed in these two states (SI Appendix, Fig. S9D, P < 0.01, FDR-corrected). A comparison between the two states revealed strong differences in phase coupling in frontocentral regions within the alpha and beta bands (Fig. 3F). In summary, whole-brain neural dynamics respond to instruction switching by rapidly increasing both local population activity and their interregional coupling, regardless of the target SMR magnitude.

Temporal Dynamics of Trained Motor Cortex Activities Are Spectrally Represented in the Medial Frontal Areas.

To test whether the voluntary control of motor cortex activity learned during the BCI task generalizes to scenarios without feedback, participants underwent evaluation blocks before and after each training session. During these evaluation blocks, only task instructions (i.e., imagery or relaxation) were presented, without real-time feedback. Over a 20-s period, the participants alternated between motor imagery and relaxation trials every 5 s, completing four intervals in total. The task instructions and timing were identical for both groups. Each block consisted of 10 trials, with participants blinded to their neural modulation success rate (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B).

To examine whether the key HMM-defined neural dynamics were preserved during the evaluation blocks, we tested whether rapid state transitions were associated with changes in task instructions, as observed during the training blocks (SI Appendix, Independent HMM Inference Across Task Datasets). An independent HMM analysis of the evaluation block data revealed preserved state transition dynamics and their associated spatiotemporal and spectral characteristics (Fig. 4 A and B). Further, the HMM-defined state transition dynamics were enhanced through BCI training only in the real group (Fig. 4C, Mixed rmANOVA, interaction of day and group: F(1,39) = 4.48, P = 0.041, ηp2 = 0.10; Post hoc t test, Day 1 vs. Day 2 in real group: t(18) = 3.48, P = 0.008, d = 0.79). The results suggest that switching-related suppression of high-amplitude sensorimotor activity may have been acquired through training. Moreover, the sustained amplitude modulation in the sensorimotor areas was also confirmed by the general linear model approach (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 and Supporting Information Text). Collectively, the self-modulation of sensorimotor neural dynamics was acquired in both groups while the training-induced enhancement was only found in the real group in the sensorimotor area activated during BCI control.

Direct Switching Control of Motor Cortex Activity Improves Behavioral Performance.

Over the 2 d of prosthesis control training, participants deliberately alternated motor imagery and relaxation to optimize control of sensorimotor neural states defined by SMR magnitude. To determine whether the learned self-regulation of neural dynamics in the motor cortex supports actual motor performance, we designed a modified simple reaction time task.

During this task, the bar height was linked to the rectified amplitude of electromyogram (EMG) signals from the right extensor muscle. The participants were instructed to execute a rapid muscle contraction as soon as a go cue appeared and to maintain the output above a target line. After 3 ± 1 s, the target line was removed, and the participants were instructed to relax their muscle as quickly as possible to set the bar height back to the baseline.

First, we tested if the neural state switching trained during BCI tasks was generalized during the motor task. As found in the data during the evaluation blocks, another independent HMM analysis of the EEG data during the motor task revealed preserved state transition dynamics and their associated spatiotemporal and spectral characteristics (Fig. 4 D and E). Moreover, for the temporal dynamics, we found a significant interaction of day and group effect for the difference in the fractional occupancy of the state 6, suppressed by the instruction change (Fig. 4F, Mixed rmANOVA, interaction of day and group: F(1,39) = 4.54, P = 0.039, ηp2 = 0.10).

In addition to the changes in the neural state dynamics, the reaction times for motor initiation and relaxation on days 1 and 2 were analyzed using the onset and offset of the EMG signals. Mixed rmANOVA revealed a significant interaction of day and group for the reaction times in both the movement onset and offset tasks (Fig. 4G, F (1,39) = 5.05, P = 0.03, ηp2 = 0.13; Fig. 4H, F (1,39) = 4.46, P = 0.04, ηp2 = 0.11). Post hoc analysis revealed significant differences in the reaction times in both the movement onset and offset tasks on days 1 and 2 in only the real group (Onset: t(18) = 3.54, P = 0.007, d = 0.91; Offset: t(18) = 3.58, P = 0.006, d = 0.81). Moreover, changes in the reaction time for onset was correlated with those in the BCI performance (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), and veritable feedback can enhance sustained task-related amplitude modulation depth in the left medial frontal cortex at IAF (Fig. 4I, mixed rmANOVA, P < 0.01, t(39) = 2.97, P = 0.005, d = 0.93). Collectively, these results suggest that the observed enhancement of task-related cortical dynamics during BCI was generalized during motor execution and contributed to the volitional motor output control, providing evidence of shared representation in the large-scale sensorimotor network involved in both mental rehearsal and actual motor execution.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether humans could learn volitional control over EEG sensorimotor activity and modulate cortical state transitions through BCI-based neurofeedback training, and whether this learned ability generalizes to other contexts and leads motor performance improvement. The training-induced reorganization of neural dynamics was evidenced by changes in alpha burst dynamics and increased volitional state transitions characterized by spectral shifts and interregional coupling in the sensorimotor network. The participants demonstrated acquired neural dynamics in both covert and overt motor task contexts, along with improved motor control performance during rapid muscle contraction and relaxation. These findings highlight a shared neural mechanism underlying volitional control over cortical states and the enhancement of motor behaviors through the neural reorganization.

We showed that BCI-based training for volitional control of endogenous neural activity involves neural populations that generates cortical dynamics during naturalistic behavior. While a number of studies employ the self-regulation training of alpha-band activity including SMR and showed human ability to control them through real-time feedback (19, 37), what remains unclear was whether humans can control the dynamics of targeted oscillations within a short time window as instructed. Our results indicate that the top–down control mechanism over the target neural feature can flexibly alter oscillatory dynamics, and extend previous demonstrations that training participants in volitional control of motor cortical activity improves reaction times (23) by demonstrating that rapid action switching can be trained.

The training employed real-time feedback of spectral power at IAF, which can originate from visual, parietal, and sensorimotor areas while the spatial distribution of task-related power change was localized around SM1. Since SMR is thought to originate from the motor-related circuit composed of basal ganglia, thalamus, and motor cortices (41, 42), the rapid control of its dynamics is supported by the frontal inhibitory control during action cancellation (8, 31) and reprogramming (43). Indeed, the HMM analysis revealed that frontal aperiodic activity and interregional coupling showed significant modulation at the moment of instruction Such whole-brain activity change mediate the BCI-based control of external objects and also support flexible motor control.

The training for repetitive cortical state transitions employed in the present study allows us to test whether the alternation of mental tasks (i.e., motor imagery and relaxation) can be linked with specific neural activities. Based on this sham-controlled experiment, we posit that the nonspecific effect of the feedback paradigm does not fully explain the increase in the number of state transitions. Notably, the key neural signatures underlying the state transitions were shared across both directions of transition; sensorimotor areas exhibit common ERSP attenuation when the target alternates. Consequently, reaching the target during the relaxation period which was associated with increased SMR magnitude was more difficult compared to the imagery period (Fig. 1G). Such excitatory activity during muscle relaxation has been reported during finger movement (44) while muscle relaxation increases intracortical inhibitory activities in M1 (44). Such bidirectional modulation of cortical excitability would be due to the transiently desynchronized activity in M1. These neural dynamics during naturalistic behavior are shared with those covertly simulated during BCI operation, and the observed learning may reflect an interaction between feedback contingent on the motor cortical activity and more general reward-based learning processes. Further studies investigating whether alternative feedback of task-irrelevant activity can be generalized to motor contexts are warranted.

The spatial distribution of aperiodic exponent during key HMM states showed that neural structures involved during BCI-based volitional state transitions are shared with those during cognitive and motor tasks. Aperiodic exponents reflect the steepness of broadband activity in the frequency space. Although multiple neural mechanisms, such as activation of the arousal system or neuromodulatory input, can influence the broadband activity, recent noninvasive neurophysiological analyses also suggest that the aperiodic exponent indicates the temporal dynamics of the excitatory and inhibitory balance in regions of interest (40, 45). For example, allocating attention to a specific modality introduces a more excitatory state in the associated sensory cortex, leading to a decrease in the aperiodic exponent (46). Given that the rapid cancellation of planned movement transiently increases frontal activities (47), one possible explanation for increase in the aperiodic exponent (i.e., steeper slope) after instruction changes is the reflection of temporally and endogenously induced potentiation of inhibitory activity by top–down control of cortical dynamics (48, 49). Collectively, these findings suggest that once volitional toggling of the cortical state occurs, the brain responds by making the cortical state less oscillatory, with dominant inhibitory activity.

Although the mechanism of motor performance improvement through covert control of regional activities has been explained from a dynamical perspective (50, 51), it has been unclear to what extent the covert process recapitulates large-scale neural dynamics and how they generalize during different contexts. In this study, we showed the covert neural state transitions trained by BCI involves modulation of bursty activities in the sensorimotor area, and the characteristic responses are also found in the posttraining open-loop and motor task blocks. This corroborates the possible mechanism of neurofeedback that the large-scale sensorimotor network that produces coordinated activity is simulated through mental rehearsal (21, 26). The experimental group-specific performance improvement during rapid contraction and relaxation of performance indicates these acquired neural dynamics through the combination of BCI operation and mental practice. Although the questionnaire responses about agency and arousal did not provide sufficient evidence for a between-group difference (SI Appendix, Fig. S13), participants in the yoked group may have experienced frustration or a reduced sense of agency, which could have influenced their behavioral performance. Further studies investigating the potential detrimental effects of yoked-sham feedback are warranted.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that volitional regulation of neural activity using a BCI can induce the required neural dynamics by bidirectionally alternating excitatory and inhibitory cortical states. The generalizability of the learned control strategy and the transition-related neural responses suggest that this prosthetic control paradigm, combined with explicit motor imagery strategies, simulates the whole-brain neural dynamics involved in actual motor behavior. These results provide neurophysiological evidence supporting the efficacy of motor imagery training for improving performance in domains such as sports, rehabilitation, and musical instrument practice.

Materials and Methods

Study Design.

We designed this parallel-group, double-blind, randomized, sham-controlled experiment, following the CONSORT 2010 checklist (52) for parallel group randomized trials and the consensus on the reporting and experimental design of clinical and cognitive-behavioral neurofeedback studies (53) (the CRED-NF checklist is included in SI Appendix). The intervention period was scheduled for 2 d on the basis of a preliminary experiment and previous neurofeedback studies (20, 54–56). The study was approved by the local ethics committee of Keio University (IRB approval number: 2022-120) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained at the beginning of the experiment.

Participants.

We recruited 44 participants without a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders through a website. The sample size was determined on the basis of the observed effect size in the preliminary experiment for differences in the learning rate of BCI control between the real and yoked-sham groups (d = 0.69). A priori power analysis was conducted using G*power 3.1 with α = 0.05 and (1 − β) = 0.8. This analysis indicated that 19 participants per group were needed, and the sample size was comparable with that of other noninvasive BCI and neurofeedback studies (20, 56–58).

All the participants were right-handed with normal or corrected-to-normal vision and were compensated for their time (1,100 JPY per hour). Data were acquired from a total of 44 participants. After data from three participants were rejected due to data errors caused by the experiment program, the data of 41 participants (real group: 19 participants, average age = 21.9 ± 2.1 y old, 5 females; yoked-sham group: 22 participants, average age = 21.7 ± 2.2 y old, 9 females) were used for analyses. The data used for the sham feedback in the yoked group were randomly selected from the participants in the preliminary or main experiment.

Experimental Setup.

We recorded high-density scalp EEG signals with an Electrical Geodesic system with a 128-channel HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net (GES 400, Electrical Geodesics, Inc.) at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz. The ground and reference electrodes for the EEG signals were CPz and Cz. Electrode impedance levels were monitored throughout the neurofeedback training and kept below 50 kΩ. In addition, surface EMG signals were acquired from the right extensor digitorum communis (EDC) using Physio 16 (Electrical Geodesics, Inc.) at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz. We used a 24-inch monitor placed in front of the participants and a custom-made motor-driven finger orthosis (59) attached to the right forearm of the participants to provide task instructions and real-time feedback. An electrical stimulator was attached to the finger orthosis (Stimulation frequency: 100 Hz, Pulse width: 1 ms, Biphasic).

Study Protocol.

The 2-d experiment was conducted either on 2 consecutive days or with a 1-d interval between sessions. Of the 41 participants, 37 completed the experiment on consecutive days, while the remaining 4 did with a 1-d interval (1 Real, 3 Yoked). The participants were pseudorandomly assigned to one of two groups, the real or sham group, after the first block (pre-evaluation) on day 1. The two groups underwent the same experimental procedure and received the same instructions but different types of feedback during training blocks: The real group received feedback on the basis of the participant’s real-time scalp EEG signals, and the sham group received feedback on the basis of EEG signals previously measured from other participants (i.e., yoked-sham feedback (19, 53, 56, 60–62); see Online Signal Processing). The group allocation was based on the strength of the resting-state IAF power in the pre-evaluation block, which was determined via a custom script. We identified IAF from the resting-state EEG derived from the electrode around SM1 (i.e., C3 channel). The across-day stability of IAF was confirmed (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). The researchers, experimenters, and participants were blinded to the group allocations throughout the experiment. At the end of each experiment, participants completed questionnaires assessing agency and sleepiness (SI Appendix, Fig. S13).

A session started with a motor task block in which participants underwent a modified version of a simple reaction time task, followed by a 3-min EEG acquisition in the resting state. The motor task block was followed by an open-loop block, in which participants performed ten trials of alternating motor imagery and relaxation tasks. A trial in the pre-evaluation block started with a 5-s rest period, followed by 1-s ready, 20-s task, and 6-s interval periods. During the task period, the participants performed kinesthetic motor imagery and relaxation tasks, alternating every 5 s, prompted by visual instruction. The imagery content was specified as the participants opening their right hand, and during the relaxation task, they were instructed to relax the muscles of their right fingers. The task order, duration, and number of trials in the training blocks and the postevaluation block were identical to those in the pre-evaluation block.

Online Signal Processing.

During the training blocks, EEG signals over the left sensorimotor cortex, derived from the C3 channel in the international 10 to 20 system, were processed to estimate and visualize cortical excitability (19, 20, 56, 60, 63). Specifically, we applied the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) to calculate the spectral power in the alpha band on the basis of the spatial and bandpass-filtered data. First, we used the Laplacian montage, rereferenced the data using the data of six EEG channels around C3, and bandpass and notch filtered the data (a fourth-order Butterworth filter with cutoff frequencies of 3 and 40 Hz for the bandpass filter and 50 Hz for the notch filter). We then used the previous 1-s of data to calculate the spectral power with the Hanning window function.

To estimate the ERSP modulation, we calculated the ERSP values, which indicate the relative spectral power change compared with the baseline (i.e., the rest period), via the following equation:ERSPf,t,%=Af,t-R(f)R(f)×100,

where f,t,A, and R represent the frequency, time, power, and reference power, respectively. Positive ERSP values indicate increased spectral power compared with the baseline (i.e., reference power).

The bar shown during the task period reflects the ERSP value at the IAF ± 1 Hz, which was moving-averaged using the data collected in the previous 1 s, which has the opposite sign (i.e., the bar indicates attenuated spectral power). The bar height was refreshed every 100 ms, and the participants aimed to reach the target height indicated by a green dashed line. For the feedback in the sham group, the identical calculation procedure was conducted for the prerecorded EEG data.

When the target was successfully reached during the motor imagery period, the motor-driven orthosis and neuromuscular electrical stimulation applied over the right EDC muscle were triggered. At this time, the instructions alternated from the motor imagery to the relaxation task or vice versa. The target value was individually determined from the ERSP distribution during the open-loop block at pre-evaluation, specifically the third quartile for the target during the motor imagery task and 0% during the relaxation task. The number of times that the participant reached the target was used as a metric to evaluate BCI control performance. For the sham group data, we recalculated the points using their own EEG signals in a pseudo-online manner. Note that the number of instruction changes was comparable between groups, as we balanced the total number of instruction changes in the two groups by employing yoked-sham feedback (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). All online processing was conducted in MATLAB 2020b.

Data Preprocessing for Offline analysis.

For the offline analysis of the EEG signals, we first reconstructed the cortical source using the sLORETA algorithm (64) implemented in the Brainstorm toolbox (65). Before source reconstruction, the quality of the EEG signals was assessed in the sensor space via the specparam algorithm (66). Channels whose aperiodic exponent showed negative values were rejected from the further analysis (67). We excluded 23.1 ± 29 channels from each participant (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). We did not find significant between-group differences in the number of excluded channels (Two-sample t test, P = 0.17).

ERSP Analysis.

To analyze the time-frequency representation of the ERSP, we calculated the switch-related response in the cortical areas defined in the standard brain atlas (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) using the STFT of the EEG signals collected from the left M1. To analyze the spatial distribution of the ERSP at the IAF and in the beta band, we employed the Hilbert transformation and calculated the switch-related response in the cortical areas. The computed spectral power was normalized using the spectral power in the 5-s rest period and subjected to a one-sample t test.

Oscillatory Burst Detection.

To characterize the temporal burst activities during BCI control, we employed the fBOSC algorithm (68). The fBOSC algorithm was designed to capture transient frequency-specific oscillations using a combination of the specparam algorithm and the wavelet transform. The amplitude threshold was determined by multiplying the aperiodic component identified by the specparam algorithm by the probability density of the chi-square distribution (P = 0.90). For the duration threshold, we used six cycles to detect sustained sensorimotor oscillations without confounding them with noise or brief nonoscillatory events.

On the basis of the spectrally resolved bursts detected by the fBOSC algorithm, we computed the burst probability (proportion of time during which a burst occurred in a window of interest), burst duration (cumulative sum of the time points when a burst occurred) and intensity (the average power during a burst). These metrics were computed in a trial-by-trial manner and subjected to statistical analysis.

TDE HMM.

To analyze whole-brain oscillatory dynamics during BCI control, whole-brain activities were modeled via HMMs. To construct the input data, the orthogonalized regional source activities were downsampled to 100 Hz and TDE (time window: 60 ms), and the dipole ambiguity was resolved (39). The TDE matrix was subjected to principal component analysis (PCA), and 54 components were used as the input to the TDE-HMM so that the twice of the original number of regions contribute to the input data. The HMM inference process was based on the HMM-MAR MATLAB toolbox (https://github.com/OHBA-analysis/HMM-MAR). The methodological details are reported in previous work (39, 69, 70). Briefly, the algorithm is used to infer a hidden sequence of a predefined number of mutually exclusive states under the assumption that the observable neural signals are sampled from a probabilistic observation model. Through the HMM inference process, the temporal (sequence of state transitions) and spatiospectral (the regional power spectra and interregional coupling during each state) information is iteratively optimized via a stochastic variational inference process.

According to the TDE-HMM results, the transition-related state change was compared with the null distribution derived by randomizing the timing of the transition. The states for which the probability was significantly modulated before and after the transition were identified. In addition, the specificity of each state, the likelihood of finding each HMM state active, was calculated and compared before and after the transition via paired t tests with Bonferroni correction. Delta fractional occupancy was calculated using the difference between before and after the transition using average of 100 ms from switch onsets. The spatiospectral representation of each state was visualized using the spectral parameters derived by the specparam algorithm applied to the average power spectral density (PSD) data for each state. After calculating the whole-brain PSD for all states, nonparametric permutation tests were conducted to compare the strength of the power and phase coupling across states. Statewise comparisons of the IAF peak power and aperiodic exponent were conducted for the switch-related changes in the states.

Modified Simple Reaction Time Task.

To test whether participants trained to control the neuroprosthesis by repeatedly manipulating the sensorimotor representation can perform rapid and smooth motor actions after training, we quantified the muscular response during a simple reaction time task modified to measure both the onset and offset of the motor output (19, 71). This task involves performing voluntary contractions of the right EDC muscle in response to a visual cue, and to rapidly decrease the output in response to a second cue. The onset cue was the appearance of a target bar on the screen, and the offset cue was its disappearance. Each trial consisted of a 3 ± 1 s resting period, a 3 ± 2 s contraction period, and a 3 ± 2 s relaxation period. We quantified both the onset and offset of the motor output via the EMG signals derived from the right EDC using the earliest time point that exceeds or below the mean + 3 SD of rectified amplitude during the rest period. The participants performed 10 trials before and after the training for each day. On the first day of the experiment, a habituation block composed of 10 trials was additionally performed.

Statistical Tests.

Statistical tests were conducted using JASP (72) and MATLAB. The EEG-PSD, spectral power, periodic peak, and aperiodic exponent were subjected to rmANOVA considering a within-participant (condition) and a between-participant (group) factor. The significance threshold of the post hoc pairwise t tests was corrected for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction.

To quantify the learned ability and performance in the BCI control and modified simple reaction time tasks, we employed rmANOVA considering two within-participant factors (block and day) and one between-participant factor (group) to test the interaction of these factors. The regional burst profiles during training trials were analyzed via linear mixed-effects models considering day and group as factors and participant as random effect. The significance threshold was set as P < 0.01 for the exploratory whole-brain analysis. False discovery rate correction for multiple comparisons was used for the HMM-based phase coupling analysis (28). The relationships between the acquired points during the relaxation task and the burst profiles were tested using repeated-measures correlation tests (73). For questionnaires acquired during the experiment, the mode values for each participant were used for the between-group comparisons using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (SI Appendix, Fig. S13).
