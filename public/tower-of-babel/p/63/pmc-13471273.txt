Implementation of a Simulation-Based Cast Saw Technique Educational Module to Improve Cast Removal Safety
Authors: Nithya Lingampalli, Elizabeth Cho, Nicolas Jozefowski, Jeffrey Liles, Teresa Cappello, Alexander Kuzma, Douglas Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the increasing operative demands in orthopaedic residency, the teaching of fundamental nonoperative skills—such as safe cast removal—remains insufficient. Improper cast saw technique can result in burns, abrasions, scarring, infection, and significant medicolegal and healthcare burdens. Although formal cast saw training programs improve safety, they often require substantial personnel and financial resources, limiting widespread implementation. A validated wax-based simulation model developed by our group offers a cost-effective, reproducible method for assessing cast saw safety. This study evaluated the effectiveness of a structured cast saw training module using this simulation model in novice learners.MethodsA total of 42 medical students without prior cast saw experience participated. Each completed cast removal on a cylindrical wax model before and after a standardized educational module. Models were casted with a consistent technique and mounted on a custom apparatus providing four discrete cutting lanes. After each session, investigators removed the cast material and quantified cast saw-related errors, including number, total length, maximum depth, and maximum width of wax deformities. Preinstruction and postinstruction results were compared within participants.ResultsParticipants demonstrated significant improvement after completing the educational module. Average errors per block decreased from 9.02 to 3.78; total error length decreased from 154.51 mm to 41.49 mm; maximum error depth decreased from 1.61 mm to 0.37 mm; and maximum error width decreased from 56.02 mm to 16.31 mm. All improvements were statistically significant.DiscussionA brief, structured cast saw training session using a validated wax simulation model markedly reduced both the frequency and severity of cast removal errors in novice learners. This simulation-based approach provides a standardized, low-cost alternative to traditional training methods, which are often resource-intensive and inconsistently implemented.ConclusionsThe proposed cast saw educational module improves cast removal safety and represents a cost-effective, time-efficient, scalable training tool suitable for orthopaedic residency programs and healthcare systems seeking to reduce cast-related complications.Key Concepts(1)Simulation-Based Cast Saw Training: A validated wax-model simulation provides an effective, low-cost platform for teaching safe cast removal techniques.(2)Improved Safety Through Education: A brief, structured educational module significantly reduces cast saw–related errors among novice learners.(3)Standardization of Nonoperative Skills: Formalizing cast removal instruction addresses a critical gap in orthopaedic training, where non-operative skills often receive less emphasis.(4)Risk Reduction and Quality Improvement: Enhanced cast saw proficiency may decrease patient complications, medicolegal events, and healthcare utilization.(5)Feasibility and Scalability: The wax-based training model is reproducible, time-efficient, and easily implemented across orthopaedic training programs.Level of EvidenceLevel II

Introduction

Considering the current volume and complexity of orthopaedic procedures, the demand for operative skills in orthopaedic surgery residency is increasing, while the teaching of nonoperative orthopaedic treatment skills, such as cast removal, often receives less emphasis. This contributes to an unfortunate disparity when considering the high numbers of casted pediatric fractures that come through orthopaedic clinics [1,2]. Inexperienced cast removal with a cast saw can lead to severe skin complications, such as burns or abrasions, permanent scars, and infections, leading to increased utilization of healthcare resources for these injuries, as well as the possibility of medicolegal action [3,4].

The previously mentioned risks associated with improper cast removal technique can be minimized through proper training [[5], [6], [7]]. In fact, a prior study that yielded a >90% reduction in cast saw complications found that the most important intervention was the development of a resident casting education program as part of a larger quality-improvement effort [8]. Recognizing this need, multiple national organizations, including the American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery Resident Skills have recommended that orthopaedic residency programs develop and implement formal cast saw training programs [9]. Unfortunately, these programs necessitated significant resources and monetary contributions from multiple departments (quality improvement and orthopaedics), and committed personnel, including multiple attending orthopaedic surgeons, cast technicians, and allied health professionals. From a practical standpoint, most orthopaedic departments are unlikely to be able to commit such resources. As such, the current paradigm of cast saw training among trainees continues to follow the “see one, do one, teach one” adage, where trainees may be handling a cast saw for the first time for use on other trainees or patients. However, this kind of hands-on training is not standardized or validated, nor is it without risk.

Further efforts to reduce the incidence of cast-related complications might focus on simulation-based educational programs that are more feasible to implement. One study evaluating the cost-benefit of simulation-based instruction on cast saw removal found an anticipated savings from averted cast-saw injury and associated medicolegal payments of $27,131, representing an 11-to-1 return on investment [10]. Members of our research team have developed and published results on a novel, validated method for evaluating cast saw safety utilizing cylindrical wax models that is cost-effective, time-efficient, and easily reproducible [11]. Our study utilized this model to measure the utility of a formal cast saw training module, as well as to further investigate the use of this wax-based simulation model for training and evaluating proper cast removal technique in novice learners.

Materials and methods

A total of 42 medical students in training were recruited on a voluntary basis from the Loyola Stritch School of Medicine student body and self-reported no prior cast saw experience or formal training. Inclusion was based on outreach to the medical school body over 1 year.

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the primary authors’ institution. A grant from the Loyola York Foundation was used to purchase cylindrical machinable casting wax models (MachinableWax.come, Traverse City, MI). All materials and methods of the simulation used to objectively analyze cast saw performance mirrored the specific protocol and set-up of the wax-based cast saw simulation model described in our previous work [11]. Briefly, casted cylindrical wax models (meant to simulate the forearm) were mounted on a custom-designed wooden apparatus to stabilize them. The wax models were first inspected and all indentations were marked with a black permanent marker to ensure that these minor indentations were correctly categorized as due to the manufacturing of the model and not due to cast saw injury. The wax models were all cast by Nithya Lingapalli (NL) to ensure model consistency using the following protocol technique: 4 overlapping layers of 3-inch cast padding (Webril) followed by 4 layers of 3-inch fiberglass rolls (Delta-Lite Plus). Care was taken to ensure ∼50% overlap of each layer of cast padding and fiberglass. The casts were allowed to dry for a minimum of 7 days to ensure complete drying and provide a more clinically relevant timeline before cast removal. The cost of each model, including the wax and cast material, was $30.

The study consisted of two phases for participants to demonstrate cast saw removal on the wax models, once before any formal cast saw training and once after. Each wax model was labeled with each participating student's unique code and a symbol to designate the “preinstruction” attempt. There were four designated “cutting lanes” premarked on each end of the casted wax cylinder. They were each individually instructed on how to properly hold the cast saw, how to turn it on and off, and its associated safety features. They were not instructed on how to remove a cast. They were instructed to make a total of 4 cuts on each mounted wax model, with the research team member present rotating the wax block into its next cutting position after each longitudinal cut. The model allows for revolver-style rotation, providing 4 discrete lanes for cutting.

After all participants completed their cuts, they completed a formal cast saw removal skills module, which lasted about 1.5 h. The video session consisted of instruction on technique and hands-on practice using wax forearm models by Alexander Kuzma (AK). Participants were able to receive immediate feedback and view their depth with one removal attempt of the casting material. After the skills module was complete, participants were then instructed, as in the preinstruction attempt, to again make a total of 4 cuts on a new mounted wax model. Similarly, these models were labeled with their code and a symbol to designate the “post-instruction” attempt, and no further instruction or technical guidance was provided during this testing phase. About 3 h elapsed between the pretest, training, and posttest.

After both phases were completed, the cast material was removed and the wax models were examined by the study coinvestigators. The number, length, and depth of deformities were identified, measured, and tallied. The unique code provided by each participant was be utilized only for comparison of each individual's preinstruction and postinstruction values. The deidentified data were analyzed by our study coinvestigators for significant findings.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all outcome measures, including the number of errors per block, total error length, maximum error depth, and maximum error width. Continuous variables are reported as means with standard deviations. Paired-samples t-tests were used to compare preinstruction and postinstruction outcomes. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Discussion

Orthopaedic casts are ubiquitous tools used around the world for the treatment of fractures and support of bones and joints postoperatively. However, inappropriate cast removal has many complications, including skin injury and subsequent medicolegal ramifications. Hence, adequate standardized cast removal training, including appropriate cast saw use, is vital during orthopaedic residency to reduce such errors. Since implementation of traditional casting programs may be cost and resource prohibitive to many programs, we previously created a validated simulation cast removal model to improve cast removal outcomes and decrease instances of complications among trainees. Our current study takes this model a step further by implementing a standardized educational module and evaluating premodule and post-module improvement in participant cast removal error. Our study shows that there is a decrease in the number of total errors as well as the severity of these errors after undergoing the cast removal educational module.

Our findings align with a growing body of literature demonstrating that brief, structured simulation-based training can produce measurable improvements in cast saw technique among novice learners. In a study using a pediatric forearm phantom equipped with temperature sensors, investigators evaluated first-year orthopaedic residents before and after a single training session, though their model relied on rater-based instruments (OSATS and a specific rating scale) rather than direct quantification of physical cutting errors, they found that trained residents achieved performance comparable to expert surgeons [6,12]. More recently, engineering-based approaches, such as the Cast Saw Injury Protection System, have introduced real-time alerts when the saw blade contacts simulated skin, offering a complementary strategy to purely didactic or simulation-based training [13]. However, the cost of this device is unknown as it was a custom prototype. Collectively, this literature supports the broader premise that focused, low-resource interventions can meaningfully improve cast saw safety, and our study extends this work by offering an objective, low-cost, and reproducible measure of cutting accuracy and depth that does not depend on subjective rater scoring.

One limitation of this study design is the lack of a control variable, which limits our ability to compare simulation-based learning of this skill to traditional learning. As such, we cannot directly measure the skills gained from our intervention by comparing simulation-based learning to traditional learning. However, to approach this limitation, future studies utilizing the wax-based simulation model to compare the skills of trainees who have participated in the cast saw safety simulations to those who have honed their skills solely through real-life practice should be considered. Another potential limitation is whether the skills gained from this simulation are transferable to clinical practice in a measurable manner. To address this, future studies evaluating institutional complication rates related to cast-saw injury before and after residency-wide implementation of this simulation may be conducted to measure the clinical impact of the intervention. Further, retention of skills was not studied here as pretests and posttests were conducted in the same session. Future studies also need to be conducted to evaluate the durability of skills. Lastly, the cast saw models premodule and postmodule were not blinded to the raters, which may introduce a degree of bias. However, we ensured that objective data (measurements) were only included in the analysis.

Conclusion

Our proposed educational module shows an improvement in cast removal safety and is cost-effective, time-efficient, reproducible, and scalable which allows for implementation across training programs and hospital systems.
