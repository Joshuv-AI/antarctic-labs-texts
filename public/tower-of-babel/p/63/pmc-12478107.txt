Previously unrecognized diversity of far-red light-using terrestrial green algae and cyanobacteria
Authors: Pa-Yu Chen, Jui-Tse Ko, Tzu-Ling Chen, Ming-Yang Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryOxygenic photosynthesis in cyanobacteria, algae, and plants typically relies on visible light, but some lineages can utilize far-red light (FRL, wavelength = 700–800 nm). While FRL-utilizing cyanobacteria are well-studied, little is known about FRL-utilizing algae. Here, we isolated ten algal and three cyanobacterial strains capable of growing under FRL from soil, brick surface, and bark in a Taiwan urban park. Ten isolates belong to four green algal genera not previously reported to use FRL. These algae showed distinct far-red absorption and enhanced long-wavelength fluorescence compared to a model green alga. Unlike cyanobacteria producing chlorophylls d and f, these algae contain only chlorophylls a or b, suggesting red-shifted chlorophyll a in their light-harvesting complexes. Phylogenetic analysis showed these algae are not distinct lineages but cluster with known genera, suggesting that their ability to grow in FRL had been previously overlooked due to the lack of functional screening.Subject areas: Ecology, Microbiology, Microbial physiology

Introduction

Oxygenic photosynthesis is the only biological process that produces oxygen in the atmosphere. Photosystems I and II (PSI and PSII) in plants, algae, and cyanobacteria harvest light energy and drive the electron transfer from water to NADP+. For a long time, photosynthesis was thought to be driven by visible light (VL), or called photosynthetically active radiation (PAR, wavelength = 400–700 nm). However, in these two decades, the discovery of using far-red light (FRL, wavelength = 700–800 nm) for photosynthesis extended PAR to longer wavelengths.

FRL can drive photosynthesis in cyanobacteria and algae but not in plants.1 Two types of cyanobacteria can use FRL, including (1) monophyletic Acaryochloris spp. that produces chlorophyll (Chl) d and (2) polyphyletic cyanobacteria that perform far-red light photoacclimation (FaRLiP).2,3 During FaRLiP, cyanobacteria respond to FRL and produce Chl d and f and remodel phycobilisome (PBS), PSII, and PSI to harvest FRL.4,5,6 More than 50 cyanobacterial strains that perform FaRLiP or have a FaRLiP gene cluster have been identified across five cyanobacterial divisions, including unicellular and filamentous strains.3,7,8

Compared to cyanobacteria, the understanding of FRL-utilizing algae remains limited. Unlike cyanobacteria, algae do not produce chlorophylls d or f, but instead utilize red-shifted forms of Chl a within their light-harvesting complexes to absorb FRL.9 FRL-using algae were mostly found in marine environments. There are marine algae such as green algae (Ostreobium sp. and Ostreobium queketii), Diatom (Phaeodactylum tricornutum and Nitzschia closterium), and an alveolate alga (Chromera velia).10,11,12,13,14,15 Conversely, only three terrestrial and freshwater algae have been isolated and characterized, including two Eustigmatophyta (Eustigmatophyte alga FP5 and Trachydiscus minutus) and one aerial green macroalga, Prasiola crispa, which belongs to the class Trebouxiophyceae.9,16,17 This limited representation suggests that the true diversity of FRL-using algae in non-marine environments remains largely unexplored.

In the past, when investigating the distribution and diversity of far-red light using cyanobacteria or algae, researchers have primarily sampled in remote areas, including stromatolites, corals, subtropical forests, caves, lakes, stream water, hot spring microbial mats, the beach rocks, and Antarctica.9,17,18,19,20,21,22,23,24 To date, only one study has reported an FRL-utilizing alga in an urban setting—an Eustigmatophyte strain isolated from a forest park in St. Louis, Missouri, USA.17 Thus, the diversity and ecological distribution of FRL-using algae and cyanobacteria in urban environments remain poorly understood.

To address this gap, we investigated whether FRL-utilizing cyanobacteria and algae exist in accessible, non-remote terrestrial environments. We collected samples from soil, concrete surfaces, and tree bark in an urban park on a university campus in Taiwan. We isolated cyanobacteria and algae from these samples and assessed their growth and photophysiological responses under VL and FRL using microscopy, spectroscopy, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Phylogenetic analysis was further used to evaluate the evolutionary relationships of these organisms. This study provides new insight into the ecological and evolutionary diversity of FRL-utilizing algae and suggests that their presence in terrestrial environments may be more common than previously recognized.

Results

Three distinct light compositions were selected for sampling at an urban park in Taipei, Taiwan

The sampling site was situated at the National Taiwan University (NTU) campus, located in the urban area of Taipei City, Taiwan. The campus features a variety of garden designs, including grassland, shrubs, trees, and buildings. Three types of sampling locations were selected at NTU: areas without shade, those shaded by architectural structures, and regions shaded by vegetation (Figure S1). These three types refer to three light conditions. High light (HL) conditions are places without shading (∼2000 μmol photons m−2 s−1). Shaded light conditions occur in the shade of architectures (SL, ∼100 μmol photons m−2 s−1 and FRL (700–800 nm)/VL (400–700 nm) < 0.5). Far-red light (FRL) conditions occur in shaded areas beneath the canopy, where Chl a strongly filters sunlight (<100 μmol photons m−2 s−1 and FRL (700–800 nm)/VL (400–700 nm) > 0.5) (Figure S2). In a total of sixteen sampling sites, four were classified under HL conditions, four under SL conditions, and eight under FRL conditions, as detailed in Table S1.

The morphology of isolated strains in VL and FRL was analyzed using microscopy. Absorption spectroscopy shows that isolated algae and cyanobacteria can harvest far-red light

The isolated cyanobacteria and algae were cultured separately in VL and FRL until the exponential phase to evaluate the potential of these strains in harvesting FRL. The cells were subsequently collected for absorption spectroscopy. Chlamydomonas sp. (Chlamydomonas) served as a model green alga, but it was previously unknown if this alga could grow in FRL.25,26 Compared to the absorption spectra of Chlamydomonas, our isolated strains either have red-shifted Qy peaks (680–684 nm) or FRL absorbance shoulders that exceed 680 nm (Figure 2). The most pronounced FRL absorbance was observed in Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7 in FRL (Figure 2F). Previous studies have demonstrated that several Eustigmatophyte algae can acclimate to and grow in FRL.16,17,27 The absorption spectra of the four additional genera of green algae (Coelastrella sp., Jenufa sp., Scenedesmus sp., and Chlorella sp. ABCL-CS01) exhibited a red shift in absorbance compared to that of Chlamydomonas (Figure 2). However, none of the previous studies showed that these green algae within these genera can utilize FRL for growth.28

Absorbance changes in VL and FRL of the isolated cyanobacterial strains were also measured (Figure S4). The increase in absorbance in FRL is consistent with that of cyanobacteria performing FaRLiP.1,3 The cyanobacterial strains isolated in this study include three Chroococcidiopsis strains. Previously studied FaRLiP strains in these two genera show similar absorption spectral changes from VL to FRL.21,29,30 These strains in VL and FRL show a Qy peak at 680 nm, representing Chl a. On the other hand, the feature of FaRLiP was only shown in FRL, namely a FRL absorbance shoulder at 710 nm representing Chl d, Chl f, and FRL-absorbing phycobilisome (Figure S4). Consequently, the cyanobacterial strains isolated in this study were expected to exhibit FaRLiP in FRL.

The isolated algae and cyanobacteria emit long-wavelength fluorescence in far-red light

Previous studies indicate that whole-cell long-wavelength emission is a characteristic of cyanobacteria and algae capable of growth in FRL.3,9,12,14,16,17,31,32,33 In the cyanobacterial strains Chroococcidiopsis sp. CAT1, CAT9, and CAT13, cells in VL exhibited a prominent peak at 684 nm, indicative of PSII. Conversely, long-wavelength emissions were observed as significant FRL peaks ranging from 732 to 739 nm with 440 nm excitation (Figure S5) and approximately 718 nm with 580 nm excitation (Figure S6). The long-wavelength emission peaks, approximately 718 nm, likely originated from FRL-PBS, while peaks ranging from 732 to 739 nm were associated with FRL-PSI and FRL-PSII, as investigated in Chroococcidiopsis thermalis. PCC 7203.34 Therefore, the Chroococcidiopsis strains isolated in this study were also likely to be FaRLiP strains. FaRLiP is a process of acclimation exclusively observed in cyanobacteria rather than algae. Algal species utilize red-shifted light-harvesting complexes to enhance the absorption of FRL.28,35 The model green algae, Chlamydomonas, showed a single peak at 684 nm, representing the emission from PSII (Figure 3). The room temperature emission only showed subtle changes in Chlorella sp. ABCL-CS01, Jenufa sp., and Scenedesmus sp. (Figures 3C, 3D, 3G, and 3K). In these three species, emission peaks shifted from 684 nm to 685–686 nm, with only Jenufa sp. CAT5 exhibiting an additional emission shoulder at approximately 705 nm (Figure 3D). In Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7, cells in either VL or FRL showed red-shifted peaks at 708 nm or 712 nm (Figure 3F). Six Coelastrella sp. CAT2, 3, 6, 10, 11, and 12, exhibited comparable emission changes in VL and FRL (Figures 3A, 3B, 3E, 3H, 3I, and 3J). The emission peaks in the VL range from 685 to 687 nm, closely aligning with the 684 nm observed in Chlamydomonas. A new long-wavelength emission peak in FRL is observed at 700–702 nm, indicating an acclimation in the photosynthetic apparatus that alters the emission properties.

The low-temperature emission spectra supported the fact that acclimation to FRL occurs in certain species. Long-wavelength emission peaks around 750 nm were observed in Chroococcidiopsis strains CAT1, 9, and 13, in FRL under 440 nm and 580 nm excitation wavelengths (Figures S7 and S8). The long-wavelength emission peaks at low temperatures are characteristic of FaRLiP cyanobacteria.3,29,31,34 In Chlamydomonas, two major peaks were found at 686 and 715 nm, corresponding to light-harvesting complex II (LHCII) and PSI, respectively. Additionally, the shoulder observed around 695 nm suggests the presence of PSII (Figure 4).26 Chlorella sp. ABCL-CS01 and Scenedesmus sp. CAT8 did not exhibit apparent red-shifted emission characteristics in FRL, although they showed subtle emission changes from 686 to ∼710 nm (Figures 4G and 4K). Acclimation to FRL has been found in Coelastrella sp. and Jenufa sp., as the strains displayed red-shifted peaks from 720 to 725 nm, suggesting the presence of red-shifted Chls or an increased quantity of red-shifted Chls produced in FRL (Figures 4A–4E and 4H–4J). Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7 displayed no noticeable emission peak at 686 nm; however, it presented a single peak at 718 nm in VL and 722 nm in FRL, indicating that photoacclimation occurs in this strain as well (Figure 4F).

Without synthesizing new chlorophylls, these algae can still grow in far-red light

Subsequently, we assessed the growth potential of the isolated cyanobacteria and algae in FRL. These cyanobacteria and algae were spotted on agar plates, and the growth was measured in VL and FRL (Figure 5). The positive control, a FaRLiP cyanobacterium, Chlorogloeopsis fritschii PCC 9212, showed growth in FRL. Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803 and Chlamydomonas sp. CC-1690 served as a negative control for cyanobacteria and algae, respectively, exhibiting growth solely in VL conditions. Chlorella sp. ABCL-CS01 (Chlorella) was initially designated as an additional negative control; however, it exhibited unexpected growth in FRL (Figure 5). Despite variations in growth rates and colony-forming efficiency, all strains exhibited significant growth in VL within 5 days (Figure 5). The growth in FRL was not apparent within 5 days but became observable by 16 days, suggesting that these strains exhibit slower growth in FRL compared to VL. All strains, except for the negative controls, exhibited varying degrees of growth in FRL (Figure 5). The colonies exhibited a yellow-green hue in FRL, indicating that the cells had undergone acclimation. The growth observed on the plates in FRL in 16 days was comparable to or slower than that in VL over 5 days, except for Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7. The growth of Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7 was further evaluated through growth rate measurement in liquid cultures (Figure S9). The measurement showed that Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7 had a doubling time of 53.0 ± 1.2 h in VL and 110.4 ± 4.4 h in FRL, suggesting that this strain grows approximately twice as rapidly in VL compared to FRL.

The three cyanobacterial strains (Chroococcidiopsis sp. CAT 1, 9, and 13) produce Chl d and f in FRL, which is consistent with the characteristic of FaRLiP strains reported previously (Table 2).3,8,32 Conversely, HPLC analysis indicates that the green algae examined in this study contain solely Chl a and Chl b, with no additional Chls present (Figure 6). The Chlamydomonas strain that cannot grow in FRL exhibited a Chl a percentage of approximately 65%, with Chl b constituting 35%. In contrast, the other green algae strains exhibited a Chl a percentage between 70% and 85% (Figure 6). Additionally, all six Coelastrella strains exhibited a significantly higher Chl a percentage in FRL than in VL (Figure 6). These elevated Chl a:b ratios might be attributed to synthesizing more Chl a or red-shifted Chl a, degradation of Chl b, or alteration of the composition of LHCs, which subsequently improved the absorption of FRL. The whole-cell absorption spectra suggest that the increase in Chl a percentage was likely associated with the rise in long-wavelength absorption (Figure 2). The higher concentration of Chl a might also correlate with increased synthesis of additional LHCs and the absorption of FRL. Comparative analysis of Chl a percentages in algal strains under VL and FRL

Isolated algal strains were cultivated in VL and FRL until the exponential growth phase was reached, followed by HPLC pigment analyses. The composition of these algae is exclusively comprised of Chl a and Chl b. The analysis involved conducting paired t-tests with false discovery rate (FDR) corrections to determine if there were statistically significant differences in the Chl a ratios between the algal strains cultured under different light conditions (FRL vs. VL). An adjust p-value greater than 0.1 suggests no statistically significant difference in Chl a ratio between algal strains cultured under FRL compared to those cultured under VL, and this is denoted as “ns.” An adjust p-value less than 0.1, marked with a dagger sign (†), suggests a marginal or unstable difference. Conversely, an adjust p-value smaller than 0.05, marked with an asterisk (∗), or smaller than 0.01, marked with a double asterisk (∗∗), suggests a significant difference in Chl a ratios between the two light conditions.

The algae utilizing far-red light do not constitute a single clade; instead, they are dispersed throughout the phylogenetic tree

Sequences of 18S rDNA from 12 algae used in this study, along with 20 reference algal strains, were employed to construct the phylogenetic tree (Figure 7; Table S3). Glaucocystis nostochinearum SAG 28.80, a strain of Glaucophyta, was set as the outgroup. The tree is separated into three subclades: Chlorophyceae (including Coelastrella, Scenedesmus, Chlamydomonas, and Jenufa), Trebouxiophyceae (comprising Chlorella and Prasiola crispa), and Eustigmatophyceae. Chlorophyceae and Trebouxiophyceae belonged to the division Chlorophyta (green algae), while Eustigmatophyceae was categorized as a class within the phylum Ochrophyta. Consequently, they were divided into two clades.

The phylogenetic tree indicated that FRL-utilizing algae were not phylogenetically distinct but were closely related to other algae within the same genera. For instance, Trachydiscus minutus CCALA 838 was phylogenetically closer to Eustigmatophyceae sp. Tow and Itas strains than to other FRL-using strains, FP5 and CAT7. The FRL-growing Chlorella sp. ABCL-CS01 was phylogenetically close to Chlorella vulgaris, which could not grow in FRL.10 Furthermore, Coelastrella, Scenedesmus, and Jenufa sp. strains isolated in this study were the only ones that could utilize FRL in the same genera (Figure 7). A recent study isolated an FRL-utilizing Neochloris strain, which formed a sister-clade to Coelastrella and Scenedesmus.40 It is important to note that only two strains, Chlamydomonas sp. CC-1690 and Chlorella vulgaris, have been experimentally verified to be incapable of growth in FRL (Figure 5).10 However, many algal strains remain untested. Determining the ability of algal strains to utilize FRL for growth requires experimental cultivation and measurement of their growth rates. A future study is necessary to decide whether FRL-utilizing capability is universal among these algal genera.

Discussion

Algae and cyanobacteria exposed to varying light conditions may induce photoacclimation. FaRLiP represents a well-studied instance of photoacclimation in FRL, wherein the pigment composition and spectral properties of cyanobacterial cells undergo alteration.3,33,41 This acclimation also affects cell morphology. In FRL, the thylakoid membrane spaces are reduced in Halomicronema hongdechloris and Chroococcidiopsis thermalis PCC 7203.42,43 The filaments of Leptolyngbya sp. JSC-1 exhibited greater width in VL compared to FRL, with cell coloration being blue-green in VL and lime in FRL.32 A significant morphological change was observed in the pennate diatom Phaeodactylum tricornutum, with cells exhibiting a round shape under red light (halogen lamp) and a fusiform shape under VL.13

In this study, we also observed morphological differences in algae and cyanobacteria between VL and FRL. Cyanobacteria Chroococcidiopsis sp. CAT1, CAT9, and CAT13 exhibited a blue-green coloration in VL and a lime coloration in FRL (Figures 1A, 1I, and 1M). Furthermore, Chroococcidiopsis sp. CAT9 exhibited a greater number of cell clusters compared to the CAT1 and CAT13 strains. The observed color differences may result from alterations in pigment composition, specifically the change in phycobiliprotein composition by cells under FRL conditions (Figure S4). Prior transcriptomic and proteomic investigations indicate that phycobiliprotein composition changes during FRL acclimation.4,43,44 No significant changes in color and size were observed in the algae Coelasterlla, Jenufa, and Chlorella strains under the two light conditions (Figures 1B–1F, 1J–1L, and 1N). We observed a significant color change in Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7 and Scenedesmus sp. CAT8, with the cells of Scenedesmus sp. CAT8 appeared thinner under FRL (Figures 1G and 1H). The observed color changes may result from variations in the 480 nm range, corresponding to carotenoid absorbance (Figures 2F and 2G). Moreover, while Coelasterlla strains exhibit no notable color changes under the microscope, there is a significant increase in the Chl a ratio in FRL (Figure 6). These observations correspond with the colonies of several algae appearing lime color in FRL (Figure 5). The color changes perceived visually were within the VL range (Figure 1). At the same time, the changes in FRL were identified through spectroscopy (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, 3, and 4). The results suggest that the cyanobacteria and algae underwent a photoacclimation process to modify their pigment composition, photosynthetic apparatus, or morphology in response to FRL.

Diverse acclimative strategies are present for cyanobacteria and algae to grow in FRL environments.1,28,35 Some cyanobacteria performed FaRLiP in response to FRL. This process includes synthesizing Chl d and Chl f and remodeling PSII, PSI, and phycobilisome in FRL.3,6,45 Analysis of the modifications in spectral properties and pigment composition in Chroococcidiopsis sp. CAT1, CAT9, and CAT13 indicated that these three strains possess the capability for FaRLiP (Figures S4–S8; Table 2). Numerous strains of Chroococcidiopsis within the Chroococcidiopsidales order have demonstrated the ability to perform FaRLiP, including C. thermalis PCC 7203 and Chroococcidiopsis sp. CCNUC1, CCNUC2, CCNUC3, CCNUM1, CCMEE 10, CCMEE 130, CCMEE 569, CCMEE 570, CCMEE 584, SAG 2025.7,8,21,46 These Chroococcidiopsis strains were isolated from various environments, including mosses on arid and humid limestones, plants in pond waters, hypolithic and endolithic niches in hot deserts, endolithic habitats in coral rock, and soil samples.8,21,47 Noteworthily, in this study, we isolated Chroococcidiopsis sp. CAT1, CAT9, and CAT13 from concrete outdoor tiles, tree bark, and soil (Figure S1 and Table S1). The fact that FaRLiP-capable Chroococcidiopsis strains are present on tree barks and concrete in urban environments contributes to understanding the diversity and distribution of these species.

This study reported 11 algal strains from five genera capable of growth in FRL, with an investigation into their diversity in spectral properties and pigment composition (Table 1). Chlorella is classified within the class Trebouxiophyceae, including the FRL-growing Prasiola crispa, as illustrated in Figure 7.9 Chlorella sp. ABCL-CS01 demonstrated growth in FRL; however, it exhibited minimal photoacclimation, as indicated by the absence of significant spectral changes in FRL (Figures 2K, 3K, and 4K). Moreover, not all Chlorella species are capable of growth in FRL conditions. Chlorella vulgaris was shown to be incapable of growth in FRL.10 The most significant spectral change associated with substantial growth in FRL was observed in Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7 (Figures 2F, 3F, and 4F; Table 1). In addition, the spectral properties of Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7 was comparable to that of Eustigmatophyceae sp. FP5.17 Nonetheless, the CAT7 strain still exhibited a faster growth rate in VL than in FRL (Figure S9), indicating that VL provided a more favorable light energy environment for FRL-growing algae. Despite being isolated from geographically distant locations (the US and Taiwan), these two strains exhibited phylogenetic proximity, and both can grow in FRL (Figure 7). The molecular mechanism of harvesting FRL has been studied in detail in another freshwater Eustigmatophyte alga, Trachydiscus minutus,16 which is located in a sister clade of Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7 and Eustigmatophyceae sp. FP5 (Figure 7). T. minutus possesses a distinctive red-shifted light-harvesting complex known as the red-shifted Violaxanthin-Chlorophyll a Protein (rVCP).48 A similar light-harvesting complex may also exist in Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT7.

Chlamydomonas sp. is a standard model organism within the Chlorophyceae class of green algae. This study demonstrated its inability to grow under FRL conditions25 (Figure 6). The three classes of green algae capable of growth in FRL are Trebouxiophyceae, Ulvophyceae, and Chlorophyceae, which collectively constitute a clade within Archaeplastida.49 The genera Coelastrella, Jenufa, and Scenedesmus are categorized under the class Chlorophyceae (Figure 7). Currently, only one species of Chlorophyceae algae from the genus Neochloris has been documented to thrive in FRL.40 The green alga Neochloris strain Biwa 5-2 is classified within the Family Neochloridaceae40 and is positioned in a distinct clade separate from the three genera (Coelastrella, Jenufa, and Scenedesmus) in the phylogenetic analysis (Figure 7). Coelastrella sp. displayed a distinctive characteristic with Neochloris strain Biwa 5-2, especially elevated Chl a to b ratios in FRL relative to VL. However, Neochloris strain Biwa 5-2 demonstrated a more significant change, with Chl b decreasing to 7%, in contrast to approximately 15% in Coelastrella sp. (Figure 6).40 Chl b molecules primarily function as accessory pigments in the LHCII of PSII in green algae, facilitating energy capture by extending the absorption range into the blue region of the spectrum.50,51 However, Chl b becomes less effective under FRL due to its poor absorption at longer wavelengths. A decrease in Chl b content is regarded as a physiologically relevant photoacclimation response. This shift may reflect a reorganization of the antenna system in response to spectral quality, potentially through either the replacement of LHCII complexes containing Chl b with those composed predominantly of Chl a or red-shifted forms of Chl a, or through the direct substitution of Chl b with Chl a within existing LHCII complexes. Further research on LHCII is necessary to investigate the alteration in Chl b to a ratios in these algae.

We categorized FRL-growing algae into three groups according to their acclimation responses: strong, weak, and no acclimation. Strong acclimation species, including most of the reported FRL-growing algae, Examples of interest include various green algae: the marine alga Ostreobium sp.,10,18 the aerial macroalga Prasiola crispa,9 the unicellular alga Neochloris sp. strain Biwa 5-2,40 and the filamentous alga Phaeophila dendroides, which was isolated from coral skeletons.52 Additionally, four stramenopiles exhibit notable acclimation: the diatom Phaeodactylum tricornutum and Nitzschia closterium,11 the alveolate alga Chromera velia,14,15 Trachydiscus minutus,53 Eustigmatophyceae sp. FP5,17 and Eustigmatophyceae sp. CAT 7, as identified in this study (Figures 2F, 3, and 4F). These species exhibited apparent alterations in their absorption and fluorescence spectra under FRL, facilitating their identification during isolation. Weakly acclimating algae, including Coelastrella sp. and Jenufa sp. identified in this study, exhibited minimal alterations in absorption spectra but noticeable changes in fluorescence emission under VL and FRL conditions (Figures 2A–2E and 2H–2J; 3A–3E and 3H–J; 4A–4E and 4H–4J). Coelastrella sp. demonstrated changes in Chl a ratios under FRL (Figure 6), suggesting the presence of photoacclimation. Non-acclimating algae, such as Scenedesmus sp. CAT8 and Chlorella sp. ABCL-CS01, exhibited minimal variation in their absorption and fluorescence spectra, as well as in their Chl a to b ratios, between VL and FRL (Figures 2G and 2K; 3G and 3K; 4G and 4K). Despite lacking active photoacclimation, both strains demonstrated red-shifted spectral features compared to Chlamydomonas sp., which cannot grow under FRL (Figure 5). This indicated that Scenedesmus sp. CAT8 and Chlorella sp. ABCL-CS01 possessed red-shifted Chl a; however, these pigments were consistently present and not regulated by FRL. The marine green algae Ostreobium sp. and Ostreobium queketii displayed uncharacterized acclimation, as the spectral and pigment compositions of these two strains were not analyzed under both VL and FRL.10,18 We did not find a clear pattern of the distribution of these three types of FRL algae in phylogenetic analyses (Figure 7), probably owing to the restricted number of FRL-capable algal species discovered thus far. More species capable of growth in FRL may need to be found for a more comprehensive analysis.

In contrast to the distinctive FaRLiP gene cluster found in cyanobacteria,3,54 the universal marker gene(s) for the FRL-growing algae is at present not known, although the mechanisms for harvesting FRL have been thoroughly examined in two algal species, and the putative genes encoding FRL-absorbing LHCs have been proposed in Trebouxiaceae family.9,16,48,55,56 Many algal species within this phylogenetic analysis were not experimentally tested for their ability to harvest FRL (Figure 7). Future culture-based experiments will be essential to determine the ability of other algae to grow in FRL. Investigating the necessary genes that facilitate FRL utilization in green algae is crucial, as this discovery may allow for the transfer of FRL utilization capabilities to crops, thereby improving light harvesting in self-shaded crop fields.1,35 The mechanism of FRL utilization has been examined in detail within FaRLiP cyanobacteria;1,57,58 however, the key gene(s) responsible for Chl d synthesis remain unidentified.58,59 Therefore, synthesizing Chl d or Chl f in plants, along with remodeling the PSI and PSII structures through the utilization of genes from the cyanobacterial FaRLiP gene cluster to harvest FRL, presents significant complexity and technical challenges. Green algae are evolutionarily closer to plants than cyanobacteria. Their acclimation in FRL alters the ratio of Chl a to Chl b but does not involve the synthesis of Chl d and f (Table 1),40,52 which may be more applicable to plants. Overall, in this study, we isolated 10 new algal strains capable of growth in FRL within a single urban park in Taiwan. A greater number of algae are likely to grow in FRL that remains unexplored in nature. In the future, isolating additional algal species and examining their genomic composition may enable the identification of key genes responsible for utilizing FRL in these green algae.

Limitations of the study

Our study identified novel green algal lineages capable of growth in FRL and characterized their phenotypes at the cellular level; however, these phenotypes may not fully represent their characteristics in natural environments, as the algae were isolated as pure cultures and grown under controlled laboratory conditions. Additionally, the detailed biochemical properties of the LHCs and the genomic information of the new strains are currently unavailable. Future research involving the purification of LHCs and comparative genomics will facilitate a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving FRL-harvesting in these algal strains.

Cyanobacterial and algal strains

PCC 680366 was gifted by Dr. Hsiu-An Chu at Academia Sinica in Taiwan. The green algae Chlamydomonas sp. CC-169025 and Chlorella sp. The other strains were isolated in this study (Table 1).

Method details

Sampling sites

Sample collection was carried out at National Taiwan University, Taipei, Taiwan (121° 32′ E, 25° 1′ N). A total of 16 samples were collected in February 2020. Sampling sites include the surface of tree barks, paving tiles, rocks, and moss (Table S1). The light intensity of each sampling site was measured by an HR-450 portable photometer (HiPoint, Taiwan) (Table S1). Sites under high light (∼2000 μmol photons m−2 s−1), shaded light (below 100 μmol photons m−2 s−1), and FRL were sampled separately for comparison (Figures S1–S3). Samples were scraped by a carving knife, which was wiped with 70% (v/v) ethanol before and after each sampling, placed in sterile 15-mL plastic screw tubes, and immediately stored in a portable icebox at room temperature. Those samples were then transferred to the laboratory for further processing on the same day. Each sample was homogenized using a stainless-steel spatula and subjected to cultural enrichment.

Culture enrichment and strain isolation

Each sample was cultured into 20 mL of B-HEPES medium [a modified BG-11 medium using 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazine-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) as the buffer60] separately in two glass tubes. The recipe for B-HEPES medium was modified to suppress heterotrophic bacterial growth by eliminating citric acid and substituting ferric ammonium citrate with ferric chloride. The two tubes are incubated at 30°C under LED visible light (VL, 120 μmol photons m−2 s−1) and LED far-red light (FRL, center wavelength = 730 nm, 40 μmol photons m−2 s−1) in a growth chamber supplemented with 1% (v/v) CO2.32 To measure cell growth, we adjusted the VL and FRL intensities to 100 μmol photons m−2 s−1 and used eight layers of green cellophane covering the FRL LED to eliminate light wavelengths below 700 nm. The VL and FRL spectra are presented in Figure S10. After incubation for 24 days, the cultures in glass tubes were homogenized and subcultured to 12-well plates in FRL. Every 3–7 weeks, the cultures in 12-well plates were diluted 100-fold and transferred to fresh medium in 12-well plates for enrichment. After being transferred twice, the enrichments were streaked on B-HEPES agar plates for single colonies.

Strains CAT1 and CAT7 were isolated through flow cytometric cell sorting utilizing the MoFlo XDP high-speed cell sorter (Beckman Coulter, USA). Enrichments were resuspended in B-HEPES medium to achieve a final concentration of 2 × 107 cells mL−1. A 488-nm laser served as the excitation source, with fluorescence emission signals collected within the wavelength range of 670 ± 15 nm for Chl a. Cell sizes of approximately 5 μm and 10 μm were utilized to select CAT1 and CAT7, respectively. Antibiotic treatments were conducted for certain algal and cyanobacterial enrichments. Imipenem (100 μg mL−1), kanamycin (50 μg mL−1), or ampicillin (100–250 μg mL−1) were utilized for algal samples. Imipenem (20–50 μg mL−1), kanamycin (50 μg mL−1), or ampicillin (50–100 μg mL−1) were utilized for cyanobacterial samples. Cycloheximide (50 μg mL−1) was employed to inhibit the growth of eukaryotic microbes. The samples were treated with antibiotics in liquid medium for 18 h in the dark before being streaked onto agar plates, or they were directly streaked onto agar plates containing antibiotics. Restreaking plates were performed several times until single colonies were obtained.

The axenic cultures were established when no other bacterial colonies were observed after the restreaking of single colonies onto Tryptone Soy Agar (TSA, Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ), R2A (HIMEDIA, PA) and Luria Bertani (LB, Bioshop, Taiwan) agar plates, which were incubated for a duration of 14 days. The morphology of cells was examined under a Leica DMR microscope (Leica, Germany) equipped with a Leica MC 170HD digital camera (Leica, Germany). The isolated strains were cultured in B-HEPES medium for further analyses.

Absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy

The isolated strains were grown in B-HEPES medium until exponential phase (OD750 nm = 0.5–1.0) and adjusted to OD750 nm = 0.2 in 1 mL. For filamentous strains, cells were homogenized before spectral measurements. Absorption spectroscopy was performed by a spectrometer UV1800 (MAPADA, China) with an equipped PC software (Datech Instruments Ltd., Taiwan). For some strains, cells were washed to minimize light scattering before absorbance measurements (Figures 2D, S4A, and S4C). Fluorescence spectroscopy was performed using a fluorescence spectrophotometer F-7000 (Hitachi, Japan). Fluorescence emission spectra were obtained using excitation wavelengths of 440 nm and 580 nm (slit width: 5 nm) for Chl a and phycobiliproteins, respectively. Emission spectra were collected within the range of 600–800 nm (slit width: 5 nm). For low-temperature measurement (77K), the samples were resuspended with 500 μL of medium and 500 μL of PEG-4000 (30%, w/v) solution.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

The procedure of pigment extraction and HPLC measurements were performed as previously described with minor modifications.4,32 Cyanobacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 2 min in a table-top centrifuge at room temperature. The pellet was washed once with 1 mL of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 7.5. After removal of the supernatant, the pellet was fully resuspended with iodoacetamide (IAA, 30 μL of an 18 mg mL−1 stock) and mixed with 270 μL of acetone: methanol (7:2). Glass beads (100 μL, 0.1 mm, BioSpec, USA) were added into a 2 mL screw tube (BioSpec, USA) with the cell suspension. The tube was left on ice for ∼1 min and mounted on a bead beater (BioSpec, USA). The cell suspension was agitated for 10 s at 3,500 rpm, kept on ice for 1 min, and then centrifugated at 13,000 rpm in a table-top microcentrifuge for 3 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.2-μm filter disk (PTFE) by a 1-mL syringe. The filtrate was stored in a new microcentrifuge tube on ice in the dark until injection. The filtrate 100 μL was subjected to Reverse Phase-HPLC analysis through an analytical Chromatorex C18 column (4.6 mm × 25 cm) (Fuji Silysia Chemical LTD., Japan) equipped on an HPLC system MD-4010/PU-4180 (JASCO, Japan). The gradient elution program [percent B, minutes] using Solvent A (methanol: acetonitrile: H2O = 42:33:25) and Solvent B (methanol: acetonitrile: ethyl acetate = 50: 20: 30) was set as [30%, 0], [100%, 50], [100%, 58], [30%, 60] at a flow rate 1 mL min−1. Elution of pigments was monitored at 705 nm, the absorbance maximum of Chl f, or other wavelengths (e.g., 665 nm for Chl a, 652 nm for Chl b, 695 nm for Chl d, and 491 nm for carotenoids).

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

18S rRNA, ITS2, and tufA sequences of algal axenic colonies were amplified by PCR and subjected to sequencing. PCR amplification of the full length of 18S rRNA genes was performed with forward (5′-CGGTAATTCCAGCTCC-3′) and reverse (5′-GGCGGTGTGTACAARGRG-3′) primers, which was used to reveal phylogenetic relationships between 12 algae in this study and 20 references sequences obtained from NCBI GenBank. However, six Coelastrella sp. strains were discovered to be indistinguishable from each other based on 18S rRNA genes alone. To enhance their phylogenetic resolutions, we incorporated other genes like ITS2 (Forward: 5′-GGCAATAACAGGTCTGT-3’; Reverse: 5′-CCTCACGGTACTTGTTC-3′), and tufA (Forward: 5′-TGAAACAGAAMAWCGTCATTATGC-3’; Reverse: 5′-CCTTCNCGAATMGCRAAWCGC-3′).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Differences in Chl a content between VL and FRL growth conditions were examined for each species individually using a paired t-test. In total, 11 paired t-tests were performed, one per species. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons.

The algal sequences in this study and from references were aligned using MUSCLE in the R package ‘msa'.62 The alignments were then trimmed to remove redundant and poorly aligned regions before phylogenetic analysis. Refined sequences were analyzed with maximum likelihood using IQ-TREE 2 version 2.4.0 software.63 The best-fit models were selected by ModelFinder,63 and the bootstrap analyses of 1,000 re-samplings were conducted by UFboot.64 The resulting phylogenetic trees were rendered by MEGA 11.65
