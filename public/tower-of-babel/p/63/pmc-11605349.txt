Potential of cattle urine as an alternative fertilizer for maize (Zea mays L.) production in Ethiopia
Authors: Getahun Yemata, Esubalew Mengistu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaize productivity has been increasing through the years due to increased use of inorganic fertilizers. However, the price and environmental impact has brought lack of interest among the farmers. Thus, this study was aimed at evaluating the growth, yield and nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) of maize varieties under cattle urine application. The field experiment was conducted in randomized complete block design with three replications. The experiment consisted of four treatments viz. Control, 100 and 50 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer. The results demonstrated a comparable growth and grain yield increment due to 100 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer. The difference between the two varieties in growth, yield and related traits, and NUE indices due to fertilizer applications was non-significant. The leaf area of maize nourished with 100, 50 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer increased by 11.15, 9.73 and 9.78 folds, respectively, in two months period relative to the controls. Fertilized plants had significantly (p < 0.05) higher grain yield (GY). The use of 100, 50 % cattle urine and inorganic fertilizers resulted in 526.76, 326.12, 542.91 % and 47.83, 36.03, 49.78 % increment in average GY and aboveground biomass, respectively, compared to controls. Similarly, plants fertilized with 50 % cattle urine showed an average GY reduction of 39.87 and 38.10 % compared to the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer and 100 % cattle urine, respectively. The use of 50 % cattle urine produced significantly higher NUE, N agronomic efficiency (NAE), N recovery efficiency (NRE) and internal efficiency (IE). Higher GY kg−1 of N applied was obtained from cattle urine applications. In a nutshell, the results confirmed the potential of cattle urine as an alternative to inorganic fertilizers in maize production. However, further studies on optimization of the cattle urine rate and scale up works are recommended.Keywords: BH540, Cattle urine, Grain yield, Inorganic fertilizer, Limu, Nitrogen use efficiency indices

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) is a common widely cultivated crop in Ethiopia. It is produced usually under rain-fed condition and has becoming an increasingly important crop in the food security of the country [1]. More than 11 million smallholder farmers cultivate maize in 2021/2022 main cropping season [2]. Maize ranks second in area coverage and first in total production volume in the country [3]. The popularity of maize is attributed to its wider adaptability to diverse agroecologies, higher calorie content, higher yield per unit area, higher value as a food crop, growing demand for the straw as animal fodder and source of fuel for rural families and better availability of improved varieties [1,2].

Maize production has shown an increasing trend in both area coverage and volume of production [4,5]. In the 2005/06 main cropping Meher season, 1 230 947 ha of land was covered by maize producing 1 625 504 quintals of grain [6]. Likewise, 105 570 935.92 quintals of maize grain was produced from 2 526 212.36 ha of land [4]. This shows that more than twofold and sixty fivefold increment in area coverage and production volume was recorded in 15 years period [4,6]. The fabulous expansion and improved productivity of maize is due mainly to increased availability and use of improved seeds and elevated use of chemical fertilizers [2]. The quantity of improved maize varieties used increased from 75 403 quintals of grain in 2006 to 585 801 quintals in 2022 [2,6]. The improved maize varieties are fertilizer responsive, stable and high yielding [1] and the application of chemical fertilizers has been hastening crop yield by 50 % [7]. Approximately 23 % of the total chemical fertilizer is applied for maize production in the country. In Amhara Regional State, about 85 % of the area under maize cultivation received inorganic fertilizers in 2013 [1]. Likewise, the amount applied increased from 841 403 quintals to 5 880 000 quintals in 2022 [2,6]. The findings of Ref. [8] have shown a significant increase in maize yield by the combined adoption of agricultural technologies (improved varieties and fertilizers).

Despite the increased maize yield due to the elevated use of chemical fertilizers, the price increased, the number of users declined [5] and the fertility of the soil decreased [9] from time to time. This intensive use has resulted in the physical deterioration and biological deactivation of the soil environment. The continuous and excessive use also caused soil acidification, depletion of organic matter due to fast mineralization and decomposition, eutrophication and emission of greenhouse gases that further contribute to climate change [9,10]. The depletion of soil nutrient in turn increased the fertilizer demand per unit area [10]. In addition, phosphate fertilizers contain traces of heavy metals that lead to accumulation, which further cause toxicity to plants and contamination of the food supply [10]. Moreover, the availability of chemical fertilizers decreased in the recent times due to the complicated importation, distribution system and lack of foreign currency [5].This calls for an immediate action to find out and use feasible agricultural wastes and byproducts. The use of wastes has triple benefits such as improved environmental, health and social conditions, reduced cost of waste removal and biofertilizers for crop production [11]. According to Ref. [12], the use of sugarcane bagasse and its biochar (2 %) reduced the impact of salinization and have enhanced shoot, root length and grain yield in maize.

Another agricultural waste that has not been optimally exploited is cattle urine. The use of cattle urine either alone or in combination with inorganic and organic fertilizers has improved the growth and yield of maize varieties [13]. Ref. [14] has obtained a significantly higher grain yield of maize (Zea mays L.) when nourished with cattle, goat and human urine. The combined application of FYM and cattle urine has resulted in remarkably greater number of seeds cob−1 (423.5), test weight (24.5 g), number of rows cob−1 (14.9), grain yield (49 quintal ha−1) and straw yield (8.5 t ha−1) of maize [15]. Similarly, Ref. [16] have found a higher green fodder and dry matter yield of maize due to the integrated application of cattle urine with recommended dose of N and FYM. On the other hand, the foliar application of cattle urine at 10 % concentration has produced higher grain and straw yield [17], plant height, number of leaves and area [18] of fodder maize. Biourine applied at the rate of 75000 L ha−1 has increased plant height, number of leaves, leaf area, leaf area index, fresh weight and biomass of maize [19]. Likewise, a significantly higher rice and tomato biomass [20,21], plant height, number of branches, number of leaves and tomato yield have been reported with the combined application of recommended dose of inorganic N fertilizer and urine [20,21]. According to Ref. [22], an equivalent grain and straw yield has been found from barley nourished with human urine and inorganic fertilizers. The application of 100 mL L−1 and 80 % cow urine have increased the growth, biomass production and yield of sorghum and mustard, respectively [23,24]. The use of 150 mL L−1 of cow urine has produced greater weight of tomato fruit compared with the control [25]. Increasing levels of cow urine from 0 to 12000 L ha−1 have increased the grain and straw yield wheat [26]. The findings of Ref. [28] have revealed that cattle urine alone is capable of supporting microalgae growth at a significant level. The use of 10 % cattle urine has produced maximum biomass in Chlorella sp. (2.6 g L−1) followed by Synechocystis sp. (2.25 g L−1) [27].

Although Ethiopia has the highest cattle population (66 260 987) in Africa [28] and a huge potential for using cattle urine as an alternative fertilizer, there are no studies dealing with its effect on crop growth and yield. One cattle produces 6 L of urine per day, resulting in an approximately 398 million liters each day [30]. Cattle urine is not a toxic waste because 95 % of its content is water, 2.5 % urea and 2.5 % is a mix of minerals, salts, hormones and enzymes [13,29,30]. The findings of several studies have shown that about 80 % of the nitrogenous materials consumed by cattle are excreted as urine and dung, of which about 52 % of N, 61–87 % of P and 82–92 % of K are returned to the environment in the form of urine [30]. The availability of N, P, K and S for plants from cattle urine is the same as inorganic fertilizers [31]. It also improves the physicochemical and biological properties of the soil [14,30]. Cattle urine is an easily available, cheap, economically viable, and environmentally friendly waste with high potential for use as a fertilizer. It is hypothesized that the application of cattle urine significantly enhances growth, yield and related traits, and nitrogen use efficiency of maize varieties. Therefore, the present study aimed to: i) evaluate the growth, yield, and related traits performance; ii) determine the nitrogen use efficiency indices of maize varieties at different fertilizer applications under field conditions.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The experiment was conducted at Bahir Dar University (Peda campus), Bahir Dar city, Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia. Geographically, Bahir Dar is found at 11°35'41.2″N latitude and 37°23' 17.2″E longitude (Fig. 1). It is the capital city of Amhara Regional State. The average elevation of the city is 1786 m above sea level. The city falls within the Woina Dega agroclimatic zone with semi-humid climate. The area has rainy and dry seasons that extend from May–September and October–May, respectively [32]. The area receives the maximum and minimum rainfall in July and January, respectively. The total annual rainfall of the city for 2020 was 1919.53 mm. The annual mean temperature of the city ranges from 16 °C to 21 °C. However, the experiment was carried out during the dry season with a temperature and rainfall values ranging from 11.00 to 29.01 °C and 0.66–38.23 mm (Fig. 1). The main soil type in the experimental site is pellic vertisol dominantly composed of clay and silt clays.

Analysis of soils of the experimental site

Soil samples were collected before planting from 11 points at 1m interval in the experimental site and pooled together to produce a composite soil sample. The composite sample was dried under air and used for the analysis of physicochemical properties. Dried soil sample was dissolved in distilled water in the ratio of 1:2.5 (w/v) and the pH was measured with standard electrode pH meter. The total nitrogen content of the soil was measured titrimetrically following Kjeldahl method. Soil organic carbon was estimated using wet oxidation method as described by Ref. [33]. Similarly, soil organic matter was calculated using the conversion factor 2 as 50 % of the soil organic matter is organic carbon [34].

Description of the study varieties

The study varieties were selected based on their yield potential, response to fertilizer application and agroecology. In this regard, BH540 and Limu (P3812W) maize varieties fulfill the above criteria and chosen for the study. The varieties are well adapted to low and mid altitudes extending from 1000 to 2000 m above sea level [35]. The seeds of the maize varieties are synthetic that are produced from intercrossed inbred lines. BH540 is a hybrid of SC22 female intercrossed with 124.b (113) male inbred lines, released in 1995. Similarly, Limu is produced when 1BMP female is intercrossed with 12 GG male inbred lines and released in 2012 [36]. BH540 has an average plant height and days to maturity of 250 cm and 145 days, respectively. It also has a yield potential of 8.0–9.0 t ha−1 and 5.0–6.5 t ha−1 on research and farmers' fields, respectively [37]. Similarly, Limu variety has an average plant height and days to maturity of 267 cm and 148 days, respectively [38]. Limu variety produces grain yield of 8.0–11.3 ton ha−1 on research sites, while 6.5–7.5 ton ha−1 on farmers’ fields [39]. The selected maize varieties were obtained from Adet Agricultural Research Center, Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia.

Experimental area preparation

The land used for the experiment has not been cultivated for 5 years. It was tilled three times with manpower until the soil particles become fine and smooth (Fig. S1). The study was carried out on an area of 55 m2 (5 m × 11 m). Since the experiment was conducted during the dry season, water line was supplied to the experimental area. Before sowing, the area was irrigated intermittently to speed up germination of weeds and exhaust their soil seed bank (Fig. S1b). Once the preparation completed, the perimeter of the area was fenced with wooden pool and wire mesh to prevent the entrance of grazing animals and apes (Fig. S1d).

Collection of cattle urine

Urine was collected from a local cattle variety near Bahir Dar University. After collection, the urine was immediately stored in 5 L capacity airtight plastic container to avoid hydrolysis of urea. Throughout the experiment, the urine was collected from one cattle herd of the same farmer and was performed for every two weeks. The urine was stored at room temperature until used. Due to the sedimentation of nutrients during storage, the urine was shaken just before application.

Experimental design and treatments

The field experiment was conducted in a randomized complete block design with three replications. The experiment consisted of two maize varieties and four treatments (control-without fertilizer, 100 % cattle urine, 50 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer). The collected urine was directly used for 100 % cattle urine, while cattle urine mixed with distilled water in 1:1 ratio represents 50 % cattle urine treatment. The recommended dose of Diammonium phosphate, DAP and urea were used as an inorganic fertilizer treatment. The blocks had 1 m spacing. In each block, there were eight plots with a size of 1 m2 and 42.9 cm far apart. In each plot, two rows with 60 cm spacing were set out. Each row was set 20 cm away from the edge to avoid edge effect. There was 30 cm spacing between plants. The study maize varieties and treatments were assigned randomly to plots. Two seeds were sown in each hole. After emergence, the seedlings were thinned to one per hole.

Application of treatments and management

Cattle urine at two concentration levels and the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizers were used during the experiment. Maize plants in the control received neither cattle urine nor inorganic fertilizers. The rate of cattle urine applied was determined based on previous scientific reports. Accordingly, the cattle urine was applied at the rate of 5000 L ha−1 as described by Ref. [40]. The application of cattle urine was started at sowing and continued for every two weeks interval. It was terminated before harvest as mentioned by Ref. [23]. Applications were carried out after 5:00 p.m. to minimize loss of nitrogen as ammonia to the air. At each application period, 83.3 mL of each cattle urine concentration (100 % cattle urine and 50 % cattle urine) was applied in a circular line around and 10 cm far from each plant. The 100 and 50 % cattle urine concentrations are equivalent to 30.55 kg N ha−1 and 15.28 kg N ha −1 respectively as described by Ref. [41]. DAP and urea were applied at the rate of 100 kg ha−1 according to Ref. [35]. DAP was applied at sowing while urea into two splits, half at sowing and the remaining at 30 days after sowing (DAS). Maize plants were irrigated with equal volume of water as required in the morning and afternoon. The common agronomic practices such as weeding, digging and pest control were carried out as required.

Measurement of growth parameters

In this study, growth parameters such as plant height, leaf length, width and other derived parameters were measured 60, 90 and 120 days after sowing. Four plants from each variety, treatment and plot were randomly selected and tagged before data collection. When tagged plants are lost, equivalent plants were used from each treatment, variety and plot for subsequent data collections. Plant height was measured from the ground level to the tip of the youngest fully opened leaf. The number of green leaves per plant was counted and recorded at each data collection period. The length of fully opened leaf lamina was measured from the leaf base to the tip while width at the maximum point using a tape meter. The leaf area of green leaves was calculated according to Ref. [42] as Leaf area (m2) = length x width x 0.75 (shape factor). Leaf area index (LAI) was calculated as leaf area/ground area of plants. Determination of yield and related traits

At maturity, grain yield and related traits were determined after harvest. The plants were hand-harvested carefully and the panicles cut from the parent plant. The outer sheath of each panicle was removed, air-dried and weighed. Before shelling, panicle diameter was measured at the widest portion while panicle length from the base to the tip using tape meter. The grains were shelled and their moisture content was adjusted to 15–20 %. The grain weight per plant was measured using a sensitive beam balance and expressed in gram. Hundred seed weight was determined by counting 100 seeds from shelled panicles. Grain number per panicle was calculated as the ratio of grain weight per plant to the corresponding 100 seed weight multiplied by 100 as described by Ref. [44] with slight modification. The shelling percentage is one of the yield quality measurements that should be estimated. The average shelling percentage of maize panicles is usually about 80 % when plants are harvested with 20–25 % moisture content, though this may depend on the agro-ecological zone [45]. Thus, shelling percentage was estimated as seed weight divided by panicle weight multiplied by 100 [46]. Grain yield was determined from grain weight per plant, which was further converted into grain weight per plot. The grain yield was finally reported as kg ha−1.

Nitrogen use efficiency and harvest index

Nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) is the ratio of outputs/yield to inputs/applied N fertilizer. Several findings have shown that crops use only 50 % of the applied N effectively, while the rest is lost to the environment [47]. In the present study, fertilizer-based (agronomic efficiency, Nitrogen recovery efficiency and nitrogen use efficiency) and plant-based (plant nitrogen uptake and internal efficiency) NUE indices were evaluated. The fertilizer-based NUEs describe the amount of fertilizer applied relative to various plant parameters such as above ground biomass, yield or N content. The plant-based NUEs, on the other hand, focus on the allocation of plant tissue N to yield or yield N for identifying plant genotypes with better capability of allocating N towards the economic portion of plants [48]. Statistical analysis

The collected data were first checked for normality and homogeneity of variances using SPSS version 22. This was followed by multivariate analysis using General Linear Model (GLM). In the process, the fixed factors entered into the model include variety and fertilizer applications/treatments. The measured variables were entered into the dependent variables box and analyzed at p < 0.05. Mean differences were performed using honestly significant difference (HSD) Tukey's test at an error probability of p < 0.05. The relationship between measured variables was evaluated by two-sided Pearson correlation and linear regression analysis at p < 0.05.

Results

The average pH, total nitrogen, organic carbon and organic matter of the soil of the experimental site were found to be 6.7, 0.09 %, 0.87 % and 1.74 %, respectively. This showed that the soil had a lower nutrient content where the addition of fertilizer was compulsory.

Growth responses of maize varieties

The application of cattle urine significantly (p < 0.05) increased growth of maize varieties during the crop growth period, relative to the controls (Fig. 2; Table 1; Table S1). Although plant height showed an increasing trend through time, the variation among treatments was non-significant in both varieties except control plants of BH540 (Fig. 2). The control had the shortest height at 60, 90 and 120 DAS (Fig. 2). In comparison to the control, greater plant height increment was noticed in BH540 maize variety due to fertilizers. In this variety, a 20.60, 21.88 and 20.60 % increment in plant height was recorded under 100, 50 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizers, respectively at 120 DAS (Fig. 2; Table S1), while height increased only by 1.5, 9.18 and 0.9 % in Limu variety. The difference between cattle urine and inorganic fertilizer effects on plant height was non-significant in both varieties throughout the crop growth period (Fig. 2; Table S1).

Similar to plant height, marked differences were recorded in fertilizer treated plants of both varieties relative to controls for leaf length, width, number of green leaves, leaf area and leaf area index at all data collection periods (Table 1; Table S1). The application of fertilizers showed an increasing growth trend in both maize varieties. For instance, leaf area showed a remarkable increase from 60 to 120 DAS. On average, leaf area increased from 0.61, 0.63 and 0.67 m2 to 6.78, 6.13 and 6.55 m2 in 100, 50 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer treated plants, respectively, relative to controls. However, the effect of fertilizers was higher on BH540 variety than Limu (Table 1; Table S1). In this regard, the leaf length and width of BH540 plants increased by 14.73, 8.40, 11.99 % and 22.93, 21.18, 22.38 % when treated with 100, 50 % cattle urine and inorganic fertilizer, respectively, at 120 DAS compared to controls (Table 1; Table S1). About 24.50, 26.35 and 10.65 % increment in number of leaves was recorded in BH540 plants treated with 100, 50 % cattle urine and inorganic fertilizer, respectively with reference to controls. Limu plants treated with 100 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer had significantly higher number of green leaves than other treatments of both varieties at 60 DAS, which reached to equivalent number at 120 DAS. Contrary to this, Limu plants had remarkably narrower leaf width under all treatments than BH540 plants treated with 100 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer at 120 DAS (Table 1; Table S1). In another development, 33.92, 28.89, 34.79 % and 34.62, 29.47, 35.39 % increment in leaf area and leaf area index were recorded by BH540 plants treated with 100, 50 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer, respectively, relative to control (Table 1; Table S1). In Limu variety, only 18.65, 6.89, 10.93 % and 15.09, 13.16, 17.05 % increment were noted in leaf area and leaf area index in plants nourished with 100, 50 % cattle urine and inorganic fertilizers, respectively, compared to control (Table 1; Table S1). In general, 100 % cattle urine and the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer had a comparative effect on all growth parameters of both maize varieties.

According to the results of the two-way ANOVA, variety, treatment and their interaction influenced the growth responses of maize varieties. Variety showed a significant effect on NGL at all data collection periods at p < 0.05 (Table 2). Treatments had a remarkable influence on all growth parameters at the three data collection days (Table 2). This shows that the growth of maize varieties was primarily determined by the fertilizer application than varietal variation and the interaction effect. The interaction between variety and treatment, on the other hand had a significant effect on PH, LL, NGL, LA and LAI at 60 DAS, LW and NGL at 90 DAS and LW at 120 DAS. The results demonstrated that NGL was significantly affected by variety, treatment and their interaction at all data collection periods (Table 2). In general, treatments and the interaction effect had a detrimental positive impact on the growth of maize varieties in this study.

Effects of cattle urine on yield and related traits of maize varieties

The cattle urine and inorganic fertilizer applications brought a positive and significant effect on yield and related traits. The 100 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer had comparable results in both varieties followed by 50 % cattle urine (Fig. 3; Table 3; Table S1). A significant difference was recorded between controls and fertilized plants of the two varieties. Fertilized plants had significantly higher PL, PD, GWPP, HSW and grain yield compared to controls at p < 0.05 (Fig. 3; Table 3). Remarkably higher HSW and GNPP were obtained from 100 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer treated plants compared to 50 % cattle urine (Fig. 3; Table S1). Similarly, 56.37, 39.62 and 56.72 % average increment in PL was found in plants treated with 100, 50 % cattle urine and the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer, respectively. PD on the other hand increased by 24.15, 16.67 and 19.97 % in fertilized plants in the order mentioned above, respectively, compared to controls (Table 3; Table S1). The application of fertilizers produced significantly higher GNPP compared to controls. Of the varieties and treatments, Limu plants fertilized with 50 % cattle urine had considerably lower GNPP (Fig. 3d). Fertilized plants of BH540 and Limu showed 3.4 and 2.9 folds mean increment in GNPP, respectively, as compared to controls. On the other hand, GWPP showed more than 7.0, 4.5 and 7.5 folds average increment in 100, 50 % cattle urine and the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer treated plants, respectively, compared to controls (Fig. 3c). HSW also showed more than 2.0, 1.7 and 2.5 fold increment in fertilized plants (100, 50 % cattle urine and inorganic fertilizer), respectively, in comparison with controls (Fig. 3b). 526.76, 326.12 and 542.91 % average grain yield (kg ha−1) increment was recorded due to application of 100, 50 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer, respectively, compared to controls (Fig. 3a). In 50 % cattle urine fertilized maize, 39.87 and 38.10 % grain yield reductions were recorded relative to recommended dose and 100 % cattle urine, respectively. However, the difference between the two varieties in grain yield and related trait responses was non-significant. Limu plants treated with the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizers had the highest shelling percentage (87.40 %) followed by BH540 plants treated with 100 % cattle urine (Table 3; Table S1).

Nitrogen use efficiency

According to the fertilizer-based determination of NUE indices, the application of 50 % cattle urine resulted in a significantly (p < 0.05) higher NUE compared to other treatments. The higher value was strictly related to the lower N fertilizer rate of the treatment (Fig. 4; Table S2). The N content in the soil was not considered in the determination of NUE. Similar results were found for NAE, NRE and IE. Higher grain yield kg−1 of N applied was produced from cattle urine applications. Consequently, on average, NAE increased by 68.32 and 69.79 % due to 100 and 50 % cattle urine application, respectively, compared to the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer (Fig. 4; Table S2). Likewise, more N was recovered from cattle urine treatments. The application of 50 % cattle urine led to a considerably higher NRE (1.31 kg of N recovered in the aboveground biomass kg−1 of N applied) followed by 100 % cattle urine (1.19 kg kg−1) compared to the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer. On average, NRE increased by 65.55 and 68.70 % with 100 and 50 % cattle urine application compared to the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer (Fig. 4).Variety wise, Limu had higher NRE than BH540 plants at 50 % cattle urine application (Fig. 4). The PNU value was found to be inconsistent between varieties and among treatments. BH540 plants treated with inorganic fertilizer had significantly higher PNU than others at p < 0.05, while plants treated with 100 % cattle urine had moderate and statistically higher PNU than controls (Fig. 4). IE on the other hand describes the conversion efficiency of accumulated plant N to grain yield. In this regard, the highest average proportion of the accumulated N was converted to grain yield (125.56 kg kg−1) in plants treated with the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer followed by 50 % cattle urine application (121.87 kg kg−1). The difference between control and fertilized plants in IE was statistically significant at p < 0.05 (Fig. 4; Table S2).

Discussion

The soil analysis results of the experimental site revealed lower nutrient (nitrogen, organic carbon and organic matter) content compared to a typical soil in Ethiopia. According to Ref. [54], a typical pellic vertisol soil has a pH of 6.8, 0.14 % total nitrogen, 1.15 % organic carbon and 2.3 % organic matter. This shows that the soil organic carbon and total nitrogen values of the current study site are below the critical concentrations 2 % [55] and 0.2 % [56] for maize, respectively, supporting the general fact that Ethiopian soils are deficient in nitrogen. This indicates that the application of fertilizers for the growth of maize in the experimental site was mandatory.

Growth responses of maize varieties

According to the findings of the present study, the application of cattle urine was found as effective as the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer. A comparable growth increment was recorded in maize varieties under both fertilizers. This is corroborated by the reports of several studies on different crops and vegetables at various areas. Ref. [18] have found higher plant height, number of functional leaves and leaf area of fodder maize due to the foliar application of 10 % cattle urine. Moreover, the findings of the current study agree with the findings of Ref. [57] and Ref. [58], who have disclosed higher number of leaves in maize varieties under NPSB fertilization and different climatic conditions, respectively. Likewise, maize plants grown under soil fertilized with cow urine have significantly higher plant height, number of leaves, leaf area and leaf area index [19]. Higher mean plant height, number of leaves and leaf area index were recorded than other maize varieties and BH540 treated with different levels of NPSB (0, 50, 75, 100, 125 and 150 kg ha−1) [57] and 100 kg N ha−1 [59]. Similar effects of cattle urine on the growth of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L), barley (Hordeum vulgare L.), sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L.) and mustard (Brassica rapa L.) have been reported by Refs. [[21], [22], [23], [24]], respectively. The application of cow urine at 100 mL L−1 concentration has brought a significant influence on plant height, leaf length and leaf width of sorghum [23]. Similarly, the use of 1.5 and 2 folds of the recommended dose of N in the form of cattle urine have significantly increased plant height, number of branches and number of leaves in tomato [21]. The suitability of cattle urine for the growth of crops might be related to the rich nutrient composition, the rapid availability of nutrients and possession of growth hormones [30]. The higher performance of the study maize varieties under cattle urine nourishment might be related to the higher plant height, leaves, leaf area and leaf area index. The higher leaf area and leaf area index under cattle urine and inorganic fertilizer applications might be related to the higher nitrogen content, indicating the significant role of nitrogen for the development of leaf in maize. This further increases the light capturing capacity of the leaf that subsequently speeds up the rate of photosynthesis and thus biomass production [59].

The results revealed the strong impact of treatments and the interaction (variety x treatment) on growth parameters than variety (Table 2). Most of the variations in growth parameters were caused by treatments and the interaction effect. The findings of Ref. [60] agree with the reports of the present study in that the interaction effect (variety x NPS fertilizers) has significantly affected plant height of the maize varieties. Opposite to our result, a significant variation in plant height [57,60,61], number of leaves, leaf area and leaf area index [57,58,61] has been found due to variety.

Effect of cattle urine on yield and yield related traits of maize

The effect of cattle urine on the grain yield and related traits of maize varieties was significantly higher than the control and equivalent to the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizers. The use of 100 % cattle urine increased grain yield and panicle length approximately by more than 500 % and 50 %, respectively. Similarly, higher grain yield has been obtained in other maize varieties treated with 10 % cattle urine [17] and different urine sources such as cattle, human and goat urine [14]. A comparable maize grain yield has also been obtained from other organic fertilizers such as liquid manure [51] and rapeseed waste [62] relative to inorganic fertilizers. Inconsistent with this, a lower grain yield (4900 kg ha−1) and grain number per panicle (423.5) have been found due to the application of 12.5 t ha−1 FYM plus 34 300 L ha−1 cattle urine [15]. According to Ref. [60], approximately 8700 kg ha−1 grain yield has been obtained due to 100 kg NPS ha−1 fertilizer application, which fits with the findings of the present study. This signifies the fact that cattle urine can be widely used as the best substitute for inorganic fertilizers.

The results of the present study demonstrated a significantly higher biomass due to the application of 100 % cattle urine (4060 kg ha−1) and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizers (4178.34 kg ha−1). A consistent increase in biomass has been produced in different maize varieties with the addition of 12.5 t ha−1 FYM +34 300 L ha−1 cattle urine [15], foliar application of 10 % cattle urine [17] and 75 000 L ha−1 biourine [19]. A similar increase in biomass has been obtained from the application of 5 t ha−1 FYM [16] and 150 kg NPS ha−1 [60]. Similarly, higher biomass production has been reported in other crops and vegetables due to application of cattle urine. For instance, application of 120 kg N ha−1 + cow urine, 80 % cow urine, 10 % cattle urine, 0–1200 L ha−1, 100 mL L−1 cattle urine have significantly increased biomass in rice [20], mustard [24], chlorella sp [27], wheat [26] and sorghum [23], respectively. About 67.19 and 66.01 % harvest index was obtained from Limu and BH540 varieties treated with recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer, respectively, followed by 100 % cattle urine. Opposite to the present study, a significantly lower harvest index ranging from 9.18 to 23.41 % has been found in maize varieties treated with NPSB fertilizers [57]. Correspondingly, lower harvest indices have been found in different maize varieties grown under 30, 60, 90 and 120 kg N ha−1 amended soils [60,63]. This indicates that the two varieties in the present study converted more than 50 % of the photosynthetic products into grain yield.

The application of fertilizers (treatments) had significantly affected all grain yield and related traits of maize varieties. The interaction had a significant effect only on hundred seed weight and grain number per panicle. In connection with this, Ref. [60] have disclosed the significant effect of treatments (NPS and N levels) on grain number per panicle in maize varieties in Ethiopia. The interaction between maize varieties including BH540 and fertilizer rates (50 and 100 kg N ha−1) has significantly affected all yield and yield related traits in maize [59]. Similarly, the interactions between variety x NPS and NPS x N levels have a significant effect on panicle diameter and grain yield [60]. A corresponding effect of treatment and interaction (fertilizers x variety) on grain number per panicle and hundred seed weight in maize have been reported due to microdosing application of inorganic fertilizers [61]. Opposite to the present study, Ref. [[59], [60], [61]] have found a significant effect of variety on panicle length, grain yield, biomass and harvest index in maize varieties. In other studies, variety and fertilization have signiﬁcantly aﬀected the total dry matter of maize varieties during the growing period with different climatic conditions [61]. These inconsistent responses of maize varieties to fertilizers might be related to the variability in environmental factors, the associated soil and water resources utilization capability of each variety [64].

The results of the present study revealed a comparable mean grain yield performance of maize varieties when treated with100 % cattle urine (7883.79 kg ha−1) and recommended dose of NP fertilizers (8119.11 kg ha−1). This disagrees with the findings of Ref. [[65], [66], [67], [68]], who have reported higher grain yield of maize varieties due to inorganic fertilizers than organic fertilizers. The reasons for the lower grain yield under organic fertilizers might be related to the slow rate of decomposition and nutrient release to the soil. Organic fertilizers have long-term effect and only one fifth of the nutrients in animal manure are released during the first season following application. The nutrients are released only very slowly at a rate of 2–4 % in a year period [68,69]. However, the comparable yield enhancement by 100 % cattle urine may be due to the availability of N as urea and ammonium and phosphorus as phosphate ion, which are forms quickly released to the soil [31]. The application of cattle urine to the soil has positive effects on the utilization and uptake of several other nutrients. The use of cattle urine adds nutrients to the soil and changes the chemical property, both of which have a significant effect on nutrient availability and uptake by plants. These facts are supported by the findings of several studies. For instance, Ref. [16] have found significantly higher nitrogen, phosphorous, potassium, iron, zinc and copper uptake by fodder maize due to soil application of cattle urine. Similarly, the application of fresh cattle urine significantly increased mineralization and thus high N, which is associated with elevated NH3 concentrations [41]. Moreover, urine-enriched biochar (BU + M) significantly improved soil pH, organic carbon, and soil nutrient levels (N, P, and K). Total N, available P, and K have been significantly higher in urine-enriched biochar treated soil and brought a corresponding increase in NPK uptake by plants [81]. The application of urine has increased the organic carbon content, total nitrogen, potassium, and available phosphorus [82]. In general, the grain yield obtained due to cattle urine application is two folds greater than the national average yield of maize (2.96 t ha−1) for the years 2003–2020 [5], indicating the big potential of cattle urine to boost the productivity of maize in the country.

Nitrogen use efficiency responses of maize varieties

The results of the present study proved a significantly greater NUE, NAE, NRE and IE due to cattle urine applications. The applications increased these indices on average by more than 65 % compared to the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizers (Table 4). Correspondingly, significant variations in NUE indices between maize varieties have been found at 30, 60, 90 and 120 kg N ha−1 rates [63]. However, the NUE index values are very much lower than the present study. The NUE, NAE, NRE and PNU values range from 0.05 to 0.2, 15.1–44.4, −0.003 - 0.003 and 0.03–0.10 kg kg−1, respectively [63]. Similarly, Ref. [61] have found NAE of 51.3–59.7 kg grain kg−1 N in drought tolerant maize varieties due to microdosing treatment of N fertilizers. Moreover, they have found out that microdosing treatments have more NAE (55 kg grain kg−1) than the recommended rate and application type (27 kg grain kg−1 N) [61]. In the present study, the highest PNU was obtained from maize plants treated with the recommended dose of inorganic fertilizer. Moreover, as the dose of N applied increases, a parallel increment in PNU was observed. This agrees with the findings of Ref. [50,52,62]. Ref. [52] have found 62, 124 and 190 % increment in PNU at 30, 60 and 120 kg N ha−1 fertilizer applications, respectively, compared to control. The increased trend of PNU with an increasing N dose might be due to the high aboveground biomass yield [50,52]. In another experiment, Ref. [62] have found a higher PNU of inorganic fertilizer followed by rapeseed waste > chicken manure > bamboo tealeaf > cow manure > bamboo compost. In a comparison study of conventional and organic farming, a higher NUE has been found in 60 %, a similar result in 37 % and lower value in only 3 % of the organic farming [70]. Organic farming is found to have the lowest N balances (the difference between N inputs and outputs) and the highest NUE. Thus, the promotion, expansion and implementation of organic farming to the largest scale minimize N balances and increase the NUE of crop production [70]. The differences between N supply dynamics of various fertilizers and their effect on grain yield and total aboveground PNU of maize have been well separated by NUE, NAE and NRE [51].

According to the results of the present study, NUE, NAE and NRE declined linearly with increasing dose of N fertilizers. This is in line with the findings of Ref. [50,52,71,72], who have found a lower NUE, NAE and NRE in maize with the use of higher doses of N fertilizers. Therefore, increasing the N dose to enhance crop productivity is not an effective and recommended strategy in agricultural ecosystems. In addition to its NUE reduction effect, application of higher doses of N fertilizers pollutes the environment through volatilization, surface runoff, leaching and others [47].

Relationship between growth, yield and yield related traits

The results of the present study demonstrated a very strong, positive and significant relationship between LAI and LL, LW, which is in line with the reports of Ref. [57]. This might be due the linear relationship among the three growth parameters. LAI was calculated as the product of LL, LW and a correction factor divided by the area around the plant. Nevertheless, the general trend showed a stronger association among yield and yield related traits than growth parameters. Evidently, grain yield had a very strong, positive and significant association with grain weight per plant, 100 seed weight, grain number per panicle, panicle diameter and length. This is corroborated by the findings of Ref. [[73], [74], [75], [76]]. According to these authors, grain yield has a strong positive correlation with 100 seed weight, grain number per panicle, panicle length and diameter under varying levels of inorganic and organic fertilizers. This implies that fertilizers have a positive effect on yield and yield related traits and grain yield is the ultimate product of several morphological and physiological processes in the life of the crop.

Relationship between biomass, grain yield and nitrogen use efficiency indices

In the current study, aboveground biomass and NRE showed a strong, positive and significant association with HI, aboveground biomass N concentration, PNU and IE. Similarly, NUE had a positive and significant correlation with NAE, grain yield and NRE. This is in line with the findings of Ref. [57,63,68,74]. The application of fertilizers enhances the ability of maize to uptake N that subsequently increase aboveground biomass, grain yield and harvest index through increased NRE, NAE and IE. Of these, the strong positive association between grain yield and NAE indicates that the applied N fertilizer has significantly increased grain yield compared to unfertilized control [49,50]. Furthermore, Ref. [[77], [78], [79], [80]] have found a strong association among N uptake, aboveground biomass accumulation and foliar N content in grasses, maize, king grass and maize, respectively. According to Ref. [80], a significantly stronger relationship has been recorded between total biomass yield and nutrient uptake in sweet corn whereby 98–99 % of the variation in total biomass could be explained by its nutrient uptake capacity. Similarly, the application of N fertilizers resulted in an increased N uptake by plants, which subsequently enhanced biomass production and foliar N content in Sanguisorba officinalis and Vicia sepium [77], Pennisetum purpureum [79] and Zea mays [78].

Conclusion

The results of the present study showed the significant effect of cattle urine on the growth, yield and related traits, and nitrogen use efficiency indices of the studied maize varieties. The use of 100 % cattle urine and recommended dose of inorganic fertilizers had a significantly higher and comparable influence on plant height, leaf area, panicle length and diameter, grain weight per plant and grain yield (kg ha−1) followed by 50 % cattle urine application. On the other hand, the use of 50 % cattle urine resulted in a significantly higher nitrogen use efficiency, N agronomic efficiency, N recovery efficiency and internal efficiency. The overall performance of the studied maize varieties under the different fertilizer applications showed the potential of cattle urine as a reliable substitute for inorganic fertilizers in maize production. This indicates that the use of cattle urine improves soil fertility and reduces environmental pollution and economic burden of farmers. However, further studies considering the chemical composition analysis of cattle urine and evaluation across seasons with the active involvement of smallholder farmers are highly recommended.
