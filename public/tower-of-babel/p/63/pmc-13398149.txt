Effect of Concomitant Medications on Adverse Event Frequency, Severity, and Time to Resolution in a Cenobamate Open‐Label Trial
Authors: Bernhard J Steinhoff, Antonio Gil‐Nagel, Elena Alvarez‐Baron, John Paul Leach, Karthinathan Thangavelu, Yaroslav Winter, Vicente Villanueva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and PurposePolytherapy in epilepsy is associated with a higher risk of adverse events, poorer adherence, and impaired quality of life. Thus, reducing concomitant antiseizure medications (ASMs) and/or using a newly added ASM such as cenobamate with only 1 other ASM (i.e., bitherapy) may improve tolerability. This post hoc analysis evaluated the impact of the number and type of concomitant ASMs on treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) that occurred in the cenobamate C021 open‐label clinical trial.MethodsAdjunctive cenobamate was initiated in adults (aged 18–70 years) with uncontrolled focal seizures who were taking 1 to 3 ASMs. The number and type of concomitant ASMs and the frequency, severity, and time to resolution of TEAEs were assessed.ResultsThe most frequently reported (≥ 10%) TEAEs in the overall population (n = 1340) were somnolence, dizziness, fatigue, and headache. The percentage of patients with dizziness, somnolence, and fatigue increased with increasing numbers of concomitant ASMs, especially when combined with benzodiazepines, chiefly clobazam, and in patients taking ≥ 2 sodium channel blockers. Median time to resolution of frequently reported TEAEs was shorter in patients taking fewer concomitant ASMs, with the exception of fatigue. The proportion of patients experiencing more severe TEAEs (except for headache) also increased with increasing numbers of concomitant ASMs.ConclusionsPatients taking fewer concomitant ASMs reported better tolerability as evidenced by fewer and less severe AEs and faster time to resolution. These findings emphasize the importance of optimizing concomitant ASM regimens through dose adjustments, ASM substitution, and treatment simplification to improve tolerability.Keywords: adverse drug event, antiseizure medications, clinical trial, epilepsy, focal seizures

Introduction

Nearly half of people with newly‐diagnosed epilepsy will achieve seizure freedom with the first antiseizure medication (ASM); ultimately, those continuing to experience uncontrolled seizures will most likely receive polytherapy [1, 2]. As the drug load increases, treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) tend to increase, necessitating medication and dose adjustments to maximize efficacy and minimize tolerability issues [3]. Newer ASMs such as cenobamate offer increased seizure control [4, 5] and improved quality of life for some people with epilepsy [6, 7]. Analyses of the cenobamate clinical studies demonstrated that the number and type of concomitant ASMs may contribute to these AEs [8, 9, 10]. A phase 3, multicenter, open‐label clinical trial NCT02535091 (Study C021; Sperling et al. 2020 Epilepsia [11]) evaluated the safety and tolerability of adjunctive cenobamate in people with epilepsy and uncontrolled focal seizures. The most commonly reported TEAEs were somnolence, dizziness, fatigue, and headache. No cases of drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) were reported, which may be attributed to initiating cenobamate at a lower dose and slowing the titration rate in this study [11] compared with earlier cenobamate clinical trials [4, 5]. These results led to the development of the current titration schedule recommended in the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) [12].

Previous post hoc analyses of cenobamate clinical trials revealed that some adverse events (AEs; eg, nervous system disorders such as somnolence, dizziness, fatigue, and headache) occurred more frequently in the titration phase than in the maintenance phase of cenobamate treatment, and the majority of these AEs occurring during titration resolved on their own, with a median duration of 15 days [8]. In response to these findings, strategies for managing cenobamate therapy to maximize effectiveness and tolerability have been proposed, including proactive dose reductions of concomitant [10, 13, 14] sodium channel blockers (SCBs), clobazam, phenobarbital, and phenytoin during cenobamate titration [13, 14, 15, 16]. The objective of this post hoc analysis was to evaluate how the frequency, severity, and time to resolution of commonly occurring TEAEs observed throughout the C021 study were impacted by the number and type of concomitant ASMs.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

Study C021 was a multicenter, multinational, open‐label safety study that enrolled adults aged 18 to 70 years with a diagnosis of focal (partial onset) epilepsy who were taking 1 to 3 ASMs, the results of which have been published previously [11]. A screening period of up to 21 days was followed by a 12‐month, open‐label treatment phase consisting of 12 weeks of titration followed by a maintenance phase. Inclusion criteria stipulated that seizures had to be uncontrolled within the previous 2 years despite therapy with at least 1 ASM. Participants were required to have been taking stable doses of 1 to 3 concomitant ASMs for ≥ 3 weeks before initiation of cenobamate therapy. Main exclusion criteria included those patients with a medical history of drug‐induced rash, those with a history of status epilepticus in the 3 months prior to screening, and those with clinically significant psychiatric illness or suicidal behavior.

For participants taking concomitant phenytoin or phenobarbital, doses were allowed to be reduced by 25%–33% during the titration phase as clinically indicated due to increased exposure when administered in combination with cenobamate [11]. For these participants, dose adjustments of cenobamate were not allowed during titration, and other concomitant ASMs were not permitted to be removed, added, or adjusted during the titration phase. For participants not receiving concomitant phenytoin or phenobarbital, cenobamate dose adjustments were allowed during the titration phase, along with adjustments to concomitant ASMs. Cenobamate doses and concomitant ASMs, including phenytoin and phenobarbital, could be adjusted during the maintenance phase. Cenobamate monotherapy was not allowed.

During titration, cenobamate was initiated at 12.5 mg/day for 2 weeks, increased to 25 mg/day for 2 weeks, and further increased to 50 mg/day for 2 weeks. Following this, the dose was then titrated up by 50 mg/day increments every 2 weeks until reaching the target dose of 200 mg/day. The minimum cenobamate dose permitted was 50 mg once daily, and further dose increases up to 400 mg/day were allowed using biweekly 50‐mg/day increments.

Study C021 was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of Good Clinical Practice according to the International Conference on Harmonization guidelines and was compliant with all applicable country‐specific regulations. The protocol was approved by an independent ethics committee or institutional review board at each site according to local regulations, and written informed consent was obtained from each person prior to participating in the study.

Concomitant ASMs

Concomitant medications recorded at baseline included those that were initiated prior to and were ongoing at the commencement of cenobamate treatment. This post hoc analysis focused on concomitant ASMs for which dose adjustments might have been needed when used with cenobamate [10, 14] and included clobazam, other benzodiazepines, lacosamide, and all so‐called SCBs (phenytoin, lamotrigine, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lacosamide, and eslicarbazepine acetate). There were too few patients taking concomitant phenobarbital (n = 82) to include as a separate analysis.

Safety Outcomes

Safety assessments included reports of TEAEs (which were coded with the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities [MedDRA] version 20.0), clinical laboratory tests, 12‐lead electrocardiographic measurements, vital signs, neurologic and physical exams, Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale scores, and monthly AE reviews for hypersensitivity and DRESS. All patients who were enrolled and received at least 1 dose of cenobamate were included in the safety analysis; data were summarized with descriptive statistics.

Data Analysis

For this post hoc analysis, the assessment of cenobamate tolerability was based on the frequency, severity, and time to resolution of the most frequently reported TEAEs, as well as by the number and type of concomitant ASMs. All enrolled patients who received at least 1 dose of cenobamate were included in the safety analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies with percentages) and risk differences (RDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to summarize the data. Where appropriate, statistical comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi‐square test and Fisher's exact test, with nominal two‐sided p‐values reported.

Safety Outcomes

Impact of Number of Concomitant ASMs on Most Frequently Reported TEAEs

TEAEs were reported in 90.2% of patients (1209/1340) included in this study (Table 2). The most frequently occurring TEAEs observed in ≥ 10% of participants included somnolence (416/1340; 31.0%), dizziness (382/1340; 28.5%), fatigue (259/1340; 19.3%), and headache (228/1340; 17.0%). A total of 177 participants (13.2%) discontinued treatment due to TEAEs.

The percentage of patients experiencing dizziness, somnolence, and fatigue increased as the number of concomitant ASMs increased, particularly for those patients taking 2 or more concomitant ASMs (Figure 1). Dizziness, somnolence, and fatigue were reported in 18.6% (45/242), 23.6% (57/242), and 11.2% (27/242), respectively, of patients taking only 1 concomitant ASM, compared to 30.8% (337/1094), 32.8% (359/1094), and 21.1% (231/1094), respectively, of those taking at least 2 other ASMs. An analysis of risk differences (RD) was calculated between those taking ≥ 2 ASMs vs. 1 ASM for dizziness, somnolence, fatigue, and headache. The RD between the groups for dizziness was 12.2% (95% CI, 6.6% to 17.8%; p < 0.001). Somnolence had an RD of 9.3% (95% CI, 3.2% to 15.3%; p = 0.0062), fatigue had an RD of 10.0% (95% CI, 5.3% to 14.6%; p < 0.001), and headache had an RD of −4.4% (95% CI, −9.9% to 1.2%; p = 0.1215).

The severity of TEAEs was also examined. Most events of dizziness, somnolence, fatigue, and headache were mild or moderate (Figure 2A–D). Severe events were observed more frequently in patients taking 2 or more concomitant ASMs, particularly for dizziness (1.3%; 14/1094) and somnolence (1.1%; 12/1094) (Figure 2B). The percentages of patients with severe events did not exceed 2.0% for any TEAE (Figure 2B–2D). The RDs for mild severity of dizziness, somnolence, and fatigue were 7.9% (95% CI, 2.5% to 13.2%), p = 0.0087; 6.0% (95% CI, 0.4% to 11.6%), p = 0.0566; and 6.5% (95% CI, 2.1% to 10.9%), p = 0.0109, respectively. Risk differences for moderate severity of dizziness, somnolence, and fatigue were 3.6% (95% CI, 1.3% to 5.9%), p = 0.0213; 2.6% (95% CI, −0.3% to 5.5%), p = 0.144; and 3.2% (95% CI, 1.6% to 4.8%), p = 0.0102, respectively. Higher percentages of patients taking ≥ 2 vs. 1 concomitant ASM discontinued treatment due to AEs (13.9% vs. 8.7%; p = 0.03) or SAEs (2.6 vs. 0.4%, p = 0.04).

Impact of Concomitant ASMs on Most Frequently Reported TEAEs

Among 278 patients taking benzodiazepines, the most common was clobazam (162 patients) followed by clonazepam (72 patients) and lorazepam (40 patients); all other benzodiazepines were used in fewer than 10 patients each. Sixteen patients took clobazam and another benzodiazepine. Higher percentages of patients taking concomitant clobazam/benzodiazepines experienced somnolence (53.7% and 42.4%, respectively) and fatigue (30.9% and 29.5%) compared to those not taking those ASMs (Figure 3A,B). The percentages reporting dizziness (39.9% vs. 25.2%, respectively) and fatigue (30.5% vs. 16.1%, respectively) were higher for patients taking the SCB lacosamide compared to those not taking lacosamide (Figure 3C). For patients on 2 or more concomitant SCBs, the percentages reporting dizziness (41.3%), fatigue (23.4%), and headache (23.9%) were generally greater compared to those taking 1 SCB or no SCBs (Figure 3D). Although dose adjustments are not recommended when adding cenobamate to levetiracetam or brivaracetam, given their frequent use by clinicians, these were included in a separate analysis. As expected, TEAE rates were similar with or without levetiracetam and brivaracetam (Table S1).

Compared to those not taking benzodiazepines, patients taking concomitant benzodiazepines were more likely to experience dizziness (RD, 10.8%; 95% CI, 4.5%–17.0%; p < 0.001), somnolence (RD, 14.4%; 95% CI, 8.0%–20.8%; p < 0.001), and fatigue (RD, 12.8%; 95% CI, 7.0%–18.6%; p < 0.001). For clobazam specifically, there was an increased risk of dizziness (RD, 12.5%; 95% CI, 4.6%–20.5%; p = 0.0013), somnolence (RD, 25.8%; 95% CI, 17.7%–33.9%; p < 0.001), and fatigue (RD, 13.1%; 95% CI, 5.7%–20.6%; p < 0.001) vs. those who were not taking clobazam. For lacosamide, there was an increased risk of dizziness (RD, 14.7%; 95% CI, 8.5%–20.8%; p < 0.001) and fatigue (RD, 14.4%; 95% CI, 8.7%–20.1%; p < 0.001) vs. those not taking lacosamide. Patients who were taking 2 or more concomitant SCBs were also more likely to experience dizziness (RD, 27%; 95% CI, 19.3%–34.7%; p < 0.001) and fatigue (RD, 9.1%; 95% CI, 2.2%–16.0%; p = 0.0115) compared to those taking none. For patients taking 2 or more concomitant SCBs compared to those taking only 1, there was an increased risk of dizziness (RD, 11.1%; 95% CI, 3.8% to 18.3%; p = 0.0025), fatigue (RD, 3.3%; 95% CI, −2.9% to 9.6%; p = 0.3321), and headache (RD, 8.9%; 95% CI, 2.7% to 15.0%; p = 0.0026). Finally, patients taking only 1 concomitant SCB had an increased risk of dizziness (RD, 15.9%; 95% CI, 10.8% to 21.0%; p < 0.001) and fatigue (RD, 5.8%; 95% CI, 0.9% to 10.6%; p = 0.036) compared to those not taking SCBs.

Time to Resolution of the First Occurrence of the Most Commonly Reported TEAEs

Following the first occurrence of the most frequently occurring TEAEs, the median time to resolution was shorter in patients taking 1 concomitant ASM vs. ≥ 2 (Figure 4). TEAEs resolved earlier in patients who were not taking concomitant SCBs compared to those who were (data not shown). For example, fatigue resolved in a median of 194 days in those taking ≥ 2 SCBs compared with 92 days in those taking no SCBs. The median time to resolution of the first occurrence of dizziness, fatigue, headache, nausea, somnolence, or viral upper respiratory tract infection was longer in patients taking 1 concomitant SCB compared with those taking ≥ 2 SCBs.

Other TEAEs of Interest

Certain bothersome or disabling TEAEs of interest reported in < 10% of patients are reported in Table S2. Diplopia, balance disorder, ataxia, fall, gait disturbance, irritability, nausea, and vomiting were reported in a higher percentage of patients taking ≥ 2 concomitant ASMs compared with 1 ASM (p < 0.05), whereas differences were not statistically significant for blurred vision, cognitive disorders, and psychotic disorder.

Discussion

This post hoc analysis demonstrated that the number of concomitant ASMs impacted the frequency, severity, and time to resolution of commonly reported TEAEs. The percentages of patients reporting dizziness, somnolence, and fatigue (but not headache) were increased when compared with patients taking fewer concomitant ASMs. Of note, while headache is a commonly reported TEAE in placebo‐controlled trials of ASMs, including cenobamate, the frequency of headache was not substantially greater among patients treated with cenobamate compared to placebo [4, 5]. The median time to resolution of the first occurrence of dizziness, somnolence, and headache was shorter in patients taking cenobamate with 1 concomitant ASM (bitherapy) compared with those taking cenobamate with 2 or more concomitant ASMs (polytherapy). The majority of TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity, and severe events were mostly observed in those taking 2 or more concomitant ASMs.

The MOAs of concomitant ASMs may influence the prevalence of select TEAEs (e.g., somnolence). This topic has been addressed previously in expert consensus papers detailing recommendations for concomitant medication dose adjustments [13, 14, 15]. Drug–drug interactions occurring with adjunctive cenobamate may cause an increase in TEAEs and may be pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic (or both) in nature [10, 13]. A pharmacokinetic interaction is likely with concomitant clobazam that could lead to increased plasma concentrations of N‐desmethylclobazam (the active metabolite of clobazam), exacerbating somnolence [13]. This potential interaction is caused by cenobamate's inhibition of CYP2C19, leading to an increase in levels of the metabolite that has a half‐life of approximately 72 h [13]. As noted in this post hoc analysis, higher percentages of patients taking clobazam or any benzodiazepine experienced somnolence, dizziness, and fatigue compared to those who were not. Whether the tolerability issues in patients taking benzodiazepines other than clobazam occur on a pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic basis has not yet been investigated. In clinical practice, proactively lowering the dose of clobazam when used alongside cenobamate is recommended to mitigate the increased rate of side effects [13]. Serum levels of clobazam and its main active metabolite were not assessed, but measuring these in clinical practice may offer insight into individualized treatment management [13]. Patients taking 2 or more SCBs experienced increased dizziness, fatigue, and headache compared to those taking 1 concomitant SCB or none, chiefly due to pharmacodynamic interactions [13]. These results were confirmed by the RDs observed between those taking or not taking the specific drug(s). In addition to pharmacodynamic drug interactions between cenobamate and these ASMs, there is potential for pharmacokinetic drug interaction via inhibition of CYP2C19 with lacosamide and higher doses of cenobamate (~400 mg/day) [10, 17]. Again, this supports the proactive management of concomitant ASMs recommended in the consensus documents [14, 15]. Both proactive and reactive lowering of doses of these concomitant ASMs is recommended to improve tolerability and treatment adherence [10, 13, 14, 15].

A limitation of the original study was that clinicians were permitted to make changes to cenobamate doses and to concomitant ASMs, which could be confounding factors. However, these adjustments may more closely reflect real‐world treatment scenarios, taking into consideration that changes to concomitant ASM dosages are likely to be made much earlier during titration in current clinical practice. Efficacy data were not assessed systematically in this tolerability‐driven trial.

Conclusions

Initiating cenobamate therapy early in the treatment paradigm before patient drug regimens become complex has been recommended by clinical experts, as polytherapy may negatively affect adherence due to an increase in the number, type, or severity of TEAEs [14]. Proactively reducing the number of concomitant medications or down‐titrating their dosages when initiating cenobamate may be recommended, especially if AEs occur [13, 14, 15, 16]. This is particularly important for patients on concomitant SCBs or high doses of clobazam, or for those with a high overall drug load. Despite reductions in concomitant ASM drug load following the initiation of cenobamate therapy, efficacy has been shown to be maintained [18].

Patients taking fewer concomitant ASMs generally reported improved tolerability (as supported by fewer and less severe TEAEs) and faster time to resolution in this post hoc analysis of the phase 3 open‐label safety study. Although TEAEs occurred early in cenobamate treatment, they tended to resolve over time. Along with conclusions from other studies and guidance from the expert consensus report, the results of this analysis underscore the benefit of reducing concomitant ASMs to improve tolerability and retention on cenobamate.
