The 68Ge/68Ga generator for enhancing productivity and reducing costs in nuclear medicine: experience from a university hospital
Authors: Alessandra Guercio, Tess Martin, Silvia Migliari, Anna Gagliardi, Maura Scarlattei, Giorgio Baldari, Nicolas Martelli, Livia Ruffini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSustainability in healthcare, particularly Nuclear Medicine, is essential, especially in managing radioactive materials. Traditional centralized production of Fluorine-18 tracers for PET imaging faces challenges like transport logistics, decay losses and supply chain vulnerabilities. Decentralized production with radionuclide generators, such as the Germanium-68/Gallium-68 (68Ge/68Ga) generator, offers on-demand isotope production, improving accessibility and precision imaging. This study evaluates the long-term performance (8 years) of 68Ga-based radiopharmaceutical production in a Nuclear Medicine Division, focusing on efficiency, sustainability, and cost.ResultsBetween 2016 and 2024, 416 batches of 68GaCl₃ and 2455 batches of 68Ga-based radiopharmaceuticals were produced. Data from systematic quality controls on generator eluates and tracers, such as 68Ga-PSMA (1800 batches) and 68Ga-DOTATOC (610 batches), were evaluated. All 68GaCl₃ eluates met Ph. Eur. specifications, with negligible 68Ge breakthrough (mean 0.0000068%) and consistently high radiochemical purity (100%). Radiopharmaceuticals showed stable quality over time, with > 98% purity for 68Ga-DOTATOC and > 99% for 68Ga-PSMA. Generators performance remained reliable, with an average elution yield of 74.8% (range 63.4–84.7%) and up to 466 elutions/year reached in 2024 without quality degradation. The correlation between estimated and measured end-of-synthesis activities was strong (R2 ≈ 1), supporting accurate production planning. Clinical PET scans increased over sixfold, from 144 in 2016 to 920 in 2025. The high-sensitivity PET/CT system helped reduce injected activity and acquisition time, improving throughput and sustaining growth. The clinical-to-production ratio progressively decreased from 1 (1 batch/exam) to 0.42 for 68Ga-PSMA (range 0.37–0.51) and 0.49 for 68Ga-DOTATOC (range 0.40–0.60), indicating better batch utilization and workflow efficiency. Cost analysis showed module-based synthesis was most efficient during high-yield phases of the generators, while kit-based approaches were more cost-effective at later stages. Supply logistics of precursors and reagents has shown to be more sustainable for in-house production of radiopharmaceuticals compared to the purchased ones from external facilities.ConclusionsThe 68Ge/68Ga generator supports reliable, sustainable, and cost-effective radiopharmaceutical production, enhancing efficiency and clinical capacity in PET imaging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41181-026-00437-z.

Introduction

Sustainable practices are gaining importance in healthcare, driven primarily by rising operational costs and limited resources (Brown et al. 2023). In Nuclear Medicine, where the use of radioactive materials requires strict handling protocols and streamlined workflows, balancing clinical performance with cost-effective and sustainable operations presents specific challenges (Currie et al. 2024).

Within the hub-and-spoke organization model of Nuclear Medicine centers, fluorine-18 (18F)-labeled probes—such as 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) and 18F-fluorocholine—have traditionally been favored for diagnostic imaging with positron emission tomography (PET) (Langbein et al. 2019). The relatively long physical half-life of 18F (109.7 min) enables centralized production in cyclotron-equipped facilities, with subsequent distribution to multiple peripheral sites. This centralization maximizes production efficiency, ensures consistent radiochemical purity.

and specific activity through standardized quality control (QC), and reduces the need for each site to operate its own cyclotron. However, it also introduces challenges related to transportation logistics, decay losses during delivery, and the vulnerability of the supply chain to production or transport disruptions (Zaman et al. 2014). Moreover, maintaining the quality and efficacy of radiopharmaceuticals during transit is crucial and temperature control is often necessary to preserve the integrity of certain radiopharmaceuticals, making the supply chain more vulnerable (Current Trends in the Supply and Utilisation of Medical Radioisotopes, NEA 2025). Recent advances have enhanced the availability of commercial radionuclide generators, fostering the expansion of PET imaging worldwide. These generators enable in-house production of isotopes, providing centers with flexible and sustainable options. This development enhances accessibility while supporting the design of molecular probes tailored to specific disease markers, such as receptors, antigens, and enzymes (Sammartano et al. 2022).

A key innovation driving this shift is the Germanium-68/Gallium-68 (68Ge/68Ga) generator, which has revolutionized PET isotope availability by allowing on-demand production of 68Ga, especially useful in the context of precision medicine. Indeed, the 68Ge/68Ga generator can be used to label ligands such as prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) inhibitors and somatostatin analogs (e.g., DOTATOC, DOTATATE), which are widely applied in cancer imaging with PET (Sammartano et al. 2022; Velikyan 2015).

In this context, our PET center has been operational since 2008 with year-over-year increase of PET procedures volume in oncology, urology, neurology and cardiovascular diseases (see Additional file, Fig. 1). 68Ga radiopharmaceutical production started in 2015 with 68Ga-DOTATOC, reaching full production capacity in 2016 with the implementation of 68Ga-PSMA (see Additional file, Fig. 2). Since then, it has expanded to develop and validate various 68Ga-labeled probes targeting specific disease pathways, supporting the advancement of precision imaging (Dash and Chakravarty 2019; Migliari et al. 2017, 2023a, 2022a, 2023b, 2024, 2025a; Sammartano et al. 2020a, 2020b). Enabling on-site supply reduces dependence on large-scale, centralized production facilities and the associated logistics, such as repeated transportation, cold-chain requirements, crises or system shocks. Finally, reducing the time between the isotope production and the patient, also reduces isotope wastage due to decay, thereby reducing environmental impact. From a resource management perspective, the use of the 68Ge/68Ga generator allows for precise, on-demand production of 68Ga, which significantly reduces unnecessary isotope overproduction needed to face decay during transport. This targeted approach to radiotracer synthesis enhances the efficiency of material usage and streamlines operational planning. These improvements not only support better allocation of technical and human resources but also translate into substantial cost savings, reinforcing the economic sustainability of PET imaging operations (Migliari et al. 2025b).

This study evaluates the strategic role of the 68Ge/68Ga generator in creating a more resilient supply chain, promoting sustainability, improving diagnostic versatility, and optimizing operational and economic efficiency within the Nuclear Medicine workflow. In particular, it reports on the multi-year performance assessment of a 68Ge/68Ga generator employed in our Nuclear Medicine Division, alongside a comprehensive cost analysis of radiopharmaceutical production over the same period.

We evaluated our 68Ga-based radiopharmaceutical production over the period 2016–2024, while the utilization of production factors to assess the clinical-to-production ratio was analyzed from 2016 to 2025. Quality requirements for both the 68GaCl₃ eluate and the labelled radiopharmaceuticals were assessed according to the European regulations (Directorate and for the Quality of Medicines and HealthCare 2026; European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines and HealthCare 2023; European Commission EudraLex 2022; Directorate and for the Quality of Medicines and HealthCare 2022; Commission and European Pharmacopoeia 2023; European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines and HealthCare. Production and quality control system

Type of used 68Ge/68Ga generators

The 68Ge/68Ga generator is a self-contained system incorporating a parent–daughter radionuclide pair in transient equilibrium (Fig. 1). It is configured as a GMP-compliant, closed apparatus (GalliaPharm®, Eckert & Ziegler Radiopharma GmbH, Berlin, Germany), featuring a pharmacopoeia-grade borosilicate glass column filled with a titanium dioxide (TiO₂) matrix. This matrix selectively adsorbs the parent radionuclide 68Ge, allowing the periodic elution of the daughter nuclide 68Ga with high radionuclidic purity (Fig. 2). With a long half-life (t½ 68Ge = 270.95 days), the generator provides a reliable and continuous supply of 68Ga (Roesch et al. 2012).Fig. 1Principal decay scheme of 68Ge. In 2016 and 2017, we purchased two generators with an initial activity of 1.11 GBq (30 mCi) at calibration, transitioning later, in 2018, to generators with 1.85 GBq activity (50 mCi). To prevent metal accumulation and ensure consistent elution performance, the 68Ge/68Ga generator is eluted at the beginning of each week, including non-synthesis days. Elution is also performed within 24 h prior to any 68Ga-radiopharmaceutical production to maintain eluate purity and optimal radiochemical yield. The elution process uses a sterile 0.1 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution (99.999% trace metal grade, sourced from Eckert & Ziegler) delivered via an automated synthesis module (Scintomics GRP®, Fürstenfeldbruck, Germany) over approximately five minutes. The elution volume is 5 mL and is measured using a dose calibrator (CRC-15 PET Capintec, Florham Park, NJ) to assess 68Ga activity, elution yield, and profile. The generator is actively used for up to one year before decommissioning, ensuring its utility is fully maximized. During its operational period, the generator can be eluted up to three times daily to support the production of 68Ga-labeled radiopharmaceuticals.

Radiolabeling procedures

Since 2014, our center has developed and validated the radiosynthesis of various radioligands, including 68Ga-DOTATOC, 68Ga-DOTATATE, 68Ga-PSMA, 68Ga-DOTA-ECL1i, 68Ga-DOTA-Pentixafor, 68Ga-NODAGA-Exendin-4, 68Ga-DOTA-FAPI-46, 68Ga-MAA, 68Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 and 68Ga-DOTA-EMP-100, each requiring specific radiolabeling procedures (Dash and Chakravarty 2019; Migliari et al. 2017, 2023a, 2022a, 2023b, 2024, 2025a; Sammartano et al. 2020a, 2020b). In our PET center, 68Ga-DOTATOC and 68Ga-PSMA are synthesized routinely, several times daily.

The synthesis of these 68Ga-labeled compounds is performed using a fully automated synthesis module (Scintomics GRP®, Fürstenfeldbruck, Germany), equipped with a single-use disposable kit, including reagents and a synthesis cassette (ABX, Advanced Biochemical Compounds, Radeberg, Germany). For radiolabeling, 68Ga is obtained by eluting the 68Ge/68Ga generator with 5 mL of sterile 0,1 M HCl solution. The eluate is collected and pre-purifed on a strong cation exchange (SCX) cartridge, which is subsequently eluted with a 5 M NaCl solution into a reaction vial loaded with the peptide. The amount of peptide-chelator complexes used for the synthesis varies depending on the specific 68Ga-labeled compound: 20 μg of DOTA0-(Phe1-Tyr3)-octreotide for 68Ga-DOTATOC and 10 μg of PSMA-HBED-CC for 68Ga-PSMA. Each peptide is prepared in a 1.5 M HEPES buffer solution (pH 4) before use. Each reaction mixture is heated at 95 °C for 10 min.

Upon completing the labeling reaction, each crude product is cooled down before undergoing radioligand purification. This process is performed using a Sep-Pak C18 light cartridge (Waters), rinsed with water for injection (Ph. Eur.). The purified product is then eluted using 2 mL of a 1:1 ethanol/water mixture. Eventually, each final product is diluted with 10 mL of PBS and sterilized through a 0.22 μm sterile inline filter (millex GV) with a poly (vinylidene difluoride) membrane. The entire radiopharmaceutical production takes 35 min.

68GaCl3 eluate

The 68GaCl3 eluate was tested according to the Ph. Eur. Monograph #2464 (European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines and HealthCare. European Pharmacopoeia. 11th ed. 2023). All QCs parameters were assessed at each generator elution, typically performed at the beginning of the week before clinical production. On the other hand, aseptic processes were validated biannually via media fill tests using Tryptic Soy Broth, simulating all production steps. Environmental and workstation microbiologic quality is monitored every six months.

Each 68GaCl3 eluate was visually inspected to ensure clarity, colorlessness, and absence of particulates. pH was measured using indicator strips (0–14). Radionuclidic purity was assessed by half-life, radiation type, and photon energy characteristic of the radionuclide (Committee for Medicinal Products for Human use (CHMP) 2025; International Council for Harmonisation 2022; International Atomic Energy Agency 2018). 68Ga activity was measured immediately after elution in the eluate and after the radiopharmaceutical synthesis with a dose calibrator (CRC-15 PET Capintec, Florham Park, NJ). Gamma spectrometry was conducted to confirm the presence of 68Ga, via its characteristic photon emission peak (499—521 keV), using a high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector (ORTEC GEM 30P4-76) connected to a multichannel analyzer system. Monitoring the presence of long-lived 68Ge breakthroughs in the generator eluate is essential, as it can negatively impact the radiolabelling process and reduce overall synthesis yield. To evaluate this, radionuclidic purity—expressed as the percentage of parent radionuclide—was assessed using high-resolution gamma spectrometry. Residual 68Ge was quantified via persistence of its gamma peak using the same HPGe detector system described above.

The RCP of the 68GaCl3 eluate was determined by instant thin-layer chromatography (ITLC). Samples were spotted onto silica gel ITLC strips (ITLC-SG, Agilent Technologies), which served as the stationary phase. Two different mobile phases were used to distinguish free 68Ga and potential 68Ga colloidal forms: a citrate buffer for detecting free 68Ga, and a 1:1 mixture of ammonium acetate and methanol for identifying colloids, respectively. In both systems, species of interest (free68Ga and colloids) exhibit retention factor (Rf) values 0–0.2. Metallic impurities (such as Fe and Zn) were quantified by ICP-MS, ensuring compliance with the established specifications (≤ 10 µg/GBq).

Bacterial endotoxins were quantified using a chromogenic method (Endosafe PTS, Charles River, Wilmington, Massachusetts, United States) with samples diluted and tested alongside positive controls (Committee for Medicinal Products for Human use (CHMP) 2025). Apyrogenicity was confirmed for endotoxin levels < 17.5 EU/mL. The sterility test followed EMA/CHMP/ICH/645592/2008 guidelines. Sterility was verified by inoculating growth broth and incubating at 25 ºC and 37 ºC for 14 days; absence of microbial growth confirms sterility.

All analytical procedures followed established standard operating procedures within our radiopharmacy (Dash and Chakravarty 2019; Migliari et al. 2017). Acceptance criteria of QC parameters for each radiopharmaceutical are listed in Additional file (Tab 1).

Similarly to 68GaCl3 eluates, each radiopharmaceutical batch underwent pH measurement and visual inspection. The latter step is a mandatory QC measure for PET radiopharmaceuticals produced in-house, ensuring patient safety and process integrity. Indeed, the detection of particulates may indicate purification failure, compromised filtration, or suboptimal environmental conditions (Langille 2013; Gillings et al. 2021; Aerts et al. 2014; Decristoforo et al. 2017).

The RCP of 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC—was evaluated by Radio-TLC, specifically used to detect potential impurities as 68Ga colloids, and Radio-UV-HPLC on samples taken from the final formulation.

For both the considered radiopharmaceuticals, the method employs a silica gel strip (ITLC-SG, Agilent Technologies) as the stationary phase and 1:1 mixture of ammonium acetate and methanol as mobile phase.

Retention factor (Rf) values are used to differentiate 68Ga colloid (Rf = 0.0–0.2) from 68Ga peptide (Rf = 0.8–1). The samples were processed using a Cyclone Plus Storage Phosphor System (Perkin Elmer) and the chromatograms are analyzed via OptiQuantTM software.

HPLC analyses were performed using a Dionex UltiMate 3000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) equipped with a AcclaimTM 120 C18 column 3 µm 120 Å (3.0 mm × 150 mm), UV detector (220 nm), and γ-detector (Berthold Technologies, Italy) (International Council for Harmonisation 2022; Decristoforo et al. 2017). The used solvents were high-grade purity and included water/acetonitrile with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). Radiopharmaceutical-specific gradients were applied.

The column was kept at 30 °C, with UV detection set at 220 nm to identify chemical impurities, unlabelled peptide, and quantify the 68Ga-labeled compound. Detection of the 68Ga ion was carried out using a gamma detector integrated into the HPLC system. Data was processed using Chromeleon 7 software.

The residual amount of 2-[4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazinyl]-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buffer was evaluated for both 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC using an alternative HPLC-based analytical approach, developed in our center as reported in a previous published study (Gillings et al. 2020). The validated method employs a Waters Xbridge® C18 column (150 mm × 4.6 mm, 3.5 μm) as the stationary phase, coupled to both a UV detector set at 195 nm and a γ-detector (Berthold Technologies, Milan, Italy). The mobile phase consists of 20 mM ammonium formate at pH 9.5, delivered isocratically at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min.

Residual ethanol content, instead, was quantitatively evaluated using the Intuvo 9000 gas chromatography (GC) System (Agilent, Santa Clara, California, USA). A 1.0 μL sample was injected into a column packed with macrogol 20,000 (length: 30 mm; internal diameter: 0.53 mm; film thickness: 1 μm), with helium used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 10 mL/min. The column was maintained at 35 °C, while the injection port and detector were set at 140 °C and 220 °C, respectively. Ethanol was identified based on a retention time within the range of 2 to 4 min. The ethanol concentration was determined by integrating the chromatographic peak area, and the final amount in the 68Ga-peptide preparation was required not to exceed 10% v/v or 2.5 g (International Atomic Energy Agency 2018).

Bacterial endotoxins were quantified as previously described in paragraph 2.3.1.

Assessment of operational efficiency

Generators

To assess production efficiency of generators over the years from 2016 to 2024, we examined key performance metrics for all the eluates and our two primary radiolabeled compounds—68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC. For eluates, we focused on elution yield trends and frequency while for the radiopharmaceuticals we analyzed differences between theoretical and actual generator activities, along with annual number of PET scans. We also evaluated the clinical-to-production ratio by comparing the number of batches produced to the number of PET scans per generator.

Weekly elution yield

Elution of the generators was performed as described in paragraph 2.1.

Data for this analysis were obtained from the internal activity log, where each generator elution is systematically recorded. The elution yield was calculated as the ratio of the activity of 68Ga eluted from the generator to the theoretical maximum activity expected based on the decay of the parent 68Ge isotope, corrected for the time of elution. This value is expressed as a percentage. Under optimal conditions, the theoretical elution yield should exceed 60% (Migliari et al. 2022b). To evaluate the consistency and long-term stability of generator performance, we monitored the weekly elution yield over an extended period. By comparing the measured yields to the theoretical benchmark, we were able to assess the operational efficiency of the generators and detect any gradual decline in performance or anomalies in generator function over time.

Elution frequency

The total number of elutions per year was obtained from detailed records maintained at our center. These records provided an accurate account of the elution frequency, date and activity (in MBq), allowing for precise tracking of generator use throughout the years.

Correlation between measured and estimated activity

Radiopharmaceutical production activities are planned on a weekly basis according to clinical needs. Planning the number of examinations per session requires estimating the activity obtainable from each batch of radiopharmaceutical produced.

The estimated activity is calculated starting from the activity obtained in the eluate at the beginning of the week, assuming a reference radiolabelling yield of 62%. This reference value was chosen as it represents the average yield measured across all the radiopharmaceutical productions during the study period (2016–2024), and considered representative of the usual yield range (59–65%). This theoretical radiolabelling yield represents the expected efficiency of the labelling process under optimal conditions, accounting for factors such as radiochemical purity, reaction kinetics, and processing losses.

To evaluate the accuracy of the adopted methodology, we systematically compared the actual radioactivity measured at the end of synthesis (EoS) of 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC with the estimated activity.

Clinical-to-production ratio

We calculated the annual number of PET scans with 68Ga-radiopharmaceuticals at our center in order to better understand the clinical demand and the utilization of the produced radiopharmaceuticals. Specifically, we determined the ratio of produced radiopharmaceutical batches to the number of PET scans, to assess how efficiently each generator was used in clinical practice.

Evaluation of environmental sustainability

To assess environmental sustainability, we analysed different key parameters related to the on-site production of 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC using the 68Ge/68Ga generator: (i) how fully each generator unit’s operational lifespan was exploited for both tracers (time before generator replacement), and (ii) the transport frequency required for 1⁸F-PSMA deliveries from external production facilities compared to our generator-based workflow. As no commercial suppliers currently offer 1⁸F-labelled DOTATOC, the comparison with an 1⁸F-based analogue was limited to PSMA-targeting radiopharmaceuticals.

For generator use, we reviewed the average number of operational days for all units purchased between 2016 and 2024 before substitution. For transport logistics, we estimated the number of yearly 1⁸F-PSMA shipments that would have been required to meet our clinical workload and compared this with the transport needs associated with generator-based production of 68Ga-PSMA.

Economic analysis and budget impact

We compared the annual production costs of “in-house” (module-based production) synthesized 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC over the years between 2016 and 2024 and, additionally, we evaluated these costs against the theoretical production cost using commercially available “ready-to-use” cold kits (kit-based production) for the same radiopharmaceuticals.

The economic and budget impact analysis was conducted from our hospital perspective. The cost of module-based production was assessed by itemizing all expenditures associated with the production of 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC including consumables, such as synthesis kits, reagents, HCl bags, vials, QC materials, and peptides (PSMA and DOTATOC), as well as the purchase cost of the 68Ge/68Ga generator, all expressed in euros. All costs collected over the 2016–2024 period were adjusted for inflation and expressed in 2024 euros, in accordance with current recommendations in the literature (RADIUS S.r.l. 2024).

Total yearly costs were normalized to the number of syntheses performed per year. An average number of PET scans per synthesis was estimated, allowing for the calculation of the mean production cost per scan per radiopharmaceutical, and per year.

The theoretical cost of kit-based production was calculated based on the 2024 market price of 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC kits. Additional costs factored in included the necessary consumables (cassette, HCl bags, QC reagents) and the cost of the generator, normalized to the number of PET scans performed annually with each radiopharmaceutical.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive variables were summarized using the mean, and where appropriate, ranges were also reported to describe data variability. Generator elution yield and production parameters over time were analyzed using descriptive statistics. The relationship between estimated and measured radioactivity at the end of synthesis (EoS) for both 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC was evaluated by linear regression analysis, with calculation of the coefficient of determination (R2).

Results

In this study, several sample sets were analyzed from 2016 to 2024. Specifically, 52 batches of 68GaCl₃ eluate per year (corresponding to one batch per week) were evaluated to assess compliance with QCs requirements and to investigate trends in elution yield. Global production of 68Ga-based radiopharmaceuticals corresponded to 2455 batches. For the radiopharmaceuticals most frequently produced for routine clinical use, data from a total of 1800 batches of 68Ga-PSMA and 610 batches of 68Ga-DOTATOC were analyzed. Furthermore, the total number PSMA- and DOTATOC-PET scans over the years 2016–2025 was reviewed to determine the clinical-to-production ratio. Finally, to explore the correlation between theoretically calculated and experimentally measured activities, 3 representative batches per month of each radiopharmaceutical were selected and analyzed for each year of observation.

Production and QC system

68GaCl3 eluate

All the obtained 68GaCl₃ eluates were clear, colorless solutions without visible particulates, consistently exhibiting a pH around 1, in full compliance with the Ph. Eur. requirement of < 2. This acidic environment is critical for preventing the formation of 68Ga colloids and preserving the eluate’s integrity for subsequent radiolabelling procedures. exhibiting a strongly acidic pH (2). The calculated half-life of 68Ga, determined using the formula \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {\mathrm{t}}_{{{1 \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {1 2}} \right. \kern-0pt} 2}}} = \,0.693\, \times \,t/ln\left( {A_{0} \, / \, A_{t} } \right) $$\end{document}, was 67.71 min across all measurements, further confirming radionuclide identity. Analysis performed over years on 68Ge eluates showed 68Ge breakthrough levels were consistently below Ph. Eur. recommended limit (0.001% of total 68Ga activity), average of 0.0000068%. Furthermore, gamma spectra revealed characteristic 68Ga peaks at 0.511 and 1.077 MeV, consistent with equilibrium with the parent radionuclide 68Ge. The RCP of the 68GaCl3 eluate was evaluated using TLC. When developed with a citrate buffer as the mobile phase, the TLC strips consistently showed free 68Ga at an Rf range of 0–0.2. In contrast, strips developed with a 1:1 mixture of ammonium acetate and methanol—used to detect potential 68Ga colloids—displayed no radioactivity in the colloid-specific region, indicating their absence. Based on these results, the eluate’s RCP was consistently confirmed to be 100%. The quantified levels of metallic impurities (Fe and Zn) were consistently below 10 µg/GBq, thereby meeting the established specifications and remaining fully compliant with regulatory requirements.

Bacterial endotoxin levels consistently remained below the 17.5 EU/mL limit, with measured values of 4.50 EU/mL.

68Ga-radiopharmaceuticals (68Ga-DOTATOC and 68Ga-PSMA)

All synthesized batches of 68Ga-DOTATOC and 68Ga-PSMA appeared clear, colorless, and free of visible particulates, with a consistent pH of 7. Radiochemical purity of the same radiopharmaceuticals was evaluated using both TLC and HPLC, and results were consistently within acceptable and optimal ranges. Particularly, TLC consistently yielded a mean RCP of 100% for both 68Ga-DOTATOC and 68Ga-PSMA, while HPLC results showed mean values greater than 98% for 68Ga-DOTATOC and exceeded 99% for 68Ga-PSMA, well above the minimum required thresholds—91% for 68Ga-DOTATOC and 95% for 68Ga-PSMA. Importantly, these measurements remained stable and reproducible throughout the years analyzed (see Additional file, Tab. 2).

Additional QC parameters also met established safety criteria. Analysis of the final radiopharmaceutical solutions by HPLC revealed that the residual HEPES content was below the concentration of the HEPES test solution (see Additional file, Fig. 3). Ethanol content remained below 10% across all batches. Bacterial endotoxin levels, assessed in three synthesis samples for each radiopharmaceutical, were uniformly below the limit of 17.5 EU/mL, with measured values of 5.00 EU/mL for both 68Ga-DOTATOC and 68Ga-PSMA.

Assessment of operational efficiency

Operational efficiency of generators

Weekly elution yield

Over a 12-month operational period, the 68Ge/68Ga generator demonstrated consistently high elution yields, with an average value of 74.83% across all years analyzed and minimal inter-annual variability (Fig. 3). Elution frequency

Data considered in this analysis showed that our center progressively optimized generator use over the years, increasing elution frequency in response to growing clinical demand (see Additional file, Tab 3).

This trend became particularly evident in 2024, when a single generator was used for clinical production with exceptional intensity, reaching a total of 466 elutions within the year, always maintaining the required quality. Evaluation of environmental sustainability

With regard to generator lifespan, over the eight-year study period (2016–2024) our center operated the 68Ge/68Ga generator for an average of 361.11 days per year before replacing it (see Additional file, Tab 4).

Considering that a single delivery of 18F-PSMA (555 MBq) allows imaging of only 2–3 exams, and that our center routinely performs two PSMA PET imaging sessions per day—one in the early morning and one in the early afternoon, each involving 2 to a maximum of 3 exams—at least two separate daily transport events would be required to meet clinical demand. Based on the total number of PSMA PET examinations performed at our center in 2024 (n = 530), this would correspond to an estimated average of approximately 210–215 transport events per year, with each delivery originating from the nearest external production facility located approximately 120 km away.

In contrast, the in-house generator-based model requires only two transport events over the entire generator lifespan (delivery and decommissioning), in addition to one monthly shipment for the materials needed for radiosynthesis. This substantial reduction in transport frequency translates directly into lower transport-related carbon emissions.

Discussion

This article aims to evaluate the long-term performance and operational impact of the 68Ge/68Ga generator in the clinical setting, with a focus on cost-effectiveness and workflow efficiency in PET radiopharmaceutical production. Over an eight-year period (2016–2024), a comprehensive dataset was collected, including generator elution yields and frequency, quality control results of both 68GaCl₃ eluates and synthesized radiopharmaceuticals (68Ga-DOTATOC and 68Ga-PSMA), production accuracy, number of PET scans, environmental sustainability indicators, and detailed economic metrics. Overall, the data derived from 416 eluates and a total of 2410 batches of radiopharmaceuticals, documented consistently high radiochemical and radionuclidic quality, stable generator performance under intensive use, a more than sixfold increase in clinical activity, progressive improvements in batch efficiency, and a clear economic and environmental advantage of in-house, module-based production. Together, these results provide a robust overview of how generator-based 68Ga production can support an increasingly demanding and evolving Nuclear Medicine environment, in agreement with previous reports highlighting the suitability of long-term generator-based workflows for high-volume clinical practice (Turner et al. 2019; Abusalem et al. 2021).

Our findings confirm the reliability and stable performance of 68⁸Ge/68Ga generators in routine clinical practice, according to previous long-term studies on generators use for clinical (Kumar 2020) and research purposes (Amor-Coarasa et al. 2016).

Elution yields consistently exceeded the manufacturer’s minimum threshold (Migliari et al. 2022b), with a mean of 74.83%, demonstrating robustness across different generator activities and over multiple years. According to the manufacturer (Eckert & Ziegler Radiopharma GmbH, Berlin, Germany), as of 2024, GalliaPharm® generators (authorisation number EU/1/24/1836) no longer have a fixed limit on the number of elutions, provided that the generator's activity has not been exhausted. In line with this update, the generator sustained intensive usage—such as the 466 elutions recorded in 2024—highlighting its operational resilience and effective capacity utilization. Quality control parameters, including appearance, pH, radionuclidic and radiochemical purity, LaL test and sterility, consistently met European Pharmacopeia standards, validating the chemical integrity and radiolabeling suitability of the 68Ga eluate even under high-throughput conditions, as similarly observed in other high-volume generator-based production models (Kumar 2020; Melidis et al. 2024).

With regard to the production of 68Ga-radiopharmaceuticals, quality parameters remained consistently optimal throughout the study period. Radiochemical purity was high, with HPLC results exceeding 98% for 68Ga-DOTATOC and 99% for 68Ga-PSMA. Notably, inter-batch variability was minimal despite potential challenges such as operator turnover. This consistency underscores the robustness and reproducibility of the radiosynthesis process validated at our center and highlights the effectiveness of standardized training and education for all operators, confirming previous findings that automated module-based synthesis mitigates operator-dependent variability (Melidis et al. 2024; Müller et al. 2024; Meisenheimer et al. 2020).

Additionally, a strong correlation (R = 1) between estimated and actual activities of produced 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-DOTATOC confirmed the reliability of the theoretical yield model used in the pre-synthesis planning. Optimizing radiopharmaceutical production requires a flow-shop processing model—generator, synthesis, and dispensing—synchronized with patient scheduling to minimize radioactive decay losses and maximize diagnostic throughput. The elevated level of predictability also contributed to more effective utilization of generators, improving batch efficiency and allowing a higher patient capacity, in accordance with studies emphasizing the role of predictive activity modeling in workflow optimization (Al Ahmed and Al-Surimi 2015).

The significance of these findings is particularly relevant in the current context of digital PET/CT technology, which is redefining clinical practice of Nuclear Medicine. The advent of fully digital systems, including those with an extended field of view, offers markedly higher sensitivity, enabling a potential increase in the number of examinations and, at the same time, a reduction of the injected dose. This technological shift also amplifies the need for robust, predictable, and cost-effective radiopharmaceutical production workflows. In our centre, the upgrade to a high-performance digital PET/CT system (Omni Legend, GE Healthcare) exemplified these advantages. The improved detection efficiency allowed for a 40–50% reduction in administered activities as well as concurrent 25% decrease in acquisition times, while preserving excellent image quality (Fulcheri et al. 2026). These optimizations translated into a higher number of examinations per session and an overall increase in annual exam capacity, without compromising diagnostic accuracy or consistency.

Furthermore, digital and extended-FOV systems facilitate broader access to PET imaging and allow for improved protocol optimization in situations where dose reduction is particularly important, such as repeated follow-up scans and imaging of paediatric or otherwise vulnerable patients. Nevertheless, the implementation of these advancements must be balanced with considerations of equipment availability, organizational and procedural aspects, and radioprotection requirements, as for example, regulatory dose limits and operator safety.

Overall, the advancements described contributed to a steady increase in exam volume, as evidenced by data collected from 2016 to 2024. This growth was also pushed by the daily participation of Nuclear Medicine physicians in the multidisciplinary teams and tumor boards of our Institution, which contributed to improved diagnostic accuracy and more appropriate patient selection for PET imaging, enhancing care quality through structured collaboration, particularly in oncology and complex, chronic cases (European Society of Radiology (ESR) 2023).

Although external disruptions—such as the COVID-19 pandemic and temporary facility closures—caused intermittent interruptions of PET clinical activity, the overall trend indicated sustained growth in service capacity. In particular, the impact of the pandemic was limited and temporary: 68Ga-PSMA PET scans decreased of 6.7% in 2019 compared to 2018, with almost complete recovery in 2020. On the other hand, the use of 68Ga-DOTATOC remained stable during the same period (100 scans in both 2019 and 2020). Indeed, in our Institution, diagnostic activity was partially slowed down due to COVID-related hygiene and safety regulations, impairing especially high intensity activities, such as PSMA PET. The lower incidence of neuroendocrine tumors may explain the minor impact on DOTATOC imaging despite the operational constraints.

Importantly, preliminary operational data collected beyond the formal study period suggest that this upward trend in clinical activity has continued into 2025, indicating a sustained and ongoing demand for 68Ga-based PET imaging. Moreover, a general improvement in efficiency was observed, with a growing number of exams performed per batch—indicating better resource allocation and scheduling strategies. When generator-based activity was first introduced in our center, each produced batch corresponded to a single PET examination (ratio ≈1). With increasing operational experience, multiple examinations could be performed from a single synthesis, improving batch utilization reflected in the progressive decrease of the clinical-to-production ratio over time. However, occasional fluctuations in annual efficiency metrics suggest operational constraints or shifts in clinical demand. In certain cases, the need to address waiting lists or accommodate urgent requests led to the performance of a third daily synthesis for a single exam, as in the year 2024 (Fig. 7). Although necessary, this practice temporarily reduced overall efficiency by dedicating an entire batch to one examination. Nevertheless, these situations offer insight into the dynamic clinical realities of PET/CT imaging and underscore the importance of adaptable scheduling and production workflows.

Our data indicate that optimization has nearly reached an operational plateau under the current production setup. Further gains may still be achieved through ongoing protocol refinement and reduced administered activity, facilitated by modern high-performance digital PET/CT systems. Alternatively, increasing the initial generator activity could enhance productivity; however, this option is not feasible at our Institution due to local regulatory limits on the handling of 68Ge/68Ga.

Beyond clinical and operational performance, the generator-based production model also demonstrated relevant advantages from an environmental sustainability perspective. The long operational lifespan of the 68Ge/68Ga generator, averaging more than 360 days per year before generator replacement, combined with in-house radiopharmaceutical synthesis, substantially reduced the need for frequent radiotracer transport. Compared with a supply model based on externally produced 1⁸F-labelled tracers—which would require hundreds of transport events per year to meet clinical demand—the generator-based approach limited transportation to generator delivery, decommissioning, and periodic shipments of synthesis materials. This reduction in transport frequency translates into lower transport-related carbon emissions and supports the growing emphasis on environmentally responsible practices in Nuclear Medicine, in line with recent European analyses focusing on sustainability and logistics in radiopharmacy services (Ligtvoet et al. 2021).

In the paper of Melidis et al. (Melidis et al. 2025), local production of all radiopharmaceuticals resulted in the least costly option compared to full delivery or delivery of SPECT tracers associated with local PET production via cyclotron. It’s to note that Authors consider the island setting, where limited transport infrastructure, flight delays, and weather-related disruptions create additional vulnerabilities and increasing costs. Despite this, beyond an initial annual threshold of approximately 650 PET scans per year, local radiopharmaceutical production can represent a cost-minimization strategy for improving accessibility to PET services, also in island regions. Although Calderoni et al. showed that 68Ga‑PSMA PET/CT images produced with cold kits and automated synthesis modules are clinically equivalent, with no significant differences in image quality (Calderoni et al. 2020), our analysis demonstrates that module-based production provides a clear economic advantage. Indeed, our study showed that module-based synthesis allowed for better optimization of per-exam costs for both 68Ga‑PSMA and 68Ga‑DOTATOC, offering lower costs compared to theoretical estimates for kit-based production. However, year-to-year variability in efficiency was influenced by external factors, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, and internal factors, including decreased generator output near the end of its operational life. These findings support the adoption of a hybrid production strategy: prioritizing module-based synthesis during the generator’s high-yield phase, and shifting to or adding kit-based approaches as performance declines.

The combined increase in PET scans volume and improved efficiency metrics reflects a growing reliance on molecular imaging and radiopharmaceutical production in clinical workflows. Nonetheless, several challenges persist. The initial investment in generator acquisition remains a significant financial burden for many institutions, particularly in resource-limited settings. Moreover, strategic planning for generator lifecycle management is essential to fully exploit the clinical, operational, and economic advantages offered by the 68Ge/68Ga generator. This includes careful planning of elution schedules, alignment of synthesis timing with clinical demand, staff training and retention, standardization of operating procedures, and continuous monitoring of generator performance over its operational lifespan. Without such organizational and technical optimization, the generator may be underutilized, leading to reduced efficiency, higher per-exam costs, and diminished economic and operational benefits.

This study has some limitations. First, the findings are derived from the specific setting of a single university hospital with high patient volume, and therefore may not be directly generalizable to smaller centers with lower patient volumes or different organizational workflows. It is important to note that the reported costs reflect the specific purchasing and operational conditions of our hospital and may not be fully generalizable to other institutions. Moreover, cost estimates for kit-based production are theoretical, based on 2024 purchase prices and extrapolated retrospectively to the 2016–2024 exams volume. Despite these limitations, the data provide a valuable framework for evaluating and optimizing generator-based radiopharmaceutical production in a real-world clinical environment.

Finally, in this study sustainability has been considered only in terms of radiopharmaceutical delivery, while the environmental footprint of nuclear medicine also includes radiopharmaceutical production, type of packaging, energy consumption and equipment lifecycle (Currie 2025). A recent French study (Godard et al. 2025) estimated that the supply chain of a PET center, performing 7,485 PET scans in the year 2022, accounted for 14% of carbon footprint, and radiotracers supply represented 86% of it, while transport of people (patients and employees) represented the largest source of total CO2 emissions (67%).

Therefore, more studies are needed in the near future to analyse the carbon footprint of the entire nuclear medicine pipeline and accurately assess its environmental impact.

Conclusions

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that high-quality, stable production of 68Ga-based radiopharmaceuticals can be achieved and maintained over many years in a clinical setting. The long-term performance of the 68Ge/68Ga generator, combined with optimized operational workflows, predictive activity planning, and integration with advanced digital PET/CT systems, enabled not only consistent radiopharmaceutical quality but also a substantial expansion of clinical capacity, improved scheduling efficiency, and broader access to molecular imaging services. In addition, the in-house, generator-based production model proved advantageous from both economic and environmental perspectives, supporting cost containment and reducing transport-related impacts. Taken together, these results are particularly significant in the current context of the growing importance and demand for radiopharmaceuticals, as molecular imaging continues to play an increasingly central role in precision medicine, early diagnosis, and treatment planning.
