Maternal capacity, twinning and fertility: the last birth matters
Authors: Richard Meitern, Mark Gortfelder, Allan Puur, Peeter Hõrak
Published: 2024

arising from I.J. Rickard et al. Nature Communications 10.1038/s41467-022-30366-9 (2022)

Background

Twinning in humans occurs at low but variable rates within and among populations worldwide1,2. Dizygotic (DZ) twinning rates are heritable3 and increase with the age and parity of mothers1. Genetic risk factors and age-specific changes in DZ twinning rates likely act through effects on key genes in the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian signalling axis, which is central for reproduction3. In some pre-industrial3–5 and contemporary Western6 populations, twinning was selected against. In others, twinners had higher fitness than mothers of singletons despite higher mortality of mothers of twins and their offspring7,8. The question about why the propensity for DZ twinning has not gone extinct4,7 or, conversely, is not more common8 still puzzles evolutionary biologists and geneticists1–3.

The prevalent evolutionary explanation for the persistence of DZ twinning views the phenomenon as a by-product of polyovulation, an insurance mechanism against embryo mortality2,9. In this view, DZ twinning is the outcome of an adaptive strategy of switching from single to double ovulation with increasing age. When coupled with the age-related decline in fertility, this conditional strategy maximises reproductive success and explains the increase and subsequent decrease in the twinning rate with maternal age observed across human populations2. (Fertility is defined as the number of offspring a woman produces in a lifetime while intrinsic fertility is used here interchangeably with fecundity, i.e., the capacity to reproduce.)

However, birthing age is not the sole source of variation in twinning rates. As indicated by the number of studies in pre-industrial populations, mothers producing twins gave birth, on average, more often than non-twinners1. This observation has been interpreted as twinners having higher intrinsic fertility—a potential to give birth irrespective of age and other factors—which has shaped additional hypotheses about why twinning persists and varies across populations1. According to the phenotypic quality/heterogeneity hypothesis7,10–12, twinners represent a set of women with robust phenotypic quality who outperform mothers of singletons on any measures of reproductive performance. This hypothesis (termed henceforth as maternal capacity hypothesis10) is based on the “silver spoon” effect arising from the higher genetic or environmental condition of twin mothers, leading to positive covariation between different fitness components11. The maternal capacity hypothesis resonates strongly with extensive evidence that mothers of twins are often healthier than mothers of singletons (reviewed by refs. 2,10). Because twins are more demanding of maternal resources than singletons, conditions that challenge maternal health are more likely to result in a miscarriage of twins than of singletons13.

Recently, the idea that twinning is positively associated with intrinsic fertility has been questioned1. Rickard and co-authors point out that previous evidence favouring the maternal capacity hypothesis is based on an incorrect interpretation of the positive association between the mothers’ total number of births and the odds of twinning. Mothers of twins do not have more children because of their higher intrinsic fertility, but just because the risk of twinning accumulates with the number of births a woman experiences. To assess the nature of the relationship between twinning propensity and fertility properly, one needs to account for variation in risk exposure between mothers. This means testing for a positive relationship between per-birth twinning probability and maternal total births.

The authors test their ideas in a dataset of 105,833 birth records from nine pre-industrial European populations (mostly Finns and Samee; one German and one Swiss sample; 21,290 mothers born between 1700 and 1899). They find (following previous work) that twinners had more total births than non-twinners. However, when the twinning status of each birth was analysed separately, the per-birth twinning probability was negatively related to total births.

Twinning and fertility in Estonian mothers born between 1850 and 1899

The finding that mothers who were more likely to have twins in any given birth event had fewer births overall presents a serious challenge to the maternal capacity hypothesis. Here we replicate the study of Rickard and co-authors in a sample of 417,418 birth records from 125,575 Estonian mothers born between 1850 and 189914 (Supplementary Table 1). In these generations, the Estonian population experienced a demographic transition and began to practice family planning in response to twinning, fatalities and sex composition of offspring14.

First, we aim to test for the generality of the finding that per-birth twinning probability is not positively related to maternal total births. Second, we propose an alternative approach for testing the prediction of the maternal capacity hypothesis that per-birth twinning probability is positively associated with fertility. This approach is based on the rationale that birth control decisions affect the association between fertility and twinning probability: parents who have set an upper limit for their planned offspring number are more likely to stop reproduction after the birth of twins12. Additionally, stopping reproduction after a twinning event might result from maternal death or reduced reproductive health1.

Twinners can be divided into three categories. The first category includes women dying or becoming reproductively compromised after the twinning event at first birth, who, by definition7, do not qualify as “individuals with high intrinsic fertility”. The second category, “real individuals with high intrinsic fertility”, comprises highly fecund twinners, which can be detected among mothers who do not cease reproduction after twinning. Such unconstrained reproduction might be either deliberate or a consequence of unplanned pregnancies. The third category comprises women who do not fulfil their potential to for high intrinsic fertility because their planned family size is exceeded by the occurrence of a twin birth (which leads to stopping reproduction). The proportion of all three types of women (that likely differs between populations and cohorts) eventually determines whether one can observe a positive relationship between per-birth twinning probability and maternal total births. Based on these premises, we assume that a positive association between twinning propensity and fertility is particularly likely to emerge in situations where the proportion of primiparous women dying after twin births is low, and family planning is not prevalent.

To test for such a scenario, we analysed the twinning rates in the Estonian dataset by following the research protocol and code of the target article. Next, we ran the same analyses after eliminating all records of the last births from both datasets. We predicted that eliminating last birth records would increase the chances of detecting the positive association between per-birth twinning probability and fertility because it removes (among others) all the women who stop reproduction after the twinning event. The remaining sample thus contains a higher proportion of individuals who do not constrain their reproduction, which inevitably results in higher fertility (in terms of total offspring production). We thus asked whether the termination of reproduction after twin birth might obscure the (intrinsically) positive association between per-birth twinning probability and maternal total births.

We first tested whether women were more likely to stop reproduction after twin than after singleton birth. This was indeed the case. For the Estonian dataset, the odds of stopping reproduction after twin birth were 1.43 times higher than after singleton birth; twinning was more common among the last births (19.3‰) than among the preceding births (13.6‰). For the European multi-country dataset, the corresponding odds ratio was 1.57, and twins comprised 23.3‰ of the last births and 15% of the preceding births (Supplementary Table 4).

Contrary to the target article, we found no association between per-birth twinning probability and maternal total births in the complete (mother-level) Estonian dataset (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). The pattern was reversed when the last birth records were removed: In the Estonian dataset, per-birth twinning probability became positively associated with fertility (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 2), while in the European multi-country dataset, the two variables became independent (which supports the conclusion that in these populations a lower parity progression after twinning is what drove the negative relationship between twinning and maternal total births1). The same pattern was confirmed when birth-level data were analysed with mixed models, including age, parity, their squared and cubed values and their interactions as predictors of per-birth twinning probability (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

Why was the association between per-birth twinning probability and fertility different in Estonia and the populations studied in the target article? One possible explanation is that studied datasets may differ in the extent of mothers’ agency over their reproduction, as the Estonian study population was in the further stage of demographic transition than the studied west- and north-European populations, where 77% of mothers were born between 1700 and 1850.

Simulation of various combinations of candidate mechanisms (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Fig. 5 in the original article) suggests that the difference between the studies is caused by different impact of twinning upon the life history of the mothers. Comparison of parity-specific twinning rates indicates that twinners in the multi-country sample comprised an excessively high proportion of young mothers who gave birth only once (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). In fact, the negative correlation between per-birth twinning probability and fertility in this sample was entirely caused by the presence of such women.

As a possible explanation, we suggest that maternal mortality/morbidity in the studied multi-country sample might have been exceptionally high for young women delivering twins at their first birthing event. If this explanation is correct, it would mean that the direction of the association between per-birth twinning probability and fertility in any population depends on the survival and health costs of twin births for primiparous mothers. If these are high, the per-birth twinning probability will inevitably decline with the number of births. This issue can be clarified by focusing attention on the parity-specific mortality of twinners.
