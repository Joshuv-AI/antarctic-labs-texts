Imaging brain development in a KCNQ2-developmental and epileptic encephalopathy mouse model: identifying early biomarkers for functional and structural brain changes
Authors: Charissa Millevert, Nicholas Vidas-Guscic, Mohit H. Adhikari, Alan Miranda, Liesbeth Vanherp, Laurent Villard, Elisabeth Jonckers, Philippe Joye, Johan Van Audekerke, Ignace Van Spilbeeck, Els De Vriendt, Marleen Verhoye, Steven Staelens, Daniele Bertoglio, Sarah Weckhuysen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundKCNQ2-developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (KCNQ2-DEE) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) characterised by early-life seizures but persistent cognitive impairment. The absence of early, quantifiable preclinical biomarkers for neurodevelopmental dysfunction limits the evaluation of new treatments. We hypothesise that key brain maturation processes are altered early in disease development and could serve as biomarkers for neurodevelopmental dysfunction.MethodsWe performed longitudinal in-vivo brain imaging in 37 kcnq2Thr274Met/+ (KI) mice and 31 wild-type (WT) controls at three developmental stages: infancy (P14-21), juvenile (P32-42), and adulthood (P83-106). Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) assessed functional connectivity (FC), [18F]SynVesT-1 PET measured synaptic density, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) evaluated white and grey matter microstructure. Linear mixed models with Bonferroni correction were used to analyse genotype-by-age interactions across brain regions.FindingsAt infant age, KI mice showed increased FC relative to WT, particularly within the default mode-like network (DMLN). During the juvenile stage, KI mice exhibited modest elevated synaptic density across brain regions, most notably in the cerebellum. By adulthood, KI mice displayed reduced FC, especially within the DMLN, compared to WT. No significant microstructural genotype-by-age interactions were found.InterpretationKCNQ2-DEE disrupts neurodevelopment, with early hyperconnectivity and delayed synaptic pruning transitioning to adult hypoconnectivity. While this pattern is too subtle to use as a standalone biomarker, these findings establish a foundation for their use in longitudinal preclinical research targeting early therapeutic intervention.FundingSupported by the 10.13039/501100007660University of Antwerp, 10.13039/501100003130Fonds Wetenschappelijk Onderzoek, the 10.13039/501100022601Queen Elisabeth Medical Foundation, the European Joint Programme on Rare Disease, and Fondation Lejeune.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

KCNQ2-related developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (KCNQ2-DEE) is the most common genetic neonatal-onset DEE characterised by seizures and severe developmental delay. Although seizures tend to diminish or resolve during early childhood, affected individuals continue to experience severe global neurological impairment. Therefore, precision therapies that can improve developmental outcome remain an unmet medical need. However, current clinical trials for DEEs such as KCNQ2-DEE predominantly focus on seizure outcome, primarily due to the lack of early quantifiable biomarkers that would allow short-term impact assessment on neurodevelopment. To address this gap, pre-clinical studies using rodent models that recapitulate the human phenotype, such as the kcnq2Thr274Met/+ knock-in (KI) mouse model, are instrumental. In contrast to traditional behavioural testing that is largely confined to later developmental stages, neuroimaging provides the unique opportunity to assess brain structure and function longitudinally from very early postnatal stages, as was recently demonstrated by Millevert et al. (2025).

Added value of this study

This study is the first to use a longitudinal, multimodal imaging approach, including resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI), [18F]SynVesT-1 PET, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), to track functional connectivity (FC), synaptic density, and white and grey matter development from infancy to adulthood in a validated KCNQ2-DEE knock-in (KI) mouse model. While significant developmental differences between the genotypes were confined to a small subset of brain regions across all imaging modalities, several notable age-dependent alterations in brain maturation occurred. A pattern of infantile hyperconnectivity transitioning to hypoconnectivity towards adulthood was identified, particularly within circuits implicated in cognition. Next, KI mice displayed subtle delayed synaptic pruning at juvenile age, while no microstructural changes were identified. These findings reveal early, quantifiable changes in brain development that precede overt behavioural deficits and are similar to observations in rodent models of some other neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD). This suggests shared or convergent mechanisms of network-level vulnerability in NDD including KCNQ2-DEE.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings indicate that KCNQ2-DEE disrupts key neurodevelopmental processes, leading to altered brain network maturation and delayed synaptic pruning. Although these genotype-related alterations are relatively subtle and regionally restricted depending on imaging technique, limiting their utility as a standalone biomarker, they nevertheless provide a framework for early detection and monitoring of neurodevelopmental dysfunction. The inclusion of infant mice is particularly relevant for disorders like KCNQ2-DEE, which are caused by dysfunction of genes expressed already during foetal development, suggesting that pathological changes likely begin well before clinical symptoms become apparent. Capturing these early processes is essential not only to identify early biomarkers, but also to understand the timing and mechanisms by which brain development diverges from the norm. This knowledge can inform the design of future preclinical and clinical studies, deepen our understanding of disease mechanisms in KCNQ2-DEE and related NDDs, and ultimately facilitate the evaluation of novel targeted therapies.

Introduction

KCNQ2-developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (KCNQ2-DEE) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) that is caused by pathogenic variants in the gene KCNQ2.1 KCNQ2 encodes voltage-gated potassium (K+) channel subunits (Kv7.2) that are widely expressed in the nervous system from early stages of foetal brain development throughout adult life.2 Individuals with KCNQ2-DEE generally present with seizures from the first days of life.1 Although seizures tend to diminish or disappear during early childhood, neurodevelopment is poor leading to severe and global neurological impairment.3 No therapeutic strategies have emerged to date that significantly improve developmental outcome in KCNQ2-DEE. Sodium channel blockers (SCBs) (e.g., carbamazepine) are recommended as first-line treatment for their positive effect on seizures.4 However, it is still unclear whether SCBs also have a significant effect on cognitive outcome, and precision therapies that also significantly influences developmental outcome remains an unmet medical need. Many ongoing research efforts aim to develop novel treatments that directly target the dysfunctional channel and improve neurodevelopmental outcomes. Recently, a first randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with the K+ channel opener retigabine (RTG) was conducted in children with KCNQ2-DEE (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04639310). Unfortunately, the trial was mainly focused on seizure outcome, because the variability in cognitive outcomes and the absence of early quantifiable biomarkers for neurodevelopmental dysfunction hamper a short-term impact assessment on neurodevelopment. Since most children become seizure-free in early childhood, the trial was prematurely terminated due to recruitment difficulties. These difficulties are not only major hurdles in precision therapy trials for KCNQ2-DEE, but for most genetic DEEs.

Identifying early biomarkers for neurodevelopmental dysfunction in KCNQ2-DEE requires pre-clinical studies in relevant animal models. A KCNQ2 knock-in (KI) mouse model carrying the KCNQ2 p.(Thr274Met) missense variant, identified in several individuals with a typical KCNQ2-DEE phenotype was developed and mirrors the human disease course.5 These mice exhibit spontaneous seizures from postnatal day (P) 20 that are rarely observed after P100, along with cognitive impairment detected through behavioural assessments at adult age.5 Indeed, while conventional behavioural testing provides valuable insights, it is restricted to later stages of development, after key neurodevelopmental processes have already taken place. In contrast, neuroimaging provides the unique opportunity to assess brain structure and function longitudinally, starting from very early postnatal stages.6 Previous brain imaging studies in other NDDs, such as Down syndrome or West syndrome, have demonstrated that alterations in brain development can predict or correlate with cognitive function in both humans and rodents.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Building on this, our recent work demonstrated that key aspects of neurodevelopment, such as maturation of functional connectivity (FC), synaptic density, and white or grey matter, can be effectively tracked using non-invasive imaging techniques such as resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI), [18F]SynVesT-1 PET, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI).6 These techniques allow us to study critical biological processes underlying neurodevelopment, such as network formation, synaptic pruning, and myelination–processes that may be disrupted in DEEs, potentially even before the onset of seizures. Thus, applying longitudinal imaging in a well-characterised KCNQ2-DEE mouse model could offers a promising strategy to identify early biomarkers for neurodevelopmental dysfunction.

We hypothesise that pathogenic KCNQ2-DEE variants disrupt key neurodevelopmental processes, including functional connectivity (FC), synaptic density, and brain microstructure. These alterations may serve as early, quantifiable biomarkers for neurodevelopmental dysfunction. By employing longitudinal rs-fMRI, [18F]SynVesT-1 PET, and DTI in the kcnq2Thr274Met/+ mouse model at different neurodevelopmental stages, we aim to non-invasively track these biomarkers across critical developmental windows. Identifying such early biological changes would not only improve our understanding of KCNQ2-DEE pathophysiology but also provide a platform for evaluating the efficacy of targeted therapeutic interventions before behavioural deficits are observable. In addition, validating the use of functional and molecular imaging in a rodent model of DEE would also provide the necessary pre-clinical evidence to support studies in (young) human patients, facilitating subsequent clinical translation.

Methods

Experimental design

Experiments were performed on a total of 37 kcnq2Thr274Met/+ (KI) and 31 kcnq2+/+ WT 129 Sv strain mice between postnatal day (P) 14 to P106. Rs-fMRI and DTI were recorded in one imaging session, while [18F]SynVesT-1 PET was recorded in another imaging session, with a minimum interval of 48 h between both sessions (Fig. 1). Animals

The kcnq2Thr274Met/+ knock-in (KI) mice were generated as previously described on a 129Sv background.5 We maintained this genetic background to preserve the validated phenotype of this model and to facilitate direct comparison with previously published studies.5,14 We received four adult kcnq2Thr274Met/+ male mice from the Human Neurogenetics team in Marseille Medical Genetics Centre (Inserm/AMU U1251) and purchased eight wild-type (WT) female mice (129Sv strain) from Janvier labs. These mice were used for breeding in our facility. The resulting litters contained WT and KI mice and both were used for the study. Ear punching was performed between P11 and P13 and served as a mean of permanent identification and a source of genomic DNA for genotype verification. Genomic DNA was analysed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The following primers were used for genotyping: a common forward primer (5′-CTTGATCTTGTCCCCTGACTTGGTAGG-3′) and a common reverse primer (5′-CCTAACATCTCCAGAGTAGGAAGGTGCG-3′). The primers amplified a 603 bp WT fragment and/or a 688 bp mutant fragment, allowing for genotype determination. Mice were initially housed with their dam. At P21 they were weaned and group-housed in cages with a maximum of 8 mice per cage in a temperature and humidity-controlled environment with a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle and access to food and water ad libitum. Both WT and KI mice were housed in the same cage to control environmental variables, social interaction effects, and potential differences in housing conditions that could influence behaviour or phenotype.

A total of 68 mice (37 Kcnq2 T274M/+ (KI) and 31 Kcnq2 +/+ (WT)) from 15 litters (2–8 pups per litter, mean: 4.5) were included in the study. Genotype distribution per litter varied, with one WT-only and one KI-only litter, but the majority (13 out of 15) contained both genotypes, minimising potential litter-specific bias. Twelve of the WT mice included in the present study were also used as controls in our previously published dataset on normative brain development.6 Not all animals were successfully imaged at each timepoint due to factors such as premature death (n = 21), unsuccessful tail vein catheterisation for PET at infant age (n = 9), or scan exclusion after our predefined quality control procedure (n = 13 for rs-fMRI, n = 5 for DTI, n = 6 for PET). Quality control included visual inspection and exclusion of extreme values at the connection/region of interest (ROI) level (±3 standard deviation (SD)), applied across all imaging modalities.

During the study period of 365 days, 27 mice died: 21 during the experimental window (P14–P93; 17 KI and 4 WT) and 6 afterwards (all KI; P131–P284). Experimental-related deaths (n = 10, 7 KI and 3 WT) occurred during isoflurane induction (n = 1, KI), imaging procedures (n = 5, 4 KI and 1 WT), or recovery (n = 4, 2 KI and 2 WT). Except for the KI mouse that died during isoflurane induction at juvenile age, which had shown outlier PET data at infant age, all animals with experimental-related death had passed imaging quality control. Although the exact cause of death is unknown for the other mice, most KI mice were found dead in their cages with signs suggestive of seizure-induced mortality, such as rigid posture and extended limbs.5

All animal experiments complied with the European Committee Guidelines (Directive 2010/63/EU) and adhered to the 3Rs principles (replacement, reduction, and refinement) as well as the ARRIVE guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee for Animal Testing at the University of Antwerp, Belgium (permit number 2021–23). Animals were housed in the central animal facility of the University of Antwerp following the standards outlined in the European guidelines (2007/526/EC).

Image acquisition and data processing

The rs-fMRI, DTI, and [18F]SynVesT-1 PET imaging protocols, including anaesthesia, data acquisition, and processing, were identical to those previously described in Millevert et al. (2025) and were applied to both WT and KI mice in this study (Fig. 1).6

Rs-fMRI and DTI procedure

MRI acquisition using 7 T Pharmascan Bruker MR scanner and ParaVision 6.0.1 software (Bruker, Germany) included rs-fMRI for functional connectivity (FC) analysis, and DTI to assess white and grey matter integrity. To ensure stable physiological conditions during rs-fMRI, an optimised anaesthesia protocol with a continuous infusion of 0.1 mg/kg/h medetomidine hydrochloride (s.c., Domitor, Pfizer, Germany, catalogue # 1070499) and 0.5% isoflurane (gas mixture of 70% N2 and 30% O2) was used.15 After each MRI session, the mice received a subcutaneous injection of 0.25 mg/kg of atipamezole (Antisedan, Pfizer, Germany, catalogue # 1300185) to reverse the effects of medetomidine. The 10-min axial rs-fMRI scan was acquired using a T2∗-weighted gradient-echo echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence (600 repetitions with repetition time (TR) 1s, echo time (TE) 19 ms, 12 slices of 0.5 mm, pixel resolution (0.267 × 0.171) mm2). Coronal diffusion-weighted (DW) MRI images were acquired using a two-shot spin-echo EPI sequence (30 diffusion gradient directions (b-value 1000 s/mm2) along with 3 non-DW images, TR 7000 ms, TE 23 ms, 16 slices of 0.4 mm, thickness pixel dimensions of (0.2 × 0.2) mm2).

Rs-fMRI data processing and analysis

Rs-fMRI data were processed using SPM12 (Statistical Parametric Mapping, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk) in MATLAB 2021a (MathWorks, USA) for realignment and spatial normalisation to their respective age-specific EPI template, in-plane smoothening (Gaussian kernel, pixel size (0.534∗0.342)mm2), and bandpass filtering (0.01–0.12 Hz, Butterworth filter), followed by ROI-based FC analysis. The following ROIs were manually delineated separately for the left and right hemispheres on each age-specific EPI template using MRIcron software (version 1.0): cingulate cortex (Cg), retrosplenial cortex (Rs), entorhinal cortex (Ent), hippocampus (HC), orbitofrontal cortex (Orb), temporal association cortex (TeA), parietal association cortex (PA), prelimbic cortex (PreL), visual cortex (VC), auditory cortex (AUD), olfactory cortex (Olf), somatosensory cortex (SS), motor cortex (Mot), caudate putamen (CPu), frontal association cortex (FrA), thalamus (Thal), and cerebellum (CB). ROI selection prioritised cortical regions with reliable signal and functional relevance, aligning with previously derived networks from independent component analysis and established functional-imaging literature.6,16

DTI data processing and analysis

Diffusion metrics (fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), and axial diffusivity (AD)) were calculated in the native space of each animal using MRtrix3 (version 3.0.4).17 DTI data were analysed using tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) in FMRIB Software Library (FSL, version 5.0) with the creation of an age-specific white matter skeleton to examine both the white matter integrity of the whole-brain as well as the corpus callosum (delineated ROI on white matter skeleton). In addition, grey matter ROIs (same as rs-fMRI ROIs) were delineated on each age-specific DTI template. Individual mean values of FA, MD, RD, and AD were quantified for the white matter skeleton, the corpus callosum ROI, and grey matter ROIs using MATLAB 2021a.

[18F]SynVesT-1 PET imaging procedure

Dynamic 60-min [18F]SynVesT-1 microPET/Computed tomography (CT) imaging was performed under sedation with 1.5–2.5% isoflurane using two Siemens Inveon PET/CT scanners (Siemens Preclinical Solution, Knoxville, USA), each capable of simultaneously accommodating two mice. To minimise potential batch effects, we aimed to balance genotype distribution by pairing one KI and one WT mouse in the same scanner whenever possible. 7.9 MBq ±4.1 SD [18F]SynVesT-1, corresponding to an injected mass of 6.2 nmol/kg ± 0.8 SD and synthesised using a Trasis AllInOne synthesiser (Belgium) with a custom-built cassette (mean molar activity: 166.4 GBq/μmol ± 44.9 SD) was administered with an automated pump (Pump 11 Elite, Harvard Apparatus, USA) at a rate of 1 ml/min. A 10-min CT scan (80 kV, 500 μA) was acquired immediately after the PET scan for attenuation correction and anatomical reference. PET images were reconstructed using a previously described in-house ordered-subset expectation–maximisation (OSEM) algorithm with attenuation correction (12 frames × 10 s, 3 × 20 s, 3 × 30 s, 3 × 60 s, 3 × 150 s, and 9 × 300 s on a [128 × 128 × 159] grid of (0.776 × 0.776 × 0.796) mm3 voxels).

[18F]SynVesT-1 PET data processing and analysis

PET data was analysed using manually delineated whole brain and bilateral regional ROIs (Olf, AUD, VC, primary SS (SS1), secondary SS (SS2), Mot, CPu, HC, Thal, pons, medulla, and CB) on an age-specific 3D MRI template in the PMOD 3.6 software (Pmod Technologies, Zurich, Switzerland). The ROIs cover both cortical and subcortical brain regions and were selected based in their biological relevance.6,18 Kinetic modelling was performed using the Logan plot with regional time–activity curves (TACs, extracted using ROIs) and an image-derived input function (IDIF) to calculate the total volume of distribution (VT), a method previously validated by our group.19 Parametric whole-brain VT images were generated using PXMOD (PMOD 3.6) with voxel-wise Logan plot analysis based on metabolite- and plasma-corrected IDIF input, and group averages were overlaid on a 3D MRI template for anatomical reference.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.4.2). Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were verified using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests, respectively. Differences in body weight between genotypes (KI vs WT) and sexes (male vs female) were assessed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test. Survival analysis was performed using a log-rank test to compare the survival probabilities between genotypes (KI vs WT). The survival time was defined as the age at death or the age of censoring (365 days).

To assess the effects of genotype and age on FC (rs-fMRI data), FA, MD, AD, RD (DTI data), and synaptic density ([18F]SynVesT-1 PET data), a linear mixed-effects model was performed using the lme4 package in R. For rs-fMRI, this model was applied separately to each of the 561 connections. For DTI and PET, the model was applied separately to each predefined ROI for each imaging metric. The model included genotype, age, and their interaction term as fixed effects, while subject ID was included as a random intercept to account for the repeated measures within subjects. In all linear mixed-effects models, age was coded as a categorical factor with infancy as the reference level, and genotype was coded with KI as the reference level. Significance of the interaction terms between age and genotype was assessed, and if any interactions were found to be significant (P < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction were conducted and Cohen's d effect sizes were calculated using the effect size package based on estimated marginal means from emmeans. If no interaction was found, the main effects of age and genotype were assessed from the same model.

Pairwise Pearson correlations were computed for changes in all imaging metrics across nine overlapping ROIs during infant-to-juvenile and juvenile-to-adult periods, separately for WT and KI groups. Between-genotype differences in correlation strength were assessed using Fisher's r-to-z transformation with Bonferroni correction. Additionally, Pearson correlations between infant and adult values of FC and synaptic density were calculated across ROIs to evaluate longitudinal associations within animals, with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. A subgroup analysis compared early infant-stage rs-fMRI, PET, and DTI metrics between KI mice that died during the experiment (n = 16) and survivors (n = 16) using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests across ROIs, applying Bonferroni correction to P-values.

All P-values provided throughout the manuscript are adjusted for multiple comparisons, unless specifically mentioned otherwise.

Sample-size estimation

For each imaging modality, we performed an a priori power analysis tailored to the linear mixed effects model used in our study, using the simr package in R (version 4.4.2). The simulation design matrix was based on the actual subject/timepoint inclusion pattern in our dataset for each modality, thereby reproducing the unbalanced sample sizes and missing data inherent to our study. Assuming a medium effect size of β = 0.15, simulations (1000 runs per modality) yielded the following estimated powers: rs-fMRI: 94.1% (95% confidence interval (CI): 92.46–95.48), DTI: 94.9% (95% CI: 93.35–96.18), and [18F]SynVesT1 PET: 95.2% (95% CI: 93.69–96.44). These values confirm that, even with real world unbalanced and incomplete data, our study design provided more than adequate power to detect the prespecified interaction effect.

Results

A total of 68 mice (37 Kcnq2 T274M/+ (KI) and 31 Kcnq2 +/+ (WT)) were included in the study. Genotype and sex were balanced across imaging modalities and timepoints (Figure S1A). A full three-timepoint datasets were obtained for 20 WT and 15 KI mice (rs-fMRI), 23 WT and 15 KI (DTI), and 14 WT and 18 KI (PET, Figure S1B).

Over a period of 365 days, we observed a statistically significant reduced survival among KI mice (14/37, 38% survival) compared to the WT mice (27/31, 87% survival, P < 0.0001) (Figure S2A). The majority of KI mice were found dead in their cages in the morning, often with a rigid posture and extended limbs, most frequently before reaching adulthood (P90-100)–suggesting increased vulnerability in this genotype, consistent with previous reports of seizure-related mortality.5

At infant and juvenile age, there was no significant effect of genotype (P = 0.710/0.244) or sex-by-genotype interaction (P = 0.960/0.836) on body weight (Figure S2B). While no sex differences were observed at infant age (P = 0.603), males were significantly heavier than females at juvenile age (P = 0.0007). At adult age, both genotype (P = 0.003) and sex (P < 0.0001) had significant effects on weight, with no significant interaction (P = 0.739). Specifically, WT mice were heavier than KI mice, and males were heavier than females.

Age-dependent genotype effects on functional connectivity in a subset of resting-state brain regions was found, mainly involving the default mode-like network

FC values between 34 different ROIs were determined by a ROI-based FC analysis, resulting in 561 functional connections. A linear mixed-effects model revealed a significant genotype-by-age interaction in 123 of the 561 connections (22%). Post-hoc pairwise comparison with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons across both the 123 connections and the three age stages, confirmed significant genotype differences in 73 of these 123 connections. Six of these 73 connections showed significant genotype differences at two developmental stages, leading to significant FC differences between KI and WT identified in 39 connections at infant age (P = 0.002–0.045), in 5 connections at juvenile age (P = 0.025–0.049), and in 35 connections at adult age (P = <0.001–0.038) (Fig. 2, Figure S3). More specifically, at infant age, mean FC of the 39 significant connections was higher in KI mice than in WT mice with the estimated difference in mean FC between KI and WT ranging from 0.08 to 0.15 (mean: 0.11 ± 0.05 SD), corresponding to Cohen's d values (d) of 0.56–0.85 (95% CI: 0.01–1.40) (Fig. 2A and B). Most of the FC values demonstrating significant differences are related to connections between the default mode-like network (DMLN) and the associated cortical network (ACN) (n = 14), and within the DMLN (n = 7). At juvenile age, FC differences were minimal: 3 connections showed significantly higher FC (estimated difference: 0.08–0.10, mean SD: 0.05, d = 0.57–0.59, 95% CI: 0.01–1.16), and 2 showed significantly lower FC (estimated difference: −0.09, mean SD: 0.04, d = −0.66, 95% CI: −1.25 to −0.08) in KI compared to WT mice (Fig. 2C and D). At adult age, FC was significantly lower in KI than in WT mice in 35 connections, with an estimated difference in mean FC ranging from −0.18 to −0.08 (mean: −0.12 ± 0.05 SD, d = −1.15 to −0.64, 95% CI: −1.77 to −0.03) (Fig. 2E and F). Correlations between the imaging modalities

ROI-wise Pearson correlations were computed between longitudinal changes in all imaging metrics for each developmental interval (from infant to juvenile, and from juvenile to adult ages) separately in WT and KI mice (Figure S11). Several modality pairs showed significant correlations within each genotype after Bonferroni correction, most consistently between diffusivity metrics, whereas correlations involving the remaining imaging modalities were generally modest and, if significant, restricted to a subset of brain regions. MD, RD, and AD were highly correlated across all ROIs in both genotypes and both developmental intervals (P < 0.0001, r = 0.72–0.99), except for the RD–AD correlation in the thalamus of WT mice. From infant to juvenile age, significant negative correlations between FA and MD, and between FA and RD, were observed in the cerebellum, thalamus, and hippocampus in KI mice (P = 0.0002–0.044, r = −0.88 to −0.74), but not in WT mice. However, Fisher's r-to-z tests comparing the correlation coefficients between WT and KI for each ROI–modality pair yielded no significant differences after Bonferroni correction, indicating that the overall strength of cross-modal coupling was comparable between genotypes in both developmental intervals.

Infant-stage biomarkers showed limited predictive power

Pearson correlation analyses between infant and adult imaging measures, including FC (rs-fMRI) and synaptic density ([18F]SynVesT-1 PET), revealed only weak and statistically non-significant relationships across brain regions. Similarly, retrospective analysis comparing infant-stage imaging metrics between KI mice that died during the experimental window (n = 16) and those that survived (n = 16) identified no significant differences in rs-fMRI, PET, or DTI measures after correction for multiple comparisons (non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, Table S1). These results indicate that early-life imaging biomarkers were not robustly associated with either later neurodevelopmental profiles or survival within the timeframe studied.

Discussion

In this study, we longitudinally characterised neurodevelopmental alterations in brain network connectivity, synaptic density, and white and grey matter microstructure in a mouse model of KCNQ2-DEE. To achieve this, we applied rs-fMRI, [18F]SynVesT-1 PET, and DTI across three key developmental stages, enabling non-invasive assessment of these critical processes. While significant developmental differences between the genotypes were confined to a small subset of brain regions across all imaging modalities, several notable trends emerged: KI mice exhibited increased FC at infant age, elevated synaptic density at juvenile age, and decreased FC at adult age compared to WT mice.

The rs-fMRI results revealed distinct developmental trajectories in KI mice, suggesting age-dependent alterations in functional network maturation. At infant age, KI mice showed higher FC in the majority of connections, with significant changes primarily related to connections between the DMLN and the ACN and within the DMLN. Intriguingly, two core regions of the DMLN, the cingulate and retrosplenial cortices, exhibited a delayed increase in FC, becoming apparent only at juvenile age. During this juvenile stage, a transitional phase emerged, and FC in KI mice became more comparable to WT mice. At adult age, KI mice displayed a general reduction in FC relative to WT littermates, with significant decreases particularly in DMLN core regions and their connections.

The observed pattern may be mechanistically linked to previously reported age-dependent changes in neuronal excitability in the Kcnq2 T274M/+ mouse model, where increased neuronal firing due to reduced M current is seen from neonatal (P7-9) to post-weaning ages (P19-21), but not at juvenile age (P28-35).14 However, to our knowledge, there are no studies to date linking early-in-life increased excitability to increased resting-state FC, specifically in humans or rodent models with epilepsy. Given that Kv7.2 channels are key modulators of neuronal excitability during early brain development, it is plausible that early life hyperexcitability in KI mice may disrupt functional network maturation, ultimately resulting in compensatory hypoconnectivity in adulthood, a possibility that warrants further investigation. Notably, compensatory hypoconnectivity and network fragmentation have been reported in children with epilepsy, where longer epilepsy duration correlates with lower intelligence and more fragmented resting-state network architecture.20 However, causal mechanisms underlying this association has not been established yet.

The transition from hyper-to hypoconnectivity observed in our study mirrors findings from juvenile and adult mouse models of NDDs, even though rs-fMRI studies examining infant-stage mouse models of NDDs are lacking. For instance, Pagani et al. (2021) reported elevated FC within the core regions of the DMLN (such as the prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate) in juvenile (P29-35) tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (Tsc2+/−) mice.21 Alvino et al. (2025) performed longitudinal fMRI in WT and LgDel mice, a model of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, at juvenile (P35 ± 2) and adult (P111 ± 7) ages. They found a transition from widespread hyperconnectivity to focal hypoconnectivity, primarily affecting connections with the DMLN such as the hippocampus.22 Multiple adult autism spectrum disorder (ASD) mouse models have also demonstrated focal hypoconnectivity within the DMLN.12,13,23,24

These converging findings raise the question of whether DMLN hypoconnectivity, observed in our adult KI mice, may be associated with specific behavioural phenotypes such as those seen in ASD or more general cognitive deficits. In adult ASD mouse models, DMLN hypoconnectivity has been linked to reduced social behaviour.12 Although ASD features are observed in some individuals with KCNQ2-DEE,25 the Kcnq2 T274M/+ mouse model used in this study did not display abnormalities in exploratory behaviour or stereotypies using the Hole board and Marble-burying tests, although social behaviour tests, such as the three-chamber social interaction test, were not directly assessed.5 Interestingly, a different KI mouse model of KCNQ2-DEE (K556E variant) also reported normal sociability.26 Therefore, it remains unclear whether DMLN hypoconnectivity is a specific hallmark of ASD in the context of KCNQ2-DEE. Notably, we observed significantly decreased FC between the hippocampi and the left entorhinal cortex, and the right prelimbic cortex in adult KI mice—all regions of the DMLN implicated in spatial, contextual and episodic memory integration.27, 28, 29 Consistent with these findings, previous behavioural work in Kcnq2 T274M/+ mice revealed cognitive deficits in spatial learning tasks like the Morris water maze and Barnes maze.5 These data suggest that in our mouse model, DMLN hypoconnectivity could point towards a more general cognitive dysfunction, which may or may not overlap with ASD-related phenotypes. Further studies including cognitive/behavioural assessments are warranted to confirm this hypothesis.

Supporting the hypothesis of disrupted network maturation, [18F]SynVesT-1 PET imaging revealed notably higher synaptic density across multiple brain regions in the KI mice at the juvenile age compared to WT, with significant genotype-by-age interactions in the cerebellum and medulla. Although only the cerebellum reached statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons, we argue that this trend of elevated synaptic density in juvenile KI mice cannot be overlooked. The transient elevated VT values in juvenile KI mice, along with the significant reduction in synaptic density from infancy to later stages, aligns with the possibility of impaired or delayed synaptic pruning. It has been shown that Kv7.2 channels are expressed in primary microglia, cells necessary for synaptic pruning and subsequent normal brain development, and that blockade of these channels with XE-911 impairs microglial migration.30,31 Next, it is possible that the early hyperexcitability in KCNQ2-DEE may interfere with activity-dependent pruning mechanisms, leading to aberrant retention of synapses during critical developmental windows. This is consistent with studies in other NDDs, such as 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, tuberous sclerosis and Fragile X syndrome, where improper synaptic elimination results in maladaptive circuit formation and long-term connectivity deficits.22,32,33 Alvino et al. (2025) found higher synaptic spine density at juvenile age, but not at adult age in the LgDel mice compared to WT and argued that elevated VT values at the juvenile age may reflect a transient excess in synaptic connections, which are later lost or insufficiently integrated into functional circuits—possibly contributing to the functional hypoconnectivity observed in adulthood.22 This shared pattern patterns of widespread but subtle functional and molecular alterations in KCNQ2-DEE as well as other NDDs12,13,21, 22, 23, 24 suggests shared or convergent mechanisms of network-level vulnerability that may not be unique to KCNQ2-DEE. Together, these hypotheses—ranging from impaired or delayed pruning processes, to the contribution of early hyperexcitability, to broader convergent mechanisms across NDDs—warrant further investigation through targeted mechanistic studies to clarify their causal roles in network development and vulnerability.

Given the observations of functional brain network maturation deficits, both encompassing genotype-dependent FC and synaptic density changes, we also looked at possible microstructural alterations between brain regions using DTI. The DTI data in the Kcnq2 T274M/+ mouse model revealed limited genotype-by-age interaction effects, suggesting minimal genotype-related alterations in WM or GM microstructure. Specifically, no significant genotype or interaction effects were found in WM skeleton metrics across FA, MD, AD, or RD, indicating that WM tract integrity is largely preserved across development in this model. Within GM regions, a small number of genotype-by-age interactions were observed, including elevated FA in the hippocampus and thalamus at infant and juvenile ages, and reduced FA or RD in cortical areas such as the parietal and orbitofrontal cortices. However, these changes did not follow a consistent spatial or developmental pattern, and no significant interaction effects were found for MD, further supporting the idea that structural alterations in this model are subtle and not widespread. Notably, our findings are in line with a study by Bertero et al. (2018) investigating a mouse model of 16p11.2 deletion, a known genetic risk factor for ASD.13 Using ex-vivo tractography, they reported no genotype-dependent differences in WM tracts, ruling out macroscale structural disruptions as a driver of the observed functional hypoconnectivity. Although they observed a widespread increase in FA, these microstructural changes did not map directly onto the functional deficits, suggesting a decoupling between structural and functional connectivity.13 These findings imply that functional deficits may arise in the absence of structural changes and that subtle synaptic or network-level dysfunction underlies the phenotype, rather than disrupted axonal architecture. This underscores the need to complement structural imaging with functional modalities to fully capture the impact of NDDs on brain network dynamics.

In addition to the subtle genotype-by-age interaction effects observed in this study, we confirmed major time-dependent changes previously reported.6 These developmental trajectories included decreasing FC and synaptic density, alongside increasing FA values in both white and grey matter from infancy to juvenile stage, followed by relative stabilisation into adulthood. Although these processes likely reflect coordinated processes of brain maturation, correlations across the different imaging modalities were limited,6 suggesting that each method captures distinct and only partially overlapping aspects of neurodevelopment. This observation is further supported by the current findings, where genotype-related effects were subtle and often not co-localised across brain regions or imaging metrics. Moreover, correlation analyses revealed no significant between-genotype differences in the strength of correlations between the imaging modalities. This reinforces the idea that molecular, functional, and structural imaging markers may reflect divergent or uncoupled dimensions of neurodevelopment and highlights the importance of a multimodal approach to fully characterise the complex impacts of pathogenic variants in neurodevelopmental genes like KCNQ2.

A key strength of our study is the inclusion of infant mice (P14–21), a developmental window that corresponds to a critical period of synaptogenesis, circuit refinement, and heightened plasticity.7 This stage is particularly relevant for disorders like KCNQ2-DEE, which are caused by dysfunction of a gene already expressed during foetal development, where pathological changes likely begin well before clinical symptoms emerge.2 Capturing these early processes is essential not only to identify potential early biomarkers, but also to understand when and how deviations from normative brain development begin. The absence of significant linear correlations between infant and adult imaging biomarkers in the same mice highlights the complexity of neurodevelopmental trajectories, which involve dynamic, region-specific changes whose timing must be considered when interpreting longitudinal data. Despite its importance, imaging studies in mice during this early neonatal period remain sparse, largely due to technical and logistical challenges related to handling (e.g., tail vein catheterisation), anaesthesia, and data analysis.7 Future studies will benefit from continued efforts to optimise imaging pipelines for young animals, enabling earlier and more sensitive assessments of therapeutic efficacy, especially in the context of precision medicine.

Study limitations

In this study, the imaging analyses were based on predefined bilateral ROIs selected for each metric according to their biological relevance and the spatial resolution achievable with each imaging technique. This targeted ROI-based approach allowed more precise quantification in regions. However, unlike a voxel-wise whole-brain analysis, this method does not assess possible genotype-related effects across all brain regions beyond those predefined. This design prioritises sensitivity within biologically meaningful regions but may miss unexpected changes in areas outside the selected ROIs.

While our subgroup analysis comparing early imaging in KI mice that later died vs those that survived did not reveal significant differences, we acknowledge that the absence of direct behavioural, neurological, or seizure assessments limits the ability to link early functional and molecular brain alterations to later neurodevelopmental outcomes. Future studies incorporating comprehensive longitudinal behavioural phenotyping alongside early imaging are warranted.

Our primary goal was to explore whether different imaging techniques could serve as a non-invasive biomarker for neurodevelopmental dysfunction, ideally enabling the early evaluation of disease progression and therapeutic efficacy before behavioural symptoms manifest in adulthood. To achieve this, we applied stringent statistical criteria, including linear mixed models and Bonferroni correction for post-hoc testing, and used relatively large longitudinal cohorts (with 19–35 animals per group and 14–23 animals imaged across all three time points). Despite this robust design, the observed alterations were modest, suggesting that these imaging metrics may not be sufficiently sensitive or specific to serve as standalone biomarkers without much larger cohorts or complementary readouts.

Lastly, while our focus was on characterising neurodevelopmental dysfunction, it remains difficult to fully separate these changes from effects of hyperexcitability or subclinical seizures. Although no overt seizures were observed during imaging sessions, and animals were excluded from analysis when they moved during recordings, we cannot entirely rule out their occurrence. However, the use of isoflurane during DTI and PET imaging is expected to suppress epileptiform activity, given its known anti-seizure properties.34 Moreover, the combination of medetomidine (100 μg/kg/h) with 0.3% ISO has been shown to suppress development of epileptic activity in a WT mouse model.35 This underscores the inherent complexity of disentangling primary developmental abnormalities from secondary consequences of network hyperactivity in models of early-onset DEEs.

In summary, this study provides a longitudinal, multimodal in-vivo characterisation of brain development in a KCNQ2-DEE mouse model, spanning critical windows from infancy to adulthood. While genotype-related alterations were subtle and regionally confined per imaging technique, the integration of functional, molecular, and structural imaging revealed age-dependent deviations in network connectivity and synaptic density that suggest disturbances in brain maturation. These alterations are likely not unique to KCNQ2-DEE and may reflect still to be validated shared vulnerabilities across NDDs. The inclusion of neonatal imaging adds valuable insight into the earliest stages of circuit development, emphasising the importance of studying brain function prior to symptom onset. Although the current findings may not yet support the use of these modalities as standalone biomarkers, they lay important groundwork for future research aimed at assessing treatment efficacy during sensitive developmental periods.
