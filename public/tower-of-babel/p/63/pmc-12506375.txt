Salivary and serum levels of high mobility group box protein 1 in periodontitis: a cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Fatma Oner, Gokhan Ozgenc, Haktan Aslan, Ozlem Guzeltan, Yerda Ozkan Karasu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh mobility group box protein 1 (HMGB1) exhibits pro-inflammatory functions in chronic inflammatory diseases and is closely associated with other inflammatory markers contributing to sustained inflammatory milieu. This study aimed to evaluate HMGB1 levels and their associations with tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) and interleukin- 1b (IL-1β) in the saliva and serum of the periodontitis patients.MethodsSixty-six systematically healthy nonsmoker individuals were enrolled and categorized into three groups: (1) Control, (2) Stage I-II Periodontitis, and (3) Stage III-IV Periodontitis. Comprehensive periodontal assessments were performed. HMGB1, TNFα, IL-1β and TGFβ concentrations were analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test assessed group differences, and Pearson’s correlation evaluated relationships between quantitative variables.ResultsSalivary HMGB1 levels were significantly elevated in both periodontitis groups compared to controls (p < 0.0001). Serum HMGB1 levels were significantly higher in the Stage III–IV periodontitis group than in both the control (p = 0.022) and Stage I–II (p < 0.001) periodontitis groups. Serum HMGB1 showed positive correlations with IL-1β levels in both periodontitis groups (p < 0.05 for Stage I–II; p ≤ 0.01 for Stage III–IV). A significant positive correlation was also observed between salivary HMGB1 and TNFα levels in the Stage I–II periodontitis group (p ≤ 0.01).ConclusionHMGB1 levels increase in saliva and serum as periodontal disease worsens, suggesting enhanced HMGB1 expression was involved in the pathogenetic shifts in the disease process at local and systemic levels.Keywords: HMGB1, Periodontitis, TNFα, IL-1β, TGFβ

Background

Periodontitis is a biofilm-induced, chronic inflammatory disease that develops due to an imbalance between the oral microbiota and the host response. The host immune response is subverted by active bacterial invasion, allowing colonization by commensal pathogen bacteria, leading to transition of the oral microbial profile [1]. The disease pattern is driven by various host-mediated mediators [2].

High mobility group box protein 1 (HMGB1), or amphoterin or HMG1, is a non-histone, chromatin-associated protein molecule. HMGB1 is a member of the alarmin family produced by monocytes/macrophages, mature dendritic cells, and natural killer cells in response to inflammatory stimuli and is actively involved in the inflammatory response. It is a subset of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [3, 4]. At the steady state, HMGB1 is mainly located in the nucleus, and regulates the DNA structure and transcription; it is released into extracellular space through passive and active ways due to cellular necrosis and bacterial stimulation, respectively [5].

Once released, HMGB1 shows pro-inflammatory features by inducing an inflammatory response and facilitating the recruitment of leukocytes by binding the receptor for glycation advanced end product (RAGE), toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) or TLR4 [6, 7]. HMGB1 is a late mediator secreted following TNFα, IL-1β, and interferon gamma (IFNγ) expressions [8]. In turn, HMGB1 induces TNFa, IL-6, IL-8, IL-1b, macrophage inflammatory protein-1a and − 1b release from monocytes [9], leading to a prolonged inflammatory environment.

HMGB1 has been associated with inflammatory diseases, including periodontitis [10–12]. IL-1β and TNF stimulate HMGB1 production by gingival cells [13, 14] through regulating nitric oxide synthesis [13]. HMGB1 has osteoclastogenic characteristics, initiating osteoclast differentiation [15] and regulating receptor activator of nuclear factor of kappa beta ligand (RANKL)-mediated osteoclastic activity [16] through RAGE activation [15, 16]. Neutralizing HMGB1 activity decreased migration and osteoclastic differentiation of macrophages recruited by periodontal ligament cells [17]; and this was further supported by suppressed periodontal inflammation and bone loss in experimental periodontitis [18]. However, HMGB1 inhibition caused in an increase in inflammatory cells and M1 macrophages (macrophages producing pro-inflammatory related factors); while osteoblast numbers and organic matrix deposition were decreased [19]. These studies highlight the contradictory effects of HMGB1 on alveolar bone metabolism, indicating the need for further research.

Studies investigating the HMGB1 response to periodontitis have primarily focused on local responses, and examined HMGB1 expression in periodontal ligament fibroblasts, gingival epithelial cells, and gingival crevicular fluid (GCF). However, the relationship of HMGB1 to disease severity and source is unclear. Therefore, we tested the hypothesis that salivary and serum HMGB1 levels increase with the severity of periodontitis and are positively correlated with other inflammatory markers.

Methods

Study Design

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted at Ataturk University, Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Periodontology, between January and August 2024. The study followed the ethical principles of the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Ataturk University School of Medicine (B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/682).

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test using the G* Power 3.1 Software Package based on the previous studies [20]. The minimum required number of participants was determined as 21 when effect size was taken as f = 0.973, with a confidence interval of 95% at 80% power. Therefore, 22 subjects were included in each group.

Patient groups

A total of 66 systematically healthy, nonsmoking patients who applied to the Department of Periodontology for periodontal examination were recruited. Participants were distributed into three groups: (1) Control, (2) Stage I-II Periodontitis, and (3) Stage III-IV Periodontitis. The exclusion criteria of the participants were as follows: (1) Periodontal therapy within the last six months, (2) Presence of systemic disease or use of immunosuppressive medication, (3) Use of antibiotics/anti-inflammatory drugs within the last three months, (4) Current smoking or cessation within the last six months, and (5) Pregnancy/breastfeeding.

Before participation, the study protocol was explained to all individuals, and written informed consent was obtained from those who agreed to be involved in the study. Medical and dental histories were recorded.

Sample collection

Patients were invited to the clinic between 9:00 a.m. and 10:00 a.m. for sample collection and were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking (except water) or chewing gum prior to their visit. Unstimulated whole saliva samples were collected. Patients were asked to keep their mouths open for 5 min, allowing saliva to accumulate in the floor of the mouth, and then expectorate into 5 ml polypropylene tubes (ISOLAB SantrifugeTube, 078.02.001, Eschau, Germany). The saliva samples were centrifuged at 1000×g for 20 min; cell debris was removed, and supernatants were transferred into Eppendorf tubes.

Venous blood (5 milliliters) was taken from the antecubital vein by standard venipuncture method into a non-ethylene diamino tetra-acetic acid-containing test tube (BD Medical 366,566 SST II Vacutainer, New Jersey, USA). Blood was let to clot at room temperature for 2 h and then was centrifuged at 1000×g for 20 min to obtain serum. All samples were stored at − 80 ◦C until biochemical analyses were done.

Periodontal examination

Periodontal examination was performed by a single examiner (GO). Plaque index (PI) [21], bleeding on probing (BOP) [22], probing depth (PD), and clinical attachment levels (CAL) were measured on the six sites (mesio-buccal, buccal, disto-buccal, lingual, disto-lingual) of each tooth except third molars using a periodontal probe (Williams, Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL). PD and CAL were determined by measuring the distance from the base of the sulcus/periodontal pocket to the gingival margin and cemento-enamel junction, respectively. Panoramic radiographs were used to evaluate the alveolar bone loss. Diagnosis was made according to the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions. Participants having < 10% BOP and < 3 mm PD were categorized as controls [23]. Patients with maximum interdental CAL up to 4 mm and alveolar bone loss not extending the coronal third of the root were categorized as Stage I-II Periodontitis. Patients with 5 mm or more CAL and alveolar bone loss extending to the mid-third of root and beyond were categorized as Stage III-IV Periodontitis [24].

Biochemical analysis

SunRedBio Human Elisa Kits (SRB Technology Company, China) with catalog numbers 201-12-1636, 201-12-0083, 201-12-0144, and 201-12- 5480 were used for biochemical analysis for HMGB1, TNFα, IL-1β and TGFβ, respectively. All analyses were done according to the manufacturer`s instructions, and concentrations were calculated using the assay standard curve.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20. Data are presented as means, standard deviations, medians, minimums, maximums, percentages, and frequencies. The normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro Wilk-W test, Kolmogorov Smirnov test, Q-Q plots, and evaluation of skewness and kurtosis. Since the data showed a normal distribution ANOVA was used to compare the continuous variables across more than two independent groups with Tukey’s for post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For comparison between two independent groups; Welch’s t-test (two-tailed) was used when normal distribution was confirmed; Mann–Whitney U test was used when normality was not met. Effect sizes for t-tests were calculated using Cohen’s d. The difference was accepted as significant when p-values were less than 0.05. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationship between two quantitative variables, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 (two-tailed).

Table 2 summarizes the clinical periodontal parameters. PI, BOP, PD, and CAL were significantly lower in the control group than in the periodontitis groups (p < 0.05). PD and CAL were significantly higher in the Stage III-IV periodontitis group than in the Stage I-II periodontitis group (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Biochemical parameters

Salivary HMGB1 (Fig. 1A) and TNαa (Fig. 2A) levels were significantly elevated in Stage I-II and Stage III-IV periodontitis groups than in the control group (HMGB1: p < 0.0001; TNFα: p = 0.016 for Stage I-II periodontitis, p = 0.01 for Stage III-IV periodontitis). No difference was observed in salivary HMGB1 and TNFaα levels between the periodontitis groups (Figs. 1A and 2A). Salivary IL-1β showed no significant differences among groups (Fig. 2B) whereas salivary TGFβ was significantly higher in both periodontitis groups compared to control group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2C).

In serum, HMGB1 release in Stage III-IV periodontitis group was significantly higher compared to both control and Stage I-II periodontitis groups (p = 0.022 and p < 0.001, respectively) with no significant difference between Stage I-II periodontitis and control groups (Fig. 1B). Serum-IL-1β levels were elevated in Stage III-IV periodontitis group compared to both Stage I-II periodontitis and control groups (p < 0.0001). No significant differences were detected in serum TNFα and TGFβ levels among the groups (Fig. 2).

Table 3 shows the significant correlations observed between HMGB1 and other parameters. In the control group, salivary HMGB1 was positively correlated with TNF and TGFβ (p≤0.01), and with IL-1 (p < 0.05). In Stage I-II periodontitis, serum HMGB1 showed positive correlations with IL-1β and TGFβ (p < 0.05) while salivary HMGB1 correlated positively with TNFa (p≤0.01). In Stage III-IV periodontitis, serum HMGB1 was positively correlated only with IL-1β (p≤0.01). Table 4 shows significant correlations between periodontal and biochemical parameters in periodontitis groups. In Stage I-II periodontitis, PI was negatively correlated with serum-TGFβ level (p < 0.05). In Stage III-IV periodontitis, PI and BOP were negatively correlated with salivary-TGFβ level (p ≤ 0.01); whereas PI was positively correlated with serum-IL-1β (p < 0.05).

Discussion

HMGB1 is an intracellular protein with diverse extracellular functions in close association with other mediators. HMGB1 is produced by both immune cells and local periodontal tissues due to immediate and/or delayed stimuli; therefore its concentrations remain high during inflammation and contribute to the progression of chronic inflammatory diseases [25]. Furthermore, the synergistic effect of HMGB1 with reactive oxygen species (ROS) and its close association with other pro-inflammatory mediators suggest that HMGB1 plays crucial roles in chronic inflammatory diseases [8, 26, 27]. HMGB1 is considered as a link between periodontitis and systemic disorders such as atherosclerosis and diabetes [28, 29]. Additionaly, HMGB1 released due to P. gingivalis stimulation has been shown to induce miyocardial infection suggesting that HMGB1 may also serve as a link between periodontitis and cardiovascular diseases [30].

In this study, we analyzed the HMGB1, TNFα, IL-1, and TGF releases and their associations in the saliva and serum samples of the patients with healthy periodontal tissues, Stage I-II and Stage III-IV periodontitis. PI, BOP, PD, and CAL of the periodontitis groups were higher than the control group; however Stage III-IV periodontitis groups had significantly higher PD and CAL than Stage I-II periodontitis group showing more severe periodontal tissue destruction. Fusobacterium nucleatum,an anaerobic bacterium associated with periodontal disease, has been shown to stimulate HMGB1 expression in gingival epithelial cells [31, 32]. Both epithelial cells and periodontal ligament fibroblasts express HMGB1 upon stimulation [17, 18] and HMGB1 in turn regulates the migration of gingival and periodontal ligament fibroblasts [33]. Previous studies have reported elevated HMGB1 levels in the GCF of periodontitis patients [14, 20, 34–36]. In our study, we also demonstrated that the increase in HMGB1 level associated with the periodontitis was also detectable in the saliva and serum samples; indicating that the systemic response triggered by periodontitis leads to elevated HMGB1 release. Consistent with earlier findings, HMGB1 concentrations in saliva and serum samples of periodontitis patients were higher compared to controls. While elevated serum HMGB1 levels in chronic periodontitis have been previously shown [37]; our study differs in that we compared periodontitis groups with different severities to evaluate changes in HMGB1 release in the progression of periodontitis. There was significantly more HMGB1 concentration in the serum of Stage III-IV periodontitis group than Stage I-II periodontitis group, suggesting a relationship between HMGB1 levels, the extent of periodontal tissue destruction, and a potential link to systemic disorders.

Lipopolysachharide (LPS) from well-known periodontal pathogens has been reported to stimulate HMGB1 expression in vitro [38, 39]. Supportively, Tanerella forsythia, a recognized periodontopathogen, has been shown to HMGB1 secretion from macrophages [40]. The effect of LPS on gingival fibroblast appears to be time-dependent and short-lived [38]. In a ligature-induced periodontitis model, HMGB1 levels decreased by day 30 [39]. Collectively, these findings suggest that prolonged exposure to a dysbiotic microbiota may aggravate tissue destruction and impair the release of HMGB1. In parallel with this, we did not observe a significant increase in salivary HMGB1 levels as the severity of periodontitis worsens. HMGB1 is a highly motile protein predominatly located in the nucleus of healthy gingival epithelial tissues but shifts to cytoplasm in inflamed gingival epithelial tissues [38, 41]; generally remaining intracellular rather than being release into the extracellular space [39]. When released extracellulary, HMGB1 enhance oxidative stress which is critical in the progression of periodontal disease; but in turn excess ROS formation may promote the nuclear transportation of the HMGB1 [42]. Therefore the location of the HMGB1 might be of critical importance to determine its local role in periodontitis. It is also plausible that the impairment in HMGB1 expression may increase the susceptibility to bacterial stimuli, contributing to severe tissue destruction. HMGB1 has three different isoforms; prolonged exposure to ROS promotes to formation of oxidized HMGB1 which has non-inflammatory properties [43, 44]. Although total salivary-HMGB1 concentration did not change in the transition to stage III-IV periodontitis, the distribution of HMGB1 isoforms needs further investigation which may vary due to excess oxidative stress seen in periodontitis.

HMGB1 is a key mediator whose secretion is closely linked to other inflammatory cytokines. Our data revealed that, in the absence of periodontal disease, HMGB1 levels were positively correlated with TNFα, IL-1β and TGFβ in saliva, however in the stage I-II periodontitis, only positive correlation was observed with salivary-TNFα levels. TNFα and IL-1 have been shown to stimulate HMGB1 release in gingival epithelial cells and fibroblasts [13, 14, 18, 40]. HMGB1 in turn induces the expressions of TNFα, IL-1, IL-6, IL-17 and IL-11 from periodontal ligament cells [27]. HMGB1 also regulates immune cell response to LPS, which results in excess TNF production [45].

In our data, the positive correlation between salivary HMGB1 and TNFα levels was not observed in serum samples in Stage I-II periodontitis group indicating that bacterial virulence factors might play a key role in this association. Previous studies have reported strong associations between HMGB1 and TNFα, and IL-1β in serum of periodontitis patients [38]. In contrast, we found that HMGB1 concentration in serum was only positively correlated with IL-1β concentrations in both Stage I-II and Stage III-IV periodontitis groups, indicating that the HMGB1-IL-1 axis might aggravate the systemic response and contribute to the progression of periodontitis. Furthermore, the close association between HMGB1 and IL-1β impair wound healing [46]. Therefore neutralizing HMGB1 could be an alternative method to prevent periodontitis when considering the delayed-secretion and long-term maintenance in chronic inflammation [25, 42].

While this study has notable strengths—such as including periodontitis groups with varying severities and measuring both salivary and serum HMGB1 levels to assess its role at local and systemic levels—it also has some limitations. First, although saliva was used for its non-invasive and practical advantages, it lacks the site-specific diagnostic precision of GCF; future studies should consider GCF sampling to better reflect local periodontal inflammation. Second, periodontitis patients were not categorized according to disease grade, which limits our ability to assess the role of HMGB1 in the rate of disease progression. Third, HMGB1 levels were not measured following periodontal therapy, which could have provided valuable information on the biomarker’s response to treatment. Lastly, as the observed associations are correlative, further studies are necessary to confirm a causal relationship and to elucidate the mechanisms by which HMGB1 contributes to periodontal disease progression.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that HMGB1 levels were significantly elevated in both saliva and serum of individuals with periodontitis compared to healthy controls, supporting its role as a potential biomarker in periodontal disease. Serum HMGB1 levels were particularly associated with advanced stages of periodontitis, indicating its possible involvement in systemic inflammatory responses triggered by severe periodontal tissue destruction. While salivary HMGB1 levels distinguished periodontitis patients from healthy individuals, no significant difference was observed between stage I-II and stage III-IV groups, suggesting that local HMGB1 release may be regulated differently at varying disease severities. Moreover, the observed correlations between HMGB1 and key inflammatory mediators such as TNFα and IL-1β reinforce HMGB1’s central role in the inflammatory cascade in periodontitis.

Future studies should focus on profiling specific HMGB1 isoforms, particularly their redox states, to differentiate between the inflammatory and non-inflammatory roles of the protein in periodontitis. Understanding these variations could help clarify the dual role HMGB1 plays in disease progression and resolution. Additionally, detailed in vitro and in vivo investigations are warranted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which HMGB1 contributes to periodontal tissue destruction and immune modulation; these mechanistic insights may pave the way for therapeutic targeting of HMGB1; the development of neutralizing agents or inhibitors holds promise as an adjunctive strategy in periodontal therapy to suppress chronic inflammation and potentially reduce the risk of systemic complications linked to periodontitis.
