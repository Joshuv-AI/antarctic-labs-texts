Increased Prevalence of Extrathymic Neoplasms in Myasthenia Gravis Patients‐A Population‐Based, Matched Case–Control Study
Authors: Keshet Pardo, Tasneem Kab, Mark A. Hellmann, Itay Lotan, Tzippy Shochat, Nadav Loebl, Bar Yacobi, Yitzhak Friedman, Leor Perl, Nitai Shimon, Yiska Loewenberg Weisband, Liya Bieber, Eitan Auriel, Adi Wilf‐Yarkoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsMyasthenia gravis (MG) is associated with thymic neoplasms. However, an increased prevalence of extrathymic neoplasms has also been reported. This study aimed to evaluate the rates of malignancy in MG patients while accounting for risk factors such as disease characteristics and immunomodulatory treatments.MethodsWe conducted a case–control study using the Clalit Health Services database, applying a machine learning (ML) algorithm to minimize diagnosis misclassification. We included MG patients aged 18 years and older, with a sex‐ and age‐matched control group in a 1:3 ratio. We compared the prevalence and hazard ratios of extrathymic neoplasms between the groups.ResultsA total of 1558 patients with a high probability of MG, according to our ML model, were included in the cohort, alongside a control group of 4674 individuals. MG patients had a higher prevalence of malignancy prior to MG diagnosis, with an odds ratio of 1.95 (95% CI, 1.65–2.08), and a higher incidence of malignancy after MG diagnosis, with a hazard ratio of 1.56 (95% CI, 1.38–1.77). The most prevalent extrathymic neoplasms after MG diagnosis were respiratory, intrathoracic, skin (specifically non‐melanoma), urinary tract, soft tissue, and myelodysplastic syndrome. Risk factors for malignancy included age, male sex, and thymoma. Immunosuppressive treatment did not increase the risk of malignancy.DiscussionMG patients have a higher prevalence of both solid and hematologic neoplasms compared to non‐myasthenic controls, regardless of immunosuppressive treatment. This supports the notion that malignancy is related to MG disease itself rather than external factors.

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is associated with thymoma in 10%–15% of patients, and 30%–50% of those with thymoma develop MG [1, 2]. Thymoma is not the only malignancy associated with MG. Numerous reports have suggested a higher prevalence of malignancies in MG patients and an increased incidence following an MG diagnosis [3, 4]. However, there is inconsistency regarding which malignancies may be associated with MG. Some studies have reported associations with skin cancers [5], male and female genital cancers [5, 6], digestive system cancers [5, 6], breast cancer, lung cancer [6], and lymphoma [3, 7].

Additional inconsistencies in the literature concern the risk factors for malignancies in MG patients. Some studies have identified treatments as potential risk factors, including intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) [6, 8] and immunosuppressive drugs, particularly azathioprine (AZA), which has been linked to a higher risk of non‐melanoma skin cancer [5, 9, 10]. Other studies, however, have not demonstrated such effects [4, 11]. Interestingly, some studies have reported thymoma as a risk factor for extrathymic malignancies [5, 12, 13, 14], while others have found no evidence that thymoma is an independent risk factor for extra‐thymic malignancies in MG patients [4, 6, 11].

Research based on administrative databases requires rigorous development and validation of algorithms for case ascertainment to accurately identify true MG patients [15, 16, 17]. We believe that the inconsistencies regarding malignancy in MG patients may stem from differences in defining the MG population. Many studies rely on large databases with ICD‐9 or ICD‐10 diagnoses made by non‐specialists [3, 4, 5, 6, 7], without validation, which may include patients with only a clinical suspicion and insufficient data to confirm MG.

Our study aimed to assess the prevalence and incidence of malignancies in MG patients and identify risk factors within this population.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

This is a descriptive, nationwide, registry‐based case–control study.

Data for this study were extracted from the Clalit Health Services (CHS) database. CHS is a health maintenance organization (HMO) that serves as both a healthcare insurer and provider. As the largest of the four HMOs in Israel, CHS operates 14 hospitals (inpatient data) and an extensive network of community care facilities. It provides comprehensive healthcare to approximately 5 million members.

The CHS electronic database integrates continuous real‐time inputs from physicians and other healthcare providers, including primary care physicians, community specialty clinics, hospitalizations, laboratory results, imaging acquisitions, and pharmacy records.

The study was approved by the Rabin Medical Center Institutional Review Board, with a waiver of informed consent due to its retrospective design (Protocol No. 163–23).

Data were extracted from the CHS database using Clalit's Data Sharing Platform, powered by MDClone (https://www.mdclone.com).

Patient Selection Using Machine Learning Based Algorithm

In order to accurately detect MG patient within the CHS database we have developed a machine learning (ML)‐based algorithm. For our model we utilized the CHS database to extract all adult CHS members (age > 18 years) who, as of January 1, 2014, had continuous membership for this period in CHS and MG‐related diagnostic codes documented based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes (G70.00 and G70.01).

Follow‐up commenced on January 1, 2014 and ended at the earliest occurrence of malignancy diagnosis, death, or the study termination date (December 31, 2024). Out of this cohort we created a reference cohort composed of patients that visited the neuroimmunology outpatient clinic at Rabin Medical Center, which serves as a tertiary center in Israel. Patients were defined as “true MG” based on typical clinical features combined with at least one supportive neurophysiology test (single‐fiber EMG, repetitive nerve stimulation, according to standard abnormality cut‐offs) and/or serology (AChR antibodies) [16]. Subjects were classified as “not MG” if they had at least one MG‐related code but did not have an MG diagnosis based on review of the medical record by a neurologist specializing in MG. This reference cohort was used to train the ML model.

We trained a CatBoost based classifier model (https://catboost.ai/en/docs/). CatBoost is a gradient‐boosted decision‐tree classifier that performs well on tabular epidemiologic data and can capture non‐linear relationships and feature interactions. The ML model incorporated demographic data, diagnostic parameters, and treatment records. We used a 90–10‐split for training and testing the model, followed by a 10‐fold cross‐validation. CatBoost model configuration: 200 iterations, learning rate 0.1, tree depth 4, Logloss objective, balanced class weights, native preprocessing (no external imputation or encoding), validation scheme (90–10 split, 10‐fold cross‐validation, 1000 bootstrap iterations for CIs), and performance metrics. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Additional information regarding our ML model can be found in the Supporting Information. This includes the full feature set, training/validation framework, hyperparameters, and interpretability outputs (feature importance and SHAP plots) to support reproducibility.

Applying of the Model on CHS Database

We applied the ML model on the CHS database for each patient with an MG‐related code. Each subject received a probability score (0–1) indicating the likelihood of true MG diagnosis. A score of zero indicates the lowest probability, while one indicates the highest probability. Out of the 4436 patients in the CHS database, 1842 (41.5%) received a high probability (≥ 0.5) of having MG according to our model. Of these, 1558 patients (84.5%) received a probability of 0.7 and above. Meanwhile, 2594 patients had low probability (< 0.5), with 2178 patients (84%) receiving a score of 0.1 and below (Figure S1).

Study Population

Our cohort included a high probability (≥ 0.7) of MG diagnosis based on our ML‐based algorithm. The control group consisted of adults without an MG diagnosis code, matched by age and sex in a 3:1 ratio.

For each patient, we collected data on sex, age, disease duration, and treatment, including pyridostigmine, prednisone, intravenous immunoglobulin, and immunosuppressive therapies (azathioprine, methotrexate, rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclosporine).

We performed a sub‐analysis of risk factors for malignancy within the MG population on all MG patients in our cohort, comparing those who developed malignancy only after diagnosis to those who did not.

Cancer Diagnosis

Cancer diagnoses were based on the CHS registry and defined by ICD‐10 diagnostic codes. We collected separate data on extrathymic cancers diagnosed before and after the MG diagnosis. A detailed list of cancer types and corresponding codes included in our analysis is provided in (Table S1), in the Supporting Information.

Additionally, we separately collected data on thymoma diagnoses.

Statistical Analysis

We used SAS Software, Version 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), based on normality distribution. Categorical variables by counts and percentages (N, %). Logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to calculate hazard ratios. For new malignancy after MG, the Fine and Gray methodology was used for treating death, with no malignancy, as a competing risk. For the cause specific analysis, by type of malignancy, the Cox methodology was used. Two‐sided p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Extra‐Thymic Malignancy After MG Diagnosis

Patients with MG had higher rates of new malignancy after the diagnosis of MG compared to the control group (Table 2) (Figure 3B). The incidence of new cancers was highest in the first 3 years after the MG diagnosis (Figure 3C) (Table 2). Relevant clinical data was available for 341 MG patients from Rabin Medical center (RMC), age and sex matched to 1023 controls. We found an increased risk for extrathymic cancer in MG RMC patients compared to controls (Table 2). A similar extrathymic cancer percentage was noticed in MG RMC (21.8%) and MG CHS database (25.2%) (Table 2).

Specific cancers that were more prevalent after an MG diagnosis included cancers of the respiratory and intrathoracic organs, particularly excluding the lung and bronchus. Additionally, skin cancers were more prevalent, specifically non‐melanoma skin cancer, as well as urinary tract cancers and soft tissue cancers. Follow‐up duration differed between MG cases and controls (Table 2).

Lymphoid and hematopoietic tissue cancers were not more prevalent in MG patients. However, when divided into subcategories, myelodysplastic syndrome was found to be significantly more prevalent in MG (Figure 4), Supporting Information (Table S3).

Risk Factors for Malignancy Within the MG Population

We performed a sub‐analysis of all MG patients in our cohort, comparing those who developed malignancy only after diagnosis to those who did not. Significant risk factors for malignancy included age, male sex, and co‐existence of thymoma. The presence of AChR antibodies was not a significant risk factor, nor was treatment (Table 3). A total of 26 and 51 malignancies were noticed before and after thymoma diagnosis, respectively, but no specific tumor was identified. The median time for malignancy diagnosis before thymoma diagnosis was 0.5 years (IQR 3.9–0.03) and 2.3 years (IQR 0.1–14.8) after thymoma.

Discussion

The results demonstrated an increased incidence of extra‐thymic malignancy both before and after MG diagnosis, with a notable rise in the years leading up to diagnosis.

The higher prevalence of extra‐thymic malignancy in MG patients has been reported in previous studies [3, 4, 6], and may be explained by immune dysregulation, which could predispose MG patients to malignancy. Similar associations between autoimmune diseases and cancer have been observed, such as in rheumatoid arthritis [15, 17], further supporting this theory.

Compared to the non‐MG population, the most prevalent extra‐thymic cancers in MG patients were skin cancers (particularly non‐melanoma skin cancer), urinary tract cancers, and soft tissue cancers. Similar findings, especially regarding skin cancers, have been reported previously [5, 6]. However, unlike earlier studies [5, 6], we did not find lung cancer to be more prevalent in MG patients. Instead, other respiratory and intrathoracic cancers (including cancers of the nasal cavities, sinuses, and larynx) were significantly more common. While we excluded thymoma, some degree of misclassification cannot be ruled out. Additionally, in contrast to previous studies [3, 10], our study found no significant difference in lymphoma prevalence between MG patients and controls. The only hematologic malignancy that was significantly more common in MG patients was myelodysplastic syndrome, an association not previously reported. These differences may stem from past misclassification in ICD coding.

Although immunosuppressive therapy has been linked to increased cancer risk in previous studies [5, 6, 7, 8], our study did not find it to be an independent risk factor. This discrepancy may be explained by our use of a time‐varying Cox model analysis. The potential link between immunosuppressive therapy and malignancy risk has been debated in other autoimmune diseases as well. A recent large‐scale study in rheumatoid arthritis patients found no association between immunosuppressive drugs and malignancy [18]. Similarly, a study on ocular inflammatory disease patients treated with immunosuppressive therapy found no increase in cancer‐specific mortality [19]. Furthermore, two large meta‐analyses of patients with various immune‐mediated diseases receiving different immunosuppressive treatments failed to demonstrate an increased cancer risk compared to untreated patients [20, 21]. Our findings support the notion that malignancy in MG patients is related to the disease itself rather than external factors like immunosuppressive therapy.

The increased prevalence of malignancy in the 3 years before and after MG diagnosis raises the possibility of a paraneoplastic disorder, a hypothesis previously suggested by Khateb et al. [22]. However, some of the malignancies identified in our study, such as non‐melanoma skin cancer, are not typically associated with paraneoplastic syndromes. Additionally, no specific tumor was consistently found in the years before MG diagnosis. This suggests that rather than being a paraneoplastic syndrome, MG may share a common underlying immune dysregulation with cancer. MG symptoms are usually fluctuating rather than progressive, unlike a typical paraneoplastic disease.

Among demographic risk factors for malignancy within the MG population, we identified older age at diagnosis as a significant factor, consistent with previous studies [4, 8, 11]. Male sex was also found to be a risk factor, aligning with the well‐documented higher incidence of most cancers in men [23, 24], including malignancies linked to autoimmune diseases, such as non‐Hodgkin lymphoma [25, 26]. The observed sex difference in malignancy risk within MG patients may also be influenced by the fact that MG is more common in men within the elderly population. Among MG‐specific disease features, thymoma was associated with an increased malignancy risk, consistent with findings from large database studies on MG patients [5, 8], and in patients with thymoma regardless of MG status [27]. It is hypothesized that thymomas contribute to immune dysregulation, impairing immune surveillance and increasing the risk of secondary malignancies [28]. In contrast, AChR antibodies were not associated with malignancy risk in our study, a finding consistent with previous reports [5].

The findings of our study suggest that malignancy screening should be considered at the time of MG diagnosis and during the following 3 years, particularly in higher‐risk patients such as older males and those with thymoma. Based on our results, we propose that screening should primarily focus on intrathoracic imaging, dermatologic examination, and complete blood count. However, further research, preferably prospective studies, is needed to establish the benefit of such screening in these patients.

Our study has some limitations; as with any retrospective cohort study based on clinical and administrative databases, potential limitations include data completeness and accuracy, particularly regarding MG and cancer misclassification. Nonetheless, the use of our machine learning algorithm, which accurately identified MG patients, likely reduced this issue and enhanced the reliability of our findings. In addition, we cannot draw conclusions regarding any association between MuSK‐MG and malignancy [29], as MuSK antibody testing was not available.

Conclusion

Our study established a significant association between MG and malignancy, both in the years before and after MG diagnosis. The cancers most strongly associated with MG were respiratory and intrathoracic cancers, non‐melanoma skin cancer, urinary tract cancers, soft tissue cancers, and myelodysplastic syndrome. Immunosuppressive treatment did not increase malignancy risk, while older age, male sex, and thymoma were identified as risk factors.
