Performance of an Arabic translation of the patient determined disease steps (PDDS) scale in Saudi patients with multiple sclerosis
Authors: Salman Aljarallah, Husam Alkhathlan, Abdullah Almushawah, Ahmed Badahdah, Nawaf Alfaifi, Durria A. Abdulmaged-Ahmed, Nuha M. Alkhawajah
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) is commonly used to measure and quantify disabilities in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS). The patient-determined disease steps (PDDS) scale is a patient-reported measure of disability that is useful in MS. However, the Arabic version of the PDDS has only been tested in Jordanian patients. Although both populations share similar Arabic languages, it is plausible that differences in dialects and educational systems could alter the generalizability of the tool. In this prospective study, patients with MS were asked to complete a printed translated version of the (PDDS), and the results were compared to their EDSS scores, functional system scores, and walking speed measures. Patients with relapsing or progressive MS were included in the study. Spearman rho rank-order correlation coefficients (P) were used to measure the correlation between the PDDS and other variables. We considered previously reported P values > .1, .3, and .5 as small, moderate, and strong correlations, respectively. A total of 79 patients completed the study. The PDDS showed a strong correlation with the EDSS (P = .69, 95% confidence interval 0.55–0.79, P < .001). PDDS is associated with cerebellar, pyramidal, and bladder dysfunctions. It was also moderately correlated with the timed-25-foot walk test and timed-up-and-go test. The Arabic version of the PDDS performed similarly to English and other languages when tested on a cohort of patients with MS.

1. Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease of the central nervous system.[1] The disease often manifests with a multitude of symptoms, most notably walking difficulties and gait impairment, in addition to other symptoms, such as fatigue, cognitive difficulties, visual impairment, muscle weakness, sensory dysfunction, and trouble with bladder or bowel control.[1] Globally, MS is the second leading cause of disability in the young.[2] MS symptoms significantly affect functioning and can reduce a patient’s quality of life.[3] Measuring and quantifying disabilities in patients with MS is challenging but crucial. Kurtzke developed a clinical scale to measure disability in patients with MS. He used individual scores representing the neurological systems commonly affected (functional systems scores) and incorporated them into a globally ranked step scale called the Disability Status Scale.[4] The Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) was later published as a modified version of the original disease steps and has since become the most widely used scoring system for disability in MS and the main outcome measure in clinical trials.[5] The EDSS has often been criticized for a few limitations. These limitations include nonlinearity, subjectivity, requirement of expertise in neurological examination, intra- and inter-rater variability, and bias toward motor disability and walking.[6] In addition, applying EDSS in routine neurological care can be cumbersome and time-consuming in busy practice. Moreover, it requires the presence of the patient and assessor at the same location,[7] which is a limitation in practice, where patients are not frequently seen or have other limitations that prevent them from attending the clinic. For example, during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, countries had to restrict hospital visits and routine care, which was replaced by virtual visits conducted through phone or video conferences. In such situations, it would have been helpful to have alternative means of disability measurement that does not require clinical visits.

The patient-determined disease steps (PDDS) is a patient-reported measure of disability in MS based on a disease step-scale model developed to overcome some EDSS limitations.[7,8] The PDDS encompasses 9 ordinal levels ranging from 0 to 8, with higher numbers indicating a worse disability. PDDS scores can be categorized as mild, moderate, or severe.[7] Studies by Learmonth et al[7] performed on a large group of English-speaking patients with MS established a strong correlation between the PDDS and EDSS scores. Since then, the PDDS has been used as a surrogate marker for disability in clinical trials and prospective MS cohorts.[9,10] As the PDDS is a written, patient-reported measure, it relies heavily on the patients’ language and ability to understand the scale. This limits the application of this scale to non-English-speaking patients with MS (pwMS). Attempts to validate the PDDS in other languages have been successful, including Brazilian, Spanish, Italian, and Turkish.[11–14]

Only 1 study has examined an Arabic version of the PPDS in a cohort of patients with MS in Jordan.[15] This study was limited by the exclusion of patients who could not ambulate independently and those with recent relapse. This limits the potential use of this scale in Arabic-speaking cohorts, and could lead to the exclusion of these patients from international studies. Therefore, this study aimed to translate and validate an Arabic version of the PDDS and correlate its results with the EDSS.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

This prospective cohort study was conducted at the King Saud University Medical City in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, from January to December 2020. We consecutively screened patients with MS who visited the MS clinic during the study period. The study was approved by the institutional review board of King Saud University (# E205578). The sample size was determined to be 80 based on a commonly used respondent-to-item ratio of 10, also considering published PDDS validation studies in other languages that enrolled 58 to 103 patients.[11,14,16]

2.2. Translation process

An English version of the PDDS has been obtained from the authors of the original manuscript. Three native Arabic-speaking researchers fluent in Arabic and English independently translated the original English PDDS. Two other native speakers performed backward translations of the 3 versions from Arabic to English, without prior knowledge of the original English scale. A panel of 5 members convened and reviewed each item in each version, and voted on the most appropriate translation. An item was considered appropriate if it resulted in exact English wording after translation, and was close to the commonly used Arabic dialect. Translations that yielded the majority of votes were adopted in the final Arabic version of the PDDS. This version was tested on a pilot of 5 patients with MS and was found to be easy to understand and achieve a reasonable output.

2.3. Subjects

Patients who had attended the MS clinic were screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were as follows: confirmed diagnosis of MS or clinically isolated syndrome according to the McDonald 2017 criteria,[17] secondary progressive MS, or primary progressive MS fulfilling the Lublin 2013 criteria[18]; age 16 years or older; ability to read in the Arabic language; and completion of at least an elementary level of school education or higher to ensure the ability to read and understand written language. We excluded patients with disabilities caused by a disease other than MS. Patients with severe vision impairment that interfered with their ability to read or severe cognitive impairment were also excluded from this study. Cognitive impairment was defined as the presence of cognitive symptoms with a Montreal Cognitive Assessment score of <10 or a Mini-Mental State Exam score of less than 18, which could affect the patients’ comprehension of instructions.[19,20]

The subjects were approached during their prescheduled clinic visits, visits for infusions, or hospitalizations for reasons related to MS such as relapse. A researcher obtained written informed consent from the patients or patient surrogates. After screening for vision and symptoms of cognitive impairment, participants were provided with a printed version of the PDDS before undergoing the EDSS evaluation, timed up-and-go test (TUG), and timed 25-foot walk test (T25FW). Trained researchers performed measurements.

2.4. Patient Determined Disease Steps Scale

The translated PDDS included a written set of instructions for selecting the best answer. The subjects would read and follow instructions without interference from study personnel. Participants selected the score that best described their status. The EDSS rater was blinded to the PDDS scores.

2.5. Extended Disability Status Scale

To determine the degree of neurological disability, all participants underwent a complete neurological examination by an experienced neurologist certified to perform the EDSS. EDSS scores range from 0 (normal examination) to 10 (no disability), indicating death due to MS.[4] To avoid bias, the EDSS examiner was blinded to the PDDS scores selected by the subjects.

2.6. T25FW test

T25FW was used to estimate the walking speed of the study participants.[21] We analyzed the average of the 2 trials measured in seconds.

2.7. TUG test

TUG tests were conducted during the visit. Patients were asked to rise from the chair, walk 3 m, turn 180° before returning to the chair, and sit down.[22]

In line with other PDDS validation studies in other languages, construct validity was determined by analyzing the correlation between the PDDS and the EDSS, T25FW, and TUG. We also examined the correlations between the PDDS, demographic variables, and individual functional system scores to determine which functional subsystems correlated best with the PDDS.

2.8. Statistical analysis

We used proportions, medians, and 25th and 75th percentiles for descriptive information to describe the numerical variables. To assess the correlation between the PDDS and EDSS and between these 2 variables and other demographic and disease characteristics, we used Spearman’s rho rank-order correlation coefficients (P). We considered previously reported P values >.1, .3, and .5, indicating small, moderate, and strong correlations, respectively.[23] Fisher’s z test was used to compare Spearman’s correlations between the 2 scales (EDSS and PDDS) for all considered variables. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA15 software. Participants with missing data were excluded from this study.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

Seventy-nine patients completed the study (Table 1). As expected, in a contemporary MS cohort, most patients (65.8%) were females. Most patients were young, with a mean age of 34 years. Most patients were college educated (approximately 81%), and only one did not complete high school. Regarding MS subtypes, 65 subjects (82%) had relapsing-remitting MS, whereas 10 subjects (13%) had progressive MS. The mean duration between the diagnosis of MS and the study visit (disease duration) was 7.6 years (SD ± 5.7). Regarding disability, 66 patients (84%) had mild-to-moderate disability (EDSS score < 4.5) and did not require walking assistance. The median EDSS score of the cohort was 2.5 (the 25th and 75th percentiles were 1.5 and 3.5).

3.2. Correlation between PDDS and EDSS

There was a strong correlation between the EDSS and PDDS scores (P = .69, 95% confidence interval of 0.55–0.79, P < .001) (see Fig. 1). Similar to other validation studies and to examine the magnitude of change, we regressed the PDDS on the EDSS scores, which resulted in an intercept of 1.44 (95% confidence interval, 1.0–1.9, P < .0001) (Fig. 1). This correlation remained strong but was more robust among patients with a higher EDSS (>4.0), where P was .75 (P < .005) compared to lower EDSS scores (P = .48).

3.3. Performance in relation to functional system scores

In terms of functional systems, we found that both the PDDS and EDSS strongly correlated with pyramidal (P = .68 and .82, respectively), cerebellar (0.68 and 0.6), and bladder scores (Table 2). Compared with the PDDS, the EDSS had a stronger correlation with the sensory and brainstem functional scores. The EDSS and PDDS had a similarly weak correlation with cognition and vision functional scores.

3.4. Impact of demographic and disease characteristics

We also examined whether demographic and other disease characteristics correlated with the PDDS and EDDS (Table 3). We noticed that PDDS and EDSS were strongly correlated with the ambulation score and the use of walking aids (all had P > −.6) and were moderately correlated with walking speed measured by T25FW and TUG (P scores were >.3 but <.5). The EDSS correlated better with age and disease duration than the PDDS did.

3.5. Performance in progressive MS

Approximately 13% of the cohort had progressive MS. The mean age was 45.2 years (SD = 9.9), which is higher than the mean age of patients with relapsing MS and clinically isolated syndrome (mean age, 32.9 years ± 7.7). Females in the relapsing group constituted 63.8%, which was lower than 80% of the progressive subtypes. Seven patients had secondary progressive MS, and three had primary progressive MS. Similar to the whole cohort, the PDDS performed well, showing a strong positive correlation between the EDSS and PDDS scores with a Spearman’s rho of 0.83 (P = .003). In addition, aPDDS correlated strongly with pyramidal scores (rho = 0.75, P = .013), ambulation scores (rho = 0.8, P = .004), and the need for walking assistance (rho = 0.78, P = .007). However, in patients with progressive MS, the correlation between the aPDDS and bladder or cerebellar scores was weak.

4. Discussion

In this study, we successfully translated the English version of the PDDS into Arabic and tested it on a cohort of Arabic-speaking pwMS. We adopted a similar methodological approach in other translation, resulting in broadly similar results, indicating excellent scale reliability across different languages. The cohort of patients in this study offers a good representation of a contemporary MS cohort in which females dominate, although our cohort had a more male representation (35%) than any other published validation cohort (20–33%).[7,14] The median age of this population is 34 years, similar to that of another Arabic cohort, the Turkish and Brazilian cohorts.[11,12,15] However, the English PDDS and Italian validation cohorts had median ages of 53.5 and 40 years, respectively.[7,13] The reasons behind differences in age and sex are likely reflective of patient selection approaches and the populations from which these subjects were selected. In any case, age and sex differences were minor and unlikely to affect the validation outcome.

Regarding physical disabilities, this study had a good representation of patients with mild and severe disabilities. The median EDSS score in this study (2.5) was higher than that in most other published validation studies, except for the North American cohort, where the median EDSS was 4.5.[7,13,15] Having a higher EDSS score compared to other studies is an advantage in ensuring the inclusion of patients with moderate and severe disabilities. Most of the patients in this cohort did not require walking support. In general, similar to most cohorts, most of the patients were not disabled. We suspect that the lower number of patients with disabilities was due to the younger age of the cohort, as disabilities associated with MS tend to accumulate over time. Another reason for the high prevalence of mildly affected patients in our study was that a large part of the study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. As patients were enrolled during routine clinic visits, and some were conducted via the telehealth system, more senior patients or patients with baseline disabilities likely chose telehealth rather than attending the clinic in person. This could have resulted in fewer patients with disabilities being enrolled in the study. The median disease duration of this cohort was 7.6 years which is within the range of most other cohorts (5.4 - 9 years), except for the Spanish cohort, which had a longer median disease duration.[7,14,15] Regarding MS subtypes, relapsing MS predominates, which is an observation seen in other PDDS validation studies, although our study, in addition to the English study, had the highest presentation of patients with progressive MS. Overall, we found no major differences in the demographic and disease characteristics of this cohort compared with other published cohorts. Nevertheless, we do not expect these differences to cause significant interference with the construct validation or interpretation of the results. More importantly, this cohort represents the population of patients with MS in Saudi Arabia regarding basic demographic features.[24]

This study confirmed the applicability of the PDDS in the Arabic-speaking pwMS as a good patient-reported measure of disability. Similar to other PDDS validation studies, there was a strong and statistically significant correlation between PDDS and EDDS scores, which was also demonstrated by logistic regression. Similar to the EDSS, the PDDS showed a significant correlation with pyramidal and cerebellar functions. This is not unexpected, as both functions are strongly linked to worsening gait, which is the leading influencer of the EDSS and PDDS. This is also the same finding that Learmonth et al[7] reported in their initial cohort. This is enforced by the strong correlation between the ambulation index and the PDDS and EDSS scores, indicating that mobility is perhaps the most substantial factor influencing PDDS. This finding was previously reported in a Brazilian validation study.[11] Therefore, it is not surprising that the increase in the PDDS was also strongly correlated with the use of walking aids in this study (rho = −0.6, P < .001).

Interestingly, in this cohort, we observed a strong correlation between bowel and bladder functional scores and both EDSS (rho = 0.507, P < .001) and PDDS (rho = 0.56, P < .001). This finding confirms that neurogenic bladder symptoms in pwMS are linked to disability and mobility impairment, as shown in previous studies.[25,26] This is probably because both sphincter disturbances and motor dysfunction in MS are commonly caused by the burden of spinal cord disease.[27]

Although the PDDS scores displayed an excellent correlation with the EDSS, it is essential to note that the PDDS and EDSS scores cannot be used interchangeably for the same subject. For example, a PDDS score of one does not necessarily translate into an EDDS score of one. This finding is important when examining patients with a low EDSS score. Therefore, care must be taken when interpreting the scores at the individual patient level. This should not affect the broader application of the PDDS in large cohorts. This variability could be due to the fact that some patients had higher EDDS scores due to abnormalities not captured well in the PDDS, such as cognition, bowel or bladder abnormalities, or vision. It is also possible that some patients with mild disabilities chose zero or one on the PDDS to avoid negative feelings regarding their disease status. This aspect should be explored further in future studies.

In this study, we observed a similar correlation between the PDDS and EDDS when applied to patients with progressive MS. However, it is worth noting that the number of patients with PMS was small, and care must be taken to draw major conclusions.

A limitation of this study was the relatively small sample size. However, this is not different from previously published PPDS validation studies. Another limitation was the small number of patients with advanced disease or high disability scores. The exclusion of patients with poor cognitive function might limit the application of the PDDS in patients with severely impaired cognition or poor education.

5. Conclusion

The Arabic version of the PPDS performed well in a group of Saudi patients with MS. The results of this study were highly consistent with and closely resembled those of previously published validation studies conducted in other languages.
