Positioning of advanced therapies in patients with moderate-to-severe Crohn’s disease
Authors: Tian Yu Qiu, Chin Kimg Tan, Jeannie Peng Lan Ong, Khoon Lin Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The treatment landscape for Crohn’s disease (CD) has evolved substantially in the 21st century with the advent of advanced therapies. Since the introduction of tumour necrosis factor (TNF) antagonists, new classes of advanced CD therapies have been approved for use in the Asia-Pacific region, including anti-integrins, interleukin (IL)-12/23p40 inhibitors, IL-23p19 inhibitors and Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi). In the last 10 years, biosimilars to infliximab, adalimumab and ustekinumab and generic JAKi have also become available. In this review, we discuss the indications for advanced therapies and whether certain classes of drugs are preferred for specific patient subsets. We also discuss the choice of first- and second-line agents for CD.

INTRODUCTION

Two major developments have shaped the management of Crohn’s disease (CD) over the past few decades. First, advanced therapies (biological agents and small molecules) were introduced for CD.[12] These therapies enabled a significant proportion of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) who had intestinal inflammation and ulcers despite treatment with steroids and optimal doses of immunomodulators to achieve endoscopic remission. The pivotal SONIC study demonstrated the superiority of infliximab (IFX) over thiopurines in CD.[3]

Beginning with the approval of IFX in 1998, the first 20 years of this era saw the approval of just four other advanced therapies: adalimumab (ADA), certolizumab, vedolizumab (VED) and ustekinumab (UST).[4] Since 2018, risankizumab (RZB), mirikizumab (MIRI), guselkumab (GUS) and upadacitinib (UPA) have been approved for CD in Singapore and/or neighbouring countries in the Asia-Pacific.[4] Among these drugs, certolizumab and MIRI have not been registered for use in Singapore. In addition, several drugs currently in phase 2/3 studies are likely to be approved for use in Singapore and the Asia-Pacific in the next 10 years. Despite these advances, a significant minority of patients with CD do not respond to a particular drug at all (primary non-response), lose response after a period of time (secondary loss of response) or develop intolerance to medication, highlighting the importance of a treat-to-target approach to guide timely treatment optimisation based on objective markers of disease activity.[5]

With the range of available advanced therapies and the arrival of affordable biosimilars (IFX, ADA, UST), there are few reasons to start monotherapy with thiopurine or methotrexate for the treatment of moderate-to-severe CD in Singapore. Thiopurines and methotrexate can have significant adverse effects, require more regular monitoring with laboratory tests and are generally less efficacious than advanced therapies. In low-to-middle income countries facing challenges with cold chain logistics, generic UPA may be easier to use for patients with moderate-to-severe CD compared with biological therapies. Current American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) guidelines also suggest that advanced therapies should be used upfront rather than after failure of steroids and/or immunomodulators.[6] With the exception of mild colonic CD, there is no indication for the use of oral mesalazine in moderate-to-severe CD.[7] Steroids, both intravenous and oral, should be used sparingly in CD.[8] Today, they are usually used to quickly bring troublesome symptoms under control when advanced therapy with a slow onset of action is selected.

The second major development is the recognition that there is poor correlation between symptoms and endoscopic disease activity in IBD,[9] and that the persistence of endoscopic lesions in the absence of clinical symptoms could still result in hospitalisation and/or structural damage requiring surgery in the long term.[10111213] The STRIDE[14] guidelines from the International Organisation for IBD first enumerated the treatment targets for IBD, which are to achieve clinical, laboratory and endoscopic remission in both ulcerative colitis and CD. The updated STRIDE II guidelines refined these targets by establishing timelines for achieving clinical, laboratory and endoscopic remission for each class of medication.[15]

This review examines what is known about the optimal use of advanced therapies for moderate-to-severe CD, including the selection of appropriate initial agents and sequencing of these drugs. We address who to treat, when to treat and the choice of treatment for different disease behaviours (inflammatory, stricturing and penetrating subtypes). In this review, we consider generic versions of UPA and biosimilars of IFX, ADA and UST as equivalent in efficacy to originator drugs.

TARGET POPULATION REQUIRING ADVANCED THERAPY

Advanced therapy is indicated for patients with moderate- to-severe CD. The severity of symptoms does not determine whether a patient has mild, moderate or severe CD, nor their need for advanced therapy. Instead, a patient with moderate-to-severe CD is one who is intolerant to or has inadequate or loss of response to conventional therapy; is steroid-dependent (defined as the inability to wean steroid below prednisolone 10 mg/day or budesonide 3 mg/day after 3 months of treatment, relapse within 3 months of stopping steroid, or requiring more than one course of steroid per year); and has poor prognostic factors and are at risk of disease progression.[26] Patients with poor prognoses include those with disease onset before the age of 17, those with stricturing or penetrating disease of the upper gastrointestinal tract or small bowel, and those with perianal disease. Poor prognostic factors predict a more aggressive disease[27] and a higher risk of requiring surgery.[28]

Because of the poor correlation between symptoms and intestinal disease activity, the absence of symptoms should not prevent a physician from offering treatment that is appropriate to the disease activity and severity to patients with moderate-to-severe CD.[9]

TIMING OF ADVANCED THERAPY INITIATION

Before the availability of biosimilars, patients are usually started on steroids and/or an immunomodulator when first diagnosed with CD, and only those who do not respond to these drugs are given advanced therapy, often 3–6 months later. This is the accelerated step-up strategy for managing IBD, which starts with conventional drugs (corticosteroids and/or immunomodulators) and escalates to advanced therapies only if the disease remains uncontrolled.

In contrast, a top-down approach introduces advanced therapies early, before patients have failed steroids and/or immunomodulators, with the aim of achieving rapid disease control and preventing long-term complications. The PROFILE study, a large multi-centre randomised controlled trial (RCT), demonstrated that patients with moderate-to-severe CD who were treated with IFX and an immunomodulator within 3 months of diagnosis had substantially better outcomes (steroid-free clinical remission and surgery-free remission) at 48 weeks compared with patients who were managed in an accelerated step-up fashion (79% vs. 15%), without increased risks of adverse events, including infections.[29] More than half of the patients achieved endoscopic remission in the top-down group (66% vs. 44%), which exceeded the usual therapeutic ceiling effect in most phase 3 clinical trials. This benefit continued to be observed beyond 48 weeks, with patients in the top-down group less likely to require surgery and, more notably, having lower risk of progression to a more aggressive disease phenotype (stricturing and penetrating).[30] This suggests the disease-modifying potential of early effective treatment of moderate-to-severe CD with advanced therapy.

Several studies (PROFILE,[29] step-up/top-down[31] and REACT-1[32]) demonstrated better long-term outcomes in patients who were treated in the top-down manner soon after diagnosis. The latest AGA guidelines suggest that advanced therapies should be started upfront rather than only after steroids and immunomodulators.[6] Despite the benefit of a top-down approach in patients with moderate-to-severe CD, there may still be a role for the step-up strategy in selected patients, particularly those with mild disease or significant contraindications to advanced therapies.[33]

Moderate-to-severe disease with inflammatory phenotype (advanced therapy naïve)

In this group of patients, high efficacy first-line treatment includes anti-tumour necrosis factor (TNF) agents, VED, UST, interleukin (IL)-23p19 inhibitors and UPA.[6]

Anti-TNF agents, in combination with immunomodulators, have often been used as first-line therapy because of their efficacy, rapid onset of action, and affordability with the availability of biosimilars.[6] They are useful as first-line agents for those with severe disease and high inflammatory burden, or those at high risk of disease progression.[34] Box 1 shows the factors associated with high risk of disease progression.[3536] Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) for IFX and ADA facilitates early optimisation in this group of patients.[37] Although uncommon, anti-TNF agents may have significant adverse effects. They should be used with caution in patients who are elderly or frail, or those with a history of latent tuberculosis (TB) or chronic hepatitis B infection (CHB). They have been associated with an increased risk of malignancy (e.g. lymphomas).[38] Hepatosplenic T cell lymphomas have been reported in patients on combination anti-TNF and azathioprine/mercaptopurine.[39] Patients may develop auto-antibodies and a lupus-like syndrome.[40] Dermatologic adverse effects include eczema and psoriasiform eruptions.[41] New onset heart failure[42] or demyelinating disease[43] have also been reported. Therefore, their use should be avoided in patients with current or recent malignancy, heart failure and demyelinating disease.

Vedolizumab is efficacious for CD, especially when used as a first-line agent early in the course of disease.[44] It has a longer time to clinical response compared with anti-TNF agents and can therefore be considered in patients without severe symptoms.[35] There is no head-to-head study comparing VED with other agents in bio-naïve patients with CD. Observational studies showed that VED is as effective as UST in inducing clinical remission with better endoscopic response,[45] and it is as effective in inducing clinical remission and has fewer adverse events compared with anti-TNF agents.[4647] There is no observed increased risk of latent TB reactivation or viral hepatitis B flares with VED.[48] Given its safety profile, it may be suitable for the elderly or frail and those with a history of latent TB or CHB.

Ustekinumab is as effective as anti-TNF agents in achieving clinical and endoscopic remission.[4950] The STARDUST study, REScUE study and real-world data showed that treatment optimisation, by reducing dosing interval or by intravenous UST re-induction, may help patients with inadequate response or secondary loss of response.[515253] Similar to VED, it has a favourable adverse effect profile with respect to risk of latent TB reactivation and CHB flare.[54]

The IL-23p19 antagonists are efficacious in advanced therapy-naïve patients with CD, with GUS being more efficacious than UST.[2122232455] In the VIVID study, MIRI was found to be as efficacious as UST.[22] Phase 3 RZB studies did not have an UST arm. Among the three drugs, GUS can be given either intravenously or subcutaneously, while RZB and MIRI are administered using intravenous induction.[21222355] Similar to UST and VED, IL-23p19 inhibitors have a favourable adverse effect profile and may be preferred in the elderly or frail, and those at risk of TB re-activation and hepatitis B flare. There is as yet no real-world data comparing the efficacy of IL-23p19 inhibitors in CD with anti-TNF agents, VED or UST in advanced therapy-naïve patients.

Upadacitinib is the only small molecule that has shown efficacy for CD.[50] Unlike many other phase 3 studies on advanced therapies in CD, patients in U-EXCEL and U-EXCEED who had enrolled in the studies while on steroids went through compulsory steroid taper at an early time point (week 4).[25] Similar to UPA in ulcerative colitis, patients with CD treated with UPA may see a rapid onset of response, including in the first week.[56] Patients on UPA are at an increased risk of infections, particularly herpes zoster, liver enzyme abnormalities, hyperlipidaemia and neutropenia.[57] While data on patients with rheumatoid arthritis on Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors suggest an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and venous thromboembolism (VTE) compared with those on anti-TNF agents,[58] real-world data on patients with IBD have not shown a similar increased risk of MACE or VTE in those on JAK inhibitors compared with anti-TNF agents.[5960] Upadacitinib is useful in patients who prefer oral therapy, and those with moderate-to-severe disease who need a rapid clinical response. However, given the increased risk of infection, it should be used with caution in the frail or elderly.

Moderate-to-severe disease with inflammatory phenotype (advanced therapy experienced)

After first-line anti-TNF agent

In patients who do not respond or lose response to IFX or ADA, reactive TDM is performed before moving on to the next drug.[5] If TDM shows the presence of antidrug antibodies with negligible drug levels, then switching to another anti-TNF agent remains an option.[61] However, these patients should be on a concomitant immunomodulator, as they tend to develop antidrug antibodies more rapidly with a second anti-TNF agent.[61]

If TDM reveals an adequate serum drug level (IFX >5 mg/mL; ADA >7.5 mg/mL), then it is thought that the predominant inflammatory pathway in these patients is not through TNF but may be through the IL-23/Th17 pathway.[62] The patient should then be switched out of class to UST, IL-23p19 inhibitor or UPA.

An open label head-to-head RCT comparing RZB and UST in patients who failed anti-TNF (SEQUENCE trial) showed the superiority of RZB in terms of clinical remission and endoscopic remission at week 48.[63] Similarly, in phase 3 GUS studies that included a comparator arm against UST (GALAXI 2 and 3), pre-specified subpopulation analyses showed the superiority of GUS over UST in both clinical and endoscopic remission.[21] Real-world data suggests that UST is more efficacious than VED in patients who have failed anti-TNF agents.[64] Upadacitinib is effective in patients who have failed anti-TNF agents in both phase 3 trial and real-world data.[2565]

After first-line UST

Patients who have failed UST as a first-line therapy can be switched to an anti-TNF agent, an IL-23p19 inhibitor or UPA. There is no data to support or refute the use of anti-TNF agents after failure of UST. All three IL-23p19 inhibitors are efficacious in patients who have failed UST.[66676869] Both phase 3 trial and real-world data showed that UPA is useful in those with failed UST.[25]

After first-line VED

Patients who have failed VED as a first-line therapy can be switched to an anti-TNF agent, IL-23p19 inhibitor, UST or UPA.

Fistulising perianal Crohn’s disease

Patients with fistulising perianal CD should first undergo surgery, if indicated, to control local perianal sepsis. Medical therapy is then initiated to control disease activity and allow for fistula healing.[70] Infliximab is often the first-line advanced therapy, with two RCTs establishing its efficacy.[7172] Subgroup analysis of the CHARM trial and a subsequent long-term extension study showed the efficacy of ADA in healing of perianal fistulas.[7374] Real-world data on UST and VED showed that they were efficacious in healing of perianal fistulas.[7576] Subgroup analysis in the U-EXCEL and U-EXCEED trials of patients with perianal fistulas who received UPA showed that it was efficacious in closing draining perianal fistulas.[77] This has been further supported by real-world data.[78]

Stricturing Crohn’s disease

Crohn’s disease strictures frequently comprise a combination of inflammatory and fibrotic components.[79] Advanced medical therapies are primarily used to target the inflammatory component, with the aim of reducing mucosal inflammation and alleviating obstructive symptoms.[80] Endoscopic dilatation or surgery should be considered in patients with symptomatic fibrotic strictures without an inflammatory component, as medical therapy alone is unlikely to be sufficient.[79] Given the current available data, we suggest using anti-TNF agents as the first-line advanced therapy. In patients with anti-TNF experience or those with contraindications to these agents, alternative options include UST, IL-23p19 inhibitors, UPA or VED.

Two prospective studies, STRIDENT[80] and CREOLE,[81] have demonstrated the clinical benefit of ADA in stricturing CD. Post hoc analyses of phase 3 data on RZB[82] and UPA[83] have shown numerically higher rates of improvement or resolution of stenoses in the treatment arms compared with placebo. Real-world data have also shown that UST is useful in stricturing CD.[84] There is less evidence for VED, although a single case series has reported that VED may be useful in stricturing CD in some patients.[85]

No currently available therapies have been shown to reverse established fibrotic strictures in IBD. Nonetheless, emerging mechanistic data suggest that the JAK-STAT (signal transducer and activator of transcription)[86] and IL-23[87] pathways play a role in stromal cell biology, providing a rationale for considering UPA, UST and IL-23p19 inhibitors as potential therapeutic options in stricturing CD.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

The Global Consensus Consortium has recommended that biologics are safe for use during pregnancy and breastfeeding.[88] While a small case series did not show obvious fetal harm in pregnant women on UPA during pregnancy,[89] the Global Consensus Consortium recommends against its use during conception and pregnancy if an alternative is available to maintain maternal health.

CONCLUSION

It is important to recognise that the severity of symptoms does not determine CD severity, and hence, a patient with minimal symptoms may still have moderate-to-severe CD and need advanced therapy. Advanced therapies should be started early in moderate-to-severe CD in a top-down fashion to offer the best chance of maintaining sustained remission, minimising irreversible bowel damage and the need for surgery. The positioning and sequencing of advanced therapies in CD should shift away from a one-size-fits-all, step-up approach to an individualised, mechanism-driven strategy that integrates disease factors (phenotype, inflammatory burden, previous biologic exposure) and patient factors (comorbidities, safety considerations, preferences).

This review does not cover the pharmacoeconomics of individual drugs. While the cost of originator drugs are usually similar, biosimilars and generic versions of advanced therapies cost a fraction of the price of originator products. In the backdrop of escalating healthcare costs, it is likely that the availability of biosimilars and generics will influence the positioning and sequencing of advanced therapies for CD.
