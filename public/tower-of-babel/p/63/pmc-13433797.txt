Discrimination stability and calibration of cardiovascular risk prediction models in the Framingham baseline cohort
Authors: Junbo Zhang, Tao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In primary prevention of cardiovascular disease, risk prediction models require not only adequate discrimination but also robust stability and reliable probability calibration to support clinical decision-making. Using data from 4434 participants in the Framingham baseline cohort, this study systematically compared the performance of three representative prediction models for cardiovascular risk assessment: an L2-regularized logistic regression model, a lightly tuned XGBoost model, and an AutoML-XGBoost model with automated hyperparameter optimization. Model performance was evaluated using repeated stratified data splitting, with discrimination assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration evaluated using the Brier score and calibration curves, stability examined across repeated splits, and consistency of risk ranking analyzed between models. The results showed that the three models achieved comparable levels of discrimination, exhibiting similar AUC distributions across repeated validations, with no clear advantage of more complex models over the traditional regression approach. The logistic regression model demonstrated the smallest performance variability across data splits and achieved the lowest Brier score, indicating the most stable and well-calibrated probability estimates. The AutoML-XGBoost model outperformed the lightly tuned XGBoost model in terms of discrimination and stability, but its calibration performance remained slightly inferior to that of logistic regression. Risk ranking consistency analysis further revealed a high Spearman rank correlation of approximately 0.95 between logistic regression and AutoML-XGBoost, with an overlap of about 71% in individuals classified within the highest-risk decile, whereas the correlation between logistic regression and the lightly tuned XGBoost model was approximately 0.83, with only about 50% overlap in the highest-risk group. These findings indicate that increasing model complexity does not necessarily lead to improved overall predictive performance in classical cardiovascular risk prediction tasks, and that multidimensional performance evaluation is essential for the appropriate selection and clinical application of prediction models.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains one of the leading causes of mortality and disability worldwide, imposing a sustained and profound burden on public health systems and socioeconomic development. Despite substantial advances in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment over recent decades, the overall burden of CVD remains high, underscoring the increasingly important role of risk prediction within disease prevention and control strategies1,2. In clinical practice, accurate risk prediction models not only facilitate the identification of high-risk individuals and the optimization of healthcare resource allocation, but also provide quantitative support for individualized intervention strategies and shared decision-making between clinicians and patients. Consequently, the development and rigorous evaluation of cardiovascular risk prediction models have long been central topics in epidemiological and clinical research.

Among numerous studies on cardiovascular risk prediction, the Framingham Heart Study occupies a foundational position. Since its initiation in the mid-20th century, this landmark cohort has systematically elucidated the relationships between multiple cardiovascular risk factors and disease occurrence through long-term follow-up, leading to the development of several classical multivariable risk prediction models3,4. Subsequently, the Framingham risk score and its updated versions have been widely adopted in clinical and public health practice, serving as essential tools for primary prevention of cardiovascular disease5. Owing to their transparent structure, explicit assumptions, and strong interpretability, these regression-based traditional statistical models have long played a dominant role in clinical guidelines and decision support systems2,6.

In recent years, advances in computational capacity and data science have facilitated the growing application of machine learning methods in cardiovascular risk prediction. Compared with traditional regression models, machine learning algorithms offer potential advantages in capturing nonlinear relationships and high-order interactions among predictors, particularly tree-based ensemble methods, which have demonstrated strong discriminative performance in various medical prediction tasks7,8. Several empirical studies have reported higher AUC values for machine learning models in predicting cardiovascular outcomes, thereby attracting considerable attention regarding their potential clinical utility9,10. However, alongside these promising findings, concerns have emerged regarding the methodological risks and clinical uncertainty introduced by increased model complexity.

Accumulating systematic evidence suggests that greater model complexity does not necessarily translate into meaningful improvements in predictive performance. A comprehensive systematic review by Christodoulou et al.11 demonstrated that, in most clinical prediction settings, machine learning models did not significantly outperform traditional logistic regression in terms of discrimination. Moreover, an increasing number of methodological studies have emphasized the limitations of relying solely on discrimination metrics for model evaluation, noting that complex models may exhibit inferior performance in probability calibration and result stability12,13. This issue is particularly critical in cardiovascular risk prediction, where clinical decisions often depend on absolute risk estimates rather than relative risk ranking alone.

From a theoretical perspective, predictive model performance is inherently multidimensional. Discrimination, commonly quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), reflects a model’s ability to distinguish between individuals who experience events and those who do not14. In addition, model stability—defined as the consistency of model performance across different data splits or repeated validation procedures—is essential for evaluating generalizability and real-world reliability, yet it remains insufficiently addressed in much of the existing literature15,16.

In response to these methodological challenges, several recent guidelines have proposed more stringent standards for the development and reporting of clinical prediction models. The TRIPOD statement and its recent TRIPOD + AI extension explicitly recommend comprehensive reporting of model development processes, validation strategies, and multidimensional performance metrics in studies involving machine learning methods, with the aim of improving transparency and reproducibility17,18. Meanwhile, the PROBAST tool provides a structured framework for identifying potential sources of bias and concerns regarding applicability in prediction model studies, further emphasizing the importance of appropriate validation and comprehensive performance evaluation19,20.

Against this background, the present study uses the Framingham baseline cohort to conduct a systematic and comprehensive comparison of cardiovascular risk prediction models. Unlike prior studies that primarily focus on a single discrimination metric, this study evaluates predictive models across three complementary dimensions: discrimination, stability, and calibration. By comparing the performance of traditional logistic regression models, gradient boosting–based tree models, and automated machine learning models under repeated stratified data splitting, this study aims to clarify the strengths and limitations of different modeling paradigms in cardiovascular risk prediction and to provide empirical methodological evidence to inform future model development, evaluation, and clinical application.

Theoretical background and literature review

Performance evaluation framework for cardiovascular risk prediction models

The primary objective of clinical prediction models is to provide reliable individual-level risk estimates; therefore, model performance should be evaluated from a multidimensional perspective rather than relying on a single metric. Steyerberg12 emphasized that predictive performance should be assessed across key dimensions such as discrimination, calibration, and clinical usefulness, as overreliance on a single indicator may obscure important limitations in model performance.

Discrimination is commonly assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), which quantifies a model’s ability to distinguish between individuals who experience events and those who do not14. As a threshold-independent metric, AUC is widely used in medical prediction studies; however, it reflects only ranking ability and does not capture the accuracy of predicted probabilities12.

Calibration refers to the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. The Brier score provides a global measure of probabilistic prediction error, while calibration curves offer a visual assessment of agreement across different risk strata13,21. Previous studies have highlighted calibration as a critical component of model evaluation, particularly in clinical contexts where decisions depend on absolute risk estimates13.

In addition, model stability is an important dimension for evaluating generalizability. It refers to the consistency of model performance across different data splits or validation procedures. Performance estimates based on a single random split may be sensitive to sampling variation, especially in settings with limited sample size or low event rates15,16. Therefore, assessing performance distributions through repeated splitting or cross-validation provides more robust evidence for model reliability.

Theoretical foundations of traditional statistical models in cardiovascular risk prediction

Traditional statistical models, most notably logistic regression, have long served as the primary methodological framework for cardiovascular risk prediction. Their key advantages lie in transparent model structure, clearly interpretable parameters, and the ability to directly quantify the relationship between risk factors and outcomes, thereby supporting strong interpretability22. In the development of the Framingham risk models and related studies, logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models have been widely used to construct multivariable risk prediction tools and have undergone extensive validation in clinical practice4,5.

From a methodological perspective, traditional regression models often demonstrate favorable calibration properties. This advantage partly arises from their parametric structure and direct optimization of likelihood-based objective functions, which facilitate closer alignment between predicted probabilities and observed event rates12. Moreover, under conditions of moderate sample size and a limited number of predictors, regression models tend to achieve a reasonable balance between bias and variance, resulting in relatively stable performance across different samples23.

Nevertheless, traditional models also exhibit inherent limitations. Their reliance on linearity assumptions and prespecified interaction forms may restrict their ability to capture complex nonlinear patterns in the data. As data volume and feature complexity increase, these constraints provide a theoretical rationale for the introduction of machine learning approaches.

Applications and controversies of machine learning models in cardiovascular risk prediction

In recent years, the application of machine learning methods in medical prediction research has expanded rapidly. Ensemble learning models, particularly gradient boosting trees, construct predictive functions by aggregating multiple weak learners and can automatically capture nonlinear relationships and complex interactions among variables, thereby offering enhanced theoretical modeling flexibility2,24. In the field of cardiovascular risk prediction, several studies have reported potential advantages of machine learning models in terms of discrimination metrics such as AUC, stimulating extensive discussion regarding their clinical value9,10.

However, an increasing body of systematic evidence has challenged the assumption that machine learning models are inherently superior to traditional approaches. Christodoulou,11, in a comprehensive systematic review of clinical prediction model studies, found that in most application settings, machine learning models did not demonstrate significantly better discriminative performance than logistic regression. At the same time, complex models may face greater challenges in probability calibration and result stability, particularly when sample sizes are limited or hyperparameter tuning is insufficient13.

Furthermore, increased model complexity may reduce interpretability and clinician trust. Rudin25 argued that in high-stakes decision-making contexts, the use of opaque “black-box” models should be approached with caution, and that preference should be given to transparent and interpretable prediction methods. This concern is particularly relevant in cardiovascular risk prediction, where model outputs may directly influence clinical decisions.

Theoretical background of automated machine learning and model optimization

To mitigate subjectivity in model selection and hyperparameter tuning, automated machine learning (AutoML) has attracted growing attention in recent years. AutoML aims to identify high-performing model configurations under given data conditions through systematic hyperparameter search and optimization26. Within this framework, Bayesian optimization–based approaches have been widely adopted for hyperparameter tuning in machine learning models.

Modern hyperparameter optimization platforms, such as Optuna, employ efficient search strategies that improve tuning efficiency while ensuring reproducibility, thereby providing technical support for the standardized application of machine learning models in medical prediction research27. Nevertheless, even within AutoML frameworks, performance improvements must be rigorously validated and assessed using multidimensional evaluation criteria, particularly in studies involving probability prediction and clinical decision support.

Summary of theoretical framework and research motivation

In summary, existing literature indicates that the evaluation of cardiovascular risk prediction models should be grounded in a multidimensional performance framework, as reliance on discrimination metrics alone is insufficient to fully capture clinical utility. Traditional statistical models possess inherent advantages in calibration and stability, while machine learning models offer potential benefits in modeling complex relationships, though performance gains are not guaranteed. Automated machine learning provides a promising technical pathway for model optimization but must be assessed within a rigorous and comprehensive evaluation framework.

Building upon these theoretical foundations and the current state of research, the present study conducts a systematic comparison of cardiovascular risk prediction models under different modeling paradigms using the Framingham baseline cohort. By evaluating model performance across discrimination, stability, and calibration, this study establishes a theoretical and methodological basis for subsequent empirical analysis and methodological refinement.

Data source and study population

The data used in this study were obtained from the publicly available Framingham Heart Study dataset. Initiated in the mid-20th century, the Framingham Heart Study has systematically recorded cardiovascular risk factors and outcome events through long-term follow-up, providing a foundational data resource for the development and validation of cardiovascular risk prediction models3–5. The dataset is publicly available through the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center (BioLINCC) and can be accessed at: https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/home/.

To avoid bias arising from repeated observations, this study focused on baseline observations, retaining only the first complete follow-up record for each participant. After data screening, a total of 4434 participants were included in the analysis. The outcome variable was ANYCHD, indicating the occurrence of coronary heart disease–related events during follow-up and defined as a binary variable. This approach is consistent with the principle of “one observation per individual” in clinical prediction modeling and helps ensure independence and interpretability of model estimates12.

In the Framingham baseline cohort, the outcome variable ANYCHD represents the occurrence of coronary heart disease events during follow-up. In line with the conventional Framingham risk prediction framework, this study implicitly considers a fixed prediction horizon corresponding approximately to a 10-year risk of coronary heart disease5.

Due to the structure of the publicly available dataset used in this study, detailed individual-level time-to-event and censoring information were not explicitly incorporated into the modeling process. Therefore, the outcome was analyzed as a binary endpoint indicating whether an event occurred during the follow-up period.

This simplification allows for a unified comparison across different modeling paradigms under a classification framework, which has been commonly adopted in methodological comparison studies between traditional statistical models and machine learning approaches11.

Ethical approval and informed consent: All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Framingham Heart Study obtained written informed consent from all participants at the time of data collection, and all study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI). The present study involved only secondary analysis of publicly available, fully de-identified data obtained through the NHLBI Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center (BioLINCC). According to BioLINCC policies, secondary analyses of de-identified datasets do not constitute human subjects research and do not require additional institutional review board approval.

Predictor variables and missing data handling

Based on the classical Framingham risk factor framework and consensus from existing literature on cardiovascular risk factors, fourteen baseline predictors were selected, covering demographic characteristics, behavioral factors, clinical measurements, and metabolic indicators. These variables have been widely used in previous Framingham-based risk prediction studies and clinical guidelines and possess clear clinical relevance4–6.

Missing values in selected variables were handled using median imputation. This approach preserves distributional robustness while reducing the influence of extreme values and is commonly recommended in prediction model research17. To prevent information leakage, all imputation procedures were performed exclusively within the training dataset, and the same imputation rules were subsequently applied to the corresponding test dataset, in accordance with the TRIPOD and TRIPOD + AI reporting standards17,18.

Predictor variables were selected based on established Framingham risk factors and prior clinical knowledge rather than data-driven variable selection techniques. No automated feature selection methods (such as LASSO or stepwise regression) were applied. This approach was adopted to ensure consistency across models and to maintain comparability with classical cardiovascular risk prediction frameworks.Details of the predictor variables and model inputs are fully described in the main text to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Model development

To systematically compare different modeling paradigms in cardiovascular risk prediction, three representative prediction models were constructed and evaluated: a traditional statistical model, a gradient boosting–based tree model, and an automated machine learning model.

Logistic regression model

Logistic regression is a classical method in clinical prediction research and is widely used for binary outcome modeling22. In this study, an L2-regularized logistic regression model was employed to mitigate potential overfitting in multivariable modeling. Continuous variables were standardized prior to model fitting to improve numerical stability of parameter estimation. This model served as the baseline model for comparison with subsequent machine learning approaches and offers strong interpretability and clinical transparency12.

XGBoost model

To represent a common machine learning modeling paradigm, this study employed the XGBoost model based on gradient boosting decision trees. XGBoost iteratively constructs and combines weak learners, enabling automatic capture of nonlinear relationships and higher-order interactions among predictors8,24. In this study, a lightly tuned strategy was adopted, whereby core hyperparameters were specified within reasonable ranges to establish a representative tree-based baseline rather than pursuing extreme performance optimization.

AutoML-XGBoost model

Building upon the XGBoost framework, an AutoML-XGBoost model was developed to evaluate the potential benefit of automated hyperparameter optimization in cardiovascular risk prediction26,27.

Hyperparameter optimization was conducted using a Bayesian optimization framework implemented via Optuna. The following key hyperparameters were tuned: learning rate (eta), maximum tree depth (max_depth), subsample ratio, column sampling ratio (colsample_bytree), minimum child weight (min_child_weight), gamma, L1 regularization term (reg_alpha), and L2 regularization term (reg_lambda).

The search space was defined as follows:

eta ∈ [0.01, 0.3],

max_depth ∈ [3, 10],

subsample ∈ [0.5, 1.0],

colsample_bytree ∈ [0.5, 1.0],

min_child_weight ∈ [1, 10],

gamma ∈ [0, 5],

reg_alpha ∈ [0, 1],

reg_lambda ∈ [0, 10].

A total of 60 optimization trials were performed, and model performance was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation within the training dataset during the optimization process. The optimization objective was to maximize the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

The final optimized hyperparameter configuration included:

max_depth = 4,

learning_rate = 0.0306,

subsample = 0.6547,

colsample_bytree = 0.9348,

min_child_weight = 5.26,

gamma = 3.25,

reg_alpha = 0.70,

reg_lambda = 0.59,

n_estimators = 403.

This model employed a Bayesian optimization–based automated hyperparameter search framework, selecting optimal parameter configurations via cross-validation within the training dataset26,27. To avoid information leakage, hyperparameter optimization was strictly confined to the training data, with the test dataset reserved exclusively for final performance evaluation.

Model validation and performance evaluation

Repeated stratified data splitting

To mitigate randomness associated with single data splits, repeated stratified data splitting was employed for model validation. A total of 20 repeated stratified splits were performed, with 70% of the data randomly allocated to the training set and 30% to the test set in each repetition, while preserving the outcome event rate15,16.

This approach was adopted to evaluate model performance variability across independent data partitions and to provide an intuitive assessment of stability under repeated sampling. Compared with single-split validation, repeated splitting allows estimation of performance distributions rather than point estimates.

Although more advanced internal validation strategies, such as repeated k-fold cross-validation or bootstrap resampling, may provide more efficient use of data and enable optimism-corrected performance estimation, the present study prioritizes transparency and comparability across different modeling paradigms under a consistent evaluation framework.

Future research may extend the current design by incorporating bootstrap-based optimism correction or nested cross-validation to further strengthen internal validation.

Discrimination assessment

Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), which reflects a model’s ability to distinguish between individuals with and without events across different thresholds and is among the most widely used discrimination metrics in medical prediction research14. In addition to reporting mean AUC values, the distribution of AUC across repeated splits was examined to characterize model stability12.

Calibration assessment

To evaluate the reliability of predicted probabilities, calibration was assessed at both global and local levels. Overall calibration was quantified using the Brier score, which summarizes probabilistic prediction error21. In addition, quantile-based calibration curves were constructed to visually examine discrepancies between predicted probabilities and observed event rates across risk strata13. This multifaceted calibration assessment provides a comprehensive evaluation of probabilistic prediction performance.

In addition to the Brier score and quantile-based calibration curves, more advanced calibration assessment methods, such as calibration intercept and slope, observed-to-expected (O/E) ratios, and flexible smoothing-based calibration curves (e.g., LOESS), are commonly recommended in the literature for comprehensive evaluation.

In the present study, calibration was primarily assessed using the Brier score and quantile-based calibration curves to ensure consistency across repeated data splits and comparability across modeling paradigms. These approaches provide both global and local perspectives on calibration performance.

Future studies may extend this framework by incorporating additional calibration metrics and uncertainty estimation, such as confidence intervals obtained via bootstrap resampling.

Risk ranking consistency analysis

Beyond discrimination and calibration, this study further examined the consistency of individual risk ranking across models. Bias control and reporting standards

Throughout study design and analysis, the TRIPOD and TRIPOD + AI reporting guidelines were followed to ensure transparent and reproducible reporting of data sources, predictor selection, model development, validation strategies, and performance metrics17,18. In addition, the PROBAST tool was used to identify potential sources of bias related to participant selection, predictor handling, outcome definition, and analytical methods, and corresponding measures were incorporated into the methodological design to mitigate these risks19,20.

All three models (logistic regression, XGBoost, and AutoML-XGBoost) were developed using the same set of predictor variables and the same study population. This design ensures a fair comparison across modeling approaches by isolating differences attributable to model structure rather than input features.

All analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.10). Logistic regression models were implemented using scikit-learn (version 1.3), and XGBoost models were implemented using the XGBoost library (version 1.7). Automated hyperparameter optimization was performed using Optuna (version 3.5). Data processing and visualization were conducted using pandas, NumPy, and matplotlib.

Net reclassification improvement (NRI) was not evaluated in the present study, as the primary focus was on discrimination, calibration, stability, and ranking consistency.

Probability calibration

Figure 3 shows the calibration curves of the three models, comparing the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event rates. Overall, the models exhibited clear differences in probability calibration.

The logistic regression model achieved the lowest Brier score (0.180), indicating the smallest overall probabilistic prediction error, followed by AutoML-XGBoost (0.185), whereas the lightly tuned XGBoost model produced a higher Brier score (0.195).

The quantitative calibration results based on the Brier score further supported these observations. The logistic regression model achieved the lowest Brier score, indicating the smallest overall probabilistic prediction error, followed by AutoML-XGBoost, whereas the lightly tuned XGBoost model produced a relatively higher Brier score, reflecting weaker calibration. Across repeated stratified splits, the Brier score was reported as mean ± standard deviation to reflect variability in probabilistic prediction performance. Lower Brier scores indicate better calibration performance, reflecting smaller discrepancies between predicted probabilities and observed event rates.

Discussion

Comparable discrimination but different performance implications

The results of this study indicate that the three models achieved broadly comparable levels of discrimination, with no clear advantage of more complex models over traditional logistic regression in terms of AUC. This finding is highly consistent with existing systematic evidence from clinical prediction research, which suggests that under conditions of moderate sample size and a limited number of predictors, machine learning models do not necessarily outperform traditional regression models in discrimination performance11,12. Importantly, discrimination metrics primarily reflect a model’s ability to rank individuals by risk rather than the accuracy of predicted probabilities, and therefore may be relatively insensitive to increases in model structural complexity12.

From a methodological perspective, the predictors included in the Framingham baseline cohort largely consist of classical cardiovascular risk factors, with a moderate number of variables and relatively strong signal-to-noise ratios. Under such conditions, logistic regression is already capable of effectively capturing the dominant risk structure, leaving limited room for complex models to leverage their enhanced flexibility. This suggests that when relationships between predictors and outcomes are stable and well characterized, increasing model complexity does not automatically translate into improved discriminative performance11.

Stability differences reveal the cost of model complexity

Although the AutoML-XGBoost model achieved slightly higher AUC values in some repetitions, its overall performance distribution was more dispersed than that of logistic regression, indicating increased sensitivity to data partitioning. The lightly tuned XGBoost model exhibited even more pronounced variability in stability, suggesting that tree-based models are more prone to performance inconsistency under changes in sample composition.

This phenomenon can be interpreted through the lens of the bias–variance trade-off, a fundamental concept in statistical learning theory29. Complex models typically exhibit lower structural bias but are more susceptible to fluctuations in the training data, resulting in higher variance. When sample size or event counts are insufficient to support increased model complexity, variance inflation can substantially undermine stability across repeated data splits15. In contrast, the relatively parsimonious parametric structure of logistic regression allows it to maintain more consistent performance under repeated resampling12.

Calibration differences and their clinical implications

Calibration analysis demonstrated that the logistic regression model achieved the best probability calibration both overall and across risk strata. While the AutoML-XGBoost model showed calibration performance close to that of logistic regression, deviations were still observed in certain risk intervals, whereas the lightly tuned XGBoost model exhibited comparatively weaker calibration. These findings further underscore that high discrimination does not necessarily imply reliable probability estimation13.

In clinical risk prediction settings, absolute risk estimates often directly inform intervention decisions, such as initiating pharmacological treatment or intensifying follow-up. Consequently, models with suboptimal calibration may introduce systematic bias in real-world applications, even if their risk ranking appears adequate. Traditional regression models, which directly optimize likelihood-based objectives, are inherently better suited to producing stable and interpretable probability estimates12, whereas tree-based and ensemble methods tend to prioritize classification error or ranking loss, potentially leading to shifts in probability outputs.

Although the present study focuses on Brier score and quantile-based calibration curves, it should be noted that additional calibration metrics, such as calibration slope, calibration intercept, and O/E ratio, may provide further insight into model performance13. The absence of these metrics represents a limitation in the current analysis.

However, the combination of Brier score and calibration curves already provides a robust and widely accepted assessment of both global and local calibration performance, particularly in comparative methodological studies12.

Risk ranking consistency and differences in high-risk identification

Risk ranking consistency analysis further highlighted clinically meaningful differences between models. The results showed that logistic regression and AutoML-XGBoost exhibited high agreement in overall risk ranking and high-risk group identification, whereas the lightly tuned XGBoost model demonstrated substantially lower consistency with the other two models. This indicates that even when models exhibit similar AUC values, they may differ considerably in identifying which individuals are classified as being at highest risk .

This finding has important practical implications. In primary prevention of cardiovascular disease, high-risk individuals are typically the focus of targeted interventions and resource allocation. Substantial discrepancies in high-risk identification across models may therefore directly influence clinical decision-making and intervention strategies. The present results suggest that increased model complexity may alter the structure of risk ranking, thereby introducing potential clinical uncertainty28.

The role and limits of automated machine learning

The AutoML-XGBoost model showed improved performance compared with the lightly tuned XGBoost model and achieved results broadly comparable to logistic regression across discrimination, stability, and calibration. This indicates that systematic hyperparameter optimization can reduce performance variability in complex models, although it does not guarantee consistent superiority over simpler approaches26,27.

These results highlight that automated machine learning should be regarded as a supportive methodological tool rather than a substitute for careful model selection. In clinical prediction settings, the choice of model should be guided by data characteristics, sample size, predictor structure, and intended use, rather than by algorithmic complexity alone11.

To ensure a fair comparison, all models were evaluated under controlled conditions using identical predictor sets and consistent data splits. Hyperparameter optimization in the AutoML framework was restricted to the training data to prevent information leakage, while the lightly tuned XGBoost model was deliberately configured using standard parameter settings to represent a non-optimized baseline.

The logistic regression model included in this study is not intended to replace established Framingham risk prediction tools. Instead, it serves as a reference model constructed under the same data and predictor conditions, enabling direct comparison with machine learning approaches.

Accordingly, the primary focus of this study is methodological evaluation rather than predictive model development. Emphasis is placed on comparing discrimination, calibration, and stability across modeling paradigms, rather than on improving existing clinical risk scores.

Methodological implications and contributions

The primary methodological contribution of this study lies in its emphasis on the multidimensional evaluation of prediction models. By jointly examining discrimination, stability, calibration, and risk ranking consistency, this study demonstrates that reliance on a single performance metric may obscure important differences between models. The results indicate that in classical cardiovascular risk prediction tasks, parsimonious traditional models retain distinct advantages across multiple key performance dimensions.

In addition, the use of repeated stratified data splitting reduced the influence of chance associated with single validation procedures and provided more robust evidence regarding model performance. This design enhances the credibility of the findings and offers a reusable methodological framework for future prediction research.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was based on a single cohort. Although the Framingham dataset is well established and authoritative, the generalizability of the findings requires further evaluation in independent populations. Second, the predictors were limited to traditional risk factors and did not include genetic information or high-dimensional biomarkers, which may restrict the potential advantages of complex models. Future studies incorporating multi-source and high-dimensional data may further clarify the contexts in which different modeling paradigms are most appropriate. Third, the outcome was analyzed as a binary endpoint without explicitly modeling time-to-event information or censoring. Although this approach facilitates comparison across different modeling paradigms, it may introduce bias due to the loss of temporal information and varying follow-up durations. Future research should extend the present framework to survival analysis methods, such as Cox proportional hazards models or machine learning-based survival models, to provide more precise estimation of time-dependent risk.

This study employed repeated stratified train–test splitting for internal validation rather than bootstrap resampling or repeated k-fold cross-validation. While this approach provides an intuitive assessment of performance variability across independent data partitions, it may not fully utilize the available data and does not yield optimism-corrected performance estimates. Future studies should consider more advanced resampling strategies to further strengthen internal validation.

The calibration assessment in this study was primarily based on the Brier score and quantile-based calibration curves. Although these methods capture key aspects of probabilistic prediction performance, additional metrics such as calibration intercept, calibration slope, and the observed-to-expected (O/E) ratio were not evaluated. Incorporating these complementary measures in future research would enable a more comprehensive assessment of model calibration.

Furthermore, confidence bands were not included in the calibration curves. While this design facilitates clearer visual comparison across models, it limits the ability to assess uncertainty in calibration estimates. Future work should incorporate confidence intervals, for example through bootstrap-based methods, to enhance the robustness and interpretability of calibration analysis.

Although ranking consistency was assessed, formal reclassification metrics such as net reclassification improvement (NRI) were not included in the present analysis. Incorporating such measures in future research may provide additional insights into the clinical impact of model differences.

Model transportability and external applicability

The transportability of the models should be interpreted with caution. The present study is based on the Framingham baseline cohort, which reflects a specific population structure and risk factor distribution. As a result, the performance of the models may not generalize directly to populations with different demographic, clinical, or environmental characteristics.

Although the methodological findings regarding model comparison are expected to be broadly informative, external validation in independent cohorts is necessary to assess the generalizability and clinical applicability of the models. Future studies should evaluate model performance across diverse populations and settings to further establish transportability.

To facilitate practical application and external validation, the modeling framework described in this study can be readily implemented using the reported predictor variables and model configurations. For example, given an individual with known risk factor values (e.g., age, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, and smoking status), predicted risk can be obtained by applying the trained model coefficients in the logistic regression model or by inputting the variables into the trained machine learning models.

Detailed model specifications, including predictor variables and parameter settings, are provided in the manuscript to support replication and application by other researchers. Future work may further enhance usability by developing user-friendly tools or interfaces for clinical implementation.

Conclusion

This study systematically compared the performance of logistic regression, XGBoost, and AutoML-XGBoost models for cardiovascular risk prediction using the Framingham baseline cohort.

The results showed that the three models achieved comparable discrimination, while logistic regression demonstrated superior stability and calibration performance. Increased model complexity did not lead to consistent improvements in overall predictive performance.

These findings highlight the importance of multidimensional evaluation in clinical prediction modeling and suggest that simpler, well-specified models may remain competitive in classical risk prediction settings.

These findings provide methodological insights that may support future development, validation, and practical implementation of cardiovascular risk prediction models.
