EphB4-decorated biomimetic nanoparticles enhance osteoclast targeting and therapeutic effect in osteoporosis
Authors: Anoop Puthiyoth Dayanandan, Woong Jin Cho, Gun Woo Lee, Yoshie Arai, Soo-Hong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe therapeutic efficacy of osteoporosis (OP) treatments is often limited by inadequate cellular precision and poor accumulation within the bone microenvironment. Although synthetic nanoparticles have been developed to address these challenges, they commonly face biological barriers such as rapid systemic clearance, inefficient transendothelial transport, and limited affinity for the complex bone niche. Here, we report on a biomimetic nanobiotechnology platform that integrates biological recognition with precision polymer engineering to overcome these limitations. We engineered a core–shell nanostructure consisting of a bilirubin-loaded Poly D L-Lactide-co-glycolide (PLGA) core cloaked with genetically modified osteoblast (OB)-derived membranes overexpressing the Ephrin type-B receptor 4 (EphB4) receptor. This biomimetic nanoparticle (NP) exploits the endogenous EphB4–EphrinB2 (EFNB2) signaling axis to achieve selective recognition and preferential uptake by EFNB2-expressing osteoclasts (OCs), displaying significantly higher internalization in OCs compared with mesenchymal stem cells (MSC), macrophages (Mφ), and OBs in vitro. Furthermore, the cell-membrane corona enables efficient transendothelial migration under inflammatory conditions, facilitating targeted delivery to OCs beyond the vascular endothelium. In vitro molecular analyses demonstrated that receptor-mediated NP uptake significantly suppressed key osteoclastogenic regulators, including nuclear factor of activated T-cells, cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1), cathepsin K, and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9). In a preclinical OP model, systemic administration resulted in bone-specific accumulation and robust restoration of trabecular microarchitecture and bone mineral density (BMD). Collectively, this work demonstrates that interfacial nanoengineering can translate complex receptor-guided biological interactions into stable, high-performance nanotherapeutics for the precision treatment of skeletal disorders.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04379-1.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Nanoparticle, Osteoclast, Bilirubin, EphB4

Introduction

Conventional (unencapsulated) drugs are often limited by poor targeting, systemic distribution to both diseased and healthy tissues, rapid clearance, low bioavailability, and inadequate aqueous solubility, all of which reduce therapeutic efficacy and necessitate frequent dosing [1–3]. Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems (NDDSs) address these limitations by enhancing drug stability and pharmacokinetics through controlled release and surface functionalization, enabling site-specific targeting while minimizing off-target effects [4–9]. Recent studies have emphasized the therapeutic potential of bioactive elements, such as zinc, magnesium, and silicon, in enhancing bone regeneration by modulating key biological processes like osteogenesis, angiogenesis, neuroregulation, and immune modulation within the bone microenvironment. ​ However, their application comes with challenges, including the necessity for precise release control and the risk of cytotoxicity at elevated concentrations [10]. Their tunable composition improves solubility and bioavailability and supports multifunctional integration of therapeutic and diagnostic capabilities within a single platform [11–17]. Recent advances in nanomedicine have highlighted the potential of microenvironment-responsive nanocarriers for targeted bone disease therapy, as these systems can respond to specific pathological cues such as pH, redox conditions, enzymatic activity, immune signals, and ionic fluctuations to achieve improved drug delivery and controlled therapeutic release [18]. Among these systems, biomimetic approaches using cell membrane nanovesicles (CMNVs) further enhance delivery performance by cloaking nanoparticles (NPs) with natural cell membranes, thereby improving biocompatibility, prolonging circulation, and facilitating biologically guided targeting [19–26]. Initially demonstrated with red blood cell and cancer cell membrane coatings and now extended to neurological, cardiovascular, and metabolic disorders, CMNV-based platforms exploit inherent membrane proteins and phospholipid bilayer structures to enable advanced surface engineering and efficient drug loading, positioning them as a powerful strategy for next-generation targeted therapeutics [27–30]. However, most current NP-based therapies for OP rely on nonspecific drug delivery or systemic inhibition of OC activity, which may disrupt physiological bone remodeling and cause off-target effects. In particular, receptor-guided nanocarrier systems capable of selectively targeting OC-associated signaling pathways within the bone microenvironment remain largely unexplored.

Osteoporosis (OP) is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by disruption of bone homeostasis, a process tightly regulated by the dynamic interplay between osteoblasts (OBs) and osteoclasts (OCs) [31]. Excessive activation of OCs accelerates bone resorption, leading to progressive bone loss and fragility fractures. Therefore, suppression of OC differentiation and activity remains a cornerstone of OP therapy [32].

Bone remodeling is governed by multiple receptor-ligand interactions mediating OB–OC crosstalk. While the Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor κB Ligand- Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor κB (RANKL-RANK) axis is essential for OC differentiation, its blockade, though effective, may be associated with immune-related side effects [33]. Other pathways, such as Semaphorin-3 A (Sema3A) and FasL–Fas, also influence OC activity; however, their broader biological functions and potential cytotoxic effects on non-target skeletal cells limit their therapeutic safety margins [34, 35]. In contrast, the Ephrin type-B receptor 4-EphrinB2 (EphB4-EFNB2) signaling axis provides a physiologically balanced regulatory mechanism. EphB4 is expressed on OBs, whereas EFNB2 is expressed on OCs. Their interaction initiates bidirectional signaling: EphB4 forward signaling promotes osteoblastogenesis through pathways such as rat sarcoma/mitogen-activated protein kinase (Ras/MAPK) and Phosphatidylinositol 3-Kinase (PI3K) / Protein Kinase B (PI3K/Akt), while EFNB2 reverse signaling suppresses RANKL-driven nuclear factor of activated T-cells, cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1) activation, thereby inhibiting OC differentiation and resorptive function [36–39]. This dual, contact-dependent mechanism simultaneously enhances bone formation and restrains bone resorption, preserving skeletal homeostasis without the cytotoxic or pro-resorptive liabilities observed in alternative targets [40–46]. To date, the EphB4-EFNB2 axis has not been translated into a functional receptor-mediated drug delivery strategy for OP therapy.

Oxidative stress is a critical driver of osteoclastogenesis, making antioxidant-based interventions a promising strategy for OP therapy. Compounds such as quercetin and gastrodin suppress OC differentiation by reducing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. Among endogenous antioxidants, bilirubin has recently gained attention for its potent ROS-scavenging and anti-lipid peroxidation properties. Beyond its classical association with heme metabolism, bilirubin has been shown to modulate osteoclastogenesis by attenuating ROS-dependent signaling pathways, thereby inhibiting RANKL-driven OC differentiation and bone resorption [47–57].

Given these properties, the EphB4-EFNB2 axis represents an attractive and potentially lower-risk strategy for OC-directed therapy. Unlike conventional anti-resorptive therapies that rely on systemic RANKL blockade or nonspecific antioxidant administration, our strategy enables receptor-guided, OC-selective targeting through physiological EphB4-EFNB2 interactions while simultaneously delivering sustained antioxidant therapy. This dual-function design combines biologically balanced signaling modulation with controlled drug release, offering improved cellular specificity and potentially reduced off-targeted effects compared with existing approaches. To exploit this pathway, we engineered OBs to overexpress EphB4, isolated their membranes, and incorporated them into a NDDS. This design enables selective engagement of EFNB2-expressing OCs while concurrently activating inhibitory signaling pathways, thereby achieving targeted suppression of OC differentiation and maturation.

The objective of this study was to develop a targeted drug delivery strategy for OP by exploiting the EphB4–EFNB2 signaling axis to selectively regulate OC activity. Bilirubin, a potent endogenous antioxidant known to inhibit osteoclastogenesis through ROS scavenging, was encapsulated in PLGA NPs to enable controlled and sustained delivery [58–60]. Although EphB4 signaling and biomimetic membrane coating technologies have each been explored in bone-related or nanomedicine studies, their combined application for OC-targeted antioxidant delivery has not been previously reported. In this work, we engineered EphB4-overexpressing OB-derived membrane-coated NPs, termed E4-Bil-PNPs, to selectively recognize EFNB2-expressing OCs. By integrating receptor-mediated targeting with antioxidant therapy in a single biomimetic nanoplatform, this system aims to enhance NP uptake by OCs and suppress osteoclastogenesis, thereby providing a potential strategy for improved OP treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, High Glucose (DMEM; SH40007.01, HyClone, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; SH30919.03, HyClone, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (SU30010, HyClone, USA). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Once cells reached 80–90% confluence, they were detached, counted, and reseeded into experimental plates to ensure uniform cell density across assays.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of bilirubin toward OCs was assessed using a live/dead viability assay. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 2000 cells per well in 96-well plates. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 50 ng/mL RANKL (CYT-320, Prospec, USA), 35 ng/mL M-CSF (CYT-439, Prospec, USA), and varying concentrations of bilirubin (B0460, TCI Chemicals, USA) at 1, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, or 50 µM. Cultures were maintained for 5 days. At the end of the incubation period, media were aspirated, and cells were washed with 1× PBS. A solution containing 2 µM Calcein AM (C3099, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 4 µM ethidium homodimer (E116, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 30–45 min at 37 °C. Cell images were then captured using a Cytation 3 imaging system (BioTek, USA) to quantify viability and cytotoxicity.

OC differentiation

To induce OC differentiation, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at 2000 cells per well in 96-well plates using DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing the same supplements but without additional factors. Osteoclastogenesis was then induced by supplementing the medium with 50 ng/mL RANKL and 35 ng/mL M-CSF. This induction regimen was maintained for 5 days, activating cellular signaling pathways critical for OC differentiation and maturation. After the induction period, cells were harvested for downstream analyses.

TRAP staining

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was performed after 5 days of OC induction (PMC-AK04F, Cosmo Bio, Japan). The culture medium was aspirated, and cells were washed with 100 µL PBS. Subsequently, 50 µL of 10% Formalin Neutral Buffer Solution (Daejung, Korea) was added for fixation at room temperature for 5 min. Wells were then washed three times with 250 µL deionized water. For staining, a vial of chromogenic substrate was dissolved in 5 mL tartrate-containing buffer, and 50 µL of this solution was added to each well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 20–60 min, with timing adjusted until TRAP activity was clearly visible. The reaction was terminated by washing with deionized water.

Fabrication of Bil-PNPs

Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA; 30–50% w/v) was dissolved in 1 mL dichloromethane (DCM; 270997, Sigma, USA), followed by the addition of 300 µL bilirubin (500 µM). The organic phase was vortexed and subjected to sonication (Sonics & Materials Inc., USA) at 40% amplitude for 40–60 s. The resulting emulsion was transferred to 50 mL of 0.5% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA; P0469, TCI Chemicals, USA) solution for stabilization and further sonicated. The emulsion was stirred overnight at room temperature to allow complete DCM evaporation. NPs were collected by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, washed 2–3 times with 0.2 μm-filtered deionized water to remove excess stabilizer and unencapsulated bilirubin, and freeze-dried (Ilshin Lab Co. Ltd., Korea) overnight to obtain dry powder formulations.

Encapsulation efficiency

To determine encapsulation efficiency, 1 mg of Bil-PNPs was dissolved in 1 mL DCM and incubated in a shaker incubator (Vision Scientific Co. Ltd., Korea) at 37 °C for 30 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min, and the resulting supernatant was analyzed using a bilirubin assay kit (MET-5010, Cell BioLabs Inc., USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; P2031, Biosesang, Korea) for 20 min at room temperature (RT), followed by three PBS washes. Permeabilization was performed using 0.5% Tween-20 (P1379, Sigma, USA) in PBS for 10–15 min at RT, followed by three washes with PBS. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA; AC1025-100-00, Biosesang, Korea) in PBS for 60 min at RT. Cells were incubated overnight at RT with anti-EFNB2 antibody (NAB-0720-Z4387, Creative Biolabs, USA; 1:250 in 3% BSA) with gentle shaking. After PBS washing, cells were incubated for 1 h at RT with Texas Red-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (T2767, Sigma, USA; 1:1000 in 3% BSA), followed by three PBS washes. Nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI (D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in PBS for 2 min, followed by two PBS washes. Fluorescence images were captured using a Cytation 3 imaging system (BioTek, USA).

Overexpression of EphB4 in Saos-2 cells

Saos-2 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates using α-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For transfection, 2.5 µg plasmid DNA encoding EphB4 (Origene, USA) was diluted in 125 µL α-MEM. In parallel, 2 µL Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was diluted in 125 µL α-MEM. After a 5-min incubation at RT, the diluted DNA was combined with the diluted Lipofectamine 2000 and incubated for 20 min to allow complex formation. Growth medium in each well was replaced with 2 mL fresh α-MEM, after which 500 µL of the DNA–Lipofectamine complex was added. Cells were incubated for 4–6 h at 37 °C, then the transfection medium was replaced with complete α-MEM. Transfected cells were cultured for an additional 48 h prior to evaluating transfection efficiency and EphB4 expression by Western blotting, qRT-PCR, or immunofluorescence.

Flow cytometry

Transfection efficiency in Saos-2 cells overexpressing EphB4-GFP was assessed by flow cytometry. Briefly, transfected cells were washed with 1 mL PBS, then treated with 0.5 mL trypsin (SV30031.01, HyClone, USA) at 37 °C for 2–3 min until detachment. Trypsinization was terminated with 1 mL complete α-MEM containing 10% FBS. Cells were collected into 15 mL conical tubes and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in 1 mL 2% FBS solution, passed through a 40 μm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension, and maintained on ice until analysis. Flow cytometry was performed on a BD Accuri C6 system.

Fabrication of E4-Bil-PNPs

Saos-2 cells overexpressing EphB4 were harvested by trypsinization and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 4 min. The pellet was resuspended in pre-cooled PBS and centrifuged again under identical conditions. The resulting pellet was then resuspended in 1 mL hypotonic buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2) containing protease inhibitors and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C on a rocker. Membranes were disrupted by homogenization, and the supernatant was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min. The final supernatant containing the isolated cell membrane was collected, and protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay. PLGA weight to membrane protein weight was maintained at a 2:1 ratio [61]. The mixture was sonicated for 3 min in an ultrasonic cleaner (Dai Han Scientific, Korea) and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting pellet, corresponding to E4-Bil-PNPs, was resuspended in PBS.

Characterization of Bil-PNPs

The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of Bil-PNPs were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS; Malvern Instruments, UK). Particle morphology and size distribution were further evaluated by energy-filtered transmission electron microscopy (EF-TEM; Carl Zeiss LIBRA 120).

qRT-PCR analysis in cell culture

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Gibco, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription mix according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed with Power SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (4368708, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All reactions were run in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. The primer sequences for the respective genes used in this study are described in Table 1.

Study of NP uptake

NP uptake was assessed using Rhodamine B-loaded PLGA NPs encapsulated within EphB4-overexpressing OB membranes (E4-RhB-PNPs). Differentiating OCs were incubated with E4-RhB-PNPs for 4 h, followed by two PBS washes. Cellular uptake was visualized using the Cytation 3 imaging system (BioTek, USA). Regarding the EFNB2 inhibition study, cells were pre-treated with anti-EFNB2 blocking antibody (10 ng) for 1 h prior to NP exposure. Untreated cells, representing endogenous EFNB2 expression without ligand inhibition, were used as the control condition.

Western blot analysis

Following culture, proteins were extracted from cells and separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (NCM; B4162-0115, Bio-Rad, USA) and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at RT to prevent nonspecific binding. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution), followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at RT. Protein bands were detected using ECL™ Select Western Blotting Detection Reagent (GERPN2235, Cytiva, USA) and visualized by chemiluminescence. The primary antibodies used included EFNB2 (NAB-0720-Z4387, Creative Biolabs, USA), EphB4 (AF446, R&D Systems, USA), NFATc1 (sc-7294, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), cathepsin K (sc-48353, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), integrin αvβ3 (SC56-07, Novus Biologicals, USA), c-fos (MA5-15055, Invitrogen, USA), DC-STAMP (MABF39-I, Millipore), and β-actin (GO43, Abcam, USA).

In vitro drug release

To assess bilirubin release, 1 mg each of Bil-PNPs and E4-Bil-PNPs was dispersed in 1 mL PBS. Samples were incubated under constant agitation (120 rpm) at 37 °C. Aliquots (200 µL) were collected at predefined time points-hourly during the first 6 h and then daily up to day 8-and replaced with fresh PBS to maintain sink conditions. The amount of released bilirubin was quantified using a bilirubin assay kit (MET-5010, Cell BioLabs Inc., USA), enabling precise temporal analysis of drug release profiles.

In vivo anti-osteoporosis effect of E4-Bil-PNPs

C57BL/6 mice (7 weeks old) were procured from Orient Bio. (Korea). Animals were housed individually in sterile, isolated environments under standard laboratory conditions, with ad libitum access to water and a standard diet. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with institutional and national guidelines and were approved by the Korean Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-2023-014-2). Animals were assigned to experimental groups at random, and the investigators evaluating outcomes were blinded to the group assignments to reduce bias.

(a) Biodistribution study of E4-RhB-PNPs

After a 2-week acclimatization period, 20 mice were randomized into four experimental groups: (1) Sham, (2) PBS, (3) RhB-PNPs, and (4) E4-RhB-PNPs. Bilateral ovariectomy was performed on 20 mice. Mice received retro-orbital injections of 50 µL PBS containing 1 mg/kg of the respective formulation, administered twice weekly for 4 weeks. At the conclusion of treatment, animals were euthanized, and femurs and tibias were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and processed for further analysis.

(b) Efficacy study of E4-Bil-PNPs

For the efficacy assessment, 20 mice were allocated into four experimental cohorts: (1) Sham, (2) PBS, (3) Bil-PNPs, and (4) E4-Bil-PNPs. Ovariectomy was performed as described above. Animals received retro-orbital injections of 50 µL PBS containing 1 mg/kg of the designated formulation, administered twice weekly for 4 weeks. At the end of the study, mice were euthanized, and femurs and tibias were collected from hind limbs and fixed in 4% PFA for subsequent analyses.

Microcomputer tomography (Micro-CT) scanning

In vivo bone architecture was evaluated by micro-CT using a Skyscan 1076 system (Skyscan, Belgium). Imaging parameters were set at a voxel size of 18 μm, 40 keV, and 250 µA, with an exposure time of 2356 ms and a frame averaging of 3. A 1 mm aluminum filter was applied, and scans were acquired at 0.4° rotational increments through 180°. Image reconstruction was performed with NRecon software (Skyscan) using the Feldkamp algorithm with beam-hardening correction. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated in trabecular and cortical compartments using CTAn software (Skyscan). Quantitative morphometric parameters were calculated, including BMD (g/cm3), bone volume fraction (BV/TV; %), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th; µm), trabecular number (Tb.N; /mm), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp; µm).

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

After micro-CT, femurs were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 5 μm thickness using a microtome (LEICA RM2245, Netherlands). Sections were deparaffinized in xylene (8587 − 4410, Duksan, Korea) and rehydrated through graded ethanol solutions. Slides were stained with hematoxylin (Gill No. 3, GHS316, Sigma, USA) for nuclear visualization, followed by differentiation in acid alcohol and rinsing in water. Counterstaining was performed with eosin Y solution (E4009, Sigma, USA) for 3 s. Sections were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted using Acryl Mount (StatLab, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue sections were prepared for immunohistochemical analysis using a mouse detection DAB kit (D02-110, Origene, USA). Briefly, sections were first incubated in a hot-air oven (WOO JOO Science, Korea) at 55 °C for 30 min to preserve tissue structure. Deparaffinization was carried out by sequential immersion in xylene, followed by rehydration through graded ethanol solutions. Antigen retrieval was performed by treating the sections with pepsin solution (Origene, USA) at 37 °C for 10 min. To minimize nonspecific binding, samples were incubated with a commercial pre-blocking solution for 10 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with the primary antibody of interest, diluted in antibody diluent, for 60 min in a humidified chamber at room temperature. After washing with PBS, slides were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-streptavidin–conjugated secondary antibody for 10 min. Visualization was achieved by applying DAB substrate solution (substrate + chromogen concentrate) for 5 min. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 min, rinsed under running tap water, and mounted using Acrymount Plus mounting medium. This standardized protocol enabled reliable immunohistochemical detection of antigens in tissue specimens.

Masson’s trichrome staining

Masson’s trichrome staining was performed using a Trichrome Stain Kit (ab150686, Abcam, USA). Tissue sections were deparaffinized by immersion in xylene for 10 min (three times), followed by rehydration through graded ethanol solutions (5 min each). After rehydration, slides were rinsed under running tap water for 3 min and immersed in Bouin’s solution at 60 °C for 1 h. Slides were cooled to room temperature and rinsed under running tap water until the yellow coloration was removed. Sections were then stained with hematoxylin for 10 min, followed by a 10 min rinse under tap water. Slides were subsequently immersed in Biebrich scarlet acid solution for 15 s, rinsed under tap water for 3 min, and treated with phosphomolybdic–phosphotungstic acid solution for destaining. Sections were counterstained with aniline blue for 15 min, treated with 1% glacial acetic acid for 2 min, and washed with distilled water. Finally, slides were dehydrated through graded ethanol and xylene and mounted with synthetic mounting medium.

Results

Bilirubin downregulates OC-associated genes

To evaluate the impact of bilirubin on OC differentiation, RAW 264.7 cells were cultured for five days in the presence of 50 ng/mL RANKL and 35 ng/mL M-CSF, two key factors required to induce osteoclastogenesis. Bilirubin was added at a range of concentrations (1 µM, 5 µM, 10 µM, 20 µM, 30 µM, 40 µM, and 50 µM) to assess its dose-dependent influence on cell viability and OC formation. A live/dead assay conducted on day 5 showed that 1 µM and 5 µM bilirubin were non-toxic, with cell viability comparable to untreated controls. In contrast, concentrations ≥ 10 µM exhibited significant cytotoxicity, progressively decreasing cell survival in a dose-dependent manner. Based on these results, 1 µM and 5 µM were selected for further analysis of their effects on osteoclastogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 1). To determine their impact on OC maturation, TRAP staining was performed. Whereas 1 µM bilirubin exerted minimal inhibitory effects, 5 µM bilirubin significantly reduced the number of multinucleated OCs compared with untreated RANKL-stimulated controls, indicating effective suppression of precursor cell fusion, a key event in OC development and function. At the molecular level, gene expression analyses of critical OC-related markers, including NFATc1, cathepsin K, DC-STAMP, and OC-STAMP, were conducted. These genes are central to differentiation, cell fusion, and bone resorptive activity [61]. Treatment with 5 µM bilirubin markedly reduced the expression of all four genes relative to both 1 µM bilirubin and untreated controls, demonstrating a more potent inhibitory effect on osteoclastogenesis (Fig. S2). Collectively, these findings suggest that bilirubin at an optimal concentration of 5 µM disrupts essential signaling pathways, including the NFATc1 axis, thereby impairing OC formation and activity.

Bilirubin downregulates OC differentiation compared with other antioxidants

Naturally occurring antioxidants, including UDCA, TUDCA, and ascorbic acid, were evaluated for their potential OC differentiation at a concentration of 5 µM, previously identified as effective for bilirubin [62–64]. To assess cytotoxicity, live/dead assays were performed, confirming that none of the antioxidants exhibited toxic effects at the selected concentration. OC differentiation was then assessed by TRAP staining, which specifically detects multinucleated OCs (Fig. S3). The results demonstrated that bilirubin significantly reduced OC formation, whereas none of the other antioxidants produced a comparable effect. On this basis, bilirubin was selected for subsequent studies.

EphB4 overexpression in Saos-2 (OB) cell membrane and characterization of E4-Bil-PNPs

Following transfection using Lipofectamine 2000, a transfection efficiency of 50.1% was achieved, as determined by flow cytometry (Fig. 1A, B). Gene expression analysis revealed a marked upregulation of EphB4, with a 373-fold increase compared to the control group (Fig. 1C). To corroborate these findings and determine the subcellular distribution of EphB4 protein, Western blot analysis was performed. Results confirmed that EphB4 expression was predominantly localized to the OB cell membrane, indicating successful targeting and membrane integration of the overexpressed protein (Fig. 1D, E).

Following successful overexpression of EphB4, Bil-PNPs were fabricated. The encapsulation efficiency of bilirubin within PLGA NPs was 50%, confirming effective drug incorporation (Fig. 1F) [65]. The average size of PLGA NPs was initially 212 nm, increasing to 246 nm upon bilirubin encapsulation, consistent with successful drug loading into the PLGA matrix (Fig. 1G). This size increase was accompanied by a shift in zeta potential from − 21.6 mV to − 24.4 mV, further supporting the incorporation of bilirubin into the NPs (Fig. 1H).

To generate E4-Bil-PNPs, Bil-PNPs were subsequently coated with cell membrane nanovesicles (CMNVs) derived from EphB4-overexpressing OBs. This modification resulted in an average size increase of 10 nm, indicative of successful membrane coating (Fig. 1G). Zeta potential analysis reinforced these findings: uncoated Bil-PNPs exhibited a zeta potential of − 24 mV, whereas CMNVs showed − 32 mV. After membrane encapsulation, the zeta potential of E4-Bil-PNPs shifted to − 29 mV, closely approximating that of CMNVs and confirming successful modification of the NPs surface (Fig. 1H) [66]. Further validation by energy-filtered transmission electron microscopy (EF-TEM) demonstrated the presence of EphB4 on the NP surface. Immunogold labeling with secondary gold-conjugated antibodies confirmed EphB4 incorporation and its expression on the surface of E4-Bil-PNPs (Fig. 1I) [67]. Drug release studies conducted over 192 h (8 days) revealed biphasic release kinetics for both Bil-PNPs and E4-Bil-PNPs. The percentage of bilirubin released over time was calculated relative to the total encapsulated amount (around 250 µM). Incomplete drug release is commonly observed in PLGA-based delivery systems because a fraction of the drug may remain entrapped within the polymer matrix due to diffusion limitations and polymer–drug interactions. In addition, drug molecules located in deeper regions of the polymer matrix may be released only during later stages of polymer degradation, which can extend over several weeks depending on polymer composition and molecular weight [68–71]. The release profile presented in this study reflects the release behavior of bilirubin from PLGA nanoparticles under experimental conditions designed to correspond to the biological evaluation window of the in vitro OC differentiation assay (5 days) [72]. An initial burst release, likely due to surface-adsorbed bilirubin, was followed by a sustained release phase driven by diffusion from the PLGA matrix. Importantly, E4-Bil-PNPs exhibited significantly delayed release compared with Bil-PNPs. This extended release is attributable to the cell membrane coating, which functions as an additional diffusion barrier, thereby slowing bilirubin release. Quantitative analysis confirmed that while both formulations displayed an initial burst, E4-Bil-PNPs maintained a substantially slower sustained release rate, demonstrating that membrane encapsulation effectively modulates release kinetics and enables prolonged drug delivery (Fig. 1J) [73, 74].

E4-Bil-PNPs exhibit enhanced internalization by OCs

For evaluating NP internalization, Rhodamine B, a red-fluorescent dye, was encapsulated within a PLGA matrix and subsequently cloaked with membranes derived from EphB4-overexpressing OBs, generating E4-RhB-PNPs. Differentiated RAW 264.7 cells were exposed to 5.89 × 10⁹ NPs, corresponding to 5 µM of bilirubin, for 4 h. The equivalence between NP number and 5 µM bilirubin was calculated based on the total encapsulated drug content per milligram of NPs. Specifically, 1 mg of Bil-PNPs corresponds to approximately 2.94 × 10¹¹ NPs and contains approximately 250 µM of encapsulated bilirubin, as determined from encapsulation efficiency measurements. Therefore, 5 µM represents 2% of the total encapsulated drug content, corresponding to approximately 5.89 × 10⁹ NPs. Based on the in vitro release profile, approximately 5.3% of the encapsulated bilirubin is released within 4 h. Thus, when NPs equivalent to 5 µM bilirubin were applied, the bilirubin concentration released during the 4-hour treatment period corresponds to approximately 0.265 µM. E4-RhB-PNPs demonstrated significantly enhanced uptake, with a 2.8-fold increase compared with bare RhB-PNPs (Fig. 2A, B). This pronounced internalization suggests that elevated EFNB2 expression on the OC surface promoted specific interactions with EphB4 on the NP membrane, thereby facilitating receptor-mediated binding and endocytosis. In addition, the incorporation of a biological membrane layer around the polymeric core likely provided complementary cell-recognition motifs, membrane proteins, and fusogenic elements that mimic natural cell–cell interactions, further augmenting NP uptake efficiency. Collectively, these findings highlight the advantage of functional membrane coating in improving the targeted delivery of PLGA-based NPs to OCs.

To confirm that E4-RhB-PNP internalization is mediated by EFNB2, its expression was quantified during OC differentiation from day 0 to day 5. EFNB2 expression peaked on day 5, with day 3 showing a slightly lower yet comparable level (Fig. 2C, D). Correspondingly, E4-RhB-PNP uptake was highest on day 5, followed by day 3 (Fig. 2E, F), indicating a strong correlation between EFNB2 expression and NP internalization. To further validate the involvement of EFNB2 in NP internalization, receptor-blocking experiments were conducted. Cells were pre-treated with an anti-EFNB2 blocking antibody (10 ng) for 1 h at 37 °C prior to NP exposure to inhibit EFNB2-mediated interactions at the cell surface. Cells that did not receive antibody pre-treatment served as the control group, representing endogenous EFNB2 expression without receptor inhibition. Following antibody incubation, E4-RhB-PNPs were added under the same conditions used for the uptake experiments, and cellular uptake was quantified based on fluorescence intensity. Pre-treatment with the EFNB2 antibody resulted in a marked reduction in NP uptake compared with the antibody-untreated control cells (Fig. 2G, H), suggesting that EFNB2 plays a role in mediating the internalization of E4-RhB-PNPs.

Specificity was further evaluated across multiple cell types present in bone marrow, including OBs, OCs, MSCs, and macrophages (Mφ), with OCs serving as the reference group. Among these, OCs exhibited the highest uptake of E4-RhB-PNPs, followed by Mφ. This observation is consistent with Western blot results confirming EFNB2 expression on the OC surface (Fig. 2I–K). Taken together, these results underscore the pivotal role of EFNB2 in enhancing E4-RhB-PNP uptake. Inhibition of EFNB2 activity significantly reduced the uptake advantage conferred by EphB4 functionalization, thereby affirming its central role in mediating targeted delivery [43, 75].

NP Translocation and uptake in an inflammatory microenvironment

OP is frequently associated with inflammatory conditions that exacerbate bone resorption and impair bone remodeling. To evaluate whether E4-RhB-PNPs could traverse the endothelial barrier and selectively target OCs, a series of Transwell assays were conducted. This model was designed to replicate in vivo bone microenvironment under both inflammatory and non-inflammatory conditions. RAW 264.7 cells were first differentiated into mature OCs using standard induction protocols. In parallel, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured in EGM-2 BulletKit medium to preserve physiological function and subsequently seeded onto ThinCert inserts to establish a confluent endothelial monolayer. Mature OCs were then cultured in the lower chamber to mimic the bone microenvironment. To reproduce OP-associated inflammation, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, 0.1 µg/ml) was added to the system for 1 h. This condition has been previously established in our laboratory to induce endothelial activation and junctional disruption, characterized by a patchy and discontinuous distribution of the adherens junction protein VE-cadherin, indicating compromised intercellular junction integrity under inflammatory conditions [67]. RhB-PNPs and E4-RhB-PNPs were introduced into the upper chamber under both inflammatory and non-inflammatory conditions (Fig. 2L). The results showed that E4-RhB-PNPs exhibited significantly greater transendothelial migration compared with RhB-PNPs, with this effect being most pronounced under TNF-α-induced inflammatory conditions. Fluorescence intensity measurements demonstrated markedly higher accumulation of E4-RhB-PNPs in OCs when inflammation was present. In contrast, unmodified RhB-PNPs displayed limited translocation and uptake, regardless of the inflammatory state (Fig. 2M, N). Notably, under non-inflammatory conditions (Fig. 2O, P), both NP formulations showed reduced penetration and uptake, underscoring the pivotal role of the inflammatory microenvironment in facilitating NP delivery. These findings highlight the critical contribution of EphB4 functionalization to the targeting efficiency of E4-RhB-PNPs. The enhanced uptake of E4-RhB-PNPs by OCs in an inflammatory setting supports the hypothesis that EphB4 coating promotes selective interaction with EFNB2-expressing target cells. Furthermore, the demonstrated ability of E4-RhB-PNPs to cross an endothelial barrier under OP-relevant inflammatory conditions underscores their translational potential as an effective in vivo delivery system.

In vitro cytotoxicity of E4-Bil-PNPs toward OCs

Following the successful synthesis and characterization of E4-Bil-NPs, cytotoxicity towards OCs was evaluated. RAW 264.7 cells were cultured for five days in the presence of 50 ng/mL RANKL and 35 ng/mL M-CSF to induce osteoclastogenesis, while being simultaneously exposed to E4-Bil-PNPs. At the end of the incubation period, cell viability was assessed using a live/dead assay. The results showed comparable viability between the E4-Bil-PNP–treated group (82.98%) and the control group (83.43%), indicating that E4-Bil-PNPs did not exert significant cytotoxicity (Fig. 3A, B). Based on the NP dose used in the in vitro experiments, 5.89 × 10⁹ NPs were administered per 1 mL of culture medium, corresponding to a bilirubin-equivalent dose of approximately 5 µM based on the total encapsulated drug content. Considering the release profile measured at day 5 under the in vitro experimental conditions, the maximal releasable bilirubin concentration under these conditions was estimated to be approximately 1.93 µM, which lies within the effective and non-cytotoxic range identified in the dose–response experiments.

E4-Bil-PNPs downregulate OC-associated genes in vitro

To further investigate the impact of E4-Bil-PNPs on osteoclastogenesis, both E4 nanovesicles (E4-NVs) and E4-Bil-PNP groups were administered at equivalent particle numbers (5.86 × 109), ensuring consistency in dosage between groups. Because EphB4 protein is known to inhibit OC maturation through signaling, E4-NVs were included as a comparator [76]. TRAP staining analysis of control, E4-NV, and E4-Bil-PNP groups revealed a marked reduction in the number of multinucleated OCs, with the lowest counts observed in the E4-Bil-PNP group, followed by the E4-NV group, while the control group exhibited the highest counts (Fig. 3C, D). This pattern highlights the synergistic effects of EphB4 signaling and bilirubin in suppressing OC fusion and differentiation.

Consistent with these findings, gene expression analysis demonstrated significant downregulation of osteoclastogenic markers, including NFATc1, MMP-9, and cathepsin K, in cells treated with E4-Bil-PNPs (Fig. 3E). Protein expression analysis corroborated these results, showing that treatment with E4-NVs in combination with bilirubin reduced the levels of NFATc1, cathepsin K, integrin αvβ3, c-fos, and DC-STAMP (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these data confirm that E4-Bil-PNPs effectively inhibit osteoclastogenesis at both the transcriptional and protein levels, supporting their therapeutic potential for OP.

Enhanced localization of E4-RhB-PNPs in bone in vivo

To evaluate the targeting efficiency of E4-RhB-PNPs, an ovariectomized (OVX) mouse model was employed to mimic OP. Four groups were analyzed: sham, OVX, RhB-PNPs, and E4-RhB-PNPs. Among these, E4-RhB-PNPs exhibited the highest degree of localization within bone tissue, confirming the role of EphB4 receptor functionalization in enhancing OC-specific targeting following ovariectomy. RhB-PNPs demonstrated greater initial localization than both the sham and OVX groups, likely due to the absence of cell-membrane encapsulation, which accelerates RhB release into circulation. This accelerated release is attributable to enzymatic degradation and other physiological factors in the blood, whereas membrane cloaking shields the NP carrier and its cargo from these influences [77–79]. These findings underscore the dual importance of receptor-mediated targeting and NP design in optimizing delivery efficiency (Fig. 4A, B). Specifically, the interaction between EphB4 and upregulated EFNB2 facilitates preferential accumulation in OCs, a critical feature in OP where OC activity is elevated. Moreover, the results emphasize that NP encapsulation strategies substantially influence biodistribution and release kinetics, thereby shaping therapeutic outcomes.

E4-Bil-PNPs inhibit bone resorption and restore OP-induced bone loss in vivo

The therapeutic efficacy of E4-Bil-PNPs in OP was further assessed by isolating femurs from OVX mice and performing micro-CT analyses following treatment. Parameters including BMD, bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) were evaluated [80]. Administration of E4-Bil-PNPs significantly increased BMD and BV/TV compared with controls, whereas no significant differences were observed in Tb.Th, Tb.N, or Tb.Sp (Fig. 4C, D). The enhanced efficacy of E4-Bil-PNPs is likely attributable to selective binding of the EphB4 receptor to its EFNB2 ligand on OCs, promoting rapid uptake and targeted inhibition of osteoclastogenesis. This suppression of OC activity may also have indirectly stimulated OB differentiation or activity, thereby contributing to bone formation. Taken together, these findings indicate that E4-Bil-PNPs not only inhibit bone resorption but also promote bone regeneration, highlighting their potential as a multifunctional therapeutic strategy for OP.

Histological evaluation

Histological evaluation revealed promising evidence of bone tissue restoration in OP-induced bone loss following treatment with E4-Bil-PNPs. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated a marked increase in bone volume and density in treated groups compared with untreated controls. The E4-Bil-PNP–treated mice displayed denser and more regularly organized trabeculae, suggesting that this intervention facilitated new bone matrix deposition and indirectly promoted activity of OBs, the primary driver of bone formation (Fig. 5A). To further assess bone regeneration, Masson’s trichrome staining was performed. As expected, the sham group exhibited normal bone architecture, characterized by well-formed trabeculae and abundant collagen deposition. In contrast, the OVX group, serving as the disease model, showed pronounced trabecular thinning, disrupted microarchitecture, and diminished collagen staining, consistent with osteoporotic pathology. The Bil-PNP group demonstrated moderate bone restoration compared with the OVX group, with partially improved trabecular organization and enhanced collagen deposition, indicating some regenerative potential. Notably, the E4-Bil-PNP group exhibited robust trabecular connectivity and extensive collagen deposition, reflecting markedly enhanced bone regeneration (Fig. 5B). These findings highlight the efficacy of EphB4-mediated NP targeting in modulating OC activity and fostering a pro-regenerative bone microenvironment. The results suggest that E4-Bil-PNPs effectively interact with the EphB4–EFNB2 signaling axis to regulate OC activity and stimulate bone repair. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of cathepsin K expression provided further insight into OC modulation across treatment groups. Cathepsin K, a protease critical for bone resorption, was highly expressed in the OVX group, reflecting pathological osteoclastic activity typical of OP. Bil-PNP treatment reduced cathepsin K expression, consistent with the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of bilirubin in mitigating osteoclastogenesis. Remarkably, the E4-Bil-PNP group demonstrated the most pronounced suppression of cathepsin K, with levels approaching those observed in the sham group (Fig. 5C, D). This superior outcome reflects the specificity of EphB4-mediated targeting, which ensures precise delivery to OCs while modulating EphB4–EFNB2 signaling, a pathway known to regulate OC differentiation and activity. Together, these findings establish E4-Bil-PNPs as a targeted dual-action therapeutic strategy for OP, capable of curbing excessive bone resorption while simultaneously creating a favorable environment for bone regeneration [81].

Discussion

The present study demonstrates the multifaceted effects of bilirubin and EphB4-functionalized NPs in regulating OC activity. These findings underscore their therapeutic potential by providing compelling evidence of efficient cellular uptake, high targeting specificity, robust efficacy in inflammatory microenvironments, suppression of osteoclastogenesis, and promotion of bone formation in vivo.

While a range of concentrations was evaluated, 5 µM was identified as the minimal functional threshold required to induce significant suppression of osteoclastogenesis. In the TRAP assay, 5 µM produced a marked reduction in multinucleated osteoclast formation compared with lower concentrations. Consistently, the expression of key osteoclastogenic markers, including NFATc1, cathepsin K, and DC-STAMP, was significantly downregulated at this concentration (Fig. S2). These findings indicate that 5 µM represents the minimum effective concentration required to disrupt the OC differentiation program. Moreover, when other antioxidants (UDCA, TUDCA, and ascorbic acid) were tested at 5 µM, none exhibited comparable inhibitory effects on osteoclastogenesis (Fig. S3). Bilirubin differs fundamentally from TUDCA, UDCA, and ascorbic acid in several mechanistically relevant aspects that directly impact osteoclastogenesis. Bilirubin is lipophilic and embeds within cellular and mitochondrial membranes, positioning it precisely at sites where RANKL-induced redox signaling and lipid peroxidation occur during OC differentiation [82–84]. In contrast, TUDCA, UDCA, and ascorbic acid are hydrophilic, limiting their ability to localize within membrane microdomains where early osteoclastogenic signaling complexes assemble [85–89]. This substantial difference indicates markedly greater intrinsic potency and physiological relevance for bilirubin. Importantly, bilirubin uniquely participates in the biliverdin-bilirubin redox cycle, allowing continuous intracellular regeneration and sustained antioxidant activity, an amplification mechanism absent in UDCA and TUDCA and only indirect in the case of ascorbic acid [90–94].

These combined properties provide a mechanistic explanation for why bilirubin more effectively downregulates osteoclastogenesis. OC differentiation depends heavily on ROS-sensitive signaling pathways, including NF-κB and MAPK activation upstream of NFATc1 [95]. By embedding within membranes, protecting mitochondria, and undergoing redox recycling, bilirubin can suppress ROS at their primary source and more efficiently regulate redox-sensitive signaling pathways compared with other tested antioxidants.

Taken together, these findings establish bilirubin at 5 µM as a potent inhibitor of multiple stages of osteoclastogenesis-including differentiation, precursor fusion, and resorptive activity-and highlight its promise for conditions characterized by excessive OC activity, such as OP and other bone-destructive disorders. Consistent with these preclinical findings, epidemiological evidence has reported a positive association between circulating bilirubin levels and BMD. The study by Wu et al. was based on a cross-sectional analysis and therefore demonstrates association rather than causality. Although several covariates were adjusted for, potential confounding factors, including nutritional status, physical activity, metabolic health, and liver function, may influence both serum bilirubin levels and bone density. Thus, while these clinical findings suggest a potential link between bilirubin and skeletal health, they do not establish a direct mechanistic role. In this context, our study provides experimental evidence supporting this hypothesis by demonstrating that controlled delivery of bilirubin through EphB4-functionalized NPs directly suppresses OC differentiation and resorptive activity, leading to improved BMD and trabecular architecture in an ovariectomized mouse model [96].

Saos-2 cells were selected as the membrane source because they are a well-established OB-like cell model widely used in bone biology and biomaterials research. These cells exhibit several osteoblastic characteristics, including high alkaline phosphatase activity, expression of osteogenic markers such as osteocalcin and collagen type I, and the ability to support mineralization, making them a commonly used in vitro model for studying OB-related cellular interactions and bone-targeting biomaterials [97, 98]. In addition, the EphB4 receptor is highly conserved between humans and mouse, particularly within the extracellular domain responsible for ligand recognition. Previous studies have reported that the amino acid sequence of EphB4 exhibits approximately 89% identity and 94% similarity between human and mouse in the extracellular region, indicating strong structural conservation of the receptor-ligand binding interface. Therefore, despite the use of human Saos-2–derived membranes for NP fabrication and murine systems in the in vitro and in vivo experiments, the conserved nature of this signaling pathway supports the feasibility of cross-species EphB4–EFNB2 binding and the biological relevance of our experimental design [99, 100]. Furthermore, cell membrane–coated NPs are generally reported to exhibit lower immunogenicity compared with conventional synthetic nanomaterials, such as polymeric NPs, lipid NPs, or inorganic nanomaterials, because the presence of native membrane proteins and lipid compositions enables them to mimic natural biological membranes and reduce immune recognition. This property further supports the suitability of using Saos-2–derived membranes in the murine experimental system.

The successful overexpression of EphB4 on Saos-2 OB membranes, followed by their incorporation onto PLGA NPs, yielded E4-Bil-PNPs with enhanced surface-targeting capabilities (Fig. 1A–E). The observed changes in particle size and zeta potential after encapsulation confirmed effective membrane coating and functionalization, highlighting the feasibility of this bioengineering strategy (Fig. 1H) [101], as further supported by TEM imaging (Fig. 2I). The biphasic release kinetics observed in both Bil-PNPs and E4-Bil-PNPs (Fig. 1J) are characteristic of PLGA-based nanocarriers, consisting of an initial burst release from surface-associated drug followed by a sustained phase driven by diffusion and polymer degradation [73, 74]. The NPs were subjected to three consecutive centrifugation and washing cycles to remove non-encapsulated and loosely surface-adsorbed bilirubin prior to lyophilization. Repeated washing via centrifugation is a standard purification approach in PLGA NP fabrication and is widely reported to effectively eliminate weakly associated surface drug while retaining matrix-entrapped cargo [102, 103]. Nevertheless, even rigorously purified PLGA NPs commonly exhibit an initial burst release phase attributed to drug associated with or localized near the particle surface. Importantly, centrifugation removes freely dispersed and loosely adsorbed drug; however, drug molecules enriched within the superficial polymer shell during emulsification and solvent evaporation cannot be removed without disrupting NP integrity [104, 105]. Such release behavior, characterized by an initial surface-associated diffusion phase followed by sustained bulk diffusion and polymer degradation, is a well-documented and intrinsic property of PLGA-based delivery systems rather than an indication of incomplete purification [106, 107]. Incorporation of a cell-membrane coating modulated this profile by serving as an additional diffusion barrier, thereby prolonging systemic circulation and improving control over drug delivery at target sites. In addition, membrane cloaking enhances biocompatibility and immune evasion, further strengthening the therapeutic potential of hydrophobic agents delivered via PLGA carriers [108, 109]. From a therapeutic perspective, this controlled release profile is highly advantageous in OP, a chronic condition characterized by oxidative stress and sustained OC overactivity. ROS are established mediators of osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption, and continuous bilirubin availability may ensure prolonged suppression of these pathways while avoiding systemic toxicity associated with transient high concentrations of free bilirubin [95, 110]. Thus, the release profile not only confirms the stabilizing function of the cell-membrane shell but also supports the rationale for E4-Bil-PNPs as a long-acting, OC-targeted antioxidant delivery system suitable for chronic management of OP.

The EphB4-mediated targeting mechanism directly addresses one of the primary limitations of nanodrug delivery systems: achieving precise and efficient delivery to intended cellular targets. By integrating selective targeting with controlled release, E4-Bil-PNPs provide a strategy to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. As shown in Fig. 2, E4-RhB-PNPs demonstrated selective and efficient internalization by OCs, offering mechanistic evidence of EphB4-EFNB2–mediated targeting. Unlike bare NPs, which rely primarily on passive uptake, E4-RhB-PNPs exploit ligand–receptor interactions to achieve superior specificity [111]. This aligns with growing evidence that biomimetic nanocarriers enhance targeting by recapitulating natural cell–cell communication [112]. Furthermore, the correlation between ephrinB2 expression during OC differentiation (Fig. 2C, D) and NP uptake (Fig. 2E, F) underscores the importance of temporally regulated receptor expression in optimizing delivery efficiency. Inhibition studies confirmed that EFNB2 (Fig. 2G, H) is indispensable for E4-RhB-PNP internalization, demonstrating that uptake is actively mediated by EphB4-ephrinB2 signaling rather than nonspecific membrane interactions. Together, these results provide a mechanistic basis for employing ligand-functionalized nanocarriers to selectively modulate OCs in bone-remodeling disorders. These findings suggest that the enhanced cellular uptake of EphB4 membrane-coated NPs is associated with EphB4-EFNB2 receptor–ligand interactions. The significantly higher internalization observed for EphB4-coated NPs, together with the reduction in uptake following EFNB2 antibody blocking, supports the involvement of this signaling axis in mediating NP-cell interactions. Collectively, these results provide functional evidence for EphB4–EFNB2–associated targeting, contributing to the improved cellular interaction and uptake observed in this system. The ability of E4-RhB-PNPs to cross endothelial barriers under inflammatory conditions further strengthens their translational relevance. In OP, inflammation exacerbates bone resorption and increases vascular permeability, and our data show that E4-RhB-PNPs exploit these changes to enhance delivery efficiency (Fig. 2L–N) [113, 114]. This suggests that inflammatory cues may synergize with receptor-mediated targeting to improve therapeutic accumulation, consistent with prior reports in other pathological microenvironments where disease-related vascular and tissue alterations facilitated NP delivery [115–117].

E4-Bil-PNPs effectively suppressed OC differentiation without impairing cell viability in vitro (Fig. 3A, B). Live/dead assays confirmed that this inhibitory effect on osteoclastogenesis was not attributable to cytotoxicity, consistent with prior evidence that bilirubin exerts protective antioxidant effects at physiological concentrations [118]. This observation is particularly important given that conventional anti-resorptive agents, such as BPs, can induce apoptosis of OC precursors and lead to long-term skeletal complications, including atypical femoral fractures [119]. Thus, our findings suggest that E4-Bil-PNPs provide a non-cytotoxic alternative for restraining OC activity. TRAP staining (Fig. 3C, D) demonstrated a marked reduction in multinucleated OC formation after E4-Bil-PNP treatment, an effect more pronounced than with E4-NVs alone. This highlights a potential synergistic mechanism in which EphB4-mediated signaling and bilirubin delivery converge to attenuate OC maturation. EphB4 is known to antagonize ephrinB2 forward signaling in OC precursors, thereby restricting their differentiation [42]. Our data extends this concept by showing that NP-mediated bilirubin delivery enhances this inhibitory pathway, suggesting dual suppression at both the signaling and redox levels. Gene expression analyses (Fig. 3E) revealed downregulation of NFATc1, cathepsin K, and MMP-9 following E4-Bil-PNP treatment. NFATc1, the master transcription factor downstream of RANKL–RANK signaling, is indispensable for terminal OC differentiation [120]. Its suppression indicates interference with canonical NF-κB/AP-1-dependent transcription. In parallel, cathepsin K-a protease essential for bone matrix degradation and MMP-9, a key mediator of cartilage and extracellular matrix remodeling, were also reduced, suggesting that these NPs impair not only differentiation but also the resorptive potential of OCs [121]. At the protein level, Western blotting confirmed consistent downregulation of NFATc1, c-fos, cathepsin K, integrin αvβ3, and DC-STAMP (Fig. 3F). Reduced c-fos expression implicates disruption of the AP-1 complex, which cooperates with NFATc1 in driving osteoclastogenesis [122]. Decreased integrin αvβ3 levels suggest impaired cytoskeletal polarization and adhesion, processes essential for sealing-zone formation and effective bone resorption [123]. Moreover, DC-STAMP suppression indicates impaired precursor fusion, providing a mechanistic explanation for the reduced multinucleated OC population [124]. Although sustained bilirubin release was observed up to 8 days, the OC differentiation experiments were conducted within a 5-day RANKL induction period, which represents the established window for OC maturation in vitro. PLGA degradation occurs through hydrolysis of ester bonds, yielding lactic acid and glycolic acid, which are subsequently metabolized through physiological pathways. Under typical NP concentrations used in cell culture, significant extracellular pH reduction is generally limited due to buffering capacity of culture media. Previous studies have demonstrated that PLGA-based NPs exhibit minimal cytotoxicity and negligible pH-mediated effects during short-term in vitro exposure. Nonetheless, prolonged accumulation of degradation products in vivo could potentially influence local microenvironmental acidity, which may affect OC activity. Future in vivo studies will be necessary to further evaluate the long-term microenvironmental impact of sustained PLGA degradation [125–127]. Taken together, these results establish E4-Bil-PNPs as multi-target inhibitors that act at multiple stages of osteoclastogenesis-including differentiation, precursor fusion, and resorptive function-thereby offering a comprehensive therapeutic strategy for modulating OC activity.

The biodistribution data (Fig. 4A, B) demonstrate that E4-Bil-PNPs accumulate in the femur and tibia to a substantially greater extent than RhB-PNPs, indicating that bone tropism is primarily mediated by EphB4-ephrinB2 interactions in conjunction with the pharmacokinetic benefits of membrane cloaking. Mechanistically, ovariectomy elevates osteoclastogenesis and surface ephrinB2 expression; thus, forward binding of EphB4 on the OB-membrane shell likely facilitates preferential docking of NPs onto OCs. This concept is consistent with the canonical bidirectional EphB4-ephrinB2 signaling axis, which regulates bone remodeling and suppresses OC differentiation upon Eph engagement [128, 129]. Concurrently, the biomimetic membrane cloak is expected to prolong circulation and reduce clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, thereby increasing the likelihood of NPs accumulation in bone niches. Such cell-membrane coatings are well documented to enhance immune evasion and extend half-life compared with uncoated PLGA formulations [109, 130]. In addition, the ovariectomized microenvironment contributes inflammatory cues-such as TNF-α–induced adherens-junction remodeling-that transiently increase endothelial permeability, thereby promoting transendothelial passage into bone marrow and facilitating enhanced NP access to OCs. This mechanistic framework is consistent with the stronger femur and tibia signal observed under disease conditions [131, 132]. Finally, formulation parameters further explain group differences: rapid drug leakage from uncoated RhB-PNPs favors nonspecific distribution, whereas EphB4-functionalized membrane shells combine sustained drug release with receptor-mediated docking on OCs. This targeting paradigm differs from conventional bone-affinity approaches (e.g., BP conjugation or αvβ3-directed carriers) yet achieves the same therapeutic goal-high skeletal residence with minimized off-target exposure-while adding disease-state responsiveness via ephrinB2 upregulation [133, 134].

The therapeutic impact of this targeting strategy was confirmed by micro-CT analyses (Fig. 4C, D). E4-Bil-PNP treatment significantly increased BMD and bone volume fraction (BV/TV) in the OVX mouse model, indicating a rapid reduction in bone resorption. However, trabecular structural parameters-including trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) showed only positive but nonsignificant trends, suggesting that architectural restoration may require longer remodeling cycles. This may reflect the relatively short treatment duration used in the present study, and from a translational perspective, sustained or extended treatment regimens may be necessary to achieve more pronounced improvements in trabecular microarchitecture. This pattern parallels findings from studies of Odanacatib (ODN), a selective cathepsin K inhibitor, in OVX rhesus monkeys. ODN significantly improved lumbar spine BMD and reduced bone-turnover markers (e.g., urinary N-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen and serum C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen) by inhibiting collagen degradation during OC-mediated bone resorption, while preserving the capacity for continued bone remodeling. Similarly, the observed increases in BMD and BV/TV following E4-Bil-PNP treatment suggest that these NPs act primarily through suppression of OC activity, potentially via oxidative-stress modulation and EphB4–ephrinB2 signaling, in a manner analogous to ODN [135].

Histological analysis corroborated these findings. H&E staining (Fig. 5A) revealed well-preserved trabecular morphology with reduced scalloping and erosion, while Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 5B) demonstrated denser collagenous matrix deposition. These features are consistent with suppressed osteoclastic degradation in the absence of impaired osteoblastic activity, a pattern characteristic of cathepsin K inhibition and certain antioxidant-based interventions [136]. In parallel, cathepsin K immunohistochemistry (Fig. 5C, D) showed marked reduction in osteoclastic protease expression, directly reflecting diminished resorptive activity and echoing observations from both genetic and pharmacological models [137].

The selection of 5 µM is also supported by in vivo considerations. According to IACUC guidelines, the total blood volume of a mouse is approximately 77–80 mL/kg. Based on the administered NP dose (1 mg/kg) and this estimated blood volume, the systemic bilirubin-equivalent concentration is calculated to be approximately ~ 3 µM in circulation. Moreover, because E4-Bil-PNPs employ EphB4–EFNB2–mediated targeting, NPs preferentially accumulate in bone tissues, as demonstrated by the biodistribution results showing enhanced localization in the femur and tibia. This targeting effect is expected to further enrich the local bilirubin concentration within the OC microenvironment, supporting the physiological relevance of the 5 µM in vitro model.

Emerging evidence indicates that bilirubin and its derivatives possess immunomodulatory properties that may be relevant to bone remodeling and OC activity [138]. In the context of bone immunity, multiple immune cell populations, including lymphocyte subsets such as T cells, have been reported to influence OC differentiation and bone resorption through cytokine-mediated signaling pathways [139, 140]. Therefore, although the primary mechanism demonstrated in this study involves direct suppression of osteoclastogenesis through EphB4-EFNB2 targeting and bilirubin-mediated antioxidant effects, potential interactions with the broader osteoimmune microenvironment cannot be completely excluded. Future studies will be required to further investigate how E4-Bil-PNPs may influence immune–bone interactions within the bone microenvironment.

In summary, E4-Bil-PNPs appear to promote bone regeneration by rebalancing the bone-remodeling microenvironment. The EphB4 coating on their OB-derived membrane likely engages ephrinB2 on OCs, activating reverse signaling that suppresses the RANKL-NFATc1 cascade and downregulates OC fusion and resorption genes such as cathepsin K and DC-STAMP, thereby reducing bone resorption. Concurrently, intracellular release of bilirubin likely scavenges ROS, attenuating MAPK and NF-κB activation and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β. These combined effects further suppress OC activity while alleviating oxidative stress in the bone niche. Moreover, by limiting excessive matrix degradation, TGF-β release, and acidosis-features characteristic of OP, E4-Bil-PNPs may also help restore conditions that favor MSC homing and osteogenic differentiation rather than adipogenic drift. Together, these mechanisms suggest that E4-Bil-PNPs simultaneously inhibit bone resorption and stimulate productive OB/MSC activity, thereby supporting net bone formation. While these results are promising, additional studies are required to establish the clinical potential of E4-Bil-PNPs. Future investigations should optimize dosage, evaluate long-term safety, and assess therapeutic efficacy in larger animal models or postmenopausal OP conditions. Overall, E4-Bil-PNPs represent a significant advancement in targeted OP therapy, with their dual ability to suppress OC activity and preserve biocompatibility, providing a strong foundation for future clinical translation.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed bilirubin-loaded PLGA NPs functionalized with EphB4-overexpressing OB membranes (E4-Bil-PNPs) as a targeted therapeutic platform for OP. The NPs demonstrated efficient uptake by OCs through EphB4-ephrinB2 interactions, sustained bilirubin release, and preferential localization to bone tissue. Functionally, E4-Bil-PNPs inhibited OC differentiation and activity, reduced bone resorption, and promoted restoration of bone mineral density and trabecular architecture in an ovariectomized mouse model. Collectively, these findings highlight the dual action of E4-Bil-PNPs in suppressing osteoclastogenesis while fostering a bone-regenerative microenvironment. Taken together, our results position E4-Bil-PNPs as a promising and biocompatible strategy for targeted OP therapy, warranting further evaluation in long-term and clinically relevant models.
