RLCFormer: Automatic roadside LiDAR-Camera calibration framework with transformer
Authors: Rui Tian, Xuefeng Bao, Yunli Chen, Feng Liu, Yiqiang Zhen, Yong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
LiDAR-Camera fusion is pivotal for perceiving and understanding complex traffic environments, particularly valuable in autonomous driving and traffic monitoring. Traditional calibration algorithms, primarily designed for onboard sensors, are inadequate for roadside setups where sensors are positioned higher and more dispersed. To address this challenge, we introduce the RLCFormer, a Transformer-based network specifically tailored for precise calibration of roadside sensors. This method innovatively integrates depth and RGB features, utilizing correlation layers and a Transformer decoder to accurately match features across modalities. Evaluated on the DAIR-V2X-I Roadside 3D detection dataset, the RLCFormer achieves an average translation error of 3.3187 cm and a rotation error of 0.0469°, surpassing existing methods. Our approach significantly enhances scene representation and calibration precision, offering a robust solution for roadside sensor calibration and advancing the state of the art in sensor fusion technology.

Introduction

As high-precision and low-latency roadside sensors, LiDAR and cameras have become integral components of modern roadside monitoring and intelligent transportation systems, owing to their ability to capture complex traffic information in real time [[1], [2], [3]]. LiDAR technology is renowned for its ability to provide accurate distance measurements, which are essential for tasks such as obstacle detection, vehicle navigation, and route planning within these systems. On the other hand, cameras are highly effective in recognizing visual features, including road signs, traffic signals, and road boundaries [4,5]. When compared to traditional monitoring approaches that rely on single-modality data, the fusion of multimodal data from roadside units offers a more comprehensive and detailed understanding of traffic conditions. This enhanced perception capability leads to significant advancements in traffic monitoring, accident detection, and overall traffic management.

In the realm of intelligent transportation systems, prevalent fusion techniques include data-level and feature-level fusion [6,7]. Data-level fusion involves the amalgamation of raw data from both LiDAR and cameras at the outset of data collection, which preserves the integrity and authenticity of the original data more effectively than feature-level fusion. This method does not depend on the computational capabilities of edge devices to conduct preliminary feature extraction, which can lead to reduced hardware costs—an advantage particularly beneficial for large-scale urban monitoring applications. However, data-level fusion is also known for a notable limitation. Its accuracy depends heavily on the precision with which sensors are aligned. Thus, in the deployment of new large-scale traffic monitoring systems that integrate both LiDAR and camera technologies, the calibration of these sensors becomes paramount [8].

Despite the extensive research on LiDAR and camera calibration, much of which has been focused on autonomous vehicles, the application of these techniques to roadside traffic monitoring presents unique challenges that render calibration more complex than in vehicle-mounted scenarios [[9], [10], [11]]. Roadside sensors, typically positioned at elevated vantage points, must contend with a broader range of environmental variables and more intricate traffic dynamics. Unlike vehicle-mounted sensors that operate within relatively controlled environments, roadside sensors are subjected to extreme angles and elevations, resulting in significant perspective distortions that complicate the accurate detection of target features. Moreover, while some calibration algorithms perform adequately under the predictable and uniform conditions of vehicle-mounted systems, their effectiveness diminishes in roadside settings. This is primarily due to the absence of standardized, geometrically consistent objects in roadside imagery, compounded by the varied and overlapping traffic patterns, diverse pedestrian activities, and the presence of numerous static and dynamic objects, all of which interfere with the algorithms' feature extraction processes, leading to compromised calibration accuracy. Additionally, these algorithms often fail to adequately address environmental challenges such as adverse weather conditions—rain, fog, snow—and vibrations from heavy traffic, which frequently disrupt sensor alignment and degrade measurement accuracy. Consequently, these limitations can result in significant inaccuracies in data fusion, adversely impacting traffic safety and operational efficiency. This highlights the urgent need for specially designed calibration techniques capable of addressing the distinct operational demands and environmental conditions encountered in roadside monitoring, thereby improving the reliability and effectiveness of traffic management systems.

In general, the objective of LiDAR and camera calibration is to determine the transformation matrix between the radar and camera coordinate systems. This matrix is determined by both the internal parameters of the LiDAR and camera sensors—such as the LiDAR's angular resolution, range accuracy, the camera's focal length, and the coordinates of its principal point—and external parameters, including the 6 Degrees of Freedom (6-DoF) transformation. For large-scale urban monitoring, where standardized monitoring cameras and LiDAR are often purchased in bulk, internal parameters can typically be obtained directly from the manufacturer. However, the external parameters of these sensors frequently lack precise measurements and detailed installation records during setup. Moreover, because cameras and LiDAR are usually positioned far from ground-level targets, minor inaccuracies in external parameters can result in significant errors in ground target localization.

Traditionally, the calibration of LiDAR and cameras has relied on artificial markers, such as checkerboards and specialized calibration boards. While these methods are known for their high precision, they are both costly and labor-intensive. As shown in Fig. 1(a–c), traditional calibration boards are impractical for roadside scenarios, where sensors are often mounted at steep angles, requiring high-quality calibration patterns. Moreover, it is unfeasible to expect vehicles to move as needed for such calibration processes [12,13]. In environments where Pan-Tilt-Zoom (PTZ) cameras are used, or where external factors such as wind, rain, snow, and vibrations—or internal component changes—can alter camera parameters, manual calibration methods are insufficient as they lack the ability to automatically adjust to these variations. Therefore, calibration methods for LiDAR and cameras in roadside monitoring must adopt fully automatic, targetless online calibration techniques to ensure both accuracy and adaptability.Fig. 1Roadside scenario. Existing LiDAR-camera calibration techniques, optimized for autonomous driving, often fail to address the complex dynamics and environmental variability typical of roadside settings. Our proposed method addresses these limitations by adapting to the unique challenges of roadside environments. This innovative approach represents a significant advancement over existing methodologies by integrating sophisticated feature extraction techniques tailored to the specific demands of traffic monitoring [14].

For the transition of LiDAR-camera calibration tasks from autonomous driving applications to traffic monitoring domains, the key lies in extracting features that enable multi-modal target matching under altered field-of-view conditions. In traffic monitoring scenarios, where targets are often positioned at greater distances from sensors, precise depth information extraction becomes more critical than in autonomous driving. Additionally, the number of targets within the field of view of traffic monitoring sensors is greater than in autonomous driving, necessitating the use of associative features among multiple targets as key indicators for feature association between multi-modal sensors. Thus, the focus of transitioning calibration tasks lies in accurately capturing target depth features and leveraging global associative features among multiple targets, both of which significantly differ from those in autonomous driving.

To address these challenges, we propose a novel calibration method, RLCFormer, designed for the automatic calibration of extrinsic parameters between roadside LiDAR and cameras. In the feature extraction phase, our method integrates depth information with RGB features and iteratively refines the depth map through recursive updates. Additionally, a convolutional network aggregates features across multiple scales to enhance depth estimation, providing a detailed representation of spatial features. In the feature matching phase, we utilize correlation layers and a Transformer decoder to effectively process and match point cloud and image features, taking into account the inherent correlations between them. Finally, the transformation parameters—translation and rotation—are regressed separately by a feedforward network. Related works

Extensive research has been conducted in the area of LiDAR-camera calibration, with this paper focusing on joint calibration techniques specifically for roadside monitoring applications. As previously established, it is assumed that the intrinsic parameters of the camera are known, which mirrors the standard practices of bulk purchasing for roadside monitoring sensors. Consequently, our emphasis is primarily on the calibration of extrinsic parameters within the LiDAR-camera setup. Depending on operational requirements and environmental factors, the calibration of these extrinsic parameters can be categorized into two methodologies: offline and online calibration.

Offline calibration

Techniques for offline calibration generally depend on specific calibration targets or instruments, like checkerboards, spherical, and V-shaped targets. These are chosen for their unique characteristics that sensors can accurately detect, aiding in the matching of features. The process typically involves identifying features through both LiDAR and cameras, followed by the application of nonlinear optimization methods to refine the calibration parameters, ensuring precise calibration between different sensors. Zhang and Pless [15] initially proposed a target-based technique for extrinsic calibration utilizing a checkerboard. The technique involves the calculation of the positions of grid corners in camera images relative to the checkerboard in order to derive the external parameters between LiDAR and the camera, thus enabling the determination of the relative positions of both devices with regard to each other and to the image. This procedure is approached as a nonlinear optimization problem. Similarly, Geiger and his team [9] employed a parallel method for calibrating cameras with 3D LiDAR systems. Beyond checkerboards, other calibration targets such as trihedrals or spherical objects are used. For example, Beltrán et al. [16] utilized a rectangular plate featuring four ArUco tags [17], analyzing and aligning the centers of these tags to establish the sensor transformations.

Although these methods offer high accuracy, they not only increase the calibration costs but also limit the flexibility of application. Additionally, they typically require manual feature selection and matching, which not only consumes a significant amount of calibration time but also increases the likelihood of human error. More importantly, offline calibration cannot adapt to dynamically changing environments and fails to automatically correct parameter deviations that arise due to environmental changes [18]. Due to these limitations in targets, such methods cannot be universally applied.

Online calibration

Compared to offline calibration methods, online calibration techniques do not require specific calibration targets, thereby enhancing the flexibility and practicality of the calibration process. Existing target-less online calibration technologies can be broadly categorized into four main groups: information-theoretic methods, motion-based methods, feature-based methods, and learning-based methods [19]. The focus of this study is on feature-based and learning-based methods.

The feature-based approaches extract and match features directly from point clouds and images, which avoids optimizing statistical similarity. These features are typically divided into geometric, semantic, and motion-based categories, each derived in real-time from the LiDAR and camera captures of the environment. Geometric features originate from structural elements of the environment like points or lines. Semantic features provide higher-level interpretations, capturing elements such as skylines, vehicles, and utility poles that embody the semantic context of the scene. Conversely, motion features detail the dynamics of moving objects, including their orientation, speed, and trajectory.

The process of feature-based calibration methods typically encompasses three principal stages: feature extraction, feature matching, and transformation estimation. The goal of the extraction phase is to identify robust and unique features from point clouds and images, which represent specific geometric or semantic information about the environment. During the matching phase, these extracted features from both modalities are paired, utilizing various descriptors and considering the spatial interrelations among features. Lastly, the estimation phase employs these matched pairs to calculate the transformation, or extrinsic parameters, necessary for LiDAR-camera calibration. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is frequently employed in this final step to achieve precise alignment. Additionally, many approaches amalgamate the feature matching and transformation estimation phases [20,21], simultaneously determining the transformation relationships as they identify feature matches.

These feature-based methods come with certain limitations. Firstly, their effectiveness heavily relies on the quantity and quality of features that can be extracted from the environment. In scenarios where features are sparse or difficult to distinguish, such as in open natural landscapes or night-time settings, these methods may struggle to achieve effective calibration. Additionally, feature matching processes are prone to mismatches, especially in areas where feature points are repetitive or highly similar, which can lead to inaccurate calibration results. The accuracy of feature extraction and matching also depends significantly on the choice of algorithms and parameter settings used. Improper selections can reduce the precision and robustness of the calibration. These challenges underscore the need for careful algorithm optimization and parameter tuning to enhance the performance of feature-based calibration methods under various environmental conditions.

Calibration methods based on machine learning can be divided into end-to-end and multi-stage strategies. End-to-end approaches, in particular, have demonstrated significant promise in LiDAR and camera calibration by processing the raw data from these sensors directly to produce calibration parameters. These methods are advantageous because they automatically learn to identify features from the data, thus obviating the need for manually designed feature extraction steps. For instance, RegNet [22] utilizes a Network in Network (NIN) architecture to process and synchronize features from LiDAR depth maps and RGB images automatically, integrating feature extraction, matching, and parameter regression. This method excels in environments with consistent and predictable features but may struggle in roadside scenarios where variable lighting, occlusions, and complex backgrounds can degrade the quality of automated feature detection. In a similar vein, CalibNet [23] integrates a 3D spatial transformation layer that optimizes its end-to-end training, striving to enhance both the geometric and photometric alignments between the point clouds and images, thus facilitating more accurate calibration. While effective in controlled environments, its performance can diminish in roadside settings due to the unpredictable presence of non-static elements and the broader range of sensor angles and distances. Additionally, RGGNet [24] employs a deep generative model to formulate a tolerance-aware loss function that targets calibration inaccuracies and their permissible variations. Although innovative, the model's dependency on generative accuracy makes it less reliable in dynamic roadside conditions where environmental factors fluctuate extensively. Furthermore, CalibRCNN [25] introduced long and short-term memory modules into the convolutional neural networks to extract temporal features between point clouds and images, and exploited geometric and photometric differences to improve the calibration accuracy. This method is potentially more adaptable to roadside scenarios. However, it requires substantial computational resources and may not perform optimally in real-time applications due to processing delays. CalibDNN [26] and CFNet [27] both focus on precise estimation of extrinsic parameters, with the former utilizing transformation losses and the latter establishing detailed 2D-3D correspondences. These methods exemplify the significant strides made in applying deep learning techniques for reliable and precise sensor calibration. While these methods provide high precision in parameter estimation, their effectiveness is often contingent on the availability of high-quality, dense environmental data, which can be a limiting factor in less structured roadside environments.

However, end-to-end methods also have their drawbacks, primarily the excessive reliance on the quality and quantity of training data. If the training data are not representative or sufficient in volume, the model's generalizability and effectiveness in practical applications can be limited. Additionally, the demand for large volumes of training data also implies significant computational costs, which can be a substantial challenge, especially for smaller organizations or projects with limited resources. These factors necessitate careful consideration of the feasibility and scalability of end-to-end approaches in real-world settings.

Multi-stage methods combine neural networks with traditional algorithms, using networks to learn specific features and then employing non-learning methods to associate these features and compute extrinsic parameters. These methods typically focus more on the extraction of geometric and semantic features. For instance, SOIC [28] utilizes semantic segmentation on both imagery and LiDAR data to determine semantic centroids in 2D and 3D, thereby reframing the calibration challenge into a Perspective-n-Point (PnP) problem. This method minimizes reliance on initial calibration data. However, while effective in controlled settings, the method's performance can be compromised in roadside scenarios where semantic segmentation might struggle with transient lighting conditions and occlusions common in urban landscapes. Meanwhile, Zhu and his team [20] concentrate on improving calibration robustness, especially in scenarios involving low-resolution LiDAR and high noise levels, by synchronizing semantic features across both images and point clouds. While this method offers improved robustness against environmental noise, its effectiveness is somewhat dependent on the consistency and clarity of semantic features, which can vary drastically in complex roadside settings.

These multi-stage approaches significantly enhance calibration robustness by combining the adaptive strengths of deep learning with the precision of traditional geometric methods. They achieve a crucial balance between the flexibility offered by learning-based techniques and the reliability of established calibration strategies. However, their dependence on high-quality semantic and geometric data can be problematic in roadside environments, where such data may be scarce or compromised by external factors like weather conditions and dynamic obstacles. Additionally, the high computational demands of these methods, particularly in terms of data processing and feature synchronization, can hinder their real-time applicability in traffic monitoring systems, where speed and efficiency are critical.

In this study, we introduce a novel depth estimation technique aimed at enhancing the correlation between multi-modal data. By leveraging the Transformer framework, our approach effectively aligns these features, enabling seamless end-to-end online calibration. This method mitigates the issue of error accumulation commonly observed in sequential processing stages while simultaneously improving the model's interpretability. By integrating depth estimation with the Transformer's ability to manage complex data relationships, our methodology not only increases calibration precision but also offers clearer insights into the interactions between different data modalities and their contributions to the calibration process.

Roadside LiDAR-Camera calibration framework

Characteristic analysis of roadside monitoring sensor calibration

Depth maps are especially effective as a unified representation for online LiDAR-camera calibration, in contrast to edge or semantic features. This is because LiDAR point clouds inherently provide precise depth information through direct measurements. Additionally, depth estimation from monocular images has been extensively studied in prior research [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. This depth-centric approach simplifies the alignment of LiDAR and camera data, as both can be integrated using common depth cues.

The depth information in roadside scenes possesses unique characteristics that are crucial for applications such as traffic monitoring and autonomous driving. Roadside environments typically contain a variety of complex elements, including multi-layered vehicles, pedestrians, traffic lights, and road signs. The sizes of objects seen from a camera's perspective can range from very small, like distant pedestrians, to very large, like nearby vehicles. Accurately parsing the depth information of these elements is essential to ensure correct environmental understanding and decision-making. Additionally, the perception of depth in roadside scenarios is significantly affected by lighting conditions and weather. For instance, nighttime or backlight situations can make capturing depth information challenging, while adverse weather conditions like rain or snow may impact the accuracy of depth perception. These factors necessitate robust depth estimation techniques that can adapt to varying environmental conditions to maintain the reliability and effectiveness of the monitoring and navigation systems.

This paper primarily addresses the challenge of depth estimation, which involves determining the depth of each pixel in an image. This task has been a fundamental issue in the field of computer vision since its early days. Depth estimation serves as a fundamental task, essential for tackling more complex applications. Drawing inspiration from the optical flow domain [35], we introduce the concept of cyclic multi-scale feature modulation, a key component of our innovative RMSFM [36] (Recurrent Multi-Scale Feature Modulation) architecture, depicted in Fig. 3. RMSFM represents an innovative, efficient, and compact deep learning framework, consisting of four key elements: a depth encoder, a depth decoder, a parameter-learning upsampling module, and a multi-scale feature modulation module. The depth encoder is a tailored version of ResNet18 [37], from which the last two blocks have been omitted to enhance the capture of precise multi-scale features. It processes a single image to generate feature maps at three different scales: 1/2, 1/4, and 1/8 of the original resolution. The depth decoder operates consistently at 1/8 of the input resolution and generates inverse depth maps through a sequence of five convolutional layers. This design approach strategically avoids the typical error propagation observed in coarse-to-fine architectures by not escalating errors from coarser to finer resolution layers. Unlike conventional bilinear interpolation, the parameter-learning upsampling module employs a learning-based strategy to adaptively restore the fixed-resolution inverse depth maps back to the full resolution, enhancing detail and accuracy. The multi-scale feature modulation module further enhances this system by adjusting content across different-scale feature maps, ensuring both semantic richness and spatial accuracy. This modulation is achieved using convolution-based Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), which dynamically adjust content integration between previous activations and current inputs. Overall, the RMSFM architecture not only mitigates error propagation but also improves the delineation of boundaries around smaller objects, making it a robust solution for depth estimation in complex visual scenes.Fig. 3RMSFM Architecture. RMSFM consists of a deep encoder, a deep decoder, an upsampling module for parameter learning, and a multi-scale feature modulation module. By combining RGB images with depth maps, the architecture better captures object boundaries and texture details within the scene. This is particularly valuable in complex roadside scenarios where accurate boundary information can significantly improve the quality of feature extraction and subsequent feature matching performance.

Matching features directly from images and point clouds presents significant challenges, as the sensors involved have distinct physical characteristics and operate based on different principles. This results in data that vary in dimensions, qualities, and types. Roadside sensors, for example, provide a wide field of view but have limited coverage, demanding a robust capability for feature integration. Moreover, not all correlated features equally contribute to calibration and it is essential to prioritize features that provide significant contributions. These include long-distance dependencies across multiple targets, intricate spatial geometric relationships, and multi-scale feature associations. To effectively address these challenges and adapt to unseen scenarios, we employ a Transformer [38] architecture, which is well-suited for managing such complex data interactions.

Using a Transformer architecture to consider the correlations between features from images and point clouds data offers several advantages. The attention mechanism of the Transformer is particularly effective at processing features from different sources, automatically identifying and enhancing the influence of important features. This enables the model to better integrate the texture and color information from images with the spatial and geometric information from point clouds. By learning the intrinsic connections between different modalities, the Transformer can enhance its adaptability to new scenes. This capability is crucial for dynamic environments where the sensory data may vary significantly due to changes in lighting, weather, or unexpected obstacles. The model's ability to generalize not only to data seen during training but also to new and unseen environmental conditions makes it highly robust and versatile.

This approach provides a comprehensive framework for sensor calibration, ensuring that the system remains effective across a wide range of scenarios. Consequently, there is a reduction in the need for frequent recalibrations, which in turn increases the overall reliability and efficiency of the monitoring or navigation systems in which it is deployed.

Network architecture

In this study, we employ a transformer-based architecture designed to predict the six degrees of freedom (6-DoF) extrinsic calibration parameters between LiDAR and camera systems. The process begins by projecting the LiDAR point cloud onto the image plane using the initial extrinsic parameters Tinit and the camera matrix K, establishing a baseline for calibration. This projection, combined with RGB images, serves as input for independent fine-grained feature extraction from both modalities. Following feature extraction, a transformer-based correlation module assesses the relationship between these features by computing a correlation matrix, which quantifies the alignment between the RGB and LiDAR features—this is crucial for subsequent calibration adjustments. The network then progresses to the regression phase, utilizing the transformer's capability to manage complex data relationships. It refines the calibration by dynamically adjusting the parameters based on the computed correlation metrics, minimizing deviations from the ground truth TLC. This iterative process ensures that the extrinsic calibration parameters are precisely adjusted, effectively aligning the camera images and LiDAR point clouds. Feature extraction

The feature extraction architecture comprises two parallel branches, each processing different modalities: RGB images and LiDAR data.

In the RGB branch, the Depth Features Fusion module plays a key role in integrating depth information with RGB features. The process begins with RGB images being fed into a modified ResNet, which extracts multi-scale RGB features. These features are then passed through the Multi-Scale Feature Modulation (MSFM) module, where depth estimation is performed at corresponding scales to generate multi-scale depth features. Subsequently, both the RGB and depth features are refined through convolutional layers to enhance their integration. The design of the LiDAR branch mirrors that of the RGB branch, maintaining consistency in structure and function. However, a notable modification in the LiDAR branch is the replacement of the ReLU activation function with Leaky ReLU, aiming to improve the activation dynamics for LiDAR data processing.

Feature matching

After extracting features from both the RGB-depth and LiDAR modalities, we employ a correlation layer [39] to determine the matching costs between corresponding pixels in the RGB-depth feature map xrgb-depth and the LiDAR feature map xLiDAR. The correlation layer enhances feature matching accuracy by constructing a cost volume that automates the matching of corresponding features across large datasets. This approach enables rapid and precise feature alignment, optimizing the determination of point-to-point correspondences in visual tasks such as optical flow estimation and stereo vision. As a result, it improves both the efficiency and effectiveness of feature matching in complex visual processing tasks. This process involves constructing a cost volume that encapsulates the matching costs, effectively representing the correlation between xrgb-depth and xLiDAR. The specific calculations for this process are detailed in Equation (7). The inputs to this layer, xrgb-depth and xLiDAR, are processed to produce fused features, termed Fcorr. This cost volume serves as a critical component in assessing the degree of alignment between the two sets of features, facilitating the subsequent calibration steps.(7)cv(p1,p2)=1N(c(xrgb−depth(p1)))Tc(xLiDAR(p2))In this method, c(p) represents the flattened vector of feature maps p, where N denotes the length of the column vector c(p), and T signifies the transpose operator. To compute the local cost volume effectively, it is essential to define a limited search window of d pixels. This constraint ensures that the absolute difference between any two pixel positions, p1 and p2, does not exceed d. Given that the input feature maps are relatively small, the value of d is set to a minimal level, specifically d = 2. The dimensionality of the resulting 3D cost volume, referred to as cv, is then established as d2 × H × W. Here, H and W are the height and width of the feature maps from xrgb-depth and xLiDAR. This configuration of the cost volume allows for a detailed assessment of the matching costs within a defined proximity, facilitating precise pixel-level comparison and alignment between the two feature maps.

To accurately estimate calibration parameters in roadside scenarios, we employ a Transformer-based architecture that integrates features from both RGB images and LiDAR data. The process initiates with feature extraction from the RGB image using a ResNet18 backbone, which generates the initial pose query Q. Specifically, the RGB image is processed through the ResNet backbone, followed by adaptive pooling, flattening, and a fully connected layer, resulting in the query Q. Simultaneously, depth features are extracted from both the RGB-Depth and LiDAR branches. The RGB-Depth branch processes the RGB image through another ResNet backbone, and the features are then enhanced using Multi-Scale Feature Modulation (MSFM) to produce depth features. In parallel, the LiDAR branch processes the mis-calibrated LiDAR data to extract corresponding features. These features from the RGB-Depth and LiDAR branches are subsequently fused to create a unified feature representation, referred to as fused features Fcorr. The Fused Features are then passed through a fully connected layer to generate the keys K and values V.

The Transformer architecture plays a central role in the feature alignment and calibration process through its cross-attention mechanism. Here, the query Q derived from the RGB image features interacts with the keys K and values V obtained from the fused RGB-Depth and LiDAR features. This cross-attention mechanism facilitates the effective alignment and matching of features by leveraging complementary information from both the RGB and LiDAR modalities. The attention mechanism employed is mathematically defined as shown in Equation (8).(8)Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKTdk)VIn this formulation, the dot product QKT quantifies the compatibility between the RGB-derived query and the fused features. The scaling factor dk , where dk is the dimensionality of the keys, ensures numerical stability during training. The resultant attention weights are applied to the values V, allowing the model to focus on the most relevant features for accurate calibration.

The Transformer then refines these features through multiple layers of self-attention. This refinement process iteratively improves the representation of the features, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the pose estimation. This process sets the stage for the subsequent calculation of precise translation and rotation parameters essential for the calibration task.

Following this, a feed-forward neural network refines the pose data. This module is capable of calculating translation and rotation parameters in distinct ways. Translation is represented as a 1 × 3 vector tpred, and rotation is represented as a 1 × 4 quaternion qpred.

Regression loss

For the translation vector tpred, we apply the smooth L1 loss. This is chosen over the standard L1 loss due to the latter's non-unique derivative at zero, which can potentially affect the convergence of the training process. The smooth L1 loss, incorporating a quadratic function near zero, offers a more gradual transition. For the rotation loss Lq, considering that quaternions primarily convey directional data, Euclidean distance fails to accurately reflect their differences. Thus, we employ angular distance to quantify the disparity between quaternions, as specified in Equation (10).(10)LR=Da(qgtruth,qpred)where qgtruth is the ground truth quaternion and qgtruth is the predicted quaternion. Da represents the angular distance between the two quaternions [43]. The overall regression loss LT aggregates both the translation and rotation losses, as shown in Equation (11).(11)LT=λtLt+λqLRwhere Lt represents the smooth L1 loss for the translation vector, and λt and λq are the weights assigned to the translation and rotation losses, respectively.

In addition to the regression loss, a point cloud distance constraint is incorporated into the loss function. This helps ensure that the spatial alignment of the point clouds from both modalities matches more accurately, enhancing the overall calibration quality. This formulation calculates the Euclidean distance for the alignment of point clouds to evaluate how closely the predicted transformation aligns with the actual LiDAR and camera setup.

Calibration inference

By integrating the predicted calibration result Tpred from the network with the initial calibration parameter Tinit, the extrinsic calibration parameters between the uncalibrated LiDAR and the camera are derived. Experiments

We assess the effectiveness of our calibration method using the DAIR-V2X-I dataset. This section elaborates on the data preprocessing steps, the training process, the evaluation metrics used, and provides a detailed discussion of the results obtained from various experiments.

Dataset Preparation

We evaluated our proposed calibration algorithm using the DAIR-V2X dataset [44], a comprehensive and diverse large-scale multimodal, multi-view dataset designed specifically for collaborative autonomous driving research involving vehicles and roadside infrastructure. A key component, the DAIR-V2X-I roadside dataset, consists of 10,084 frames captured independently by roadside sensors, offering a robust framework for testing under real-world conditions. These frames include both LiDAR and corresponding camera data, sourced from urban intersections equipped with high-resolution cameras and advanced 300-beam LiDAR systems.

The dataset was meticulously compiled from 28 strategically selected intersections in Beijing, chosen for their varied traffic patterns and complexities. This setup captures a wide array of vehicle dynamics and traffic behaviors, crucial for validating the accuracy and versatility of calibration algorithms. Additionally, the equipment at each site is designed to operate under a variety of weather and lighting conditions, thereby ensuring the dataset's richness and applicability to diverse operational scenarios. This extensive and meticulously gathered data not only reinforces the relevance and applicability of our research but also underscores the potential of the DAIR-V2X-I dataset in pushing the boundaries of current calibration technologies.

To ensure the robustness of the training set, we introduce random deviations ΔT uniformly distributed within a specified range in the extrinsic parameters of the dataset. The initial extrinsic parameters Tinit are calculated as ΔT × TLC. This parameter, along with the corresponding point cloud and image data, are used as input to the training process. The true value of the training is defined as Tgtruth = ΔT. By generating these random deviations, we are able to create a large amount of training data, thereby enhancing the robustness of the calibration method.

Implementing details

Training details

The images from the DAIR-V2X dataset are initially padded to a resolution of 1920 × 1080, then resized to 512 × 256 to serve as inputs for our model. For optimization, we utilize the Adam Optimizer with an initial learning rate set at 3 × 10−4. The Transformer decoder in our network is configured with four layers to effectively process the feature correlations and regress the calibration parameters.

Our calibration network is trained using four Nvidia V100 GPUs, with a batch size of 120 and a training regimen that spans 240 epochs. This extensive training is designed to ensure that the model comprehensively learns and adapts to variations in the calibration data, thereby enhancing its accuracy and robustness in real-world scenarios.

Evaluation metrics

We use translation and rotation parameters to evaluate the calibration results. For the translation parameters, we calculate the mean absolute error in the X, Y, and Z directions. The overall accuracy of the translation vector is determined by calculating the Euclidean distance between the actual translation vector and the predicted translation vector. The specific calculation is as shown in Equation (15).(15)Et=‖tpred−tgtruth‖2

The translation errors are quantified using the 2-norm, which calculates the Euclidean distance of the translation vector. For rotation, while quaternions could be used, we instead measure the rotation errors in terms of Euler angles—roll, pitch, and yaw—to provide a more intuitive evaluation. The mean absolute errors for each of these angles are documented separately, allowing for a detailed analysis of the rotational accuracy.

Limitations and future work

Despite the RLCFormer's robust calibration capabilities, it exhibits limitations in environments with sparse or low-quality data, such as under adverse weather conditions or in poorly lit areas. Its performance also remains less tested in chaotic, real-world settings where dynamic obstacles and sensor noise are prevalent. Additionally, the high computational demands of the Transformer architecture may hinder its application in real-time scenarios, where quick data processing is essential.

For future work, efforts will be directed towards improving the adaptability and efficiency of RLCFormer. This includes developing mechanisms to dynamically adjust to varying data quality and environmental conditions, and optimizing the model to reduce computational requirements for better suitability in real-time applications. Expanding the diversity of training data and engaging in real-world testing will also be crucial to enhance the model's robustness and generalizability across more complex and unpredictable environments.

Conclusion

In this paper, we presented RLCFormer, a Transformer-based network for LiDAR and camera extrinsic calibration specifically designed for roadside scenarios. This network represents a significant advancement in the estimation of extrinsic calibration parameters for LiDAR and cameras in roadside environments. Our approach effectively utilizes fused depth and RGB features, along with correlation layers and a Transformer decoder, to enhance scene representation and cross-modal feature matching. The RLCFormer model was subjected to evaluation on the DAIR-V2X-I dataset. It demonstrated exceptional precision, resulting in a notable reduction in the mean translation and rotation errors. Compared to other traditional and learning-based calibration methods, RLCFormer operates faster and delivers more accurate inference results. These outcomes demonstrate that our method is particularly well-suited for roadside extrinsic calibration tasks, contributing to reliable sensor data fusion and enhancing the environmental perception capabilities of intelligent transportation systems. This work paves the way for extending these functionalities to a broader range of applications and scenarios, thus broadening the impact and applicability of our approach in the field of roadside monitoring and beyond.
