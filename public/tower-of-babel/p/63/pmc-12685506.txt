Prioritizing interventions for echinococcosis prevention in China: a population attributable fraction analysis
Authors: Shuaiming Xu, Quzhen Danzeng, Bing Guo, Peidi Xu, Lingxi Gu, Yajie Zhao, Xiong Guo, Yuling Tang, Yang Chang, Gonghua Wu, Xing Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDespite population-level control efforts, human echinococcosis continues to pose a public health challenge in China. Targeting modifiable risk factors at the individual level could be another recipe, but the priority targets for intervention remain unclear. Our aim was to quantify the potential health gains of hypothetical interventions on modifiable risk factors for human echinococcosis, informing effective control strategies.MethodsWe collected and aggregated data on the association between modifiable risk factors and human echinococcosis and national total echinococcosis (cystic echinococcosis [CE], alveolar echinococcosis [AE], and other unclassified cases) incidence during 2007–2020 in China. A Bayesian hierarchical model was developed to pool effect estimates for modifiable risk factors associated with total echinococcosis and its subtypes (CE and AE), while accounting for the adequacy of confounding adjustment. Additionally, we evaluated the preventable burden of human echinococcosis via the annual number of total echinococcosis cases attributable to modifiable risk factors deriving from the population attributable fractions (PAFs).FindingsTwelve risk factors for human echinococcosis were identified from 25 studies across nine endemic provinces and autonomous regions in China, with PAFs ranging from 2.2% to 41.2%. The highest PAFs were observed for surface water sources for drinking (PAF: 41.2%; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 18.9–55.9) and the presence of stray dogs around residences (PAF: 34.9%; 15.8–55.1), accounting for 1437 (95% CI: 658–1947) and 1216 (552–1921) preventable annual total echinococcosis cases, respectively. Drinking unboiled water followed, with a PAF of 33.5% (6.2–54.8) and 1167 (217–1910) preventable cases annually. In contrast, the lowest PAF was estimated for exposure to foxes or fox-derived materials (PAF: 2.2%; 0.5–4.3). Surface water sources for drinking and free-roaming owned dogs showed the highest PAFs for CE (42.6%; 19.7–56.5) and AE (49.4%; 34.8–60.2), respectively. Simultaneously eliminating the presence of stray dogs around residences and surface water sources for drinking was the most-effective among available pair-wise combination of risk factors, in which 75.3% (1621/2152) of preventable total echinococcosis cases are concentrated in Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region (833; 519–1005), Qinghai Province (396; 247–478), and Sichuan Province (392; 244–473).InterpretationInterventions targeting drinking water safety and responsible dog management could reduce echinococcosis incidence in China. Public health efforts should prioritize high-prevalence regions such as Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, Qinghai Province, and Sichuan Province to maximize impact.FundingNHC Key Laboratory of Echinococcosis Prevention and Control (no. 21H1234) and Postdoctoral Fellowship Program of CPSF (no. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Echinococcosis is a major neglected zoonotic disease with the highest burden reported in China, where both cystic echinococcosis (CE) and alveolar echinococcosis (AE) co-circulate, creating a complex epidemiological landscape. Previous studies have identified a range of behavioral risk factors, such as dog ownership, home slaughter, feeding dogs with livestock viscera, and use of unsafe drinking water, and meta-analyses have pooled odds ratios for some of these factors. However, most research focused on subsets of individual risk factors in limited endemic areas but rarely quantified their relative contributions to disease burden at the population level. Importantly, no study has estimated population attributable fractions (PAFs) and region-specific preventable cases for modifiable risk factors across China, leaving policymakers without clear evidence on which risk factors to prioritize or which regions would benefit most from targeted interventions.

Added value of this study

This study fills a critical gap by systematically quantifying the impact of modifiable risk factors on the burden of echinococcosis in China. Using a Bayesian hierarchical model, we pooled adjusted effect estimates from 25 studies covering over 1.2 million individuals across nine endemic provinces to estimate PAFs and the region-specific annual preventable cases for modifiable risk factors. Surface water sources for drinking and the presence of stray dogs around residences showed the highest PAFs (41.2% and 35.3%, respectively) and the greatest potential for prevention of the two-factor combination of these two modifiable risk factors, in terms of annual preventable total echinococcosis cases, was observed in Xinjiang (833 cases per year), Qinghai (396 cases per year), and Sichuan (392 cases per year), providing evidence on which modifiable risk factors should be prioritized for intervention and where such efforts were likely to yield the greatest public health benefits.

Implications of all the available evidence

This study provides important insights into designing targeted interventions to reduce echinococcosis burden in China. By quantifying the preventable burden of disease, our findings offer a roadmap for prioritizing interventions. Specifically, improving water sanitation and controlling dog-related transmission could be critical for reducing disease incidence, especially in remote, endemic geographical regions of western China. These results may have significant implications for public health policy, guiding resource allocation and intervention planning to mitigate the impact of echinococcosis and support sustainable control in endemic regions.

Introduction

Echinococcosis, a neglected zoonotic disease caused by the Echinococcus genus, poses a significant global public health.1,2 Currently affecting over 1 million people, echinococcosis causes approximately 19,300 deaths annually, with a global burden of 871,000 disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) and economic losses exceeding $3 billion.3,4 The disease manifests mainly as cystic echinococcosis (CE) and alveolar echinococcosis (AE), transmitted through a complex life cycle involving definitive hosts (canids, occasionally felids) and intermediate hosts (ungulates for CE, rodents/lagomorphs for AE).2,5 Humans, as accidental intermediate hosts, acquire infection by ingesting parasite eggs shed by definitive hosts,2,5 leading to overlapping pathways for risk factors, although AE is more wildlife-related while CE is mainly domestic.5,6

China bears the highest burden of human echinococcosis, with an estimated 322,400 DALYs7 and around 60 million people at risk of infection,8 and an average of 3485 cases reported annually between 2007 and 2020. Notably, 40% of CE and 91% of AE cases worldwide occur in China.6 Whereas most endemic countries report predominance of either CE or AE, China experiences the co-circulation of both CE and AE2,5 resulting in a complex epidemiological landscape that challenges prevention and control. Although the annual number of human echinococcosis is relatively small, CE primarily causes long-term disability, whereas AE is highly fatal, with untreated cases having a 10-year mortality rate of approximately 94%.4,5 The disease is concentrated in underdeveloped western regions of China,9 where infection not only leads to personal disability but also reduces household labor capacity, exacerbating poverty and health inequities. In response, China has implemented coordinated control policies, including the National Key Parasitic Disease Prevention and Control Program (2006–15) and subsequent Echinococcosis Control Action Plan (2010–15), to reduce transmission in high-risk geographical regions.10 Although these efforts have contributed to a measurable decline in disease prevalence,11 echinococcosis remains a substantial burden,10 particularly in the pastoral and semi-pastoral regions of western China.9,12,13

Recent studies highlight that reducing individual or household behaviors could further reduce the risk of echinococcosis infection.9,14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Shared behavioral determinants of both CE and AE include individual risk behaviors such as not washing hands before meals, drinking unboiled water, and unsafe contact with dogs, as well as household practices such as irresponsible dog ownership, home slaughter without proper facilities, and unsafe disposal of livestock offal.6,19 In addition, CE is strongly linked to livestock-related practices, including home slaughter and feeding dogs with livestock viscera, whereas AE is more associated with wildlife-related exposures, such as contact with foxes or contaminated environments, and the peri-domestic presence of small mammals.17,19,20 These risk factors, which can be modified through public health interventions to reduce future disease burden, are termed modifiable risk factors and hold clear public health significance. However, robust evidence is scarce on which modifiable risk factors are most effective in preventing infection at the individual or household level. It is also unclear which geographical regions could benefit the most from modifying individual-level risk factors. This knowledge gap hinders the prioritization of interventions, the efficient allocation of resources to high-risk communities, and the development of targeted prevention strategies on individuals and households.

This study quantified the relative contributions of modifiable risk factors to human echinococcosis in China. Using the population attributable fractions (PAFs), we estimated the proportion and absolute number of cases that could be prevented through targeted interventions. These findings provide an evidence-based framework for refining national strategies to simultaneously control both CE and AE in China.

Methods

The study was designed as follows. First, studies on modifiable risk factors of CE, AE, and total echinococcosis (CE, AE, and unclassified cases) were simultaneously reviewed under a unified literature retrieval strategy. Second, a unified Bayesian hierarchical model was constructed for each identified risk factor to simultaneously pool effect estimates across the three outcomes. Finally, the parameters obtained from the model were used for separately estimating the PAFs and number of preventable cases for corresponding outcome.

Data sources

Data for this study were obtained from three main sources. First, modifiable risk factor associations with echinococcosis, including CE, AE and total echinococcosis, and their uncertainty intervals were sourced via systematic review and meta-analysis, adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.21 The protocol is registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024540398). Second, the population-level prevalence for each risk factor was derived from Chinese epidemiological surveys9,22 and surveillance data after 2010. For proportions of risk factors not reported in a nationwide study, data were collected from studies in endemic regions of China, as defined by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC),8 including Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region (Xinjiang), Tibet Autonomous Region (Tibet), Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region (Inner Mongolia), Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region (Ningxia), and the provinces of Qinghai, Sichuan, Gansu, Shaanxi, and Yunnan. Lastly, the annual incident number of total echinococcosis during 2007–2020 was obtained from China CDC.

Evidence synthesis of modifiable risk factors

Search strategy and selection criteria

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across both English (PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Medline, Cochrane Library) and Chinese (CNKI, Wan Fang, VIP, SinoMed) databases for articles related to echinococcosis, risk factors and China from their inception to January 2025. The full search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Materials (pages 1–2). Population-based studies (cross-sectional/case-control/cohort) were eligible. Echinococcosis must be diagnosed via imaging confirmation (ultrasonography/CT/MRI). Studies were required to report complete data for acquiring OR estimates and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) in Chinese populations. Duplicate reporting information and studies without data on modifiable risk factors were excluded. Studies on AE, CE, and total echinococcosis, as well as those reporting total echinococcosis without subtype classification, were all included. Importantly, statistical significance of risk factor ORs was not a prerequisite for inclusion; instead, risk factors were further evaluated based on their biological plausibility and relevance to transmission pathways, and only those with practical public health significance were retained for analysis.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent researchers (Shuaiming Xu and Peidi Xu) screened the articles and extracted relevant data, with discrepancies resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (Xing Zhao). Risk factors investigated in at least three separate studies were included in the meta-analysis.23 Study quality was assessed by two authors (Shuaiming Xu and Peidi Xu) using appropriate scales for cohort or case-control studies (Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, NOS)24 and cross-sectional studies (AHRQ).25 Studies were categorized as low, medium, or high quality based on their scores. Covariate adjustment was also evaluated, with adequate adjustment defined as including at least five confounders.26

Summarizing effects of risk factors

A single Bayesian hierarchical model was used for each risk factor to simultaneously pool its effects on total echinococcosis and its subtypes (CE and AE). In this framework, total echinococcosis was treated as the reference outcome and was set as the model intercept. Additional terms were introduced for CE and AE to describe the effect deviation of a specific risk factor from its effect on total echinococcosis. This allowed us to obtain estimates for total echinococcosis directly, while also quantifying subtype-specific differences. Confounding adjustment status (fully adjusted versus unadjusted) and its interaction with outcome indicators were further induced to consider the influence of confounder control. Modeling all outcomes together enabled borrowing of information across subtypes,27,28 avoiding the aggregation bias,29, 30, 31 improved precision in settings with sparse data,32,33 and provided a coherent basis for comparing CE, AE, and total echinococcosis. Factors with negligible or biologically implausible effects for a specific subtype are reflected in estimates close to zero, avoiding implausible inferences.

Given that all modifiable factors were risk factors for total echinococcosis, we assumed that the adjusted ORs were greater than one and followed a half-normal prior on the log OR scale with SD = 1.48 (median = 1, mean = 1.18, 95th percentile = 2.91), corresponding to an OR prior with median 2.718, mean 3.254, and 95th percentile 18.357. As a sensitivity analysis, we also considered a weakly informative normal prior N(0,2), implying a best guess of OR = 1 and a wide 95% interval of (0.063, 15.988), centered on the null value and allowing for both protective and harmful effects.34 Further details of the Bayesian modeling approach are provided in the Supplementary Materials (pages 1–3).

Model convergence was evaluated using the Gelman–Rubin statistic, with values near 1.0 indicating satisfactory convergence and values >1.1 being problematic.34 The hierarchical Bayesian model also provides an estimate of between-study heterogeneity τ. Publication bias was assessed using contour enhanced funnel plots,35 Egger's test,36 and the trim-and-fill method.37

Estimation of PAF and preventable cases

Unlike relative risk (RR), odds ratio (OR), or hazard ratio (HR) which quantify only the strength of association, PAF is a useful measure that combines the strength of association with the prevalence of exposure, estimating the proportion of disease burden that could be prevented by removing or reducing a risk factor.38 By enabling direct comparison across multiple risk factors, PAF provides a population-level perspective that is highly relevant for identifying the most urgent interventions and informing prevention policies.39 Given the rare disease assumption (prevalence <5%) in our study population, PAF for modifiable risk factors associated with echinococcosis was estimated separately for total echinococcosis and its subtypes (CE and AE) using the re-expressed Miettinen formula40,41:

PAFM=π×ORunadj1+π(ORunadj−1)×ORadj−1ORadj, where π is the population-level prevalence for each risk factor, and OR represents the odds ratio measuring the effect estimate of association between the risk factor and echinococcosis. Specifically, ORunadj refers to estimates that were not fully adjusted for confounding, while ORadj refers to estimates that were sufficiently adjusted.

To estimate the potential reduction in disease burden if multiple modifiable risk factors were addressed simultaneously, We also estimated the combined PAF, using the formula: CombinedPAF=1−∏i=1n(1−PAFi), where PAFi is PAF of ith risk factor.42, 43, 44 This approach sequentially applies each PAF to the proportion of disease not already attributed to previous risk factors. In other words, if risk factors are causally independent and their combined effect multiplicative, 1−PAFi represents the fraction of disease not caused by risk factor i. The product of all 1−PAFi terms is therefore the fraction of disease not attributable to any of the n risk factors. Subtracting this product from 1 gives the proportion of disease attributable to the combined effect of all n risk factors. However, given the likely interdependence among risk factors, the combined PAF estimates should be interpreted as the theoretical maximum achievable impact under idealized conditions.

To provide a more realistic and actionable assessment, we grouped modifiable risk factors by echinococcosis transmission pathways: definitive host exposure, intermediate host-related practices, and hygiene habits (Fig. 2). We first calculated combined PAFs for every two-factor combination across different transmission pathways (definitive host exposure and intermediate host-related practices, definitive host exposure and hygiene habits, intermediate host-related practices and hygiene habits), then for three-factor combinations selecting one factor from each pathway, and finally for all factors combined. Only risk factors with statistically significant PAFs were included in the combined PAF analysis.

The annual number of total echinococcosis cases attributable to modifiable risk factors n was estimated as: n=N×PAF, where N is the average annual number of total echinococcosis cases.

Sensitivity analysis

The prior for between-study heterogeneity was specified as τ∼HC(0,0.280), with τ∼HC(0,0.587) used for sensitivity analysis.45 In addition, we conducted a sensitivity analysis including global studies to evaluate the robustness of our findings.

Program and packages

All analyses were performed with R 4.4.2 software. The Bayesian hierarchical models were implemented in JAGS, and the 95% confidence intervals for the PAF estimates were computed with the graphPAF package.46

Ethics approval

This study was based entirely on published and publicly available data and therefore did not require ethical approval.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Population attributable fractions and the annual preventable cases

Fig. 4 presents the PAFs of 12 modifiable risk factors for echinococcosis, including total echinococcosis, CE and AE, across endemic geographical regions in China. For total echinococcosis, surface water sources for drinking showed the highest PAF at 41.2% (95% CI: 18.9–55.9), corresponding to 1437 (95% CI: 658–1947) preventable cases annually. The presence of stray dogs around residences and drinking unboiled water followed, with PAFs of 34.9% (15.8–55.1) and 33.5% (6.2–54.8), accounting for 1216 (552–1921) and 1167 (217–1910) annual preventable cases, respectively. In contrast, exposure to foxes or fox-derived materials had the lowest PAF of 2.2% (0.5–4.3), corresponding to 77 (17–152) preventable cases annually. Surface water sources for drinking and free-roaming owned dogs showed the highest PAFs for AE (49.4%; 95% CI: 34.8–60.2) and CE (42.6%; 19.7–56.5), respectively. The PAF point estimates for free-roaming owned dogs and feeding dogs with livestock viscera were more than 10 percentage points higher for CE than for total echinococcosis. Combined modifiable risk factors

Supplementary Tables S14–21 present the results of joint interventions targeting modifiable risk factors. For total echinococcosis, the highest two-factor combination was the presence of stray dogs around residences and surface water sources for drinking, with a theoretical maximum combined PAF (combined PAF) of 61.8% (38.5–74.5), corresponding to an estimated 2152 (1341–2596) preventable cases. The highest three-factor combination was the presence of stray dogs around residences, surface water sources for drinking, and household livestock slaughter, yielding a combined PAF of 73.3% (54.1–82.9) and an estimated 2553 (1885–2890) preventable cases. For CE, the highest two-factor combination was free-roaming owned dogs and drinking unboiled water with a combined PAF of 64.2% (36.4–78.3), while the highest three-factor combination included free-roaming owned dogs, drinking unboiled water, and feeding dogs with livestock viscera, with a combined PAF of 77.2% (58.9–86.3). For AE, the highest two-factor combination was surface water sources for drinking and household livestock slaughter, with a combined PAF of 65.2% (46.2–75.7), whereas the highest three-factor combination was dog ownership, surface water sources for drinking, and household livestock slaughter, with a combined PAF of 74.0% (58.1–82.8). Together, we estimate that up to 3395 (95% CI: 3264–3441) total echinococcosis cases could be prevented annually in endemic geographical regions of China by targeting all identified modifiable risk factors, corresponding to 97.4% (93.7%–98.7) of the total annual disease burden. For CE and AE, the overall PAFs were 98.2% (95.0–99.2) and 82.9% (69.7–89.4), respectively.

Robustness of results and model evaluation

Supplementary Materials provide detailed Bayesian meta-analysis results for each risk factor and assessments of publication bias (Supplementary Figures S1–3). All models had good convergence. Sensitivity analysis (including alternative priors and inclusion of global studies) confirmed the robustness of the results (Supplementary Tables S20–24).

Discussion

Principal findings

This study provides a comprehensive synthesis of the most frequently studied modifiable risk factors for human echinococcosis and their potential prevention benefits across endemic regions in China. By estimating PAFs and the corresponding number of preventable cases, we identified high-priority targets for individual- and household-level risk factors, as well as the optimal two-factor intervention combinations. Our findings underscore the important role of improving water safety, managing dog-related exposures, and promoting hygienic practices to reduce the burden of echinococcosis in nine endemic provinces and autonomous regions, with the combination of surface water sources for drinking and free-roaming owned dogs emerging as one of the most promising two-factor interventions.

Top-priority risk factors for intervention

Among the 12 modifiable risk factors, surface water sources for drinking showed the highest PAF (41.2%), while drinking unboiled water ranked third, both highlighting the important role of waterborne transmission. Although direct microbiological evidence of waterborne transmission of Echinococcus is scarce, the mechanism is biologically and environmentally plausible. In remote and resource-limited geographical regions, especially in pastoral and semi-pastoral zones, surface water from rivers, springs, ponds, or ditches is often the primary drinking source due to limited access to treated water infrastructure.47 Echinococcus eggs excreted in canine feces could be washed into these surface water sources by rainfall.48 Nomadic and semi-nomadic households, constrained by the distance to water sources, limited fuel availability, and time pressures, frequently consume this water without boiling,9,48 thereby increasing the risk of echinococcosis.

Importantly, surface water sources for drinking and drinking unboiled water are not independent risk factors, but different points along the same causal pathway—contaminated surface water serving as the upstream source and unboiled consumption as the downstream behavioral conduit to infection. Consequently, an intervention that ensures access to safe, piped or community-treated water could concurrently eliminate both upstream and downstream exposures, effectively capturing their combined impact through a single, synergistic policy measure. Although access to clean water has improved through infrastructure investments,49 unsafe water consumption remains the top risk factor identified. In nomadic and semi-nomadic communities, cultural practices and mobility limit use of improved water sources, underscoring that infrastructure alone cannot fully address exposure risks. Given the substantial logistical and temporal challenges of improving water infrastructure in these remote regions, promoting household-level water boiling and safe storage practices may offer an immediate and feasible intervention, while longer-term infrastructure improvements serve as sustainable solutions.

The high PAFs of dog-related modifiable risk factors are unsurprising, considering that dogs serve as the definitive hosts for Echinococcus. The presence of stray dogs around residences ranked second (PAF: 34.9% for total echinococcosis; 39.1% for CE; 27.1% for AE), emphasizing their central role in maintaining the life cycle of the parasite. In Tibetan and other pastoral communities, religious beliefs often prohibit killing stray animals, leading to uncontrolled dog populations and elevated infection risk.47,50 These dogs were typically not dewormed and could contaminate surface water sources and pastures with Echinococcus eggs through their feces, particularly in geographical regions lacking adequate environmental sanitation,51 thereby directly driving the drinking water risks described above. The PAF for dog contact behaviors was higher than that for dog ownership, suggesting that direct contact with dogs represents a more immediate and significant risk factor than ownership alone.52 Free-roaming owned dogs had a notable PAF for total echinococcosis and showed the highest PAF for CE (42.6%). Such dogs were more likely to interact with stray dogs or come into contact with Echinococcus eggs present in contaminated soil or on pasture surfaces. During human–dog interactions, people may inadvertently be exposed to parasite eggs that adhere to the dog's fur, thereby increasing the risk of infection.22

Similarly, promoting responsible dog management could generate intervention synergy by simultaneously reducing several correlated canine-related risks. Notably, dog deworming, a cornerstone intervention that directly disrupts both upstream and downstream transmission within this chain, has long been implemented nationwide as a mandatory control measure in China,8 achieving substantial success in reducing transmission.11,53 As this intervention is already universally enforced and only few studies have reported corresponding effect estimates, it was not included as a modifiable factor in our analysis. Beyond deworming, further reduction in disease burden could be achieved through strengthened management of stray and free-roaming dogs and by limiting close human–dog contact to maximize the synergistic benefits of integrated interventions.

Modest-risk factors: food safety and livestock practices

Household livestock slaughter and feeding dogs with livestock viscera reflect important links between intermediate and definitive hosts. These practices facilitate direct ingestion of viable Echinococcus larvae by dogs, particularly during slaughter seasons, when access to raw offal increases.54, 55, 56 Despite existing policies, decentralized and informal slaughtering remains common in western China, with a high proportion of households engaging in such practices. Interventions should strengthen centralized slaughter facilities, educate households about offal disposal, and restrict dog access to slaughter waste. Consumption of raw or undercooked food and free-roaming owned dogs also represented modifiable behaviors with population-level impacts. In resource-limited settings, poor access to fuel and cooking facilities may lead to the consumption of unwashed or undercooked vegetables, facilitating oral transmission.54,57 Meanwhile, owned dogs that are not tethered or confined could roam freely, increasing environmental contamination and interaction with intermediate hosts.

Lower-impact exposures

Although the PAF for poor hand hygiene before meals was lower than other factors, this likely reflects the high uncertainty due to limited data for AE, and it remains an important behavioral contributor. In agricultural and pastoral settings, water scarcity or competing labor demands often limit handwashing, especially before meals.47,54 This allows parasite eggs from contaminated surfaces or soil to enter the body through ingestion. In contrast, livestock rearing and exposure to foxes or fox-derived materials showed comparatively lower contributions. While livestock keeping is widespread, its direct role in human infection may be mediated through downstream practices like slaughter. The limited impact of foxes likely reflected their lower interaction frequency with humans and greater spatial separation in most settings. However, foxes remain a relevant reservoir for AE, especially in wildlife-adjacent or hyper-endemic foci,58 where subtype-specific estimates are highly uncertain due to limited data. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and warrant further targeted research.

Combined risk factors for intervention

For total echinococcosis, the PAF increased from 41.2% for the single most effective intervention to approximately 61.8% when combining two transmission pathways, representing an additional gain of about 20%. Expanding to three-way combinations further increased the PAF to 73.3%, but with a much smaller incremental benefit of around 10%. Similar patterns were observed for both CE and AE, indicating that the marginal benefit of adding more risk factor interventions decreases substantially as the number of targeted factors increases. From a prevention standpoint, prioritizing joint interventions targeting definitive host exposures (the presences of stray dogs around residences, free-roaming owned dogs, and dog ownership) and hygienic habits (surface water sources for drinking and drinking unboiled water) are likely to deliver a higher population-level benefit. Meanwhile, intermediate host-related practices, particularly the standardization of livestock slaughter, remain essential components of comprehensive control programs.

Endemic provinces and autonomous regions

The highest endemicity of echinococcosis was observed in Xinjiang, Qinghai, and Sichuan in this study. Risk factors related to dogs, including presence of stray dogs around residences and dog contact behaviors, and water sanitation, including surface water sources for drinking and drinking unboiled water, would partly explain the highest burden in these regions. Besides the highest PAF for these risk factors, high prevalence of these risk factors in these regions would be another reason. It was reported that all these regions had lower tap water usage rates and larger number of owned dogs per household compared to other provinces and autonomous regions,9 resulting in increased possibilities for exposure to the aforementioned risk factors. In addition, nationwide studies have also highlighted environmental determinants, such as high altitude, low temperature, and precipitation, as important contributors to both CE and AE.6,9 Qinghai is located on the Tibetan Plateau, western Sichuan lies on the plateau, and southern Xinjiang is at the plateau's edge; these high-altitude, low-temperature environments, combined with high densities of dogs, livestock, and small mammals,9 provide favorable conditions for the development and transmission of Echinococcus. These regions are also primarily pastoral, with relatively underdeveloped socioeconomic conditions, limited access to healthcare, and generally lower levels of education, hygiene practices, and awareness of disease prevention,14,22 which collectively contribute to the high endemicity of echinococcosis in these areas.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study to systematically evaluate and quantify the preventable annual cases of echinococcosis attributable to modifiable risk factors in China using PAFs. The use of a Bayesian hierarchical model allowed integration of sparse data, adjusted for study-level confounding, and jointly modeled CE and AE outcomes.32 Additionally, we applied the re-expressed Miettinen formula, which provides more accurate estimates of PAFs in the presence of confounding than the traditional Levin's formula.40

Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the quality and quantity of available evidence varied across risk factors. For certain exposures, such as free-roaming owned dogs, the presence of stray dogs around residences, feeding dogs with livestock viscera, and poor hand hygiene before meals for AE, as well as exposure to foxes or fox-derived materials for CE, adjusted effect estimates were lacking or sparse, resulting in wide credible intervals. Second, only observational studies were included, and potential residual confounding and selection bias cannot be ruled out. Third, given the relatively small number of studies and the heterogeneity across them, we declared that the results should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, the calculation of combined PAFs assumes independence among risk factors. While we used the most commonly employed approach to combine multiple risk factors,42, 43, 44 it does not fully account for correlations among factors within the same transmission pathway. This assumption could overestimate the preventable burden when multiple correlated factors are targeted simultaneously. For factors in the same transmission pathway in particular, the combined PAFs should be interpreted as the theoretical maximum impact that could be achieved under ideal conditions. This limitation can be addressed when individual-level data are available.59 However, accounting for such correlations remains challenging in analyses based on aggregated data. Future methodological developments could incorporate sensitivity analyses using plausible correlation ranges or sequential removal approaches to better reflect the combined effects of multiple factors.46 Finally, the PAFs assume full elimination of exposure, which may not be feasible in real-world interventions, so estimates should be interpreted as upper bounds of potential impact.

Public health implications

Echinococcosis remains a pressing public health issue in northwestern China, where modifiable risk factors play an important role in shaping the disease burden. Building on existing policies, our findings offer a policy-relevant framework for prioritizing feasible, high-impact interventions. In practice, controlling upstream drivers of shared transmission pathways could leverage the synergistic impact of integrated interventions across multiple linked risk factors. For example, improving access to safe or piped water—together with promoting household boiling practices—could substantially reduce waterborne transmission, while coordinated responsible dog management through regular deworming and control of stray and free-roaming dogs could interrupt the main domestic transmission cycle.

Furthermore, with more than 75% of preventable cases concentrated in Xinjiang, Qinghai, and Sichuan, prioritizing these high-burden regions would yield the greatest public health returns. Implementation in pastoral and semi-nomadic communities must also account for sociocultural realities, including religious constraints on dog culling, mobility, and limited infrastructure. Integrating community participation, culturally appropriate education, and incentive-based programs could enhance the uptake and long-term sustainability of interventions. Prioritizing feasible, synergistic intervention pairs could yield the greatest near-term gain, while promoting household-level water boiling and safe storage practices provides an immediate, practical measure as longer-term water infrastructure solutions are developed. In summary, focusing on these feasible, synergistic intervention pairs could provide the greatest near-term gains while laying the foundation for broader, long-term infrastructure improvements.

Conclusion

This study quantifies the preventable burden of echinococcosis in China and identifies high-priority modifiable risk factors and geographic targets. The findings highlight that coordinated, context-adapted interventions—focusing on water safety and responsible dog management—offer the most practical path toward reducing transmission, particularly in western provinces and autonomous regions, where the majority of preventable cases are concentrated. These results offer a useful evidence base for informing targeted control strategies and guiding national policies toward the sustainable reduction of echinococcosis.
