Sintilimab plus chemotherapy for first-line treatment of advanced pulmonary lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma: Phase 2 trial
Authors: Xinqing Lin, Haiyi Deng, Nianxin Fang, Likun Chen, Rui Chen, Xiaohong Xie, Zhanhong Xie, Jiexia Zhang, Ming Liu, Laiyu Liu, Ni Sun, Fei Wang, Yilin Yang, Xinze Lv, Zhou Zhang, Shangli Cai, Ting Hou, Shiyue Li, Jianxing He, Chengzhi Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPulmonary lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma (PLELC) is a unique non-small cell lung cancer associated with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), lacking a standard treatment. This phase 2 study evaluates sintilimab plus gemcitabine and carboplatin (4 cycles), followed by sintilimab maintenance in 30 untreated patients with locally advanced or metastatic PLELC. At a median follow-up of 42.4 months, the objective response rate is 73.3% and disease control is 100.0%. The median progression-free survival (PFS) is 13.5 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 12.2–24.5), and median overall survival is 46.9 months (95% CI, 29.5-not reached [NR]). Grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events occurred in 70.0% of patients, mainly neutropenia (46.7%). Higher T cell-inflamed gene-expression profile score is associated with longer PFS (p = 0.003). Plasma EBV-DNA levels decrease with treatment response and increase with disease progression. Sintilimab plus chemotherapy shows promising efficacy with manageable safety as first-line treatment for advanced PLELC. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04312204).

Introduction

Pulmonary lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma (PLELC) is a rare and distinct histologic subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), comprising approximately 0.7% of all NSCLC cases. It predominately affects Southeastern Asians, with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection being a crucial etiological factor.1,2 Differing from other NSCLC subtypes, the pathologic feature and genomic landscape of PLELC more resemble those of undifferentiated nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC).3,4,5 The 2015 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Lung Tumors moved PLELC from large cell carcinoma to other and unclassified carcinomas.2 Recently, it has been reclassified under squamous cell carcinomas by the 2021 WHO classification.6 The current treatment strategy for PLELC is similar to squamous cell carcinomas, including surgery for early-stage PLELC and multimodality treatment, including platinum-based chemotherapy and radiation for unresectable PLELC.7,8,9,10 However, due to its association with EBV infection, PLELC has distinct genetic profiles and tumor microenvironment from squamous cell carcinomas. Thus, the standard treatment of squamous cell carcinoma may not apply to PLELC. Prospective clinical trials establishing standard treatment for PLELC remain lacking because of its rarity. Thus, a new standard therapy for PLELC is urgently needed.

Actionable driver alterations (e.g., EGFR mutations, ALK rearrangements, etc.) are infrequently found in PLELC,3,5,11 indicative of the ineffectiveness of target therapy in this disease.12 The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors has revolutionized the treatment for non-oncogene-addicted NSCLC, either as monotherapy or combined with chemotherapy.13,14,15,16,17 Positive expression of programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1) has been reported in 66.3%–75.8% of PLELC, which rationalizes the implementation of programmed cell death-1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 blockade.11,18 Sporadic case reports have demonstrated the efficacy of PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab or pembrolizumab in advanced PLELC.8,19,20 Narayanan et al. reported a patient with stage IV PLELC responding to a PD-L1 inhibitor, atezolizumab.21 Combining nivolumab with gemcitabine also resulted in stable disease in two PLELC cases.11 A recent retrospective study compared first-line treatment with chemotherapy (n = 49), immunotherapy (n = 7), and chemoimmunotherapy (n = 12) in patients with advanced PLELC and reported a median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 6.9 months, 11.0 months, and 11.8 months in the three groups, respectively. Immunochemotherapy achieved a significantly longer 2-year PFS than chemotherapy (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.38, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.18–0.78, log rank p = 0.007), suggesting it may be a better first-line treatment option for advanced PLELC.22 However, a well-designed prospective clinical trial is warranted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of immunochemotherapy while elucidating optimal therapeutic regimens to inform future treatment optimization.

Since PLELC shares identical histopathologic characteristics with NPC, it is often treated similarly to EBV-positive NPC rather than NSCLC.10 Gemcitabine plus cisplatin (GP) has been demonstrated as a superior chemotherapy backbone in NPC.23 Several double-blind, randomized phase 3 studies have shown that the PD-1 inhibitor combined with GP resulted in PFS benefit compared with GP alone as first-line treatment in recurrent/metastatic NPC. The JUPITER-02 study showed a significant PFS improvement in the toripalimab vs. the placebo arm (mPFS: 11.7 vs. 8.0 months, HR = 0.52, 95% CI, 0.36–0.74, p = 0.0003).24 The CAPTAIN-1st study revealed an mPFS of 9.7 months in the camrelizumab group compared with 6.9 months in the placebo group (HR = 0.54, 95% CI, 0.39–0.76, p = 0.0002).25 The RATIONALE-309 study also demonstrated longer PFS in the tislelizumab vs. the placebo arm (mPFS: 9.6 vs. 7.4 months; HR = 0.50, 95% CI, 0.37–0.68).26 The aforementioned evidence indicates that combinational treatment with a PD-1 inhibitor and GP may be a promising therapeutic option for patients with unresectable PLELC.

Sintilimab is a selective anti-PD-1 antibody that inhibits interactions between PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1. Combining sintilimab with GP demonstrated an objective response rate (ORR) of 64.7% and manageable toxicity as first-line therapy for patients with squamous NSCLC in a phase 1b study (NCT02937116).27 Herein, we conducted a prospective, single-arm, phase 2 trial to investigate the efficacy and safety of sintilimab combined with gemcitabine and carboplatin as first-line therapy for locally advanced or metastatic PLELC and explore potential predictive biomarkers.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between April 2020 and November 2021, 45 patients were screened for eligibility, of whom 30 were eventually enrolled and received study treatment (Figure S1). At the data cutoff in August 2024, the median follow-up time was 42.4 months (95% CI, 39.8–52.4). Among the 30 intent-to-treat (ITT) patients, all patients (100.0%) had completed all assigned treatment cycles, and twenty patients (66.7%) were in a long-time follow-up at the data cutoff. Of 30 ITT patients, two (6.7%) discontinued treatment after four cycles of immunochemotherapy due to treatment-unrelated death, five (16.7%) remained on immunotherapy for two years, and the remaining 23 patients (76.7%) received at least one cycle of sintilimab maintenance therapy. Of these 23 patients, four patients (17.4%) discontinued immunotherapy for more than 12 weeks, surpassing the maximum allowable withdrawal period; one patient (4.3%) experienced pregnancy during treatment; three patients (13.0%) underwent grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), and the remaining 15 patients underwent disease progression. During the treatment period, death occurred in 14 patients. Eight of these patients died due to disease progression, two succumbed to sudden death from accidental falls, one patient died as a result of cerebrovascular disease, one patient died due to a systemic infection caused by pressure ulcers, one patient’s death was associated with exacerbated conditions from COVID-19 infection, and one patient died due to tumor rupture. Ultimately, 30 patients were eligible for efficacy and safety assessments. The median age was 55 (range, 35–72). Most patients were female (n = 21, 70.0%) and non-smokers (n = 26, 86.7%). The majority had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) score of 1 (n = 26, 86.7%) and were presented with stage IV diseases (n = 22, 73.3%) (Table 1). Efficacy

Of the 30 efficacy-evaluable patients, 22 achieved confirmed partial response (PR), and 8 reached stable disease (SD), resulting in an ORR of 73.3% (95% CI, 54.1–87.7) and a disease control rate (DCR) of 100.0% (95% CI, 88.4–100.0) (Table S1). The median time to response was 3.8 months (95% CI, 2.3–5.1) (Figure 1A). Twenty-eight (93.3%) patients had shrinkage on the target lesion from baseline (Figure 1B). Twenty-six (86.7%) patients developed disease progression (PD) or died at the data cutoff. The median PFS was 13.5 months (95% CI, 12.2–24.5), and the 1-year, 2-year, and 3-year PFS rates were 70.0% (95% CI, 55.4–88.5), 30.0% (95% CI, 17.4–51.8), and 16.7% (95% CI, 7.5–37.1), respectively (Figure 1C). Fourteen OS events were documented. The median OS was 46.9 months (95% CI, 29.5-not reached [NR]), and the 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, and 4-year OS rates were 90.0% (95% CI, 79.9–100.0), 70.0% (95% CI, 55.4–88.5), 60.0% (95% CI, 44.8–80.4), and 42.4% (95% CI, 22.2–80.9), respectively (Figure 1D).Figure 1Efficacy of sintilimab combined with gemcitabine and carboplatin(A) Duration of patients’ responses (n = 30).(B) Best percentage change from baseline in target lesion size (n = 30).(C) The Kaplan-Meier curve of progression-free survival (n = 30).(D) The Kaplan-Meier curve of overall survival (n = 30).PFS, progression-free survival; OS, overall survival; PR, partial response; SD, stable disease; PD, progressive disease; BOR, best of response.

Safety

TRAEs of any grade were observed in 30 patients (100.0%). Anemia (n = 25, 83.3%), neutropenia (n = 25, 83.3%), white blood cell count decrease (n = 22, 73.3%), pruritus (n = 19, 63.3%), fatigue (n = 16, 53.3%), and decreased appetite (n = 15, 50.0%) were the most common AEs (Table 2). Twenty-one (70.0%) patients experienced grade 3 or above TRAEs, including neutropenia (n = 14, 46.7%), anemia (n = 4, 13.3%), thrombocytopenia (n = 4, 13.3%), decreased white blood cell count (n = 3, 10.0%), pruritus (n = 2, 6.7%), aspartate transaminase (AST) elevation (n = 1, 3.3%), immune-mediated pneumonitis (n = 1, 3.3%), dental ulcer (n = 1, 3.3%), elevated phosphocreatine kinase(n = 1, 3.3%), and rash (n = 1, 3.3%). Three patients had ≥ grade 3 TRAEs, which led to treatment discontinuation: immune-mediated pneumonitis (n = 1, 3.3%), anemia (n = 1, 3.3%), and AST elevation (n = 1, 3.3%). Six patients had TRAE leading to dose interruption (n = 5, 16.7%). No treatment-related death was reported. Eighteen (60.0%) patients experienced immune-related adverse events (irAEs) of any grade (Table 3). The most common irAEs were pruritus (n = 7, 23.3%), AST elevation (n = 5, 16.7%), and rash (n = 5, 16.7%). Biomarker analysis

We first explored the association between clinical outcomes and biomarkers generated from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples. DNA sequencing was performed with archived FFPE samples from 28 patients. Due to the low prevalence of single-nucleotide variation (single-nucleotide variant [SNV]) in PLELC, there was generally no relationship between genetic variation and response to sintilimab plus gemcitabine and carboplatin (Figure 2A). The cohort generally had low tumor mutational burden (TMB) with a median 1.00 mutations/Mb (interquartile range [IQR], 1.00–2.99, Table 1). Thus, we did not assess TMB subsequently. PD-L1 expression was determined in 25 out of the 30 patients. The ORR was 66.7%, 60.0%, and 100.0% in patients with PD-L1 expression <1%, 1%–49%, and ≥50%, respectively (Table S2). A total of 24 patients had sufficient FFPE samples that were eligible for RNA sequencing and T cell-inflamed gene-expression profile (GEP) score calculation. By comprehensively assessing the dynamic variation of HRs and p values across all potential thresholds, the optimal cutoff for the GEP score was identified as −0.03. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) analysis using the fixed cutoff showed consistent prognostic separation across folds, with HRs >1 and generally stable p values, supporting the internal validity of the GEP score as a prognostic biomarker (Figure S2). With the best cutoff of −0.03, patients were stratified into two subgroups, and patients with a high GEP score had better PFS than those with a low GEP score (16.3 vs. 7.1 months, p = 0.003; HR = 0.21, 95% CI, 0.07–0.66, Figure 2C). Objective responses were observed in 80.0% (4/5) patients with a low GEP score versus 68.4% (13/19) patients with a high GEP score (Table S2). In the multivariable analysis, the association between GEP scores and PFS remained significant (HR = 0.19, 95% CI, 0.06–0.61, p = 0.005) (Figure 2D).

Since PLELC is usually associated with EBV infection, we explored the association between baseline plasma EBV DNA and clinical outcomes of sintilimab plus gemcitabine and carboplatin. The PFS was shorter in patients with plasma EBV DNA ≥1,500 copies/mL than those with plasma EBV DNA <1,500 copies/mL (HR = 2.30, 95% CI, 1.00–5.30, p = 0.045, Figure 3A). The ORR was 64.3% in patients with a concentration <1,500 copies/mL and 81.3% in those with a concentration ≥1,500 copies/mL (Table S2). The dynamic analysis of the plasma EBV DNA load was performed in 13 patients. It showed a significant decline when the best response was achieved after treatment (p = 0.006) and an increase (p = 0.006) along with PD in all evaluable patients (Figure 3B). These results suggested that EBV DNA concentrations may be used to monitor responses to treatment in patients with PLELC. At last, we analyzed the association between EBV DNA and tumor cell infiltration. Discussion

The standard treatment for locally advanced or metastatic PLELC patients is still lacking. In the present single-arm study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of sintilimab plus GP in PLELC. Sintilimab plus GP showed promising anti-tumor activity with an acceptable safety profile. Exploratory analysis revealed that a higher GEP score was independently associated with longer PFS, and the dynamic change of plasma EBV DNA load may be used to evaluate the efficacy of sintilimab plus GP.

Patients with PLELC are often treated similarly to NPC rather than NSCLC in the clinic. However, since LELC typically expresses squamous cell lineage markers, such as CK5/6, p63, and p40, recently, PLELC has been reclassified as a specific histologic entity of pulmonary squamous cell carcinomas by the 2021 WHO classification.6 We compared the efficacy outcomes between the present study and published studies with similar treatment settings but different cancer types in Table S3. The ORIENT-12 study showed an ORR of 44.7% (95% CI, 37.3%–52.3%) in first-line sintilimab-GP-treated patients with advanced or metastatic squamous NSCLC.28 Sintilimab plus capecitabine had an ORR of 58.2% (95% CI, 52.3%–64.2%) in patients with gastric cancer/gastroesophageal junction cancer.

EBV infection is responsible for 2% of malignancies, including a variety of lymphoid cancers (Hodgkin lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma, etc.) and epithelial cell cancers (NPC and 10% gastric cancer). By comparison, camrelizumab combined with GP demonstrated an ORR of 87.3% (95% CI, 80.5–92.4) and an mPFS of 9.7 months (95% CI, 8.3–11.4) in the same setting of patients with NPC.25 The combination of PD-1 inhibitor and GP has demonstrated similar beneficial clinical outcomes in patients with recurrent or metastatic NPC in several other randomized controlled trial studies.24,25,26 In our study, sintilimab combined with GP chemotherapy achieved similar outcomes with an ORR of 73.3% (95% CI, 54.1–87.7) and a mPFS of 13.5 months (95% CI, 12.2–24.5) in untreated locally advanced or metastatic PLELC, similar to two recent retrospective studies.29,30 The median OS achieved 46.9 months (95% CI, 29.5-NR). The 1-year and 3-year OS rates were 90% (95% CI, 79.9–100) and 60.0% (95% CI, 44.8–80.4), respectively. These results not only showed the promising efficacy of sintilimab plus GP in PLELC but also suggested a similar genomic nature between PLELC and NPC.

Sintilimab plus GP demonstrates a manageable safety profile in our cohort, consistent with previous reports on the same regimen in squamous NSCLC. In a phase 3 trial (ORIENT-12), 86.6% of patients with squamous NSCLC from the sintilimab plus GP group reported ≥ grade 3 AEs, of which decreased neutrophil count (48.6%) and platelet count (45.3%) were the most common ones.28 In our cohort, 70% of patients had ≥ grade 3 TRAEs, and decreased neutrophil count ranked first (46.7%). Other ≥ grade 3 TRAEs occurred in less than 15% of our cohort, less frequent or comparable to those in the ORIENT-12 trial.

Regarding biomarker analysis, 73.3% of our cohort had positive PD-L1 expression, and seven (23.3%) patients showed high expression (TPS ≥50%). This distribution resembles that previously reported in PLELC.4,11,31 PD-L1 expression has been considered a predictive marker for immunotherapy in NSCLC.13,32 However, its predictive significance for immunotherapy plus chemotherapy remains controversial.14,15,17,31 In our study, despite a higher ORR in the subgroup with high PD-L1 expression vs. other subgroups, no significant PFS benefit was observed, which may be partially due to the small number of participants.

Intriguingly, we identified a robust marker, GEP, associated with combined therapy’s efficacy. The GEP signature of 18 genes that are indicative of a T cell-activated tumor microenvironment was initially developed in melanoma to predict response to pembrolizumab and has been validated in pan-cancers.33,34,35 Recent findings from the RATIONALE-309 phase 3 trial in recurrent/metastatic NPC demonstrated that GEP identified immunologically “hot” tumors and an activated dendritic cell signature linked to PFS benefits with tislelizumab-chemotherapy.26 In our study, a higher GEP is independently associated with longer PFS in advanced PLELC patients treated with sintilimab plus GP, indicating GEP as a more sensitive biomarker than PD-L1 expression. The low-GEP subgroup revealed an mPFS (7.1 months) similar to that previously reported in GP-treated advanced PLELC patients (8.8 months). These results suggested that patients with a high GEP score may benefit more from chemoimmunotherapy.

Since PLELC is associated with EBV infection, we further explored the predictive values of baseline and dynamic change of plasma EBV DNA concentration. At baseline, patients with a low EBV DNA concentration had a better PFS than those with a high EBV DNA concentration. Concurrently, the low EBV DNA group showed significantly elevated infiltration of tumor PD1+/CD8+PD1+/CD3+PD1+ cells, a phenotype linked to a favorable prognosis.36,37 This may be partly explained by the fact that a low EBV DNA concentration is associated with tumor infiltration of immune cells and a better prognosis. A recent study demonstrates that longitudinal monitoring of EBV DNA dynamics enables real-time prediction of NPC recurrence risk.38 In our study, the plasma EBV DNA load declined first and then increased in all 13 evaluable patients, corresponding to benefit from treatment and disease progression, respectively. This observation suggests that the change of plasma EBV DNA may serve as a potential non-invasive biomarker for monitoring treatment efficacy and disease progression in patients with advanced PLELC. However, as multiple biomarkers are explored, the predictive significance of the GEP score and the dynamic change of plasma EBV DNA load need to be taken with caution. In addition, further validation studies are needed before clinical application.

Limitations of the study

This study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample size and single-arm design may constrain the generalizability of the findings. This limitation could account for the absence of a significant correlation between PD-L1 expression and therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, the predictive value of the GEP score and the dynamic changes in plasma EBV DNA load require validation through larger, randomized studies before they can be integrated into clinical decision-making processes. The mechanistic relationships and causal links between these indicators and therapeutic efficacy remain unelucidated. Lastly, the lack of long-term survival data impedes the assessment of sustained clinical impact and hinders further analysis of survival outcomes stratified by the variables identified in this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we presented a prospective clinical trial to assess the efficacy and safety of treatment in PLELC. Sintilimab, in combination with gemcitabine and carboplatin, revealed an encouraging anti-tumor activity and acceptable safety and may serve as a first-line treatment in patients with advanced PLELC. Furthermore, the GEP score is a potential predictive biomarker for chemoimmunotherapy, and the dynamic change of EBV DNA load has the potential to monitor treatment efficacy in this clinical setting.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and participants

In this multicenter, single-arm, phase II trial (NCT04312204), eligible patients were adult patients (age between 18 and 75 years), male or female, with histologically confirmed primary PLELC with positive EBV, stage IIIB to IV (according to the eighth edition of TNM classification by the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer Staging project), who had at least one measurable lesion meeting the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1) and had not received previous systemic therapy. Patients must have an ECOG PS score of 0 or 1, adequate hepatic, renal, hematological, and blood coagulative function, and a life expectancy of at least three months. Patients were excluded if they had tumors eligible for curative treatments, including surgery, radiotherapy, or chemoradiotherapy. Receiving other drugs within four weeks before initiating the first drug or having any active or a history of autoimmune diseases or congenital or acquired immune deficiency were also not allowed. The complete eligibility criteria are provided in the Data S1/Methods S1.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Guangzhou, China, No. 2020-5) and done following the Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Method details

Procedures

Eligible patients received four cycles of sintilimab 200 mg (3mg/kg when the weight <40 kg) intravenously (IV) on day one every three weeks (Q3W) combined with gemcitabine (1000 mg/m2, IV, on day one and day 8, Q3W) and carboplatin (AUC 5 mg/mL/min, IV, on day 1, Q3W). Patients would continue maintenance treatment with sintilimab monotherapy 200 mg if no progression disease (PD) after combination treatment was observed, Q3W until disease progression, intolerable toxicity, death, start of new treatment, or withdrawal of consent. Sintilimab could be administered for up to 24 months.

Investigator evaluated tumor responses per RECIST v1.1 using computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging at baseline, the 6th week (±7 days), the 12th week (±7 days), and every nine weeks (±7 days) after the 12th week until 30 days after the last dose of sintilimab, and every 12 weeks (±7 days) after that until the occurrence of disease progression, death, or the initiation of a new anti-cancer therapy. Complete response (CR) and PR were confirmed by repeated imaging assessment at least four weeks after documenting an initial response.

AEs were assessed according to common terminology criteria for AEs version 5.0 (CTCAE 5.0) from the day the patient signed the informed consent form until 30 days after the last dose of sintilimab.

Immune cell profiling by multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

In this study, mIHC analysis was conducted on specimens obtained from eligible patients (n = 23), followed by a comparative analysis of immune cell infiltration between cohorts stratified by plasma EBV DNA levels (≥1500 vs. <1500 copies/mL). Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) was conducted on 5-μm FFPE tumor tissue sections using the PANO 7-plex IHC Kit (10004100100, Panovue, Beijing, China). The protocol included antigen retrieval, blocking, sequential staining with primary antibodies, and tyramide signal amplification. Two antibody panels were employed to characterize the tumor immune microenvironment: one focused on PD-L1, PD-1, CD3, CD8, and Pan-CK; the other on CD68, CD163, CD56, and Pan-CK. After staining, slides were counterstained with DAPI for nuclear visualization. Multispectral images were captured using the Mantra Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (PerkinElmer, USA), and spectral unmixing was performed using a pre-established reference library to distinguish true signals from autofluorescence. Image analysis was performed with inForm software, allowing quantification of immune cell markers in both the tumor parenchyma and stroma. Cell density (cells/mm2) and positive cell proportion were calculated for CD3+ T cells, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, NK cells, and M1/M2 macrophages based on marker combinations (e.g., PD-1, PD-L1, CD68, CD163, CD56).

Biomarker analysis

Exploratory biomarker analysis was performed with blood and FFPE tumor samples. Blood samples were collected at baseline, at the observation of best response, and at the time of disease progression, which were used for quantifying plasma EBV DNA concentration using the EB virus DNA fluorescent quantitative PCR kit (DA0150/DA0153, Daan Gene, Guangzhou, China) by the site lab. A concentration of 1500 copies/ml was chosen as the cut-off according to a previous study in NPC.39 PD-L1 evaluation, DNA sequencing, and RNA sequencing were performed with archived FFPE samples. PD-L1 expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry 22C3 pharmDx assay (Agilent Technologies) and measured using TPS, defined as the percentage of PD-L1 stained viable tumor cells at any intensity. TPS>1% was considered as PD-L1 positivity.

DNA-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed using a 520-gene panel (OncoScreenPlus, Burning Rock Biotech, Guangzhou, China) as previously described.40,41 Sequencing data in FASTQ format were mapped to the reference human genome (hg19) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner v.0.7.10.42 Local alignment optimization, duplication marking, and variant calling were performed using the Genome Analysis Tool Kit v.3.2 and VarScan v.2.4.3.43,44 Variants were filtered using the VarScan fpfilter pipeline, and loci with a depth <100 were filtered out. Variants with population frequencies over 0.1% in the ExAC, 1000 Genomes, dbSNP, or ESP6500SI-V2 databases were grouped as single-nucleotide polymorphisms and excluded from further analysis. The remaining variants were annotated with ANNOVAR (2016-02-01 release) and SnpEff v.3.6.45,46

TMB was calculated for a given sample as the ratio between the number of detected somatic mutations with the total size (1.003 Mb) of the coding region of the 520 panel. The mutation count included non-synonymous SNVs and small insertion-deletion variants (Indels) detected within the coding region and ±2bp upstream or downstream region and does not include hot mutation events, copy number variants (CNVs), structural variants, and germline SNPs. Only mutations with allelic fraction (AF) of ≥2% were included in the mutation count. For accurate TMB calculation, maximum AF (maxAF) should be ≥5% for tissue samples. The upper quartile of TMB (3 mutations/Mb) was set as the cut-off.

RNA was extracted from FFPE samples using an All-prep DNA/RNA FFPE kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). After strand-specific cDNA synthesis, dA-tailing, unique molecular identifier (UMI) adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification, the products were hybridized with the capture probe of the OncoRNA panel (Burning Rock Biotech, Guangzhou, China). The prepared NGS libraries were sequenced with a threshold of >25 million reads set per sample. After deduplication and removing UMI from the sequence header, adaptors and low-quality reads were removed using Trimmomaticv 0.36. The cleaned reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) by STAR(2.7.3a).

The T-cell-inflamed Gene expression profiling (GEP) score was calculated as a weighted sum of normalized expression levels for 18 T-cell-inflamed genes, including CCL5, CD27, CD274 (PD-L1), CD276 (B7-H3), CD8A, CMKLR1, CXCL9, CXCR6, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DRB1, HLA-E, IDO1, LAG3, NKG7, PDCD1LG2 (PDL2), PSMB10, STAT1, and TIGTT.33 Briefly, GEP scores were computed by first normalizing the TPM+1 by subtracting the average of the log10(TPM+1) of the house-keeping genes (STK11IP, ZBTB34, TBC1D10B, OAZ1, POLR2A, G6PD, ABCF1, C14orf102, UBB, TBP, SDHA) from the log10(TPM+1) of each of the predictor genes, and then a weighted sum of the normalized predictor gene values was calculated using the weights for each of the 18 genes (CCL5 = 0.008346;CD27 = 0.072293;CD274 = 0.042853;CD276 = 0.0239;CD8A = 0.031021;CMKLR1 = 0.151253;CXCL9 = 0.074135;CXCR6 = 0.004313;HLA.DQA1 = 0.020091;HLA.DRB1 = 0.058806;HLA.E = 0.07175;IDO1 = 0.060679;LAG3 = 0.123895;NKG7 = 0.075524;PDCD1LG2 = 0.003734;PSMB10 = 0.032999;STAT1 = 0.250229;TIGIT = 0.084767).

The optimal cutoff value for the GEP score was determined by comprehensively evaluating the dynamic changes in HRs and p-values across all potential thresholds. To assess the internal robustness of the selected GEP score cutoff, we conducted leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). In each iteration, one patient was excluded, and the remaining dataset was used to fit a Cox proportional hazards model using the fixed cutoff. The HR and p-value for GEP group were recorded for each fold. Consistency across folds was used as a measure of robustness.

Outcomes

Primary endpoints included the investigator-assessed ORR, defined as the proportion of patients with investigator-assessed CR or PR according to RECIST v1.1, and safety. Secondary endpoints included PFS (time from the first dose to PD or death from any cause, whichever occurred first), OS (time from the first dose to death from any cause), and DCR (the proportion of patients with a confirmed CR, PR, or stable disease [SD]). The exploratory endpoint was predictive biomarkers, including PD-L1 expression, TMB, GEP score, and EBV DNA concentration.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The sample size was determined based on CI estimation for a single proportion using the Clopper-Pearson method. Assuming the ORR is 70% in patients treated with PD-1 inhibitor combined with GP, a sample size of 28 was required to produce a two-sided 95% CI with a half-width equal to 18%. We eventually enrolled 30 patients.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 3.4.1. The 95% CI for ORR and DCR was calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. ORR was compared between subgroups using Fisher’s exact test. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier analysis. The difference in survival between subgroups was assessed using the log rank test. HR and associated 95% CI were calculated using a Cox proportional-hazards model. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to analyze paired measurements of EBV-DNA concentration, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for unpaired comparisons between the two groups. Patient characteristics and safety assessments were summarized with descriptive statistics. All tests were two-tailed, and the significance level was set at α < 0.05.
