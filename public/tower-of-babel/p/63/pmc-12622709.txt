Lifestyle factors and rotator cuff injury: A Mendelian randomization analysis of smoking, alcohol, and coffee consumption
Authors: Chao Li, Jie Yin, Yifan Zhou, Diping Cao, Bingli Liu, Baoqing Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The causal relationship between some lifestyles and rotator cuff injury is unclear. This study aims to elucidate the genetic causality between smoking initiation, alcohol intake frequency, and coffee consumption in relation to rotator cuff injury. We conducted a 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis utilizing summary-level data from extensive genome-wide association studies. Single nucleotide polymorphisms achieving genome-wide significance (P < 5 * 10‐8) were employed as instrumental variables for each exposure. The inverse variance weighted method served as the principal statistical technique, complemented by the weighted median, MR-Egger regression, and MR-PRESSO methods for sensitivity analyses, accommodating some of the assumptions inherent in IVs. The initiation of smoking was found to have a genetically predictive inverse association with rotator cuff injury incidence. The inverse variance weighted approach indicated a pooled odds ratio for rotator cuff injury of 0.70 (95% confidence interval 0.58–0.85; P = 3 * 10‐4) per standard deviation increase in smoking initiation rates. Similarly, coffee consumption demonstrated a negative genetic causality with rotator cuff injury, evidenced by an odds ratio of 0.46 (95% confidence interval 0.29–0.72; P = 8 * 10‐4). Conversely, alcohol consumption did not show a statistically significant causal relationship with rotator cuff injury incidence. The findings suggest a genetic predisposition that links smoking initiation and coffee consumption with a decreased risk of rotator cuff injury. Conversely, the genetic evidence to assert a causal relationship between alcohol intake and rotator cuff injury was found to be inconclusive.

1. Introduction

Rotator cuff injuries are prevalent in sports medicine and are a leading cause of shoulder pain among patients. As the population ages and the shoulder is subjected to increased strain in both sports and occupational activities, the prevalence of rotator cuff injuries has seen an uptick. Epidemiological studies indicate that the incidence among adults is 20%, with a striking 54% noted in individuals over the age of 60.[1] Untreated rotator cuff injuries can progress to shoulder instability or secondary joint contracture, culminating in joint dysfunction that profoundly impacts quality of life and imposes a significant burden on families and society. Timely and accurate diagnosis, followed by early repair of rotator cuff tears, is crucial for favorable outcomes. The pathogenesis of rotator cuff injuries, while not completely understood, is thought to be multifactorial, with age-related degeneration, chronic strain, trauma, compromised vascularization, and genetic factors being implicated.[2–5]

Lifestyle factors, including smoking, alcohol, and caffeine consumption, have been recognized as potential risk factors for the development of rotator cuff injuries. However, the data regarding the correlation between these factors and injury risk is inconsistent, with the causal relationship obscured by confounding variables and the possibility of reverse causality. Additionally, ethical considerations constrain the scope of high-quality randomized controlled trials, as prolonged exposure to known risk factors could result in ancillary health damages, contravening research ethics.

In recent years, the advent of genomic epidemiology and the application of Mendelian randomization (MR) have advanced medical research significantly. MR utilizes single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) closely associated with exposure factors as instrumental variables to deduce causal relationships between exposures and health outcomes.[6] This method addresses the limitations of traditional observational studies, which are often hampered by confounding and reverse causality, impeding their capacity for causal determination. The MR approach, through its methodological advantages, minimizes the impact of confounding biases and has emerged as a robust tool for examining the links between exposure factors and disease onset.[7,8] This study employs a 2-sample MR analysis to investigate the causal relationships among smoking, alcohol, and coffee consumption, and the etiology of rotator cuff injuries. It aims to uncover genetic markers that could illuminate the risk factors contributing to both the development and progression of rotator cuff injuries, thereby providing a novel foundation for enhancing preventative and therapeutic strategies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study overview

Published SNPs from the genome-wide association studies (GWAS) database served as instrumental variables in this Mendelian randomization study to evaluate the impacts of smoking, alcohol, and coffee consumption on the risk of rotator cuff injury. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was primarily utilized to deduce the causal relationship between these exposures and the injury. For the MR analysis to be valid, 3 fundamental assumptions must be satisfied: First, the selected SNPs should exhibit a significant association with the exposures in question—namely, smoking, alcohol consumption frequency, and coffee intake. Second, the SNPs must not be related to any potential confounders that could influence the association between the exposures and the rotator cuff injury. Lastly, the SNPs should not be directly associated with the injury itself, ensuring that any causal inference drawn is exclusively through the intermediation of the exposure factors. Institutional review board approval was not required for this work as the data used in this research are publicly available and were obtained with corresponding ethical approval.

2.2. Data source for rotator cuff injury

The summary dataset for the associations between SNPs and rotator cuff injury was retrieved by searching GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST90044700 on the website https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/ (accessed on March 10, 2024). This GWAS is the latest 1 regarding rotator cuff injury and was updated in 2020. It includes 5701 cases with rotator cuff injury and 406,310 controls, with a number of 16,110,542 SNPs being tested. All the participants are from European ancestry.

2.3. Data source for exposures

In the context of smoking initiation, 93 SNPs were selected as instrumental variables (IVs) following a recent meta-analysis of GWAS encompassing 311,629 individuals of European ancestry.[9] Regarding alcohol consumption frequency, 44 SNPs were ascertained from a GWAS meta-analysis involving 336,965 individuals of European descent. Concurrently, we procured a GWAS dataset for coffee consumption, which comprised data from 428,860 individuals, available via the IEU Open GWAS project website. During the data harmonization phase, palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were excluded. Additionally, SNPs not represented in the rotator cuff injury dataset were discarded. Ultimately, a refined selection of 69 SNPs for smoking initiation, 41 SNPs for alcohol consumption frequency, and 32 SNPs for coffee consumption were established as the instrumental variables for this study.

2.4. Mendelian randomization analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 2023.06.2) with the Two-sample MR (0.5.10)[6] and Mendelian Randomization (0.5.10)[10] packages, along with the MR-PRESSO package.[11] P-values were 2-tailed throughout. Odds ratios (ORs) and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for rotator cuff injury were scaled per 1-unit increase in genetically predicted exposure levels to smoking, alcohol intake frequency, and coffee intake. F-statistics for each exposure were computed employing the previously outlined approximation method.[12]

Our primary analytic strategy employed the standard IVW method under a random-effects model, which presumes the validity of each SNP as an instrumental variable (IV). While the IVW method synthesizes Wald ratios and a meta-analytic framework to yield precise association estimations, it remains susceptible to pleiotropic effects of invalid IVs.[13] Variance between genetic variants was assessed via Cochrane Q-statistics.[13]

For sensitivity analysis, we used methods that moderate certain IV presuppositions, including the weighted median, MR-Egger regression, and MR-PRESSO. The weighted median method delivers consistent estimates assuming a majority of the analysis weight is contributed by valid instruments.[12] The MR-Egger regression is instrumental in identifying and adjusting for directional pleiotropy, providing a pleiotropy-corrected causal estimate, albeit at a cost to statistical power.[12] The P-value for the MR-Egger intercept was employed to signal the presence of pleiotropy.[12] The MR-PRESSO methodology is designed to identify outliers through a global test and recalculates an estimate post-outlier elimination.[11] The P-value from the MR-PRESSO distortion test served as a marker for significant deviations in the estimates before and after outlier adjustment.[11]

3. Results

3.1. Effects of smoking initiation on rotator cuff injury

Genetically predicted smoking initiation was inversely correlated with the odds of a rotator cuff injury. However, the MR-Egger regression analysis did not corroborate this association (Table 1). The IVW method yielded a pooled OR of 0.70 (95% CI 0.58–0.85; P = 3 * 10‐4) per standard deviation increase in the prevalence of smoking initiation, a finding echoed by the weighted median method (OR: 0.66, 95% CI 0.49–0.89; P = 5 * 10-3).

3.2. Effects of alcohol intake frequency on rotator cuff injury

Regarding alcohol consumption, the data did not reveal a causal link with rotator cuff injury (Table 1), with pooled ORs standing at 1.15 (95% CI 0.89–1.48; P = .30). The MR-Egger regression analysis indicated no significant evidence of directional pleiotropy for alcohol (P = .28) consumption. 3.3. Effects of coffee consumption on rotator cuff injury

A negative genetic causality was observed between coffee intake and rotator cuff injury, with an OR of 0.46 (95% CI 0.29–0.72; P = 8 * 10‐4). The MR-Egger regression analysis indicated no significant evidence of directional pleiotropy for coffee (P = .76) consumption. 4. Discussion

While various factors contributing to rotator cuff injury development have been identified, the specific etiology and pathogenesis remain elusive, and treatment protocols continue to be debated. Comprehensive insights into rotator cuff injuries are essential for devising effective preventative and therapeutic strategies. Advances in genetic genomics and bioinformatics have sharpened the precision of genetic variation measurement, curbing bias from measurement errors and bolstering MR’s application in medical research.[14] Consequently, disease-related SNPs, curated from GWAS data as instrumental variables, facilitate the assessment of associations between exposures and outcomes. MR’s robustness, stemming from large sample sizes and environmental non-susceptibility, provides association results with greater validity than observational studies or even randomized controlled trials.[15] This pioneering MR analysis investigates the correlation between smoking, alcohol, and coffee intake with rotator cuff injury, suggesting that smoking initiation and coffee consumption may be inversely related to injury risk, whereas alcohol intake demonstrates no significant association.

The relationship between smoking status and rotator cuff disease is inconsistently reported in existing literature.[16] Clinical studies examining the impact of smoking on patient-reported symptoms and shoulder dysfunction suggest that men who currently smoke or have a history of smoking for more than 20 pack-years, and women who have smoked for 10 to 20 pack-years, are more likely to experience unilateral and bilateral shoulder pain. However, smoking does not appear to correlate with chronic rotator cuff tendinitis.[16] Conversely, Mallon et al[17] found that smoking negatively impacts patients with confirmed rotator cuff tears. Provider-reported outcomes on smoking and rotator cuff disease offer varied conclusions. Fehringer et al[18] did not observe a significant statistical association between smoking and increased prevalence of rotator cuff injuries in a sample of 200 shoulders from 104 patients aged 65 years. Titchener et al[19] reported no association between current smoking status and rotator cuff disease diagnoses; however, a history of smoking was linked to rotator cuff disease. This association ceased to exist after accounting for the general practice consultation rate per year. Studies examining the relationship between smoking and the degree of rotator cuff pathology, including research by Carbone et al,[20] Kane et al,[21] Kukkonen et al,[22] and Lundgreen et al,[23] identified a connection between smoking and an increased severity of rotator cuff pathology. Their findings suggest that smokers are more likely to have larger tears, more advanced fibrous degeneration, and a higher incidence of tears at a younger age. These observations are clinically relevant, considering that larger rotator cuff tears are more challenging to treat and are associated with higher rates of retear post-repair.[24,25] Anatomical and histological evidence indicates a region of relative hypovascularity within the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons, proximal to their insertion on the greater tuberosity.[26,27] Smoking, through its nicotine-induced hypoxic effect, may contribute to tendon degeneration by restricting blood flow to this area,[28] creating a hypoxic environment that could impair metabolism and diminish the healing response. Taku Hatta et al[29] concluded in in vitro studies that nicotine exposure significantly reduced the expression of MMP-9 and TIMP-3 genes, and the enzymatic activity of MMP-9 also decreased in a dose-dependent manner due to nicotine exposure. These mechanisms may affect the mechanical properties of the extracellular matrix of rotator cuff tendon cells. In addition, a study by Ichinose et al[30] found that tendons exposed to nicotine had a higher elastic modulus than the control group. This may increase tendon stiffness by increasing collagen synthesis and reducing the production of matrix metalloproteinases, and tendons with higher stiffness also have a correspondingly higher tear rate. In contrast to previous studies, our findings suggest that initial smoking may reduce the risk of rotator cuff injuries, possibly due to the lower frequency and dosage characteristic of initial smoking phases.

The association between alcohol consumption and rotator cuff injury risk is also debated. A prior systematic review found no evidence of a positive correlation between alcohol consumption and rotator cuff injury.[31] However, a case-control study comprising 249 consecutive patients with rotator cuff injuries revealed that these patients consumed more alcohol daily and over a longer period compared to healthy controls. Daily consumption exceeding 3 alcohol units for men and 2 alcohol units for women was identified as a significant risk factor for rotator cuff injuries. Additionally, consuming alcohol in excess of recommended levels was significantly associated with tendon damage and tear severity.[32] The supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons possess a hypovascularized section approximately 15 mm long at their insertion on the greater tuberosity.[33] Factors that can adversely affect capillary microcirculation, such as arterial hypertension, cardiopulmonary disease, obesity, and hypercholesterolemia, may lead to tendon degeneration and rupture.[34] Habitual high-dose ethanol consumption has been shown to negatively impact capillary microcirculation and tissue perfusion in both human and animal studies.[35,36] Given its pervasive influence on both the musculoskeletal system and other bodily systems, the complex impact of alcohol consumption may explain its ambiguous association with rotator cuff injury risk. Although the current MR study did not find a causal link between alcohol consumption and rotator cuff injury risk, the potential for a weak association that has been overlooked cannot be discounted.

No prior study has reported a causal link between coffee consumption and the risk of rotator cuff injury. Ojeh et al[37] discovered that topical caffeine impedes the epithelialization of damaged tissue, slows down the proliferation of keratinocytes, and retards the migration of keratinocytes to the site of injury. Donejko et al[38] found that caffeine suppresses collagen synthesis in fibroblasts by inhibiting DNA biosynthesis and the expression of β1-integrin and insulin-like growth factor receptors, both of which are essential for tissue healing. Coffee’s protective effect could relate to caffeine-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling via adenosine receptors, aligning with prior findings on its antioxidant properties. These molecular mechanisms support the causal interpretations from our MR analysis. This study is the first MR investigation to indicate a protective effect of coffee consumption on rotator cuff injury risk, proposing coffee intake as a beneficial factor.

It is known that the current study is the first MR study to assess the impact of smoking, alcohol and coffee consumption on the risk of rotator cuff injury, explaining whether there is a causal association between the 3 factors and the risk of rotator cuff injury at the genetic level. however, our study has some limitations. First, the focus of this study was confined to a European population as the MR was performed mainly on a European sample, thereby creating uncertainty regarding the generalizability of the results to populations worldwide. Hence, our results need further validation in diverse populations across various geographical regions. Second, due to the utilization of a publicly available GWAS database, it was unfeasible to assess potential sample overlap. Third, the number of SNPs used in our investigation are limited, and we hope larger GWAS studies will identify more associated SNPs in the future to increase the power of MR. Finally, our study can be further explored to investigate the dosage and frequency of different influencing factors, making the results more directional.

5. Conclusions

In summary, this MR study has unearthed a novel association, indicating that the initiation of smoking and coffee consumption may be linked to a reduced risk of rotator cuff injury. However, no substantial evidence has been found to establish a causal relationship between alcohol intake and rotator cuff injury. Given the current lack of decisive evidence pinpointing the impact of smoking and coffee intake on the risk of rotator cuff injury, we contend that these findings warrant further investigation. Subsequent research should continue to probe the complex interplay between these factors and rotator cuff injury to elucidate the potential mechanisms at play.
