Non-destructive prediction of nitrogen, iron and zinc content in diverse common bean seeds from a genebank using near-infrared spectroscopy
Authors: Tatiana Garcia, Mónica Carvajal-Yepes, Darren T. Drewry, Luis Guillermo Santos, Jorge Duitama, Peter Wenzl, Maria Isabel Chacón-Sánchez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is the world's most important legume crop and a vital staple food for millions of people in Latin America and Africa. Given the increasing trend in bean consumption and its importance for nutrition and food security in these regions, there is an urgent need to enhance common bean seeds' nutritional value through breeding. This requires rapidly assessing large and diverse germplasm collections to uncover key nutritional traits in the available genetic diversity. To address this challenge, Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) offers a large-scale, cost-effective and non-destructive approach for accurately predicting nutrient content in intact common bean seeds. This study describes the development of predictive models based on NIRS to predict nitrogen (N), iron (Fe) and zinc (Zn) content, using whole common bean seeds from a germplasm core collection held at the International Center for Tropical Agriculture. Spectra were captured for 1754 accessions (wild and domesticated), and reference values for N, Fe, and Zn content were measured with conventional destructive methods in a panel of 401 accessions. Prediction models of N content achieved a concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) of 0.84, while for Fe and Zn, CCC was 0.4. NIRS quantification detected higher N content in wild accessions than in domesticated accessions. These results demonstrate that NIRS can effectively estimate the N content of common bean seeds in a non-destructive manner, while providing valuable nutritional information to enhance access to large genebank collections for bean breeding.

Introduction

Common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is one of the world's most consumed legumes, providing protein, vitamins, minerals, starch, and dietary fiber (Broughton et al., 2003; Kotue et al., 2018; Lindorff-Larsen, Røgen, Paci, Vendruscolo, & Dobson, 2005). Recognized by the United Nations' Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) as a key food in dietary guidelines (Comerford et al., 2021), beans contribute to reducing the risk of heart disease, obesity, anemia, and cancer (Mojica, Luna-Vital, & González de Mejía, 2016; Fan & Beta, 2016; Gomes, Zielinski, Wojeicchowski, Nogueira, & Demiate, 2018). In 2023, global common bean production reached 28.5 million tons harvested from 36.7 million hectares (FAO, 2024), highlighting their role in providing global food security. At worldwide, 3.1 billion people cannot afford a healthy diet, and face protein and micronutrient deficiencies, notably iron (Fe) and zinc (Zn) (FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP and WHO, 2022). Iron deficiency—the main cause of anemia worldwide—affects nearly one-third of the global population (Elstrott et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2021), while Zn deficiency compromises immunity, growth, and reproduction (Zoroddu et al., 2019). Moreover, nitrogen (N) is a key component of amino acids, which are essential building blocks of proteins (Tomé & Bos, 2000).

With rising global bean consumption, enhancing the nutritional content of bean germplasm cultivated in low-income countries in Latin America and Africa could strengthen global nutrition outcomes. Rapidly and effectively assessing the genetic diversity in germplasm collections is key to identifying material for nutritionally targeted breeding programs. The International Center for Tropical Agriculture (CIAT) maintains the world's largest Phaseolus collection, with over 38,000 accessions. This extensive collection includes 30,571 cultivated and 1804 wild and weedy accessions of common bean. The remaining accessions correspond to other bean types such as runner bean, Lima bean, tepary bean, among others (Debouck, Santaella, & Santos, 2021). In 1995, CIAT established a P. vulgaris core collection of 1420 domesticated accessions to capture the genetic and phenotypic diversity of the complete collection (Tohme, Jones, Beebe, & Iwanaga, 1995). The composition of the core collection was based on multiple factors, including germplasm sourced from traditional bean growing areas, agroecological origin, and morphological characteristics such as growth habit, seed color and size, among others. However, the seeds' nutritional traits were not considered in establishing the core collection. Subsequent studies partially characterized the nutritional profile of common bean accessions, evaluating nutrients and anti-nutrients with conventional methods such as inductively coupled plasma (ICP) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Beebe, 2012; Beebe, Gonzalez, & Rengifo, 2000; Blair, 2013; Caproni, Raggi, Talsma, et al., 2020; Islam et al., 2001; Islam, Basford, Jara, Redden, & Beebe, 2002). These studies revealed high genetic variability in protein and mineral content across the two major gene pools (Mesoamerican and Andean), between wild and domesticated beans, and among races such as Chile, Jalisco, and Durango (Guzmán-Maldonado, Acosta-Gallegos, & Paredes-López, 2000; McClean et al., 2017; Paredes, Becerra, & Tay, 2009;). Overall, Mesoamerican beans showed lower phaseolin and Fe, but higher calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), sulfur (S), and Zn content, compared to Andean beans, with the highest Fe levels detected in the Northern Andean gene pool (Beebe et al., 2000; Islam et al., 2001).

Previous studies screened only a small fraction of CIAT's bean collection, because traditional nutrient phenotyping relies on costly, labor-intensive, and destructive biochemical assays. Such methods are particularly unsuitable for large genebank screenings and accessions with complex regeneration cycles, as they reduce the seed stocks needed for conservation and distribution. A promising alternative is near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), a rapid, non-destructive screening method that detects the unique absorption of molecule bonds (e.g., carbon‑hydrogen (C—H), single carbon‑carbon bonds (CC), carbon‑carbon double bond (C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="20.666667pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 20.666667 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.019444,-0.019444)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> C), hydroxyl group (O—H), and nitrogen‑hydrogen (N—H)) in the 700–2500 nm range, enabling large-scale characterization of seeds' nutritional and other traits. NIRS interactions with molecular bonds produce characteristic peaks that can be analyzed using multivariate methods (Prananto, Minasny, & Weaver, 2020). This method is advantageous because it is non-destructive, allows rapid data acquisition, easy sample preparation, and is environmentally friendly since no chemical reagents are needed. However, NIRS requires reference data from conventional analytical methods for accurate quantitative analysis (Sohn et al., 2021).

NIRS has been widely applied in plants to identify nutritional profiles, seed quality traits, and impact of disease on legumes (Acuña-Gutiérrez, Schock, Jiménez, & Müller, 2021; Hang, Shi, Neufeld, Bett, & House, 2022; Puspitasari et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2024). In common bean, partial least-squares (PLS) regressions have been developed to predict the protein content of ground and intact seeds. Ground-seed models show higher accuracy (R2 up to 0.99) (Carbas et al., 2020; Hermida, Rodriguez, & Rodriguez-Otero, 2006; Plans, Simó, Casañas, Sabaté, & Rodriguez-Saona, 2013) than intact-seed models (R2 ∼ 0.84) (Hacisalihoglu, Larbi, & Settles, 2010), largely due to overtones from N—H, C—H, and carbon‑oxygen (CO) bonds that reflect N content, essential for protein formation (Soriano, Alvarado-López, Zúñiga-Sánchez, Orozco-Segovia, & Gamboa-deBuen, 2015). However, PLS regressions have been less successful for micronutrient detection; Vranić et al. (2019) reported low correlations (0.31 and 0.39) for Fe and Zn, respectively in Croatian common bean landraces. In another study, Wafula et al. (2022) evaluated wavelength selection and PLS-R models to predict Fe, Zn, phytates (Phy), tannins (Tan), and their molar ratios (e.g., Phy:Fe, Phy:Zn, Tan:Fe and Phy + Tan:Fe) from ground seeds. The best models predicted Phy:Zn, Tan:Fe, and Phy + Tan:Fe with coefficients of determination above 0.75. These predictions rely on indirect estimation, as the NIRS detects minerals binding to organic compounds through overtones of O—H and C—H bonds (Cozzolino & Moron, 2004).

Research indicates that NIRS is a promising tool for predicting seed N content, from ground or intact seeds, as shown in chickpea (Padhi et al., 2022). It also offers a cost-effective phenotyping approach for advancing foodomics (Ahmed et al., 2022; Cozzolino, 2015). For Fe and Zn, however, studies have relied on ground seeds only, so NIRS' potential to predict these minerals in intact, diverse germplasm still needs evaluating. NIRS offers great potential to accelerate seed nutritional characterization and facilitate the identification of superior accessions for breeding programs. Therefore, it is essential to assess NIRS' usefulness on intact, common bean seeds for predicting key nutritional traits in large genebank collections. In this study, we reported a large-scale NIRS screening of the CIAT common bean core collection, including long-term stored seeds, in a non-destructive manner. We also generated new biochemical measurements of hundreds of accessions to provide baseline data for model development. Using both datasets, we evaluated predictive models for seed N, Fe and Zn content to generate valuable nutritional information that will enhance the use of germplasm in large genebank collections for bean breeding.

Materials and methods

A protocol for high-throughput screening of seed samples in genebanks

We developed and applied a non-destructive protocol to capture spectral profiles of common bean accessions exhibiting wide variation in seed size, shape and color (Supplementary File 1). Because the samples originated from seeds maintained under long-term storage, the protocol was designed to address conservation constraints, including stable moisture content and temperature control, while ensuring the quality, viability and conservation of the germplasm (FAO, 2022; Offord & Makinson, 2009).

The protocol consisted of four steps. In the first step, seeds from CIAT's genebank—stored in plastic-lined/poly-lined, aluminum bags in cold rooms at −18 °C—were placed in cold rooms at 10 °C for 24 h to defrost. Second, the bags were then placed in a stainless steel trolley in a room known as the ‘packing area’, which is maintained at a temperature of 23 °C and 40 % relative humidity. These conditions ensure long-term seed conservation, by minimizing moisture absorption, as seeds need to be maintained at a water content of 4–6 %. Third, seeds were placed in a plastic Petri dish (7 cm diameter), ensuring a uniform seed distribution and that enough seeds are included to reach the top of the Petri dish. A turntable (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, United Kingdom) was used to maintain a constant distance between the seed sample, the optical fiber tip, and the light source, thereby improving measurement consistency. Five spectra were collected per accession as technical replicates with a spectroradiometer ASD FieldSpec 4 Standard-Resolution (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, United Kingdom). Finally, in the fourth step, seed samples were vacuum-packed and returned to the cold rooms for long-term conservation at −20 °C.

Spectra data collection and analysis

As stated in Section 2.1, absolute reflectance data were obtained from intact bean seeds with a spectroradiometer ASD FieldSpec 4 Standard-Resolution (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, United Kingdom), in the spectral range of 350–2500 nm collected at 1 nm intervals. Five spectra per accession were captured and averaged. Using in-house scripts in R v.4.3.3, the standard deviation of absolute reflectance was calculated for both wild and domesticated accessions. Additionally, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. The results were interpreted based on accession classification by biological status and primary seed color.

The spectral analysis was conducted using the Waves v.0.2.5 R package (Hershberger et al., 2021) and interval partial least squares regression (iPLS) analysis was performed with the mdatools-R library (Kucheryavskiy, 2020) in three steps. First, 13 pretreatment methods (described fully in Supplementary File 1) were evaluated, followed by the calculation of PLS or iPLS. In the second step, for each combination of pretreatment and PLS analysis, the samples were randomly assigned to training (70 %) and testing (30 %) datasets. For each pretreatment and PLS analysis or iPLS, the following metrics were estimated: (1) Root Mean Squared Error of Prediction (RMSEP); (2) squared Pearson's correlation between predicted and observed test set values (R2p); (3) Ratio of standard deviation of observed test set values to RMSEP, known as Ratio of Performance to Deviation (RPD); (4) Ratio of Performance to InterQuartile difference (RPIQ); (5) Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC); (6) average difference between the predicted and observed values (mean bias); (7) Standard Error of Prediction (SEP); (8) Root Mean Squared Error of Cross-Validation (RMSECV); (9) coefficient of multiple determination of cross-validation for PLS models (R2cv); (10) squared Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (R2sp) between predicted and observed test set values; and (11) the best number of components in a PLS model (best.ncomp). Finally, in the third step, the best model for each trait was used to predict the N, Fe and Zn content in intact seeds from CIAT's core common bean collection (1394) and from 360 wild accessions.

Nitrogen quantification

Four grams of whole seeds from 401 accessions were ground in a blade mill (Nidec Motor, St. Louis,MO,USA) for ∼2 min at 1715 rpm. The bean flour was sieved to remove particles of >0.5 mm. Any remaining coarse particles were manually macerated using a mortar and pestle before being sieved again. A 0,1 g sample of flour was used to determine total N content using the Kjeldahl Method, measured with a Skalar San++ segmented flow autoanalyzer (Skalar Analytical B.V., Breda, the Netherlands). One in 25 samples was measured in triplicate. The analyses were conducted at CIAT's Analytical Service Laboratory (Palmira, Colombia). For N quantification with an independent validation dataset, a total of 70 common bean accessions (58 domesticated and 12 wild) were randomly selected based on their predicted N values. In these accessions, N content was measured as described above.

Iron and zinc quantification

Intact seeds from 301 domesticated common bean accessions were manually cleaned to remove impurities, damaged grains, broken grains, and any signs of deterioration. For each accession, 25 g of seeds were washed with deionized water (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), followed by ultrapure 18 MΩ-cm water (MilliQ Merck-Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) to remove possible contaminants from the harvest. The washed seeds were then sieved in a plastic strainer, and excess water was removed using sterile gauze. The seeds were dried at 60 °C for 3 days in a convection oven (Binder, Tuttlingen,Germany). Once dried, seeds were ground using a batch mill (IKA, Staufen, Germany) and then stored at 4 °C in polyethylene containers until further analysis. To determine each accession's Fe and Zn concentrations, we used an energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (XRF-Bruker,-S2 PUMA, Karlsruhe, Germany), as described by Paltridge, Palmer, Milham, Guild, and Stangoulis (2012), applying three replicates per accession. All equipment and utensils used were previously washed with a solution of nitric acid (5 %) and then rinsed with 18 MΩ-cm water (MilliQ Merck-Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The analyses were carried out in CIAT's Nutritional Quality Laboratory in Palmira, Colombia.

Results

Screening the common bean core collection using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)

Supplementary File 1 explains the full protocol used for capturing NIRS data from whole seeds held in CIAT's genebank collection. Spectra—spanning a wavelength range of 350–2500 nm, at 1 nm intervals—were captured for 1754 common bean accessions from the Mesoamerican and Andean gene pools (1394 domesticated and 360 wild) (Supplementary Fig. 1), generating a total of 8770 whole-seed spectra (five spectra per accession). A subset of 401 accessions (301 domesticated and 100 wild) with high variation was selected to be used in the predictive models. This subset represents all the spectral variability observed in the core collection according to F-tests (Supplementary Fig. 2). Absolute reflectance was measured for all samples, showing higher variability in the visible spectrum region (400–700 nm), compared to the infrared region (Fig. 1A). This variation was mainly due to the diversity of seed colors in the domesticated genebank accessions. Standard deviation of absolute reflectance was calculated to compare the spectra of domesticated and wild accessions per wavelength (Fig. 1B). The highest variance for domesticated accessions occurred at 772 nm, while for wild accessions it was at 803 nm. The 700–800 nm region is part of the third and fourth spectral region where the molecular excitation occurs due to non-fundamental molecular vibrations (known as overtone regions) (Burns & Ciurczak, 2007). In this case, C—H and O—H organic molecules absorb energy. The O—H group is a constituent of phenols and carboxylic acids, which are components of both amino and fatty acids (Stuart, 2004; Ciurczak, 2001). A principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out on the spectra generated to identify clusters of accessions or potential sources of variation. Separate analyses were performed for the visible region (350–749 nm), near-infrared region (750–2500 nm), and the complete set of wavelengths sampled (350–2500 nm) (Supplementary Fig. 4). In each analysis, the percentage of contributions per wavelength in the first two principal components was estimated, resulting in a contribution of less than 1 % for each analysis (Supplementary Table 2). Fig. 1 (C-E) shows the first two principal components for each case, explaining 97.4 % of the variance in the visible region, 94.4 % in the near-infrared region, and 88.4 % in the complete spectrum. In the visible region (Fig. 1C), the first principal component differentiates the accessions based on seed color. Accessions with black testa (predominantly wild accessions) are clearly separated from those with white testa. According to this distribution, the large variance observed in the visible region arises from the diversity of primary seed-coat colors across the screened accessions. Similar color-based distributions were also observed in the other two PCAs in the second principal component (Fig. 1D and E).

Estimated reference values for nitrogen, iron and zinc content

Total N content was estimated for 301 domesticated and 100 wild accessions (see Supplementary Table 1). The domesticated and improved common bean variety BIO-101, biofortified for both Fe and Zn, was included in all analyses. Total N content in domesticated accessions ranged from 22.13 g/kg (G8897) to 44.13 g/kg (G14212), with an average of 31.09 g/kg. In wild accessions, total N content oscillated between 26.75 g/kg (G23440) and 42.65 g/kg (G51382), with an average of 33.14 g/kg (Fig. 2A). Statistical analysis of average N content by biological status revealed that the wild accessions have significantly higher N content than domesticated accessions (p-value = 5.509e-07, Wilcoxon test). These differences among wild and domesticated accessions also held when analyses were conducted within gene pools. Highly significant differences between both Andean wild and Andean domesticated accessions (p-value = 1.314e-06, Wilcoxon test) and between Mesoamerican wild and Mesoamerican domesticated accessions (p-value = 0.011, Wilcoxon test) were observed. Five of the top-ten accessions were from Argentina, three from Costa Rica, and one each from Ecuador and Colombia. The biofortified line BIO-101 showed an N content of 34.4 g/kg and was in the upper quartile of the distribution for domesticated accessions. In the wild accessions' gene pool, the N content in 35 wild accessions of the Andean gene pool fluctuated between 27.59 g/kg and 42.65 g/kg, with an average of 34.16 g/kg. While in 65 wild accessions from the Mesoamerican gene pool, it varied between 26.75 g/kg and 38.92 g/kg, with an average of 32.61 g/kg. Significant differences in N content were found between gene pools in wild accessions (p-value = 0.03301, Wilcoxon test).

Of the 300 domesticated accessions evaluated, and considering the phaseolin type and passport data (Gepts & Bliss, 1986; Singh, Gepts, & Debouck, 1991; Toro, Ocampo, & Debouck, 2007), 131 accessions belong to the Andean gene pool and 169 to the Mesoamerican gene pool. Total N content (Supplementary Table 1) in the Andean gene pool ranged between 23.46 g/kg and 40.55 g/kg, with an average of 30.81 g/kg (Fig. 2B). Accession G17112 from Ecuador had the highest N value (40.55 g/kg), while accession G16129 from Peru had the lowest N value with 23.46 g/kg. In the Mesoamerican gene pool, the average N content was 31.3 g/kg, and its distribution varied between 22.13 g/kg (G14212) and 44.13 g/kg (G8897). No significant differences in N content were found between gene pools in domesticated accessions (p-value = 0.3329, Wilcoxon test).

Iron and Zn contents were only estimated in domesticated accessions (Supplementary Fig. 5). The following results therefore focus on comparisons between the two gene pools (Mesoamerican and Andean) and not between biological status. We acknowledge this is a limitation of our study and future research should include wild common bean accessions to establish a reference dataset. Given that the seeds have been stored for an average of 17 years, a correlation analysis between measurements and the seed storage time was performed to assess possible biases caused by the seed storage time. Although the correlations were significant (p-value < 0.0001, Pearson's correlation), the correlation value was relatively low for Zn (−0.31), while it was positive for Fe (0.21).

Iron content in the Andean gene pool ranged between 40.4 mg/kg and 105 mg/kg, with a mean value of 72.2 mg/kg (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 3). Seven of the top-ten accessions were from Peru, and three were from Ecuador. Three of the seven Peruvian accessions belonged to the variety known as popping bean or nuñas. A total of 57 Peruvian accessions were evaluated, with an average Fe content of 75.39 mg/kg (Supplementary Fig. 6). A statistically significant difference in Fe content was found between the Peruvian accessions and the remaining Andean accessions (p-value = 0.03465, Wilcoxon test).

For the Mesoamerican gene pool, the minimum and maximum Fe content was 44.4 mg/kg and 100.4 mg/kg, respectively, with a mean value of 70.39 mg/kg (Fig. 2C). No statistical difference was found for Fe content between the Andean and Mesoamerican gene pools (p-value = 0.1721, Wilcoxon test). The trend of higher Fe content in the Andean accessions of CIAT's core collection was previously reported by Beebe et al. (2000), with values ranging from 34 to 89 mg/kg, with higher Fe content observed in the Peruvian accessions. The mean Fe content for the BIO-101 biofortified line has been reported as 83 mg/kg, measured over nine geographic locations, with minimum and maximum values of 74 and 106 mg/kg, respectively (HarvestPlus, 2021).

Higher Zn content values were observed in accessions from the Mesoamerican gene pool (mainly from Guatemala and Mexico) (Supplementary Fig.7), with a maximum value of 50.7 mg/kg, compared to accessions from the Andean gene pool, which showed a maximum value of 41.3 mg/kg (Supplementary Table 3). The minimum Zn values for both gene pools were around 17 mg/kg. The average Zn content was 32.23 mg/kg and 29.86 mg/kg for the Mesoamerican and Andean gene pools, respectively (Fig. 2D). The mean Zn content for the BIO-101 biofortified line was 44 mg/kg, exceeding the maximum value observed in Andean accessions. A statistically significant difference in Zn content was found between the Andean and Mesoamerican gene pools (p-value = 0.001189, Wilcoxon test). Beebe et al. (2000) evaluated the CIAT's bean core collection and reported an average value of 35 mg/kg. They also reported higher values of Zn in germplasm from Guatemala. In the present study, a total of 20 accessions from Guatemala and 89 accessions from Mexico were evaluated. The mean Zn content found was 32.97 mg/kg and 31.77 mg/kg, respectively, and there was no statistical difference between accessions from Guatemala and the other accessions in the Mesoamerican gene pool (p-value = 0.8897, Wilcoxon test).

Machine learning analysis of NIRS spectra to predict nitrogen, iron and zinc content

We tested different approaches to predict N, Fe and Zn content using NIRS spectra. Since N content measurements were available for both wild and domesticated accessions, three datasets were analyzed according to their biological status (301 domesticated accessions; 100 wild accessions; and both combined, i.e., 301 domesticated +100 wild accessions). For Fe and Zn, only data from domesticated accessions were available. We applied three approaches to evaluate the spectra. In the first approach, the complete spectral range (350–2500 nm) was analyzed. The second approach analyzed only the infrared range (750–2500 nm). In the third approach, specific wavelengths were selected using the forward iPLS as described by Kucheryavskiy (2020) and Hershberger et al. (2021). Initial data and 13 spectral pretreatments were evaluated for each dataset (domesticated, wild, or wild+domesticated) (Supplementary Figs. 8–10) prior to modeling with PLS or iPLS. Additionally, 11 metrics were estimated to identify the best model in each dataset (details in Supplementary Table 4). Table 1 summarizes the best models, according to each trait, wavelength selection approach, and biological status (only for N content).

From the initial dataset, the prediction of N content in domesticated accessions showed slightly higher accuracy when using the infrared range (750–2500 nm) showing a CCC of 0.82 compared to a CCC of 0.8 achieved using the full spectrum range. For the 100 wild accessions, the iPLS wavelength-selection analysis yielded a marked improvement over the full spectrum model, predicting an increase in CCC from 0.7 to 0.8 and a reduction in RMSEP from 2.16 to 1.78. In this case, the iPLS analysis selected four wavelength regions (1171–1205; 1311–1345; 1591–1625 and 2116–2150) (Supplementary Table 5). Finally, for the combined analysis of domesticated and wild accessions, the iPLS model outperformed the domesticated accessions-only model for estimating N content, predicting a CCC of 0.84 compared to 0.82. In this case, iPLS selected 11 wavelength ranges (961–995; 996–1030; 1031–1065; 1,06–61,100; 1206–1240; 1240–1275; 1276–1310; 1311–1345; 1556–1590; 1591–1625 and 1941–1975) (Supplementary Table 5). The RMSEP obtained with the combined dataset (1.99) was slightly higher than that obtained using only the wild accessions model (1.78). This difference could be attributed to the larger sample sizes used for training and testing this model. To further assess the performance of the prediction models for N content, we evaluated the best model for domesticated accessions (Initial data + infrared region) to predict the N content of wild accessions. As shown in Fig. 3B, the model achieved a CCC of 0.8 between predicted and observed values. We also performed the reverse experiment, using the best model built with wild accessions to predict the N content of domesticated accessions. As expected, this experiment yielded a lower correlation coefficient of 0.65 (Fig. 3C). Besides the number of samples, this result can be explained by the lower variance in N content observed among the wild accessions, compared to the domesticated accessions.

For Fe, Savitzky–Golay and first derivative (window size = 5) (SG.d1,w5) (Table 1) pretreatment achieved the best performance using the iPLS approach and six wavelength regions (1311–1345; 1346–1380; 1416–1450; 1556–1590; 1591–1625 and 1976–2010 nm). The iPLS approach improved the predictive model's performance with respect to the whole spectrum, specifically with an increase in CCC from 0.4 to 0.45 and a decrease in the RMSE from 11.72 to 11.07. For Zn, the best results were achieved using initial data in combination with the iPLS model. The iPLS model selected four wavelength regions (996–1030; 1171–1205; 1241–1275 and 1941–1975) and it performed substantially better compared to the whole spectrum, with an increase in CCC from 0.28 to 0.46 and a decrease in RMSE from 5.89 to 5.2. For both minerals, the correlation coefficient between predicted and observed values was 0.5 (Fig. 3D-E).

Informative wavelengths for predicting nitrogen, iron, and zinc content

To identify the most informative wavelength regions for predicting the compounds evaluated, we used the iPLS analysis. In this analysis, the spectra spanning the wavelength range 750–2500 nm were divided into 50 subintervals or wavelength regions. Within each subinterval, 34 consecutive wavelengths were grouped. Each subinterval's contribution to improving the model was evaluated exhaustively and sequentially, until there were no further improvements. As a result, wavelength region combinations were identified for all traits, as shown in Fig. 4F. In the last part of this section (see 3.5), we report the wavelength regions identified for N prediction in both the wild dataset and the wild+domesticated dataset. For the domesticated dataset 10 wavelength regions were identified (1031–1065; 1066–1100; 1171–1205; 1241–1275; 1276–1310; 1311–1345; 1451–1485; 1486–1520; 1591–1625; 1801–1835). Nitrogen in seeds is combined in groups of organic compounds primarily as part of amino acids, which are essential for protein formation (Bos & Tomé, 2000; Soriano et al., 2015; Zeffa et al., 2020). Consequently, N content is likely estimated based on overtones related to N—H, C—H and C0 bonds. The first overtone of N—H bonds oscillates between 1400 and 1600 nm. Subintervals overlapping this overtone were identified using iPLS in the three datasets. The second overtone of N—H occurs at 1150–1200 nm and iPLS identified subintervals overlapping this overtone in the domesticated and wild datasets. The third overtone of N—H is located between 800 and 900 nm and, in this case, none of the subintervals identified by iPLS overlapped this overtone. Finally, one subinterval overlapping in the N—H combination region that occurs between 2100 and 2200 nm was identified only in wild accessions.

Iron and Zn do not exhibit strong overtone signals. However, like N these minerals interact with organic compounds, enabling their indirect estimation through overtones associated with O—H and C—H bonds (Cozzolino & Moron, 2004; Weyer & Lo, 2006). For predicting Fe content in domesticated accessions, six subintervals were identified (Fig. 3F). Of these, three subintervals (1311–1345; 1346–1380; and 1416–1450) corresponded to the absorption region of the first C—H overtone combinations and the first O—H overtone. Of the other three subintervals (1556–1590; 1591–1625; and 1976–2010), the last one overlapped with the absorption of the O—H combination region. For Zn, four subintervals were identified (Fig. 3F). Of these, three subintervals suggested a relationship between Zn and specific overtones: the second O—H overtone (996–1030), the second C—H overtone (1136–1170), and the O—H combination region (1941–1975).

Prediction and validation of nitrogen content in CIAT'S common bean core collection

The best model—based on subintervals (described in the previous section)—was used to predict N content from NIRS spectral data collected for 1092 domesticated and 260 wild accessions (Supplementary Table 6 and 7). The average predicted N content was 30.70 g/kg and 33.66 g/kg for domesticated and wild accessions, respectively (Fig. 4A). These values are similar to the ones reported for domesticated and wild accessions in the reference dataset (31.09 g/kg and 33.14 g/kg, respectively). The Wilcoxon test (p-value < 2.2 × 10−6) indicated a significant difference between biological status (wild higher than domesticated accessions).

For the domesticated gene pool, the maximum predicted N content was 40.44 g/kg. The minimum predicted N content was 21.44 g/kg in accessions from the Andean gene pool. The average N content was 30.88 g/kg (Fig. 4B). For the Mesoamerican gene pool, the maximum predicted value for N content was 42.05 g/kg, and the minimum was 17.65 g/kg, with an average of 30.52 g/kg (Fig. 4C). A significant difference was identified for predicted N content among the domesticated gene pools (p-value = 0.005998, Wilcoxon test). For the wild accessions of the Andean gene pool, the predicted N content varied between 25.68 g/kg and 40.99 g/kg, with an average value of 33.90 g/kg. Wild Mesoamerican accessions had a minimum predicted N value of 24.71 g/kg, a maximum value of 45.63 g/kg, with an average value of 33.51 g/kg. No statistically significant difference in predicted N content was found among wild gene pools (p-value = 0.1028, Wilcoxon test). A significant difference in predicted N values was found between wild and domesticated accessions (p-value < 2.2e-16, Wilcoxon test) in both the Andean and Mesoamerican gene pools, with the wild accessions having a higher N content than the domesticated accessions.

The top-ten wild accessions with the highest predicted N content (∼40–45.6 g/kg) included accessions from Honduras (G50722B, G50722A, and G50723C), Costa Rica (G51062, G23418, G51665A, G50898A), Argentina (G51365 and G51378) and Mexico (G51066). The highest predicted N content (∼39–42 g/kg) among domesticated accessions was predicted for landraces from Peru (G1386, G11723A, G11724, G12556 and G14737), Guatemala (G11640), Ecuador (G17085 and G17112), India (G14212) and Haiti (G18141).

To ensure the reliability, accuracy, and applicability of the best predictive N model calibrated in this research, total N content was measured through the conventional destructive method in an independent validation dataset. For this, a total of 70 common bean accessions (58 domesticated and 12 wild) were randomly selected based on their predicted N values. The total N in the external validation dataset ranged from 25.8 to 49.9 g/kg, with an average value of 39.4 g/kg (Supplementary Table 8). A strong positive correlation (0.90–0.91) was observed between the predicted N content and the values obtained using the conventional destructive method, as estimated for domesticated accessions across all three approaches (full spectrum, infrared region and iPLS) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Similarly, correlations between 0.87 and 0.91 were identified in the model that integrated both domesticated and wild accessions, as shown in Fig. 4D. In contrast, models that exclusively used wild accessions showed a lower correlation, ranging from 0.60 to 0.84 (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Discussion

Using NIRS spectral variability to rapidly characterize CIAT'S common bean core collection

In this study, we used near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) to screen 1754 accessions (including domesticated and a representative set of wild accessions) of CIAT's international common bean core collection, including long-term storage accessions, to predict N, Fe, and Zn content. To achieve this goal, we standardized a fast and non-destructive screening protocol to streamline the process of capturing NIRS signals. The NIRS data generated from this experimental protocol constitute a valuable new resource for characterizing genebank accessions. The results obtained indicate that the protocol was effective for screening common bean seeds of different shapes, colors, and sizes. The use of a single facility avoided the possibility of confounding effects as a result of laboratory environment variables, such as temperature or luminosity. This paper and its supplementary materials describe the protocol in sufficient detail to allow replication and further use by researchers in other institutions. Once the protocol was standardized, NIRS data collection for one accession required approximately three minutes, which is significantly faster than directly measuring nutrient content through conventional methods. Additionally, the protocol is non-destructive, preserving key seed characteristics, such as moisture and temperature, that are essential for their management and optimal conservation (Offord & Makinson, 2009).

Hence, it may be feasible to apply this method to characterize the entire CIAT genebank collection of 37,936 bean accessions. This information would be highly valuable for the collection's use. The protocol can also be integrated to rapidly evaluate breeding programs' intermediate objectives, thereby reducing the time and costs associated with identifying promising breeding materials for nutrient content.

A detailed analysis of spectral variability revealed that the visible and near-infrared segments exhibited the highest variability across the screened accessions. As expected, the visible segment primarily differentiated accessions based on seed color. However, when combined with the near-infrared segment, it effectively captured variability in N content. The most informative spectral ranges for N prediction were 961–1100 nm, 1206–1345 nm, 1556–1625, and 1941–1975 nm, which are commonly associated with nitrogen reflectance in their various overtones (Bos & Tomé, 2000; Soriano et al., 2015; Zeffa et al., 2020).

The spectral variability of the materials selected for constructing the predictive models, was not significantly different from the spectral variability observed for the complete common bean core collection. This suggests that models built using this subset of accessions should adequately capture the variability of the accessions for which N content was predicted. However, future studies focusing on collecting NIRS data from CIAT's whole common bean collection could offer the opportunity to establish a core collection based on NIRS features and discover common bean accession with promissory nutritional compounds among the collection.

Variability in nitrogen, iron and zinc contents in common bean seeds

Significant differences in N content were observed based on biological status. Wild accessions exhibited higher N content than domesticated accessions. This result remained consistent when comparisons were restricted to either the subset of Andean accessions or the subset of Mesoamerican accessions. Previous studies have shown that variations in seed compounds and changes in the crops' metabolite profiles may have been influenced by the process of domestication (Alseekh et al., 2021). For instance, domestication in soybeans led to an increase in seed size and oil content, but a decrease in protein content when compared to wild counterparts (Wang et al., 2020). A similar trend has been reported in common beans. Sotelo, Sousa, and Sanchez (1995) analyzed a small group of five wild and five domesticated accessions and found higher protein content in wild accessions. Likewise, Peña-Valdivia, García-Nava, Aguirre, Ybarra-Moncada, and López (2011) compared six Mexican wild accessions with three landraces and three improved cultivars, confirming higher protein and tryptophan levels in wild genotypes compared to the domesticated genotypes.

Another important N content comparison emerged between the Andean and Mesoamerican gene pools. When analyzing only wild accessions, significant differences in N content were observed, with higher N content in the Andean accessions. However, when only domesticated accessions were analyzed, no differences in N content were observed among the two gene pools. This result may be attributed to the domestication process that, in almost all crops, has led to a loss of genetic diversity through a bottleneck effect (Wang et al., 2020). The present results suggest that, in common bean, domestication has resulted in a loss of diversity in the domesticated gene pool, which has translated into a decrease in nutritional value, as indicated here by the lower N content. Although this aspect of domestication has not been studied in detail, previous studies for common bean (Beebe et al., 2000; Islam et al., 2002; Peña-Valdivia et al., 2011), soybean (Wang et al., 2020) and other pulses (Maass & Usongo, 2007) support this finding. Hence, wild accessions represent a valuable resource for developing new varieties with enhanced nutritional value.

The most important micronutrients in common beans are Fe and Zn, making them a significant source of nutrients in the human diet, especially in developing countries where a large percentage of the population suffers from Fe, Zn, and folic acid deficiencies (Beebe, 2020). As a result, biofortification programs have prioritized common beans (Blair, 2013; Bouis & Saltzman, 2017). In this study, we observed a mean Fe content of 72.2 mg/kg and 70.39 mg/kg for domesticated accessions from the Andean and Mesoamerica gene pools, respectively. Previously, Beebe et al. (2000) and Islam et al. (2001) evaluated Fe and Zn contents in 1031 domesticated accessions from CIAT's core collection, reporting Fe contents of 34–89 mg/kg, with an average of 55 mg/kg. The differences between the studies are likely due to three factors. First, the difference in the methods used to estimate mineral content. Beebe et al. (2000) and Islam et al. (2002) used ICP, while this study employed X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometry as the conventional-destructive method, and NIRs as a non-destructive method. As previously documented (Arai, 2006; West et al., 2010), the XRF method has a lower sensitivity, particularly for elements at very low concentrations, compared to ICP. However, multiple studies (Montanha et al., 2022; Nguli, Gatari, Shepherd, Njenga, & Boman, 2022) have used XRF to estimate mineral contents as a fast and affordable way to scan a large number of accessions. The confidence level for estimating mineral contents with XRF, compared to ICP, has been reported at 95 % (Paltridge et al., 2012).

The second factor to consider is the minerals' polygenic inheritance, which can be influenced by environmental conditions, resulting in continuous distribution of mineral content within each bean accession (Blair, Astudillo, Grusak, Graham, & Beebe, 2008; Izquierdo et al., 2018). The third factor that could affect the mineral content in the seed of long-term storage is related to environmental conditions in the conservation room. These conditions include temperature and relative humidity, which affect seed moisture content (Copeland & McDonald, 2001). Different seed moisture contents (6.4 %, 11.7 %, 12 %, 14 %, 16 %, 17.3 %, and 20 %), temperature (4 °C, 10 °C, 20 °C, 30 °C, and 40 °C), relative humidity (RH) (65 %, 70 % and 75 %), and storage time (108 days, 16 weeks, and 12 months) have been evaluated in previous studies to estimate the impact of storage conditions on common bean nutritional content (Rani et al., 2013; De Almeida et al., 2017; Kibar & Kibar, 2019). Kibar and Kibar (2019) reported a significant decrease in mineral contents (calcium, iron, magnesium, manganese, potassium, and zinc) after 12 months' storage compared with their initial conditions. For instance, Fe and Zn content before storage were estimated at 5.38 and 4.52 mg/100 g, respectively (6.4 % moisture content, 4 °C temperature, 65 % RH). After 12 months, the Fe and Zn values decreased to 1.74 and 0.89 mg/100 g, respectively (Kibar & Kibar, 2019). The exact seed storage conditions at the CIAT genebank (moisture content between 4 and 6 %, and temperature of −18 °C for long-term storage) have been designed to minimize the loss of nutrients caused by long-term storage. Although a negative correlation between storage time and Zn content was observed, the correlation value was relatively small and Zn content values above 35 mg/kg were observed for seeds stored for more than 25 years. Moreover, the correlation value for Fe was positive. This suggests that storage time was not a main determinant of the mineral content in this case.

In the present study, higher Fe content was observed in accessions from the Andean gene pool (mainly from Peru), compared to accessions from the Mesoamerican gene pool. This trend is consistent with previous data reported for common bean (Islam et al., 2002). Regarding Zn content, we observed that the Mesoamerican accessions were richer in Zn than the Andean accessions. This result aligns with the study by Islam et al. (2002), who reported an average Zn content of 35.8 mg/kg for the Mesoamerican accessions and 32.9 mg/kg for the Andean gene pool. The highest Zn concentrations were observed in materials from Guatemala. Additionally, we identified accessions with high Fe and Zn contents, similar to the BIO-101 fortified breeding line: G10912, G16824, G5459, G3514, G857, G3970, G1261 and G11419 (Supplementary Table 3). These accessions could be valuable in breeding programs and for developing countries for enhancing food security, particularly in populations deficient in Fe, Zn, folic acid, and β-carotene (FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP and WHO, 2022).

NIRS as a valuable alternative for the non-destructive characterization of common bean seed nutritional compounds

The availability of both NIRS spectra and direct measurements of nutritional compounds enabled the development of our predictive models for estimating N, Fe and Zn in common bean seeds. After evaluating of many alternatives, we selected the iPLS model, which exhibited the highest accuracy across the entire dataset (including wild and domesticated accessions). This model identifies the wavelengths that best fit the training data through successive rounds of PLS analysis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first predictive model trained with the largest number and variability of intact common bean seeds to date. Previous research has used intact seeds from fewer accessions to estimate various traits, such as seed weight, protein, and starch content. For instance, Hacisalihoglu et al. (2010) evaluated 91 common bean genotypes from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) National Plant Germplasm System and reported a 0.82 coefficient of determination for predicting protein content. This coefficient is lower than the 0.85 value reported in the present study, highlighting the importance of calibrating the model with many samples that represent the variation in the collection. Other researchers have used ground seeds. For instance, Hermida et al. (2006) evaluated 121 bean samples from farms in Galicia (Spain) and estimated protein content with an R2 of 0.88. More recently, Carbas et al. (2020) used near-infrared and mid-infrared spectroscopy from 21 common bean accessions to estimate protein content. Their model, which focused on the low-frequency region, reported a coefficient of determination of 0.95 for ground seeds. Future research could explore whether using ground seeds improves model accuracy within the same set of accessions. This test would help to determine if applying a destructive method is worthwhile for improving accuracy in predicting N content and selecting promising materials.

The model with the highest accuracy predicted seed N content in the complete set of 1094 domesticated and 260 wild accessions for which NIRS data were obtained. As was observed for the reference panel of 301 domesticated and 100 wild accessions, the model also predicted higher N content in wild accessions compared to domesticated accessions. Similarly, within both the Andean and the Mesoamerican gene pools, wild accessions were predicted to have higher N content than domesticated accessions. Furthermore, the predicted N values provided an opportunity to assess the effect of domestication on this trait. By applying this model, we estimated that domestication has led to an average reduction of 2.9 g/kg in seed N content. The reduction was 3.01 g/kg in the Andean gene pool and 2.98 g/kg in the Mesoamerican gene pool (wild vs domesticated). A decrease in nutritional compounds due to domestication has also been reported in species of Vigna, another important legume genus for human consumption (Fernández-Marín et al., 2014; Harouna, Ndakidemi, Venkataramana, & Matemu, 2024). In this genus, Harouna et al. (2024) reported wide variation in nutrient characteristics such as protein, ash, and carbohydrates across 87 wild Vigna accessions. They identified several wild accessions with higher nutrient contents (protein, lipid, ash, and carbohydrates) compared to domesticated ones. Similarly, Fernández-Marín et al. (2014) reported an average decrease of 48 % in total carotenoid content in domesticated accessions, compared to their wild counterparts, from ten legume species (Arachis, Cicer, Glycine, Lens, Lupinus, Phaseolus, Pisum, Vicia, Vicia faba, and Vigna). These findings emphasize the importance of quantifying the reduction in seed nutritional compounds due to domestication. Researchers need to continually characterize wild and cultivated germplasm to support breeding programs aimed at enhancing nutritional quality.

Predicting seed mineral content in common bean seeds represents a challenge, particularly in intact seeds, as minerals are primarily stored in the cotyledons at varying concentrations (Blair, Li, Nekkalapudi, Becerra, & Paredes, 2023). In the present study, the best prediction model for Fe and Zn achieved a concordance correlation coefficient of approximately 0.45. This result is consistent with previous studies that have evaluated both intact and ground seeds using NIRS. For instance, Wafula et al. (2022) reported Fe and Zn predictions in 65 ground common bean accessions, obtaining an R2 of 0.4 for Fe and 0.52 for Zn using iPLS analysis. Similarly, Plans et al. (2013) evaluated 54 accessions from Spanish landraces and inbred lines, estimating an R2 of 0.4 for magnesium in intact seeds and 0.7 in ground seeds using PLS analysis. Thus, this study has shown that NIRS taken from whole common bean seeds has limitations for accurately predicting these minerals and consequently has limited applicability. Therefore, future efforts to estimate mineral content in intact common bean seeds could be oriented towards using an array of methodologies, including techniques such as XRF and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). Both XRF and XAS have been used to provide high-resolution micronutrient distribution and quantification in pea seeds (Ashe et al., 2025). As non-destructive methods, their main advantage is that they can be used directly on the seed. However, XRF spectroscopy measurements must be calibrated using known concentrations of each element to ensure accurate quantification (Bamrah et al., 2019). Some successful examples of XRF use have been reported in a biofortification program, where XRF was applied as an accurate and cost-effective method to estimate Fe and Zn content in several crops (Benedito, de Bem, Guilherme, Carneiro, & Guerra, 2025; Guild, Paltridge, Andersson, & Stangoulis, 2017; Nguli et al., 2022). A complementary option to X-rays is micro-computed tomography (SR-μCT), which provides ultra-high resolution 3D imaging of seed structures. However, several factors must be considered, including the seed development stage, sample preparation, and the settings for tomography capture (e.g., photon energy selection and sample rotation) (Ashe et al., 2025; Indore et al., 2023).

Validation is a critical step in developing predictive models, ensuring their reliability, accuracy and applicability to the research objectives. Optimal prediction models are not those delivering the best statistical performance during testing, but those that demonstrate the greatest reliability when applied to validating new data (Lopez, Etxebarria-Elezgarai, Amigo, & Seifert, 2023). This is why we selected a set of 70 common bean accessions (58 domesticated and 12 wild) and determinated their total N content using the Kjeldahl method for validation (Sáez-Plaza, Navas, Wybraniec, Michałowski, & Asuero, 2013). We found a strong positive correlation (0.90–0.91) between predicted and measured N values in domesticated accessions, which supports the model's accuracy. However, the lower correlation (0.60–0.84) observed in models using only wild accessions suggests greater variability in this group, highlighting the need for further refinement when applying the model to genetically diverse materials. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of our predictive approach for N content and emphasize the importance of validation in ensuring model accuracy across different genetic backgrounds.

The Fe and Zn prediction models developed here in this study were less efficient compared to the N model. A possible explanation is that these two minerals are not absorbed directly in the near-infrared spectrum, but are instead estimated indirectly through their linkage with organic compounds (Ciurczak, 2001). Considering this, the iPLS analysis consistently identified spectral regions associated with Fe and Zn concentrations. Of the six spectral regions identified for Fe in this study, four (1311–1345, 1556–1590, 1591–1625 and 1976–2010 nm) shared common wavelengths with regions previously reported by Wafula et al. (2022). A similar pattern was observed for Zn, where two of the four identified regions (996–1030 and 1941–1975 nm) overlapped with those reported in previous studies (Wafula et al., 2022). These findings suggest that wavelength selection may be robust across different types of germplasm and could reflect organic compounds that contain trace amounts of these minerals.

Finally, the accuracy achieved in predicting N supports the use of NIRS as a useful non-destructive method for characterizing nutritional compounds in larger sets of accessions across different genebanks. These findings can aid in selecting promising materials for bean breeding programs aimed at improving nutritional value. Furthermore, similar models could be developed for other legumes or other nutritional compounds that play a crucial role in global food security.
