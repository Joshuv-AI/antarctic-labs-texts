LED red light for atrophy and microcirculation in pathologic myopia (RAMP): study design and protocol for a multicentre randomised controlled trial
Authors: Juzhao Zhang, Jun Yu, Xiaojuan Wang, Tao Sun, Yue-lan Zou, Haidong Zou, Yan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPathologic myopia (PM) is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, yet effective therapeutic interventions remain limited. Although repeated low-level red light (RLRL) therapy has demonstrated significant efficacy in controlling myopia progression among children, its application in patients with PM remains unexplored. This study aims to evaluate the regulatory effects of RLRL on choroidal vascular density and thickness in patients with PM and verify its safety and efficacy in delaying the progression of fundus atrophic lesions.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective, randomised, controlled trial enrolling 158 patients with PM aged 18–55 years. Participants will be randomised (1:1) to an intervention group or a control group. The intervention group will receive light-emitting diode–based RLRL therapy (wavelength, 660 nm; power density, 65 mW/cm²) administered two times daily for 3 min per session, 5 days per week, over a 12-month period. The control group will receive a sham treatment (power density, 5 mW/cm²) following an identical schedule. The primary outcome measure is the change in choroidal vascular density and choroidal vessel volume index at 12 months.Ethics and disseminationThis study has been approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shanghai Eye Disease Prevention and Treatment Centre (EC-20250506-04). This study will be conducted in adherence to the approved protocol, Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study results will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal and presented at both local and international congresses.Trial registration number NCT07312214 Keywords: Myopia, THERAPEUTICS, Medical retina

Background

Pathologic myopia (PM) is a leading cause of irreversible blindness and visual impairment worldwide.1 PM-associated complications, including posterior staphyloma, myopic maculopathy and myopic optic neuropathy, account for approximately 3% of global blindness cases, ranking them among the top three causes of vision loss.2 The number of individuals suffering from PM-induced vision loss is projected to increase sevenfold by 2050 compared with 2000 levels.3 In China, PM constitutes the primary cause of irreversible blindness in the working-age population.4 This rapid escalation from a public health issue to a major societal burden represents a critical medical challenge requiring urgent attention.

Despite this burden, clinical management remains limited. Current interventions focus on three main strategies: structural reinforcement, complication management and emerging experimental therapies. Posterior scleral reinforcement (PSR) can slow axial elongation but is invasive, exhibits variable efficacy (44%–97.2%) and fails to reverse established lesions.5 6 Similarly, while anti-VEGF agents effectively treat myopic choroidal neovascularisation (mCNV), they do not address underlying fundus degeneration and impose a significant burden via repeated intravitreal injections. Furthermore, potentially curative modalities like stem cell and gene therapy remain in early developmental stages with limited clinical translation.7 Ultimately, existing treatments predominantly target mid-to-late-stage complications rather than halting disease progression. Therefore, there is an urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies that enable early intervention to prevent the onset of retinal pathology in PM.

The choroid, located between the retina and the sclera, plays a pivotal role in maintaining visual function. Increasing evidence suggests that alterations in choroidal haemodynamics often precede structural atrophy in high myopia. Reduced choroidal perfusion is closely linked to retinal degeneration and myopia progression.8 Su et al demonstrated that choriocapillaris flow deficits are significantly greater in high myopia and most severe in PM.9 Similarly, Zhang et al found that the annual decline in choroidal vascular density in highly myopic eyes significantly exceeds that of healthy controls (−1.44%/year vs −0.11%/year, p=0.001), with axial length negatively correlated with vascular density preservation.10

Based on these findings, we hypothesised that choroidal perfusion deficits are a key driver of PM pathogenesis. The proposed mechanism is as follows: excessive axial elongation induces mechanical stretching and thinning of the choroid, leading to decreased capillary density and reduced perfusion. This compromise triggers ischaemic damage to the outer retina, ultimately precipitating irreversible degenerative lesions, such as atrophy and mCNV. Consequently, targeting choroidal microcirculation offers a promising strategy to halt PM progression.

Repeated low-level red light (RLRL) therapy has emerged as a novel intervention for myopia control. Using specific wavelengths of approximately 650 nm to mimic the protective mechanisms of outdoor light exposure, RLRL offers advantages such as non-invasiveness and operational simplicity.11 12 A multicentre randomised controlled trial established its efficacy in controlling myopia progression in children,13 with subsequent studies supporting its role in prevention and axial shortening.14–17 Crucially, RLRL has been shown to increase choroidal thickness (ChT) and improve retinal perfusion, providing a strong rationale for its use in early PM.18 19 However, the therapeutic efficacy and safety of RLRL in patients with PM have not yet been reported.

This trial aims to evaluate the efficacy of light-emitting diode (LED)-based RLRL in PM. Primary objectives include assessing its impact on choroidal vascular density (ChVD) and ChT, progression of atrophic fundus lesions and functional outcomes including visual acuity and microperimetry.

Methods

Study design and aims

We designed a prospective, randomised, controlled trial with 1-year follow-up. Participants in the intervention group are assigned to receive a desktop LED-based RLRL therapy (Airdoc PBM-LED, Model No.: AI-PBM01, Beijing Airdoc Technology Co.). It has a working wavelength of 650 nm, with an output power of 1.39 mW at the light source, and the output power is 1.39 mW ± 0.1 mW at a position 30 mm away from the observation window glass. Since it is a portable desktop device, patients can take it home for convenient continuous treatment. This allows them to complete the treatment regimen of two times daily, 5 days per week for 12 months, with each session lasting 3 min. The control group received a sham treatment using identical-appearing devices emitting light at 660 nm with a non-therapeutic power density of 5 mW/cm², adhering to the same treatment schedule.

The primary objective of this trial is to evaluate the impact of RLRL on ChVD and ChT in patients with atrophic fundus lesions secondary to PM. Furthermore, the study aims to verify the treatment’s efficacy in slowing the progression of atrophic lesions and enhancing visual function.

Recruitment

Participants will be recruited from clinical outpatient services. The research team will screen and contact eligible candidates. Prior to enrolment, investigators will provide a detailed study description. Enrolment is confirmed on the signing and dating of these documents by the participant. All enrolled participants will be followed for the duration of the study. Recruitment has started on 31 December 2025, with an anticipated completion of enrolment within 2 years. The entire study is expected to conclude by 30 December 2028.

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculation was based on findings by Zhang et al, who reported a significantly faster annual decline in parapapillary ChVD in highly myopic eyes (−1.44%/year) compared with healthy controls (−0.11%/year, p=0.001). For this trial, we hypothesised that RLRL intervention would ameliorate the decline rate to levels comparable with healthy eyes. Consequently, the expected effect size was set at 1.33% (difference between −1.44% and −0.11%). An SD of 2.8% was estimated based on comparable literature. 10Using a significance level=0.05 (two-tailed) and statistical power of 80%, the calculated sample size was 63 participants per group. To account for a potential 20% dropout rate, the final recruitment target was set at 158 participants (79 per group).

Intervention

Intervention group (n=79): received LED RL therapy (660 nm, 65 mW/cm², administered two times daily for 3 min per session, 5 days per week, over a period of 12 months).

Control group (n=79): received sham LED RL therapy (using identical-appearing devices emitting light at 660 nm with a power density of 5 mW/cm², following the same schedule of two times daily for 3 min per session, 5 days per week, for 12 months).

Compliance: ‘full compliance’ is defined as completing 10 sessions per week (30 min total). ‘Actual compliance’ is the cumulative duration of usage recorded by the device. ‘Individual compliance rate’ is calculated as follows: (actual compliance duration / full compliance duration) × 100%. Participants achieving a compliance rate of at least 80% will be considered adherent.

Compliance will be objectively monitored via automatic data synchronisation from the device to a central server. If no treatment is recorded for two consecutive days, the system will automatically send reminders to both the participant and the research staff.

Concomitant care and safety

An AE is defined as any untoward medical occurrence in a participant, regardless of its relationship to the investigational device, including events associated with mydriatics or study procedures. A serious AE (SAE) refers to any event that results in death, is life-threatening, requires inpatient hospitalisation, causes disability or is otherwise medically significant. Unexpected SAEs related to the study must be reported to the ethics committee. In the event of an SAE, the investigator must immediately notify the relevant regulatory authorities and preserve all medical records.

Quality control

To ensure data accuracy, comprehensive testing and calibration of the devices will be conducted prior to the formal initiation of the study. Additionally, all research personnel will undergo standardised training, and inter-observer consistency testing will be completed before the study begins to ensure the reliability and comparability of the results.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome measure is the change in ChVD and choroidal vessel volume index. Secondary outcome measures include ChT (defined as the distance from Bruch’s membrane to the choroid–sclera interface), retinal thickness (RT), retinal vascular density and structural progression. Structural progression will be assessed by the proportion of diffuse chorioretinal atrophy (DCA) progression, defined as the expansion of existing DCA or the emergence of new lesions in eyes with baseline DCA. Additional outcomes include changes in microperimetry, BCVA, AL, SE and intraocular pressure (IOP).

Safety evaluations will monitor retinal structural integrity (specifically the photoreceptor layer and ellipsoid zone), the presence of retinoschisis and the progression of posterior staphyloma. The study will also track the incidence of allergic reactions, duration of after-images, self-reported adverse effects and elevated IOP.

Assessments and data acquisition

General ophthalmic assessment and questionnaires

Clinical examinations include UCVA and BCVA, IOP and slit-lamp biomicroscopy. A structured questionnaire will be administered to collect demographic data, birth and family history, near-work habits, prior myopia management and systemic or allergic history.

Cycloplegia and refraction

Cycloplegia will be induced using 1% cyclopentolate hydrochloride. The regimen consists of one drop of proparacaine hydrochloride (anaesthetic), followed 15 s later by the first drop of cyclopentolate and a second drop 5 min later. Pupillary status is assessed 45 min after the final drop; cycloplegia is defined as the absence of a pupillary light reflex. If incomplete, a third drop is administered with re-assessment after 15 min. Post-cycloplegic autorefraction is performed binocularly using the KR-8900 (Topcon, Japan). The recorded value is the mean of three consecutive measurements; acquisition is repeated if the difference between any two readings exceeds 0.50 D.

Axial length measurement

AL will be measured using the IOLMaster 700 (Carl Zeiss, Germany), calibrated daily with a model eye. The final value represents the mean of three consecutive measurements. Measurements are repeated if the difference between readings exceeds 0.05 mm.

Swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography

Chorioretinal imaging will be performed using the BM-400K BMizar (TowardPi Medical Technology, China). Following automated segmentation of the choroidal boundaries, segmentation lines will be manually verified and corrected if necessary. Retinal and ChVD and ChT will be quantified across nine regions (subfoveal, inner, middle and outer sectors).

Microperimetry

Retinal sensitivity and fixation stability will be assessed using the MP-3 Microperimeter (Nidek, Japan). Testing will be conducted with the non-tested eye occluded under a background luminance of 4–10 cd/m². The device records the participant’s response to light stimuli to generate retinal sensitivity maps and fixation locus characteristics.

The study will last approximately 1 year. All examinations will be performed at the same centre by trained personnel at baseline and at scheduled follow-up timepoints (table 1).

Randomisation and masking

Eligible participants will be randomised to either the intervention or control group in a 1:1 ratio. The randomisation sequence will be generated by a blinded statistician using SAS 9.4 software (Cary, NC). Allocation concealment will be maintained using sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. A designated unblinded study coordinator—who is independent of participant recruitment and outcome assessment—will open the envelopes to assign interventions. This coordinator is solely responsible for device training, compliance monitoring and logistical scheduling. To ensure study integrity, all other personnel, including clinical examiners, ophthalmologists and the statistical team, will remain blinded to treatment allocation throughout the study.

Data management

Data management will be conducted using a specialised electronic data capture system (OPERA-EDC). All authorised users will undergo training and receive unique, role-based credentials. The database will be configured with logical verification rules to enforce data validity before formal entry. Investigators are responsible for entering data via secure accounts; discrepancies identified by data managers will generate system queries, which investigators must resolve within 1 week. The system maintains a comprehensive audit trail of all data entries and modifications. No external timed audit or trend analysis is planned during the study. However, if the intervention is confirmed to be efficacious, patients in the control group who voluntarily agree to receive RLRL treatment will be provided with 1 year of RLRL treatment free of charge.

Before database locking, a blind data review meeting involving the principal investigator, statisticians and data managers will be convened to finalise analysis populations and verify case counts. Once all queries are resolved and the statistical analysis plan is finalised, the database will be locked. Post-lock modifications are prohibited unless authorised. Following the lock, the finalised dataset will be transferred to the statistician for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Primary efficacy analysis will be conducted using the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, supplemented by per-protocol (PP) analysis, to verify the robustness of the findings. The primary outcome will be compared using two-sample t-tests; if baseline imbalances exist, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) will be employed to adjust for confounding factors. Secondary continuous outcomes will be analysed using ANCOVA. Binary outcomes, such as the proportion of DCA progression, will be assessed using logistic regression. Model assumptions (linearity, normality and homoscedasticity) will be verified, and non-parametric methods will be utilised if these assumptions are violated. Safety data will be analysed using descriptive statistics to summarise AEs, whereas baseline characteristics and compliance will be regularly monitored throughout the trial.

Ethics and dissemination

Regulatory and ethical compliance

This study has been approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shanghai Eye Disease Prevention and Treatment Centre (EC-20250506-04). The study will be conducted in strict adherence to the approved protocol, Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. This study has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07312214).

Informed consent

Written informed consent, approved by institutional review board/independent ethics committees (IRB/IEC), must be obtained from all participants before baseline assessment. The process of obtaining informed consent should be documented in the subject source documents. Investigators are responsible for fully explaining the study’s objectives, risks and benefits, ensuring participants understand their rights and consent voluntarily. Consent forms are executed in duplicate, with copies retained by both the participant and the research institution.

Responsibilities of the investigator and institutional review board/independent ethics committees

Before trial initiation, the investigator/institution shall obtain approval or a favourable opinion from the IRB/IEC. The principal investigator and the entire clinical research team shall conduct the study in strict compliance with the approved protocol.

Data handling and record keeping

To ensure confidentiality, all research data will be pseudonymised using participant identification codes. The study data will be retained for a minimum of 5 years before being destroyed or deleted.

Patient and public involvement

Patients participated in the study design. Communication methods, like WeChat, were selected based on patient preferences for convenience.

Financing and insurance

This is an investigator-initiated clinical trial. The sponsor will provide financial support to the clinical site for the implementation of the study. In compliance with relevant national regulations, the sponsor shall also maintain adequate insurance coverage to compensate study participants for any trial-related injuries arising from study procedures and/or interventions.

Publication policy

On completion of the study and finalisation of the study report, the trial results will be prepared by the investigators for submission to a peer-reviewed journal.

Protocol adherence and amendments

Investigators ascertain that they will avoid protocol deviations. Only amendments that will be required for subject safety may be implemented, and the reviewing IRB/IEC will be subsequently notified by protocol amendment. Should significant protocol amendments occur, ethical re-approval will be sought, and participants will be re-consented.

Discussion

PM remains a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide. Although interventions such as orthokeratology, atropine and optical defocus strategies effectively retard axial elongation in children and adolescents, these approaches predominantly target low-to-moderate myopia or pre-myopia. Consequently, there is a paucity of therapeutic strategies specifically addressing PM, the stage most responsible for severe vision loss. This protocol proposes a 1-year randomised controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of RLRL therapy in patients with PM. The study aims to determine whether RLRL can serve as a novel clinical intervention to halt PM progression.

The rationale for this study is supported by the growing prominence of photobiomodulation (PBM) in retinal therapeutics. Preclinical studies have demonstrated the protective effects of RL: 670 nm LED irradiation (4–9 J/cm²) has been shown to alleviate retinal toxicity and hyperoxia-induced retinopathy in rodent models by reducing microvascular occlusion and inhibiting neovascularisation.20 21 Clinically, PBM has demonstrated efficacy in treating diabetic macular oedema, reducing central foveal thickness and improving oedema.22 Most notably, PBM has achieved a breakthrough in the management of dry age-related macular degeneration. The Valeda Light Delivery System (660 nm, 65 mW/cm²) received approval from the Food & Drug Administration in November 2024, supported by the LIGHTSITE III trial, which confirmed that 24 months of treatment improved visual acuity and reduced the progression of geographic atrophy.23 These findings provide a robust evidence base for the safety and efficacy of RL therapy in retinal degeneration, establishing a strong theoretical foundation for extending its application to the treatment of PM.

Within the myopia control domain, RLRL therapy has garnered substantial attention as an emerging intervention. Its proposed mechanism involves stimulating mitochondrial function in the retinal pigment epithelium and choroid using 650 nm light, thereby enhancing cellular metabolism and modulating the release of signalling molecules such as dopamine and nitric oxide.24 Although systematic reviews confirm the efficacy of RLRL in delaying myopia progression, its application in PM remains unexplored.25 Existing data are limited to paediatric high myopia; notably, a multicentre randomised controlled trial reported that 60% of participants in the RLRL group exhibited significant axial shortening (>0.05 mm) after 12 months—a structural reversal not observed in controls.26 Our preliminary investigations corroborate these findings, additionally demonstrating increased ChT and RT following intervention.27 Based on this evidence, we hypothesise that RLRL therapy may delay the progression of atrophic fundus lesions in PM, offering a novel therapeutic approach for this blinding eye disease. Notably, enrolment was restricted to ages 18–55 years, as patients aged >55 years with high myopia show higher rates of established complications, and our target range captures the majority of patients with PM before severe disease onset. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted within these boundaries.

This study aims to establish a robust evidence base for the management of PM. By rigorously evaluating disease progression and structural outcomes, the trial will elucidate the efficacy profile and optimal application protocols for RLRL in this high-risk population. Ultimately, these findings will inform future therapeutic strategies and provide a novel clinical approach to mitigate the risk of PM-related irreversible blindness.
