Pre-pregnancy BMI, gestational weight gain, and telomere length in amniotic fluid: a causal graph analysis
Authors: M Barchitta, A Maugeri, C La Mastra, G Favara, M C La Rosa, R Magnano San Lio, Y Gholizade Atani, G Gallo, A Agodi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious investigations have suggested a potential association between pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and gestational weight gain (GWG) with telomere length (TL) in various tissues of pregnant women and newborns. Nonetheless, as association does not imply causation, our objective was to investigate the causal connections among pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and TL in amniotic fluid. The analysis included 136 mother-child pairs from the Mamma & Bambino cohort, and three causal graph models were developed to depict the interconnections between pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and TL. Causal graph analysis was conducted utilizing the do-operator to estimate the causal effect of GWG and the controlled direct effect of pregestational BMI. We revealed that transitioning from non-adequate to adequate GWG had a positive impact on the probability of having “long” TL (i.e., a value greater than the population median) in all three models. When considering the effect of pre-pregnancy BMI, the highest probability of “long” TL was observed in normal weight women with adequate GWG. In contrast, the effect of adequate GWG became minimal among overweight women. These results shed light on the potential causality between pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and TL in amniotic fluid, emphasizing the importance of appropriate weight management before and during pregnancy for optimal TL outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74765-y.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Risk factors

Introduction

The prenatal period is a critical phase of human development, where the health and well-being of both the mother and the foetus are influenced by various factors1. Among these factors, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and gestational weight gain (GWG) have garnered significant attention due to their potential impact on maternal and foetal health outcomes2–7. Pre-pregnancy BMI reflects the woman’s weight status before conception, while GWG refers to the total weight gained during pregnancy. It is important to highlight that the optimal amount of GWG is influenced by a woman’s pre-pregnancy BMI. This means that the recommended weight gain during pregnancy varies depending on the woman’s BMI before becoming pregnant8. Higher pre-pregnancy BMI and excessive GWG are associated with an increased risk of gestational diabetes and hypertensive disorders (e.g., preeclampsia and gestational hypertension)9–13. Additionally, higher pre-pregnancy BMI and excessive GWG are associated with an increased risk of delivering large-for-gestational-age infants, which can lead to birth complications and an elevated risk of childhood obesity and metabolic disturbances in the offspring10,14–17. On the other hand, pregnant women who have an underweight status are at higher risk of delivering a baby with low birth weight (LBW) and premature birth9,18–20. Similarly, insufficient GWG raises the likelihood of restricted foetal growth, premature birth, and LBW19. It is crucial to recognize that although associations between pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and the aforementioned outcomes have been identified, establishing causality can be intricate. Additional factors, including genetics, lifestyle, and socioeconomic status, age can significantly influence these relationships. Therefore, unravelling the underlying mechanisms connecting these variables becomes essential. By gaining insights into these mechanisms, we can potentially identify specific targets for interventions that aim to enhance maternal and foetal health, ultimately improving outcomes for both.

In recent years, considerable research attention has been directed towards telomeres, which act as protective caps at the ends of chromosomes and hold promise as potential markers of cellular aging and overall health21. Previous investigations have established a significant link between higher BMI and shorter telomeres in various tissues of non-pregnant individuals22,23. This association suggests an acceleration of cellular aging and an elevated vulnerability to age-related diseases21,23–25. Indeed, extremely shortened telomeres have been linked to cellular senescence, the development of a secretive phenotype associated with senescence, heightened genetic instability, and ultimately apoptosis26–29. In adults, shorter telomeres have been associated with an increased risk of diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease21,23–25.

Building upon this knowledge, compelling evidence has emerged to demonstrate a correlation between maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and telomere shortening specifically in cord blood and placental tissue30,31. Moreover, recent evidence have indicated a potential association between GWG and telomere Length (TL), implying that the amount of weight gained during pregnancy may influence cellular aging processes in both the mother and the developing foetus32. Our preliminary analysis of the “Mamma & Bambino” cohort has yielded fascinating insights into the association between GWG and TL of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) obtained from amniotic fluid32. Amniotic fluid has indeed emerged as a potential source of biomarkers for prenatal diagnosis, offering an alternative to traditional methods33. It envelops the foetus, facilitating continuous exchange with foetal organs and gestational tissues34,35. Notably, amniotic fluid contains a higher quantity of cfDNA compared to maternal serum36–38. This abundance of cfDNA makes amniotic fluid an attractive source for analysis39, as it is less contaminated by maternal and trophoblast-derived nucleic acids. Therefore, amniotic fluid represents a relatively pure foetal sample, and its supernatant holds great value as a readily available yet underutilized resource40. In our previous study, we specifically observed a distinctive U-shaped pattern in the relationship between GWG and TL of cfDNA from amniotic fluid. Strikingly, women who achieved adequate GWG exhibited significantly longer TL compared to those with reduced or excessive weight gain. To further validate these results, logistic regression models were employed while adjusting for potential confounding factors32. The analysis confirmed a significant association between TL of cfDNA and adequate GWG, underscoring the importance of GWG in influencing telomere dynamics during pregnancy.

However, it is important to note that the presence of an association between two variables does not automatically imply a causal relationship. Establishing causation requires meeting several necessary criteria beyond association alone41. While the observed relationship between GWG and TL provides valuable insights, additional evidence and rigorous analysis are required to determine the direction and nature of causality. The investigation of causal relationships between variables usually requires experimental data that can be costly, unethical, or even impossible to acquire. A possible way to circumvent this difficulty is the use of causal Directed Acyclic Graph (cDAG) to model the interactions of relevant factors in a system42–44. cDAGs provide a clear and transparent method for observational research to identify and analyze causal relationships between variables45. By examining a cDAG’s structure, researchers can determine the appropriate adjustment set needed for accurately estimating causal effects, either through visual inspection or algorithmically, depending on the cDAG’s complexity45. While the accuracy of these causal estimates depends on how well the cDAG represents the true data-generating process, the act of creating and sharing a cDAG makes these underlying assumptions explicit and open to scrutiny45. Despite these advantages, cDAGs are relatively uncommon in applied health research, likely due to a lack of awareness and cultural ambivalence towards their use. In general, a cDAG is constructed by supposing casual relationships suggested from domain knowledge. In cDAGs, nodes and arrows represent random variables and directed causal paths respectively and they may rigorously map all a priori assumptions surrounding a causal question of interest. Using cDAGs it is possible to model the causal relations between factors in a system in a visual and intuitive way. It is important to recognize that a causal graph is essentially a working hypothesis that aims to capture the causal effects within a system. However, it is crucial to validate or refute this hypothesis using empirical data. In this regard, the principles of Bayes theory in probability provide robust mathematical techniques to evaluate a graph model based on observational data46. These techniques stem from the field of Bayesian Networks, which holds immense value as a powerful tool in the domain of Artificial Intelligence47.

In this paper, we utilize causal graph analysis to explore potential causal relationships between BMI, GWG, and TL in amniotic fluid, while accounting for other confounders such as age and total energy intake. By constructing a graph, we aim to visually represent the relationships among these variables and identify potential causal pathways. Through this analysis, we hope to shed light on the intricate connections between these factors and provide a foundation for future research and interventions in the field of maternal-foetal health.

The present analysis comprised a total of 136 mothers who met the inclusion criteria. The characteristics of the study population are described in Table 1, while Fig. 1 displays the distribution of women based on pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG categories. Taking into account both pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG, the women were further classified as having reduced (29.4%), adequate (42.6%), or excessive GWG (27.9%). There was a significant association between pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG (p = 0.023), whereby normal weight women were more likely to have adequate GWG. Conversely, the proportion of reduced and excessive GWG was higher among underweight and overweight women, respectively (Fig. S1).

Causal graph model definition

Building on prior research7,30,32,48,49 and the current findings, we have constructed three distinct cDAGs to represent potential causal relationships among the variables of interest (Fig. 3). These models have been thoroughly examined using dependency tests and conditional dependency tests, ensuring their compatibility with the observed data. Importantly, all three models are consistent with the observed data and do not conflict with it.

Causal directed acyclic graphs (cDAGs) employed to visually depict the causal relationships among the variables of interest. Three graphical models were constructed (G1, G2, and G3), where the variables GWG, BMI, TL, Age, and total energy intake were denoted as G, B, T, A, and E, respectively. These models provided a structured representation of the causal connections among the variables in a directed acyclic manner.

Initially, the analysis began with the simplest graphical model (G1), which considered GWG, BMI, and TL variables. However, considering the biological perspective, it was recognized that the effects of total energy intake and age cannot be entirely disregarded in relation to GWG, BMI, and TL7,30,32,48,49. Therefore, the analysis was expanded to include graphical models G2 and G3. Model G2 and G3 diverge in terms of incorporating the influence of age on pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG. The distinction arises due to the existing limitations and conflicting evidence surrounding these potential relationships.

For all three graphical models, two different settings were employed to analyse the GWG variable. In one setting, GWG was treated as a binary factor, distinguishing between adequate and non-adequate GWG. In the other setting, GWG was categorized into three classes: reduced, adequate, and excessive GWG. Furthermore, TL was consistently categorized as “long” when its value exceeded the median and as “short” otherwise.

Potential causal effect of GWG on TL

By evaluating the differences in probabilities denoted as Prob(TL = t|do(GWG = g)), considering all classes of GWG, we have successfully estimated the potential causal effect of GWG on TL. The estimation of the causal effect, considering GWG divided into two classes (adequate and non-adequate), is displayed in Fig. 4. The computed causal effect values are reported for all three graphical models (G1, G2, and G3). From Fig. 4, we can observe that transitioning from non-adequate to adequate GWG has a positive impact on the probability of having a “long” TL. In model G1 (Fig. 4A), this increase in probability is quantified as 15%. In models G2 and G3 (Fig. 4B,C), the same increase is estimated at 14% and 12%, respectively.

Potential causal effect of pre-pregnancy BMI on TL

We proceeded to estimate the potential causal effect of pre-pregnancy BMI on TL in graphical models G1 and G3. This was done by calculating the differences in probabilities, denoted as Prob(TL = t|do(BMI = b), GWG = g), where b represents all classes of BMI (underweight, normal, overweight) and g represents the adequate/non-adequate classes of GWG. In the case of graphical model G2, we estimated the causal effect of pre-pregnancy BMI on TL by examining the probability value Prob(TL = t|do(BMI = b), do(GWG = g)).

The results for all three graphical models (G1, G2, and G3) are presented in Fig. 6. These findings indicate that the impact of BMI on TL varies depending on the inclusion of Age and Total energy intake. In Fig. 6A, it is clear that transitioning from underweight to normal and then overweight BMI values decreases the likelihood of TL being “long” in both the adequate and non-adequate GWG classes. However, Fig. 6B and C demonstrate distinct causal effects of BMI on TL within the two GWG classes. In the non-adequate GWG class, shifting from underweight to overweight reduces the probability of TL being “long.” Conversely, in the adequate GWG class, transitioning from underweight or overweight to normal BMI positively affects the probability of TL being “long.”

Discussion

Emerging evidence points to maternal factors including diet48,50–53, stress levels54–56, exposure to chemicals and toxins57,58, and parity59 as potential influencers of newborn TL. While uncovering these connections provides valuable insights into the fundamental mechanisms of cellular aging, it is only through a thorough understanding of potential causal relationships that we can effectively inform the development of interventions aimed at promoting optimal maternal health and fetal development. However, research remains inaccurate and controversial due to the heterogeneity of studies, approaches, evaluated exposure factors, and types of samples considered.

In this context, to our knowledge, our study represents the first attempt to employ causal graph analysis in exploring the intricate interplay between pre-gestational BMI, GWG, and TL in amniotic fluid, which is an optimal source of biomarkers for prenatal diagnosis33. Once the cellular components are removed, the remaining cell-free supernatant provides a clear indicator of maternal and fetal health35,60–67. Notably, amniotic fluid contains a significantly higher concentration of cfDNA than maternal serum, and this cfDNA is largely free from maternal and trophoblast-derived nucleic acids37,38,36–69. Consequently, amniotic fluid offers a relatively pure fetal sample70. Despite its potential, the invasive nature of amniocentesis has led to the under-utilization of this source in favor of less invasive alternatives such as maternal serum and urine70. Our research leverages the availability of amniotic fluid samples obtained through routine care for specific indications. While our study does not aim to modify current clinical practices, it seeks to inform future research on maternal factors that may influence neonatal health from the earliest stages of life.

To achieve this, we utilized causal graph analysis, a tool that is increasingly recognized for its value in understanding the complex dynamics of public health issues71. By examining the relationships and interdependencies among a range of factors, causal graph analysis offers a framework to investigate the possible causal pathways and underlying mechanisms involved. One of the key advantages of causal graph analysis is its ability to consider both direct and indirect effects of variables, enabling researchers to disentangle the intricate web of relationships and identify the most influential factors72. This analytical approach helps elucidate the causal links between exposures, interventions, outcomes, and potential confounders, leading to more robust and evidence-based public health interventions71. It is particularly valuable for addressing public health issues that often involve multifaceted interactions between social, environmental, behavioural, and biological factors. Through the application of causal graph analysis, researchers can identify key risk factors, protective factors, and mediators involved in the development and progression of public health issues. By quantifying the strength and direction of causal relationships, interventions and policies can be more effectively targeted to address specific risk factors or modify influential determinants72.

Regarding the issue addressed in our study, we utilized causal graph analysis to model the presumed causal pathways among pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and TL. These variables were represented as nodes in a graph, with their causal relationships depicted as directed edges. Previous studies have indeed suggested that both pre-gestational BMI and GWG may independently influence TL. Higher pre-pregnancy BMI has been associated with shorter telomeres, indicating accelerated cellular aging and potential adverse effects on foetal well-being30. Additionally, excessive GWG has also been linked to shorter telomeres, suggesting potential detrimental effects on cellular aging processes during pregnancy32. Conversely, insufficient GWG may reflect inadequate maternal nutritional intake or inadequate foetal growth, both of which have been associated with shorter telomeres and potential health risks for the offspring48,51,73. Through the present analysis, we have reported an association between adequate GWG and TL in amniotic fluid, as previously documented in our earlier study that also included obese women32. Moreover, by excluding women with obesity, we sought to simplify the model and explore the causal relationship between pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and TL.

Through careful consideration of the theoretical background and available evidence, we constructed a causal graph to investigate the causal associations between these variables. The constructed causal graph includes pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG as predictors, while TL is considered as the outcome. Our hypothesis postulated that pre-pregnancy BMI exerts an influence on both GWG and TL, with GWG serving as a mediator between BMI and TL.

We initially examined the causal effect of GWG on TL. Intriguingly, maintaining an appropriate GWG, as opposed to an inadequate GWG, resulted in a 15% higher likelihood of exhibiting TL higher than the median value observed in the study population. A similar effect was observed when comparing adequate GWG with reduced and excessive GWG. Subsequently, we assessed the control direct effect of BMI on TL while assuming GWG as constant. When GWG was adequate, the probability of having TL above the median value was comparable for underweight and normal weight women but lower for overweight women (approximately 22–26% less). However, in the case of inadequate GWG, the likelihood of exhibiting TL greater than the median value was highest among underweight women, followed by a decrease among normal weight women, and even more pronounced among overweight women.

In addition, we incorporated relevant confounding variables, such as maternal age and total energy intake, into the analysis to account for their potential influence on the relationship of interest. With regard to the causal effect of GWG on TL, results were consistent with those obtained by the simple three-variable model. However, when examining the control direct effect of pre-pregnancy BMI on TL, there were some differences that should be discussed. The results indeed confirmed a decline in the probability of having TL greater than the median value as we moved from the underweight category to the overweight category, particularly when GWG was inadequate. Conversely, if GWG was adequate, the highest probability of exceeding the median value for TL was observed among women with normal weight, rather than underweight. This implies that the best outcome is achieved by combining a normal pre-pregnancy weight status with adequate GWG. Conversely, the effect of an adequate GWG becomes minimal or even negligible in the case of overweight women.

This study has several limitations that should be taken into consideration when interpreting the findings. Firstly, the study relied on observational data, which inherently lacks the ability to establish a definitive causal relationship between variables. It is important to note that causal graph analysis provides a valuable framework for generating hypotheses and guiding subsequent analyses, but it does not establish definitive causality. We constructed the causal graph drawing from our previous experience in the field and the findings of previously published observational studies. We also incorporated potential variables that could confound the relationships under examination. Although efforts were made to control for confounding factors, the presence of residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out, potentially affecting the observed associations. Future studies utilizing experimental designs or randomized controlled trials would provide stronger evidence of causality. Secondly, the study focused on a limited sample composed of a specific population of women eligible for amniocentesis, which introduces certain limitations. When generalizing the results to other demographic groups, caution is necessary. The characteristics and behaviors of the study participants may not reflect those of individuals in different settings or with diverse backgrounds. Consequently, the findings might not be fully applicable beyond the study population. To address this, further research involving broader and more diverse samples is warranted. This would help ensure that the results are more widely generalizable and representative of various populations. Another potential limitation is the reliance on self-reported data for variables such as pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG. Self-reported data are subject to recall bias, which could introduce inaccuracies or inconsistencies in the measurements. The use of objective measurements or longitudinal data collection methods could enhance the accuracy and reliability of the findings in future studies. Furthermore, the study exclusively assessed TL in amniotic fluid, which may not fully reflect TL dynamics in other tissues or cell types. While amniotic fluid is considered a relatively pure foetal sample, it is crucial to acknowledge the possibility of a low proportion of cell-free DNA originating from the placenta. TL can vary across different cell types and tissues, and these variations may have distinct implications for health outcomes. As a result, caution should be exercised when extrapolating the findings of this study to other organs or tissues, as the dynamics of TL may differ in those contexts. Finally, the study design did not allow for the assessment of long-term health outcomes associated with TL. While TL is considered a biomarker of cellular aging, its direct clinical implications and associations with specific health outcomes were not evaluated in this study. Future research should aim to investigate the long-term implications of TL in relation to GWG and pre-pregnancy BMI to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their effects on health.

Despite these limitations, our study has provided valuable insights into the intricate mechanisms that govern the relationships between pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and TL in amniotic fluid. The use of the “do”-operator offers a robust method for estimating causal effects, which is particularly advantageous in observational studies. Traditional observational studies often struggle with distinguishing between mere associations and true causality due to numerous hidden biases and confounding variables. The “do”-operator addresses this issue by simulating an intervention on a specific variable (in our case GWG or BMI), allowing researchers to estimate the causal effect of that variable on the outcome (TL in amniotic fluid). This approach enhances the reliability of the findings in several ways: (i) by setting specific variables and using causal graphs, the study effectively controls for other influencing factors; (ii) the “do”-operator provides a clearer interpretation of how changes in one variable causally influence another, moving beyond simple correlations; (iii) the structured approach of using causal graphs helps identify and adjust for biases that might otherwise affect the outcomes; Iv) The causal inference derived from this method adheres to rigorous statistical principles, making it less susceptible to the pitfalls of observational data, such as selection bias and confounding41–43,74,75.

By exploring the different pathways, we have identified a potential causal effect of GWG on TL, partially mediating the impact of pre-pregnancy BMI. These findings enhance our understanding of the complex interplay among these factors and their influence on telomere biology. Consequently, the current recommendations from the IOM regarding appropriate weight gain during pregnancy, initially aimed at optimizing maternal and infant outcomes8,76–79, also have implications for mitigating the phenomenon of biological aging in the foetus. This recognition highlights the broader significance of adhering to these guidelines beyond immediate health considerations. However, to fully comprehend the public health implications, it is vital to consider these findings holistically. By achieving a normal weight status before pregnancy and maintaining appropriate GWG throughout, we can maximize the likelihood of longer TL. This integrated approach holds significant promise in counteracting biological aging, starting from the initial stages of life. It is however important to acknowledge the need for further research to validate and expand upon our findings. Future investigations should employ rigorous study designs, explore long-term health outcomes associated with TL, and examine diverse populations. By addressing these gaps in knowledge, we can strengthen our understanding of the impact of pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and TL on health outcomes and develop targeted interventions for promoting healthy aging.

Methods

Study design

The “Mamma & Bambino” cohort is an ongoing prospective study aimed at investigating the impact of pre-conceptional, perinatal, and early life exposome on the health of both mothers and children7,80–82. Since 2015, all pregnant women receiving prenatal genetic counselling at the Azienda Ospedaliero Universitaria Policlinico “G. Rodolico - San Marco” in Catania, Italy, have been invited to participate in the study. Mothers are recruited between 4 and 20 weeks of gestation and followed up at delivery, as well as one and two years after birth. Exclusion criteria include multiple pregnancies, pre-existing autoimmune and/or chronic diseases, pregnancy complications (such as preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, and gestational diabetes), intrauterine foetal death, and congenital malformations. For the present analysis, we utilized data specifically from mothers who had a pre-pregnancy BMI ≤ 30, completed a singleton pregnancy, and had available information regarding GWG at delivery and TL in amniotic fluid. It is imperative to clarify that the amniocentesis was conducted as part of standard clinical practice and in accordance with specific indications (i.e., advanced maternal age, abnormal prenatal screening results, family history of genetic disorders, structural abnormalities in the fetus, and maternal anxiety). Assessment of pre-pregnancy body mass index and gestational weight gain

During the recruitment process, women were requested to provide their height and pre-pregnancy weight, which were then used to calculate their pre-pregnancy BMI in kg/m2. Based on the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria83, women were classified into three categories: underweight, normal weight, or overweight, according to their pre-pregnancy BMI.

To assess GWG, the maternal weight achieved at delivery was collected from clinical records. Next, the total GWG was calculated by subtracting the self-reported pre-pregnancy weight from the weight at delivery. Following the guidelines established by the Institute of Medicine (IOM)84, the GWG was categorized as reduced, adequate, or excessive, taking into consideration the individual’s pre-pregnancy BMI. This classification considered the recommended weight gain ranges specific to each BMI category (Table 2).

Covariate assessment

In addition to anthropometric measures, our analysis accounted for various covariates that could potentially influence the relationship between pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and TL. Trained epidemiologists administered structured questionnaires to gather demographic, socio-economic, and lifestyle information during the recruitment process. Maternal age was considered as it could impact TL. Regarding lifestyle factors, we assessed daily energy intake of its effects on BMI and GWG. Dietary data were collected using a 95-item semiquantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ), which inquired about consumption frequency and portion sizes over a period of 30 days before recruitment7,80. Daily energy intake was calculated using the food composition table provided by the US Department of Agriculture (http://ndb.nal.usda.gov/), adapted to typical Italian foods.

TL assessment

Biological samples were collected from mothers and newborns participating in the “Mamma & Bambino” cohort at recruitment and delivery32,51,52,82. Specifically, women who underwent amniocentesis were invited to donate an aliquot of amniotic fluid. For the current analysis, amniotic fluid samples were used for cfDNA extraction. A 1 ml aliquot of uncultured amniotic fluid was first centrifuged at 12,500 g to remove any residual cells. The supernatant was then utilized for cfDNA extraction using the QIAamp Blood Kit (Qiagen, Milan, Italy) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA purification was automated using the QIAcube instrument (Qiagen, Milan, Italy). The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using the NanoDrop 1000 spectrometer and the Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer with the dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The relative TL of cfDNA was evaluated using the Relative Human TL Quantification Assay Kit (ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) using two sets of primers: the telomere (T) primer set amplified telomere sequences, and the single-copy reference (S) primer set amplified a 100 bp-long region on human chromosome 17, serving as a reference for data normalization. Each reaction included 1 µl of DNA (5 ng/µl), 2 µl of primer solution (telomere or SCR), 10 µl of 2X GoldNStart TaqGreen qPCR master mix (ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and 7 µl of nuclease-free water. The PCR conditions consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 32 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 52 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 45 s. All reactions were performed in duplicate, and the relative TL was expressed as the average telomere/single copy reference (T/S) ratio.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was carried out with SPSS v.26. Descriptive statistics were initially computed using frequencies (percentage, %) or median and interquartile range (IQR) due to the skewed distribution of quantitative variables. Bivariate analyses were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for quantitative variables and the Chi-squared test for categorical variables. Moreover, plots were generated to visually depict the bivariate relationships between pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and TL. All tests were conducted as two-sided tests with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Causal analysis via the “do”-operator

The “do”-operator, as referenced in74,85, plays a vital role in validating a causal graph. This operator extends the classical concept of “a posteriori” probability into a causal framework. In traditional “posterior” probability, expressed as Prob(Y = y| X = x), we consider the probability of observing value y for variable Y given that value x has been observed for variable X. However, this formula fails to fully account for the direct effect of X on Y when other confounding factors influence both variables. In contrast, the “do”-operator, denoted as Prob(Y = y|do (X = x)), allows us to calculate the direct effect of X on Y within a robust cDAG model. By utilizing this operator, we can employ the “adjustment formula” and various criteria such as “back-door,” “front-door,” and “mediation” to examine the direct causal effects between variables. An important strength of this approach lies in the established assertion that, in the absence of unmeasured confounders, all causal effects are identifiable75,86,87. The following paragraphs summarize the steps for estimating the potential causal effects of GWG and pregestational BMI on TL.
