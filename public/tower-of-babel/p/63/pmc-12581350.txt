Student experiences and learning outcomes of an interprofessional and international service-learning program: an exploratory study
Authors: Patrick Pui Kin Kor, Han Zhi Tan, Alex Pak Lik Tsang, Kin Cheung, Shuk Ping Cheung, Jed Ray Gengoba Montayre, Ka Yan Ko, Ida Ayu Triastuti, The Maria Meiwati Widagdo, Yacobus Christian Prasetyo, Kwanjai Amnatsatsue, Priyoth Kittiteerasack
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare professionals face increasing challenges in cultural diversity and multidisciplinary collaboration, particularly in addressing complex health issues. Traditional training often neglects critical skills such as cultural sensitivity and interprofessional collaboration. International Service-Learning (ISL) and Interprofessional Education (IPE) have been individually shown to develop these competencies. However, integrating ISL and IPE into a single programme has not been widely explored. This study aimed to: 1) explore the outcomes associated with an Interprofessional and International Service-Learning programme (IPISL) in supporting cultural awareness, readiness for interprofessional learning, interprofessional education collaborative competency, and perceived collective efficacy among undergraduate students; 2) explore students’ experiences with the program; and 3) to determine facilitators and barriers to the use of IPISL programme.MethodsA single-group pre-post design was adopted. One hundred twenty-two students from Hong Kong, the Philippines, Thailand, and Indonesia participated in a 2-week IPISL programme from May 2024 to August 2024. Outcomes were measured at baseline and post-programme, including cultural awareness, interprofessional collaborative competency, readiness for interprofessional learning, and perceived collective efficacy. Semi-structured focus group interviews were conducted to explore students’ experiences.ResultsParticipants included undergraduate students from Hong Kong (66.4%, n = 81), Indonesia (26.2%, n = 32), Thailand (4.1%, n = 5), and the Philippines (3.3%, n = 4). Significant improvements were detected in cultural awareness (p < 0.01), interprofessional collaborative competency (p < 0.001), readiness for interprofessional learning (p < 0.01), and collective efficacy (p < 0.05). The qualitative findings indicated that the program facilitated teamwork and group decision-making, enhanced professional skills and experience, promoted reflection on future professional conduct, and enhanced cultural awareness and inclusiveness.ConclusionsIntegrating IPE into ISL may be associated with improvements in students’ cultural awareness, interprofessional collaborative competency, interprofessional learning readiness, and collective efficacy. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples are recommended to evaluate additional teaching and learning outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07970-6.

Introduction

Healthcare professionals frequently encounter challenges rooted in cultural diversity in a multidisciplinary setting, where they engage with individuals whose health philosophies and life experiences differ significantly from their own [1], or the need to collaborate in a multidisciplinary team to handle complex clinical problems [2]. For instance, in the global pandemic, varying cultural attitudes toward health and healthcare have been linked to disparities in patient outcomes [3], while multidisciplinary collaboration is essential to effectively handle the evolving disease and complex aetiologies [4]. It is therefore important to prepare our future healthcare providers to develop culturally sensitive communication and interprofessional collaboration skills. For example, cultural sensitivity can foster trust and improve patient outcomes by respecting diverse health beliefs and practices [5], while collaboration across disciplines is essential for managing complex health challenges, like pandemics or under-resourced care. However, traditional training often neglects these critical skills, leaving professionals unprepared for these upcoming problems.

International service-learning (ISL), which integrates service learning (SL) with study abroad and international education, has been recognised as an effective pedagogy to prepare our future healthcare professionals to become responsible global citizens capable of making significant contributions to their professions and communities [6, 7]. While traditional SL emphasises addressing community needs, ISL places a greater emphasis on cultural awareness [8]. Underpinning ISL is the Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity (DMIS), which describes six stages of growing intercultural understanding. It starts with Denial, where individuals are unaware of cultural differences and avoid engagement with other cultures. This shifts to Defence, where differences are seen as threatening, leading to an “us vs. them” mindset. In Minimisation, people recognise basic similarities across cultures but often overlook deeper distinctions. As understanding deepens, Acceptance emerges, fostering respect for diverse values and behaviours. In Adaptation, individuals adjust their actions and communication to align with other cultures, showing greater flexibility. Finally, in Integration, people incorporate multiple cultural perspectives into their identity, enabling seamless interaction across cultures.

The ISL process can therefore enhance students’ intercultural adaptation through the participatory development of cross-cultural relationships [9]. Previous research has demonstrated the effectiveness of ISL. A study of American nursing students participating in an ISL programme in Guatemala revealed significant improvements in cross-cultural communication, clinical skills, and global health awareness [10]. Other studies found that students who participated in ISL programmes reported an enhanced understanding of global health issues and a strengthened resolve to address these challenges [11, 12]. This approach fosters benefits for both healthcare professional students and the international communities they serve. By gaining insight into global civic health issues and health practices, students will develop a comprehensive global perspective on health education and a more nuanced understanding of the behaviours of individuals from diverse cultural backgrounds.

However, ISL programmes often lack multidisciplinary diversity, which can limit meaningful interactions and group learning [13]. While ISL does involve teamwork, students typically originate from the same discipline or field. This homogeneity can result in a lack of diverse perspectives, thereby limiting the breadth and depth of problem-solving approaches. In the increasingly intricate healthcare system, healthcare professionals from multidisciplinary teams must collaborate to achieve shared objectives and enhance patient experiences and outcomes [14, 15]. In response, the Framework for Action on Interprofessional Education and Collaborative Practice in 2010 from the WHO (World Health Organization) has emphasised the importance of interprofessional education (IPE), which is learning that occurs when two or more professionals learn about, from, and with each other to enable effective collaboration and improve health outcomes [16]. In recent years, there has been a consensus among the international community that healthcare professional students should be prepared for practice through exposure to IPE [17].

There are four core competencies underlying the implementation of IPE outlined by the Interprofessional Education Collaborative (IPEC): (i) Values/Ethics for Interprofessional Practice; (ii) Roles/Responsibilities; (iii) Interprofessional Communication; and (iv) Teams and Teamwork [18]. Shared values and ethical principles are crucial for cultivating mutual respect and trust among healthcare professionals, which in turn facilitates patient-centred decision-making and the effective resolution of potential conflicts of interest or cultural differences [19]. Definitive role definitions reduce ambiguity and conflict in group settings, and a clear understanding of roles prevents disruptions and improves the efficiency of the system as a whole, with each role contributing to the whole [20]. By integrating diverse perspectives and expertise, IPE promotes innovation, such as problem-solving in complex medical problems through different perspective thinking [21]. Additionally, it builds resilience within the team to better adapt to the rapidly evolving healthcare environment [22]. Furthermore, a process wherein students from diverse healthcare professions learn and collaborate collectively has been shown to positively impact teamwork in routine healthcare settings [23].

Previous research has demonstrated that students who have undergone IPE training display significantly higher interprofessional collaborative practice competencies compared to their counterparts who have not received such training [24]. This can be attributed to students’ more positive attitudes toward one another, a deeper understanding of each other’s competencies, the capacity to share knowledge and skills, and an enhanced sense of team identity. A systematic review has also demonstrated the positive impact of IPE on improving learners’ knowledge, skills, and attitudes toward collaborative teamwork [25]. For students in the healthcare profession, IPE also promotes patient safety in nursing practice and the development of a professional identity [26, 27].

While both ISL and IPE possess distinct advantages in preparing future healthcare professionals as outlined above, they are compatible for integration and can promise a more holistic approach to further improving the pedagogical outcomes of student learning while saving educational resources. A common ground in IPE and ISL is an emphasis on social constructivist learning, which emphasises that learning can occur through social interaction, in which collaborative and interprofessional learning in ISL offers students opportunities to construct knowledge in real-world environments [28]. It has been suggested that combining IPE with ISL enhances students’ teamwork skills and understanding of professional roles, leading to more impactful and sustainable learning outcomes [29]. Interdisciplinary collaboration in ISL can also improve cultural sensitivity and teamwork skills, increasing the success of ISL projects [30]. The combination of ISL and IPE has a dual benefit for students. Students are exposed to the challenges of both clinical skill acquisition and interprofessional learning in culturally diverse settings, and they may demonstrate greater perseverance in the face of challenges, thereby promoting personal growth [30]. The combination of IPE and ISL optimises the distribution of educational resources and reduces duplication in pedagogy [31]. It also enhances the advantages of cross-institutional collaboration, sharing resources and reducing implementation costs. This model of collaboration is becoming increasingly important to meet educational goals and ease the pressure on independent institutions to fund education [32]. Overall, IPE and ISL provide a safe learning environment that promotes collaboration among students from diverse cultural and disciplinary backgrounds to address community health challenges. Considering the parallel advancements of IPE and ISL, it is imperative to ascertain how the integration of ISL and IPE can effectively enhance the individual and team competencies of healthcare professional students.

There is limited evidence on the deep integration of ISL and IPE. In previous IPISL initiatives, for example, whilst John and Howell (2017) included reflective journals, there was a lack of extended exposure to IPE [30]. Moreover, previous IPISL programmes lacked the incorporation of Enquiry-Based Learning (EBL).

Grounded in the four core competency domains outlined by the IPEC, EBL can be integrated to facilitate each core competency. EBL tasks are suitable for complex, open-ended public health scenarios, requiring students to identify learning needs and propose collaborative cross-disciplinary solutions. Values and ethics were explored through culturally nuanced multimedia cases, prompting discussion on health equity and mutual respect. Roles and responsibilities were clarified during group planning sessions, as students negotiated task allocation and disciplinary boundaries. Interprofessional communication was strengthened through continuous dialogue during joint health project design and peer feedback. Teamwork was practised and assessed during the delivery of health promotion activities in international community settings. These EBL elements can facilitate learner autonomy, cross-disciplinary synthesis and reflective decision-making, making them particularly ideal for building interprofessional and intercultural competence in diverse learning contexts [33].

While previous IPISL initiatives offer important pedagogical insights, there remains an opportunity to develop a more structured curriculum that integrates [29, 30, 34]. Given the positive outcomes in previous initiatives, a curriculum with structured components informed by relevant pedagogical theories is crucial to ensure sustained, theory-informed development of interprofessional competencies. For instance, IPISL programmes that incorporate preparation, community-based engagement, and iterative reflection offer students deeper and more transferable learning outcomes, particularly in culturally diverse contexts [35]. Previous IPISL initiatives were primarily situated in Western contexts, taking place in North and South American countries with participants who shared broadly similar cultural frameworks. In contrast, our study engaged students from multiple culturally distinct Asian regions and intentionally embedded interprofessional learning within an ISL setting. This design provides additional insights into the cultural dynamics of Eastern cultures within an IPISL programme.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of an interprofessional and international service-learning (IPISL) programme among health professional students from Hong Kong, Indonesia, the Philippines, and Thailand. The objectives of this study were as follows: (1) To examine the learning outcomes associated with an IPISL programme in raising cultural awareness, readiness for interprofessional learning, interprofessional education collaborative competency, and perceived collective efficacy among health professional students; (2) To explore the students’ experiences with the IPISL programme; and (3) To determine facilitators and barriers to the use of the IPISL programme. With a sufficient sample size and a mixed-methods design, this approach offers a more comprehensive evaluation of interprofessional learning in global community health education through ISL.

Methods

Research design

A concurrent mixed-methods design [36] was used to examine changes in students’ experiences and learning outcomes of the IPISL programme among health professional students. This approach is well-suited for examining the complex and dynamic nature of medical education interventions, which involve multifaceted initiatives and interactions amongst diverse stakeholders [37].

The quantitative component utilised a pre-post design to assess the programme’s effectiveness in enhancing students’ cultural awareness, readiness for interprofessional learning, IPE collaborative competency and collective efficacy. These learning outcomes were measured before (T0) and after the programme (T1; Week 2).

The qualitative component involved semi-structured focus group interviews conducted at T1 to address two primary research questions: (1) How do students experience and perceive the IPISL programme? and (2) What are the facilitators and barriers to implementing the programme? This approach provided an in-depth exploration of students’ lived experiences and the contextual factors influencing their learning outcomes.

Data integration was guided by the overarching mixed-methods research question: To what extent do the qualitative findings on students’ experiences complement and explain the quantitative results? This integrative approach offered a holistic evaluation of the IPISL programme’s impact, enabling insights that could not be obtained through a single-method approach.

Study participants and setting

The sample size was determined based on the effect size reported in a meta-analysis of similar educational interventions, which identified a small to medium effect size (SMD = 0.28, p < 0.05) [38]. Using G*Power 3.1, an a priori power analysis indicated that a minimum of 81 participants was needed to detect this effect with a one-tailed paired t-test, with an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.80. To account for an anticipated 20% attrition rate, we set a conservative recruitment target of 100 participants.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling in collaboration with international partners, including Duta Wacana Christian University (Indonesia), Ateneo de Manila University (Philippines), Thammasat University (Thailand) and Mahidol University (Thailand).

All students enrolled in the SL modules ‘Healthy Lifestyle Challenges for Developing Communities’ and ‘Engaging in Workforce Health for Healthcare Workers at Nursing Homes’ during the study period were invited to participate. Recruitment was facilitated via email invitations sent by an independent research assistant (RA). Participation was entirely voluntary and anonymous, with informed consent obtained prior to enrolment. Students were assured that their participation would not affect their academic performance.

The IPISL

The IPISL programme was designed to enhance the four competency domains outlined in the IPEC report: values/ethics, teamwork and team-based practice, communication, and roles and responsibilities for collaborative practice [18]. Through these domains, students can explore their professional identity, gain a deeper understanding of team members’ roles and responsibilities, develop essential communication skills, and learn to collaborate effectively within diverse, interprofessional teams. Additionally, the programme was aligned with the teaching and learning outcomes of the two aforementioned SL subjects.

As part of the IPISL programme, Hong Kong students travelled to either Indonesia, Thailand, or the Philippines to collaborate with local students in conducting health assessments and designing health promotion activities for community participants. The program began with ice-breaking activities, during which students from four participating universities shared traditional foods and lifestyles while discussing the implications of healthy living. To support student learning, an e-library was launched to host open-source teaching materials, including websites, videos, and news articles, on health issues such as unhealthy eating, lack of physical exercise, and addictive behaviours. Students reviewed these pre-workshop materials to build an understanding of the social and cultural factors influencing healthy lifestyles.

EBL shifts students from passive recipients to active constructors of knowledge, fostering deeper learning, critical inquiry, and self-directed skill development [33]. EBL supports both personal responsibility and social interaction, making it particularly well-suited for interdisciplinary and intercultural contexts where learners must navigate ambiguity and diversity. Embedding EBL within a competency-based, interprofessional framework thus offers a pedagogical innovation that enhances cultural awareness, teamwork, and transferable problem-solving abilities. Using an EBL approach [33], the programme incorporated culturally relevant micro-movies adapted for each host country. In the Philippines, students viewed a scenario featuring a Filipina woman living in Hong Kong who encountered difficulties in providing culturally appropriate care to a Chinese adult. In contrast, the Indonesia and Thailand cohorts watched a different but thematically aligned video in which a mixed-heritage daughter brings her Indonesian mother to live with her in Hong Kong, revealing tensions related to daily routines, dietary preferences, and expectations surrounding elderly care. The video highlighted unhealthy lifestyle habits, such as the foreigner consuming fast food and junk food and the older adult engaging in minimal physical activity. It also illustrated the foreigner’s challenges in providing culturally appropriate dietary and physical activity care due to a lack of knowledge about older adults’ needs and cultural differences. Although both scenarios addressed similar cross-cultural health challenges, they differed in narrative perspective and cultural framing. These variations allowed students to reflect on shared themes through culturally specific lenses, encouraging the application of local knowledge and interdisciplinary insights during group discussions and problem-solving activities. Following this, students from diverse cultural backgrounds formed small groups to analyse these challenges, utilizing various resources to propose solutions.

Throughout the programme, students applied lecture-based skills while collaborating with international peers to consider cultural contexts in developing health promotion initiatives. They delivered group presentations, critically evaluated their peers’ health promotion plans, and refined their ideas through constructive feedback. After the workshop, students implemented their plans in international and interprofessional teams, spending two weeks delivering health promotion services directly within local communities.

Data collection

Quantitative data

The following learning outcomes were assessed at T0 and T1.

Cultural awareness

The Modified Cultural Awareness Scale (MCAS) is a 35-item instrument measuring students’ cultural awareness across four subscales: general educational experience, cognitive awareness, clinical issues, and behaviours/comfort with interactions [39, 40]. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater cultural awareness. The MCAS demonstrated high internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.91 at T0, 0.93 at T1).

IPE collaborative competency

The Interprofessional Collaborative Competency Attainment Scale (ICCAS) [41], assessed IPE competencies across six domains: communication, collaboration, roles and responsibilities, patient/family-centred care, conflict management and resolution, and team functioning. The 20-item scale uses a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater competency. The ICCAS showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.98 at T0, 0.97 at T1).

Readiness for interprofessional learning

The Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale (RIPLS) [42], measured students’ attitudes toward shared learning in healthcare education. This 19-item instrument comprises four subscales: teamwork and collaboration, negative professional identity, positive professional identity, and roles and responsibilities. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater readiness for interprofessional learning. The RIPLS demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.88 at T0, 0.89 at T1).

Collective efficacy

Perceived collective efficacy was measured using a modified version of the Generalised Self-Efficacy assessment [43] adapted for work groups [44]. This 4-item scale uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater perceived collective efficacy. The scale showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.90 at T0, 0.88 at T1).

Qualitative data

To better understand students’ learning experiences, we employed semi-structured focus groups for the qualitative component. Focus groups offer a valuable means of capturing interactive reflections and shared experiences, especially when exploring how learners engage with cultural and professional diversity [45]. The semi-structured format also allows flexibility for the facilitator to pursue emerging themes and elicit deeper insights [46]. This approach has been widely employed in IPE settings to explore learners’ development of interprofessional competencies, role understanding, and collaborative behaviour [47]. Participants were selected through purposive sampling to ensure diverse characteristics relevant to their learning experiences, such as major studied and year of study. Four focus groups were conducted, each comprising 4 to 5 students, which ensures in-depth discussion with the coverage of prominent themes [48, 49]. Each focus group was moderated by a trained senior RA, with a second RA serving as note-taker. Each focus group session lasted approximately 45 min. The semi-structured interview guide was developed through literature review and consultation with three nursing education experts. Sessions began with the broad question: “What was your learning experience in the IPISL programme?” Follow-up questions explored programme impacts on their studies, programme elements they valued or found challenging, and perceived gains in skills, knowledge, and other benefits (see Supplementary File 1 for the semi-structured interview guide).

Data analyses

Quantitative data

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS software. Following an initial examination of demographic characteristics and key study variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to assess the normality of data distributions for key study variables, ensuring the selection of appropriate statistical approach for paired pre-post comparisons. Statistical significance was set at an alpha level of 5%.

Qualitative data

Data analysis was performed using NVivo software. All focus groups were audio-recorded, transcribed, and translated into English. Interview data were inductively analysed using Braun and Clark’s [50] reflexive thematic analysis approach. Initially, two researchers independently read and listened to the transcripts and interview recordings repeatedly. After conducting double-coding and collaborative discussions, initial codes were sorted into themes. The researchers analysed the data until reaching the point of data saturation, when no new findings emerged [51]. As suggested by Guest et al. (2017), four focus groups are often sufficient to achieve data saturation, especially when exploring focused issues across heterogeneous yet purposefully selected participant groups [49]. An initial coding framework was generated and reviewed by a third researcher. When analysing the focus group data, the research team recognised the importance of researcher positionality and reflexivity. The researchers acknowledged their roles as embedded in academic settings and regularly challenged how this would shape their interpretation of the focus group data [46]. They maintained a reflexive journal throughout the analysis process. When discrepancies arose during data analysis, the researchers approached and discussed them with the research team until consensus was reached. The final coding framework was developed through ongoing discussions and continuous refinement of themes. The researchers then wrote a report analysing the results, which was circulated to and approved by the entire team [52].

To enhance the trustworthiness of the qualitative data, Lincoln and Guba’s [53] criteria were adopted: credibility (achieved through peer review and participant interview text review), dependability (met through careful data record-keeping, rich descriptions, and external supervisor examination), confirmability (achieved through review by experts in qualitative research and international health education), and transferability (met through detailed documentation of the research process).

Data integration followed a concurrent approach, which enabled the simultaneous exploration of patterns (quantitative) and in-depth meanings (qualitative) [36]. This approach was ideal for exploring the multifaceted nature of experiences in the IPISL programme. The integration of data during analysis employed a side-by-side comparison approach. Additionally, we created a joint display to visually represent the integration of quantitative and qualitative findings.

Results

Quantitative findings

A total of 122 students participated in the IPISL programme, with a mean age of 20.55 years (SD = 1.39; Table 1). Students from Hong Kong comprised the majority of participants (66.4%), followed by those from Indonesia (26.2%), Thailand (4.1%), and the Philippines (3.3%). Female students represented 60.7% of participants, and a large proportion (69.7%) reported no prior foreign travel experience. Only 18% of participants had previously received cultural training. The Shapiro-Wilk tests revealed significant deviations from normality for all key learning outcomes at both T0 and T1 (p < 0.01). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were therefore used to compare the changes in pre- and post-scores on key learning domains. Statistically significant differences were observed across all measures: cultural awareness (pre: 4.93 ± 0.66, post: 5.20 ± 0.76, Z = 2.75, p < 0.01, r = 0.25), interprofessional collaborative competency (pre: 72.84 ± 14.31, post: 80.99 ± 12.63, Z = 3.89, p < 0.001, r = 0.35), readiness for interprofessional learning (pre: 68.36 ± 7.34, post: 71.63 ± 7.72, Z = 2.61, p < 0.01, r = 0.24), and collective efficacy (pre: 15.60 ± 2.54, post: 16.98 ± 2.30, Z = 2.23, p < 0.05, r = 0.20).

In addition, in a subjective assessment of perceived interprofessional collaboration competence, 77% of the students reported a positive change, indicating that their skills were either much better or somewhat better than before. The remaining 23% perceived no difference compared to their previous level, and none of the students reported a decline in their skills following the IPISL programme.

Qualitative findings

Eighteen students participated in the focus groups. Nine were health sciences majors, while nine came from other academic disciplines. Half of the students in the focus group were from Hong Kong, while the remaining half were evenly distributed among the other three countries. The participants represented all four academic years of study. Two themes and six sub-themes were identified regarding students’ experiences in the IPISL programme shown in Fig. 1.

Perceived benefits

The perceived benefits of the program were categorised into three main areas: (1) cooperation and role allocation, (2) professional development and reflective practice, and (3) cultural competence and inclusiveness.

Facilitated the appreciation of role allocation within interprofessional settings

Students described the IPISL programme as a novel and unique opportunity to develop effective role allocation within interprofessional collaboration. The programme required them to work across disciplines, navigate team dynamics, and assign roles to accomplish shared tasks. A health sciences student reflected:“Our non-medical friends didn’t know what posyandu or posbindu were, so as medical students, we had to explain the procedures to them. Through this, we learned about teamwork, where each student had their own role, but we worked towards the same goal. The bonding was already strong from the beginning, and everyone was open and supportive.” (C1).

Broadened cultural awareness and inclusivity

Cultural competence was another key benefit of the IPISL programme. Students reported developing a deeper understanding of cultural differences and learning to adapt to local customs to work effectively within culturally diverse teams. One participant reflected:“I learned to appreciate the differences between the Hong Kong students and our own group, as well as the differences within the community. I also picked up some Javanese phrases since I was the only one who didn’t speak Javanese.” (C4).

Data integration

Quantitative and qualitative data aligned in several key domains, such as collaborative competency, collective efficacy and cultural awareness. Improvements in collaborative competency and collective efficacy seen in the quantitative results were supported by qualitative themes, such as cooperation-driven learning and role allocation, as well as reflective accounts of teamwork and mutual support. Similarly, growth in cultural awareness was corroborated by qualitative insights into cultural competence and inclusiveness, with students describing meaningful intercultural and interprofessional exchanges during the programme, where they became more adept at collaborating to solve practical problems.

However, the findings on readiness for interprofessional learning revealed a nuanced understanding between the quantitative and qualitative data. While quantitative results indicated significant improvements, qualitative findings highlighted challenges, including motivational barriers and uneven participation amongst group members. Some students perceived a lack of motivation amongst certain partners. In group activities, particular individuals exhibited reduced effort, commonly referred to as ‘slacking off’. Although these individuals tended to be in the minority, their behaviour could negatively impact the overall satisfaction and effectiveness of the collaboration [54, 55]. For these individuals, emphasising learning readiness and engagement is crucial. Rather than contradicting the quantitative results, the qualitative insights offer valuable contextualization, helping to explain why learning outcomes may vary and highlighting the importance of facilitating team motivation and engagement. Discussion

Summary of key findings

Overall, the findings of this study suggest positive learning experiences and outcomes associated with the IPISL programme, particularly in improving cultural awareness, readiness for interprofessional learning, collective efficacy, and interprofessional education collaborative competency among healthcare students from Hong Kong, Indonesia, the Philippines, and Thailand. Several perceived benefits of the IPISL programme were reported. Students described how the programme facilitated an appreciation of role allocation within interprofessional settings, as they learned to coordinate tasks through shared responsibilities across disciplines. They also reported professional growth through hands-on experiences, reflected in increased confidence and greater clarity regarding their future roles. Additionally, students highlighted how the programme broadened their cultural awareness and inclusive practice by exposing them to diverse values and customs. However, several challenges were also identified, including tensions arising from cultural norm conflicts, miscommunication due to language barriers, time constraints on activities, and discrepancies in team motivation.

Comparisons with existing literature and theoretical frameworks

Significant pre- and post-programme improvements in cultural awareness were observed, aligning with previous research on ISL programmes, which report similar gains [8]. These findings may be interpreted through the lens of Transformative Learning Theory [56], which posits that learning occurs when individuals are confronted with disorienting experiences that challenge their prior assumptions. In ISL programmes, immersion in unfamiliar cultural settings often generates dissonance, prompting students to re-evaluate their expectations and develop greater intercultural sensitivity [57]. The integration of cultural activities in the IPISL programme, such as community health engagement, promotional activities, and small-group discussions, exposed students to unfamiliar social norms and practices. These experiences disrupted their taken-for-granted understandings, thereby creating opportunities for critical reflection and perspective transformation [58]. The structured inclusion of reflective debriefings and interdisciplinary dialogues further enabled students to examine their own cultural assumptions and recognise how culturally embedded norms influence communication, roles, and expectations in collaborative practice [59]. This process aligns with the notion that transformative learning involves moving from uncritical acceptance of one’s worldview to a more inclusive and integrative understanding of difference. Furthermore, our qualitative findings revealed that students developed a deeper understanding of cultural differences, learned to adapt to local customs to work effectively within culturally diverse teams, and gained an appreciation for inclusivity and cultural sensitivity in healthcare. Developing cultural awareness was particularly important for early-stage undergraduates, as it enabled them to recognise how cultural assumptions influence communication, team roles, and collaboration in care delivery, thereby facilitating the formation of professional identity. During the ISL activities, cultural awareness also allowed students to interpret unfamiliar norms respectfully and contributed to more effective and ethical engagement in interprofessional teams [60]. These findings suggest that the IPISL programme has the potential to foster the development of cultural awareness, which may, in turn, contribute to more effective interprofessional collaboration in a multicultural context.

Notably, the IPISL programme was also associated with pre- and post-programme improvements in collective efficacy. Perceived collective efficacy is a critical factor influencing the goals groups choose to pursue, the effort they invest, and their perseverance when initial efforts fail to yield immediate results, all of which significantly impact learning outcomes [61, 62]. Within the IPISL programme, students were required to communicate and collaborate to achieve shared objectives while navigating the challenges posed by interprofessional and cultural differences. Students reflected critically on the importance of effective communication, which appeared to encourage them to refine role allocations within professional settings. This was evident in the qualitative findings, as students described improvements in articulating their expertise and aligning team objectives to enhance collaboration. The observed improvements in perceived collective efficacy may also suggest a heightened appreciation of the value of collective effort in achieving shared goals [63]. For example, collaborative reflections and hands-on interactions appeared to reshape students’ understanding of their interdependent roles, enhancing their belief in the group’s capacity to achieve shared goals despite challenges [64, 65]. These findings highlight the potential of the IPISL programme to equip students with the attitudinal and collaborative foundations necessary to succeed in the complex and globalised healthcare environment [66].

Importantly, we observed significant pre- and post-programme improvements in collaborative competency, a finding consistent with other studies on IPE [67]. The ISL phase provided students with opportunities to translate their insights into actionable knowledge, such as refining communication strategies or redefining their roles within collaborative tasks. This phase enabled them to test and apply their newly developed strategies in real-world settings, further reinforcing their transformative learning [68]. Our qualitative findings indicate that students valued the IPISL experience as a novel and unique opportunity to develop effective role allocation within professional collaboration. Role allocation functions as a cognitive task that requires students to reflect on their professional identities, question role boundaries, and make sense of interdependent team dynamics. By negotiating tasks and addressing differences in expectations, students are encouraged to challenge their assumptions and adopt a more flexible and complementary understanding of interprofessional and intercultural teamwork. This conceptual shift fosters the development of collaborative competency by encouraging students to embrace more inclusive, adaptable, and team-oriented approaches to shared practice [69]. By engaging meaningfully with diverse communities, students are better equipped with the skills and perspectives needed to navigate the complexities of global healthcare in their future careers [70].

A notable finding is the seemingly contradictory results, where the quantitative data indicated an improvement in readiness for interprofessional learning, while the qualitative findings revealed that some students, particularly those from Hong Kong, exhibited low motivation. By contextualising these findings within the framework of Self-Determination Theory [71], we interpreted the results as complementary, offering a nuanced understanding of the learning that occurred in the IPISL programme. Self-Determination Theory emphasises that intrinsic motivation is achieved by fulfilling the psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness [71]. The observed improvements in readiness for interprofessional learning can perhaps be interpreted as the fulfilment of competence, where reflections during the transformative learning experiences in the EBL activities helped students develop a more holistic understanding of interprofessional learning [72]. However, the gap in intrinsic motivation may signal unmet needs in the domains of autonomy and relatedness. For instance, as supported by the qualitative findings, the programme’s tight schedule and separate accommodations for visiting students may have restricted their sense of autonomy in the learning process. Additionally, limited opportunities for social interaction, compounded by miscommunication due to language barriers and tensions arising from cultural differences, may have hindered students’ ability to develop a sense of relatedness. Without a sense of belonging or strong interpersonal connections, some students may have felt isolated or disengaged, which likely impacted their intrinsic motivation to fully invest in collaborative activities [73]. These findings highlight the importance of designing interprofessional and intercultural learning experiences that go beyond developing competence to actively nurture students’ autonomy and foster meaningful interpersonal connections, thereby sustaining their intrinsic motivation and long-term engagement.

Practical implications and recommendations

To foster students’ intrinsic motivation and enhance learning outcomes, it is vital to address the perceived barriers they face. For example, students reported that tight schedules often compelled them to adopt a task-oriented approach, leaving limited opportunities for meaningful social engagement with international peers. Grounded in Self-Determination Theory, extending preparatory time prior to the exchange can support both autonomy and relatedness. When these fundamental psychological needs are fulfilled within a structured learning environment, students are more likely to exhibit greater autonomy and participate in socially constructed learning experiences. This, in turn, strengthens their sense of relatedness, promoting deeper engagement and sustained motivation, particularly in intercultural and interdisciplinary contexts [74]. Thus, providing additional time for preparatory learning prior to the exchange is crucial, as it fosters a sense of participation in collaborative projects and helps balance workloads among students, thereby promoting autonomous exchange.

Pre-exposure to commonly used expressions, cultural norms, and role expectations can significantly enhance students’ sense of relatedness during their intercultural and interprofessional exchanges. Structured online briefings and guided reviews of open-access materials—hosted in a shared e-library and featuring practical scenarios such as guidance on dress codes, language use, gesture norms, and local health beliefs—can equip students with greater cultural awareness, thereby reducing misunderstandings during fieldwork. By developing initial knowledge and familiarity with intercultural settings, these strategies facilitate more meaningful and effective collaboration [75], while also promoting sustained motivation and emotional engagement throughout the programme [76]. This targeted preparation boosts students’ perceived competence by providing them with tools for effective interaction and reducing the cognitive burden of navigating unfamiliar environments [74]. Such pre-exposure strategies establish a psychologically supportive foundation that nurtures both interpersonal confidence and sustained motivation, maximising students’ ability to learn and contribute in culturally diverse, interprofessional contexts.

Pre-orientation sessions can also play a pivotal role in fostering early social connections and skill acquisition, thereby increasing students’ motivation and engagement in subsequent team-based activities [77]. These sessions provided students with a guided and contextualised entry point into intercultural collaboration, enabling them to engage with cultural norms and communication practices in a more authentic and experientially grounded manner. As the majority of community members often communicate in their native languages or dialects, pre-exposure to commonly used phrases and expressions can significantly improve communication and collaboration, enriching the ISL experience [78]. This pre-orientation to local expressions directly enhanced students’ sense of relatedness, allowing them to initiate conversations and build meaningful connections with community members. By reducing reliance on translation and facilitating more immediate interpersonal interactions, the experience fulfilled the need for relatedness, which is essential for sustained engagement [79]. Furthermore, developing an understanding of fundamental customs, such as traditional attire and gesture preferences, can foster cultural respect, a critical component in promoting effective teamwork [80].

Strengths of this study

A key methodological strength of this study lies in our mixed-methods approach, as previous IPISL research has predominantly relied on small-sample qualitative case studies [29, 30, 34]. Mixed-methods research has been recognised as particularly valuable in IPE research, as it enables the comprehensive evaluation of complex learning processes, including attitudinal, behavioural, and interpersonal changes [81]. This holistic approach facilitates the iterative refinement of programme design by integrating feedback across multiple dimensions of learning [82]. Given the exploratory nature of IPISL and the diversity of learner backgrounds, this methodology allows us to capture both the breadth and depth of student learning outcomes through data triangulation. In turn, these findings provide critical insights that can inform the ongoing optimisation of the IPISL programme, ensuring it maximises students’ transformative learning experiences.

Furthermore, previous ISL programmes were often exclusive to nursing students, focusing primarily on enhancing their cultural awareness and ability to address the healthcare challenges presented by culturally diverse clients [8, 10, 83]. In contrast, the IPISL programme not only retained the core objectives of ISL but also expanded its scope through interprofessional collaboration. By involving students from a range of disciplines, the programme can broaden their cultural awareness through interprofessional teamwork [84], which is critical for addressing complex patient needs, fostering mutual understanding, and ensuring equitable care delivery [85]. Research on IPE has demonstrated its positive impact on students’ perceptions in several key areas: facilitating the exchange of discipline-specific knowledge, enhancing understanding of individual and collective professional responsibilities, reducing communication barriers within hierarchical systems, and improving healthcare delivery for patients [24]. By embedding interprofessional interactions within an international context, the IPISL programme encouraged students to navigate both professional and cultural differences in care delivery. These experiences prompted students to critically reflect on challenges such as language barriers, different teamwork approaches, and cultural norms. This reflective process enabled students to construct new meanings in relation to their professional identity, aligning with the principles of transformative learning by fostering conceptual transformation and personal growth [86]. Quantitative analysis further revealed that students who participated in the programme demonstrated significant improvements in interprofessional collaborative competencies, with over 70% reporting that the programme enhanced their interprofessional collaboration skills.

Given the increasing complexity and challenges of delivering culturally appropriate care [5], the IPISL programme may also offer a valuable opportunity for early exposure to international engagement. Notably, over 60% of students participating in the IPISL programme were in their first or second year of study. Furthermore, more than 80% of students reported that they had not previously participated in any cultural workshops or training, and approximately 70% had never experienced an international exchange. The early cultural exposure facilitated by the IPISL programme thus creates a learning environment that mirrors the real-world settings of multicultural care delivery, as knowledge is most effectively acquired through engagement in authentic, practice-based contexts [87]. After participating in the IPISL programme, students reported developing critical skills in culturally sensitive care, which are essential for improving patient outcomes, enhancing care adherence, and reducing cultural barriers in healthcare [8].

Limitations and directions for future research

Several caveats, however, must be considered when interpreting these positive findings. First, it should be noted that the cultural composition of this study was heavily imbalanced, with approximately 70% of the participants coming from Hong Kong. This imbalance was primarily due to recruitment difficulties and challenges in aligning academic schedules across participating institutions. Consequently, the quantitative results were predominantly influenced by the outcomes of the Hong Kong students, limiting the generalisability of the findings to students from other nationalities. Although we sought to address this limitation by probing the learning experiences of international students through focus groups, the findings of this study should not be generalised to other IPISL programmes with a more balanced cultural composition. Future research could address this limitation by adopting quota-based recruitment strategies to ensure a more balanced cultural composition. Collaborating institutions could coordinate academic calendars and co-develop promotional efforts to enhance student recruitment across sites. Despite these challenges, our IPISL programme provided authentic opportunities for intercultural and interprofessional learning by enabling students from partner countries to engage with peers from diverse educational and cultural backgrounds.

Despite these limitations, our IPISL programme offered authentic opportunities for intercultural and interprofessional learning by enabling students from partner countries to interact with peers from diverse educational and cultural backgrounds. While the programme was primarily delivered in a one-to-many model, where students from host countries interacted with visiting students rather than directly engaging with one another, it still provided meaningful opportunities for transformative learning, as evident in our positive findings. This approach allowed students to gain insights into the complexities of global healthcare through exposure to diverse interprofessional settings, fostering their global awareness and cultural sensitivity [75]. Although direct interaction between students from different host countries was limited, the structure of the programme underscored the importance of intercultural understanding and cross-disciplinary collaboration. To further enhance future IPISL programmes, designs should prioritise multicultural and multidisciplinary peer-level engagement. For example, mixed-nationality student groups could be tasked with jointly designing and delivering community activities to increase intercultural exposure and collaborative learning. Institutional support, such as harmonised timetables, bilingual facilitation, and shared reflection platforms, could further encourage these meaningful interactions across contexts.

Second, while we detected statistically significant changes in learning outcomes, the effect sizes ranged from small to moderate. However, in the context of applied educational interventions, even modest effect sizes can represent meaningful learning gains, particularly when scaled across diverse student groups [88]. Previous IPE research has similarly reported small but significant positive effects on IPE attitudes, which can play a critical role in shaping the early stages of professional development [89]. Moreover, our qualitative findings provide valuable context for interpreting the quantitative results. Specifically, group collaboration enabled students to actively recognise and reflect on cultural differences within their groups, fostering greater inclusiveness and cultural sensitivity through EBL learning activities. This phenomenon is closely tied to the inclusion of international and interprofessional students, whose diverse perspectives enrich the overall learning experience [90, 91]. Future research should consider employing longitudinal designs to track changes in students’ attitudes and learning outcomes over time. Longitudinal studies are better suited to capturing delayed or cumulative effects as students integrate new knowledge and skills into their academic and professional trajectories [92]. This approach would provide a more robust understanding of the IPISL programme’s long-term impact, clarifying whether initial learning gains are sustained, deepened, or translated into professional practice.

In addition, as participants were enrolled in a standard teaching course, implementing a control group was not feasible, as this could have introduced inequities among the students. Without randomisation, it is likely that participants were self-selected individuals with a predisposition towards international exchange experiences or a preference for teamwork, thereby introducing the potential for selection bias in the study. As a result, it remains unclear how the outcomes of the IPISL programme compare to those of students who did not participate, making it difficult to attribute the observed changes exclusively to the programme. Although randomisation poses ethical and logistical challenges in educational settings, future studies could consider matched comparison groups or waitlist controls. These approaches would help isolate the specific impacts of the IPISL programme while reducing the influence of confounding factors.

Conclusion

Notwithstanding these limitations, the IPISL programme was associated with pre- and post-programme improvements in students’ cultural awareness, collective efficacy, interprofessional collaboration competencies, and readiness for interprofessional learning. Participants reported a range of benefits, including enhanced collaboration skills, professional development, and a stronger sense of cultural inclusiveness. These findings suggest that the IPISL programme may be a promising pedagogical approach, as it immerses students in interprofessional, culturally diverse settings while preparing healthcare students to navigate globalised and diverse professional environments. By fostering these skills, the programme has the potential to contribute to improved patient outcomes in increasingly interconnected healthcare systems.
