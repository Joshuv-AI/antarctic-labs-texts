The multiple mediating effects of emotional distress and inhibitory control between physical activity and adolescent internet addiction
Authors: Pingfan Liu, Haioou Peng, Geng Li, Binbin Zhao, Wenju Pang, Xiwen Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to examine the effect of physical activity (PA) on adolescent Internet addiction (IA), as well as the chain-mediating roles of emotional distress (EDs, including depression, anxiety, and stress) and inhibitory control (IC). The findings are intended to provide empirical evidence for the prevention and intervention of adolescent IA. In October 2023, a convenience sampling approach was employed to recruit 900 middle school students from six schools across Shandong, Hebei, Henan, and Hunan provinces in China. Participants included 437 boys (48.6%) and 463 girls (51.4%). Data were collected using the International Physical Activity Scale, Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scale, Internet Addiction Scale, and Inhibitory Control Subscale. Normality tests, descriptive statistics, and Pearson correlation analyses were conducted with SPSS 26.0, while mediation effects were tested using PROCESS 4.1 Model 80. PA showed a significant negative correlation with adolescent EDs and IA, and a significant positive correlation with IC. Model testing revealed that depression significantly mediated the relationship between PA and IA. Furthermore, only stress significantly mediated the relationships among PA, IC, and adolescent IA. This study clarifies the complex relationships among PA, EDs, IC, and adolescent IA. The results suggest that promoting PA and strengthening IC may be effective strategies for reducing IA among adolescents, with particular attention to the dimensions of depression and stress within EDs. These findings have important implications for prevention and intervention efforts.Keywords: Adolescents, Physical activity, Internet addiction, Inhibitory control, Emotional distressSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Internet addiction (IA), alternatively referred to as internet dependence or over-use of the internet, denotes troublesome, obsessive, and overindulgent internet engagement [1, 2]. It can exert detrimental impacts on an individual’s physical and mental health, behavior, and social connections [3–5]. As smartphones and wireless networks have evolved, convenient access to the internet has become a reality [6]. Children and adolescents, whose cognitive, emotional regulation, and social skills are still developing, constitute a high-risk group for internet addiction [7, 8]. In Asian regions, the prevalence of IA among adolescents and young adults can reach 40% [9–12], while in the United States, this proportion can be as high as nearly 10% [13, 14]. Therefore, preventive research on IA, especially among adolescents, commands broad-based societal concern.

Physical activity (PA), as a beneficial and achievable lifestyle behavior, has attracted widespread attention regarding its relationship with IA. The self-regulation resource theory suggests that the essence of IA is that after the depletion of self-regulation resources, individuals find it difficult to resist the impulsive use of the internet [15]. PA can improve the function of the prefrontal cortex of individuals, enhance the reserve of neural cognitive resources, and strengthen the ability of individuals to inhibit excessive use of the internet [16]. Studies have shown that there is a significant negative correlation between PA and adolescent IA [17–21]. PA can also mitigate the negative physical and mental problems resulting from IA [21–24]. Moreover, a review study indicates that different types of exercise can improve the negative psychological problems related to an individual’s IA [25]. Therefore, increasing PA can effectively prevent adolescent IA [26, 27]. Conversely, studies have shown a direct link between lack of PA and adolescent IA [28]. Adolescents who fail to comply with the 24—hour activity behavior guidelines tend to exhibit problematic online behavior [29].

In studies regarding PA and adolescent IA, Emotional distress (EDs), including depression, anxiety, and stress, should also be taken into account. Research has revealed notable positive associations between depression, anxiety, stress, and adolescent IA [30–33]. These EDs also have a positive predictive effect on IA [34–38], and this holds true in longitudinal studies as well [39]. Based on the General Strain Theory [40, 41], when individuals experience EDs, they often seek solace in the virtual world of the internet to avoid these unpleasant emotional states [42–49]. On the other hand, research has shown that acute PA enhances the connections among brain structures in adolescents, substantially decreasing the levels of EDs including depression [50]. In various types of research, PA has been shown to lower the levels of anxiety and depression in adolescents [51–54]. When physical inactivity and sedentary behavior increase, adolescents are more likely to have higher levels of depression [55]. The alleviation of EDs by PA may be related to the shaping of the hippocampal structure [56], and the regulation of hormone levels [57], and during the process of an individual engaging in PA, neurotransmitters such as endorphins and serotonin are secreted [58], and through improving the individual’s emotional regulation ability, it reduces depression, anxiety and stress levels [59, 60], thereby reducing compensatory use of the internet. It decreases the levels of depression, anxiety, and stress by improving individuals’ emotional regulation capacities [61, 62].

To avoid excessive internet use, individuals need to restrain their behaviors. Therefore, the psychological factor that inhibits the undesirable behavior of IA, namely inhibitory control (IC), plays a crucial role [63–66]. IC refers to an individual’s deliberate regulation of behavior, particularly the suppression of those detrimental behaviors [67, 68]. Research has shown that when individuals perform IC tasks, the abnormal activation levels of brain regions are significantly positively associated with IA scores [69, 70], and the association between IA and IC is most pronounced among junior high school students [71]. Both experimental investigations and review research have also found that individuals with IA generally perform poorly in IC tasks [72–74], and IC has a crucial function in the prevention and treatment of IA [75]. Nevertheless, IC has a considerable level of plasticity. Numerous studies have found that both long-term and acute physical activity can enhance an individual’s IC level [76–81], including specific adolescent cohorts [82]. Regular PA can promote the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors, reduce the damage of inflammation to the neurons of the prefrontal cortex, and maintain the structural integrity of the brain regions related to inhibitory control [83]. Therefore, based on the above—mentioned review, this study posits that IC may act as a mediator between PA and adolescent IA. Moreover, the previously mentioned depression, anxiety, and stress also are somewhat related to inhibitory control. Different from PA, these EDs may impair an individual’s IC ability. Studies have found that compared with the healthy control group, depressed patients exhibit notably slower reaction times in IC tasks [84], suggesting compromised IC [85]. Moreover, anxiety can also disrupt the neural recruitment of individuals when they complete IC tasks [86], and undermine the level of IC [87, 88]. Additionally, stress and the resulting stress response can damage the activation of brain regions related to individual inhibitory control, thereby affecting IC ability [89], and stress negatively predicts an individual’s IC level [90].

Overall, this study, based on the prevalence of adolescent IA and the health benefits of PA, and using the core logic of self-regulation resource theory and neuroplasticity theory, constructed a multi-mediator conceptual framework centered on IC(as shown in Fig. 1). Based on the above review, the hypotheses of this study are as follows:

Assumes a mediation model.

H1

There is a significant negative correlation between PA and adolescent IA.

H2

EDs (depression, anxiety, and stress) as mediators between PA and adolescent IA.

H3

IC as a mediator between PA and adolescent IA.

H4

EDs and IC play chain-mediating roles between PA and adolescent IA.

Methods

Participants

In Nov. 2025, via convenience sampling, 1015 middle—school students were recruited from six schools across Shandong, Hebei, Henan, and Hunan provinces in China. Prior to questionnaire distribution, investigators apprised participants of anonymity, confidentiality, and data—handling details. All participants enrolled voluntarily and submitted written informed consent from both themselves and their guardians. Questionnaire completion took under 30 min. Post—data collection, incomplete and patterned or invalid responses were excluded. The final analytical sample included 900 participants (437 boys, 463 girls), with a mean age of 13.85 years (SD = 1.62). Ethical approval was granted by the author’s university ethics committee.

Measures

Physical activity

Use the PA Level Scale to measure the PA level of adolescents. The scale was initially developed by Hashimoto [91], and later translated and validated by Liang et al. [92]. The scale consists of three parts, which evaluate the intensity, duration, and frequency of PA. Each item has five response levels, ranging from 1 to 5. The overall PA score is obtained by multiplying the intensity, duration by 1, and frequency. The higher the score, the higher the level of PA among teenagers. In the current sample, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is 0.660. Previous studies have shown that if the observation frequency of a variable is less than 6 times, a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient higher than 0.6 indicates that the scale is reliable [93].

Internet addiction

This study used the IA Scale developed by Wei based on the social media usage of Chinese adolescents [94]. This scale was originally developed by Elphinston et al. as the "Facebook Invasion Scale" [95], and later revised, adapted, and localized by Wei, and translated into Chinese, it is mainly used for measuring factors related to behavioral addiction and the degree of mobile phone usage [96] and has a high degree of consistency with the core manifestations of internet addiction. As social media has gradually become the main form of internet usage among contemporary teenagers, more and more studies have begun to use the adapted version of this scale to assess the problematic internet use of teenagers. This scale consists of 8 items and is scored using a 5-point Likert scale. In the initial study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.87. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.847, further verifying its internal consistency.

Emotional distress (depression, anxiety and stress)

Using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) to measure EDs in adolescents. This scale was initially developed by Lovibond et al. [97], and later translated and validated by Gong et al. [98]. This scale consists of 21 items and is divided into three subscales: depression, anxiety, and stress. Each subscale contains 7 items. These answers are rated on a 4-point scale, ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’. The total score of each subscale reflects the degree of the corresponding emotional disorder, and the higher the score, the stronger the presence of this emotion in adolescents. In previous studies on adolescent populations, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for depression, anxiety, and stress subscales were 0.77, 0.79, and 0.76, respectively. For this sample, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for depression, anxiety, and stress were 0.823, 0.866, and 0.801, respectively.

Inhibitory control

The inhibitory control sub-scale of the "Executive Function Scale" developed by Huang et al. [99] was used to assess the inhibitory control ability of adolescents. This sub-scale consists of 6 items and uses a three-level rating scale from “never” to “often”. The total score represents the level of inhibitory control ability. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this sub-scale in the original standardized sample was 0.78 and the test–retest reliability was 0.75. Initially, the higher the score, the lower the inhibitory control ability of the adolescents. To conform to the general assumptions of this study, the scores of this scale were reversed-coded. For the current sample, the Cronbach’s coefficient was 0.791.

Statistical analyses

Before conducting formal analyses, the data were screened to ensure compliance with statistical assumptions through data screening. Subsequently, descriptive analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software, and Pearson correlation analysis was used to test the associations between all core variables, providing preliminary empirical evidence for the theoretical model. To test the hypothesized chain mediation effect, this study used the PROCESS macro program (version 4.1) and selected model 80 [100]. Combined with the variable settings of this study, PA was the independent variable (X), IA was the dependent variable (Y), EDs (including depression, anxiety, and stress) were the mediating variables of the first stage (M1-1-M1-3), IC was the core mediating variable of the second stage (M2), and gender, age, only-child status status, and left behind status were the control variables (covariates). The study used bias corrected Bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations) to evaluate the significance of direct effects, individual indirect effects, and total indirect effects. 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for each effect—if CI does not include 0, the effect is considered statistically significant. Simultaneously report the standardized regression coefficient (β) to compare the effect sizes of different pathways, and set the significance level of the two-sided test for all analyses to α = 0.05.

Since this study involves similar variables (i.e., the sub-dimensions of EDs), it is necessary to conduct multicollinearity diagnostics for them, even though they are not subjected to regression analysis with each other. If all Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values are below 5 [101], it is considered that there are no VIF issues among the data. Additionally, this study requires a normality test. Following Kim’s test, data can be regarded as approximately normally distributed if their absolute skewness is below 2 and their absolute kurtosis is below 7 [102]. Furthermore, to further examine the stability of the model, this study conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on the model using AMOS 28.0 and reported the fit indices: χ2/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. The model is considered to have an excellent fit when χ2/df < 3, RMSEA and SRMR < 0.05, and CFI and TLI > 0.9.

Inclusion of covariates

This study included gender, age, only-child status status, and left behind status as covariates because these demographic and group characteristics may have a confounding effect on the relationship between core variables (PA, EDs, IC, IA). Previous studies have shown that gender is significantly associated with the incidence of adolescent IA and PA participation tendency [103]; Age, as a key stage marker for the development of cognitive function and emotional regulation ability in adolescents, is closely related to IC level, internet usage behavior, and negative emotional intensity [104]; There are inherent differences in online social behavior between only children and only-child status, such as the higher number of Weibo posts and original content sharing frequency among only children compared to only-child status, while the breadth of social interaction is relatively weak. These differences may indirectly affect the risk of developing IA [105]; Left behind (groups where both parents or one of them is forced to stay in registered residence for a long time (usually defined as half a year or more) to work as an immigrant and supervised by grandparents, grandparents or other relatives due to job hunting and other reasons) and on left behind adolescents are related to lack of family support, accumulation of EDs and increased frequency of Internet use. Left behind adolescents are more likely to seek emotional compensation through the Internet, which may interfere with the true correlation between core variables [106].Therefore, including the above covariates can effectively control for potential confounding effects and ensure that the testing results of the mediating mechanism of PA affecting IA are more accurate and reliable.

Results

Common method deviation test

Findings from Harman’s single—factor test indicated that, in the absence of principal component factor rotation, three factors had eigenvalues exceeding 1. The first factor explained 34.70% of the variance, and this percentage was lower than 40% [107]. Hence, this study did not exhibit notable common method bias.

Discussion

This study aims to explore the relationship between PA and adolescent IA, as well as the potential roles of depression, anxiety, stress and IC in this relationship. The results show that PA in adolescents is negatively correlated with IA, depression, anxiety and stress, and positively correlated with IC. IA is positively correlated with depression, anxiety and stress in adolescents. IC, as a mediating variable, is negatively correlated with IA, depression, anxiety and stress in adolescents. Meanwhile, PA is also negatively correlated with adolescent IA directly or indirectly through IC.

Relationship between physical activity and adolescent internet addiction

This research uncovers an inverse correlation between PA and adolescent IA, lending support to the first hypothesis. Prior studies have likewise indicated a negative relationship between PA and IA [17]. PA not only has a negative correlation with IA, but also mitigates the link between IA and physical and mental symptoms [22], reduces loneliness and tenuous interpersonal relationships [25], and inhibits reward—seeking impulsivity [108]. Hence, promoting PA as an intervention strategy for adolescent IA [26, 27] is a beneficial lifestyle option. Based on the social adaptation theory [109], frequent engagement in PA enhances physical fitness, promotes psychological and social development, and aids in developing interpersonal communication abilities, enriches their coping mechanisms and problem—solving methods [110, 111]. Consequently, comprehensive social skills, interpersonal relationships, and other social adaptation capabilities are enhanced, decreasing the probability of IA.

Mediating role of emotional distress

The research results indicate that depression and stress play a partial mediating role in the association between PA and adolescent IA, with the correlation of stress being relatively high. This result is consistent with previous studies [40, 105, 112, 113]. Adolescents frequently encounter various stressors, and interpersonal stress and academic performance stress are key factors leading to adolescent IA [114]. As the level of stress increases, the degree of psychological needs satisfaction decreases [115, 116]. According to the self-determination theory [117], when psychological needs are unmet, adolescents often turn to the internet for compensation [118]. In our model, stress shows the most significant mediating role, followed by depression, whereas anxiety does not demonstrate significant mediating importance. Among these three types of EDs, stress may be a crucial psychological factor given its prevalence among adolescents owing to common stressors such as interpersonal and academic problems [119, 120].

Mediating role of inhibitory control

In this study, IC plays a crucial mediating role through two pathways. Firstly, IC acts as an independent mediator between PA and IA. The sequence of its effect is that PA enhances the IC ability of teenagers, which indirectly reduces the level of IA. The neuroplasticity theory suggests that PA can promote neuroplasticity in brain regions closely related to IC, promote neuronal and synaptic remodeling, and enhance an individual’s ability to inhibit impulsive behaviors and make controlled behavioral decisions [121]. When teenagers have stronger IC ability, they can better reduce the occurrence of impulsive behaviors such as IA. Previous classic neuropsychological studies using the Stop-Signal Task and the Go/No-Go Task have found that when adolescents have stronger IC abilities, they are able to better reduce the occurrence of impulsive behaviors (such as IA) and exhibit a lower error response rate [122]. Furthermore, studies conducted by Li et al. using self-report tools such as the Behavioral Inhibition Questionnaire also indicate that adolescents who frequently engage in physical activities typically possess higher levels of behavioral inhibition ability and self-regulation skills [123], stronger inhibitory control abilities can help teenagers reduce impulsive internet usage behaviors, thereby reducing the risk of developing internet addiction.

Secondly, there is a pathway from PA to stress and subsequently to IC from PA to stress, and then to IC, which affects IA. Previous studies [121, 124–126], have shown that stress causes changes in the brain structure and cognitive decision-making and IC ability [79, 127] of teenagers, thereby affecting impulsive behaviors such as IA [128]. Previous studies have also pointed out that adolescents in early and middle adolescence are very sensitive to stress, especially in the prefrontal cortex regions related to IC and substance use sensitivity [129]. The cognitive impairment model of stress indicates that long-term stress leads to excessive release of cortisol, thereby reducing synaptic transmission of neurons in the prefrontal cortex and lowering the IC level of individuals [89], causing the occurrence of IA behaviors. Therefore, IC may mediate the association between stress and IA [130], thereby generating addiction related to stress.

Advantages, limitations, and future directions

This study adopts a cross provincial research sample design, covering 6 middle schools in four provinces of Shandong, Hebei, Henan, and Hunan. The final effective sample is 900, covering adolescents from different regions to avoid the limitations of studying in a single region; Creatively incorporating multidimensional neural networks such as depression, anxiety, and stress as mediating factors, constructing a parallel mediation model with multiple pathways of "PA → EDs/IC → IA", integrating cognitive and multidimensional emotional mediation, quantifying the effects of each pathway, and systematically revealing the underlying mechanisms. However, this study also has some drawbacks. The cross-sectional design can only reveal variable associations and cannot clarify temporal causality, making it difficult to exclude reverse effects and confounding variables; Relying on self-report scales and lacking objective indicator cross validation may lead to subjective bias; A limited sample size may affect the interpretation of results across different regions and cultures. In the future, the sample coverage should be expanded, objective behavioral and neurophysiological indicators should be integrated, and measurement bias should be reduced; Conduct longitudinal tracking and use a cross lagged panel model to clarify causal time series; Introduce moderating variables to construct a moderated mediation model and explore group differences.

Conclusion

This study provides an important understanding of the core psychological factors of the relationship between PA and IA in adolescents. PA not only directly reduces the possibility of IA in adolescents, but is also negatively correlated with depression and stress, and thus is associated with the reduction of IA. In addition, PA is positively correlated with IC in adolescents, while IC is negatively correlated with IA. Meanwhile, PA is negatively correlated with pressure, and stress will further affect IC and IA.
