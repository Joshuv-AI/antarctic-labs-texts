Effectiveness of the 10-valent pneumococcal non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae protein D conjugate vaccine (PCV10) against hypoxic pneumonia in children in Maha Sarakham Province, Thailand
Authors: Kengkaj Unrit, Kulkanya Chokephaibulkit, Supasin Deeraksa, Pope Kosalaraksa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypoxic pneumonia is a major cause of morbidity and mortality among children in low- and middle-income countries. This study evaluated 10-valent pneumococcal non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae protein D conjugate vaccine (PCV10) effectiveness in preventing hypoxic pneumonia among children in Maha Sarakham Province, Thailand. We conducted a retrospective case–control study using medical records from Maha Sarakham Hospital between January 2020 and December 2022. Cases were children aged 1 month to 5 y hospitalized with hypoxic pneumonia; controls were hospitalized with non-hypoxic pneumonia. PCV10 vaccination status (unvaccinated, incomplete [1–2 doses], or complete [≥3 doses]) was compared. Vaccine effectiveness (VE) was estimated using logistic regression, with propensity score adjustment and multiple imputation to address confounding and missing data. Among 398 children hospitalized with pneumonia, 207 (52.0%) had hypoxic pneumonia and 191 (48.0%) had non-hypoxic pneumonia. Overall vaccine coverage was low: 18.3% were fully vaccinated, 3.5% partially vaccinated, and 78.1% unvaccinated. The unadjusted VE against hypoxic pneumonia was 28% (95% CI: 3–55%; P < .001). After propensity score adjustment, VE increased to 31% (95% CI: 8–62%; P < .001). Using multiple imputation, the unadjusted and adjusted VE were 34% (95% CI: 5–61%; P < .001) and 35% (95% CI: 4–65%; P < .001). In conclusion, PCV10 vaccination was associated with a reduced risk of hypoxic pneumonia among Thai children. These findings support the integration of PCVs into national immunization programs and underscore the importance of achieving and maintaining high vaccine coverage to prevent severe pediatric pneumonia.KEYWORDS: Vaccine effectiveness, PCV10, hypoxic pneumonia, severe pneumonia, pediatric pneumonia, case-control study, immunization programs, Thailand

Introduction

Pneumonia is a common respiratory infection and remains a leading cause of mortality among children under five years of age worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1–3 The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that pneumonia and diarrhea together account for 23% of under-five mortality, resulting in approximately 1.17 million deaths annually among children under five years of age globally.4 Despite substantial progress during the Millennium Development Goal period (2000–2015) – when the global incidence of pneumonia declined by 30% (from 178 million to 138 million episodes) and mortality decreased by 51% (from 1.7 million to 0.9 million deaths)3 – pneumonia remains a major, largely preventable cause of childhood death. The WHO fact sheet underscores that achieving the Sustainable Development Goal target for reducing child mortality (SDG 3.2.1) will require urgent prioritization of efforts to eliminate preventable pneumonia-related deaths.5

Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcus), which comprises more than 100 identified serotypes,6 is a leading bacterial cause of severe pneumonia and pneumonia-related mortality in children. The WHO estimates that S. pneumoniae causes approximately 300,000 deaths annually among children under five years of age worldwide.7 Childhood pneumonia frequently presents with severe clinical manifestations and may lead to complications such as pleural effusion, lung abscess, sepsis, and severe hypoxia, which can result in rapid clinical deterioration and death.8–10

Vaccination represents a cornerstone of childhood pneumonia prevention. The introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) has led to substantial reductions in invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) and pneumonia among vaccinated children.11–13 In addition to direct protection, PCV programs have demonstrated robust indirect (herd) effects, whereby vaccination of young children reduces pneumococcal transmission and disease among unvaccinated populations, including older adults.14–16 A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that childhood PCV immunization confers significant indirect protection against IPD in unvaccinated age groups, with herd immunity effects accumulating over time and achieving approximately a 90% reduction in disease caused by vaccine serotypes after nine years of program implementation.16 These effects are mediated through reductions in nasopharyngeal carriage and transmission of vaccine serotypes among vaccinated children, who serve as the primary reservoir of S. pneumoniae within the community.17

Randomized controlled trials have shown that PCVs reduce overall childhood pneumonia incidence by approximately 20–37%.18 Real-world effectiveness studies from LMICs have demonstrated comparable or greater benefits. In Papua New Guinea, PCV13 was associated with a 28.7% reduction (95% CI: 9.9–43.6%) in hypoxic pneumonia and a 57.4% reduction (95% CI: 38.0–70.7%) in pneumonia hospitalizations.19 Furthermore, a multi-country modeling study estimated that PCV13 could prevent approximately 697,000 deaths (95% credibility interval: 359,000–1,040,000), avert 46.0 million disability-adjusted life-years, and prevent 131 million cases of disease across 112 LMICs between 2000 and 2030.13 Collectively, these vaccines have contributed to substantial reductions in infant and child mortality, including pneumonia-related deaths, particularly in resource-limited settings.20 Accordingly, the WHO recommends inclusion of PCVs in all national immunization programs, emphasizing their role as one of the most effective interventions for preventing childhood pneumonia deaths.17

In Thailand, PCVs are not currently included in the National Immunization Program. To inform national policy decisions regarding feasibility and acceptability, a pilot immunization program was initiated in Maha Sarakham Province in 2020. This program utilizes the 10-valent pneumococcal non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae protein D conjugate vaccine (PCV10; Synflorix®, GlaxoSmithKline Biologicals, Rixensart, Belgium), administered according to a 2+1 schedule at 2, 4, and 12 months of age, consistent with WHO recommendations for resource-limited settings.17 The program currently provides vaccination only to the newly eligible age cohort according to the schedule, without a catch-up component.

Although the efficacy and effectiveness of PCVs have been well documented across diverse populations,18–20 real-world data on PCV10 effectiveness against severe clinical outcomes – particularly hypoxic pneumonia – remain limited in Southeast Asia. This evidence gap is important, as hypoxic pneumonia represents a critical disease endpoint associated with high morbidity, mortality, and healthcare resource utilization. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the real-world effectiveness of PCV10 in preventing hypoxic pneumonia among children hospitalized in Maha Sarakham Province, Thailand, to provide locally relevant evidence to inform national vaccination policy and pneumonia prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective case-control study was conducted at Maha Sarakham Hospital, a 500-bed provincial hospital in Northeast Thailand providing secondary care to approximately 950,000 residents. We analyzed data from children admitted between January 2020 and December 2022. The study protocol was designed following the test-negative design framework, a widely accepted epidemiological approach for estimating vaccine effectiveness (VE) in real-world settings.21 The study received ethical approval from the Maha Sarakham Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (Reference: MSKH_REC 66–01-025.COA 66/021) on June 1, 2023. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Study population and case definition

Eligible participants were children aged 1 month to 5 y who were admitted during the study period with a discharge diagnosis of pneumonia. Cases were defined as children with hypoxic pneumonia, characterized by clinical pneumonia – diagnosed according to hospital guidelines based on clinical signs (including fever, cough, tachypnea, and respiratory distress) together with radiographic evidence of consolidation or infiltrates on chest X-ray – and documented hypoxia requiring supplemental oxygen therapy to maintain peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) ≥90%, or persistent SpO2 <90% on room air during hospitalization. Controls were children admitted during the same period with non-hypoxic pneumonia, defined as clinically and radiographically confirmed pneumonia without documented hypoxia meeting the case definition. Children were excluded if they had: (1) incomplete medical records regarding vaccination status or clinical outcomes; (2) transfer from another facility without a confirmed vaccination history; or (3) an age greater than 5 y. Participant selection followed a systematic process, as detailed in the study’s flowchart (Figure 1).

Data collection and variables

Data were retrospectively extracted from paper-based hospital medical records using a standardized data collection form. Potential cases were identified using ICD-10 codes for pneumonia (J09–J18.9). Hospital numbers were used to retrieve medical records. All records were scanned in full, and original paper documents were retained as backup. No medical records were missing. Trained research assistants, under the supervision of a pediatrician, reviewed all eligible records to ensure data accuracy and completeness. Variables collected for analysis included demographic and socioeconomic characteristics: age (months), sex, type of community (urban or rural residence), year of admission (2020, 2021, or 2022), number of household members, number of children under 5 y of age in the household, and household smoking exposure.

Clinical and anthropometric variables included weight-for-age z-scores calculated using WHO growth standards, with malnutrition defined as a z-score <−2 standard deviations (SD). Data on comorbidities were collected, including congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease, asthma, neurological disorders, and immunodeficiency. A documented history of COVID-19 coinfection during hospitalization was also recorded.

Treatment and outcome variables included receipt of antibacterial therapy during hospitalization, length of hospital stay (days), and discharge disposition (improved or cured, transferred to a higher-level facility, or death).

PCV10 vaccination status was obtained from the National Immunization Registry and hospital medical records. Although immunization records from private hospitals are not linked to the national immunization registry, healthcare workers in both private and public hospitals record administered vaccines in each child’s personal vaccination booklet as proof of vaccination. This is done for all Thai children. Vaccination status was categorized as unvaccinated (no documented doses), incomplete (1–2 doses), or complete (≥3 doses administered according to Thailand’s national immunization schedule at 2, 4, and 12 months of age).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized patient characteristics using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. Bivariable analyses compared characteristics between cases (hypoxic pneumonia) and controls (non-hypoxic pneumonia) using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed continuous variables. The association between PCV10 vaccination and hypoxic pneumonia was estimated using multivariable logistic regression. The primary exposure was PCV10 vaccination status (unvaccinated, incomplete, or complete vaccination as the reference category). Covariates were selected for inclusion in the adjusted model based on: (1) bivariable associations with p-values <.10; (2) established confounding based on prior literature; or (3) clinical relevance. Final model variables included: type of community (urban/rural), presence of comorbidities, receipt of antibacterial treatment, and PCV10 vaccination status. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using standard logistic regression techniques.22

The primary analysis included participants with complete data on all variables (complete case analysis, n = 364). To assess the robustness of findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted using multiple imputations to address missing data. All variables were imputed using Stata’s “mi impute” command, and analyses were performed on the imputed dataset (n = 398).

Propensity score (PS) adjustment was performed as a secondary approach to control confounding. PS for complete PCV10 vaccination (≥3 doses) was calculated using logistic regression with covariates: type of community, comorbidities, and antibacterial treatment. Both unadjusted and PS-adjusted vaccine effectiveness estimates were calculated for complete case and imputed datasets. VE was estimated as (1–aOR) × 100%. Statistical significance was defined as p < .05. Analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp LP. College Station, TX, USA).23

Results

Study population and characteristics

The study included 398 children with clinically- and radiographically-confirmed pneumonia (median age, 17.5 months; IQR, 10–36 months), of whom 207 (52.0%) had hypoxic pneumonia and 191 (48.0%) had non-hypoxic pneumonia (Table 1). One hundred and fifty-one cases enrolled in 2020, when Alpha and Beta strains of SARS-CoV-2 were most prevalent; 56 cases enrolled in 2021, when Delta, and subsequently, Omicron strains of SARS-CoV-2 were most prevalent; and 191 cases enrolled in 2022, when the Omicron strain and its subvariants were most prevalent (Table 1).

The cohort was predominantly male (56.8%) and had a median weight-for-age z-score of −0.29 (IQR, −1.57 to 1.07). Comorbidities were present in 39.7% of children. PCV10 vaccination coverage was low: 78.1% were unvaccinated, 3.5% were incompletely vaccinated (1–2 doses), and 18.3% were completely vaccinated (≥3 doses). Of these vaccinated cases (n = 87), 7 children (8.0%) received a PCV10 dose through private healthcare centers. The overall case-fatality rate was 1.0% and 1.4% among children with hypoxic pneumonia. Bivariable comparisons

Cases and controls did not differ significantly with respect to age, sex, nutritional status, year of enrollment, household size, household smoking exposure, or discharge outcomes (all P > .05). However, several factors were significantly associated with hypoxic pneumonia. Children with hypoxic pneumonia were more likely to reside in rural areas (56.5% vs 44.0%, P = .012), have a greater number of children under 5 y of age in the household (median, 2 vs 1; P = .023), and have documented COVID-19 coinfection (4.4% vs 0.5%, P = .021). Comorbidities – primarily congenital heart disease, prematurity, and low birth weight – were more common among cases than controls (47.8% vs 30.9%; P < .001; Table 1). In addition, cases were more likely to have received prior antibacterial treatment during hospitalization (87.9% vs 72.8%, P < .001) and experienced longer hospital stays (median, 3 vs 2 d; P < .001).

Most notably, PCV10 vaccination status differed significantly between groups (P < .001). Among children with hypoxic pneumonia, 94.2% were unvaccinated, 1.9% were incompletely vaccinated, and 3.9% were completely vaccinated. In contrast, among children with non-hypoxic pneumonia, 60.7% were unvaccinated, 5.2% were incompletely vaccinated, and 34.0% were completely vaccinated, suggesting a strong protective association of complete PCV10 vaccination against hypoxic pneumonia (Table 1).

Multivariable analysis of risk factors for hypoxic pneumonia

The results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis examining factors associated with hypoxic pneumonia were presented in Table 2. After adjustment for potential confounders, receipt of antibacterial treatment during hospitalization was significantly associated with increased odds of hypoxic pneumonia (aOR, 2.79; 95% CI, 1.55–5.02; P = .005). PCV10 vaccination status demonstrated the strongest association with hypoxic pneumonia. Compared with children who completed the three-dose vaccination series, unvaccinated children had markedly higher odds of developing hypoxic pneumonia (aOR, 33.68; 95% CI, 13.39–84.70; P < .001). Children with incomplete vaccination (1–2 doses) also showed elevated odds of hypoxic pneumonia (aOR, 4.38; 95% CI, 0.99–19.33; P < .001). These associations remained robust in the multivariable model, indicating a strong protective effect of complete PCV10 vaccination independent of other risk factors.

Children residing in rural areas had increased odds of hypoxic pneumonia in crude analysis (odds ratio [OR], 1.66; 95% CI, 1.11–2.46; P = .012); however, this association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after multivariable adjustment (aOR, 1.28; 95% CI, 0.79–2.07; P = .312). Vaccine effectiveness against hypoxic pneumonia

PCV10 VE against hypoxic pneumonia by number of doses received is presented in Table 3. For children who completed the three-dose vaccination series, the unadjusted VE in the complete-case analysis was 28% (95% CI, 3–55%; P < .001), increasing to 31% (95% CI, 8–62%; P < .001) after PS adjustment. Analyses using multiple imputation yielded comparable results, with an unadjusted VE of 34% (95% CI, 5–61%; P < .001) and an adjusted VE of 35% (95% CI, 4–65%; P < .001).

A dose–response relationship was observed. In both complete-case and multiple imputation analyses, two vaccine doses were associated with slightly greater protection against hypoxic pneumonia (adjusted VE, 14–16%; P < .05) compared with a single dose (adjusted VE, 5–7%; P > .05), which conferred minimal and non-significant protection. The consistency of VE estimates across analytical approaches supports a significant protective effect of complete PCV10 vaccination against hypoxic pneumonia among hospitalized children.

Discussion

This retrospective test-negative case–control study provides important real-world evidence on the effectiveness of PCV10 against hypoxic pneumonia among children hospitalized in Maha Sarakham Province, Thailand. We demonstrated a protective effect of complete PCV10 vaccination, with VE ranging from 31% to 35% across analytical approaches. These findings have significant implications for Thailand’s immunization policy and pneumonia prevention strategies, particularly given the substantial burden of severe pediatric pneumonia in this region.

In multivariable analysis, unvaccinated children had more than 30-fold higher odds of developing hypoxic pneumonia compared with children who completed the three-dose vaccination series, while those who received incomplete vaccination (1–2 doses) had more than four-fold increased odds. These strong associations translated into clinically meaningful VE estimates. The consistency of VE estimates across unadjusted, PS–adjusted, and multiple imputation analyses reinforces the robustness of our findings. Moreover, we observed a clear dose–response relationship: receipt of two doses provided approximately half the protection observed with the complete three-dose series, whereas a single dose conferred no significant protection. These findings underscore the importance of completing the full vaccination series to achieve optimal protection against severe pneumonia.

Receipt of antibacterial treatment during hospitalization was also associated with hypoxic pneumonia, likely reflecting reverse causation, whereby more severely ill children were more likely to receive empiric antibiotic therapy either before or shortly after hospital presentation. This observation highlights the challenge of disentangling disease severity from treatment effects in retrospective observational studies.

Our findings are consistent with accumulating evidence from diverse settings demonstrating pneumococcal conjugate VE against severe pneumonia outcomes. In Papua New Guinea, Blyth et al.19 reported that PCV13 was associated with a 28.7% reduction (95% CI: 9.9–43.6%) in hypoxic pneumonia and a 57.4% reduction (95% CI: 38.0–70.7%) in pneumonia hospitalizations. Similarly, a study from Lao PDR reported PCV13 effectiveness of 35% (95% CI: 13–52%) against hypoxic pneumonia among children under five years of age.24 Our observed VE estimates of 31–35% for PCV10 fall within this range, suggesting comparable protection despite differences in vaccine valency, epidemiology, and population characteristics. In The Gambia, a randomized trial of a nine-valent PCV demonstrated a similar trend, with 37% efficacy (95% CI: 27–45%) against radiologically confirmed pneumonia.19 Importantly, our study extends the existing evidence base by demonstrating PCV10 effectiveness specifically against hypoxic pneumonia–a critical, life-threatening manifestation–in a Southeast Asian setting where such data have been limited.

The proportion of hypoxic pneumonia cases in our study (52.0%) was notably higher than that reported in studies from Northern India (35.9%)25 and Lao PDR (34.5%).24 Several factors may explain this difference. First, our case definition required radiographic confirmation of pneumonia, potentially selecting for more severe cases. Second, delays in healthcare-seeking behavior may have resulted in children presenting to hospital at more advanced stages of illness. Third, variations in hypoxia definitions, SpO2 thresholds, and measurement protocols across studies may have influenced case classification. Despite the high proportion of hypoxic pneumonia, the in-hospital case-fatality rate among hypoxic cases (1.4%) was comparable to that reported in other LMIC settings,25 suggesting effective inpatient management. Nevertheless, the substantial burden of hypoxic pneumonia observed highlights the urgent need for effective preventive interventions.

PCV10 vaccination coverage in our study population was notably low, with only 18.3% of children completing the three-dose series – substantially lower than the 32.7% coverage reported in a comparable study from Papua New Guinea.19 However, our three-dose coverage was comparable to the overall service results of the PCV10 program in Maha Sarakham Province from September 2021 to September 2022, which reported a vaccination coverage rate of 10.0% for three doses.26 The low uptake likely reflects several factors, including the introduction of PCV10 in Maha Sarakham Province in 2020 coinciding with disruptions to healthcare services during the COVID-19 pandemic, challenges in timely completion of multi-dose schedules, potential gaps in parental awareness or access to vaccination services, and reduced overall vaccination accessibility due to inadequate management of distributed vaccines in the province, particularly during the pandemic. This pilot program is ongoing and will expand nationwide in September 2026. Although settings with low vaccine coverage may theoretically have higher vaccine-serotype coverage due to limited serotype replacement,15,16 the low uptake observed represents a missed opportunity for population-level impact. The WHO has emphasized that PCVs are among the most effective interventions for preventing childhood pneumonia deaths,17 and achieving high coverage is essential for both direct and indirect (herd) protection.14–16 Our findings – demonstrating substantial VE despite low coverage – provide compelling evidence to support intensified vaccination efforts and policy decisions regarding national PCV implementation.

This study has several notable strengths. Pneumonia diagnoses were made by pediatricians using standardized hospital protocols integrating clinical and radiographic criteria, ensuring diagnostic consistency. Hypoxic status was objectively assessed using calibrated pulse oximeters with documented SpO2 measurements, minimizing outcome misclassification. Comprehensive vaccination data from the provincial immunization registry eliminated recall bias. The use of multiple complementary analytical approaches – including PS adjustment and multiple imputation – enhanced confidence in the robustness of our findings.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. As a retrospective study, information bias may have occurred due to incomplete documentation of potential confounders such as detailed socioeconomic indicators, viral coinfections, and duration of illness prior to admission. The wide CIs for vaccination-related estimates reflect the small number of fully vaccinated children, limiting statistical precision. The single-center design may limit generalizability to other Thai regions with different vaccination coverage, healthcare access, or pneumococcal epidemiology. Additionally, residual confounding related to nutritional status, indoor air pollution, or healthcare-seeking behaviors cannot be excluded. Lastly, we were unable to assess serotype-specific VE due to the lack of microbiological confirmation. However, data concerning serotype distribution of S. pneumoniae-causing IPD in children ≤5 y across Thailand between 2017 to 2025 was recently presented as an abstract at the World Society for Pediatric Infectious Diseases.27 From 121 pneumococcal isolates from sterile-site specimens, the predominant serotypes included ST14 (30%), ST19A (15%), ST5 (10%), ST6B (10%), and ST23F (10%).27 Future province- and serotype-specific research is warranted.

Future research should also focus on evaluating strategies to improve PCV accessibility, uptake, and timely completion of the vaccination series in resource-limited settings, alongside implementation studies to identify barriers to vaccination such as parental awareness, socioeconomic factors, and health system constraints. Longitudinal and prospective studies are needed to assess the long-term impact of PCV on hypoxic pneumonia, invasive pneumococcal disease, and nasopharyngeal carriage, including the duration of vaccine-induced protection. In addition, strengthened surveillance of S. pneumoniae serotypes in both disease and carriage is essential to monitor serotype replacement and inform future vaccine strategies. Economic evaluations, including cost-effectiveness and budget impact analyses, would further support evidence-based decision-making for sustainable PCV implementation.

In conclusion, this study provides real-world evidence that PCV10 vaccination is effective in reducing the risk of hypoxic pneumonia among hospitalized Thai children. Complete vaccination with three doses was associated with significant protection. Despite the substantial burden of hypoxic pneumonia, vaccine coverage in the study population was low. These findings support the inclusion of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines in Thailand’s National Immunization Program and highlight the importance of achieving timely completion of the full vaccination schedule to prevent severe pediatric pneumonia.
