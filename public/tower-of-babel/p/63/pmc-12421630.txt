Reprogramming the immunologically cold landscape of prostate cancer through MAOA inhibition
Authors: Anil K Singh, Boyang Jason Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer (PC) is notoriously known for exhibiting an immunologically cold phenotype in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), leading to the need for interventions to enhance immunotherapy efficacy. Recent findings by Zhao et al. in the Journal for ImmunoTherapy of Cancer identified stromal monoamine oxidase A (MAOA), a key enzyme that degrades monoamine neurotransmitters and plays a role in the neuroendocrine system, as a critical regulator of the immune response to PC. Altering MAOA levels in myofibroblastic cancer-associated fibroblasts, either genetically or pharmacologically, can reprogram PC’s TIME to modulate CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity through the WNT5A-Ca²+-NFATC1 signaling axis, highlighting the stromal influences on CD8+ T cell cytotoxic activity within the TIME. The inactivation of MAOA synergizes with immune checkpoint blockade therapies to reverse the trajectory of prostate tumor growth. This work offers a promising therapeutic avenue for PC by positioning MAOA as a stromal modulator of immune response as well as a target for combination immunotherapies. The current commentary aims to present our perspective on how a metabolic enzyme can change the immune landscape of the tumor microenvironment, what we have learned, and what we can develop in the future.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Prostate Cancer, Stroma

Introduction

Immunotherapy has transformed the treatment landscape for several types of cancer over the past decade, including liquid tumors, melanoma, and non-small cell lung cancer. However, success in treating other malignancies, particularly those with an immunologically cold phenotype, remains limited.1 These cancers exhibit little to no pre-existing immune activity, rendering them resistant to conventional immunotherapies like immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies (e.g., programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors), which suppress immune checkpoint proteins and their inhibitory signals of T cell activation to promote antitumor immune responses. An excellent representative of immunologically cold tumors, prostate cancer (PC) is often characterized by low immune cell infiltration, a lack of effective T-cell responses against cancer cells, and a high tissue density of immunosuppressive FOXP3+ regulatory T cells, making it primarily unresponsive to ICB therapies.1 2 The stromal microenvironment poses a central obstacle to effective immune engagement in PC. Stromal cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) coordinate immune exclusion and therapeutic resistance.3 To address this challenge, the recent study by Zhao et al. investigated the role, mechanism, and targeting potential of stromal monoamine oxidase A (MAOA) in PC tumor immunity and immunotherapy response,4 where MAOA is a mitochondrial membrane-bound enzyme best known for breaking down monoamine neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine, producing the reactive oxygen species (ROS) hydrogen peroxide as a by-product, thus influencing mood and behavior.5 MAOA also contributes to the regulation of the neuroendocrine system, impacting stress response and hormone release through neurotransmitter metabolism.6 Zhao et al. provide a glimpse of hope and a compelling strategy to therapeutically target the TIME by modifying the levels of stromal MAOA to enhance CD8+ T cell-dependent cytotoxic killing of prostate tumors. These findings open up the possibility of reprogramming the stromal compartment of the TIME to favor antitumor immunity in PC treatment and provide a strong rationale for incorporating MAOA inhibition into combination immunotherapy strategies for PC and likely other cancers.

Targeting stromal MAOA in the immunological interventions of PC

The uniqueness of Zhao et al.’s work lies in their demonstration that MAOA, predominantly expressed in myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs) of the stromal fibroblasts, acts as an immunosuppressor. Inhibiting MAOA, either through gene knockdown or pharmacological antagonists, confers the transition of myCAFs to foster a stromal environment conducive to immune activation. Mechanistically, the reprogramming effect is partly mediated by increased CAF secretion of WNT5A, which activates the Ca²+-NFATC1 signaling pathway in CD8+ T cells, thereby enhancing their cytotoxic function. Collectively, Zhao et al.’s findings advance our understanding of the immunomodulation of PC from a stromal perspective.

Another salient discovery of this study is that combining MAOA inhibition with PD-1 blockade synergistically restricts the growth of ICB-refractory cold prostate tumors in a syngeneic mouse model. MAOA inhibitors are well-established neuropsychiatric medications, such as phenelzine, which is already US Food and Drug Administration-approved for neurological conditions like depression.5 Notably, MAOA inhibitors have shown effectiveness when repurposed to treat PC outside the immune context in recent years.7 Current work further highlights the potential compatibility of MAOA inhibitors with existing ICB therapies to achieve therapeutic synergy. Exploring optimal dosing strategies for the combinatorial targeting of MAOA and its downstream effector, WNT5A, through WNT5A mimetics, along with ICB therapies, may also have potential beyond PC for other immunologically cold tumors. Additionally, this study encourages further evaluation of the predictive value of stromal MAOA in identifying likely responders to PC immunotherapies, both alone and in combination with MAOA inhibitors.

The monoamine metabolism, ROS and immune regulation axis

A cascade connecting MAOA-dictated monoamine metabolism, ROS, and immune regulation holds promise for a pivotal next chapter in our understanding and treatment of PC. In a significant advance, inhibiting MAOA enzymatic activity reduces ROS levels through monoamine metabolism, correlating with an upregulation of WNT5A expression and release. The presence of ROS is known to disrupt T cell signaling pathways, impairing their ability to recognize and attack tumor cells.8 Thus, lowering ROS levels within the stroma could be an effective approach for reforming immune dynamics to favor antitumor responses. Activation of MAOA elevates ROS production, suppressing WNT5A expression and diminishing CD8+ T cell recruitment to tumors. Conversely, inhibiting MAOA reduces ROS levels while increasing WNT5A levels, which subsequently induces CD8+ T cell cytotoxic activity. This interconnected framework outlines a mechanistic model of MAOA that may introduce a new class of immuno-oncology therapies, where metabolic leverages and immune suppression can be therapeutically challenged in the TIME, unlocking more durable and effective antitumor immune responses.

WNT5A: an effector of MAOA-driven stromal reprogramming

The role of WNT5A as a non-canonical WNT ligand that regulates immune responses is highlighted in the study by Zhao et al. WNT5A triggers a series of mechanistic steps downstream of MAOA inhibition, which enhances T cell activation markers (interferon gamma, granzyme B (GZMB), and CD69) and reduces PD-1 expression, mimicking the reversal of the immune exhaustion phenotype. This makes WNT5A an effector that mediates stromal-immune crosstalk through calcium signaling during stromal reprogramming and T cell reinvigoration, which warrants further clinical validation of WNT5A expression levels, along with its association with disease progression and response to MAOA inhibitors and ICB therapies. Additionally, this study provides a basis for proof-of-concept applications of WNT5A mimetics as stand-alone or combined agents in preclinical models for PC treatment.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the promising outcomes in the study by Zhao et al., several limitations should be considered before implementing MAOA inhibition as an immunotherapy-coupled therapeutic intervention for PC. Murine and humanized mouse models may not accurately represent the human tumor microenvironment of PC, especially considering the recent identification of additional subtypes and lineages of human CAFs.9 Further investigation is needed using human primary CAF cultures and clinical samples. For example, the expression levels of MAOA in antigen-presenting CAFs and their role in regulating CD8+ T cells in PC warrant further clarification. Furthermore, given the recent report of myeloid-mediated ICB resistance in advanced metastatic castration-resistant PC specifically through a distinct secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1)-expressing tumor-associated macrophage subset and adenosine signaling,10 Zhao et al.’s study would have benefited from investigating the effects of stromal MAOA on additional immune cell populations, such as natural killer cells, regulatory T cells, double-negative T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and macrophages, for a more comprehensive understanding of the role and mechanism of stromal MAOA in PC tumor immunity.

Conclusions

The study by Zhao et al. presents compelling evidence that targeting stromal MAOA reprograms the fibroblastic compartment of PC’s TIME, unleashing immune infiltration and cytotoxicity through a WNT5A-Ca2+-NFATC1-mediated stromal-immune paracrine pathway (figure 1). These findings pave the way for a novel class of combination immunotherapy that integrates stromal reprogramming, immune signaling, and therapeutic synergy. Future immunotherapy regimens incorporating MAOA inhibition could potentially revolutionize the management of PC and other stroma-rich malignancies for improved therapeutic efficacy. This strategy underscores the broader principle in oncology: to conquer cancer, we must treat not only the tumor but also the entire tumor ecosystem.

Stromal MAOA reprograms the cold immune microenvironment of prostate tumors. MAOA, highly expressed in myCAFs, confers a ‘cold’ TIME by producing elevated levels of ROS that deactivate CD8+ T cells (exhaustion markers: PD-1, IFN-γ, and GZMB). Pharmacological inactivation of MAOA using MAOA inhibitors in myCAFs reduces ROS levels, activating a WNT5A-Ca2+-NFATC1 stromal-immune cell paracrine signaling axis, which enhances CD8+ T cell tumor infiltration and converts the TIME to a ‘hot’ phenotype.
