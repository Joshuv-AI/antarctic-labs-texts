Associations of Graded Chronic Non‐Cancer Pain Severity With Well‐Being and Modifiable Lifestyle Behaviour: Findings From a Danish Population‐Based Cross‐Sectional Survey
Authors: Sophie Sell Hellmann, Henrik Bjarke Vaegter, Gitte Handberg, Pernille Lykke Petersen, Geana Paula Kurita, Per Sjøgren, Lau Caspar Thygesen, Ola Ekholm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe Graded Chronic Pain Scale‐Revised (GCPS‐R) framework enables a standardised measure of disease severity and was incorporated into the national disease surveillance in Denmark in 2023. Novel dose–response associations between GCPS‐R‐systemised disease severity, general health, well‐being, and modifiable lifestyle behaviour were analysed to enhance awareness of risk associations related to chronic non‐cancer pain disease severity.MethodsA cross‐sectional study including 8329 non‐cancer participants aged 16 years and older from the Danish National Health Survey 2023. The participants completed a comprehensive self‐reported questionnaire on general health, well‐being, and modifiable lifestyle behaviour, including the GCPS‐R framework. Multivariate‐adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using logistic regression models for dichotomous outcomes related to general health, well‐being, and modifiable lifestyle behaviour adjusted for sex, age, area of residence, BMI, country of origin, education and marital status.ResultsThe GCPS‐R score had strong positive dose–response associations with odds of poor/fair general health, showing ORs 1.66 (95% CI: 1.32–2.10) in mild‐impact, 6.08 (5.02–7.37) in bothersome‐impact, and 13.53 (11.53–15.88) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain. The GCPS‐R score had overall strong positive associations with odds of poor well‐being outcomes and moderate positive associations with odds of unhealthy modifiable lifestyle behaviour outcomes concerning illicit drugs, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, unhealthy diet and tobacco.ConclusionsThe GCPS‐R framework is applicable in national surveys enabling graded measures for associations related to chronic non‐cancer pain disease severity. Unified cross‐country strategies for national surveillance could strengthen the evidence base for equitable public health policies.Significance StatementChronic non‐cancer pain is a highly prevalent disease associated with elevated risks of several bio‐ psychosocial health conditions. However, whether risks related to chronic non‐cancer pain vary by disease severity is currently unknown. Hellmann et al. found the validated GCPS‐R framework useful in national survey‐based disease surveillance. The GCPS‐R‐systemised disease severity measures classified in no, low‐impact, bothersome‐impact, and high‐impact chronic non‐cancer pain enabled nuanced knowledge about such risk associations, being a fundamental base in planning targeted healthcare strategies for improved chronic non‐cancer pain management.Keywords: chronic pain, GCPS‐R, lifestyle, risk factors, well‐being

Introduction

Global high prevalences of adult chronic non‐cancer pain conditions raise concerns emphasising the necessity for more nuanced evidence on distinct risk measures related to disease severity to promote healthier outcomes (Blyth and Huckel Schneider 2018; Zimmer et al. 2022). Chronic non‐cancer pain conditions are currently not formalised within the United Nations classification for non‐communicable diseases, but some common risk patterns seem to be shared, among others, related to mental health risks and unhealthy lifestyle behaviours (Blyth and Huckel Schneider 2018; Mamudu et al. 2011; N. C. D. Countdown Collaborators 2022).

The clinical appraisal of chronic non‐cancer pain has changed in recent decades from being merely considered a symptom of other diseases to being recognised as a multifactorial disease condition itself, lasting or recurring for 3 months or longer, according to the most recent ICD‐11 classification (Steingrimsdottir et al. 2017; Treede et al. 2019). The current evidence about risk associations related to chronic non‐cancer pain exposure in general adult populations is mainly founded on relatively unnuanced dicho‐ tomous measures via population‐based surveys in the absence of distinctly graded disease severity measures (Breivik et al. 2006; Dahlhamer et al. 2018; Rometsch et al. 2025).

The global adult prevalence of chronic pain was 27.5% according to the World Health Survey conducted on data between 2002 and 2004, and has since shown plateauing or increasing tendencies with very large cross‐country and regional variations (Zhu et al. 2024; Zimmer et al. 2022). In Denmark, the prevalence of chronic non‐cancer pain has plateaued since 2010, with an overall increase of approximately 8 percentage points over the past two decades (Ekholm et al. 2025), reaching 28.1% (95% CI: 27.2%–29.0%) in 2023 (Hellmann et al. 2025).

The public health consequences associated with chronic non‐cancer pain exposure are likely determined, among others, by the disease severity impact on daily life activities functioning (Von Korff et al. 2020). The GCPS‐R framework is used worldwide to identify the disease severity of chronic non‐cancer pain systemised in mild, bothersome, or high‐impact disease severity measures (Von Korff et al. 2020). Incorporating the GCPS‐R framework in national survey‐based studies could enable more nuanced and distinct insights into risk associations related to disease severity in general adult populations.

The study explores associations of GCPS‐R‐systemised disease severity measures of adult chronic non‐cancer pain with outcomes for self‐perceived general health, well‐being, and modifiable lifestyle behaviours in the general adult population resident in Denmark in 2023. Such more refined and nuanced disease severity measures could be beneficial in the future planning of agile public health initiatives to promote healthier disease outcomes.

Methods

Overview

A nationwide representative study sample composed of 25,000 Danish residents aged 16 years and older was randomly selected from the Danish Civil Registration System by the Danish Health Data Authority (Hellmann et al. 2025; Jezek et al. 2024). The study sample was invited via official mailbox on index date 1 January 2023 to participate in the nationwide Danish National Health Survey 2023 by answering a comprehensive questionnaire about health‐related topics in the Danish language (Hellmann et al. 2025; Jezek et al. 2024). Non‐respondents received up to three consecutive reminders (Hellmann et al. 2025; Jezek et al. 2024). The response proportion was 40.8%, which was equal to 10,196 participants (Hellmann et al. 2025; Jezek et al. 2024). Danish residents are given a 10‐digit personal identifier enabling a unique individual‐level linkage of survey data with comprehensive nationwide Danish national registries of very high validity for objective personal characteristics (Pedersen et al. 2006; Thygesen et al. 2011).

Records in the study sample of histologically verified cancer diagnoses prior to the index date were excluded to ensure that the GCPS‐R‐scored exposure estimates were unrelated to cancer as described in a related study providing national Danish prevalences of graded chronic non‐cancer pain conditions (Hellmann et al. 2025). The survey information related to health outcomes for general health, well‐being, and modifiable lifestyle behaviour had primary research interest.

Classification of Graded Chronic Non‐Cancer Pain Exposure

Graded chronic non‐cancer pain exposure was systemised as a four‐level ordinal exposure variable in no chronic non‐cancer pain, mild‐impact, bothersome‐impact, and high‐impact chronic non‐cancer pain using the publicly accessible Creative Commons‐licensed Graded Chronic Pain Scale‐Revised (GCPS‐R) framework translated into Danish language by the steering committee of the Danish National Clinical Pain Registry (Vaegter et al. 2021; Von Korff et al. 2020). The GCPS‐R framework is structured in six separate questions about self‐perceived pain in daily life activities during the past 3 months, systemised firstly by ordinal scale (never, some days, most days, or every day) and secondly by continuous scale via the Pain, Enjoyment, and General Activities (PEG) tool for average pain intensity, life satisfaction, and general activity during the past 7 days on zero (lowest intensity) to ten (highest intensity) rating scales (Von Korff et al. 2020).

Classification of Dichotomous Health Outcomes for General Health and Well‐Being

Self‐perceived general health and well‐being outcomes (see Supporting Information for details Table S1) included the single‐item question on self‐rated health from the 12‐Item Short Form Survey (SF‐12) (Ware Jr. et al. 1996), dichotomised in poor/fair versus very good/good for the responses excellent, very good or good; events of sleep deprivation dichotomised in sleep deprivation for the responses sometimes/never feeling well‐rested versus sleep non‐deprivation for the response mostly feeling well‐rested; self‐perceived stress was indexed in accordance with the Cohen's Self‐Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) (Cohen et al. 1983), which calculates a sum score from ten questions about stressful events in the past 4 weeks. Points were assigned to each response: (i) none of the time (0 points), (ii) rarely (1 point), (iii) some of the time (2 points), (iv) often (3 points), and (v) very often (4 points), defining a fixed range (0–40) for the continuous PSS sum score, with higher scores indicating higher stress levels (Cohen et al. 1983). The PSS continuous sum score was dichotomised into a high stress score defined as the top 20 percentile cut‐off in accordance with the methods outlined in a previous Danish study on high‐level stress characteristics (Nielsen et al. 2008), equal to a PSS score of 20 in the current data, versus non‐high stress score reciprocal to high stress score; mental well‐being was indexed in accordance with the Short Warwick–Edinburgh Mental Well‐Being Score (SWEMWBS) (Stewart‐Brown et al. 2009), which calculates a sum score from seven questions about self‐perceived mental well‐being in the past two weeks. Points were assigned to each response: (i) none of the time (1 point), (ii) rarely (2 points), (iii) some of the time (3 points), (iv) fairly often (4 points), and (v) all of the time (5 points), defining a fixed range (7–35) for the SWEMWBS sum score, with higher scores indicating better mental well‐being. The SWEMWBS continuous sum score was transformed into a categorical variable in low, moderate, and high mental well‐being in accordance with the conversion principles by Stewart‐Brown et al. (2009) and further dichotomised into poor mental well‐being versus moderate/high mental well‐being; loneliness was indexed in accordance with the three‐item short form (T‐ILS) of the revised UCLA loneliness scale (Hughes et al. 2004) calculated as a sum score from three questions about feelings of loneliness. Points were assigned to each response: (i) hardly ever (1 point), (ii) some of the time (2 points), and (iii) often (3 points), defining a fixed range (3–9) for the T‐ILS score, with higher scores indicating higher degrees of loneliness. The T‐ILS continuous sum score was dichotomised in accordance with the UCLA cut‐off principles (Steptoe et al. 2013) for defining loneliness in lonely (≥ 6 points) versus non‐lonely (< 6 points).

Classification of Dichotomous Health Outcomes for Modifiable Lifestyle Behaviour

The outcomes for self‐perceived modifiable lifestyle behaviour were dichotomised (see Supporting Information for details Table S2) in alcohol > 10 units versus ≤ 10 units per typical week defined in standard drinks (12 g of pure alcohol) following the Danish Health Authority 2022‐guideline based on an evidence‐based threshold of almost equal risks of alcohol‐related mortality in men and women below 10 standard drinks per week (Danish Health Authority 2022); current illicit drug use versus non‐current illicit drug use was defined from the use or non‐use, respectively, of illicit drugs such as, among others, hash, amphetamine, ecstasy, cocaine, heroin, etc. during the past year; obese versus non‐obese body mass index (BMI) calculated as self‐perceived weight in kilos divided by self‐perceived height in meters squared (kg/m2) WHO‐classified in obese (≥ 30 kg/m2) and non‐obese (< 30 kg/m2); physical inactivity versus non‐physical inactivity defining physical inactivity as non‐adherence to the WHO weekly recommendations for moderate‐intensity physical activity for 150 min per week/high‐intensity physical activity for 75 min per week; sedentary versus non‐sedentary lifestyle defined as a sedentary lifestyle when the main spare time activities during the past year were reported to be of mainly sedentary character; current versus non‐current tobacco product use defining current tobacco use as daily/sometimes use and non‐current use as never/previous tobacco use. Tobacco product use was modelled firstly as independent exposures of smoking tobacco, E‐cigarettes (vapes), or smokeless tobacco and secondly as any type of tobacco summing exposures of smoking tobacco, E‐cigarettes (vapes), and smokeless tobacco products; and unhealthy versus healthy/average healthy diet using the Dietary Quality Score (DQS) (Rostgaard‐Hansen et al. 2023) as a shortened version of the 48‐item Food Frequency Questionnaire based on eight questions about the daily/weekly dietary intake of fats, fruits, greens, fish, and processed/unprocessed meats. Points were assigned to each response: (i) none of the time (0 points), (ii) 1–2 times (1 point), (iii) 3–4 times (2 points), and (iv) 5–7 times (3 points), defining a fixed range (1–9) for the DQS sum score. The continuous DQS sum score was transformed in accordance with the DQS principles (Rostgaard‐Hansen et al. 2023) into a categorical variable in unhealthy (1–3 points), average healthy (4–6 points), and healthy (7–9 points) diets and further dichotomised into unhealthy versus healthy/average healthy diets.

Classification of Covariates

Information about covariates included sex: male or female; age from date of birth: 16–25, 26–35, 36–45, 46–55, 56–65, 66–75, and ≥ 76 years; area of residence: metropolitan: capital and metropolitan city areas of > 100,000 inhabitants, province: province city areas of 30,000–100,000 inhabitants, and country: country and upland city areas of < 30,000 inhabitants; WHO‐classified BMI: < 18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25.0–29.9, ≥ 30 kg/m2; country of origin following the United Nations definition: Danish, Western or non‐Western; and marital status: married/in registered partnership, unmarried, divorced, or widowed obtained from the Danish Civil Registration System (Pedersen et al. 2006). Information about socioeconomic characteristics included highest attained education (basic school, high school and vocational education, short theoretical, medium theoretical, long theoretical, and unknown) obtained from the Danish Education Register providing records on completed education in Denmark and abroad to account for academic mobility (Jensen and Rasmussen 2011). Information about comorbidity (see Supporting Information Table S3) diagnosed at any time during a 10‐year retrospective period before the index date was obtained from the Danish National Patient Register providing somatic in‐ and outpatient diagnoses at nationwide Danish hospitals (Lynge et al. 2011). Comorbidity was indexed in accordance with the Charlson Comorbidity Index (Charlson et al. 1987) and further dichotomised into comorbid versus non‐comorbid conditions. Covariate information was collected on 31 December 2022, as closely as possible before the index data, to ensure data temporality.

Statistical Analysis

The baseline characteristics were reported by means (SD) for continuous variables and by total counts (n, %) for categorical variables. Sample proportions (p̂) to estimate prevalences with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for the GCPS‐R score were calculated using the binomial distribution. Associations of the GCPS‐R score as an exposure variable for chronic non‐cancer pain disease severity with the dichotomous health outcomes for general health, well‐being, and modifiable lifestyle behaviour were analysed by independent logistic regression models calculating the number of events (N) and odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs. The logistic regression models were modelled as independent age‐ and sex‐adjusted models (Tables S4 and S5) and multivariate‐adjusted models adjusted for confounders of sex, age, area of residence, BMI, comorbidity, country of origin, education and marital status. The multivariate‐adjusted logistic regression model for obesity was not adjusted for BMI as a confounder to avoid spurious findings. For simplicity, the results for the multivariate‐adjusted logistic regression analyses were presented.

The logistic regression analyses were modelled using calibration sample weighting incorporating characteristics of sex, age, country of origin, education, income and marital status to reduce the potential impact of non‐response bias. Complete case analysis was applied for an adjustment of confounders excluding missing values on the GCPS‐R score, confounders, or outcomes from the analyses. Data were processed in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and graphics in R statistics version 4.2.1 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) in the secured server environment of Statistics Denmark.

Results

An analytic non‐cancer study sample of 8329 (34.4% of the total non‐cancer invited) adult Danish participants was included after having excluded 437 participants because of clinically veri‐ fied cancer diagnoses and 1430 participants because of missing values on the analysed variables.

Characteristics

The analytic study population included 3658 (43.9%) male parti‐ cipants and 4671 (56.1%) female participants of mainly Danish country of origin with residence in Denmark (Table 1). The mean (SD) age was 55.3 (18.2) years in male participants and 53.1 (18.2) years in female participants (Table 1). Comorbidity was more prevalent in male participants than in female parti‐ cipants, showing 369 (10.1%) male participants compared with 352 (7.5%) female participants diagnosed with comorbidity prior to the index date (Table 1). Vocational school was the highest attained education for most (Table 1). Mean (SD) BMI was 26.6 (4.5) kg/m2 in male participants and 25.8 (5.5) kg/m2 in female participants (Table 1). Female participants had elevated preva‐ lences of self‐perceived poor/fair general health, high stress score, and loneliness compared with male participants (Table 1).

Graded Chronic Non‐Cancer Pain

Graded chronic non‐cancer pain prevalences systemised by the GCPS‐R framework showed about 72.2% (95% CI: 71.2%–73.1%) of the participants self‐reporting no chronic pain, 7.5% (6.9%–8.0%) mild‐impact, 7.0% (6.5%–7.5%) bothersome‐impact, and 13.4% (12.6%–14.1%) high‐impact chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 2). Lower prevalences of chronic non‐cancer pain were found in male participants compared with female participants (Table 2). High‐impact chronic non‐cancer pain was most prevalent in obese participants (Figure 1).

General Health and Well‐Being

The GCPS‐R score seemed to be strongly positively associated with outcomes related to poor general health and well‐being, showing notable dose–response associations (Table 3). The odds of poor/fair self‐perceived general health showed multivariate‐adjusted odds ratios (ORs) 1.66 (95% CI: 1.32–2.10) in mild‐impact, 6.08 (5.02–7.37) in bothersome‐impact, and 13.53 (11.53–15.88) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 3). A stressful health impact on well‐being associated with chronic non‐cancer pain exposure seemed to result in elevated odds of high stress score on the Cohen's perceived stress scale of multivariate‐adjusted ORs 1.20 (0.95–1.52) in mild‐impact, 2.69 (2.20–3.29) in bothersome‐impact, and 3.99 (3.40–4.67) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 3). Specifically for mental well‐being, odds of a poor mental well‐being by the Short Warwick‐Edinburgh Mental Well‐Being Score (SWEMWBS) seemed to imply elevated ORs 1.25 (0.98–1.60) in mild‐impact, 2.71 (2.20–3.32) in bothersome‐impact, and 3.21 (2.73–3.78) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 3). Furthermore, feelings of loneliness classified by high stress scores on the Three‐Item Loneliness Scale (T‐ILS) seemed to be associated with chronic non‐cancer pain exposure, showing elevated ORs 1.40 (1.02–1.91) in mild‐impact, 2.20 (1.68–2.88) in bothersome‐impact, and 3.47 (2.83–4.26) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 3). The possible detrimental health effects associated with chronic non‐cancer pain exposure to general health and well‐being seemed to be supported by findings of elevated odds of sleep deprivation, showing multivariate‐adjusted ORs 1.51 (1.26–1.81) in mild‐impact, 3.11 (2.56–3.79) in bothersome‐impact, and 3.43 (2.94–4.00) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 3).

Modifiable Lifestyle Behaviour

The GCPS‐R score seemed to be moderately positively associated with most outcomes related to unhealthy modifiable lifestyle behaviour, showing elevated odds in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 4). Chronic non‐cancer pain exposure seemed to impact odds of obesity positively, showing multivariate‐adjusted ORs 1.56 (1.26–1.93) in mild‐impact, 2.22 (1.82–2.71) in bothersome‐impact, and 2.53 (2.16–2.95) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 4). Furthermore, elevated odds of having had a sedentary lifestyle the past year as well as of WHO‐classified physical inactivity the past week seemed to be associated with chronic non‐cancer pain, the latter seemingly showing elevated multivariate‐adjusted ORs 1.10 (0.92–1.32) in mild‐impact, 1.42 (1.18–2.72) in bothersome‐impact, and 1.90 (1.63–2.22) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 4). An unhealthy dietary lifestyle seemed also to be associated with high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain exposure of elevated multivariate‐adjusted OR 1.41 (1.19–1.67) (Table 4). Likewise, current use of any type of tobacco seemed to be positively associated with odds of a more pronounced disease severity, showing multivariate‐adjusted ORs 1.47 (1.19–1.81) in bothersome‐impact and 1.50 (1.27–1.77) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain (Table 4). These associations seemed to be mainly explained by effects from current use of E‐cigarettes (vapes) and smoking tobacco (Table 4). Alcohol consumption and current illicit drug use both seemed to be unrelated to the GCPS‐R score, though current illicit drug use did seem to be associated with an elevated multivariate‐adjusted OR of 1.51 (1.08–2.10) in high‐impact compared with no chronic non‐cancer pain.

Discussion

Graded chronic non‐cancer pain systemised by the GCPS‐R score seemed notably to be strongly positively associated by dose–response patterns with outcomes of poor self‐perceived general health and well‐being. Furthermore, the GCPS‐R score seemed to be moderately positively associated with outcomes of unhealthy modifiable lifestyle behaviour, particularly in high‐impact chronic non‐cancer pain for current illicit drug use, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, unhealthy diet, and current tobacco product use from main effects of E‐cigarettes (vapes) and smoking tobacco.

The available evidence (Birkinshaw et al. 2024; Dahlhamer et al. 2018; Fillingim et al. 2014; National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2021; Nijs et al. 2020; Presto et al. 2024; Ray et al. 2024; Rometsch et al. 2025; Von Korff et al. 2020) is hete‐ rogenous and based on mainly dichotomous measures for chronic pain exposure, showing predominantly elevated risks related to outcomes for poor self‐perceived general health and well‐being as well as outcomes related to unhealthy lifestyle behaviour. A GCPS‐R‐systemised score for graded measures of chronic (non‐cancer) pain disease severity in survey‐based research could enable a more nuanced understanding of the possible health‐related consequences of chronic non‐cancer pain in general adult populations. Our study pioneers, to the best of our knowledge, nationwide survey‐based public health data on risk associations pertaining to daily life health based on the validated GCPS‐R framework as a standardised and validated score for monitoring chronic non‐cancer pain disease severity (Hellmann et al. 2025; Von Korff et al. 2020). The results are therefore not directly comparable to current evidence measuring, for the vast majority of studies, dichotomous measures for chronic pain as being present or non‐present during daily life activities (Birkinshaw et al. 2024; Dahlhamer et al. 2018; Fillingim et al. 2014; National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2021; Nijs et al. 2020; Presto et al. 2024; Ray et al. 2024; Rometsch et al. 2025; Von Korff et al. 2020). A large longitudinal study (Ray et al. 2024) (including malignancies) based on data from the American National Health Interview Survey 2016 and 2017 found prevalences of about 20% having chronic pain and about 8% having high‐impact chronic pain, which seemed to be less than our Danish GCPS‐R‐systemised adult prevalences. The American National Health Interview Survey study (Ray et al. 2024) reported elevated risks of poor well‐being, among others, psychosocial well‐being and dysfunctional sleep. Furthermore, this study (Ray et al. 2024) reported elevated risks of an unhealthy modifiable lifestyle behaviour for alcohol consumption, obesity, physical inactivity, and smoking associated with chronic pain and high‐impact chronic pain exposure, respectively, compared with no chronic pain (Ray et al. 2024). These results were overall in line with our findings except for the risk of having a current alcohol intake, which was contrary to our null findings positively associated with high‐impact compared with no chronic pain in the American National Health Interview Survey (Ray et al. 2024).

Insufficient evidence still exists to firmly conclude on our GCPS‐R‐ systemised associations for a general lack of population‐based validated graded measures on chronic non‐cancer pain exposure, risks of study participation bias, and small sample sizes. Furthermore, the current evidence (Birkinshaw et al. 2024; Dahlhamer et al. 2018; Fillingim et al. 2014; National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2021; Nijs et al. 2020; Presto et al. 2024; Ray et al. 2024; Rometsch et al. 2025; Von Korff et al. 2020) could benefit from future studies on co‐occurrences of risk behaviours, interaction, and mediating associations for more clarity about the possible complex interplay between graded chronic non‐cancer pain exposure measures and outcomes for mental well‐being and modifiable lifestyle behaviour (Axon and Smith 2024; Whibley et al. 2019). To further deepen the understanding of the explored associations, future research should also adopt longitudinal study designs to explore causal pathways and temporal dynamics.

The GCPS‐R framework was applied for measuring population‐based chronic non‐cancer pain disease severity, being a validated score described to have a high sensitivity in measuring particularly high‐impact chronic pain conditions, which may explain the higher Danish prevalences compared to those reported in the American National Health Interview Survey (Hellmann et al. 2025; Ray et al. 2024; Von Korff et al. 2020). The prevalence distribution found for the GCPS‐R score was based on nested data from the main nationwide surveillance data (Hellmann et al. 2025) on adult chronic non‐cancer pain prevalences to monitor the disease severity of chronic non‐cancer pain in the general adult population in Denmark. The prevalence distribution was in line with previous findings in Denmark (Hellmann et al. 2025), indicating that the excluded participants from missing values on the analysed variables were non‐differential without biasing the results. Generalisable study findings were furthermore supported by the aligned overall prevalence of chronic non‐cancer pain with the expected tendencies from repeated cross‐sectional data of the ongoing Danish Health and Morbidity Survey programme measuring chronic non‐cancer pain dichotomously as being present or non‐present in daily life in the past 3 months (Ekholm et al. 2025). The results were furthermore adjusted for important objective confounders using comprehensive data collected from the Danish national registries of high generalisability. However, the analytical study population represented a 41% response fraction of the total invited random sample (Hellmann et al. 2025; Jezek et al. 2024). The results should therefore be interpreted in accordance with the known weaknesses pertaining to survey‐based studies concerning, among others, differences in response patterns (Galea and Tracy 2007; Hellmann et al. 2025; Jezek et al. 2024; Tolonen et al. 2005) and possible measurement biases from an underreporting or overreporting on the self‐reported outcome variables (Maukonen et al. 2018; Prince et al. 2020). Non‐response is for example typically more frequent in male, elderly, and low socioeconomic individuals (Galea and Tracy 2007; Hellmann et al. 2025; Jezek et al. 2024; Tolonen et al. 2005). In addition, possible tendencies in the data of underreporting weight and overreporting height would most likely imply an overall underreporting of BMI and thereby an underestimation of the associations for WHO‐systemised overweight (Maukonen et al. 2018). Furthermore, the cross‐sectional study design hinders any causal interpretations of the results (Rodriguez‐Ramirez and Kim 2022). Sparse evidence on the relatedness of non‐response in survey‐based studies with chronic pain characteristics seems not to indicate differential response patterns, but this aspect remains to be explored in future studies (Grol‐Prokopczyk 2017). The generalisation of the findings was ensured by excluding clinically verified cancer records and by applying calibration sample weighting in the statistical modelling strategy to correct for possible skewed effects from non‐responses.

In closing, the findings demonstrate the utility of the GCPS‐R framework as a standardised score applicable for national public health surveillance. The cross‐sectional findings indicated multi‐factorial public health consequences from chronic non‐cancer pain exposure to general health, wellbeing, and modifiable lifestyle behaviour. Investigating the causal mechanisms underlying these cross‐sectional findings in future longitudinal studies with repeated measurements could become useful in developing targeted healthcare strategies. Expanding the research by applying similar methods in measuring chronic non‐cancer pain disease severity across different countries could importantly strengthen the evidence base and support the development of equitable public health policies for an improved management of chronic non‐cancer pain conditions.
