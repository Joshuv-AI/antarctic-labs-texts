Bite-sized self-compassion: a pilot cohort study of a well-being tool for healthcare workers
Authors: Kathryn C Adair, Melissa Powell, J Bryan Sexton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealthcare worker (HCW) well-being is an urgent priority in the wake of the COVID-19 Pandemic. Poor worker well-being has been linked to greater turnover, lower quality of care, and higher patient safety risks. Brief, simple tools to increase HCW well-being are of considerable interest. The current pilot study examined a bite-sized Self-compassion Tool (SCT) for HCWs. 216 participants completed baseline resilience measures (Emotional Thriving and Emotional Recovery), engaged in the SCT (5–7 min in length), and the following day, completed post resilience and tool evaluation measures. Participants reported significant improvements in Emotional Recovery, which is the ability to bounce back from adversity. Participants did not report changes in Emotional Thriving, however they did evaluate the tool favorably. This pilot study offers promising evidence for the efficacy of the SCT. HCWs are in need of brief tools alongside system changes to improve overall well-being, turnover, and patient care.Keywords: Burnout, Well-being, Resilience, Self-compassion, Positive psychology, Bite-sized interventionSubject terms: Quality of life, Public health

Introduction

Healthcare worker (HCW) well-being has reached critically low levels in the aftermath of the COVID19 pandemic1. HCW well-being is associated with better patient outcomes and quality of care, as well as lower turnover2. Despite the urgent need to improve HCW well-being, relatively few interventions have been broadly implemented, largely due the nascent state of intervention evidence and the high financial and time investments. For instance, recent compelling evidence suggests that coaching3 and mindfulness training4 have a significant impact on HCW well-being. Nevertheless, the months and dollars required to implement these strategies have proved difficult in an industry grappling with low initiation energy, shrinking budgets, and packed calendars.

Randomized control trials and cohort studies of bite-sized positive psychology interventions have evidenced their efficacy in improving HCW well-being, with effects that endure 12 months later5. These interventions typically ask participants to reflect on positive experiences (e.g., write down three good things that happened each day) to appreciate the good and the bad, without focusing on one at the expense of the other.

Rigorous research on cultivating self-compassion (SC) has found that it improves well-being and reduces secondary traumatic stress, with effects lasting at least 3 months6. Moreover, Self-Valuation (SV), a construct similar to SC, is significantly lower in Physicians than workers in other fields, and is associated with higher burnout.7,8 Despite the evidence of short and long-term benefits for well-being9, to date, no study has tested the efficacy of a simple, stand-alone bite-sized SC intervention. The current study is a pilot of the SC Tool (SCT) for healthcare worker well-being.

Methods

Participants

Participants learned of the study by participating (or through colleagues who participated) in a continuing education talk given by the first or third authors. All HCWs (clinical and non-clinical) 18 and older were eligible. Participation occurred between July 31, 2019, and May 20, 2023.

This study was approved by the Duke University Health System Institutional Review Board (Pro00063703) and was performed in accordance with all relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Materials

Emotional Recovery (ER) and Emotional Thriving (ET) Resilience Scales. ER (4 items) assesses the extent to which one is able to “bounce back” from emotional upheavals. ET (4 items) assesses respondents’ level of flourishing. ER and ET share about 10% of their variance so high levels of recovery does not ensure high levels of thriving, and vice versa. ER and ET are responsive to well-being interventions5,9. Both scales are responsive to interventions in randomized controlled trials5,9, demonstrate good internal consistency5,9 (current study: ER α = 0.91; ET α = 0.85; Baseline ER and ET correlation, r = 0.33).

Tool Satisfaction. Three items used across our bite-sized tools5 assessed tool satisfaction.

Procedure

This was a repeated measures cohort study of a web-based SCT. Participants responded to a prompt to reflect on an aspect of themselves about which they felt embarrassed, ashamed, or generally negative. Next, participants were asked to write themselves a letter from the perspective of a good friend or loved one about the negative aspect from the previous prompt. This letter constitutes the SCT.

Statistical analysis

Paired t-tests were used to detect change in ER and ET.

We calculated the percent of participants who agreed slightly or strongly to the tool satisfaction questions. 80.2% “liked the self-compassion tool,” 85.3% found the tool relatively easy to use, and 76.4% would recommend the tool to others.

Discussion

This pilot study of a bite-sized intervention to briefly reflect on an issue with self-compassion found that HCWs reported improvements in Emotional Recovery. Emotional Recovery is the component of well-being wherein one is able to quickly and easily bounce back from emotional upheavals. Participants overwhelmingly reported that they liked the tool, found it easy to use, and would recommend it to others.

Randomized controlled trials are needed to further examine and extend the SCT’s efficacy. Additionally, the current study suffered from losses to follow-up in that only 38% of participants completed day two assessments. Attrition of this level is similar to many positive psychology tool studies, in part due to the low initiation cost to participants. Nevertheless, this attrition could have biased the results. As a pilot study, the sample size precluded examining efficacy by role and demographic factors; larger samples in future studies should investigate this. Finally, the ET and ER measures suggest a continuous state of resilience and may not indicate the right time frame to adequately measure a change in resilience after only 24 h. Future studies would benefit from a longer follow-up period for these scales due to their short length, psychometric soundness, and ease of interpretation.

Bite-sized well-being tools have been shown beneficial to healthcare workers who need immediate help for well-being5,9. Policy, structural and system changes are essential to improving HCW well-being, and ultimately long-term solutions will entail a variety of evidence-based solutions for a weary workforce that has earned the right to have their well-being made a global priority.
