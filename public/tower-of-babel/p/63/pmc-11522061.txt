A graph theoretical approach to experimental prioritization in genome-scale investigations
Authors: Stephen K. Grady, Kevin A. Peterson, Stephen A. Murray, Erich J. Baker, Michael A. Langston, Elissa J. Chesler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The goal of systems biology is to gain a network level understanding of how gene interactions influence biological states, and ultimately inform upon human disease. Given the scale and scope of systems biology studies, resource constraints often limit researchers when validating genome-wide phenomena and potentially lead to an incomplete understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Further, prioritization strategies are often biased towards known entities (e.g. previously studied genes/proteins with commercially available reagents), and other technical issues that limit experimental breadth. Here, heterogeneous biological information is modeled as an association graph to which a high-performance minimum dominating set solver is applied to maximize coverage across the graph, and thus increase the breadth of experimentation. First, we tested our model on retrieval of existing gene functional annotations and demonstrated that minimum dominating set returns more diverse terms when compared to other computational methods. Next, we utilized our heterogenous network and minimum dominating set solver to assist in the process of identifying understudied genes to be interrogated by the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium. Using an unbiased algorithmic strategy, poorly studied genes are prioritized from the remaining thousands of genes yet to be characterized. This method is tunable and extensible with the potential to incorporate additional user-defined prioritizing information. The minimum dominating set approach can be applied to any biological network in order to identify a tractable subset of features to test experimentally or to assist in prioritizing candidate genes associated with human disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00335-024-10066-z.

Introduction

In systems biology experiments, data is often collected for every gene in the genome and represented as experimentally derived networks, such as gene co-expression networks (Zhang and Horvath 2005), Bayesian networks of genes and phenotypes (Ziebarth et al. 2017; Friedman et al. 2000; Pe’er 2005), networks of temporal relations among genes (Song et al. 2009), and many others. Balancing practical concerns against the extent of networks, researchers typically must focus on a very limited subgraph, or even a single “hub” node, when performing experimental validation of network perturbations. This approach is often criticized as “cherry picking,” even though it is usually impractical to evaluate the entire graph to confirm the estimated relations between nodes. Thus, improved computational methods are needed to identify those experiments that would provide information about relations across the entire graph in an unbiased, cost effective, and efficient manner.

Large-scale ‘omics data can be incorporated in graphs that represent various forms of similarity and other relationships among members of biological networks. Examples include graphs for gene function similarity (Vafaee et al. 2013), phenotype similarity (Peng et al. 2018), disease similarity (Wei et al. 2019), and genotype–phenotype associations with disease (Halu et al. 2019). In graphs such as these, information pertaining to an entity represented by a vertex (i.e. node) can be applied in various “guilt-by-association” algorithms to infer information about its neighbors (Wolfe et al. 2005). It is widely appreciated, however, that such graphs are often sparse, and that there are substantial disparities in the extent of characterization of elements. In research on gene function, this disparity in knowledge has led to the designation of an ignorome (Pandey et al. 2014; Riba et al. 2016), which denotes a set of poorly studied genes. The factors leading to disparities in knowledge of gene function are multifaceted, including technical constraints, reagent availability, and the propensity of researchers to give further attention to previously studied genes that may already be plausibly associated with a specific disease (Stoeger et al. 2018). The ignorome requires examination not only to enable a more comprehensive understanding of biological systems, but also because such a large number of poorly characterized genes can leave researchers and medical professionals in the dark during critical moments such as the recent COVID-19 pandemic (Stoeger and Nunes Amaral 2020).

Completing the functional annotation of the mammalian genome using gene knockouts is the primary goal of the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium (IMPC) with particular emphasis on understudied genes. The IMPC strives to produce a loss-of-function (null) allele for every protein coding gene and perform extensive characterization using a standardized phenotyping pipeline to create a comprehensive catalogue of gene function in disease related traits (Brown and Moore 2012). To date, the IMPC has generated and analyzed null alleles for over 8500 protein-coding genes out of the approximately 17,000 orthologous protein-coding genes shared between mouse and human (Cacheiro et al. 2019). Over the next 5 year funding period, the IMPC plans to generate and phenotype ~ 1500 new knockout mouse lines focusing on genes with limited biological information associated with them. Members of the IMPC currently utilize a variety of approaches to select genes which include: community nominations, biological domain of interest, or mining databases such as gnomAD to prioritize genes by functional constraint (Karczewski et al. 2020). As the number of genes lacking a mouse model dwindles, new approaches are needed to augment these methods. Minimum dominating set is an appealing option since it provides a graph theoretical approach to select a diverse and informative set of genes that is sized to meet current IMPC animal production goals while also providing broad coverage across the entire knowledge space.

The representation of biological information in an association graph is a powerful method to highlight relationships among genes and thus can help discriminate between two classes of genes—(1) poorly connected genes that may enhance topology if studied further due to the new information that places the gene in context, and (2) highly connected genes for which functional similarity already provides evidence of gene function. To test this prioritization strategy, we perform gene selection using association graphs, similarity, and the notion of domination. A dominating set in a finite, simple, undirected graph G = < V, E > is defined as a set, S, of vertices such that every vertex in V is either in S or adjacent to an element of S (Fig. 1). A minimum dominating set (MDS) is the one of smallest size. MDS and its variants can be applied to a variety of applications. In the biological realm, these include controllability of biological networks (Grady 2022a), biomarker selection for clinical profiling (Macartney-Coxson et al. 2017), and identification of novel drug targets (Asgari et al. 2013). Recent work in the application of MDS to biological networks can be found in Grady (2022b). In this study, we evaluate the suitability of MDS for prioritizing gene sets that optimize breadth of coverage of functional information and then apply this approach to the problem of resource-constrained gene selection in the IMPC to generate a prioritized list of genes currently lacking functional data.Fig. 1Example of a minimum dominating set for a simple graph where the black vertices represent those selected to be in a minimum dominating set. Methods

Gene-similarity graph construction

We estimated gene functional similarity from annotation data to construct a gene-similarity graph. Vertices were used to represent genes, while edges were weighted by an appropriate similarity metric. This metric was determined using random walk with restart from network enhanced similarity search (NESS) (Reynolds et al. 2020) algorithm to interrogate mouse-centric heterogeneous graphs constructed from Gene Ontology (GO) (Resource 2021; Ashburner et al. 2000), GeneWeaver (Baker et al. 2012), and STRING (Szklarczyk et al. 2018). NESS applies a random walk with restart to construct networks using a restart parameter of 0.35 as previously described (Reynolds et al. 2020). Each seed is iteratively visited and its affinity to local and global genes are determined through a whole graph traversal until a convergence threshold of 10–8 is reached. The output denotes the probability of visiting a gene from a starting seed. Probabilities between each pair of genes were normalized and assigned as edge weights. This process was carried out for 16,897 protein coding genes with high confidence human orthologs and resulted in a fully connected edge weighted gene-similarity graph.

Mouse allele annotation

To allow for the incorporation of domain expert knowledge and to guarantee the production of previously unavailable mouse resources, we incorporated a weighting scheme into the MDS algorithm that considers prior work for a given gene. Weights were generated using the number of known null allele counts for each gene (Supplementary File 1) and assigned to their corresponding vertex. This weighting enriched for the selection of poorly studied genes as opposed to genes that already have an existing well-characterized loss-of-function allele. The number of distinct loss-of-function alleles for a given gene in the gene-similarity graph was obtained from MouseMine (Motenko et al. 2015). Custom queries were constructed to filter for allele type, attribute (contains null), and transmission to account for alleles generated using either embryonic stem cells (targeted) or CRISPR/Cas9 (endonuclease mediated). Null allele types not accounted for in this query include those generated by other forms of random mutagenesis (e.g., spontaneous, gene trap, and ENU mutagenesis) as well as conditional-ready alleles without a germline null reported.

Minimum dominating set algorithm for gene selection

We formulated MDS as an integer linear programming problem, adding constraints for vertices known in advance to require removal. These vertices, which we denote by R, corresponded to genes with more null alleles than a given threshold or that had a null allele generated by the IMPC. The size of an MDS depends on the size, density, and topology of an input graph. In order to select approximately 1500 genes, we produced unweighted graphs using thresholds ranging from 0.01 to 0.99 in increments of 0.01, where an edge was retained if and only if its weight was at least as great as the threshold (Fig. 3C). We performed the following three steps for each unweighted graph. First, we added to R all genes that had an IMPC generated null allele or that had more than two null alleles, and we removed any edge between two vertices in R (Fig. 3D). Second, with the modified graph as input, we computed an MDS with the above formulation (Fig. 3E). And third, after an MDS was selected, we utilized a procedure from Hagan et al. (2020) that takes as input a graph G and an MDS S to determine vertices in the complement of S that can be swapped with vertices in S to produce an alternate MDS solution. This final step was used to determine possible alternate genes while preserving the size of the original MDS. This was done in case a selected gene proved challenging to produce as a null allele due to haploinsufficiency or potential locus constraints. After an MDS was computed at each edge-weight threshold, one of size closest to 1500 was retained for further study and model generation.

Computational milieu

All MDS computations were performed on the Cori high performance computing cluster maintained by the National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center (NERSC) (NERSC xxxx). Gene set selections for null allele production were performed on one of Cori’s large memory nodes containing two AMD EPYC 7302 (Rome) 3.0 GHz processors, 2048 GB DDR4 memory, and Cori’s CSCRATCH read/write system. Utility tests were performed on a shared version of the same large memory node that was limited to 50% CPU capacity and 42 GB DDR4 memory. For MDS we used Cbc (Coin-or branch and cut) (John Forrest et al. 2022), an open-source mixed integer linear programming solver available through the Python-MIP package (Túlio et al. 2021).

Genome editing in mouse zygotes

The generation of new mouse lines was done using standard methods employed by KOMP/IMPC (Peterson et al. 2023). Briefly, electroporation was used to deliver ribonucleoprotein complexes generated by incubating Cas9 protein (IDT) with duplexed cRNA::tracrRNA (IDT) specific to the gene of interest. Embryos obtained from C57BL/6NJ (The Jackson Laboratory, stock # 5304) were used for all experiments. All animal use was done in accordance with the Animal Welfare Act and the AVMA Guidelines on Euthanasia, in compliance with the ILAR Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and with prior approval from institutional animal care and use committee (IACUC).

Evaluation of MDS performance on biological term coverage

We compared Gene Ontology (GO) annotations for gene sets produced by MDS to those of pseudorandomly selected gene sets in order to gauge MDS’s utility in providing a representative sample of knowledge coverage. Often genes are studied in a specific context and frequently lack comprehensive phenotyping outside the biological domain of interest. For this analysis, we restricted it to genes for which the IMPC has already generated null allele models due to the standardization of phenotyping. Only vertices that correspond to IMPC’s already studied genes were used to induce an “assessment subgraph” of our gene-similarity graph. To the assessment subgraph, we applied a range of thresholds, extracted connected components for each, and then computed an MDS for every component while leaving R empty, thus equally weighting all vertices. A pseudorandom set of genes with matching cardinality was also selected at each iteration. We computed the Jaccard similarity (Jaccard 1912) between GO biological process terms associated with the members of each gene set and its respective complement, and termed this the set’s “representational proportion.” This score is reflective of how similar the dominating set is to the rest of the graph based on gene functional information, and thus the extent to which one can learn about the whole graph from this subset of vertices.” Lower thresholds of the assessment subgraph imposed staggering computational demands, and so we limited testing to the range [0.40, 0.95] in increments of 0.05, repeating this process ten times at each threshold, and constantly shuffling the vertex order to toggle any tie breakers that might have occurred. Given the relative speed and simplicity of pseudorandom selection, we repeated the process 100 times at each threshold. MDS produced higher representational proportions at every tested threshold than did pseudorandom selection (Fig. 4). These results provide support for MDS as a reliable and dependable sample of the entire gene-similarity graph.Fig. 4Gene Ontology term coverage tests for genes characterized by the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium. While MDS is an exact metric, its decision version is NP-hard (Garey and Johnson 1990) and thus exceedingly time consuming to compute. We therefore sought to establish whether minimality, a mere approximation metric, would suffice. A minimal dominating set is simply one that cannot be made smaller by vertex deletions. Computing such a set requires only O(|E|) time (that is, the time to find a minimal dominating set grows in proportion to the number of edges in a graph.). For example, a greedy method can merely select a vertex with the most undominated neighbors, add it to the solution, and repeat the process until a minimal dominating set is discovered (Chvatal 1979). This of course compares favorably with the worst-case exponential time currently needed to solve MDS using state-of-the-art brute-force methods. Applying MDS to prioritize gene selection

Our comparison analyses demonstrated that calculating MDS for our input knowledge graph captures a greater breadth of functional information as measured by GO term coverage. This is a desirable quality when trying to prioritize genes with unknown biological function and allows for sampling more of the search space. Given these advantages, we applied MDS to guide gene selection and identify genes that would be good candidates for generating null alleles. Specifically, we excluded genes from our input graph that have two more extant null alleles. After adding vertices associated with genes possessing two or more extant null alleles to the excluded set R and removing edges between all vertices in this set, we solved MDS on the resultant subgraph across an array of thresholds to obtain an MDS with size closest to our target of 1,500 genes, along with possible alternate genes (Supplementary File 2). Notably, this subgraph had a much lower edge density than did the assessment subgraph and was thus much easier to solve even at very low thresholds. The selected target gene set size was attained at a threshold of 0.08. The vertices in this MDS corresponded to 1513 genes, 1370 of which do not have a current null allele. 50 of these genes are represented by isolated vertices (they have no neighbors) and are of particular interest for mouse model production because their evaluation could provide information that connects them with other members of the heterogeneous association graph. Unfortunately, this lack of information is largely prescient, because 27 of them were comprised of olfactory receptors that have remained poorly studied due to phenotyping challenges and a lack of cross-species homology. In contrast, genes represented by non-isolated vertices were found to be involved in a variety of biological processes, with RNA processing (p-value = 1.75 X 10−4) and ribosome biogenesis (p-value = 0.014) found to be significantly enriched with an FDR < 0.05 (Holm–Bonferroni Corrected). These findings are consistent with recent analysis highlighting factors involved in core biological processes comprise a large fraction of genes lacking a null allele (Peterson and Murray 2021). No significant enrichment was observed for other GO categories. In total, these findings provide supporting evidence that our MDS-based strategy selected a diverse set of genes covering a broad range of biological processes. Understanding their function will enhance our current knowledge with the potential to identify and interpret novel connections between previously unrecognized relationships.

For additional evaluation, we compared subgraph coverage by MDS, pseudorandom selection, and domain expert nominations. Pseudorandom selection was performed as previously described. Domain expert nominations were derived from models previously generated by the IMPC (Birling et al. 2021) and the broader biomedical community. Coverage varied greatly over a subgraph of order 360 (Fig. 6). By definition, MDS covers 100% of the subgraph and only required five vertices (Fig. 6A). Subgraph coverage was reduced to 23.3% when five vertices were selected pseudorandomly (Fig. 6B), with Clusters 1 and 3 lacking any coverage at all. Next, we compared the coverage over this subgraph by identifying the genes/vertices that have been previously studied by domain experts. This analysis identified 60 genes with null allele models but collectively they only provide 67.5% coverage (Fig. 6C). There was no increase in coverage when we compared domain expert selected genes versus 60 genes chosen pseudorandomly (71.7% coverage; Fig. 6D). These findings demonstrate the power of MDS to represent the entire graph and not oversample particular gene sets. Functional assessment of MDS-selected genes

To evaluate the genes prioritized by the MDS method, we selected 360 genes from this set to create null alleles and characterize as part of KOMP2/IMPC. To date, we have targeted 217/360 genes and have completed broad-based phenotyping for 135 mouse lines. While many of the tested genes had only one phenotype hit, there were 23 lines that had two or more hits evidence of possible pleiotropic effects of targeted gene (Fig. 7A). These phenotype hits were distributed across a broad range of physiological domains with the most frequently occurring phenotype associated with preweaning lethality (Fig. 7B), perhaps a reason why many of these genes were poorly studied. To assess primary viability, we perform intercrosses between heterozygous mice followed by genotyping (N > 28 individual animals) at wean to determine if homozygous null animals are viable. The absence of homozygous animals at wean indicates that the gene is lethal. Of the lines completed, 100 (74.1%) were viable, 14 (10.4%) were subviable and 21 (15.6%) were lethal. Further, characterization of the lethal lines, confirmed that 55% were early lethal (prior to E9.5), 10% were dying between E12.5 and E15.5 (mid) and the remaining 35% were present at E18.5 suggesting either late embryonic or perinatal lethality (Fig. 7C). Gross morphological assessment uncovered novel phenotypes at different developmental timepoints and often with varying degrees of penetrance. Discussion

We have developed, implemented, and applied a novel, high-performance MDS strategy to select genes that can be used to infer global functional knowledge while maximizing the use of experimental resources. Unsurprisingly, we found that MDS convincingly outperforms pseudorandom selection in retrieving genes annotated to a greater representation of information and is thus expected to sample broadly and thoroughly the roles of gene neighborhoods in disease. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application in which MDS has been used to guide experimental selection.

MDS is particularly advantageous in this setting because it samples all parts of the knowledge graph to deliver a diverse set of entities that possess high overall similarity with the whole while also minimizing the number of entities chosen, thereby providing a tractable set for experimentation. Further, simple bootstrapping reveals that the gene-similarity graph used in this work and others like it conform to a scale-free (power-law) distribution. Thus, the results of Nacher and Akutsu (2012) indicate that an MDS for gene-similarity graphs should be quite manageable relative to that for pseudorandom graphs, but also for regular graphs, graphs of bounded degree, cyclic graphs of large girth, and a wide assortment of other graph topologies.

Currently, our validation study is limited by the relatively small number of null alleles generated from the selected gene set. While our method provides a promising approach to discover additional information about the “ignorome,” more of the selected genes must undergo functional testing before definitive claims of general utility may be made. In addition, methods such as ours are limited by the veracity and completeness of the chosen input data sources used to construct an initial association graph. The source and construction of this graph will quite naturally influence the outcome. It is therefore essential that this graph reflect the experimental application. Incorporating additional sources of information such as tissue specific co-expression data may help to provide context-dependent information critical for elucidating causal relationships in complex disease. For example, a gene-similarity graph containing neural co-expression data may increase the utility for identifying genes involved in behavior. Further, validation of different computational methods on biological networks are limited by what researchers in the past have prioritized. Such biases may have produced a more homogenous subset of genes when compared to the entirety of the gene-similarity graph. Thus, a small number of genes could potentially represent a large proportion of the GO terms associated with the set.

In our efforts to reduce the number of genes in the ignorome, the genes identified by MDS displayed promising properties for the acquisition of new knowledge in sparsely characterized regions of the association graph. First, most of the selected genes have not been included in loss-of-function studies in the mouse. Second, there was minimal enrichment for GO term annotations among these genes, suggesting that they comprise a diverse sample of a broad range of biological or unknown functions. Currently, 217 genes selected using our approach have entered the IMPC animal production pipeline and 135 have completed the broad-based phenotyping pipeline which highlighted embryonic lethality as the most frequently occurring phenotype hit. While the percentage of lethal lines from the MDS-selected gene set is slightly lower compared to the historical rate of 25% for lines generated by the IMPC, they do support the idea that there are still undiscovered essential genes within the “dark” portions of the genome lacking functional studies (Cacheiro et al. 2020; Dickinson et al. 2016). It will be important to monitor this rate of lethality as additional lines complete the pipeline. Furthermore, 18/135 genes within the tested MDS set have a pLI score (Lek et al. 2016) greater than 0.85, indicating that these genes are also highly constrained in humans (Karczewski et al. 2022). It is critical to obtain additional functional data for these understudied genes as 30/53 genes have a potential relationship to human disease and may assist in prioritizing candidate genes identified from genome-wide approaches such as whole-genome sequencing or exome sequencing. For example, our phenotyping data support a novel association of Pdp2 and Fbrs with cataracts. Additionally, Fbrs homozygous mutants were lethal but heterozygous animals displayed multiple phenotype hits in clinical chemistry measurements. Together, these data highlight a critical biological role for a number of genes that have not previously been studied and can be prioritized using MDS methodology, challenging the conventional wisdom that many poorly studied genes are simply unimportant for health and disease, and that the most important genes have already been studied. Rather, it appears that many poorly studied genes may have been difficult to study or detect because variation is constrained by evolution. The information obtained from these tested mice will help to fill knowledge gaps and increase connectivity of our association graph.

In addition, we developed a vertex weighting scheme for the integration of domain expert input to guide MDS selection. In the case of the IMPC, the purpose was to eliminate or reduce the development of redundant mouse models. In other contexts, it might be used to filter out experiments for which reagents are cost prohibitive or infeasible. For example, in microbiome related work it is often difficult to obtain particular microbial cultures for manipulation, and in antibody based assays there may be many challenges in the development of suitable selective antibodies. An MDS would allow identification of an adjacent microbe or antigen from an association graph, allowing investigation of the role of members of the network neighborhood, from which evidence may be developed to support further and more cost intensive investigation of the entire network.

We note that large swaths of the gene-similarity graph may be represented by a single gene in the MDS (Fig. 6A). The MDS is quite small compared to the size of the graph and may therefore comprise far fewer entities than resources allow for testing. In such a case, our method may be expanded with additional procedures to narrow or extend the selected set to a desired size. For instance, one could recursively select a neighborhood covered by a member of the MDS, for example Cluster 1, and then select an MDS of Cluster 1 at a more stringent threshold and repeat until sufficient coverage is achieved (Fig. 6).

Finally, our method is highly amenable to application in other systems biology contexts where resource, time, or methodological constraints do not allow comprehensive testing of all elements in a system. Other such applications include reducing the scope of a compound library screen and prioritizing members of a microbiome to perturb its metabolic network. Each application will likely be sensitive to input data, and thus it is critical to investigate further how different input data sources influence network topology.

In summary, graph-theoretical approaches toward maximization of association graph coverage provide a rational approach to help large-scale research programs such as the IMPC maximize limited resources to perform strategic functional characterization of poorly studied genes. The work reported here should be fully generalizable to virtually any context in which MDS can exploit network topology in order to ensure uniform sampling whenever it is not feasible to evaluate all of a network’s elements.
