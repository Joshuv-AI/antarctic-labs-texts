Enhanced Left Ventricular Diastolic Function Following Diabetic Foot Treatment: A Prospective Observational Study Investigating the Impact on Cardiac Health
Authors: Maziar Moayerifar, Mahboobeh Gholipour, Behrad Eftekhari, Anita Khalili, Mani Moayerifar, Mohammad Taghi Ashoobi, Hossein Hemmati, Mohammad Ali Yazdanipour, Golshan Ghasemzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDiabetes mellitus increases the incidence of diabetic foot syndrome and its concomitant cardiovascular complications, notably diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM). Approximately 15% of diabetic individuals experience Diabetic Foot Syndrome (DFS), significantly elevating mortality rates. This study evaluates the impact of diabetic foot treatment on cardiac diastolic function in patients with DCM.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted on 62 diabetic patients with confirmed DFS. An initial echocardiography assessment was performed before treatment. Comprehensive echocardiographic assessments of left ventricular diastolic function were performed before and 3 months after diabetic foot treatment. Diastolic function parameters were analyzed.ResultsPost‐treatment, there was a significant improvement in diastolic function indices, including increased normal left ventricular ejection fraction, reduced left and right atrial volume indices, and enhanced Doppler‐derived diastolic parameters. These findings indicate an overall improvement in cardiac diastolic status following the resolution of DFS.ConclusionDiabetic foot syndrome is associated with impaired diastolic function due to diabetic cardiomyopathy. Echocardiography, particularly assessing left atrial volume index, can detect early diastolic changes, potentially improving outcomes and reducing cardiovascular risk. Treatment of diabetic foot syndrome can enhance diastolic function, indicating cardiac benefits. Therefore, echocardiography is valuable for early detection and management of diabetic cardiomyopathy in this population.Keywords: diabetic cardiomyopathy, diabetic foot treatment, diabetic foot ulcer, diastolic dysfunction, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, LV diastolic dysfunction

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic and severe metabolic disorder and a major contributor to ischemic heart disease, in addition to major vascular complications [1]. Amongst the vascular complications associated with diabetes, foot ulcers under the label ‘Diabetic Foot Syndrome’ (DFS) emerge as one of the chief causes of hospital admissions and are significantly burdensome in terms of healthcare, accompanying almost 15% of these patients during their lifetime [2, 3, 4]. DFS pathogenesis is intricate, incorporating various causative factors, including Peripheral Artery Disease (PAD), Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathy (DPN), foot deformity, limited joint mobility, excessive foot pressure, and trauma [5].

Diabetic patients with DFS exhibit increased cardiovascular risk and incidence of vascular events compared to those without DFS [6]. Diabetes‐associated cardiovascular complications are predominantly coronary artery disease (CAD) due to diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM), along with cardiac autonomic neuropathy (CAN), and increased atherosclerosis [7]. According to the joint guidelines by the European Society of Cardiology and the European Association for the Study of Diabetes, DCM is described as ventricular dysfunction occurring without primary coronary hypertension and atherosclerosis in DM patients [8]. However, the coexistence of diabetes with other cardiac conditions demands a broader definition of DCM [9]. Therefore, some authors have extended it to cardiac disorders not justified by other cardiovascular or systemic diseases, but that can be a result of DM [10]. The estimated prevalence of DCM in the general and diabetic populations is approximately 1.1% and 16.9%, respectively [11]. The underlying pathogenesis of DCM can be associated with biochemical disturbances leading to the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, which are linked to hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and inflammation [7]. Other factors contributing to DCM development involve reduced cardiac compliance, endothelial dysfunction, defective coronary microcirculation, increased oxidative stress, and insulin resistance [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Research implies that the elevated inflammatory response surrounding DFS can induce endothelial dysfunction and insulin resistance, potentially resulting in the progression of DCM [6]. The available data reveals a significant association between DFS and an elevated risk of cardiovascular events and mortality in diabetic individuals. Startlingly, individuals with DFS and foot ulcers face over double the mortality risk compared to those without ulcers, disregarding patient demographics [18].

Hallmark pathologies of DCM consist of myocardial hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction; however, as the disease advances, there is an eventual compromise in systolic function [19]. Echocardiography remains the preferred modality for assessing cardiac morphology and performance, attributed to its wide accessibility and cost‐effectiveness. Diastolic function is evaluated through pulse wave Doppler, particularly assessing the velocities of transmitral and pulmonary venous flow, along with tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) and measurement of left atrial volume [20]. These echocardiographic parameters aid in tracking the progression of the disease from mild (impaired relaxation) to more advanced stages (pseudonormalization or restriction) of diastolic dysfunction [21]. There is limited literature surrounding the association between DFS and DCM, particularly regarding the effects of therapeutic interventions for DFS on diastolic function. We hypothesize that Diastolic Dysfunction (DD) may improve following diabetic foot treatment. Therefore, the present study is designed to assess potential enhancements in Diastolic function via conventional echocardiography, conducted both before and after diabetic foot treatment.

Materials and Methods

The present study is the third part of a comprehensive research project focusing on evaluation of left ventricular function through strain echocardiography subsequent to diabetic foot treatment. This research integrates with our previous studies by utilizing the same research data and case, ensuring a cohesive and thorough analysis. In line with the protocol of the main prospective study from which this manuscript is derived, all key clinical confounders—including metabolic, hemodynamic, and cardiovascular variables—were collected for the same cohort, although they are not re‐tabulated in detail here [22].

Participants' Characterization and Study Design

We conducted this research in the outpatient section of the Razi Vascular Surgery Department, targeting individuals diagnosed with diabetic foot from July 2022 to April 2023 via convenience sampling. The adult (over 18) patients were categorized as diabetic based on a history of taking oral antidiabetic medications or use of insulin or fasting blood sugar measuring ≥ 126 mg/dL on two occasions. The diagnosis of diabetic foot was determined by a vascular specialist in accordance with the 2018 Diabetic Foot Study Group Guidelines. Each participant underwent a precise medical history check and a physical exam. We utilized a pre‐designed checklist to gather demographic information and measurements, including age, sex, high blood pressure (BP), body mass index (BMI), body surface area (BSA), hyperlipidemia, smoking, cardiovascular disease, chronic lung disease, and history of stroke. Associated cardiovascular and systemic non‐cardiac conditions, in addition to pharmacological treatments, were documented. The stratification of diabetic foot ulcers was executed utilizing the Wagner‐Meggitt system, which classifies the ulcers according to their severity, depth, and extent of gangrene width [23]. We diagnosed hyperlipidemia based on low‐density lipoprotein levels exceeding 130 mg/dL or a history of cholesterol‐lowering medication. Cigarette smoking was defined as regular smoking within the preceding 12 months. Coronary artery disease was ascertained for patients previously diagnosed via angiography or with a history of myocardial infarction. We classified patients with systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg or those on antihypertensive drugs as hypertensive. Our exclusion criteria encompassed acute coronary syndrome, end‐stage or acute liver disease, end‐stage chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bleeding time disorder, acute or end‐stage renal disease, prior hemorrhagic stroke history, malignancy, active infection within last 2 weeks, severe aortic and mitral valve disease, individuals with a life expectancy under 1 year, patients classified within New York Heart Association functional class III or those with IV heart failure and left ventricular systolic impairment (EF < 40), and individuals who did not want to partake in. DCM was confirmed in participants with diabetes showing left ventricular diastolic dysfunction on echocardiography (E/e′ > 14 or e′ < 8 cm/s and/or elevated LAVI) in the absence of hypertension, coronary artery disease, or valvular pathology. We followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for reporting observational studies when designing and conducting this study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants aged 18 or older, including consent for publication of data, in accordance with the journal's policy. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethics approval from Guilan University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.GUMS.REC.1401.241) on July 20, 2022.

Echocardiographic Protocol

Echocardiographic acquisition and analysis (Samsung HS70A ultrasound system (Samsung Medison Co. Ltd., Seoul, South Korea; software version 2.01.04.0528) were performed by an experienced cardiologist who was blinded to all clinical information, treatment modality, and follow‐up status to minimize observer bias. Each cardiac parameter was measured thrice while participants were in sinus rhythm, with the computed mean value being documented. Evaluation of LV volumes and LVEF was conducted via a modified biplane Simpson's method. This technique incorporated two‐dimensional (2D) and M‐mode echocardiographic imaging from apical 2‐ and 4‐chamber perspectives within a frequency range of 2.0 to 3.5 MHz. Left atrial (LA) volume at the end‐systolic phase was measured employing the biplane area‐length method, captured through apical 2‐ and 4‐chamber views. These measurements were then standardized according to BSA to derive the left atrial volume index (LAVI). Concurrently, at the juncture of ventricular systole, the maximal volume of the right atrium was quantified, extending from the lateral boundary of the tricuspid annulus to its septal counterpart, with the interleaflet and annular region omitted from this estimation. Additionally, the examination of transmitral LV inflow via an apical 4‐chamber view facilitated the assessment of peak mitral inflow velocities, including the A and E waves. The E/A ratio was calculated during atrial systole alongside the early diastolic velocities (e') at the septal and lateral segments of the mitral annulus, also via an apical 4‐chamber view. The peak velocity of tricuspid regurgitation (TR) was ascertained by measuring the right ventricular inflow, acquiring the highest value from the parasternal short‐axis orientation within apical 4‐chamber observations. Lastly, algorithms provided by the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines were systematically applied to evaluate diastolic function. The echocardiographic assessment included measurements of E and A wave velocities, the E/A ratio, septal and lateral e′ velocities, the E/e′ ratio, left atrial volume index (LAVI), right atrial volume index (RAVI), and pulmonary artery pressure (PAP). Diastolic dysfunction was defined based on the 2016 ASE/EACVI recommendations as the presence of one or more of the following criteria: average E/e′ > 14, septal e′ < 7 cm/s or lateral e′ < 10 cm/s, LAVI > 34 mL/m², or tricuspid regurgitation velocity > 2.8 m/s [24].

Management of Diabetic Foot and Follow‐up

At the diabetes outpatient clinic, systematic screening assessments were performed by a certified vascular surgeon to evaluate both vascular and neuropathic Impairments in the lower limbs of patients. The Wagner–Meggitt classification was employed to categorize the severity of diabetic foot ulcers based on their depth and width. Samples for wound cultures were obtained from areas of infection, following which empirical antimicrobial therapy was initiated. Ankle‐brachial index (ABI) measurements were obtained from all patients using a Doppler device (an ABI value between 0.91 and 1.3 was deemed normal, while values below 0.90 were considered abnormal). Radiographs were taken to assess for osteomyelitis, and Doppler ultrasonography was performed to detect peripheral arterial disease. The treatment provided was comprehensive, with medical, surgical, or vascular interventions as needed, involving a multidisciplinary team of internal medicine specialists, endocrinologists, infectious disease specialists, plastic surgery specialists, and orthopedists. Treatment ranged from pressure relief and antibiotic therapy to debridement and amputation. The choice of antibiotic nwas based on infection severity and wound culture results. In cases of inadequate response to antibiotics, limb amputation was considered based on tissue loss, ischemia severity, and the presence of severe infection or sepsis. Follow‐up evaluations, including echocardiography, were conducted 3 months post‐treatment for diabetic foot ulcers.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), utilizing the packages “stats,” “Hmisc,” “ggplot2,” “dplyr,” and “caTools.” Continuous variables were articulated as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were denoted as frequency and percentage. The Shapiro‐Wilk test was employed to examine the data distribution for normality. Comparative analyses of echocardiographic outcomes pre‐ and post‐intervention for diabetic foot were conducted utilizing the Wilcoxon Signed‐Rank test (two‐sided) and the paired samples t‐test (two‐sided). An a priori threshold of significance was set at p < 0.05. All p values are reported according to standard conventions for decimal places as recommended by the SAMPL guidelines, and all statistical tests were two‐tailed unless otherwise specified [25].

Results

Demographic and Clinical Data

Among the 85 patients who met the inclusion criteria of this study, 5 (5.8%) were deceased during the follow‐up interval, and a total of 18 patients (21.1%) were omitted due to not participating in control echocardiography post‐therapy; therefore, our study's participant pool contained 62 diabetic foot patients with a mean age of 61.6 years, displayed a nearly equal distribution of males and females. Their average BMI was near the threshold between normal and overweight at 24.9 kg/m2, with the patients almost evenly distributed between the two groups. Many patients had accompanying health conditions such as hypertension (93.5%) and hyperlipidemia (91.9%), with less than half having coronary artery disease (24.2%), cerebrovascular accidents (24.2%), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (12.9%).

Discussion

The primary outcome of our study suggests that treatment of diabetic foot ulcers can lead to an enhancement in cardiac diastolic function, as evidenced by echocardiography. We observed a significant improvement in patients' Diastolic Dysfunction (DD) severity after diabetic foot treatment. DD applies to the heart's relaxation and filling abnormalities during diastole as a result of myocardial hypertrophy and stiffness, often leading to impaired left ventricular filling [26]. A model hypothesized by Starcevic et al. indicates that myocardial stiffness in DCM stems from elevated intrinsic stiffness at the cellular and extracellular matrix levels and altered cell‐matrix interactions, predominantly due to cardiomyocyte cytoskeletal dysfunction [21]. In early phases, before clinical presentation, structural changes in the myocardium, such as slight hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and collagen accumulation, can be identified [27]. These are coupled with substantial ultrastructural modifications at the microscopic level [28]. The myocardial stiffening in diabetes is further exacerbated by specific pathways involving nitric oxide pathway disturbances, increased inflammation, sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 (SGLT‐2) mediated, microvascular dysfunction, and oxidative stress [21]. Furthermore, this dysfunction can directly influence the phasic functions of the left atrium, encompassing its reservoir, conduit, and pump functions, which are the passive left atrial contributions to left ventricular filling throughout early and mid‐diastole [26]. Therefore, the left ventricle's diastolic dysfunction, common in DM, can impair the left atrium's phasic function, resulting in an abnormal left atrial volume index (LAVI) [26]. Our study showed significant decreases in atrial volume following DFS management. A study by Tadic et al. revealed a progressive decrease in both atria's deformation and phasic function from controls to prediabetics to diabetics, correlating with both blood sugar and HbA1c [29]. Furthermore, Poulsen et al. reported that LAVI can independently predict cardiovascular mortality and morbidity in diabetic patients with no previous history of cardiovascular disease, even following adjustment for age and hypertension [30]. Diabetes impacts the atrium through various mechanisms, including causing subendocardial fibrosis, dysregulating the autonomic nervous system, elevating BP, and promoting inflammation, all factors leading to reduced wall elasticity and alterations in atrial structure and function [31]. The identification of these changes in atrial volumes following the treatment of DFS represents a novel and intriguing finding in our study, albeit necessitating further validation through additional investigations.

Prior to treatment, almost 70% of our patients exhibited normal systolic function, while the rest presented mild systolic dysfunction according to LVEF, in contrast to a study by Löndahl et al., in which they detected LV dysfunction in 78% of DFS cases [32]. After the treatment, we observed a minor, insignificant rise in mean LVEF, unlike other echocardiography parameters that improved considerably, which was compatible with the findings of Demirtas et al., who found no significant alteration in LV‐EF value 3 months after treatment (48.6% ± 7.0% vs. 48.5% ± 5.9% and p = 0.747) [33].

The finding that patients with more severe Wagner grades showed a higher percentage of diastolic improvement is physiologically plausible. Severe diabetic foot ulcers are associated with greater systemic inflammation, tissue hypoxia, metabolic stress, and circulatory strain. Successful treatment—whether through debridement, infection control, or revascularization—results in a marked reduction in these stressors. Consequently, patients who begin with a worse baseline physiological state may experience a larger ‘recovery window,’ producing more noticeable early improvements in diastolic function. Although the difference between groups did not reach statistical significance, the trend supports the concept that reduction in high inflammatory burden can rapidly translate into measurable myocardial relaxation benefits, even within a short 3‐month follow‐up [34].

Notably, cardiovascular disorders stand as the predominant reason for mortality and morbidity in DM patients, leading to mortality rates 2‐6 fold higher compared to non‐diabetics [35]. Additionally, among diabetic patients, those with DFS exhibit elevated cardiovascular mortality rates in contrast to diabetic patients without it [36]. A review conducted by Dietrich et al. revealed that the mortality rate in diabetic patients with DFS exceeds two‐fold when contrasted with non‐ulcerated diabetics [18, 36]. Moreover, the majority of DFS patients exhibit central obesity, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and hypertension, which are key features of metabolic syndrome [37]. These findings, congruent with the outcomes of our study, are linked with a higher rate of significant cardiovascular events [37]. Consequently, DFS now emerges as an indicator of cardiovascular events and mortality [36]. In our study population, hypertension (93.5%) and hyperlipidemia (91.9%) were commonly observed as cardiovascular risk factors. Furthermore, individuals with DFS frequently exhibit DPN, thereby amplifying the risk of silent myocardial ischemia (MI) [38]. A study conducted by Baltzis et al. illustrates a strong correlation between DPN and asymptomatic MI as assessed with Technetium‐99m sestamibi single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging in DM patients with no prior cardiovascular events [38]. Consequently, it is evident that cardiovascular evaluation should be an essential component of DFS patients' examination; however, the predominant focus of diabetic foot care centers tends to be on managing foot health rather than conducting comprehensive cardiac evaluations. Remarkably, conventional first‐line diagnostic tools are insufficient in providing comprehensive insights into cardiac health, highlighting the necessity of echocardiographic assessment [32]. While improved overall clinical monitoring during foot treatment could offer some benefit, background medical therapy was largely unchanged, making it unlikely that medication adjustments accounted for the diastolic improvement. The rapid, consistent improvement across diastolic parameters aligns more closely with the physiological effect of reduced inflammation and hemodynamic stress following ulcer healing rather than general medical optimization [39]. Our findings suggest that timely management of diabetic foot ulcers not only promotes local healing but may also improve cardiac diastolic function, highlighting the potential clinical benefit of early intervention. Early treatment of diabetic foot complications could therefore play a preventive role in reducing cardiovascular risk among diabetic patients, underscoring the importance of integrating cardiovascular assessment into routine diabetic foot care.

Limitations and Strength of the Study

The relatively limited sample size and single‐center design may reduce statistical precision, limit the ability to identify stronger associative trends, and restrict generalizability. Moreover, the absence of patients with severe diastolic dysfunction (based on DD) further limits applicability across the full spectrum of disease. The specific treatment modalities for diabetic foot ulcers were individualized and not detailed, as the focus was on changes in cardiac function post‐treatment, with healing as the primary outcome. Future research should investigate whether different interventions exert varying effects on cardiac outcomes. A major strength of this study is the demonstration that clinically meaningful improvements in cardiac function occur within a relatively short, 3‐month period following diabetic foot treatment. This early improvement—particularly in diastolic parameters and myocardial deformation indices—shows that cardiac recovery can begin rapidly once the systemic inflammatory and metabolic burden associated with diabetic foot lesions is reduced. Capturing this short‐term response provides valuable insight into the dynamic interaction between peripheral healing and cardiac performance, highlighting the potential for early cardiovascular benefit in this high‐risk population.

Conclusion

Patients with diabetic foot may experience a decrease in diastolic function due to DCM. Conventional echocardiography can be used to measure objective parameters such as DD severity, LAVI, RAVI, and other diastolic parameters to determine changes in LV diastolic function during the early stages of diabetic foot treatment. The timely identification and intervention of cardiac abnormalities is crucial for enhancing patient prognoses and diminishing the incidence of cardiovascular incidents among individuals with diabetic foot syndrome. Furthermore, an improvement in cardiac diastolic performance was observed as a result of DFS treatment.
