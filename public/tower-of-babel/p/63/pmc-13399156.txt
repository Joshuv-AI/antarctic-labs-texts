Highlights of the 2026 European Crohn’s and Colitis Conference: redefining the IBD care continuum
Authors: Ahmed Al-Hindawi, Alexander Siegel, Clive Jude Miranda, Gautam Maddineni, Mohammad Shehab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and PurposeInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) care is moving from a symptom-driven step-up model of care toward earlier effective intervention, complication-specific management, and more personalized treatment selection. This review synthesizes selected ECCO 2026 studies and offers an expert appraisal of their clinical relevance, methodological strengths, and remaining uncertainties. Particular emphasis is placed on studies most likely to influence current practice and further research, including late-breaking trials, complication-directed therapy, pediatric evidence, and technology-assisted assessment.Summary of FindingsStudy findings spanned both ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). New therapeutic studies presented included the phase 3 study of vedolizumab in pediatric UC, phase 2 efficacy data for picankibart in UC, and regenerative fistula data of AVB-114 in perianal CD. Real-world data presented included comparative data on different Janus kinase inhibitors in UC. Updates on interleukin-23 included durable extension data in UC, as well as efficacy data in advanced therapy‑exposed patients (mirikizumab in UC and risankizumab in CD). Innovative technologies included transperineal ultrasound for early identification of steroid non-response in acute severe UC, and multimodal artificial intelligence for Mayo endoscopic scoring. Beyond drug therapy, the 10-year LIR! C follow-up provided further data on surgical intervention as an early strategy in selected uncomplicated ileal CD, while the Preventing IBD ONset in Individuals at Risk study investigated potential benefits of a whole-food diet in high-risk first-degree relatives.Conclusion and Next StepsECCO 2026 emphasized durable, precision-oriented, multidisciplinary, and technology-enabled IBD care across the full continuum, from prevention to long-term modification. Apart from phase 3 and extension trials, other results remain preliminary and need broader comparative and practice-embedded studies before widespread adoption.

Introduction

Over the past decade, the management of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has moved from a stepwise, symptom-based approach toward earlier use of advanced therapies (“top-down approach”), tighter objective monitoring, and treatment targets centered on sustained corticosteroid-free remission, mucosal healing, and long-term bowel preservation.1,2 Simultaneously, the scope of IBD research has expanded beyond conventional induction and maintenance paradigms to encompass disease prevention, treatment sequencing, fistulizing complications, surgery as a strategic rather than salvage intervention, and the integration of novel imaging and digital tools into routine care.

Against this backdrop, the studies presented at the European Crohn’s and Colitis Conference (ECCO) 2026 provide a timely snapshot of a field that is becoming both more biologically sophisticated and more clinically individualized. The meeting highlighted durable extension data for interleukin-23 (IL-23)-directed therapies, comparative effectiveness data for Janus Kinase inhibitors (JAKi), new phase 3 evidence in pediatric ulcerative colitis (UC), and emerging data on prevention, regenerative therapy, and bedside risk stratification, among many others. These themes are particularly relevant as they extend beyond short-term efficacy and address issues that increasingly shape real-world decision-making, including durability, sequencing, patient selection, and implementation.

Herein, abstracts and oral presentations in the ECCO 2026 abstract book were screened, and duplicate or overlapping reports were consolidated at the study level. Candidate studies were then assessed for clinical relevance, novelty, methodological rigor, generalizability, and guideline/policy relevance, while preserving thematic diversity across key domains of IBD care. Beyond summarizing the presented data, we provide an expert clinical appraisal of how these findings may inform current decision-making, where they remain insufficient for policy change, and which uncertainties should guide future investigation. Accordingly, this review synthesizes the ECCO 2026 studies most likely to influence contemporary practice and future research, emphasizing their clinical relevance, methodological strengths, and remaining uncertainties in the management of Crohn’s disease (CD) and UC. Phases 2 and 3 randomized controlled trials

Efficacy and safety of intravenous vedolizumab in pediatric patients with moderate-to-severe UC: results from the KEPLER phase 3 trial

Study overview

Moderate-to-severe pediatric UC remains a high-burden disease in which effective long-term steroid-sparing options are comparatively limited, and therapeutic development has historically lagged behind adult UC.3 Vedolizumab, an anti-α4β7 integrin biologic, has demonstrated efficacy in adult UC in the GEMINI 1 trial,4 but pediatric evidence was mainly derived from retrospective and observational cohorts.5–8 Among the most informative observational studies, the prospective multicenter VEDOKIDS cohort reported encouraging 14-week effectiveness and safety of vedolizumab in pediatric IBD, with particularly favorable remission rates in children with UC.8 Unfortunately, due to the paucity of head-to-head trials within the population, vedolizumab has been prescribed off-label on a case-by-case basis. To this end, KEPLER addresses an important evidence gap by evaluating intravenous vedolizumab for induction and maintenance therapy in children and adolescents with active UC who had failed conventional therapy and/or anti-TNF agents.

KEPLER (NCT04779307) was a phase 3 trial with a 14-week open-label induction phase followed by a 40-week randomized, double-blind maintenance phase.9 Eligible patients were aged 2 to 17 years, weighed at least 10 kg, and had moderate-to-severe UC defined by a modified Mayo score of 5 to 9 with an endoscopic subscore of at least 2 after prior failure of steroids, immunomodulators, and/or TNF antagonists. Vedolizumab was dosed by body weight during induction and patients were randomized 1:1 during maintenance to low- or high-dose intravenous vedolizumab every 8 weeks: 100 versus 150 mg for 10 to 15 kg, 100 versus 200 mg for more than 15 to less than 30 kg, and 150 versus 300 mg for at least 30 kg. The primary endpoint was week-54 clinical remission, defined as a stool frequency subscore of 0-1 and a decrease of 1 or more from baseline, rectal bleeding score of 0, and endoscopy subscore of 0-1, using the modified Mayo score.

A total of 121 patients were enrolled, 120 were dosed, and 93 entered maintenance randomization. Among randomized patients, the mean age was 11.9 years, 48.4% were male, 79.6% weighed at least 30 kg, 40.9% had prior anti-TNF exposure or failure, 44.1% were receiving corticosteroids at baseline, and 37.6% had severe disease. Real-world effectiveness and safety of three JAK inhibitors for UC: an IPTW analysis from IN-IBD

Study overview

JAK inhibitors have altered the treatment landscape for moderate-to-severe UC by providing oral, rapidly acting, advanced therapy options after failure of conventional or biologic therapy.18–20 However, comparative treatment selection among tofacitinib, filgotinib, and upadacitinib remains difficult because randomized head-to-head trials are lacking and trial populations differ. The IN-IBD IPTW analysis is therefore clinically relevant because it attempts to approximate comparative effectiveness among these agents in a large real-world cohort while adjusting for baseline imbalances.21

This multicenter retrospective observational study evaluated the comparative effectiveness and safety of the three approved JAK inhibitors using an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) approach to balance baseline characteristics. A total of 633 patients were included pre-IPTW, with 627 patients in the weighted analysis (upadacitinib [UPA] n = 313, tofacitinib [TOFA] n = 181, filgotinib [FILGO] n = 139).22

Clinical remission rates increased over time across all agents but were consistently highest with UPA. At week 52, clinical remission rates were 90.5% with UPA, 82.4% with TOFA, and 76.9% with FILGO (P = .010). Steroid-free remission followed a similar pattern (88.5% vs 77.0% vs 73.4%, P < .001). These findings were supported by time-to-event analyses, which showed a lower likelihood of achieving remission with TOFA (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.62-0.99) and FILGO (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.54-0.84) when compared to UPA. Endoscopic remission at week 52 was also higher with UPA (36.6%) than with TOFA (18.8%) or FILGO (19.5%) (P = .039).

In contrast, biochemical remission rates were similar across all three agents, suggesting that differences in clinical outcomes were not fully explained by normalization of systemic inflammatory markers alone. Time-to-symptom resolution further favored UPA, with faster improvement in bowel urgency than with either TOFA or FILGO. Across all agents, remission rates improved over time, though UPA consistently demonstrated earlier and more robust responses.22 These findings align with prior meta-analyses demonstrating that upadacitinib has superior efficacy and a rapid onset of action compared to other JAK inhibitors.23

Overall, adverse-event incidence per 100 patient-years differed across agents, with the highest exposure-adjusted rate reported for TOFA (135.18), followed by UPA (36.07), and lowest with FILGO (15.227) (P < .0001), while serious adverse events and colectomy rates were broadly similar. Importantly, colectomy rates and surgery-free survival did not differ significantly between treatments.22

The major strength of the study is its large multicenter real-world design with propensity weighting. Its main limitation is the residual confounding inherent to non-randomized treatment selection. Patients receiving different JAK inhibitors may differ in unmeasured disease severity, prior therapy trajectory, urgency burden, clinician preference, or contraindication profile. IN-IBD therefore supports upadacitinib as a strong real-world option when rapid and robust clinical response is prioritized, but it does not eliminate the need for individualized selection across the JAK inhibitor class.

Serious adverse events, serious infections, discontinuations due to adverse events, and other prespecified safety outcomes were infrequent with exposure. TRANSperineal ultrasound parameters for identifying steroid non-response in patients with seveRE UC (TRANSPIRE): an interim analysis

Study overview

Transperineal ultrasound (TPUS) is an imaging modality that allows for direct assessment of rectal inflammation, extending the benefits of point-of-care intestinal ultrasound to the evaluation of distal colonic disease.40 In acute severe UC (ASUC), early identification of patients who do not respond to corticosteroids is crucial, as up to 33% will require escalation to rescue therapy with infliximab or cyclosporine.41 Delays in recognizing steroid non-response prolong hospitalizations and increase the risk of colectomy.42

TRANSPIRE is a prospective, single-center observational study evaluating the role of TPUS in predicting steroid non-response in patients admitted with ASUC.43 Adult patients meeting Truelove and Witts criteria underwent TPUS at admission (T1) and again at 48 ± 24 hours (T2), with assessment of rectal bowel wall thickness (BWT) and Doppler activity.

Twelve patients were included (median age 40 years, 66.7% female), of whom 41.7% required further rescue medical therapy. Rectal BWT was consistently higher in non-responders at both time points. At admission, median BWT was 8.4 mm in non-responders versus 7.0 mm in responders (P = .09), with a T1 rectal BWT ≥ 8.4 mm predicting steroid failure (sensitivity 60%, specificity 100%, AUROC 0.80). This separation was also observed at T2, with a median BWT of 6.6 mm in non-responders versus 5.6 mm in responders (P = .02), and a threshold of T2 rectal BWT ≥ 6.6 mm predicting steroid non-response (sensitivity 60%, specificity 100%, AUROC 0.80).43

Importantly, the dynamic change in BWT appeared especially informative, suggesting that early reduction in rectal inflammation may be more useful than a single baseline measurement. A ≤20% reduction in rectal BWT between T1 and T2 predicted non-steroid response with sensitivity 80%, specificity 85.7%, PPV 80%, NPV 85.7%, and AUROC 0.83 (OR 24, 95% CI 1.0-45).43 Interestingly, TPUS outperformed baseline Ulcerative Colitis Endoscopic Index of Severity (UCEIS; AUROC of 0.59) for identifying steroid non-response.44

While indeed promising, TRANSPIRE should be viewed as an early feasibility study rather than a definitive diagnostic tool. The study is limited by a small cohort of 12 patients and inevitably wide confidence intervals. Furthermore, operator dependence remains a concern, and external validation is lacking. Larger multicenter studies with standardized scanning protocols and prespecified treatment algorithms are needed before implementation.

Study overview

Accurate endoscopic evaluation remains central to the management of UC. The Mayo Endoscopic Score (MES) is commonly used to guide treatment decisions and define mucosal healing. However, interobserver variability, particularly in distinguishing mild disease (MES 1), remains a limitation among experienced gastroenterologists.45

This study evaluated the performance of five contemporary multimodal large language models, including GPT-5, GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Grok-4, and Qwen-VL-Max, in grading MES using endoscopic images. A total of 402 colonoscopy images were collected, of which 283 achieved consensus grading by three expert IBD physicians and served as the reference standard.46

Diagnostic accuracy among the two expert reviewers were 81.6% and 78.4%, with strong interobserver agreement (k = 0.692). Among the models, findings were more heterogeneous. GPT-5 achieved the highest overall F1 score among the tested models and approached the lower range of expert diagnostic accuracy, while the other models performed substantially worse than experienced clinicians (F1: GPT-5 0.720 > GPT-4o 0.602 > Gemini-2.5-Pro 0.480 > Grok-4 0.415 > Qwen-VL-Max 0.338; all P < .001).

Model performance varied substantially by intestinal segment. The sigmoid colon was the most accurately assessed region (mean F1 across models = 0.682), whereas the rectum (mean F1 = 0.447) and the ileocecal region (mean F1 = 0.493) were consistently the most challenging. Both models and human readers struggled most with MES 1, the category in which clinical interpretation is already most subjective. Incorporating anatomical segment information (Phase B) improved model performance, particularly among lower-performing models, suggesting that contextual input enhances diagnostic accuracy.46

Ileocecal resection versus infliximab for ileal CD: retrospective 10 year follow-up of the LIR!C trial

Study overview

With approximately 40% of CD patients having disease involving the ileocecal region,47 treatment decisions involving this patient population often sit at the interface between escalation to advanced medical therapy anchored in biologics and referral for ileocecal resection (ICR). Despite the current ECCO guidelines proposing ICR to be a reasonable alternative to infliximab in uncomplicated ileocolic CD that failed conventional treatment (ie, corticosteroids and immunomodulators),48 ICR remains reserved for patients with advanced or medically refractory CD in conventional medical practice. The landmark LIR!C trial, an open-label RCT conducted between 2008 and 2015 at 29 centers, demonstrated that ICR is an effective alternative to infliximab in patients with uncomplicated ileocecal CD who have failed conventional treatment. Indeed, at 12 months, ICR demonstrated similar or better quality of life and improved cost-effectiveness, without an increase in serious adverse events.49 A subsequent retrospective study with a median follow-up of 63.5 months reported that the ICR group required lower rates of care escalation than the infliximab group.50

This 10-year retrospective follow-up study of the LIR!C trial participants aimed to assess whether ICR was associated with more durable long-term beneficial outcomes compared with infliximab (IFX) therapy beyond the medium term. With a median follow-up of 11 years, 129 of the 143 randomized patients had sufficient long-term follow-up data for analysis, including 66 in the ICR group and 63 in the IFX group.51 Primary outcomes were therapy-free remission and clinical remission. Therapy-free remission was defined as clinical remission without initiation of CD-related therapy in the ICR group, and, in the IFX group, by the same criterion plus complete discontinuation of IFX.51 Clinical remission was defined more pragmatically as successful continuation of allocated treatment without initiation or addition of new CD-related therapy.

Baseline characteristics were broadly similar, although active smoking, concomitant immunomodulator use, and perianal disease were numerically more frequent in the IFX arm than in the ICR arm. At 10 years, a statistically significant difference in therapy-free remission rates was observed, with 35.8% (95% CI 25.6-50.1) in the ICR group compared with 13.2% (95% CI 6.1-23.1) in the IFX group (P = .004). Interestingly, while the 10-year clinical remission rates were similar between the two groups (36.5% for ICR vs 28.4% for IFX; HR 0.79 [95% CI 0.52 to 1.20]; P = .27), post hoc interaction analyses suggested a significant treatment benefit with ICR in younger patients. An 10-year clinical remission rate of 54% with ICR versus 24% with IFX was estimated for a 20-year-old patient, whereas the difference narrowed to 38% versus 28% in a 30-year-old patient.51

While promising, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as overall clinical remission was similar between groups, and the apparent age-dependent benefit was driven by exploratory post hoc modeling. Generalizability may be limited by the highly selected trial population, retrospective non-protocolized long-term follow-up, absence of systematic endoscopic assessment, and the fact that discontinuation of IFX was not routinely attempted, which may have influenced the therapy-free remission endpoint.

Clinical implications

In selected patients with limited, uncomplicated ileal CD, early ICR was associated with higher 10-year therapy-free remission than IFX, aligning with contemporary ECCO surgical guidance and strengthening the evidentiary basis for discussing early ileocecal resection.

The Tasty&Healthy whole food diet improves calprotectin in high-risk first-degree relatives of patients with CD: the PIONIR randomized crossover trial

Study overview

As the burden of IBD continues to rise globally, preventative intervention in CD remains an unmet need. The concept is becoming more actionable as preclinical IBD stages are increasingly recognized before symptom onset.52,53 Recent work in healthy first-degree relatives (FDRs) has shown that multiplex-family FDRs have approximately a 3-fold higher risk of Crohn’s onset, and that persistent fecal calprotectin elevation can identify presymptomatic disease, supporting biomarker-enriched prevention studies.54,55 Against this background, alongside evidence supporting the Tasty&Healthy (T&H) whole-food diet in inducing remission in mild-to-moderate CD,56 the Preventing IBD ONset in Individuals at Risk (PIONIR) study evaluated whether this dietary strategy could modify intestinal inflammatory biomarkers in asymptomatic high-risk FDRs at a “Potential Pre-CD” stage.

PIONIR was a multicenter randomized two-period crossover trial conducted in Israel, Canada, and Portugal. Healthy asymptomatic FDRs with repeated fecal calprotectin >70 µg/g underwent pan-enteric capsule endoscopy or ileocolonoscopy. Participants with CD-like mucosal lesions were excluded; those with nonspecific or no mucosal inflammation were classified as “Potential Pre-CD” and randomized to T&H or habitual diet for 8 weeks, followed by crossover for 8 additional weeks.57 The T&H diet excluded processed foods, gluten, red meat, and most dairy products, without requiring formula supplementation.56 Fecal calprotectin was measured every 4 weeks, and treatment effect was analyzed by intention-to-treat generalized estimating equations, adjusting for treatment, period, sequence, and baseline fecal calprotectin.

Overall, 28 randomized participants were included (13 T&H-first, 15 habitual-first). The cohort had a mean age of 25.5 years ±7.3 years with a slight female predominance (59%). Similar baseline fecal calprotectin levels were observed at week 0 between the groups, although the T&H group had slightly higher, non-significant values (218 µg/g [106-264] vs 174 µg/g [91-352]; P = .78). By week 8, 86% were adherent to the T&H diet. At week 4, median fecal calprotectin was 45 µg/g (30-180) with T&H compared with 311 µg/g (137-413) in the habitual diet group (P = .004); at week 8, corresponding values were 45 µg/g (33-54) and 112 µg/g (56-215) (P = .047).58 After crossover, fecal calprotectin continued to fall in participants switching from habitual diet to T&H, whereas those moving from T&H to habitual diet showed only partial rebound, suggesting carryover from the prior diet.

Overall, in the intention-to-treat generalized estimating equation model, T&H diet was associated with a 37% lower fecal calprotectin than habitual diet (fecal calprotectin ratio 0.63, 95% CI 0.51-0.80; P < .001). Sensitivity analysis was performed by assessing outcomes based on which diet was initiated first, revealing minimal differences, further supporting the validity of the results. After 8 weeks, 54% of participants receiving T&H achieved fecal calprotectin <50 µg/g, compared with 20% receiving a habitual diet (RR 1.73, 95% CI 0.92-3.3; P = .09).57

PIONIR is the first and only randomized prevention trial in IBD currently. The study’s strengths lie in its targeting of screened preclinical risk states and extension of prior T&H data from established CD into a prevention-oriented setting. Yet, the small sample, short 16-week follow-up, selected biomarker-enriched FDR population, crossover design with possible carryover effect, and reliance on a surrogate endpoint rather than incident CD or endoscopic progression necessitate further study.

Conclusion

All in all, the ECCO 2026 conference showcased several impactful studies across the entire spectrum of IBD care. The field is entering a more mature phase in which durability, treatment positioning, and individualized precision are arguably becoming as important as demonstrating efficacy itself. Together, the aforementioned studies featured in this review extend the IBD research agenda from preclinical disease interception to chronic disease modification and complication management, while also highlighting the growing importance of multidisciplinary and technology-enabled care. Nevertheless, apart from phase 3 and long-term durability studies, much of the data remains either preliminary, responder-enriched, uncontrolled, or derived from small and selected cohorts, and should therefore be interpreted as practice-informing rather than practice-defining. The priority now is to validate these findings through rigorous comparative studies and to integrate them into coherent, patient-centered care pathways across diverse IBD populations worldwide.
