Blood Lactate as a Prognostic Biomarker for Survival and Weight Loss in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis: An Exploratory–Validation Study
Authors: Ryutaro Nakamura, Frederik J Steyn, Shuhei Kobashi, Nobuhiro Ogawa, Akihiro Kitamura, Isamu Yamakawa, Robert D Henderson, Shyuan T Ngo, Makoto Urushitani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLactate is increasingly recognized as an energy substrate, but its relevance to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) remains unclear. We examined whether blood lactate is associated with survival and weight loss in ALS.MethodsThis retrospective study included an Australian exploratory cohort and a Japanese validation cohort with clinical data matched to measures of blood lactate. The primary outcome was survival from onset to death or tracheostomy. In the exploratory cohort, survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by the first quartile (Q1) of lactate and multivariable Cox regression. This cutoff was applied for log‐rank analysis in the validation cohort. The secondary outcome was 3‐month body mass index (BMI) change (ΔBMI). Associations between baseline lactate and ΔBMI were assessed using multivariable regression in the exploratory cohort, whereas Q1‐based stratification was used in the validation cohort.ResultsThe exploratory cohort included 110 patients with ALS (57 with ΔBMI data) and 86 healthy controls; the validation cohort included 36 patients (17 with ΔBMI data). In the exploratory cohort, lower lactate levels (below Q1; 1.05 mmol/L) were associated with shorter survival (p = 0.031) and remained independently predictive of higher risk for mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] per 1 SD = 0.57, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.35–0.91). This association was confirmed in the validation cohort (p = 0.003). Lactate was independently associated with ΔBMI (β = 0.53, p = 0.032), and patients with lower lactate had a greater BMI decline (p = 0.010).InterpretationLower blood lactate is associated with increased risk for earlier death and greater weight loss in ALS, suggesting that lactate is a prognostic biomarker of nutritional status. ANN NEUROL 2026;99:1526–1535

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by the degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons. 1 Multiple molecular abnormalities have been implicated in the pathogenesis of ALS, including dysregulated RNA metabolism, protein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, glial cell dysfunction, and impaired axonal transport. 2 , 3 However, the full pathophysiology of the disease remains incompletely understood.

Beyond cellular and molecular hallmarks, systemic abnormalities in energy metabolism have gained recognition as a key feature of ALS. Approximately half of patients exhibit elevated resting energy expenditure (hypermetabolism), 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 which has been associated with faster functional decline and shorter survival. 4 , 7 , 8 , 9 Furthermore, weight loss often precedes the onset of motor symptoms 10 and is directly linked to shortened survival duration. 11 These findings suggest that metabolic dysfunction may not be a mere secondary phenomenon but could actively contribute to disease progression. Supporting this notion, patients who gain weight after diagnosis tend to have longer survival, 12 and those with higher oral energy intake per ideal body weight show improved prognosis. 13 Moreover, high‐calorie nutritional interventions have been shown to prolong survival in patients with rapidly progressing ALS. 14 Trimetazidine, an anti‐ischemic drug that also promotes glucose utilization and partially inhibits β‐oxidation, 15 has been shown to reduce oxidative stress markers and suppress energy expenditure in ALS models and in patients. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

These observations highlight that impaired energy metabolism, including inefficient glucose utilization and mitochondrial dysfunction, may play a central role in ALS pathophysiology. Within this framework, lactate, the product of anaerobic glycolysis, represents a key intermediary of energy transfer between tissues. Traditionally regarded as a marker of anaerobic metabolism and poor prognosis in systemic conditions, such as myocardial infarction 20 and sepsis, 21 lactate also serves as an important energy substrate 22 and has been shown to facilitate both glucose 23 and lipid utilization. 24 In ALS, elevated blood lactate following exercise has been linked to greater functional decline and faster disease progression, 25 consistent with oxidative capacity and impaired lactate clearance due to mitochondrial dysfunction. 26 However, the clinical significance of resting blood lactate levels, which may reflect baseline metabolic efficiency rather than acute stress, remains unclear. Experimental data suggest that lactate may exert protective effects; in superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) mutant mouse model of ALS, spinal cord lactate levels decline prior to the onset of a disease phenotype, and lactate supplementation prolongs motor neuron survival. 27 We therefore hypothesize that resting blood lactate levels could be associated with impairments in systemic energy metabolism and thus may play a protective role in ALS.

To clarify the clinical significance of resting lactate in ALS, we conducted an international exploratory–validation study across independent Australian and Japanese cohorts to examine the associations between lower lactate levels and faster disease progression, reflected by shorter survival and short‐term weight loss.

Methods

Study Design

This multicenter, retrospective study included 2 independent cohorts: an Australian cohort from the University of Queensland and the Royal Brisbane and Women's Hospital in Australia, and a Japanese cohort from Shiga University of Medical Science in Japan. The Australian cohort contributed data for exploratory analyses, and the Japanese cohort served as the validation set (Fig 1). Patients across both sites were eligible if they had a diagnosis of ALS according to the revised El Escorial criteria, and had available blood lactate data. 28 Participants receiving therapies that could directly impact metabolism and body weight were excluded. Participants with a diagnosis of diabetes were excluded, although by different procedures: in Australia, they were deemed ineligible at recruitment; in Japan, they were screened but subsequently excluded from analysis. We adhered to the relevant Strobe checklist.

Participants

The Australian cohort included participants who underwent blood lactate testing between May 2017 and September 2022, as well as healthy controls who were friends and relatives of the patients. Control participants had no cognitive or neurodegenerative disease. All participants were enrolled in the Metabolic Exploration of Neurodegenerative Disease (MEND) natural history study, which investigates systemic metabolic alterations in ALS. Clinical and metabolic data were collected on the same day as blood lactate measurement. Clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), site of symptom onset, diagnostic delay (months from symptom onset to diagnosis), revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS‐R), 29 and percent predicted forced vital capacity (%FVC). ALSFRS‐R scores were assessed by trained research staff. Rate of disease progression was inferred from the change in the ALSFRS‐R over time (ΔFRS), and was calculated as the monthly rate of loss of total ALSFRS‐R points since symptom onset. 30 Body composition, including fat mass (%) and fat‐free mass (FFM; kg), was assessed using air‐displacement plethysmography (BodPod, Cosmed), as previously described. 7 Follow‐up BMI data obtained within 3 months (±2 weeks) of baseline were included when available to capture meaningful weight change while minimizing confounding from changes in care or nutrition.

The Japanese cohort included patients assessed between March 2016 and October 2024 at Shiga University of Medical Science. All patients met the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as the Australian cohort. Clinical data were obtained at the time of blood lactate measurement and included age, sex, BMI, site of symptom onset, diagnostic delay, ALSFRS‐R score, and %FVC. ALSFRS‐R scores were determined by neurologists. The rate of disease progression was calculated as done for the Australian cohort, and BMI data obtained within the same 3‐month (±2 weeks) window were considered for analysis. In addition to these shared variables, a detailed metabolic and nutritional panel was measured concurrently with blood lactate levels. Parameters included high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL; mg/dl), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL; mg/dl), triglycerides (TGs; mg/dl), fasting blood glucose (Glu; mg/dl), pyruvic acid (mg/dl), total ketone bodies (μmol/l), non‐esterified fatty acids (NEFA; μEq/l), creatinine (Cre; mg/dl), prealbumin (PreAlb; mg/dl), immunoreactive insulin (IRI; μU/ml), and C‐peptide immunoreactivity (CPR; mg/dl). Insulin resistance was estimated using the homeostasis model assessment (HOMA‐IR), calculated by multiplying IRI and glucose values and dividing the product by 405. 31

Lactate Evaluation

For the Australian cohort, venous whole blood was collected after overnight fasting and analyzed immediately using the i‐STAT CG4+ cartridge (Abbott Point of Care, USA). Samples were obtained at rest during baseline assessment. For the Japanese cohort, venous serum lactate was measured at rest following overnight fasting as part of routine diagnostic biochemistry performed at hospital admission.

Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome was survival from disease onset, defined as the interval from symptom onset to death or tracheostomy invasive ventilation. Patients were censored on January 1, 2024 (Australia) and October 1, 2024 (Japan). Survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank tests comparing groups stratified by the first quartile (Q1) of lactate concentration. Multivariable Cox regression included age, sex (female), BMI, bulbar onset, diagnostic delay, %FVC, and ΔFRS as covariates. The secondary outcome was change in BMI at 3 months (ΔBMI, defined as BMI at 3 months minus BMI at baseline). In the Australian cohort, associations between lactate levels and ΔBMI were assessed using multiple linear regression including age, sex (female), baseline BMI, ALSFRS‐R, and ΔFRS as covariates. In the Japanese cohort, given the limited sample size, we focused on group‐based comparisons using stratification by the Q1 lactate threshold derived from the exploratory cohort, and ΔBMI was compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Additional exploratory analyses were performed separately in each cohort. For Australian participants, logistic regression analysis was performed with ALS case–control status as the dependent variable and blood lactate level as the independent variable, adjusting for age and sex. Associations among lactate and BMI, FFM, fat percentage, and ALSFRS‐R scores were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation. In Japanese participants, correlations between lactate and metabolic or nutritional markers (pyruvate, CRP, NEFA, NEFA/ketone body ratio, PreAlb, TG, HDL, LDL, glucose, ketone bodies, HOMA‐IR, IRI, and Cre) were examined using principal‐component and Spearman correlation analyses. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied to exploratory analyses, as these analyses were hypothesis‐generating in nature. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two‐sided p values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Missing data were not imputed.

Use of Artificial Intelligence

The authors used ChatGPT (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA) to generate R and R Markdown scripts for statistical analyses. All code outputs were verified and executed by author Ryutaro Nakamura. No patient‐level data were interrogated, or manuscript text was generated by artificial intelligence (AI).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

Ethical approval was obtained from the research ethics committees of the University of Queensland (2015/HE000022), the Royal Brisbane and Women's Hospital (HREC/14/QRBW/495), and Shiga University of Medical Science (R2023‐072). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in the Australian cohort. In Japan, informed consent was obtained using an opt‐out approach in accordanckge with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Results

Participant Selection and Baseline Characteristics

Screening and inclusion of study participants are summarized in Figure 1. In the exploratory cohort, 149 patients were screened; 39 were excluded (n = 37 with no lactate data and n = 2 receiving therapies that impact metabolism), leaving 110 patients for the primary outcome analysis. Among these, 57 patients with follow‐up BMI data at 3 months (±2 weeks) were included in the secondary outcome analysis. Eighty‐six non‐neurodegenerative disease individuals were enrolled as controls. In the validation cohort, 113 patients were diagnosed with ALS during the study period; 77 were excluded (n = 76 with no lactate data and n = 1 with a history of diabetes mellitus), leaving 36 patients for the primary outcome analysis. Of these, 17 patients with 3‐month (±2 weeks) follow‐up BMI data were included in the secondary analysis. Baseline characteristics of included and excluded patients are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

In the exploratory cohort (Table 1), blood lactate levels were significantly higher in people living with ALS (plwALS) than in healthy controls (p = 0.001). The plwALS were older (p = 0.014) and more likely to be women (p < 0.001). Higher lactate levels remained significantly associated with ALS after adjustment for age and sex (odds ratio = 2.29, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.17–4.51). There was no significant correlation between lactate levels and the duration from symptom onset to testing (rs = 0.15, p = 0.114). Although not statistically significant, male patients tended to have higher blood lactate levels than female patients (median = 1.34 mmol/L, interquartile range [IQR] = 0.50 vs 1.14 mmol/L, IQR = 0.63, p = 0.054). When comparing cohorts, Australian plwALS were younger (p = 0.005), had higher BMI (p < 0.001), and more frequently reached study end points (p < 0.001).

Primary Outcome: Blood Lactate Levels and Survival

In the exploratory Australian cohort, patients with ALS were stratified by the first quartile of blood lactate (1.05 mmol/L). The log‐rank test showed that plwALS with lower lactate levels had significantly shorter survival than those with higher levels (median survival = 39 vs 49 months, p = 0.031; Fig 2A). In multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, lower lactate levels remained independently associated with a higher risk of death (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] per 1 mmol/L = 0.36 and HR per 1 SD = 0.57, p = 0.019; Table 2). When considering the validation cohort, applying the same cutoff demonstrated a stronger association between lower lactate levels and shorter survival (median survival, low 21 months; high not reached, p = 0.003; Fig 2B). Post hoc sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative lactate cutoffs (0.90, 1.10, and 1.31 mmol/L [median in the exploratory cohort], and 1.58 mmol/L [third quartile]). For these post hoc cutoff‐based analyses, p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg) procedure. Significant survival differences were observed at thresholds between 0.90 and 1.31 mmol/L; Supplementary Fig S1). At 1.58 mmol/L, the association remained significant in the Australian cohort only (see Supplementary Fig S1A), with significant associations observed at 0.9, 1.1, and 1.31 in the Japanese validation cohort (see Supplementary Fig S1B).

Secondary Outcome: Blood Lactate Levels and BMI Change

We next evaluated the association between baseline blood lactate levels and change in BMI (ΔBMI) over a 3‐month follow‐up period. BMI, and not body weight, was used to account for differences in patient body size to enable standardized comparison across cohorts. In the Australian cohort, the median ΔBMI was −0.18 kg/m2 (IQR = −0.72 to 0.37), indicating substantial interindividual variability. In the Japanese cohort, the median ΔBMI was −0.28 kg/m2 (IQR = −0.61 to 0.01). ΔBMI did not differ between cohorts (p = 0.355; see Table 1).

Multiple regression analysis showed that higher baseline blood lactate levels were significantly associated with greater ΔBMI in the Australian cohort (β = 0.532, standard error [SE] = 0.241, p = 0.032; Supplementary Table S2 and Fig 3A). Patients with lower lactate levels (< 1.05 mmol/L) had significantly greater BMI loss than those with higher levels in the validation cohort (p = 0.010; Fig 3B).

Exploratory Outcomes: Relationship Between Blood Lactate and Clinical Characteristics

In the exploratory Australian cohort, we assessed whether blood lactate levels were associated with measures of functional decline and body composition. Higher blood lactate levels correlated positively with BMI, fat mass percentage, and the bulbar domain subscore of the ALSFRS‐R, and negatively with the motor domain score (Supplementary Fig S2). In the validation Japanese cohort, we examined the relationship between blood lactate levels and additional nutritional or metabolic biomarkers (Supplementary Table S3). Principal component analysis (PCA) and correlation clustering were used to identify shared patterns among metabolic variables. The first 2 principal components explained 62.9% of the total variance (PC1 = 35.0% and PC2 = 27.9%; Fig 4A). Along the PC1 axis, lactate loaded in the same direction as pyruvate, PreAlb, TG, and the NEFA/ketone body ratio, and in the opposite direction to NEFA, ketone bodies, and HDL. Correlation analysis confirmed significant associations between lactate and these biomarkers, except for NEFA, which did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.062).

Discussion

In this study, we found that lower resting blood lactate levels were associated with shorter survival and greater weight loss 3 months after assessment. In addition, lactate levels in Japanese patients correlated with circulating biomarkers linked to nutritional status and lipid metabolism. Together, these findings support the hypothesis that resting lactate reflects systemic metabolic efficiency, and that lower levels are associated with an increased likelihood of weight loss, faster disease progression, and earlier death in plwALS.

Previous studies have primarily examined exercise‐induced changes in blood lactate in ALS, reporting elevated concentrations both before and after exercise initiation. 26 , 32 Impaired lactate clearance during exercise has been linked to greater functional decline and faster disease progression, 26 with mitochondrial dysfunction proposed as an underlying mechanism. 25 , 33 Whereas exercise‐induced lactate accumulation has been considered a biomarker of disease progression, the prognostic and metabolic significance of resting lactate levels has not been systematically investigated. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively evaluate resting lactate in relation to body weight and clinical outcomes in ALS.

For the primary outcome of survival, lower blood lactate levels were associated with shorter survival. Each 1 SD increase in lactate was associated with a 43% lower risk of death, an effect stronger than that of %FVC (exploratory cohort). This association was validated in an independent Japanese cohort, where an association was even greater, possibly reflecting the lower median lactate level in that population. Sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoffs (0.90, 1.10, and the exploratory cohort median of 1.31 mmol/L) consistently reproduced findings, whereas significance diminished at higher thresholds (1.58 mmol/L). Collectively, these results indicate that reduced lactate levels, rather than elevated levels conferring further benefit, are associated with poorer prognosis in ALS.

Consistent with this interpretation, lower blood lactate was also associated with greater weight loss, a key marker of disease progression and metabolic imbalance in ALS. In the exploratory cohort, lactate levels were independently associated with ΔBMI after adjustment for baseline BMI. Notably, at lower lactate values, the estimated 95% CI for ΔBMI lay entirely below zero, whereas at higher lactate values the CI included zero. In the validation cohort, all patients with lactate < 1.05 mmol/L showed BMI loss within 3 months. Few biomarkers currently predict future weight loss in ALS; previous studies have implicated ferritin, LDL cholesterol, and creatinine as markers of FFM loss, 34 , 35 but these require serial measurement. In contrast, our data suggest that blood lactate may provide prognostic information from a single, readily obtainable test. Although these findings are encouraging, the limited sample size of the present study means that larger, independent cohort studies will be required to confirm the robustness and generalizability of the observed associations.

Exploratory analyses further suggested that resting lactate levels are related to systemic metabolic state. In Australian plwALS, lactate correlated positively with BMI, fat mass percentage, and ALSFRS‐R bulbar domain scores. In Japanese plwALS, blood lactate was positively associated with PreAlb, a sensitive marker of protein–energy availability. Together, these findings support the interpretation that lower blood lactate levels may reflect poorer nutritional status in ALS. These associations may be related to the role of lactate in facilitating substrate utilization. Lactate was inversely correlated with NEFA, a surrogate marker of glucose intolerance in plwALS, 36 suggesting that higher lactate reflects preserved glycolytic flux. Furthermore, lactate was positively associated with TGs, and the NEFA/ketone body ratio, and negatively with HDL cholesterol and ketone bodies, indicative of a metabolic profile favoring lipid utilization over accumulation.

Recent studies have increasingly examined the associations between circulating lipid profiles and the risks of ALS onset 37 and mortality, 38 yielding mixed results. Higher HDL levels 37 and long‐term statin use 39 have been associated with a lower risk of ALS onset, suggesting that lipid‐lowering mechanisms may have protective effects against disease development. In contrast, higher LDL and lower HDL levels have been reported to correlate negatively with mortality risk. 38 , 40 , 41 Furthermore, patients with slower disease progression exhibit enhanced β‐oxidation, 42 and greater body fat percentage predicts longer survival. 43 , 44 Together, these findings suggest that improved lipid metabolism may play a protective role against mortality after ALS onset. The association between lower lactate levels and poorer survival, along with the observation that lower lactate was accompanied by higher HDL levels, is directionally consistent with prior reports linking higher HDL concentrations to increased mortality risk in ALS.

Although our observational study cannot provide definitive answers, we posit that increased expression of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4) in ALS muscle 45 might contribute to increased lactate levels in ALS and the negative correlation with ALSFRS‐R motor domain scores. In cancer, PDK4 upregulation increases anaerobic glycolysis and lactate production, 46 suggesting a similar adaptive mechanism may occur in ALS. Indeed, elevated lactate has been observed in the skeletal muscle of SOD1 mice. 33 Given that skeletal muscle is the primary source of circulating lactate, 47 increased muscle lactate production may therefore reflect a shift toward adaptive glycolytic metabolism under energy stress.

Whereas offering new insights, we note several limitations. The retrospective design of this study limits our ability to infer a causal effect of blood lactate levels on survival time, body weight loss, and substrate utilization efficiency. Moreover, we note that our sample size, especially for end points within the Japanese cohort, was modest. Nonetheless, the validation cohort reproduced key findings, and its detailed metabolic profiling provided mechanistic insight into lactate's association with energy metabolism. Although lactate measurement methods differed between cohorts, plasma and whole‐blood concentrations are strongly concordant below 2.2 mmol/L, 48 and consistent associations with both survival and BMI change suggest that this relationship is robust. In addition, data on blood neurofilament light chain (NfL), an established prognostic biomarker in ALS, 49 were not available in the present study. Malnutrition and skeletal muscle loss have been associated with higher blood NfL levels in ALS. 50 Given our findings that resting blood lactate relates to markers of nutritional status, future studies should test whether lactate is associated with blood NfL, and whether any association is independent of nutritional status and disease severity. Finally, as a cross‐sectional study, sampling time points were not standardized; however, lactate was not correlated with the interval from symptom onset to testing, suggesting minimal impact of sampling variability.

In conclusion, lower resting blood lactate levels were associated with greater weight loss and shorter survival in ALS. These findings suggest that lactate may reflect systemic metabolic efficiency and serve as a convenient biomarker of metabolic status. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether lactate‐guided nutritional or metabolic interventions can improve outcomes in ALS.
