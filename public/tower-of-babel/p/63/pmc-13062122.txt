Assessment of the carcinogenic potential of particulate matter and organic compound mixtures generated from 3D printing devices in Balb/c 3T3-1-1 cells
Authors: DongSeok Seo, CheolHong Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fused deposition 3D printers using acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) and polylactic acid (PLA) and various materials used as inks in 3D printers are used without risk assessment. Thus, hazards associated with 3D printer materials need to be determined. As a follow-up to previous research on particulate matter (PM) generated during 3D printing using ABS and PLA filaments, this study aims to investigate the carcinogenic potential of mixtures containing 3D printing-emitted PM and organic solvents. Cytotoxicity, cell transformation, cell cycle and apoptosis assays, cancer-related gene expression, telomere analysis, and miRNA analysis were performed using 1% and 10% ABS and 1% and 10% PLA. No effects on telomeres and apoptosis were observed; however, the expression of carcinogenesis-related genes, HMGAs, was slightly increased in the lactic acid-exposed group. Cell cycle analysis showed an increase in the number of cells in the S phase in the test article-exposed group. Up- and down-regulated microRNAs associated with carcinogenesis were also observed, while cell transformation assays could not be concluded as positive. Additionally, transformed foci were observed in some test article-exposed groups. While the mixture of particulates and organic solvents produced during 3D printing with ABS and PLA filaments exhibited no definitive carcinogenicity, some variations were evident in the cell transformation assay. The changes in HMGA gene expression, cell cycle, and miRNA analysis are thought to be attributed to exposure to the test substances. Therefore, further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms of action underpinning the observed effects to definitively establish whether they are related to exposure to the test substances.

Introduction

3D printing continues to grow as an industry with promising prospects and is used in a variety of industries. In particular, it is expected that the use of this technology will increase due to its ease of customisation, reduced prototyping time, efficient production with less waste of materials, and flexibility in small batch production.

A fused deposition method (FDM) 3D printer is a form of 3D printer that melts thermoplastics and builds them up layer by layer to create multi-dimensional objects. These methods are relatively inexpensive and are widely used as science teaching aids in schools. In particular, in South Korea, 3D printers have been widely distributed since 2014. By 2020, 18,324 3D printers were distributed nationwide to 5,222 elementary, middle, and high schools (43.45% penetration rate). Of these, 1,184 elementary, middle, and high schools (9.85%) reported using acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) as a printing material. FDM-based 3D printers, which mainly use ABS and poly lactic acid (PLA) filament materials, have been widely used in education.

The release of particulate matter along with volatile organic compounds (VOCs) during 3D printing has raised potential risks for users. Particulate matter generated during 3D printing can be inhaled deeply in the respiratory tract, reaching the alveoli and distributed throughout the body, raising concerns about local and systemic toxicity1. It has been reported that various volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are emitted alongside particulate matter (PM). These include formaldehyde (Group 1), styrene (Group 2 A), acetaldehyde (Group 2B), ethylbenzene (Group 2B), methyl methacrylate (Group 3), toluene (Group 3), caprolactam (Group 3), and lactide (unclassified), as categorized by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)1–6.

However, to date, there is very limited information on the toxicity of substances generated during 3D printing with ABS and PLA filaments: In vitro cytotoxicity studies showed a dose-dependent increase in cytotoxicity4,7. Impairment of cardiovascular function was induced in rats exposed to ABS printer emissions8. And in South Korea, sarcoma cancer has been reported in three science teachers who used 3D printers with ABS and PLA filaments9.

Although three science teachers involved in 3D printing died of sarcoma, the use of 3D printers has not yet been recognized as a public health disaster due to the lack of a causal link to cancer. This is because a variety of carcinogenicity assessments are required to prove the carcinogenic relevance of 3D printing; however, toxicity data for carcinogenicity assessments are still lacking. Therefore, in this study, which is a follow-up to a study conducted in 2023 to assess the carcinogenicity of the particles generated during 3D printing with ABS and PLA filaments10, we aimed to determine the carcinogenicity of particles generated during 3D printing operations in combination with major organic solvents.

Results

Cytotoxicity assay

The results of the cytotoxicity assays are shown in Fig. 1; Table 1. The styrene and ethylbenzene mixtures (5:2 ratio) showed more than 90% cell viability up to a concentration of 500 µg/mL on a styrene basis (Fig. 1A); however, ABS particles were added to the styrene and ethylbenzene mixtures to produce an ABS particle concentration of 1%. All cells died at a particle concentration of 5 µg/mL, with cell viability of 67.1% at 2.5 µg/mL, 72.0% at 1.25 µg/mL, and 78.2% at 0.63 µg/mL in the prepared 1% ABS mixture. All cells died at a particle concentration of 25 µg/mL, with cell viability of 59.9% at 12.5 µg/mL, 79.3% at 6.25 µg/mL, and 82.0% at 3.1 µg/mL in the 10% ABS mixture (Fig. 1B).

Based on these results, the exposure concentration of the test substance was set to 12.5 ug/ml as the highest concentration for a 10% mixture of both ABS and PLA, and 2.5 µg/ml as the highest concentration for a 1% mixture, and then three exposure concentrations were set by applying common ratio 2.

Cell transformation assay

No transformed foci were observed in the negative control group exposed to 0.1% DMSO, whereas 21 transformed foci were observed in the positive control group exposed to MCA and TPA (Fig. 2; Table 2). One and two transformed foci were observed at 2.5 µg/mL and 1.25 µg/mL, respectively, in the 1% ABS group, while one focus was observed at 12.5 and 3.13 µg/mL each in the 10% ABS group (Fig. 3; Table 2). Only one focus was identified at an exposure concentration of 3.13 µg/mL in the 10% PLA group (Fig. 4). These results showed statistically significant changes in the positive control group (p < 0.05), but no statistically significant changes were observed in the test substance exposure groups (p > 0.05), so they were not judged to be positive. However, similar to the results of previous studies10, the small number of transformation foci identified in ABS and PLA cannot be ignored, as none were observed in the negative control group (Figs. 3 and 4; Table 2).

Gene expression assay

Analysis of changes in the carcinogenesis-related genes HMGA1 and HMGA2 after exposure to each agent showed that under the conditions of this study, the positive control group had approximately 5.2-fold higher levels of HMGA1 and 6.1-fold higher levels of HMGA2 than the negative control group, whereas the lactic acid-exposed group (PLA0) had approximately 2-fold higher levels. No significant changes were observed in the groups exposed to the particle and organic solvent mixtures (Fig. 5).

Cell cycle assay

The cell cycle distribution in the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases was examined after exposure to each test substance. Percentages of cells in the G0/G1, S, and G2M phases were 66.5%, 1.3%, and 32.2% in Assay-1 (1-d exposure during the initiation period) (Fig. 7A, B); 62.6%, 1.8%, and 35.6% in Assay-2 (3-d exposure during the initiation period and 1-d exposure during the promotion period) (Fig. 7C, D); and 79.1%, 2.2%, and 18.7% in Assay-3 (3-d exposure during the initiation period and 2-wk exposure during the promotion period) (Fig. 7E, F), respectively, in the negative control. While the positive control and exposure groups did not differ from the negative control in Assay-1, the proportion of cells in the S phase in the positive control increased to 27.3% in Assay-2 (Fig. 7C, D). In Assay 3, the percentage of S-phase cells in the positive control group decreased to 2.4%; however, the percentage of S-phase cells increased to 12.9% in 10% ABS and 9.3% in 10% PLA groups (Fig. 7E, F).

miRNA assay

miRNA analysis after exposure to each agent revealed that among 3,163 genes, 278 genes were up- or down-regulated by more than 2-fold the fc value compared to the negative control (data not shown). The rate of change was 4.11% in the positive control group, 1.49% in the group exposed to the styrene and ethylbenzene mixture, and 1.45% in the 1% ABS mixture and 3.38% in the 10% ABS mixture, which was more than twice higher. The rate of change was 1.14% in the 1% PLA mixture, which was not different from 1.45% in the lactic acid-only exposure group. However, the rate of change in the 10% PLA mixture was 2.69%, which was lower than that in the positive control group and 10% ABS exposure mixture, but 1.86 times higher than that in the lactic acid-only exposure group (Table 3).

Discussion

3D printers using filaments, such as ABS and PLA, are currently in widespread use owing to their ease of use and cost-effectiveness, despite concerns about their hazards to humans and the environment. Consequently, a systematic and comprehensive assessment of the carcinogenicity of 3D printing is required to address the safety and health measures for 3D printing and the under-reporting of work-related accidents. Therefore, in this study, we evaluated the carcinogenic potential of mixtures of particles generated from 3D printing devices using ABS and PLA filaments and organic solvents generated during the 3D printing process.

Cytotoxicity assays were performed to determine the exposure concentration of the cells and showed that both the mixture of styrene and ethylbenzene and lactic acid alone did not affect cell viability up to a concentration of 500 µg/mL; however, the mixture of ABS and PLA particles with organic solvents increased cytotoxicity. Particularly, the 1% ABS exposure group had 67.1% cell viability at a 2.5 µg/mL exposure concentration, while the 10% ABS exposure group had 72.5% at a 6.25 µg/mL exposure concentration. In the 1% PLA exposure group, cell viability was 78.9% at a concentration of 2.5 µg/mL, while it reached 59.9% at 12.5 µg/mL in the 10% PLA group. These findings align with previous reports; in our earlier study on particulate matter alone, ABS showed 85.5% viability at 10.0 µg/mL, whereas PLA exhibited negligible cytotoxicity even up to 40 µg/mL10. Such a contrast suggests that the cytotoxicity observed in the current study is primarily driven by the organic solvents. Furthermore, it is plausible that the mixture of PLA micro-particles and lactic acid may exert a synergistic cytotoxic effect.

Based on the results of these cytotoxicity assays, a cell transformation assay was performed. While no transformed foci were observed in the negative control group, 21 transformed foci were observed in the positive control group, indicating that the conditions for this assay were adequate. The number of foci observed in the ABS and PLA mixtures was less than or equal to two foci in each group. In the present study, no transformed foci were observed in the negative control, which is consistent with previous findings10. In contrast, historical data from Bhas 42 CTA in our laboratory showed approximately 10 transformed foci in negative controls (data not shown). These results suggest that the Balb/c 3T3 cells used in this study exhibit an exceptionally low rate of spontaneous transformation. Consequently, the observation of two or fewer transformed foci cannot be considered definitive evidence that ABS and PLA are devoid of carcinogenic potential10. These results could be attributed to the effect of organic solvents, as the mixed exposure groups (ABSL and PLAL) were treated with the same dosage of solvents as the solvent-only groups (ABS0 and PLA0). However, given that distinct impacts on cell viability were specifically observed in the mixed exposure groups, the influence of particulate matter cannot be ruled out.

Studies of the effects on the cell cycle and apoptosis following exposure to experimental agents have shown that cell cycle effects result in the loss of cell cycle checkpoint control, promoting cell proliferation and genetic instability. Cell cycle dysregulation is one of the basic mechanisms underlying cancer development, with cell cycle regulatory pathways implicated in a variety of cancer pathways, including metabolic remodeling and immune evasion11,12. In this study, cells in Assay-1 (exposure for 1 d during the initiation period) did not differ from the negative control in any of the exposed groups, including the positive control. However, in Assay-2 (exposure for 3 d during the initiation period and 1 d during the promotion period), the positive control had 27.3% cells in the S phase, compared to 1.8% in the negative control. Conversely, in Assay-3 (exposure for 3 d during the initiation period and 2 wk during the promotion period), the percentage of S-phase cells in the positive control group did not differ from the negative control group, but increased to 12.9% at the 12.5 µg/mL exposure concentration of 10% ABS and 9.3% at the 12.5 µg/mL exposure concentration of 10% PLA compared to the negative control group. These results suggest that ABS and PLA particles and their mixtures with organic solvents may affect the cell cycle.

Apoptosis is a systematic process that occurs under both physiological and pathological conditions. The role of apoptosis in cancer is based on the scenario in which minimal apoptosis occurs, resulting in malignant cells that do not die13. In this study, the rate of apoptosis in the negative control group was 33.1%, and the rate of apoptosis in the positive control group was 23.6%, which was lower in the positive control group than that in the negative control group. However, this was not thought to be related to cancer because similar levels of apoptosis were observed in the exposed groups.

Subsequently, we investigated the changes in the expression of HMGA1 and HMGA2, which were selected as carcinogenesis-related genes based on previous studies10. HMGA proteins are small nuclear proteins with high mobility and high HMGA1 expression has been suggested to play an essential role in embryonic development; however, the HMGA1 protein in mature differentiated tissues is either undetectable or detected at very low levels. Nevertheless, studies on several malignancies have provided convincing evidence of increased HMGA1 expression14,15. For example, HMGA1 functions as an oncogene through transcriptional regulation and protein-protein interactions16. HMGA2 is also expressed in embryonic stem cells during embryonic development but is absent or present at very low levels in adult tissue cells17. In contrast, HMGA2 is re-expressed in human malignancies, suggesting that HMGA2 is essential for development and carcinogenesis18. In the present study, HMGA1 and HMGA2 increased 5.2- and 6.1-fold, respectively, in the positive control group compared to that in the negative control group, which is similar to the results of previous studies. However, the group exposed to lactic acid alone also exhibited a roughly 2-fold increase in expression.

Telomeres are complex DNA-protein structures found at the ends of all eukaryotic chromosomes. In normal cells, telomere length shortens with repeated replication, and, in general, normal human cells cannot divide more than 90 times; therefore, they lose cell function, stop dividing, and undergo apoptosis. When normal cells become malignant, telomerase, which lengthens telomeres, is ideally activated, and telomeres can no longer be shortened19. Under the conditions used in this study, the telomere lengths of all test substances, including the positive control, were not more than 2-fold different from those of the negative control. The shorter telomere lengths of the positive control and the test substances than those of the negative control may be due to the fact that the total number of cell divisions did not reach 50 under the conditions of this study. Instead, only the rate of cell division was accelerated by exposure to the positive control or the test substances, resulting in shorter telomere lengths. These results highlight the paradoxical role of telomeres in cancer cells, as reported previously, wherein telomeres are shortened in cancer cells20,21. Therefore, the experimental design presented in the manuscript cannot explore the association between telomeres and cancer.

miRNAs are a family of non-coding (non-protein-making) RNAs that regulate gene expression, and have been identified as epigenetic markers of carcinogenesis. The over-expression and down-regulationn of miRNAs lead to complex interactions with a variety of genes that regulate cell proliferation, tumor invasion, and various markers of carcinogenesis. miRNAs are key regulators of the cell cycle, and abnormalities in their expression contribute to tumor development by disrupting key cell cycle regulators. More than 1000 miRNAs have been identified in humans that regulate the expression of approximately 60% of these genes. Consequently, miRNAs are being actively investigated as indicators of carcinogenesis and therapy and have been identified as diagnostic markers in many cancers, including breast, lung, liver, stomach, colorectal, pancreatic, prostate, ovarian, skin, kidney, thyroid, non-small cell lung, and B-cell lymphoma22–24.

In this study, 130 genes in the positive control group, 47 genes in the styrene and ethylbenzene mixture group, 46 genes in the 1% ABS 2.5 µg/mL group, and 107 genes in the 10% ABS 12.5 µg/mL group showed a 2-fold or greater change in fc values. In addition, 46 genes in the lactic acid group, 36 genes in the 1% PLA 2.5 µg/mL group, and 85 genes in the 10% PLA 12.5 µg/mL group showed changes. In the positive control, 13 out of 33 up-regulated miRNAs (≥ 2-fold) and 9 out of 44 down-regulated miRNAs were found to be associated with oncogenesis. Regarding the experimental groups, the ABS-exposed groups (ABS0, ABSL, ABSH) showed 1, 6, and 3 up-regulated miRNAs, respectively, with 5 down-regulated miRNAs observed in each group. For the PLA-exposed groups (PLA0, PLAL, PLAH), 8, 2, and 4 miRNAs were up-regulatedd, while 2, 4, and 2 miRNAs were down-regulatedd, respectively. miRNAs function as pivotal regulators of gene expression, orchestrating critical processes such as cell proliferation, tumor invasion, and the modulation of oncogenic biomarkers. The alterations in miRNA profiles observed in this study can be interpreted as epigenetic markers that potentially contribute to tumorigenesis by inducing cell cycle dysregulation. Ultimately, these findings suggest that exposure to mixtures of ABS/PLA particles and organic solvents can significantly modulate miRNA expression, thereby influencing epigenetic stability.

In conclusion, although no effect on telomere length or apoptosis was observed under the conditions used in this study, a slight increase in the expression of HMGA1 and HMGA2 was observed in the lactic acid-exposed group. Additionally, changes in the cell cycle were observed due to exposure to the test agents, along with up- or down-regulation of carcinogenesis-related miRNAs. Furthermore, although the cell transformation assay yielded a negative result overall, it is noteworthy that while no transformed foci were observed in the negative control group, one to two foci were identified in some of the groups exposed to the test substances. Despite the lack of clear evidence of carcinogenicity, the results of the cell transformation assays, HMGA gene expression, cell cycle analysis, and miRNA analyses suggested carcinogenicity. Although the expression of the HMGA gene increased only slightly in the lactic acid-exposed group, HMGA2, a structural transcription factor, is known to regulate the transcription and activity of numerous genes involved particularly in the cell cycle and apoptosis25,26. In this study, the increased number of cells in the S phase within the 10% ABS 12.5 and 10% PLA 12.5 µg/mL exposure groups suggests a potential impact on the G1 to S phase transition. In this regard, it is reported that analyzing Cyclin D1 and CDK6, which are key proteins influencing the G1/S transition, is crucial27. Furthermore, miRNA analysis suggests that genes with a fold change (Fc) of two or more may also be associated with chemical exposure28. Therefore, further detailed functional analyses are required to elucidate the underlying carcinogenic potential.

Methods

Generation of test materials

The experimental materials used were particulate matters, organic solvents, and mixtures of particulate matter and organic solvents generated from ABS (white; Cubicon Inc. Seoul, Republic of Korea) and PLA (white and cubic) filaments in a 3D printing device.

The composition of the organic solvent mixed with the ABS and PLA particles was based on the results of Jung et al.29. The study results reported that VOCs including acrolein, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene, styrene, and phenol are emitted from ABS filament. Among these, styrene was the most prevalent, accounting for 35.3–58.0%, while ethylbenzene accounted for 18.3–36.3%. Based on these results, a mixture of styrene and ethylbenzene in a 5:2 ratio was selected as a vehicle for particles generated from ABS filament. Upon exposure of the cells to this mixture, no macroscopic accumulation was detected within the culture medium. In addition, among the organic solvents generated from PLA filaments, lactic acid was generated up to 67.8%, and other solvents such as acrolein, acetic acid, normal butyl alcohol, methyl methacrylate, toluene, and styrene were all generated at less than 5%. Therefore, lactic acid was selected as an vehicle for particles generated from PLA filaments.

According to Vaisanen et al.30, PM2.5 and PM10 particles generated from 3D printer filaments were measured in the ranges of 0.2 to 2.22 µg/m3 and 5 to 20 µg/m3, respectively, while VOCs ranged from 41 to 56 µg/m3. Based on these findings, the concentrations of fine particles were set at 1% and 10% relative to the organic solvents. Consequently, the maximum concentrations of 2.5 and 12.5 µg/m3 in this study - determined via cytotoxicity assays - were established to reflect the cumulative exposure levels that 3D printing operators might encounter over long periods. These concentrations were specifically selected to clearly elucidate the intracellular molecular mechanisms induced by particle exposure. Additionally, 0.1% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich D8418) was used as the negative control, while MCA (Sigma-Aldrich 213942) and TPA (Sigma P8139) were employed as positive controls.

Cell line and subculture

Balb/c 3T3-1-1 (JCRB0601) cells were obtained frozen from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank (Tokyo, Japan). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator with 10 mL of M10F culture medium [(MEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA; cat. # 11095080), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ATCC 302020), and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Cytiva Hyclone, Marlborough, MA; cat. # SV30010)].

Cytotoxicity assay

Cells maintained in M10F medium were passaged once in DF5F medium [DMEM (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA; cat. #11320033) supplemented with 5% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin] and then seeded at a density of 400 cells per well in 96-well plates. Following exposure to the test agents, cell viability was determined using a WST-8 assay (Assay Genie, Ireland; cat. #MAE0207-500). The exposure schedule was established based on the cell transformation assay design. Specifically, viability was measured after 1–4 days after exposure during the initiation phase and 8–15 days after exposure during the promotion phase. Detailed information is provided in Fig. 9.

Cell transformation assay

Four hundred cells were seeded in each well of a 96-well plate and incubated in a CO2 incubator (day 0). Cell transformation assay was performed with reference to the improved Balb/c 3T3 cell transformation assay by Poburski and Thierbach31. In the Balb/c 3T3 cell transformation assay, the treatment schedule was designed to align with the distinct stages of neoplastic transformation. The initiation phase (72 h) targeted cells in the exponential growth stage to ensure the fixation of genomic damage. Subsequently, an extended promotion period of 4 weeks was maintained, as shown in Fig. 10, to allow for the clonal expansion of transformed cells and the emergence of morphologically identifiable foci, characterized by the loss of contact inhibition.

Schedule for cell transformation assay.

Additionally, 0.1% DMSO was used as a negative control, MCA was used during the initiation period, and TPA was used as a positive control during the promotion period. On day 39, the cells were fixed with methanol (Merck, cat. # 1.06009.1011), stained with 5% Giemsa solution (Muto Pure Chemicals; cat. # 1500-3), and examined under a microscope to identify transformed foci. Telomere assay

To provide a molecular basis for the morphological alterations observed in transformed foci, the telomere length assay was established based on the cell transformation assay protocol, as illustrated in Fig. 13.

Fig. 13Schedule for telomere assay.

Schedule for telomere assay.

Telomere analysis was performed on day 35 after seeding 400 cells in each well of a 96-well plate (day 0) and exposing them to each experimental agent, including the negative and positive controls, for an initiation period of days 1–4 (3 d) and a promotion period of days 8–22 (14 d), according to the following schedule. Each test substance was treated in four wells. Then, the cells were harvested and placed in a single tube, and the length of the telomeres was analysed twice. The assay was performed using the Absolute Mouse Telomere Length Quantification qPCR Assay Kit (ScienCell, Carlsbad, CA, USA; cat. # M8918). Genomic DNA was isolated from cells using a PureLink™ Genomic DNA Kit (Invitrogen, USA; cat. # K-1820-01).

Cells harvested from the cell culture plates were incubated with Proteinase K and RNase for 2 min at room temperature. Then, PureLink Genomic Lysis/Binding Buffer was added and the cells were proteolyzed at 55 °C for 10 min, followed by ethanol addition to prepare cell lysates. The cell lysates were loaded onto a PureLink Spin Column, centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 min at room temperature to attach DNA to the column, and eluted with PureLink Genomic Elution Buffer. The isolated DNA was quantified using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA; cat. # Q32851), adjusted to 5 ng/mL, and used for telomere analysis. The genomic DNA and PCR mix, telomere primer or single copy reference primer, and nuclease-free water were added to the PCR tube, and then telomeres were analyzed using RT-qPCR (at 95 °C for 10 min once, at 95 °C for 20 s, at 52 °C for 20 s, and at 72 °C for 45 s, 32 cycles). Telomere quantification was performed using the comparative ∆∆Cq (Quantification Cycle Value) method.

Cell cycle and apoptosis assay

The time points for cell cycle analysis were strategically selected, as shown in Fig. 14, to elucidate the temporal progression of neoplastic transformation. The apoptosis assay was performed as shown in Fig. 15, reflecting the previous mRNA microarray finding that gene expression changes were more pronounced on day 1 of the promotion phase10.

Fig. 14Schedule for cell cycle analysis.

Schedule for cell cycle analysis.

Fig. 15Schedule for apoptosis assay.

Schedule for apoptosis assay.

Cell cycle and apoptosis were confirmed using BD FACSVerse™ flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA). Each test substance, including the negative and positive controls, was treated in 2-well, harvested, and analysed for cell cycle and apoptosis. The cell cycle assay was performed by seeding 8,000 cells in each well of a 6-well plate and incubating in a CO2 incubator (day 0).

In Assay-1, to identify the immediate cellular response to the test agents during the promotion phase, cells were treated with each agent on day 7 of the promotion phase, and the cell cycle was subsequently measured on day 8 (Fig. 14A). Assay-2 was established to investigate how the early genetic fixation occurring during the initiation phase influences the cell cycle upon entering the promotion stage. Cells were treated with the test agents from days 1 to 4 and subsequently on days 8 and 9, with the cell cycle analysis performed on day 9 (Fig. 14B). Assay-3 was designed to evaluate the cumulative impact of chronic exposure by applying a two-week promotion period following the initiation phase. After exposure to the test agents for 3 days during initiation and 13 days during promotion, the cell cycle was analyzed on day 21 (Fig. 14C). The assay was performed by flow cytometry after staining with the BD Pharmingen™ APC BrdU Flow Kit (BD Biosciences, USA; cat. # 552598).

Apoptosis was assessed by seeding 8,000 cells in each well of a 6-well plate incubated in a CO2 incubator (day 0), treated on days 1–4 and 8–9, followed by staining with the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD Biosciences, USA; cat. # 556547) on day 9, and analysis via flow cytometry.

miRNA assay

The test design for the miRNA microarray was also performed on the same schedule as the apoptosis assay for the same reasons. Cells were seeded at 8,000 cells/well in a 6-well plate. Each test substance, including the negative and positive controls, was treated in 2-well, and the cells were harvested for miRNA analysis. RNA was isolated from cultured cells and labelled using the FlashTag™ Biotin HSR labelling kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. # 901910), hybridized in a GeneChip® Hybridization Oven, scanned using GeneChip® Scanne, and analyzed using Affymetrix® GeneChip Command Console® software (AGCC). miRNA analysis was performed by Macrogen, Inc. (Platform: GeneChip™ miRNA 4.0 Array).

Statistical analysis

The results of the cytotoxicity assay are plotted as the mean and standard deviation. The transformed foci were validated using a one-tailed chi-square test. The microarray data were assessed by visually checking the expression distribution of each sample using percentile, median, 25th percentile, 75th percentile, maximum, and minimum values. The reproducibility between each sample was tested by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient. miRNAs, carcinogenesis-related gene changes, and telomere lengths were compared with those of the negative control. In addition, the extent of changes in gene expression and telomere length ratio were plotted as means and standard deviations. Cell cycle and apoptosis were plotted as percentages of cells in each interval. Changes in miRNA expression levels were analysed by calculating the fc values for each test substance exposure group and then comparing the differences in fc values between the test group and the control group.
