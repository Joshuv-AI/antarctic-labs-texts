Qualitative approach to assess maternal knowledge, attitude and practice regarding oral rehydration solution preparation and administration among under-5 children suffering from diarrhoea in Dhaka, Bangladesh
Authors: Md Ridwan Islam, Md Fuad Al Fidah, Syed Jayedul Bashar, Rukaeya Amin, Sneha Paul, Chowdhury Ali Kawser, Tahmeed Ahmed, Sharika Nuzhat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral rehydration salt (ORS) solution has been instrumental in substantially reducing diarrhoea-related mortality, particularly in resource-constrained settings such as Bangladesh. However, the full effectiveness of ORS is often undermined by inadequate preparation, improper storage and incorrect administration. We aimed to evaluate mothers’ knowledge, attitude and practice regarding ORS preparation and administration for children suffering from diarrhoea in this study.MethodsThe study was carried out at Dhaka Hospital, ICDDR,B, using a qualitative research approach. Based on data saturation, in-depth interviews were conducted with 31 mothers of children under 5 who sought care at the hospital between February and April 2024. The transcribed data were analysed using qualitative content analysis to describe and analyse data.ResultsAlthough the majority of the mothers had a positive attitude regarding ORS preparation and administration, they lacked proper knowledge on several points, which hampered correct practices. Ten mothers did not know the correct amount of sachet powder to use, while nine mothers lacked the proper knowledge regarding the right amount of water to prepare ORS, which was rather alarming. Only five mothers recognised that ORS does not transfer to the baby through breast milk. Fourteen mothers provided ORS to their child after each episode of purging while the rest did not. Only 15 mothers adhered to the recommended feeding practices while administering ORS during their child’s illness.ConclusionThis study underscores mothers’ need for proper health education regarding the preparation and administration of ORS as well as targeted policies promoting the efficacy of ORS in diarrhoea.

Introduction

Diarrhoeal disease ranks as the third leading cause of death among children aged 1–59 months and is considered a major public health concern globally.1 Each year, nearly 1.7 billion episodes of diarrhoeal disease occur among children worldwide.1 According to the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), diarrhoea accounts for around 9% of all deaths among under-5 children, which translates to about 444 000 children in a year, with the burden being highest among South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.2 In Bangladesh, diarrhoea remains one of the leading causes of premature death among under-5 children, with a mortality rate of 12.69 per 100 000 children in 2019.3

Severe dehydration and fluid loss have always been the primary causes of morbidity and mortality associated with diarrhoeal diseases.2 Consequently, fluid and electrolyte replenishment has always been pivotal in the management of diarrhoea.4 Since its introduction in 1960, the oral rehydration solution (ORS), a simple mixture of water, salts and sugar, has proven to be an effective and affordable treatment to prevent and manage dehydration due to diarrhoea, ultimately reducing mortality and morbidity by replenishing the body’s lost fluids and electrolytes,57 especially in resource-limited settings like Bangladesh. Although ORS has been credited with saving nearly 54 million lives since 2007,8 its use has been stagnant in many regions over the past decades.9 Currently, ORS is not administered to more than half (nearly 57%) of individuals with diarrhoea in the world.10 The situation is more dire in developing countries where, according to UNICEF, in 2009, only 33% of children suffering from diarrhoea received ORS.11 In 2017, approximately 300 00 children died in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) that can be attributed to diarrhoeal diseases.12 The picture becomes more complex when we consider that the full potential of ORS is often compromised by improper preparation, storage and administration, particularly in LMICs.13

The correct preparation and administration of ORS is crucial to maximising its benefits in children who are suffering from diarrhoeal diseases. Despite its availability and simplicity, many caregivers lack the necessary knowledge regarding proper ORS use, leading to its underutilisation.14 Various alarming practices have been highlighted previously, such as using untreated water to prepare ORS and improper storage.15 Improper ORS practices also have significant consequences like electrolyte imbalance, gastrointestinal disturbance, etc.16 Moreover, inadequate ORS usage results in persistent dehydration, increasing the risk of severe complications and even death in young children.17 A nationally representative data set in 2019 presented that only 71.1% of under-5 Bangladeshi children were treated with commercially available packaged ORS.18 This gap in practice underscores the urgent need for health education on ORS practices to curb the high rates of childhood diarrhoea and related morbidity. Other knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) studies have explored caregivers’ understanding of ORS across LMICs.13 Studies conducted in Bangladesh revealed a lack of knowledge regarding ORS among mothers and underscored the importance of maternal knowledge on ORS use.1921 A study in Malaysia revealed misconceptions regarding ORS storage and preparation despite overall good knowledge about diarrhoea and ORS.15 Similarly, in India, research has shown significant gaps in mothers’ knowledge and attitudes towards ORS, emphasising the need for targeted interventions.22 23 However, there is a paucity of data on maternal KAP, particularly using a qualitative approach, which is known to provide a deeper insight.24 Also, quantitative studies are inherently limited in their ability to capture the depth and complexity of human attitudes. Quantitative KAP studies typically operationalise attitudes through structured questionnaires using Likert scales or binary responses. This imposes a limitation on inherently complex constructs. Attitudes are shaped by affective, cognitive and behavioural components that interact dynamically with sociocultural contexts. Such depth cannot be distilled into discrete, precoded response categories without significant loss of meaning. Qualitative methods provide the methodological tools necessary for accessing the multifaceted, culturally situated and often contradictory nature of attitudes. For an authentic and contextually resonant understanding of attitudes, qualitative inquiry is indispensable. If we are able to evaluate mothers’ accurate attitudes through our study, we would be able to improve the practice of ORS preparation and administration in children through targeted interventions. Given these gaps, this study aims to investigate mothers’ KAP on ORS preparation and administration for children under 5 with diarrhoea.

Methods

Study design and settings

This was a qualitative study. We conducted 31 in-depth interviews (IDIs) among mothers whose under-5 children were admitted to Dhaka Hospital, ICDDR,B due to diarrhoea. The study setting and the participants were selected purposively in our study. The Dhaka Hospital is the largest diarrhoeal disease hospital in the world, consisting of short stay unit (SSU), longer stay unit (LSU) and intensive care unit (ICU) providing cost-free treatment to patients of all ages according to the severity of diarrhoeal disease.25 Although the hospital provides care to approximately 200 000 patients each year, most of them are children aged <5 years.26 Mothers ≥18 years old with an under-5 child admitted to the SSU, LSU or ICU were deemed eligible for enrolment in our study.

Data collection

Data were collected through IDIs from 1 February 2024 to 30 April 2024. The investigators who conducted the IDIs were medical professionals and public health specialists with at least a master’s degree and prior experience in conducting IDIs. The interview guidelines and preinterview questionnaire were developed (online supplemental file 1) by the research team who had extensive expertise in diarrhoeal diseases, paediatric illnesses and research methodology. The preinterview questionnaire used in this study included sociodemographic data, while the IDI guidelines were designed to gather insights on maternal KAP regarding the use of ORS prior to reporting to the hospital. We conducted the IDIs within 2 hours of the children’s admission to the hospital to capture responses prior to their exposure to the health education provided routinely by the hospital. The interviews, lasting approximately 30–40 min, were conducted in a separate room and were recorded using audio recorders after obtaining written informed consent from the participants. Only the interviewer, interviewee and note-taker were present during data collection, ensuring the anonymity and privacy of the participants. Data were considered saturated when the investigators determined that no additional significant information was being obtained through the IDIs. In our study, stopping criteria were used to determine the point of data saturation. This approach involved continuing interviews until no new codes emerged or the number of newly identified codes dropped below 5%. Furthermore, the repetition of individual codes three to five times was used as a key indicator of data saturation, consistent with established practices in qualitative research.2729 No new themes emerged after 31 interviews in our study.

Data analysis and handling

The audio recordings were transcribed manually into Bangla. The qualitative content analysis method was used to analyse the transcribed data.27 30 The analysis was conducted by an expert research team with experience in treating children with diarrhoea, paediatric diseases and public health. A manual coding approach was used to determine themes that illustrate the KAP of mothers regarding ORS preparation and use. Each team member individually reviewed the transcripts and identified relevant themes, which were then finalised via consensus of the members to mitigate the analyst bias. Following this, two members separately translated these themes and quotes into English, and then all team members reviewed and finalised them. We used numerical identification codes to ensure participants’ anonymity. Manual Microsoft Office and Excel matrices were used for the analysis. No qualitative software was used in the study.

The generated codes were defined before and during analysis to assess KAP. To enhance the overall quality, the researchers scrutinised and cleaned the data at first. This process also facilitated an understanding of the data’s dimensions and structure. Subsequently, the researchers developed codes to evaluate KAP in accordance with WHO guidelines and previously published literature.31 32 A comprehensive coding plan was established to ensure a rigorous and systematic application across the data set. To review and refine the codes, weekly research meetings were conducted throughout the coding phase. A matrix display was created for each descriptive code, incorporating relevant section from the raw transcripts for each participant. Data were then interpreted from these matrices, and the coded information was systematically analysed to identify emerging themes. Codes that deviated from the study’s objectives were set aside, while only those aligned with the data were considered valid. The emergent themes were subsequently named and clearly delineated. Criteria for assessing each theme were determined based on both the WHO guidelines and the raw data, ensuring a robust and methodologically sound analysis. Finally, for a meaningful presentation of the data, it was quantified and depicted accordingly.

To thoroughly assess maternal knowledge, 18 criteria were identified, which are presented in table 1. Participants who provided accurate responses were classified as possessing ‘correct knowledge’, whereas those who failed to do so were categorised as having ‘incorrect knowledge’. For assessing maternal attitude regarding ORS, the investigators produced five codes after reflecting on the transcripts (table 1). If the responses were favourable towards the criteria, they were declared as ‘positive’ while the opposites were marked as ‘negative’; mothers who were undecided were categorised as ‘neutral’. The number of codes to document maternal practice regarding ORS preparation and use was 16 (table 1). The reported practices were reviewed and quantified on the basis of ‘proper practice’ and ‘improper practice’.

Reliability and validity

Various techniques were utilised to ensure the reliability and validity of the data. Our interview team members were familiar with local norms and beliefs and were also able to interpret usual expressions of the participants. The team members were public health experts who possessed previous experience, training and knowledge regarding ORS preparation and administration as per the WHO guidelines.31 Also, they were able to easily communicate with the participants to establish a reliable relationship and comprehend their perspectives.27 A team-based bilingual consensus review method was used to validate the transcription and translation process.33 Our study team of bilingual researchers collaboratively reviewed transcripts and translations, resolving discrepancies through discussion and consensus. This method enhances intercoder reliability and cultural sensitivity. Transcribers also used annotated transcripts to explain ambiguous terms and culturally specific references. During data analysis, regular peer debriefings were conducted among the members, and feedbacks were incorporated to ensure the reliability and validity of the methods.30 The interviewers also confirmed and verified their notes and actively probed participants for further information to ensure confirmability throughout the interview process.27 Internal validity was established by triangulating themes among interviews and maintaining an audit trail. The validity of the findings was assessed and verified using qualitative approaches. Participants’ statements were reported in their own language.30

The research team has followed ‘Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR)’ guidelines to structure the manuscript.34 Multiple reporting elements in SRQR overlap with those outlined in the ‘Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ)’ checklist.35 We have added additional sections to the manuscript for better correspondence to COREQ.

Research team and reflexivity

MRI, MFAF and SP conducted the IDIs. MRI holds MBBS and MPH degrees, while MFAF and SP possess qualifications in MBBS, MPH and MPhil, and BDS and MPH, respectively. All interviewers had undergone formal training in qualitative research methodologies and had previously published qualitative works in international peer-reviewed journals. In addition, MRI has received research grants for qualitative research projects in the past. MRI and MFAF are male researchers, whereas SP is female. At the time of the study, MRI served as an Assistant Scientist, MFAF as a Research Physician and SP as a Research Assistant. Investigators (MRI, MFAF, SJB, SP, RA, CAK, TA and SN) with public health training and prior ORS experience maintained ongoing reflexivity through team discussions and written logs, acknowledging their potential influence on data interpretation.

The research team did not have any contact with participants prior to obtaining informed consent. Researchers had no professional or ongoing relationship with the participants. Participants were aware that this was a research project. The team explained to the participants that the interviews were related to ORS and diarrhoea before taking informed consent. The research team had an interest in the incorrect preparation and administration of ORS in children.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

We interviewed a total of 31 mothers, including 10 from the SSU, 11 from the LSU and 10 from the ICU, whose children were admitted to the corresponding units. In our study, most (n=16) mothers were aged between 21 and 30 years, while the largest age group for the children was between 7 and 24 months (n=20). Most of the mothers were housewives (n=26) by profession, while only two were students. Knowledge regarding ORS

In this study, we facilitated 31 IDIs and explored mothers’ knowledge on different aspects of ORS. Knowledge on awareness regarding ORS

During the interviews, it was clear that all mothers had at least heard about ORS. Most (n=25) of them understood that it is used for treating diarrhoea in children. A few mothers (n=11), however, showed deeper knowledge. They explained that ORS is mainly used to prevent dehydration, not just to stop diarrhoea. This was captured in a mother’s statement: ‘ORS can be used to prevent dehydration due to diarrhoea, and ensure the body retains water and salts’ (participant 18). However, only one mother could report that ORS is used solely for the correction of dehydration.

Knowledge on preparation

Mothers displayed different levels of understanding about how to prepare ORS. Most (n=27) knew that washing hands and utensils is important. Many (n=21) also mentioned the need to use clean water. One mother reflected on this awareness, stating, ‘I use filtered water. We boil the water, filter it, and then use that water for the preparation of ORS’ (participant 17). However, some gaps were seen in measuring water accurately. A number of mothers (n=22) knew that the right amount of water is important, but others followed their own ideas. One mother described, ‘We would cool boiled water, dissolve one packet of ORS in half a litre of that water, and give approximately 10 spoonsful after each episode of diarrhoea’ (participant 19).

There was also confusion about adding extra ingredients. Some mothers said they mix sugar, salt or lemon with ORS to improve the taste. One said, ‘… especially during Ramadan, we sometimes mix a packet of saline with sugar, salt, or lemon to enhance its taste before consuming it’ (participant 10). A few mothers (n=10) also lacked clear knowledge of how much powder to use. One shared, ‘I take two to three spoonfuls of water and mix in oral rehydration salts based on my presumption. Then, I administer the prepared solution to my baby’ (participant 30). Another added, ‘I take a bottle feeder and fill it completely with water. Then, I take one spoonful of saline (the powder), mix it, and feed it to the baby …’ (participant 2).

Many mothers reported not reading the instructions on the ORS sachet, often due to limited time or assumptions that they already knew the preparation process. As one mother shared, ‘No, I never actually read instructions on the packet’ (participants 6 and 13). This indicates a broader issue of over-reliance on prior informal knowledge. Similarly, only a handful of mothers knew the correct amount of ORS required based on the child’s age. The view of most of the mothers’ was depicted in a comment, ‘No, I don’t know how much ORS to give based on the child’s age. I just mix it and feed it’ (participant 5).

Knowledge on administration

Many mothers (n=23) understood that ORS should not be stopped if the child is vomiting. One mother shared her experience, stating, ‘… I had ORS at home. I mixed the ORS, and gave it 3–4 times. But even after that, it wasn’t improving. He was still vomiting …’ (participant 27). Some mothers (n=21) knew that ORS is better than juices or other drinks for dehydration. Still, others (n=10) did not have this knowledge, which can affect how they manage their child’s illness.

There was also confusion about how often ORS should be given. Only a few mothers (n=8) said that ORS should be offered as much as the child wants, without pressure. One mother remarked, ‘The child usually doesn’t want to drink it, so I have to make them drink it forcefully’ (participant 2). A major misunderstanding was the idea that ORS can reach the baby through breast milk. Many mothers (n=26) believed this, based on advice from family members. Regarding this issue, a mother stated, ‘The elders in our household say that if I drink it, it helps the child, and they can get it through breast milk’ (participant 2). Another mother claimed, ‘If the baby refuses to drink oral saline, the mother should consume it herself … so, the saline can transfer through her breast milk and provide benefits to the baby’ (participant 28).

Knowledge on storage and safety

Some mothers (n=10) understood that incorrect ORS preparation can cause health problems, which shows that a few mothers think about possible harm. One mother explained, ‘If the amount of water is less and too much salt is added, the salt level in the body increases’ (participant 14). Most mothers (n=23) knew that ORS should not be stored for long periods. Some even took extra care to discard it quickly. This understanding was reflected in comments from a mother, who stated, ‘I never keep it for too long—if it exceeds an hour, I discard it because it loses its effectiveness’ (participant 04). Another mother claimed, ‘Oral saline turns to poison if stored for an extended period of time’ (participant 6).

Attitude regarding ORS

Attitude towards effectiveness

In this study, mothers generally (n=27) expressed a positive attitude towards the effectiveness of ORS in diarrhoea. This sentiment was reflected in the saying of one participant: ‘ORS is more effective for diarrhoea than antibiotics. As long as there is diarrhoea, ORS should be given’ (participant 01) (figure 3).

Distribution of positive attitude towards ORS among the mothers. ORS, oral rehydration solution.

Attitude towards cost

Some mothers (n=14) felt that ORS is still affordable, but many others (n=13) were worried about rising prices. One mother shared her concern, ‘It used to be 5 taka (Bangladeshi currency), but now it’s around 6 taka. A lower price would be better. If it rises further, it will be challenging for buyers like us and even harder for others to afford’ (participant 31) (figure 3).

Attitude towards availability

Most mothers believed that ORS is easy to find and usually kept at home. One mother shared, ‘It’s usually kept at home. I buy a whole carton at a time, so it’s there most of the time—like a box’ (participant 20). However, some mentioned difficulties when trying to buy it at odd hours. She said, ‘It is almost impossible to procure a saline packet during late night or early morning …’ (participant 24) (figure 3).

Attitude towards preparation and administration

A few mothers had negative feelings about preparing or giving ORS to their children (n=9). One mother stated, ‘As for disadvantages, children often refuse to drink ORS because it has a slight medicinal smell …’ (participant 27). Another mother commented, ‘Saline made using home materials is better, the packets sold outside can be fake ones …’ (participant 24) (figure 3).

Attitude towards learning about ORS and its use

Most mothers (n=22) showed a neutral attitude when asked about learning more about ORS. When probed whether the mother followed the instructions written on the backside of the packet, she commented, ‘I don’t usually get the chance to read the instructions on the back of the packet thoroughly with my child around … I don’t read all of it …’ (participant 04) (figure 3).

Regarding the administration of ORS

Many mothers (n=18) acted quickly when their child had diarrhoea. One participant said, ‘From the moment I noticed loose stools, I started giving him the ORS (participant 16)’. However, some (n=13) did not recognise the urgency. One mother responded, ‘No, no, I didn’t give ORS though there was loose stool and vomiting … (participant 29)’.

Most (n=28) mothers used proper instruments to prepare the solution. Twenty-one participants used safe drinking water as a mother put forward ‘I wash my hands and spoon, then mix the ORS packet in a half-litre glass using bottled water (participant 05)’. Almost all mothers (n=29) reported using antibiotics without proper consultation, while the majority (n=24) used proper appliances like glasses, cups, syringes or spoons to feed ORS to their children. One reported, ‘… I use a medicine spoon or a dropper to give it little by little (participant 03)’. An essential aspect of ORS administration is its timely provision after each episode of purging. Unfortunately, few (n=14) mothers followed this practice diligently. Among the mothers who did not, one stated ‘I gave saline to my child once in the morning, once in the afternoon, and once at night, like medicine (participant 12)’.

Regarding preparation

Reading the instructions was not a common practice. One out of 17 mothers who did not read shared, ‘Educated individuals read the instructions on the back of the saline packet, but I personally haven’t read or heard them’ (participant 22). Some mothers (n=14) used only part of the packet. One explained, ‘My baby does not want to drink the whole solution if I prepare it in one glass of water, I have to throw it away. That is why I take a smaller amount of water in a glass, approximately 200 ml … then I dissolve about one-fourth of the ORS powder in the glass to prepare the saline (participant 03)’. Others used too much or too little water. One said, ‘First, I opened the saline packet, then I took a little water in a bowl. After that, I mixed half of the saline in the water and gave it to my baby to drink (participant 12)’. Some participants also diluted the solution by using more water than required. One mother commented, ‘… I dissolved the whole packet in one-litre water (participant 08)’.

Alarmingly, few mothers (n=11) mixed additional substances like sugar or lemon into the ORS; a mother stated, ‘Yes, I have added lemon, sugar … to improve the taste of the ORS so that it gets easier to drink (participant 28)’.

Storage practices

The storage of ORS in a hygienic manner painted a less favourable picture. Less than half (n=15) mothers stored the prepared ORS in the proper hygienic manner. One of the mothers commented, ‘I keep the prepared ORS in the same glass I mixed it in, covering it with a lid or a plate (participant 31)’. The majority (n=28) stored ORS for no longer than 12 hours.

Safety and continued feeding

We found that almost half (n=15) mothers continued the recommended feeding practices alongside providing ORS during their child’s illness, which is reflected in a mother’s comment, ‘I gave him banana, grape juice, but the problem worsened after giving these. After that, I didn't offer anything else (participant 05)’.

The majority (n=28) of mothers refrained from offering additional drinks or juices, which was reflected in a mother’s statement ‘In diarrhoea, if we drink fruit drinks, such as mango juice or any other type available in the market to address fluid loss, it won’t help; it will make things worse (participant 12)’. However, one mother responded, ‘We have given SMC Plus and other juices like Frutika and Sezan (local brands). I believe these juices are safe to give to the child when they are not sick … I think there might be no issues if I use them during diarrhoea also, as long as these juices are from reputable companies (participant 14)’.

When it came to providing the proper amount of ORS based on the child’s age, 23 mothers reported improper practice. One mother reported, ‘I gave the saline once in the morning, once in the afternoon, and once at night, administering 1–2 drops each time (participant 03)’.

Discussion

Bridging the gap in ORS coverage is essential to saving thousands of lives. Recognising the pivotal role mothers play in managing their children’s diarrhoeal illnesses through ORS, this qualitative study addressed the maternal KAP regarding the preparation and administration of ORS for under-5 children in Dhaka, Bangladesh. The findings highlight several key areas in which mothers demonstrated strong awareness and practices, as well as areas where gaps in knowledge and practices persist, providing valuable insights for health education and intervention strategies.

This study revealed that most mothers were aware of ORS as the treatment for diarrhoea, with more than one-third of them recognising its role in correcting dehydration. However, a knowledge gap still persists with mothers mentioning its use beyond the correction of dehydration. This indicates that while mothers generally understand the core use of ORS, there may be a need for more comprehensive education on its broad utility in combating diarrhoeal mortality and morbidity. In their study, Blum et al also found such a knowledge gap among Kenyan caregivers, as did Nuzhat et al among Bangladeshi women.32 36 They suggested that caregivers often prefer western medicines like antibiotics over ORS, which may be due to this gap in comprehension.

Regarding the preparation of ORS, the majority of mothers demonstrated knowledge of essential steps such as washing hands and utensils before preparation, using safe drinking water and adhering to the instructions on the sachet. These findings align with other studies that emphasise the importance of hygienic practices in the preparation of ORS to avoid contamination.37 However, a significant proportion of mothers still struggled with understanding the correct amount of ORS to use based on their child’s age. This finding indicates that despite widespread use, there is still a lack in the detailed knowledge of its preparation and administration.

Considering the administration of ORS, the majority of mothers endorsed continued ORS use even when their children were vomiting, which reflects a satisfactory comprehension and trust of mothers towards ORS’s life-saving role in diarrhoea. Around 70% of mothers have prioritised ORS over other drinks and juices to improve dehydration. A notable finding was that most mothers thought ORS could be transferred via breast milk to the child. There is a common belief that a mother’s dietary habits directly influence her baby’s health.38 39 While frequent breastfeeding is strongly encouraged, the misconception that breast milk alone can serve as a substitute for ORS may have adverse consequences for the child.40 This approach alone may fail to provide adequate salt and water replenishment, potentially exacerbating dehydration and leading to further complications. Conversely, the unnecessary consumption of ORS by mothers without medical indication may pose health risks to them.41

We observed that around 30% of mothers had the knowledge that incorrect preparation or administration of ORS can lead to potential health complications. This indicates a significant need for health education, emphasising the use of ORS as a medicine that should be prepared carefully and taken in the proper dosage.

A greater number of the participants displayed positive attitudes towards ORS use, particularly regarding its effectiveness in managing diarrhoea. Around 90% of mothers believed that ORS is more effective than antibiotics for treating dehydration due to diarrhoea. This perception may be rooted in the widespread promotion of ORS as a simple and accessible treatment, which is particularly important in resource-limited settings like Bangladesh. However, a small minority of mothers expressed scepticism about its benefits, suggesting that misconceptions about ORS and antibiotics may persist, particularly among those who may not have seen significant improvements in their child’s condition despite using ORS. In regard to the cost of ORS, although around 60% of mothers viewed it as affordable, the remainder were concerned about recent price increases. This finding highlights an ongoing challenge to ensure the continued accessibility of ORS for low-income households. This concern mirrors findings in other studies where cost is identified as a barrier to accessing essential healthcare products, including ORS.13 From the analysis, it was also evident that only 25% of mothers held a positive attitude towards learning about ORS and its utilisation. It can be hypothesised that mothers did not think they needed to learn about ORS preparation and administration, beyond what they already knew.

Our study found that while many mothers adhered to basic hygienic practices, such as using safe drinking water and proper utensils, there were areas where practices could be improved. For instance, more than 50% of the mothers mixed ORS incorrectly. A study in Bangladesh found that around 43% of participants lacked the proper knowledge regarding the amount to provide.20 The study by Nuzhat et al reported that only around 3% of the Bangladeshi mothers properly prepared ORS.32 This deficiency in awareness impacts the effective practice of ORS among mothers. Preparing ORS solution in smaller amounts with less sachet powder can potentially cause serious health consequences like electrolyte imbalance and death.42 This alarming finding needs more attention from policymakers and researchers as incorrect preparation not only causes serious health complications but also fails to attain the objective of ORS, which is the correction of dehydration. One potential countermeasure would be the development of ORS in smaller, paediatric-friendly packets, allowing for the preparation of reduced solution volumes, such as 150 or 200 mL, to better suit the needs of young children.

Several contextual factors appear to influence maternal practices regarding ORS preparation and administration. For example, limited formal education may prevent some mothers from reading instructions on ORS sachets or understanding the importance of measurement accuracy. As one mother noted, she did not read the packet because she believed only educated people do so. This reflects a broader literacy barrier that affects health behaviours. Cultural beliefs also shaped misconceptions, particularly regarding ORS passing through breast milk.28 Such intergenerational knowledge, while well intentioned, may delay direct administration to the child and reduce the solution’s effectiveness. Additionally, limited access to ORS at night or in emergency settings discouraged timely administration. All the findings related to discrepancies with proper practice suggest a need for more targeted health education on the correct preparation of ORS, emphasising the precise amounts of water and powder to avoid dilution or overconcentration. The study also assessed storage practices. More than 90% of mothers reported that ORS was stored in a hygienic manner. However, fewer mothers were aware that ORS should not be stored for more than 12 hours. Regarding complementary feeding and care, most mothers did not continue recommended feeding practices alongside ORS. A systematic review on harmful practices in diarrhoeal management indicates that caregivers frequently believe specific items can lead to indigestion or exacerbate the condition’s severity.43 This finding highlights the need for nutritional education for caregivers of children with diarrhoea. The government should consider using community health workers to educate mothers in local communities on the correct preparation and use of ORS. Furthermore, pictogram-based packaging and participatory workshops among local communities can improve the correct practice of ORS among caregivers.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Our study used a timely qualitative approach which preserved the depth and interpretive richness of qualitative methods. The findings can inform decisions, interventions and policy responses while a phenomenon is still unfolding. We also interviewed participants from the diarrhoeal hospital within a short timeframe after admission, which ensured proximity of the interview to the care event.

We did not use member checking in our study, which can be considered a limitation. However, this method only works when participants have sufficient education level and can work with the investigators. The participants were also mobile and hard to reach, which made it difficult to re-establish contact with them. We employed alternative strategies to ensure reliability and validity, which have been mentioned earlier. It is important to note that selection bias and social-desirability bias might limit the study’s findings also.

Conclusion

This study provides important insights into the KAP of mothers in Bangladesh regarding ORS use. While there is a high level of awareness about the role of ORS in treating dehydration caused by diarrhoea, significant gaps remain in terms of correct preparation, storage, administration and complementary feeding practices. Health education programmes should focus on reinforcing the proper preparation and storage of ORS, as well as addressing misconceptions about its use. Additionally, interventions aimed at reducing the cost of ORS and ensuring its consistent availability in both rural and urban areas are essential for maximising its impact in managing diarrhoea-related dehydration. By improving maternal knowledge and practices surrounding ORS, we can significantly reduce morbidity and mortality from diarrhoea, contributing to better child health outcomes in Bangladesh.
