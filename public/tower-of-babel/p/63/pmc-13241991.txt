Sugar rationing in the first 1000 days of life and risk of frailty: evidence from a natural experiment
Authors: Rui Zhang, Yuelan Gao, Hong Li, Yuhan Wei, Wanyang Zhong, Kai Tong, Zhibo Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the association between early-life sugar restriction and frailty in adulthood, and to evaluate whether frailty mediates the relationship between early-life sugar restriction and chronic diseases.DesignA population based quasi-experimental cohort study.Setting and participantsThis study was conducted using data from the UK Biobank. A total of 63,793 participants were included and classified according to early-life exposure to sugar restriction (never exposed, in utero only, in utero plus the first year postnatal, and in utero plus the first two postnatal years).MeasurementsFrailty was assessed in adulthood using Fried’s 5-item phenotype criteria (unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, slow walking speed, and low physical activity), with frailty defined as ≥ 3 components. Multivariable linear and logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations between early-life sugar restriction and frailty. Mediation analyses were conducted to assess the role of frailty in the associations between early-life sugar restriction and multiple chronic diseases.ResultsEarly-life sugar restriction was associated with lower frailty levels and reduced odds of frailty in adulthood, with stronger associations observed for longer exposure durations (P for trend <0.01). In mediation analyses, frailty partially mediated the associations between early-life sugar restriction and selected chronic diseases, including osteoporosis and heart failure, while minimal mediation effects were observed for metabolic diseases.ConclusionEarly-life sugar restriction is associated with a lower risk of frailty in adulthood. These findings highlight the potential long-term benefits of early-life nutritional environments on healthy aging and suggest that strategies targeting early-life diet and frailty prevention may help reduce the burden of age-related diseases in clinical and public health settings.Keywords: Early-life nutrition, Sugar restriction, Frailty, Chronic diseases, Life-course epidemiology

Introduction

Early-life nutrition plays a critical role in shaping long-term health trajectories [1,2]. Accumulating evidence from life-course epidemiology suggests that nutritional exposures during sensitive developmental windows can exert lasting effects on metabolic regulation, physiological resilience, and susceptibility to chronic diseases in adulthood [3,4]. Among these exposures, dietary sugar intake has attracted increasing attention due to its widespread consumption and its associations with insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, dyslipidemia, and increased risk of cardiometabolic disorders [[5], [6], [7]].

Historically, abrupt changes in population-level sugar availability have provided unique natural experiments to investigate the long-term health effects of early-life sugar exposure. The termination of sugar rationing in the United Kingdom in 1953 represents one such opportunity, enabling quasi-experimental comparisons between individuals exposed to low-sugar environments during early development and those who were not. Previous studies leveraging this historical context have linked early-life sugar restriction to lower risks of cardiometabolic disorders, reduced biological aging, and improved longevity [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. However, the biological pathways through which early-life sugar restriction translates into broad health benefits across the lifespan remain incompletely understood.

Frailty is characterized by cumulative declines across multiple physiological systems, including musculoskeletal, metabolic, immune, and cardiovascular domains [[13], [14], [15]]. Unlike single disease outcomes, frailty reflects reduced systemic resilience and impaired capacity to maintain homeostasis in response to stressors. Frailty is increasingly recognized not merely as a geriatric syndrome, but as a dynamic life-course construct influenced by early-life conditions and predictive of adverse health outcomes long before old age [16,17].

Growing evidence suggests that frailty is associated with a wide range of chronic diseases, including osteoporosis [18], type 2 diabetes [19,20], cardiovascular disease [21,22], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [23,24], and liver disease [25]. Frailty may therefore represent a shared biological pathway linking early-life exposures to diverse age-related conditions. From this perspective, early-life sugar restriction may confer long-term protection against chronic diseases in part by attenuating the accumulation of frailty over the life course.

Despite these insights, few studies have formally examined frailty as a potential mediator between early-life nutritional exposures and later-life disease risk [[26], [27], [28]]. It remains unclear whether the protective associations of early-life sugar restriction with multiple chronic diseases operate primarily through frailty-related pathways, or whether these effects are largely independent of frailty and driven by disease-specific mechanisms.

In this study, leveraging the natural experiment created by the end of sugar rationing in the United Kingdom, we aimed to investigate the relationship between early-life sugar restriction and frailty in adulthood, and to evaluate the mediating role of frailty in the associations between early-life sugar restriction and multiple chronic diseases. By integrating life-course epidemiology with mediation analysis across musculoskeletal, cardiometabolic, respiratory, and hepatic outcomes, our study seeks to clarify the role of frailty in adulthood as a central pathway linking early-life sugar exposure to long-term health.

Methods

Study population

We employed a quasi-experimental cohort design using data from the UK Biobank, which exploits a historical policy shift in sugar availability as a natural experiment. The termination of sugar rationing in September 1953 created an abrupt change in early-life nutritional conditions [8,29]. A total of 63,793 participants were included in the present study. Participants were assigned to exposure categories according to their date of birth, which determined the timing and duration of exposure to sugar-restricted conditions during early development. Exposure windows were defined to capture critical developmental periods, including the prenatal stage and the first two years after birth. Accordingly, participants were classified into four groups: individuals with no exposure to sugar rationing (n = 23,414), those exposed during the prenatal period only (n = 10,535), those exposed during the prenatal period and the first year after birth (n = 14,806), and those exposed during the prenatal period and the first two postnatal years (n = 15,038). A schematic overview of the exposure and group definitions is presented in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the Cohort Selection.

Frailty assessment

Frailty was assessed using Fried’s validated 5-item phenotype criteria (unintentional weight loss, self-reported exhaustion, grip strength, slow gait speed, and low physical activity) [13]. This phenotype is widely adopted in geriatric research for capturing physiological decline and has been validated within the UK Biobank [30,31]. Each frailty component was scored as 0 or 1, yielding a cumulative frailty score ranging from 0 to 5. Participants were categorized as non-frail (score = 0–2) or frail (score ≥3). For the primary analyses, frailty was treated as a binary variable (frail vs non-frail), the continuous frailty score was additionally used in sensitivity analyses. Detailed definitions of each frailty component are provided in Table S1.

Chronic diseases outcome assessment

Chronic diseases were considered as secondary outcomes for mediation analyses. Incident cases were identified during follow-up using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes from hospital inpatient records and primary care data [32,33]. The diseases assessed included osteoporosis, heart failure, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Follow-up time was calculated from the date of the participant’s first UK Biobank assessment to the earliest of the following events: diseases diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or the administrative censoring date of October 29, 2022. Specific codes are listed in Table S2.

Covariates

A comprehensive set of covariates was considered based on prior knowledge. Sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex, ethnicity (White vs. non-White), education level (college/university degree vs. others), and the Townsend deprivation index (TDI). Behavioral and anthropometric factors encompassed smoking status (current/former/never), alcohol consumption (current/non-current), and body mass index (BMI). Baseline comorbidities, including hypertension, coronary heart disease (CHD) were ascertained through self-report and linked medical records. These variables were included as covariates in the primary analyses because the main outcome of this study was frailty, and to control for potential confounding effects.

Calculation of the frailty polygenic risk score

We constructed a polygenic risk score (PRS) to capture genetic susceptibility to frailty [[34], [35], [36]]. The PRS was derived from previously published genome-wide association studies identifying single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with frailty-related traits in populations of European ancestry. Only independent SNPs reaching genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) and with a minor allele frequency greater than 0.05 were included in the score construction (Table S3). For each participant, the frailty PRS was calculated as the weighted sum of risk alleles, with weights corresponding to the effect sizes reported in the original studies. The resulting PRS was standardized and subsequently categorized into low, medium, and high genetic risk groups based on tertiles of the PRS distribution for downstream analyses.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study population were presented as mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous variables and as n (%) for categorical variables, stratified by frailty status (non-frail and frail).

To evaluate the association between early-life sugar restriction and frailty, linear regression models were used for continuous frailty score, and logistic regression models were applied for binary frailty status (frail vs. non-frail). The same adjustment strategy as in the Cox regression models was applied: Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for ethnicity, education level, birthplace, Townsend deprivation index, body mass index, smoking status, and alcohol intake.

Time-to-event analyses were primarily designed to evaluate the association between early-life sugar rationing and subsequent frailty risks. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Two progressively adjusted models were constructed: Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for ethnicity, education level, birthplace, Townsend deprivation index, body mass index, smoking status, and alcohol intake. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, with no significant violations detected (all P > 0.05).

To further explore the contribution of genetic susceptibility to frailty, we constructed a polygenic risk score (PRS) for frailty based on genome-wide significant variants. The PRS was analyzed as a continuous variable and additionally categorized into tertiles representing low, medium, and high genetic risk. We evaluated the independent association between frailty PRS and frailty phenotype, as well as the joint effects of early-life sugar rationing and genetic predisposition on frailty, by including multiplicative interaction terms and conducting stratified analyses.

To elucidate the role of frailty as a central biological pathway linking early-life sugar restriction to later-life disease susceptibility, we conducted formal mediation analyses with frailty as the key intermediate phenotype. Early-life sugar rationing during the first 1,000 days was specified as the exposure, frailty status as the mediator, and each age-related disease was analyzed separately as the outcome. Natural direct effects (NDE) and natural indirect effects (NIE) were estimated to decompose the total effect.

To ensure the robustness of our observational findings, we performed multiple sensitivity analyses: (1) physical activity level was additionally included in the fully adjusted models to account for potential confounding by adult lifestyle behaviors; it was not included in the primary models due to missing data; (2) models were further adjusted for major cardiometabolic conditions, including diabetes and hypertension, to minimize residual confounding related to underlying chronic diseases; and (3) excluding participants with missing information on key covariates. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sugar rationing and baseline characteristics

A total of 63,793 participants were included, of whom 23,414 were not exposed to sugar rationing in the first 1000 days after conception and 40,379 were exposed (Table 1). Participants in the rationed group were slightly older at baseline, while sex distribution was comparable between groups. Small but statistically significant differences were observed in the Townsend deprivation index and body mass index. The majority of participants were born in England and were of White ethnicity, with modest differences in ethnic composition between exposure groups. Birth month distribution differed significantly, reflecting the timing of exposure to sugar rationing. Lifestyle factors, including smoking and alcohol consumption, were largely similar between groups, as were physical activity levels and educational attainment. Maternal smoking around birth differed modestly between exposure categories. Significant differences were observed in the prevalence of frailty status between participants with and without prenatal exposure to sugar rationing.

Early-life sugar restriction and frailty index

We examined the association between early-life sugar restriction and frailty index in adulthood. In linear regression models adjusted for age and sex (Model 1), sugar restriction during the first 1,000 days of life was significantly associated with lower frailty levels (β = −0.20, 95% CI: −0.26 to −0.14, P < 0.01; Table 2; Fig. 2A). This association remained robust after further adjustment for socioeconomic factors, lifestyle behaviors, and comorbidities (β = −0.22, 95% CI: −0.29 to −0.09, P < 0.01).

We further explored the association according to the duration of early-life sugar restriction exposure. Exposure limited to the in utero period was significantly associated with lower frailty index in adulthood (β = −0.10, 95% CI: −0.22 to −0.02, P < 0.01). Longer exposure durations were associated with progressively greater reductions in frailty. Participants exposed during the in utero period plus the first year of life exhibited a stronger association (β = −0.26, 95% CI: −0.38 to −0.16, P < 0.01), while those exposed during the in utero period plus the first two years of life demonstrated the greatest reduction in frailty levels (β = −0.37, 95% CI: −0.54 to −0.13, P < 0.01). A clear dose–response relationship was indicated between the duration of early-life sugar restriction and frailty in adulthood (P for trend <0.01).

Early-life sugar restriction and frailty status

To further evaluate the association between early-life sugar restriction and frailty, participants were classified into frail and non-frail groups. Logistic regression analyses revealed a significant inverse association between prenatal exposure to sugar restriction and the risk of frailty in adulthood (Table S4; Fig. 2B).

In the model adjusted for age and sex, individuals exposed to early-life sugar restriction exhibited a significantly lower risk of frailty compared with those who were never exposed (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.75–0.92, P < 0.01). This association remained robust after further adjustment (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.76–0.93, P < 0.01). We further examined the association according to the duration of exposure to sugar restriction during early life. Compared with participants who were never exposed, those exposed only in utero showed a modest but significant reduction in frailty risk (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.84–0.99, P = 0.031). Stronger associations were observed with longer exposure durations. Participants exposed in utero plus up to 1 year after birth exhibited a further reduced risk of frailty (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.81–0.96, P < 0.01). The greatest risk reduction was observed among individuals exposed in utero plus up to 2 years after birth (OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.74–0.91, P < 0.01), indicating that longer exposure during critical developmental windows was associated with progressively lower odds of frailty in adulthood (P for trend <0.01).

Sex-stratified analyses of early-life sugar restriction and frailty

To explore potential sex differences in the association between early-life sugar restriction and frailty, sex-stratified analyses were performed separately in males and females (Table S5; Fig. 2C). Among females, early-life sugar restriction was significantly associated with lower frailty levels. In the model adjusted for age, prenatal exposure to sugar restriction was associated with reduced frailty (β = −0.18, 95% CI: −0.37 to −0.03, P < 0.01), and this association remained significant after further multivariable adjustment (β = −0.17, 95% CI: −0.31 to −0.09, P < 0.01). When stratified by duration of early-life sugar restriction, a clear dose–response relationship was observed among females. Compared with those never exposed to sugar rationing, in-utero exposure alone was associated with a modest reduction in frailty (β = −0.08, 95% CI: −0.14 to −0.01, P < 0.01), while stronger associations were observed with longer exposure durations, including in utero plus up to 1 year after birth (β = −0.15, 95% CI: −0.29 to −0.02, P < 0.05) and in utero plus up to 2 years after birth (β = −0.35, 95% CI: −0.50 to −0.04, P < 0.01).

In contrast, although the direction of the association was similar among males, the effect estimates were attenuated and did not consistently reach statistical significance in fully adjusted models. Among males, the corresponding associations across exposure durations showed similar directions but were weaker in magnitude and largely not statistically significant in fully adjusted models. A significant interaction by sex was observed (P for interaction = 0.003), indicating heterogeneity between females and males.

Joint effects of frailty genetic susceptibility and early-life sugar restriction

We next examined the joint effects of genetic susceptibility to frailty and early-life sugar restriction on the risk of frailty. The frailty PRS showed a strong and graded association with frailty, such that higher PRS was consistently associated with greater frailty risk (Tables S6–S7; Fig. 3A–B). Compared with individuals in the low-PRS group, those with medium PRS had a moderately increased risk of frailty (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.23–1.39, P < 0.01), whereas individuals in the high-PRS group exhibited a substantially elevated risk (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.48–1.71, P < 0.01), indicating a strong genetic contribution to frailty susceptibility.

To assess whether early-life sugar restriction modified this genetic predisposition, we evaluated their joint effects by classifying participants according to sugar rationing exposure and PRS tertiles, using individuals without sugar rationing and with low PRS as the reference group. Early-life sugar restriction was consistently associated with lower frailty risk compared with the corresponding non-rationed groups. Among participants with low genetic susceptibility, early-life sugar restriction was associated with a modest reduction in frailty risk (HR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.90–0.99, P = 0.042). Similar protective associations were observed among individuals with medium PRS, sugar-rationed participants exhibited a lower risk than non-rationed counterparts (HR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.08–1.17, P < 0.01). Among individuals with high genetic susceptibility to frailty, early-life sugar restriction partially attenuated the elevated frailty risk. Compared with the non-rationed high-PRS group (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.44–1.67, P < 0.01), those exposed to sugar restriction had a lower risk of frailty (HR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.36–1.54, P < 0.01).

Formal tests for interaction did not reveal a statistically significant multiplicative interaction between early-life sugar restriction and frailty PRS (P for interaction > 0.05; Table S8). This suggests that the protective association of early-life sugar restriction with frailty operates largely independently of genetic susceptibility and is consistently observed across different levels of frailty genetic risk.

Mediation role of frailty between sugar restriction and chronic diseases

To further elucidate the role of frailty in the long-term health effects of early-life sugar restriction, we conducted mediation analyses to quantify the extent to which frailty mediates the association between early-life sugar restriction and multiple chronic diseases (Table S9).

Early-life sugar restriction was associated with lower osteoporosis risk (HR = 0.7410, 95% CI 0.6048–0.8053; P < 0.01). Mediation analysis demonstrated a substantial contribution of frailty to this association, with a statistically significant natural indirect effect (HR = 0.9353, 95% CI 0.8153–0.9732; P < 0.01). Approximately 22.3% of the total protective effect was mediated through frailty. A moderate mediating role of frailty was also observed for heart failure. Early-life sugar restriction was associated with reduced risk of heart failure (total effect HR = 0.7346, 95% CI 0.5847–0.8538; P < 0.01), of which 12.8% was mediated through frailty (natural indirect effect HR = 0.9613, 95% CI 0.8432–0.9857; P < 0.01).

In contrast, frailty accounted for no significant proportion of the associations with type 2 diabetes, hypertension, COPD, and MASLD. Although early-life sugar restriction was significantly associated with reduced risks of these conditions. The mediating role of frailty appears strongest for musculoskeletal and cardiovascular outcomes, whereas other chronic diseases may be influenced predominantly through alternative metabolic or organ-specific mechanisms.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses (Table S10). First, we additionally adjusted for physical activity level to account for potential confounding by adult lifestyle behaviors. Second, we further adjusted for major cardiometabolic conditions, including diabetes and hypertension, to minimize confounding by underlying chronic diseases. We also performed sensitivity analyses by excluding participants with missing data on key covariates and by restricting the analysis to participants with longer follow-up durations. Across all sensitivity analyses, the associations between early-life sugar restriction and frailty risk remained consistent with the primary analyses, with no substantial changes in effect estimates or statistical significance. Overall, these sensitivity analyses support the robustness and stability of our main findings.

Discussion

In this large quasi-experimental cohort study leveraging a historical sugar rationing policy as a natural experiment, we provide novel life-course evidence that early-life sugar restriction is associated with a reduced risk of frailty in adulthood. We demonstrate that early nutritional environments may influence skeletal health decades later through long-term programming of physiological reserve and aging trajectories. Our findings align closely with the DOHaD hypothesis, which posits that nutritional exposures during the prenatal and early postnatal periods can exert lasting effects on organ structure, endocrine regulation, and disease susceptibility later in life [37].

In this study, we observed a clear dose–response relationship between the duration of early-life sugar restriction and frailty risk. Previous epidemiological studies have linked early-life under-nutrition or famine exposure to adverse skeletal outcomes; however, most of these contexts involved severe caloric deprivation [[13], [14], [15]]. In contrast, the sugar rationing policy examined here represents a more moderate and population-wide reduction in sugar intake, allowing us to isolate the effects of early sugar exposure independent of extreme malnutrition. Our findings therefore extend existing literature by suggesting that moderate restriction of refined sugar in early life may have long-term protective effects on ageing organ and tissue health [8].

From a biological standpoint, early sugar restriction may influence lifelong frailty trajectories through several mechanisms. Excessive sugar intake in early life has been associated with dysregulated glucose metabolism, oxidative stress, and epigenetic alterations affecting muscle, adipose tissue, and immune function [1,8,38]. Over decades, these processes may accelerate biological aging and erode physiological reserve, ultimately manifesting as frailty and chronic diseases [20]. Our findings provide empirical support for this integrated life-course model.

Another important finding is that early-life sugar restriction appeared to attenuate the adverse effects of genetic susceptibility to frailty [34,35]. Consistent with prior studies, higher frailty polygenic risk scores were strongly associated with increased frailty. However, across all genetic risk strata, individuals exposed to early-life sugar restriction exhibited lower risks compared with their non-rationed counterparts. Formal interaction tests did not indicate a statistically significant interaction between sugar restriction and frailty PRS, suggesting that the protective association of early-life sugar restriction operates broadly across different levels of genetic risk. This pattern is consistent with the concept of “developmental buffering,” whereby favorable early-life environments may partially offset inherited vulnerability to aging-related decline [39,40]. These findings underscore the importance of early environmental exposures in shaping lifelong resilience, even in the presence of genetic predisposition [41].

From a public health perspective, our findings have important implications. They suggest that nutritional policies targeting early-life sugar exposure may yield benefits that extend far beyond metabolic health, influencing frailty trajectories and chronic diseases development decades later [14]. While contemporary nutritional guidelines emphasize sugar reduction primarily to prevent obesity and diabetes, our results highlight potential long-term benefits for population aging.

Key strengths of this study include the use of a natural experiment design, a large and well-characterized cohort, long-term follow-up, and the integration of phenotypic frailty measures with genetic susceptibility [34,35]. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, exposure classification was based on birth timing rather than individual dietary intake, although this approach minimizes recall bias and reflects population-level exposure. Second, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded despite extensive adjustment and sensitivity analyses [42]. Third, frailty was assessed in adulthood and may not fully capture dynamic changes across the life course.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling life-course evidence that early-life sugar restriction is associated with a lower risk of frailty in adulthood and that this association partly mediating the risk of chronic diseases. Our findings highlighting frailty as a key biological pathway linking early nutritional environments to later-life health as a developmental and integrated model. These results underscore the long-term importance of early-life nutrition and suggest that interventions targeting frailty may help reduce the burden of chronic diseases.
