Effect of wheat straw protein hydrogel on the mechanical and compressibility behavior of dispersive soil
Authors: Ali Nisari Tabrizi, Mahzad Esmaeili-Falak, Mohammadreza Rasouli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDispersive soils pose serious challenges in geotechnical engineering due to their high susceptibility to internal erosion upon contact with water. This study investigates a sustainable bio-stabilization approach using a natural hydrogel synthesized from gluten protein extracted from wheat straw, combined with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and boric acid as cross-linking agents. The hydrogel was incorporated into dispersive ML soil collected from the Jafariyeh region of Qom, Iran, at dosages of 1%, 3%, 5%, and 7% by dry soil weight. All specimens were compacted at optimum moisture content and maximum dry density determined in accordance with ASTM D698, cured for 7, 14, and 28 days, and evaluated through geotechnical index tests, dispersivity assessments (double hydrometer and total dissolved solids), mechanical tests (unconfined compressive strength, direct shear, and California Bearing Ratio under soaked and unsoaked conditions), mineralogical analysis (XRD), and baseline consolidation testing on untreated soil. The untreated soil exhibited a high degree of dispersion (63–79%), classifying it as critically dispersive. XRD analysis identified Quartz, Albite, Calcite, and Bassanite as dominant minerals, with higher carbonate–sulfate contents associated with increased dispersivity due to pore-water ionic imbalance and reduced interparticle attraction. Hydrogel treatment reduced dispersivity to below 30% for dosages up to 5%, while a rebound to 39% was observed at 7%. Unconfined compressive strength increased from 0.80 MPa in untreated soil to 1.60 MPa at a 5% dosage after 28 days of curing, and the internal friction angle exceeded 31° in treated samples. The highest CBR improvement (up to 50%) was achieved at 3%. While 3% hydrogel provided the most efficient strength-driven performance, a multi-criteria decision analysis (TOPSIS) identified 5% as the overall optimal dosage by balancing mechanical improvement and dispersivity control.Keywords: Dispersive soil, Biostabilization, Wheat straw, Gluten hydrogel, Unconfined compressive strength, Direct shear, CBR, Sustainable geotechnicsSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Dispersive soils are among the most enduring geotechnical issues due to their unique physico-chemical characteristics1–3. Unlike surface erosion, dispersity is a molecular and internal-scale phenomenon in which repulsive forces between clay particles outstrip attractive forces, leading to detachment and mobilization of fine particles even under relatively minor hydraulic gradients4. This impact is exceptionally common in highly exchangeable sodium soils, which extend the thickness of diffuse double layers surrounding clay platelets and reduce interparticle attraction5,6. The engineering consequences are severe: dispersive soils are susceptible to internal erosion, piping, and failure of earth dams, canal linings, embankments, and roadbeds7,8. Historical failures demonstrate how piping progresses unnoticed until it results in calamitous collapse9,10.

To counteract dispersivity, conventional methods have greatly relied upon the application of chemical stabilizers such as lime, cement, and gypsum that cause flocculation by altering the soil chemistry11,12. While such additives can reduce dispersivity and increase shear strength, their downsides are high energy consumption, undue carbon effect, and potential long-term leaching of by-product13–15. Physical reinforcement strategies, such as the incorporation of synthetic or natural fibers, have also been investigated. For example, palm fibers, rice husk, and corn silk were discovered to enhance ductility, reduce brittleness, and enhance shear strength of soils at various levels of stresses16–18. Nonetheless, their performance is typically limited compared to the combined chemical and hydraulic deficiencies of dispersive soils19,20. This has triggered a shift towards bio-stabilization techniques, whereby the application of natural hydrogels and polymers is employed to achieve both environmental compatibility and geotechnical performance1,19,21,22. Hydrogels are particularly hydrophilic, three-dimensional polymer networks that can absorb and retain huge amounts of water and, in doing so, regulate soil moisture, enhance aggregation, and reduce internal erosion susceptibility23–25. Renewable-resource-based bio-based hydrogels are compared with acrylate synthetic hydrogels as having the benefit of biodegradability and regional adaptability, promising alternatives to widespread uses26–29. Dispersive soils are not only vulnerable to internal erosion and piping upon water exposure, but they may also exhibit collapse-type compressibility under loading, particularly when wetting and chemical instability weaken interparticle bonding and trigger rapid fabric rearrangement30,31. The resulting unstable, open structure can undergo abrupt void ratio reduction and excessive settlement when subjected to stress increments, making dispersive soils problematic from both strength and serviceability perspectives32. Recent studies on dispersive clays treated with sustainable additives have similarly reported that mitigating dispersion is often accompanied by improved compressibility behavior and reduced settlement potential, highlighting the coupled nature of chemical stability and consolidation response33,34. Accordingly, one-dimensional consolidation testing is included in this study to provide baseline insight into the collapse-prone compressibility of the untreated dispersive soil and to better justify the need for stabilization beyond dispersivity identification and strength improvement34,35.

Soil stabilization through bio-based hydrogels and biopolymers is through multiple mechanisms operating at multiple scales. At the microstructure scale, polymer chains adsorb to soil particles through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interaction, and van der Waals forces, to form interparticle bridges that increase soil cohesion21,36,37. Experimental research illustrates dramatic enhancements in shear strength: for example, xanthan gum and guar gum have been shown to enhance cohesion by several orders of magnitude after weeks of curing3,38,39. Casein and sodium caseinate, in tests with sandy soils, have improved both cohesion and friction angle, whereas guar gum was seen to maximize effective cohesion at concentrations approaching 0.5% by weight40. These experiments indicate how biopolymers can influence soil texture and curing duration-related strength gain40,41. Hydraulically, hydrogels are like moisture reservoirs: by absorbing water during wet conditions and releasing water slowly during drying conditions, they reduce pore water pressure fluctuations and prevent crack growth during cyclic wetting–drying42,43. Recent studies have demonstrated that sustainable industrial by-products, including plaster of Paris kiln dust, fine marble dust, and potassium-rich waste materials, can effectively reduce dispersibility, piping, and internal erosion in sodium-rich clays by modifying pore-water chemistry and suppressing sodium-induced repulsive forces. These approaches provide environmentally responsible stabilization strategies for the construction industry while improving soil durability and fabric stability31. Also, other studies have emphasized sustainable soil improvement approaches, including the valorization of biomass residues, recycling of polymer-based wastes, and the use of geosynthetics to enhance mechanical performance, compaction behavior, and slope stability across soils with varying plasticity. These developments highlight the growing integration of environmental sustainability, geotechnical performance, and circular economy principles in modern soil stabilization practices44. Recent developments in environmental geotechnics emphasize the transition toward a green and circular economy framework, promoting the reuse of industrial by-products, excavated materials, and waste-derived additives in ground improvement applications45. Such approaches aim not only to enhance engineering performance but also to reduce environmental footprint and carbon emissions associated with conventional cement- and lime-based stabilization. Within this context, bio-based and residue-derived stabilizers represent promising alternatives aligned with sustainable construction principles46.

The utilization of crop waste to soil stabilization resonates with the sustainability and circular economy. Wheat straw, the most prevalent crop residue in the world, is most often underutilized or readily burned with environmental effects such as air pollution and emission of greenhouse gases47. Current studies recognized the potential of using wheat straw-based gluten protein to form hydrogels when cross-linked with substances such as boric acid and blended with polyvinyl alcohol13,48. The hydrogels strengthen soil structure by means of interparticle adhesion but are still biodegradable and environmentally friendly49,50. Interestingly, tests have shown that wheat straw protein hydrogels have no considerable influence on soil pH or produce toxic leachates, but enhance mechanical properties such as UCS and CBR51. Such performance alongside economic and logistic appropriateness of local production in agricultural regions renders wheat straw an extremely coveted raw material52.

Although the efficacy of biopolymers and hydrogels has been demonstrated in different environments, there are knowledge gaps that are significant. While the efficacy of biopolymers and hydrogels has been demonstrated in different environments, existing studies exhibit several important limitations. Most prior research has focused on synthetic hydrogels or a limited number of polysaccharide-based biopolymers (e.g., xanthan and guar), with comparatively fewer investigations addressing protein-based hydrogels derived from agricultural residues such as wheat straw2,39,40. Moreover, reported improvements in mechanical properties are often not systematically linked to dispersivity characterization and pore-water chemistry (e.g., TDS, SAR, and EC), particularly under combined laboratory and field-representative conditions51,53. A second constraint is the absence of systematic approaches for determining the ideal stabilizer dosage; most research is conducted based on single-parameter instead of multi-criteria evaluation. Accordingly, the main objectives of this study are to: I)evaluate the effectiveness of a wheat straw protein–based hydrogel in reducing soil dispersivity, II)quantify the improvement in mechanical performance (UCS, shear strength, and CBR) under different hydrogel dosages and curing periods, III)investigate the microstructural modifications induced by hydrogel treatment, and IV)identify the optimal hydrogel dosage using a multi-criteria decision-making approach (TOPSIS) that jointly considers strength and dispersivity. Recent studies on waste-derived soil modifiers have also demonstrated non-linear mechanical responses with increasing additive content, where strength initially improves and subsequently declines beyond an optimal dosage due to changes in density, stiffness, and particle interaction mechanisms54.

To address these deficiencies, this paper develops a novel hydrogel from wheat straw gluten protein, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and boric acid as a cross-linking agent, and investigates its stability to stabilize dispersive soil in the Jafariyeh district of Qom, Iran. A variety of tests—double hydrometer, TDS, UCS, direct shear, consolidation, and CBR (soaked/unsoaked)—were carried out on 1%, 3%, 5%, and 7% hydrogel-by-weight-mixed samples. Laboratory results indicated significant increases in shear strength, compressive strength, and bearing capacity with reductions in dispersivity and permeability. From an environmental perspective, the proposed stabilization approach contributes to sustainability by valorizing wheat straw, an abundant agricultural residue, into a functional soil stabilizer. Unlike conventional additives such as lime and cement, which are associated with high energy consumption and carbon emissions, the bio-based hydrogel offers a low-carbon and biodegradable alternative. The use of renewable raw materials and waste-derived resources aligns with circular economy principles by reducing agricultural waste disposal and promoting environmentally responsible geotechnical practices. Finally, multi-criteria decision-making by using the TOPSIS method was also applied to select the optimum dosage, and the findings revealed that the 5% blend possessed the best balance between mechanical strength and chemical stability. By this comprehensive approach not only is hydrogel-soil interaction enriched but also wheat straw protein hydrogel is identified as a low-cost, green alternative to traditional stabilizers such as lime and cement. Materials

This subsection includes the experimental design used to evaluate the effect of gluten-based hydrogel on geotechnical properties of dispersive soils. The strategy was set up to simulate realistic field conditions and provide under controlled laboratory conditions to allow for replicability and precision. The procedures consist of the choice of a location and soil sampling in a representative area of dispersive soil, extraction and synthesis of a bio-based stabilizer, and conditioning of treated and untreated soil samples with controlled compaction and curing regimes. A comprehensive set of laboratory tests was conducted, including physical characterization, dispersivity testing, and mechanical performance. All procedures were carried out following standardized ASTM procedures.

Study area and soil sampling

This research was conducted on soil samples collected from the Qom Province’s Jafariyeh plain (Saveh Aquifer) which is part of the Saveh Aquifer system, a region characterized by fine-grained dispersive soils and groundwater-related geotechnical challenges that is characterized by semi-arid climatic conditions, heavy seasonal variation in moisture, and fine-grained soils that are vulnerable to internal erosion. The study location, as indicated in Fig. 2, is positioned near residential and infrastructural development zones, thereby making it a highly suitable location for assessing geotechnical risks in engineering practice.

Geographical location of the study site.

Field observations revealed clear manifestations of dispersive behavior. Surface-dipping sinkholes, cracks, and piping channels were easily observed across large areas of the site (Fig. 3). These findings in conjunction with earlier reports validated the vulnerability of the site to water-induced degradation and posed it as an effective test case for evaluating bio-stabilization techniques under field representative conditions.

Visible signs of surface erosion and piping in the study area.

To account for spatial heterogeneity, three points were selected in triangular configuration, roughly 500 m apart. At a depth of 0.5 m below the surface, disturbed samples of soil were collected, targeting the active structural layer but not affected by the topsoil. For consolidation and direct shear testing, carefully hand-cut cubical block samples were extracted with the aim of minimizing disturbance and preserving relative soil fabric and moisture condition. While complete preservation of in-situ structure in fine-grained dispersive soils is inherently difficult, this approach provides a closer representation than fully remolded specimens. As seen in Fig. 4, such a process entailed careful wrapping and confinement of the soil block with sealing materials to preserve its in-place condition and water status. The sample was subsequently transported to the laboratory in rigid protective containers to ensure the integrity of subsequent mechanical tests.

Preparation of gluten-based hydrogel stabilizer

The preparation of the gluten-based hydrogel stabilizer was developed based on previously reported protein-derived and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)–based hydrogel systems. Protein extraction from agricultural residues such as wheat straw has been shown to be feasible through thermal and aqueous pretreatment, enabling the release of gluten-rich fractions suitable for polymer network formation55.Furthermore, PVA–boric acid cross-linking is a well-established approach for producing physically cross-linked hydrogels, in which borate ions form reversible di-diol bonds with hydroxyl groups on PVA and protein chains, resulting in a three-dimensional network with enhanced mechanical stability and water retention capacity56.Similar formulations have been successfully applied in soil and biomaterial applications to improve cohesion, reduce permeability, and enhance durability57. In the present study, this established framework was adapted and optimized for dispersive soil stabilization by combining wheat straw-derived gluten protein with PVA and boric acid, ensuring both environmental compatibility and effective soil–polymer interaction. According to Table 1 that detailed physicochemical characterization of the synthesized hydrogel (such as FTIR, rheological properties, or swelling capacity) was beyond the scope of this study. The hydrogel formulation was adapted from previously reported protein-based and PVA–boric acid cross-linked systems, whose chemical structure and gelation behavior have been extensively characterized in the literature. Therefore, the present research focused on evaluating the hydrogel’s effectiveness at the soil scale through mechanical performance, dispersivity reduction, and microstructural observations rather than polymer-level material development. Although fiber reinforcement has been widely used to improve soil strength and ductility, its effectiveness in dispersive soils is often limited to mechanical improvement and does not directly address chemical instability or clay dispersion in the presence of water. Fibers mainly act through tensile resistance and crack-bridging mechanisms, with minimal influence on soil–water interactions. In contrast, hydrogel–protein systems provide a coupled stabilization mechanism by forming hydrogen-bonded networks that enhance interparticle bonding, regulate moisture movement, and reduce dispersivity. Therefore, a gluten-based hydrogel was selected in this study to simultaneously improve mechanical performance and mitigate dispersive behavior, which cannot be effectively achieved by fiber reinforcement alone. In this study, a biodegradable hydrogel was derived from wheat straw to be utilized as a bio-based soil stabilizer. The two main stages were the extraction of gluten protein from raw agro-residue and the hydrogel composite preparation by mixing the obtained protein with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and boric acid as a crosslinking agent.

As shown in Fig. 5, initially wheat straw was air-dried and pulverized into fine powder. In the hydrogel preparation process, air-dried and pulverized wheat straw was mixed with distilled water at a solid–liquid ratio of approximately 1:10 (by weight) and heated to about 60 °C to facilitate protein extraction. After mechanical stirring for 30–60 min, the protein-rich suspension was filtered and oven-dried to obtain gluten powder. For hydrogel synthesis, a 15% (w/v) aqueous the PVA solution was completely dissolved at 80 °C, into which the gluten powder was gradually added at a gluten-to-PVA mass ratio of approximately 1:2 under continuous stirring. Boric acid was then introduced at about 2–3% by weight of PVA to induce physical cross-linking and form a three-dimensional hydrogel network. The resulting hydrogel had a paste-like consistency with high water content (approximately 80–85%) and was stored in airtight containers prior to soil mixing.

illustrates the laboratory workflow of gluten extraction and hydrogel formulation.

To provide basic physicochemical characterization of the synthesized hydrogel, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed, and the resulting spectrum is presented in Fig. 6. A broad absorption band centered at approximately 3320.93 cm⁻¹ is attributed to O–H and/or N–H stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of hydroxyl and protein-related functional groups. The band observed at 2928.24 cm⁻¹ corresponds to aliphatic C–H stretching. The strong peaks at 1659.45 and 1532.54 cm⁻¹ are assigned to amide-related vibrations, which are consistent with the proteinaceous nature of the wheat straw-derived component. Additional bands at 1447.36, 1239.67, 1156.19, 1081.25, and 1028.28 cm⁻¹ are associated with C–H bending and C–O/C–O–C stretching vibrations, reflecting the contribution of the polymeric matrix and possible interactions within the hydrogel network. The peak around 927.18 cm⁻¹ may be related to borate-associated bonding or out-of-plane vibrations within the crosslinked structure. The small signal near 2353.48 cm⁻¹ is attributed to atmospheric CO₂. Overall, the FTIR spectrum confirms the presence of the main functional groups expected in the wheat straw protein–PVA–boric acid hydrogel and supports the formation of an interactive polymeric network suitable for soil stabilization applications.

Soil-hydrogel mixing and specimen preparation

Four hydrogel dosages (1%, 3%, 5%, and 7% by dry soil weight) were selected based on preliminary trials and the expected non-linear response of hydrogel-stabilized soils. Dosages below 1% were found to be ineffective, as the limited amount of hydrogel was insufficient to form a continuous polymer network or establish meaningful interparticle bonding. In contrast, hydrogel contents exceeding 7% led to excessive moisture retention, oversaturation of the soil matrix, and difficulties in compaction, resulting in loss of structural coherence. Therefore, the upper limit was constrained to 7%. Non-linear dosage increments were intentionally adopted to efficiently capture threshold behavior at low contents and potential saturation effects at higher contents, which are commonly reported in hydrogel- and biopolymer-based soil stabilization studies. This approach allows identification of an optimal dosage range without unnecessary intermediate testing. A control group with no additive (0%) was also prepared for baseline comparison. Each batch of soil was first oven-dried at 105 °C for 24 h to eliminate existing moisture. The dry soil was then thoroughly mixed with the pre-synthesized hydrogel, followed by the addition of water to reach the optimum moisture content as determined by standard Proctor tests. Uniform mixing was ensured either manually or with a mechanical mixer to achieve consistent distribution of the hydrogel throughout the soil matrix. The prepared mixture was compacted into standard cylindrical molds in two layers, with each layer tamped using a Proctor hammer to achieve maximum dry density. Immediately after compaction, the specimens were sealed with plastic wrap to minimize moisture loss and cured at ambient temperature for 7, 14, and 28 days depending on the test schedule.

Methodology (Testing procedure)

To assess the influence of the gluten-based hydrogel on dispersive soil’s physical and mechanical properties, a series of laboratory tests were conducted under normal conditions and as per relevant ASTM standards. The test program was planned in three main categories: geotechnical index tests, dispersivity tests, and mechanical performance tests. Geotechnical index tests were conducted to establish baseline soil properties. Grain size distribution was determined by combined sieve and hydrometer testing (ASTM D6913 and D7928), thereby determining the classification of the soil as fine-grained with high silt and clay content. Atterberg limits like liquid limit and plastic limit were determined using ASTM D4318, and the results determined the presence of low plasticity behavior, classifying the soil in the ML group (low-plasticity silt). Soil particle specific gravity was measured with a pycnometer test (ASTM D854) and natural moisture content was measured by drying representative samples in an oven for 24 h at 105 °C according to ASTM D2216. These tests provided key input parameters for stabilization performance design and analysis. All specimens were cured under controlled laboratory conditions to ensure consistent development of the hydrogel–soil matrix. After compaction at optimum moisture content and maximum dry density (ASTM D698), the samples were sealed to prevent moisture loss and stored at an ambient temperature of approximately 20–25 °C. Curing periods of 7, 14, and 28 days were adopted to evaluate time-dependent strength evolution. Maintaining stable temperature and moisture conditions was essential to allow proper hydrogel swelling, interparticle adsorption, and progressive network formation within the soil matrix. Variations in curing duration significantly influenced mechanical behavior, as longer curing periods promoted partial dehydration and structural rearrangement of the hydrogel phase, leading to enhanced particle bonding, fabric densification, and improved load-transfer capacity. Therefore, strength development in this study reflects not only hydrogel dosage but also curing-controlled microstructural evolution under stable environmental conditions.

To determine the dispersivity of the soil, two lab tests were selected as being significant. Double hydrometer test (ASTM D4221) was applied to quantify the percentage of suspended clay-sized particles under both non-dispersed and dispersed states. This way, direct determination of the potential of the soil to disperse when exposed to water was made possible. In addition to that, total dissolved solids (TDS) were measured through electrical conductivity of soil-water extracts (ASTM D1125 and D5903). Larger values of TDS in untreated samples represented the quantity of soluble salts accountable for dispersivity, while smaller values in treated specimens illustrated improved chemical stability upon addition of hydrogel. Also, analysis by X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed in order to determine the mineralogical composition of the representative soil samples. The samples were taken from three samples selected from the study area and analyzed using Cu–Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA within the scan interval of 2θ = 5–80°. Phase identification was carried out by comparison with standard PDF reference data. The major crystalline minerals were labeled as Quartz (Q), Albite (A), Calcite (C), and Bassanite (B), and these major peaks in the diffractograms were labeled likewise for convenience in the results section.

Mechanical tests were designed to determine strength, deformation, and load-carrying capacity of the untreated and treated soils. Unconfined compressive strength (UCS) tests were conducted as per ASTM D2166, in which cylindrical specimens with curing times of 7, 14, and 28 days were tested. The results showed notable strength improvement with increasing hydrogel dose, with a significant effect at 3%. Standard Proctor compaction tests (ASTM D698) were utilized to determine the optimum moisture content and maximum dry density, for the preparation of all the specimens. Finally, the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test (ASTM D1883) was utilized to evaluate the subgrade strength under soaked and unsoaked conditions. The 3% hydrogel mix consistently exhibited the greatest CBR values, which indicated the effectiveness of the stabilizer in increasing load-bearing capacity. Because dispersive soils cannot be reliably identified using conventional classification methods such as Atterberg limits or grain size distribution, specialized tests are required to evaluate their behavior in the presence of water58. Commonly used methods include the double hydrometer test (ASTM D4221), pinhole test (ASTM D4647), crumb test (ASTM D6572), and complementary chemical indicators such as electrical conductivity (EC), sodium adsorption ratio (SAR), exchangeable sodium percentage (ESP), and total dissolved solids (TDS). These physical and chemical tests are widely employed to characterize clay dispersion potential and provide a reliable basis for identifying dispersive behavior6. In engineering practice, dispersivity assessment is often combined with mechanical and durability testing—such as unconfined compressive strength (UCS), California Bearing Ratio (CBR), and shear strength tests—to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of soil stabilization measures and their implications for structural performance8,53. For each mechanical test (UCS, direct shear, and CBR), a minimum of three replicate specimens were tested for each hydrogel dosage and curing period. Reported values represent the average of the replicate measurements.

To evaluate compressibility and shear behavior, block samples extracted by careful hand cutting were used. These specimens are not claimed to be perfectly undisturbed but were prepared to reduce disturbance relative to remolded samples, allowing qualitative assessment of consolidation and shear response. This involved pushing cutting rings and shear box molds directly into trimmed blocks’ exposed surfaces, thereby allowing specimens to maintain their natural structure and moisture condition. These undisturbed test samples played important roles in one-dimensional consolidation tests (ASTM D2435) and direct shear tests (ASTM D3080), as disturbance at any point could alter soil behavior and yield unsuitable results. Preservation of layering and fine pore structure was extremely essential in order to quantify realistic consolidation and shear parameters. Left panel shows a square mold from direct shear test pressed into the soil block, while the right panel shows a cylindrical sample cut for consolidation test. Both samples were handled with extreme care to prevent drying, cracking, and deformation before testing. Results and discussion

Geotechnical index properties of dispersive soil

The native soil samples were classified as ML (low-plasticity silt) according to the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS), based on particle size distribution and Atterberg limits. As shown in Table 3, fines content ranged from 71.79% to 87.64%, while sand content varied between 12.36% and 28.21%. The liquid limit (LL) was nearly constant across samples (20.11%–20.18%), and the plasticity index (PI) remained low (4.66–5.41%), indicating limited plastic behavior. The specific gravity (Gs) varied slightly between 2.67 and 2.78, while natural water content ranged from 3.59% to 7.24%. Maximum dry densities were measured between 1.71 and 1.75 g/cm³, and optimum moisture content values ranged from 20.50% to 26.31%. These geotechnical characteristics confirm the fine-grained, moisture-sensitive nature of the soil and support the necessity of stabilization interventions.

Dispersivity behavior of untreated and treated soils

The dispersivity of the native soil was quantitatively evaluated using the double hydrometer test (ASTM D4221) and TDS-based chemical analysis. In addition, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was employed as a complementary approach to interpret dispersivity indirectly through mineralogical composition, particularly the presence of carbonate–sulfate minerals associated with chemical instability. The dispersivity of the native soil was evaluated using the double hydrometer test, TDS-based sodium percentage analysis and XRD analysis. As shown in Table 4, the untreated field samples exhibited highly dispersive behavior, with dispersion degrees ranging from 63% to 79%, classifying all three as critically dispersive according to ASTM D4221. The dosage-dependent dispersivity response observed in this study is consistent with recent biopolymer stabilization findings. For example, Xu et al.59 reported that guar gum contents above approximately 1.5% were effective in eliminating soil dispersion, with the best overall mechanical performance at about 3% after curing. Similarly, the present wheat-straw protein hydrogel reduced dispersion below 30% for dosages up to 5%, while excessive dosage (7%) resulted in a rebound in dispersivity, indicating that biopolymer-type stabilizers can exhibit an optimum range beyond which hydraulic and fabric-related trade-offs emerge.

As presented in Table 5, hydrogel contents of 1%, 3%, and 5% lowered the dispersion degree below 30%, moving the classification towards the non-dispersive region. On the other hand, at a dosage of 7%, the dispersion degree increased once more to 39%, moving the sample towards the intermediate region due to oversaturation effects associated with excess hydrogel content, which increases water retention and reduces effective particle–particle contact. The presence of a continuous, water-rich polymer phase at higher dosages may facilitate particle separation under hydraulic loading, leading to increased dispersivity despite higher strength values. These results confirm the efficacy of the hydrogel against physical and chemical forces of soil dispersion, with the optimal dose discovered to be 3%. The observed reduction in dispersivity can be attributed to several coupled mechanisms. The hydrogel appears to form a hydrophilic polymer network around and between soil particles, which enhances interparticle adhesion and limits the detachment of fine particles under hydraulic action. In addition, partial pore filling and improved aggregation reduce the susceptibility of the soil fabric to internal erosion. This mechanism differs from conventional mineral stabilizers, which mainly act through ionic exchange or cementation, whereas the present hydrogel-based system appears to combine particle bridging, moisture regulation, and fabric stabilization. The increase in dispersivity at 7% dosage further suggests that excessive hydrogel content may create an over-saturated, water-rich phase that weakens effective particle–particle contact and partially offsets the stabilization benefit.

Mechanical strength enhancement using bio-hydrogel

The results of the direct shear tests, presented in Fig. 9a and b, demonstrate the significant influence of the wheat straw-derived protein hydrogel on enhancing soil shear strength. Figure 10a shows the variation in peak shear strength across different hydrogel dosages (1% to 7%) at four curing durations (3, 7, 14, and 28 days). The data clearly indicate a consistent increase in shear strength with both increasing hydrogel content and curing time, which can be attributed to progressive interparticle bonding and pore-filling effects induced by the gluten-based hydrogel. At early curing stages, strength gain is mainly associated with rapid adsorption of hydrogel chains onto soil particle surfaces, enhancing apparent cohesion. With extended curing time, partial dehydration and restructuring of the hydrogel network lead to stronger particle bridging and a denser soil fabric, resulting in sustained shear strength improvement. The highest strength was recorded at 7% hydrogel after 28 days of curing, reaching approximately 3.35 MPa, which marks a substantial improvement compared to the lowest strength observed in the 1% mix after just 3 days. Figure 10b supports this trend, illustrating the development of shear strength over time for each dosage level. Strength increased progressively with curing time for all hydrogel dosages, confirming that curing conditions strongly govern shear strength development. While higher hydrogel contents accelerated early-age strength gain, extended curing (up to 28 days) was essential to achieve stable and sustained shear resistance across all treated specimens. These results confirm that the addition of gluten-based hydrogel accelerates strength gain not only by increasing initial interparticle adhesion, but also through curing-dependent evolution of the hydrogel matrix. Over time, the hydrogel transitions into a more rigid and interconnected structure, which reinforces load transfer between soil particles and explains the continuous increase in UCS with curing duration, as also reported for other biopolymer-treated soils. Accordingly, a hydrogel content of 3% combined with a 28-day curing period can be considered mechanically optimal, highlighting that sufficient curing time is a prerequisite for mobilizing the full stabilizing potential of the hydrogel. From a strength-driven perspective, the 3% dosage represents an efficient stabilization level, beyond which additional hydrogel content yields diminishing mechanical benefits. The observed strength development with curing time aligns with mechanisms reported for other biopolymer-treated dispersive soils. Xu et al.59 attributed curing-related strength gain to progressive particle wrapping, hydrogel bridging, and void filling, which densify the fabric and enhance load transfer. Moreover, the diminishing efficiency observed beyond the 3% dosage in the present study is consistent with saturation-type behavior reported in other material systems, where excessive additive content provides limited additional strength improvement relative to the added material. A similar mechanism is inferred in the present study. The wheat straw protein hydrogel likely improves strength by forming interparticle bridges that increase apparent cohesion and facilitate stress transfer across the soil skeleton. With curing time, partial dehydration and restructuring of the hydrogel network appear to produce a denser and more interconnected fabric, which explains the sustained increase in shear strength and UCS. This interpretation is also supported by the SEM observations, which show enhanced aggregation, particle linkage, and pore filling in treated specimens. Therefore, the improvement in mechanical performance is not merely a consequence of additive inclusion, but rather the result of progressive microstructural stabilization within the soil matrix.

To evaluate the shear behavior of the dispersive soil, direct shear tests were conducted using undisturbed samples extracted from the prepared soil blocks. As shown in Fig. 11a, the shear strength of the untreated reference soil increased with higher applied normal stress, indicating a typical stress-dependent strength response. Based on the regression analysis illustrated in Fig. 11b, the internal friction angle (φ) and cohesion (c) were determined to be 26° and 13.58 kPa, respectively.

Based on the results of direct shear tests conducted on stabilized soil specimens with varying percentages of wheat straw protein hydrogel, a clear trend in shear strength improvement was observed. The stress–displacement and shear stress–normal stress relationships indicated a notable increase in shear strength parameters, including internal friction angle (φ) and cohesion (c), with increasing hydrogel content. As illustrated in Fig. 12, the increase in shear strength was most significant up to the 3% dosage, beyond which the rate of improvement diminished, indicating a saturation effect commonly reported in biopolymer-stabilized soils. Similar non-linear trends have been observed in previous studies on xanthan gum and protein-based stabilizers, where excessive polymer content led to reduced efficiency due to oversaturation and loss of effective particle contact. This saturation behavior suggests that beyond an optimum content, excess hydrogel does not further enhance particle bonding but instead introduces a more compliant polymer phase with higher water affinity, limiting additional stiffness gain. The values corresponding to 5% and 7% dosages showed minimal difference, suggesting that higher additive contents may lead to performance saturation. Therefore, under a 28-day curing period, 3% hydrogel can be identified as the mechanically optimal dosage, as it provides the most efficient strength improvement based on individual UCS and shear strength results. It should be noted that consolidation testing in this study was limited to untreated dispersive soil specimens and was intended to qualitatively characterize their inherent compressibility and collapse-prone structure. As a result, key compressibility parameters such as the compression index (Cc), recompression index (Cr), and yield stress were not explicitly derived, nor were quantitative comparisons made between treated and untreated soils. Consolidation results are therefore discussed in a baseline and qualitative context, while the influence of hydrogel treatment on soil behavior is primarily evaluated through dispersivity reduction, mechanical performance, and microstructural observations. The observed reduction in dispersivity and improvement in mechanical performance are consistent with recent studies that employed sustainable stabilizers derived from biomass residues, industrial by-products, and potassium-rich materials31.

Consolidation behavior

The void ratio–pressure curve in Fig. 13 corresponds to an undisturbed dispersive soil specimen, directly taken from a cubical block and consolidated by one-dimensional consolidation test. Initially, the void ratio was approximately 1.4, being high and indicating an unstable, porous, and loose structure of the soil in its natural state. This excess porosity is probably due to soluble substances, such as leachable salts, which should have been leached away by surface or groundwater action over a period and left behind residual unfilled pores between the soil particles. As pressure was applied during testing, the void ratio decreased slowly and stabilized at around 0.8. This sudden drop is an indicator of the collapse of the soil structure under load due to its dispersive nature, causing rearrangement and compaction of particles. This property enhances the widespread susceptibility of the soil to structure failure when under loading or saturation, hence requiring stabilization in engineering application. Left uncured, the soil poses a threat of excessive settlement and loss of bearing capacity under structural loads or environmental stress shifts. It should be noted that one-dimensional consolidation testing in this study was conducted only on the untreated dispersive soil in order to characterize its inherent compressibility and collapse-prone structure. Accordingly, Fig. 13 represents the behavior of the untreated soil only. No consolidation tests were performed on hydrogel-treated specimens; therefore, no quantitative conclusions regarding the compressibility behavior of treated soils are drawn in this study. The consolidation results are presented solely as a baseline reference to demonstrate the unstable structure of the natural dispersive soil and to further justify the need for stabilization.

Void ratio–pressure curve for an undisturbed dispersive soil sample under one-dimensional consolidation.

Accroding to Fig. 14, this reduction cannot be attributed solely to densification because specimens were compacted at the same dry density. Therefore, the change is associated with stabilization of the soil fabric. The hydrogel forms a flexible interparticle network rather than rigid cementation. This explains why the recompression index (Cr) slightly increased while remaining within typical structured soil ranges. The soil retained recoverable deformation capacity, indicating reinforcement rather than brittle bonding. Such behavior differs from lime or cement stabilization, where Cr usually decreases due to rigid bonding. At the initial stress level, both specimens exhibited relatively similar void ratios, with values of approximately 1.43 for the untreated soil and 1.40 for the treated soil. At low to moderate pressures, the difference between the two curves remained limited; for example, at around 50 kPa, the void ratio was about 1.32 for the untreated soil and 1.33 for the treated soil, and at 100 kPa, the corresponding values were approximately 1.26 and 1.30, respectively. However, as the applied pressure increased further, the difference became more pronounced. At about 200 kPa, the void ratio of the untreated soil dropped to nearly 1.11, whereas the treated soil still retained a higher value of approximately 1.24. These results indicate that the untreated soil underwent a steeper reduction in void ratio, reflecting greater susceptibility to particle rearrangement and structural collapse under loading. In contrast, the 5% hydrogel-treated soil showed a more gradual compression path and retained a comparatively higher void ratio throughout the higher stress range. Quantitatively, the reduction in void ratio from the initial to the final loading stage was about 0.61 for the untreated soil, whereas it was approximately 0.36 for the treated soil. This smaller reduction suggests that hydrogel treatment improved the stability of the soil fabric and reduced its compressibility. Such behavior may be attributed to the formation of a hydrogel-induced interparticle network, which enhanced bonding between particles and limited excessive volumetric contraction under increasing pressure.

CBR performance under dry and saturated conditions

Standard Proctor compaction tests were conducted solely to determine the optimum moisture content and maximum dry density required for consistent specimen preparation. The effect of wheat straw protein hydrogel on soil performance was therefore evaluated primarily through California Bearing Ratio (CBR) tests conducted under dry and saturated conditions. The e–logσ′ curves reveal a clear modification of the compressibility behavior after hydrogel treatment. The untreated soil exhibited a distinct structural collapse, characterized by a relatively small void ratio reduction up to approximately 100 kPa followed by a sharp compression. This response corresponds to the breakdown of a metastable fabric typical of dispersive silty soils. The calculated compression index (Cc ≈ 0.49) confirms the highly compressible nature of the untreated material. After treatment with 5% hydrogel, the compression response changed substantially. The yield stress increased from about 100 kPa to nearly 180 kPa, indicating the development of apparent bonding between particles. Instead of a sudden collapse, the treated soil showed a gradual transition into virgin compression. Concurrently, the compression index decreased to approximately 0.33, demonstrating a significant reduction in compressibility. As shown in Table 6; Fig. 15, the use of 1% hydrogel increased dry CBR to 9.6%, which shows an increase of 20% relative to the control. The best result was found at 3% dosage, where dry CBR was 12%, which is a 50% increase. The trend was also similar under saturated conditions. CBR increased from 4% for the reference sample to 6% with hydrogel content of 3%. Above this, increases were limited; 5% and 7% additive samples only made small improvements of approximately 2–3%. It can be observed from this that the stabilizing influence of the hydrogel was maximized at 3%, with additional material above this having little improvement. Alongside this, the additive demonstrated reduced strength loss under saturated conditions, which can be attributed to the moisture-regulating behavior of the hydrogel and its ability to maintain interparticle bonding during water ingress. Similar improvements in soaked CBR have been reported in hydrogel- and biopolymer-stabilized soils, where pore filling and polymer–soil bonding limit softening under wet conditions. Hence, 3% hydrogel dosage can be considered optimal in order to improve the dry and wet CBR performance of dispersive ML soils. Based on dispersivity reduction, hydrogel dosages up to 3% were effective in shifting the soil behavior toward the non-dispersive range. In addition, individual mechanical tests such as CBR and direct shear indicated that the 3% dosage provided the most efficient strength-driven improvement. However, when both mechanical performance and dispersivity were considered simultaneously, the multi-criteria TOPSIS analysis identified the 5% dosage as the overall optimal mix design.

Weighting method (ENTROPY)

The entropy method was used to determine the objective weight of selected performance indices, i.e., unconfined compressive strength (UCS), shear strength at 100 kPa normal stress, and dispersion percentage. This statistical technique minimizes subjectivity by assigning higher weights to parameters that exhibit higher variability across the dataset. Figure 16 illustrates that the normalized contribution of the three criteria varied with varying dosages of hydrogel. UCS was always the highest contribution and increased steadily with hydrogel content, whereas shear strength was relatively stable but lower in all cases. Contrary to this, dispersivity decreased in the direction of increasing hydrogel content, which indicates improved chemical stability of the soil.

Entropy-based normalized contributions of UCS, shear strength at 100 kPa, and dispersivity across different hydrogel dosages.

TOPSIS (technique for order preference by similarity to ideal solution)

The TOPSIS technique was used to order the hydrogel dosages taking into account beneficial factors (UCS and shear strength at 100 kPa) and the non-beneficial factor (dispersivity) simultaneously. The procedure entailed four steps: (i) construction and normalization of the decision matrix, (ii) normalization of the values by entropy-based weights, (iii) calculation of the Euclidean distance of each alternative from the ideal (optimal) and anti-ideal (deteriorated) solutions, and (iv) determination of the relative closeness coefficient for each dosage. The higher coefficient signifies a solution that is closer to the ideal and far from the anti-ideal. This organized approach ensures the optimal dose of hydrogel is achieved through an optimal compromise between mechanical properties and chemical stability, rather than relying on one factor.

TOPSIS results

The predicted rankings and scores obtained for the four hydrogel dosages are listed in Table 7. As is apparent from Fig. 17, the top score (0.956) was obtained by the 5% hydrogel dosage, showing the most optimal compromise between chemical stability and mechanical enhancement. Ranking second best (0.802) was the 3% dosage, which presented good performance but with slightly reduced strength compared to the 5%. Though 7% dosage possessed the highest UCS and shear strength, its high dispersivity (39%) reduced the overall score significantly (0.534) and placed it in the third rank. The worst performance of 1% dosage in all the three parameters meant that it also possessed the lowest TOPSIS score value of 0.336. The distinction between a mechanically efficient dosage and an overall optimal dosage under multi-criteria evaluation has also been reported in other studies. For instance, Benemaran et al.60 used TOPSIS to select an optimal composite design based on multiple performance indices and found that the best-ranked mixture was not necessarily the one with the maximum strength alone, but rather the one that achieved the most balanced performance. This supports the present interpretation that 3% represents the mechanically efficient optimum from individual tests, whereas 5% is optimal when strength and dispersivity are jointly considered.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that wheat straw-derived protein hydrogel can significantly improve the geotechnical behavior of dispersive ML soils. Through a series of controlled laboratory experiments, the additive was shown to reduce dispersivity and enhance key mechanical properties, including unconfined compressive strength, shear strength, and California Bearing Ratio under both dry and saturated conditions. Mineralogical analysis using X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirmed the presence of Quartz, Albite, Calcite, and Bassanite, and indicated that higher carbonate–sulfate contents were associated with greater dispersivity. Laboratory results showed that the 3% hydrogel dosage provided the most efficient performance in terms of individual mechanical improvement, yielding notable increases in shear strength parameters, substantial gains in unconfined compressive strength after 28 days of curing, and up to 50% higher CBR values compared with the untreated soil. However, when a multi-criteria decision analysis (TOPSIS) was applied by jointly considering UCS, shear strength, and dispersivity, the 5% hydrogel dosage was identified as the optimum mix design. Although the 7% dosage produced the highest strength values, its increased dispersivity (39%) reduced its overall suitability. In addition, the one-dimensional consolidation test performed on the untreated dispersive soil revealed a highly porous and collapse-prone structure. The void ratio decreased from approximately 1.4 in the initial state to about 0.8 under increasing vertical pressure, indicating substantial structural compression and rearrangement under loading. Although consolidation testing was not performed on hydrogel-treated specimens, these baseline results highlight the inherent compressibility and instability of the natural soil and further justify the need for stabilization in engineering applications. The results demonstrate that the proposed bio-based hydrogel is a promising and sustainable alternative to conventional chemical stabilizers for dispersive soils, particularly where both dispersivity control and mechanical improvement are required. The study also shows that optimum dosage selection should be based on a balanced assessment of engineering performance and dispersivity reduction rather than strength improvement alone.
