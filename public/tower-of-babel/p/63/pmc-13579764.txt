Psychological wellbeing of healthcare professionals in regional, rural and remote emergency departments: an integrative review
Authors: Nimali Lakmini Munasinghe, Renee Fiolet, Alison M. Hutchinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare professionals working in regional, rural, and remote emergency departments are at increased risk of psychological distress, burnout, and emotional exhaustion due to demanding work environments and ongoing workforce pressures. Poor psychological wellbeing contributes to reduced workforce retention and may compromise the delivery of safe, equitable emergency care. However, evidence regarding their psychological wellbeing in these settings remains fragmented and lacks comprehensive synthesis.MethodsAn integrative review of global evidence on the psychological wellbeing of healthcare professionals working in regional, rural, and remote emergency departments was undertaken, focusing on challenges, contributing factors, supports, and reported outcomes. Five electronic databases (MEDLINE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Embase, and Scopus) were systematically searched from inception to June 2025.ResultsSeventeen primary studies from ten countries met inclusion criteria and were appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. Findings revealed multifactorial stressors, including workplace violence, excessive workloads, professional isolation, inadequate leadership, moral distress, and limited access to training, with nurses and female staff most affected. Supports were clustered into four domains: education and professional development, peer and social support, organizational support, and individual coping strategies. Most supports were descriptively reported, with outcomes identified as both positive and negative. In only two studies, structured interventions were formally evaluated, including a self-care program and the implementation of a Serenity Room.ConclusionThe review highlights that psychological distress among healthcare professionals working in regional, rural, and remote emergency departments is influenced by context-specific, systemic, and individual factors, yet evidence-based interventions addressing these issues remain scarce. Multi-level, co-designed strategies integrating organisational safety, training, and wellbeing supports are recommended as potential approaches to support healthcare staff psychological wellbeing, workforce sustainability, and equitable, high-quality emergency care in regional, rural, and remote settings.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251079431.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12873-026-01660-2.

Background

Healthcare professionals (HCPs) in emergency departments (EDs) provide critical, time-sensitive care in high-pressure environments. International studies indicate that ED HCPs experience substantial psychological burden, including burnout, anxiety, depression, emotional exhaustion, post-traumatic stress symptoms, and, in some cases, suicidal ideation [1–3]. Burnout prevalence among emergency medicine professionals has been reported to exceed 70% in some studies, highlighting a substantial occupational mental health burden within this workforce [4]. Resilience is defined as the ability to manage psychological stress and adapt effectively while maintaining functioning under conditions of occupational adversity [4]. Within this review, resilience is conceptualised as a protective capacity influencing psychological wellbeing, whereas burnout and related symptoms are conceptualised as adverse psychological outcomes reflecting occupational strain and distress.

The psychological strain on ED professionals intensifies during large-scale emergencies, such as pandemics, natural disasters, and mass casualty events [5, 6]. In such circumstances, staff often face extended working hours, limited resources, moral injury, and concerns about personal and family safety. Together, these cumulative pressures underscore the importance of structured and sustainable strategies to protect the mental health of ED healthcare workers in both routine operations and crisis situations [7, 8].

HCPs working in regional, rural and remote (RRR) EDs operate within healthcare systems characterised by geographical dispersion and inequities in service access and resource distribution [9]. These contextual factors may influence workforce sustainability and healthcare delivery in underserved settings. In addition, researchers have reported that HCPs in these settings may experience heightened psychological distress associated with systemic and contextual factors relevant to their working environment [3]. Collectively, these conditions may negatively affect staff wellbeing and the overall quality of patient care [10].

Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, research into healthcare workers’ psychological wellbeing has expanded. However, this evidence is characterised by heterogeneity in study designs, populations, settings, and outcome measures [11, 12]. While this growing body of literature highlights the importance of healthcare worker mental health, evidence remains fragmented and insufficiently synthesised for RRR ED contexts. In particular, existing studies do not provide a consolidated understanding of psychological wellbeing in these settings, including the range of challenges experienced, influencing factors, and the nature and effectiveness of available supports and interventions.

The aim of this study was to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence on psychological wellbeing among healthcare professionals working in RRR EDs worldwide. This integrative review focused on three interconnected aspects: challenges and factors contributing to psychological distress, available supports and interventions, and, where reported, the effectiveness of these interventions. The study was intended to generate actionable findings for health service leaders, researchers, and policymakers seeking to enhance the psychological wellbeing of RRR ED staff.

Methods

Study design

An integrative review provides a systematic method for synthesising diverse evidence by collating and critically appraising findings across studies. This approach facilitates a higher-level analysis that identifies recurring patterns, knowledge gaps, and practical implications not easily apparent from individual studies [13]. Therefore, an integrative review design was selected, with the review process including a systematic literature search, study selection, data extraction, critical appraisal, and synthesis of findings from eligible studies. The integrative approach enabled the inclusion of diverse study designs, providing a comprehensive understanding of psychological wellbeing challenges, contributing factors, and effective support strategies for healthcare professionals in RRR EDs globally. This integrative review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [14], with the PRISMA checklist provided in the Supplementary Material 1. The complete review protocol is registered in PROSPERO: CRD420251079431 [15]. Table 1 presents the inclusion and exclusion criteria for study selection.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in consultation with two expert university librarians. Five electronic databases were searched from inception to the date of extraction, with an English language restriction applied: MEDLINE (29 June 2025), CINAHL (29 June 2025), PsycINFO (29 June 2025), Embase (27 June 2025), and Scopus (27 June 2025).

The search strategy for each database included combinations of keywords and controlled vocabulary terms related to ED settings (e.g., “emergency department,” “accident and emergency,” “trauma centre”), healthcare professionals (e.g., “emergency physicians,” “doctors”, “nurses”), geographical settings (e.g., “regional,” “rural,” “remote”), and psychological wellbeing related outcomes and associated constructs (e.g., “stress,” “burnout,” “resilience,” “coping”, “mental health”). Boolean operators, truncation, proximity operators, and database-specific controlled vocabularies were applied to optimize sensitivity and comprehensiveness. The complete search strategies are available in the PROSPERO-registered protocol [15], and reproduced in Supplementary Material 2. Supplementary searches included hand-searching reference lists of included studies and searching Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar to identify relevant peer-reviewed studies not captured in database searches.

All retrieved references were exported to EndNote, where duplicates were removed. The remaining references were imported into Covidence for collaborative screening by the research team, and any further duplicates identified by Covidence were also removed.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts against the predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text articles were then retrieved and assessed independently by both reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through consultation with a third reviewer. For articles in which the study setting was unclear, corresponding authors were contacted via email to clarify the rurality of the site. In one instance, the article identified the study setting as a “regional ED,” but the corresponding author advised that this referred only to an administrative region rather than a RRR setting, and therefore, this study was excluded. Extracted information was entered into a structured data extraction template developed in Covidence by the research team. The template was piloted to determine its usefulness across studies. Following familiarization with the data extraction tool, both researchers independently extracted data from one paper before meeting to discuss similarities and discrepancies. Finding no discrepancies, both reviewers then extracted all other articles independently and then compared the extracted data. A third researcher (AH) was involved in resolving one discrepancy related to extraction. Extracted information included:

Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of included studies was appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) version 2018 [16]. Each study was independently assessed by two reviewers. A small number of discrepancies between the two researchers were noted during quality appraisal, so the third researcher led a team discussion where all three researchers deliberated on the items until consensus was reached. Studies were not excluded based on MMAT quality; inclusion was determined strictly according to the predefined eligibility criteria to ensure a comprehensive synthesis. The detailed results of the quality appraisal are illustrated in the Supplementary Material 3.

Data synthesis

Findings were summarised in tables, before commonalities were organized under predefined thematic categories guided by the review questions, and synthesised narratively in alignment with the review objectives. Subthemes were developed only where sufficient detail and recurring patterns were evident across studies, through systematic comparison and iterative refinement. Reviewer discussion ensured consistency and analytical rigour.

Ethical considerations

As this review involved analysis of published studies, ethical approval was not required. The review adhered to the principles of transparency, data integrity, and proper citation of all original sources.

Results

A total of 1,890 articles were retrieved from the databases. After removing 573 duplicates, 1,317 articles remained for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 121 articles were eligible for full-text assessment. Of these, 17 articles, each reporting a separate study, met the inclusion criteria and were included for data extraction and synthesis. The included studies spanned the years 2012 to 2025 and employed a range of methodologies, including quantitative (n = 12), qualitative (n = 4) [17–20], and mixed-method approaches (n = 1) [21]. Most quantitative studies were cross-sectional (n = 8) [22–29], providing a snapshot in time, while a small number employed repeated-measure or pre–post approaches (n = 4) [30–33], including two interventional studies [32, 33]. One evaluated an individual self-care intervention [33], and the other examined the implementation of a Serenity Room at a healthcare facility [32].

A wide range of tools was used to assess psychological wellbeing across the included studies, reflecting substantial heterogeneity in measurement approaches. Validated instruments included the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI), the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), and a variety of other validated scales, with the MBI being the most frequently employed. In addition, some studies used investigator-developed or adapted questionnaires.

Several articles explicitly described unique rural stressors affecting healthcare professionals in these settings, including isolation, transport delays, and lack of backup support, although only two studies tested tailored solutions to address these challenges [32, 33].

Supplementary Material 4 provides an integrated overview of the key findings and summarises the three main themes identified across the included studies: Psychological challenges/contributing factors, Support and interventions, and Reported outcomes. The following narrative describes these themes, drawing on the evidence summarized in Supplementary Material 4.

Psychological challenges and contributing factors

The studies included in this review consistently revealed that healthcare professionals working in RRR EDs were exposed to a unique and multifactorial set of psychological challenges (Supplementary Material 4). Foremost among these was the high prevalence of workplace violence [22, 23, 30], with authors of one study reporting that nearly two-thirds of ED staff experienced verbal or physical violence in a year, though under-reporting was widespread [22]. Reasons for underreporting were found to include perceptions that the incident “was not important,” reporting would be “useless,” or fear of negative consequences. Evidence also indicated that nurses were disproportionately affected by workplace violence compared with doctors in RRR Eds [22]. Where workplace violence was reported, it was associated with emotional exhaustion, lowered morale, absenteeism, and post-traumatic stress symptoms. Similarly, another study demonstrated that workplace violence in EDs persisted despite existing safety and security measures [23]. Workplace violence, including verbal abuse and physical assault, was found to affect staff performance, alter interactions with patients, contribute to PTSD, and lead some staff to consider leaving their job [22, 23].

Beyond violence, studies highlighted multiple organizational and environmental stressors. Busy, crowded, and unstable ED settings, coupled with unpredictable workloads, frequent interruptions, and high patient acuity, were repeatedly identified as sources of stress [22, 24, 25, 31]. These factors not only increased staff stress but also raised the likelihood of adverse events. Experiencing or managing adverse events further contributed to psychological distress [24, 30]. Findings also highlighted challenges related to inadequate resources, mismatched patient-to-staff ratios, and absence of essential supplies, particularly in rural settings [17–19, 31]. Isolation, long transport delays, limited organizational support, professional support, insufficient leadership guidance [19, 32], and poor communication or coordination with transport and emergency services further exacerbated stress, especially for solo or locum physicians, international medical graduates, and new nurses [18, 19]. In ED staff, poorer communication skills were found to be associated with higher emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, indicating that deficits in communication skills may contribute to greater psychological distress and burnout risk [26].

Occupational stressors included fear and anxiety about working beyond one’s scope of practice, potential legal or professional consequences, exposure to trauma, and emotional strain from caring for seriously ill or young patients, or patients known to the community, such as family members, relatives, and neighbours [17, 19, 24]. Nurses and medical specialists reported the highest emotional impact, with repeated exposure to trauma contributing to burnout, compassion fatigue, and symptoms of post-traumatic stress [18, 27, 31]. Stressors were compounded by operational factors, such as long working hours, role conflict and ambiguity, multi-unit responsibilities, and unclear job descriptions [28, 29]. Perceived work overload, strongly associated with long work hours, was particularly high among medical staff, with interns experiencing the greatest stress, highest workload pressure, and lowest sense of personal efficacy [29]. Additionally, physical demands and workplace complexity were notable contributors to stress. Nurses reported fatigue, muscle stiffness, sleep disturbances, and difficulty concentrating due to high workload, non-nursing duties, and technical challenges inherent to ED practice [25]. Limited access to continuing education and insufficient training tailored to rural practice were identified as contributing factors to psychological distress. In addition, a lack of confidence in managing mental health presentations, stemming from limited foundational knowledge and fear of aggression in drug- or alcohol-related cases without immediate backup, further heightened stress and moral distress [18, 21]. Finally, the COVID-19 pandemic introduced additional acute stressors for ED staff. Rapid patient influx, high-pressure conditions, frequent changes to clinical guidelines, inadequate protective equipment, and fear of infection increased psychological strain and contributed to higher rates of post-traumatic stress symptoms, particularly among rural ED physicians [20, 31]. Collectively, these findings highlight that psychological challenges in RRR EDs arise from a combination of workplace violence, high workload, environmental instability, resource limitations, role stress, trauma exposure, social isolation, and systemic constraints, with certain staff groups, particularly nurses, female staff, and mid-career medical specialists, experiencing disproportionately high impact.

Support systems and interventions

The following support strategies are drawn from the evidence summarized in Supplementary Material 4. Of the 17 studies synthesized, authors of 15 (88.2%) reported some form of support or intervention for the psychological wellbeing of ED staff. Among these, one focused solely on security measures aimed at preventing workplace violence [23], while only two articles described formal support interventions [32, 33]. Beyond these two interventions, the majority of reported supports remained descriptive, informal, or locally adapted rather than rigorously implemented or systematically evaluated.

Of the two studies of implementation and formal evaluation of structured interventions, one examined a four-week self-care programme that incorporated education on multiple self-care pathways, guidance on selecting preferred activities, and sustained practice over four weeks [33]. The programme was found to be effective, with significant reductions in stress, burnout, and medication errors among emergency nurses. The other study showed high levels of perceived success following the introduction of a staff-informed serenity room, which enabled short periods of recovery and renewal, though barriers such as staffing shortages and lack of time-constrained access [32]. The serenity room incorporated strategies such as reclining chairs, aromatherapy, a sound machine, a tea station, mindfulness tools, and brief restorative resources.

Education and professional development

Across the studies synthesised, education and professional development emerged as key strategies to enhance competence, confidence, and wellbeing among ED staff. Continuing education tailored to the rural context was highlighted as essential for improving nurses’ confidence and capacity in rural emergency settings [21]. Financial support was shown to increase uptake of continuing education opportunities, while online, enquiry-based modules promoted deeper learning through reflective practice [21]. Career development programs were reported to build competencies and support long-term professional growth, with periodic job training enhancing competence, self-efficacy, and staff retention [28]. Specialized training in trauma care aligned with the latest evidence further supported the development of professional skills [28].

Other authors emphasized the role of training in mitigating fear and anxiety and enhancing teamwork through locally organized interprofessional continuing medical education (CME) opportunities, including equipment and skills training, simulation-based learning, and team-building activities [18]. Better communication skills have been associated with lower burnout among ED staff, suggesting that communication skills training may help reduce burnout risk and enhance psychological well-being [26]. Brief education sessions on self-care pathways encompassing physical, mental, emotional, spiritual, and relational aspects have also been reported as beneficial [33].

Peer and social supports

Peer and social supports emerged consistently across studies as important strategies for promoting the wellbeing of ED staff. Within the workplace, informal and interpersonal supports included peer support and brief debriefing following critical events [19], colleague back-up arrangements [18], flexible ED shift scheduling to reduce individual workload, and strong professional relationships within healthcare teams [18, 20, 27]. Informal encouragement from managers and colleagues was also reported as a source of reassurance and collective resilience [20, 27].

Outside the workplace, reliance on family, friends, and wider community networks was frequently described as an important source of emotional support. Many healthcare workers drew strength from faith, prayer, spirituality, or spending time in nature, which helped buffer workplace stress [19, 29].

Organizational support

Organizational support was consistently identified as a key factor influencing the safety, functioning, and retention of ED HCPs. This support encompassed managerial strategies, structural systems, safety measures, and environmental arrangements. Several studies emphasized the importance of managerial encouragement to report adverse events and the establishment of structured reporting systems to promote accountability and organizational learning [24]. Broader organizational strategies included continuous environmental and administrative support to reduce role conflict and occupational stress, provision of organizational support to encourage commitment and long-term retention, and establishing legal definitions of scope of practice to reduce ambiguity and strengthen role clarity [28]. Leadership approaches characterised by clear, frequent communication and responsiveness were highlighted as essential, along with providing ethical support to address moral stress during high-pressure situations such as the COVID-19 pandemic [20].

Workplace violence protocols and prevention strategies were also reported to support the recognition, reporting, and management of workplace violence [22, 23]. These measures included locked units, metal detectors, passive weapons screening, and variable security staffing, including direct ED security staff coverage [23]. They were intended to enhance staff safety and psychological well-being, although inadequate ED security was reported in the majority of these settings.

Regional transfer arrangements and “no-refusal” policies with referral centres were reported to facilitate service delivery and reduce operational stress in these contexts [18]. Such context-specific measures illustrate the role of organizational support in addressing the unique needs of diverse working environments.

Individual coping strategies

In the absence of formal supports, many healthcare workers described personal strategies for managing stress. Commonly reported methods included physical activity, journaling, meditation, music, gardening, and problem-solving approaches [19]. One study specifically identified active coping, acceptance, and planning as the most frequently used strategies, while denial, religion, and substance use were less commonly reported, with substance use being notably more frequent among medical interns [29]. Other authors reported a range of coping mechanisms, including crying, humour, blocking emotions, and taking pride in resilience or a “survivor” mindset to continue working in the stressful ED environment [17]. The use of alcohol as a stress management strategy was also noted in some settings [17, 29]. Avoidance and emotional suppression were further described as coping mechanisms [27].

Reported outcomes

The following outcomes are derived from the evidence summarized in Supplementary Material 4. Of the 17 included studies, authors of eight (47.1%) reported outcomes or effectiveness data, while only two (11.8%) formally evaluated a support intervention. These outcomes reflect both the negative consequences of workplace stressors and the positive effects of targeted training and organizational supports on the psychological wellbeing of ED staff.

Negative outcomes

Psychological impacts included adverse effects on mental wellbeing, lowered job satisfaction, reduced confidence, and poorer staff morale following workplace violence [22]. Feelings of fear, anxiety, being overwhelmed, and reduced enjoyment of work, sometimes prompting consideration of quitting, were also described [18]. In addition, role conflict was correlated with occupational stress and lower perceived organizational support [28]. Consideration of leaving was quantified in one study: 37% of staff occasionally considered leaving their job because of patient-safety concerns and 33% considered leaving risky or difficult tasks to colleagues; adverse event involvement and emotional strain were linked to thoughts of quitting, while formal reporting of adverse events was very low (3.6%) with underreporting > 90% [24]. Workforce outcomes further included reduced job performance and lower quality of patient care, increased absenteeism, higher turnover, and loss of experienced staff, which reduced health-system efficiency [22]. Turnover was also observed, with two nurses leaving nursing for a period of time due to sustained work-related stressors [17].

Positive outcomes

Several authors reported positive outcomes related to training, local adaptation and organizational supports. Rural background factors, such as rural upbringing, outdoor lifestyles, and strong rural training, were associated with better adaptation to and greater enjoyment of rural practice [18]. Locally organized interprofessional training, effective patient-transfer policies and inter-facility transport teams were reported to improve patient care and ease workload [18]. Training and organizational responses during the COVID-19 pandemic period were associated with increased team cohesion, enhanced preparedness, and strengthened knowledge and competence among staff [27]. Organizational supports and periodic job training were associated with higher intent to stay among specialist trauma nurses and were linked to job satisfaction and perceived workload factors [28].

Only two studies formally evaluated the effectiveness of interventions [32, 33]. A structured four-week self-care programme produced statistically significant reductions in stress, burnout, and medication errors post-intervention [33]. A Serenity Room intervention yielded high perceived effectiveness: mean rating 7.4/10, 71% of staff reported feeling moderately to extremely renewed after use, and 94% believed similar facilities would benefit other departments [32].

Discussion

This integrative review synthesized evidence from 17 primary studies exploring the psychological wellbeing of HCPs in RRR EDs. Collectively, the findings highlight a high and multifactorial burden of psychological distress in RRR ED staff, influenced by factors such as workplace violence, operational pressures, role conflict, professional isolation, repeated exposure to trauma, and limited context-specific training opportunities and support systems. Nurses and early-career staff were consistently identified as especially vulnerable. Although several descriptive initiatives were reported, only two studies formally evaluated interventions [32, 33], underscoring a significant gap in robust, evidence-based responses in these settings.

This review highlights a notable geographic imbalance in wellbeing studies. This finding is in line with previous findings, as most wellbeing interventions for rural HCPs originated in the United States, with a few in Australia, and a scarcity in low- and middle-income countries [34]. Similarly, most studies included in this review were conducted in high-income contexts, with only one study from a developing country (Indonesia). This geographic imbalance suggests that the findings are primarily transferable to high-income healthcare contexts and may have limited applicability to low- and middle-income settings, highlighting the need for context-sensitive research in underrepresented settings.

The review further indicates that gender plays a critical role in shaping psychological wellbeing. Female HCPs in RRR EDs reported greater psychological impact, consistent with systematic reviews documenting gender disparities in burnout among emergency physicians [35, 36]. Female staff commonly report higher emotional exhaustion and lower personal accomplishment, driven by both occupational stressors and societal expectations. While these studies largely examined urban or mixed settings, our review extends this evidence to RRR contexts. Therefore, developing targeted, culturally and contextually tailored strategies, such as flexible scheduling, mentorship programs, and gender-sensitive support systems, may enhance psychological resilience, workforce sustainability, and staff retention in RRR ED settings.

Educational and professional development opportunities were identified as protective factors for psychological wellbeing. This finding is consistent with findings of broader international scoping reviews of ED workforce wellbeing [37, 38], which highlight the importance of training in building resilience, improving clinical competence, and enhancing retention. Yet, in RRR EDs, systemic barriers, including geographical isolation, limited staffing, and restricted funding, constrain access to such opportunities. This suggests that locally relevant, flexible programs such as tele-education, simulation training, and trauma-informed care workshops could be particularly beneficial [39, 40]. Furthermore, integrating self-care, burnout awareness, and stress management into nursing curricula and hospital orientation programs may provide sustainable impact [33, 41]. Such tailored professional development not only improves workforce capability but also mitigates occupational stress, bolsters self-efficacy, and promotes long-term workforce sustainability [21, 28].

Workplace violence was consistently reported across RRR ED studies, with verbal and physical assaults highly prevalent. Similar patterns have been documented in urban Eds [42]. However, in RRR Eds, security gaps are often amplified: limited infrastructure, inconsistent staffing, and lack of dedicated security personnel remain common [43]. These deficiencies may contribute to increased staff vulnerability and occupational stress in these settings. Addressing physical safety alongside psychological support is therefore essential to improve wellbeing outcomes. Moreover, this study revealed that RRR ED staff have limited expertise in managing patients with mental illness and substance misuse, despite their frequent presentations. These combined challenges of high patient demand and limited capability in this area may contribute to psychological stress among staff. Addressing safety concerns through robust prevention programs, consistent risk assessment [44], zero-tolerance policies [45] and accessible training in de-escalation [46] is important to protect both staff and patients.

The review also revealed that coping strategies vary widely, with both adaptive and maladaptive responses evident. Adaptive strategies such as acceptance, planning, and teamwork were linked to enhanced resilience, whereas maladaptive strategies, including denial, avoidance, emotional suppression, and substance use, posed additional risks. Similar patterns have been reported in urban ED contexts [47], suggesting that maladaptive coping may be a universal risk across emergency care [47]. Therefore, interventions must address not only workplace stressors but also foster adaptive coping, combining organizational supports with individual skill-building to yield sustainable benefits.

While psychological distress is common among staff in both urban and RRR EDs, the underlying drivers differ. In urban EDs, challenges centre on high patient volume, crowding, and ED boarding, (i.e., holding admitted patients in the ED when inpatient beds are unavailable) [48]. In contrast, RRR ED staff face distinct stressors, including professional isolation, limited backup, transfer delays, resource limitations, and scope-of-practice anxieties. Additionally, community and relational stressors arising from caring for patients known personally create ethical and emotional complexities [17–19]. These context-specific stressors may exacerbate moral injury. Furthermore, limited expertise and resources for managing psychiatric crises and substance-related presentations further compound the problem. These differences suggest that interventions developed for urban centres cannot be directly applied to RRR EDs without careful adaptation to their unique challenges, ideally through co-design approaches that integrate evidence-based strategies with local knowledge and lived experience [49, 50].

A further key finding of this review is the scarcity of rigorously evaluated interventions specifically tailored to RRR EDs. Although a structured self-care program and the use of serenity or restorative rooms demonstrated promising benefits, including reductions in stress, burnout, and perceived medication errors, the absence of controlled trials and long-term evaluations limits the reliability and generalisability of these results. This pattern reflects broader trends in integrative and scoping reviews, which frequently report innovative approaches but with little rigorous evaluation [34, 37, 51].

Thus, addressing this gap requires pragmatic, co-designed evaluations of multi-level wellbeing programs that are contextually relevant to RRR ED settings.

These insights should be interpreted in light of important limitations in the available evidence. Most quantitative studies were cross-sectional [22–29], and applied diverse measurement tools, with a small number of studies using intervention and repeated-measure designs [30–33], limiting understanding of wellbeing trajectories over time and restricting interpretation of causal relationships within the available evidence base. This diversity of measurement tools, while reflecting varied approaches, can limit comparability of outcomes and synthesis of findings. Additionally, the predominance of studies from high-income settings may limit the transferability of findings to other contexts. These factors reduce the external validity of findings and highlight the need for context-specific, rigorously evaluated interventions in underrepresented settings.

Strengths and limitations

This review’s strengths include its systematic approach to ensure methodological rigour, the use of an integrative methodology to include a variety of evidence, and its focus on a workforce that, despite its critical importance, remains under-researched. The inclusion of diverse study designs enabled a broad synthesis of psychological wellbeing and support mechanisms.

Limitations include restriction to English-language records, thereby excluding any relevant non-English articles. Although five major databases were searched, supplemented by hand-searching the reference lists of included studies, as well as searches of Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar, no additional eligible studies were identified. Relevant studies indexed elsewhere may have been missed. Inclusion was limited to primary peer-reviewed studies and theses/dissertations, excluding other grey literature such as government reports and conference abstracts, which may have contained relevant evaluations. Variations in definitions of rural, regional, and remote, together with the exclusion of pre-hospital and community-based emergency care research, may limit the precision and scope of the conclusions. Finally, variation in study quality and design across included studies limited confidence in some syntheses and prevented quantitative pooling of findings.

Recommendations and future directions

For practice

• Implement multi-level strategies that integrate individual, organizational, and system-level supports, co-designed with healthcare professionals to ensure relevance and uptake

• Foster visible and responsive leadership through regular staff engagement, recognition of contributions, and timely feedback to build trust and morale

• Ensure clear, consistent, and timely communication of operational updates, policy changes, and safety information, particularly during crises, to reduce uncertainty and stress.

• Ensure adequate staffing, essential equipment, and reliable infrastructure, with timely logistical support and effective patient transfer and inter-facility communication, to reduce workload pressures and safety risks.

• Establish clear role definitions and scope of practice, particularly in RRR EDs, to reduce role conflict and moral distress.

• Provide mandatory training in managing psychiatric crises, drug- and alcohol-affected patients, and de-escalation strategies.

• Expand access to flexible, context-specific, and locally accessible training via telehealth and online learning platforms, supported by government funding

• Integrate wellbeing, stress management, and burnout awareness into professional education and orientation programs.

• Promote a psychologically safe workplace culture by promoting voluntary reporting of adverse events and facilitating organizational learning and improvement.

• Expand peer support, formalised debriefing, and structured wellbeing initiatives that promote adaptive coping, mentorship, and timely access to mental health professionals after critical incidents.

• Develop and evaluate gender-sensitive strategies tailored to the needs of female ED staff.

• Ensure wellbeing initiatives are regularly evaluated, enabling continuous improvement and adaptation to local needs.

For policy

• Embed wellbeing initiatives into organizational policy and planning, ensuring adequate funding and resources for sustainable interventions.

• Establish zero-tolerance policies for workplace violence, supported by adequate security measures, risk assessments, and mandatory reporting and escalation procedure.

• Develop and validate core outcome measures for ED wellbeing research to facilitate comparability.

• Conduct pragmatic trials of low-cost interventions (restorative rooms, self-care curricula, tele-peer support, and resilience-building programs).

• Increase research in low- and middle-income RRR contexts to improve global relevance.

Conclusion

Healthcare professionals in RRR EDs face significant psychological challenges, shaped by workplace violence, high workloads, professional isolation, and inadequate resources. Limited training in managing mentally ill and drug- or alcohol-affected patients, coupled with insufficient security infrastructure, intensified these stressors. While adaptive coping and educational programs offer protective benefits, the lack of rigorously evaluated, context-specific interventions remains a critical gap. Addressing these challenges requires multi-level, co-designed strategies that integrate safety, education, and wellbeing supports. Strengthening the psychological wellbeing of RRR ED staff is essential to workforce sustainability and to ensuring equitable, safe, and high-quality emergency care.
