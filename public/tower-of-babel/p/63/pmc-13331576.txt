Early Mobilization After Pacemaker Implantation
Authors: Jiří Šmíd, Vlastimil Vančura, Martin Brada, Richard Rokyta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe increasing use of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED) is leading to a rise in procedure‐related complications.ObjectiveThis trial aimed to assess the safety and feasibility of early mobilization and the possibility of same‐day discharge following permanent pacemaker implantation.Methods200 patients were enrolled from 3/2019 to 12/2023 in this trial. Participants were randomly assigned to the Early Mobilization arm with 4 h of post‐procedure bed rest or the Late Mobilization arm with 16–24 h of post‐procedure bed rest. Primary endpoints include a composite of common complications: hematoma, major bleeding, wound infections, pneumothorax, and lead dislodgement. Secondary endpoints compared the incidence of individual complications from primary endpoints and changes in the technical parameters of the CIED between the two groups. Follow‐ups were at 1 and 6 months.ResultsTwo hundred patients were enrolled, 191 (95.5%) had the 6‐month follow‐up. About 150 patients received a dual lead device, and 50 received a single lead device. There were no significant differences in primary composite complications (EaM vs. LaM; four cases, 4% vs. seven cases, 7%; p = 0.548). There were no significant differences in secondary endpoints EaM vs. LaM; atrial lead dislodgement (2 vs. 2), ventricular lead dislodgement (0 vs. 0), and wound infection (1 vs. 0), respectively. Technical parameters remained stable in both arms without any significant differences, for example, a drop in sensing or an increase in the stimulation threshold.ConclusionThe early mobilization protocol is safe, and the incidence of complications is no higher than that of late mobilization.Keywords: CIED, complication, implantation, mobilization, permanent pacemaker

Background

The use of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) has become a commonplace procedure in recent years. According to the available data, the median number of permanent pacemaker implants in the EU was 652.2 (IQR 267.5–874.7) per million inhabitants. For member countries of the European Society of Cardiology, the median number of implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) implants was 105.0 (IQR 37.1–153.3) implants per million inhabitants, and the median number of cardiac resynchronization therapy implants (CRT) was 63.6 (IQR 22.8–126.8) implants per million inhabitants in 2020 [1]. The rise in CIED implantations has also been associated with a rise in the prevalence of complications. Available data indicate that complications occur in 5.5%–8.2% of cases within 90 days of implantation. The most common causes of complications following implantation are lead dislodgement, hematomas, and impaired healing, which are often linked to the duration of post‐procedure immobilization [2, 3]. Due to the aging population and the increasing number of patients with cardiac disease, further increases in implant procedures can be expected. The growing number of procedures is placing greater demands on the healthcare system, including an increased need for nursing care and an expanded need for hospitalization for these procedures. As such, there is a need to reduce the length of CIED hospitalization through the use of outpatient procedures with same‐day discharge.

Our trial aimed to confirm the safety and feasibility of same‐day discharge (using reduced periods of immobilization) in a randomized trial of patients undergoing permanent pacemaker implantation.

Study Design

Early mobilization after pacemaker implantation is a prospective, randomized, single‐center study that enrolled patients undergoing pacemaker implantation at our institution. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with ID NCT04111354.

Inclusion criteria for study patients included: (1) minimum age of 18 years, (2) classes I and II recommendations for pacing according to ESC guidelines [4], (3) mobile, cooperative patients, and (4) signed informed consent for the study. Exclusion criteria included: (1) implantation of biventricular devices, (2) device upgrade or revision, (3) patients with the usual contraindications to permanent pacemaker implantation, and (4) pregnancy [5, 6, 7].

Study Protocol

Patients indicated for permanent pacemaker implantation were randomized into two arms. (1) The Early Mobilization (EaM) arm, that is, patients rested in bed for 4 h with light wound compression after uncomplicated CIED implantations, and (2) the Late Mobilization arm (LaM), that is, patients rested in bed for 16–24 h after uncomplicated implantations until the following morning with 4 h of light wound compression. During our study, there was no difference in the discharge time. Absorbable sutures were used in all cases. The wound was covered by surgical wound dressing “Curapor” for 3 days after discharge, with 4 h of light wound compression in both trial arms. Patients in both groups were instructed on wound care, including avoiding lifting the limbs above the head and lifting heavy loads for 10 days.

All patients (both arms) underwent a standard follow‐up of wound status, a chest X‐ray to rule out pneumothorax or other complications, and an evaluation of the device's technical parameters before discharge. Patients were given wound care instructions, including suture removal, as recommended by the implanting physician. Follow‐up visits were scheduled for 1 month (±7 days) and six months (±14 days) after the procedure. Primary and Secondary Endpoints

The primary composite endpoint included the most commonly encountered complications associated with CIED implantation, that is, (1) hematoma or major bleeding (requiring intervention/revision, blood transfusion, or prolonged hospitalization), (2) wound infection, (3) pneumothorax, (4) atrial lead dislodgement, (5) ventricular lead dislodgement, and (6) other, extracardiac complications. The secondary endpoint compared the incidence of each complication listed as part of the composite primary endpoint and any changes in the technical parameters of the CIEDs.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics, including counts and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables, were calculated at baseline and for follow‐ups. Comparisons between the EaM and LaM cohorts were conducted using Fisher's exact test for categorical data and the t‐test for continuous data. The study was powered at 80% to detect a clinically meaningful difference at a significance level of 0.05.

Patient Characteristics

A total of 200 patients were enrolled in the study, 100 in each arm. The mean patient age was 74.89 years (±7.5), with 121 (60.5%) male patients. The most common indications for implantation were atrioventricular block (79 patients; 39.5%), sick sinus syndrome (71 patients; 35.5%), and atrial fibrillation with bradycardia (50 patients; 25%). This corresponds to the device types implanted, with 150 patients receiving a dual lead device and 50 receiving a single lead device. There was no significant difference in the indication for implantation or the type of implanted device. Anticoagulation was used in 115 patients. Patients with DOAC anticoagulation therapy discontinued therapy on the day of surgery, and therapy was restarted, in uncomplicated cases, the following day. For patients on warfarin, the procedure was performed without therapy interruption with a target international normalized ratio (INR) < 2.5. We found a significant difference only in the use of dabigatran (EaM vs. LaM; 8 pts. vs. 1 pt.; p = 0.036). Antiplatelet therapy was used in 60 patients. There was a marginal difference between arms regarding the use of acetylsalicylic acid (EaM vs. LaM; 17pts. vs. 33pts.; p = 0.057) (Table 1).

Results

Of the 200 enrolled patients, all had their first (i.e., the 1‐month) follow‐up and 191 patients attended the 6‐month follow‐up. Of the nine patients who missed the 6‐month follow‐up, six had died before the follow‐up, and three patients simply failed to attend. After 6 months, patients were followed by their outpatient cardiologist, which included device checks. There were no deaths related to the implantation procedure. Of the six deaths, two were related to COVID, one to cancer, one to pneumonia‐related sepsis, one to decompensated heart failure, and one cause of death could not be verified.

A total of 11 procedure‐related primary endpoint complications were recorded. There were no statistically significant differences between the two arms. There were four endpoints in the EaM arm and seven in the LaM arm, p = 0.548. Three endpoints were assessed as other or extracardiac complications (left upper extremity thrombosis, lead fracture, ICD upgrade). The thrombosis and the lead fracture were in the LaM arm.

There were no statistically significant differences in the incidence of individual complications between the two arms during secondary follow‐up. No large hematomas were observed. There were only patients with minor hematomas who did not require any intervention and were managed conservatively. Two cases of pneumothorax were identified in the LaM arm. However, this was not attributed to the different post‐procedural rest protocols but rather to the procedure itself. There was no difference in the incidence of atrial and ventricular lead dislodgement between the two arms (atrial dislodgement: EaM vs. LaM; 2 vs. 2; p = 1, ventricular dislodgement: 0 vs. 0). One wound infection after implantation was recorded in the EaM arm. It was a superficial infection in a scar after implantation, which required local treatment and therapy with oral antibiotics. Staphylococcus aureus was found on culture. The patient was treated for diabetes mellitus, and there were no deviations in the standards of care, procedure time and wound management. One case where the lead perforated the ventricular wall was recorded in the LaM arm. The perforation required cardiac surgery (Table 2).

Discussion

The rising number of CIED implantations has naturally increased the number of CIED‐related hospitalizations. These hospitalizations have varying periods of post‐procedural bed rest, the length of which depends on the practices of each department. We know that the occurrence of periprocedural complications is relatively low. Consequently, the question arises as to whether prolonged post‐procedural bed rest can effectively prevent post‐procedural complications, particularly lead dislodgement or wound complications. The first studies demonstrating the safety of overnight hospitalization following permanent pacemaker implantation were published in the 80s, for example, by Haywood et al. in 1989 [8]. Since then, few trials have been published studying this issue, and even fewer were prospective randomized trials like ours. A meta‐analysis by Trongtorsak et al. in 2021, which included 61,602 patients, provides a good overview. However, in the meta‐analysis, only one trial was randomized; three collected data from prospective studies, and four were retrospective studies. Same‐day discharge (SDD) in the meta‐analysis was defined as patients discharged the same day after cardiac device implantations. An overnight hospital stay (HO) was defined as spending the post‐procedural night in the hospital. The meta‐analysis suggested that SDD after cardiac device implantations might be a safe and feasible alternative to HO without any differences in procedure‐related complications, readmission rates, or mortality rates [9].

Smaller randomized trials by Refs. [10] and [11] have also confirmed that shorter periods of post‐procedure bed rest were safe and were not associated with a higher risk of complications. Budano et al. enrolled 200 patients, with most of the pacing leads implanted using passive fixation (97% atrial leads, 88% ventricular leads); unfortunately, there were no specifics regarding the exact location of lead implantation (e.g., the apex or septum) (D‐F). In our trial, all leads were implanted using active fixation, and all leads were implanted in the septum.

One issue with SDD revolves around what is the most appropriate observation period. This issue was addressed by Wadhwani et al. in their 2021 trial. In this retrospective analysis of 2421 patients, the time‐to‐occurrence of acute complications after implantation was studied. Patients were assessed, including chest radiography, at 6‐ and 24‐h post‐procedure. They recorded 48 acute complications, of which 43 (90%) occurred within 6 h after implantation and only three (0,12% of all cases) required intervention [12].

In our study, no acute complications (e.g., slowly developing pneumothorax or lead dislodgement) were observed in the EaM arm within the 4‐h follow‐up window. In only one case did we find atrial lead dislodgement, and that was 1 month after the procedure and was associated with chest trauma resulting from a fall. These findings were confirmed in a study by Misra et al., where the mean time for post‐operative chest X‐rays was 2.6 h [13]. In general, these findings indicate that accelerated discharge strategies are safe when used in patients who have been appropriately followed before discharge.

Our prospective, randomized trial provides a comprehensive evaluation of the safety and feasibility of early mobilization (EaM) following CIED implantation compared to the traditional approach of late mobilization (LaM). Our results confirm that early mobilization is not associated with an increased risk of complications, which has significant implications for clinical practice and healthcare management.

The primary composite endpoint included complications such as pocket hematomas, major bleeding, wound infections, pneumothorax, atrial lead dislodgement, ventricular lead dislodgement, and other extracardiac complications. The lack of statistically significant differences between the EaM and LaM arms (4 vs. 7 complications, respectively, p = 0.548) indicates that early mobilization does not compromise patient safety. This is a critical finding since it challenges the conventional wisdom that prolonged bed rest is necessary to prevent post‐CIED implantation complications.

In terms of secondary endpoints, complications were similarly distributed between the two arms. There were no large hematomas in either group. There were two cases of pneumothorax in the LaM group; however, both were attributed to procedural factors rather than bed rest times, which further underscores the safety of early mobilization. The similar incidence of atrial and ventricular lead dislodgement (EaM vs. LaM; atrial: 2 vs. 2, p = 1; ventricular: 0 vs. 0) also supports the conclusion that early mobilization does not increase the risk of lead displacement.

In our study, one wound infection was observed in the EaM arm, and in the LaM arm, there was one ventricular lead perforation that required a surgical intervention. This shows that while complications can occur, their frequency and severity do not appear to be influenced by the length of post‐procedural bed rest. Our findings are consistent with multiple previous studies, further reinforcing the robustness of our results [5, 7, 9, 12, 13, 14].

Our conclusions are additionally supported by a study from Ref. [14]. The study was a non‐randomized trial with 812 patients with SDD versus 921 patients with an overnight hospital stay (p = 0.87). The 90‐day complication rate was similar in both groups (4.14% vs. 4.07%, p = 0.95). The composite early post‐procedural complication rates and late post‐procedural complication rates were also similar (0.97 vs. 1.18%, p = 0.70 and 0.73% vs. 0.64%, p = 0.83, respectively) [14].

Misra et al. further confirmed the safety of early mobilization in their study involving 4543 CIED implantation procedures, of which 1557 (34%) involved early mobilization (3 h of bed rest). Patients in the early mobilization group had lower rates of complications (1.3% vs. 2.1%, p = 0.0487) and required less acute care post‐discharge (9.6% vs. 14.0%, p < 0.0001) [13].

The main limitation of these trials was that they were non‐randomized. Archontakis S. et al. prospectively collected data with findings obtained retrospectively using a recent historical cohort. The decision to enroll a patient in the SDD protocol was clinical, so there might have been a bias to include lower‐risk patients in the group. In the retrospective trial of Misra et al., one limitation was associated with patient selection and the prevalence of younger patients with fewer comorbidities in the SDD group.

Due to concerns regarding higher complication rates in patients requiring ICD or CRT‐D implantation, these patients were excluded from our study. This is one of limitations of our trial. Permanent pacemakers are smaller and the procedure is less risky compared to implantation of defibrillators and CRT devices. These concerns have not been confirmed in other trials that studied immobilization times after cardiac device implantation. The safety of a short immobilization period for CRT and ICD implantations was confirmed by the studies of Archotakis et al. and Misra et al. where 11.3% and 8.3% of ICD and CRT procedures, respectively 31% and 20.6% of ICD and CRT procedures were included.

Clinical Implications

Patients discharged on the same day benefit from recovering in the comfort of their homes, potentially reducing stress and the risk of hospital‐acquired infections. Furthermore, early mobilization aligns with the broader trend in healthcare towards minimally invasive procedures and enhanced recovery protocols, which aim to optimize patient outcomes and resource utilization.

Limitations and Future Research

The single‐center nature of our study is a notable limitation since it could affect the generalizability of our findings. To address this, future studies, which should include multiple centers with diverse patient populations, are needed to confirm the applicability of early mobilization protocols in various settings. Our study's follow‐up period of 6 months provided valuable mid‐term data and was sufficiently long for complications after 6 months to be considered unrelated to the SDD protocol. Another limitation was the relatively small sample size of 200 patients, which may not be sufficient to detect rare but potentially significant complications; however, our results have been confirmed by several recent publications with much larger patient numbers as well as a recent meta‐analysis [9, 13, 14].

Conclusion

Our EMAPI study presents compelling evidence that early mobilization after CIED implantation is safe and effective. The absence of significant differences in periprocedural complications between the EaM and LaM arms supports the feasibility of early discharge, which could lead to substantial healthcare savings and improved patient experiences. Our findings advocate for a reconsideration of current post‐implantation care protocols and promote early mobilization as the new standard of care.
