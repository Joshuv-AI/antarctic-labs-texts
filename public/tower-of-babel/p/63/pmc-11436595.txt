Aeromonas veronii-associated bacteremia in the course of treatment of acute myeloid leukemia: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Jiashan Zhao, Tianjiao Wu, Songyun Wang, Huirui Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to report a case of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) complicated by Aeromonas veronii infection-induced bacteremia and to review relevant literature on the etiology, prevention, treatment, and prognosis of bacteremia in immunocompromised populations, aiming to reduce mortality in individuals with hematologic and other end-stage diseases and improve patient outcomes.Methods and resultsWe reported the case of a 23-year-old male patient with relapsed AML characterized by AML1:ETO and ASXL positivity, classified as a high-risk group. The patient presented with fever, abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting after consuming partially cooked fish. The patient was admitted with high leucocytes, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and interleukin-6 levels. Peripheral blood high-throughput sequencing (Next-Generation Sequencing NGS) confirmed infection with Aeromonas veronii, while an abdominal CT scan indicated a liver abscess with gas formation. Culture of the drainage fluid from the ultrasound-guided liver abscess puncture demonstrated growth of Aeromonas veronii. Based on the sensitivity results, the patient was treated with intravenous ciprofloxacin and cefoperazone-sulbactam. After treatment with antibiotics, blood transfusion, liver protection, and azacitidine 100 mg ih, combined with dry white sand(interferon alpha-1B, interleukin-2, and thalidomide), the critical condition of the patient improved, and he was discharged. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Medical Research in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Science and Technology. Informed consent was obtained from this patient and we have obscured the patient's identifying information. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.ConclusionWhen patients with a recurrence of AML have a history of consuming or contacting aquatic products, clinicians should be vigilant about Aeromonas veronii infection. The presence of Aeromonas veronii in peripheral blood must alert clinicians to the possibility of severe sepsis and septic shock. Early diagnosis and prompt treatment are crucial to reducing patient mortality.Keywords: Aeromonas veronii, Bacteremia, Acute myeloid leukemia, Liver abscess, Case report

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a malignant clonal hematologic disorder originating from hematopoietic stem cells. The rapid proliferation of abnormal white blood cells in AML patients impairs the production of normal blood cells, significantly increasing the risk of infection, especially in those undergoing intensive chemotherapy or those who are severely immunocompromised with hyperuricemia. Aeromonas veronii is a small, gram-negative rod bacterium commonly found in water, seafood, meat, vegetables, and occasionally in the feces of healthy individuals [1]. In humans, it is mainly associated with gastrointestinal diseases, but it can also cause cellulitis, localized soft tissue infections, and septicemia in patients with compromised immunity [2]. Mortality rates of 24%–63% have been reported in patients with Aeromonas [3]. Additionally, antibiotic resistance in Aeromonas is increasing due to the overuse of antibiotics in agriculture, fish farming, and clinical settings [4], and this high mortality rate and resistance also suggest that clinicians should be vigilant, which necessitates vigilance for Aeromonas infections among clinicians. In catfish farming and shrimp industries, highly virulent clones, such as Aeromonas hydrophila ST251, have emerged [5], highlighting the importance of being vigilant for variant strains and highly virulent clones of single-cell bacteria in patients with a history of exposure to aquatic products. Consequently, next-generation sequencing (NGS), bacterial culture, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing are exceptionally important in clinical practice. Currently, extraintestinal infections caused by Aeromonas veronii, particularly those affecting the bloodstream and hepatobiliary system, are increasingly reported, warranting greater attention from clinicians.

In this case, the patient underwent peripheral blood NGS early on, and clinical imaging revealed a hepatic abscess with gas accumulation. Immediate liver abscess aspiration and antibiotic susceptibility testing were performed, and appropriate antibiotics were administered promptly. This approach prevented the progression of bacteremia to sepsis or even septic shock, leading to a relatively successful clinical outcome. In clinical practice, the majority of patients with hematologic diseases have compromised immune systems, making them a high-risk population for infection. Early attention to a patient's history of consuming or coming into contact with aquatic products, identification of infections caused by Aeromonas veronii, and administration of appropriate antibiotic treatment are crucial to preventing adverse clinical outcomes in patients with severe diseases.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old Chinese male presented to our Hematology clinic with symptoms of fever, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting. His medical history included a diagnosis of AML with AML1:ETO and ASXL positivity, identified as high risk, following a bone marrow aspiration performed at an outside hospital in March 2022. He had completed three cycles of chemotherapy and depicted elevated blood uric acid levels upon follow-up. The patient’s personal and family histories were otherwise unremarkable. On physical examination upon admission, he was found to be mildly anemic, with tenderness in the lower sternum, coarse breath sounds bilaterally, and tenderness in the liver area. No other significant abnormalities were observed. Laboratory tests at admission revealed a white blood cell count of 9.09 × 109/L, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, interleukin-6, hemoglobin, bilirubin, albumin, and uric acid levels of 127.72 mg/L, 2.908 ng/mL, 388.98 pg/mL, 52 g/L, 27.22 µmol/L, 32.5 g/L, and 452.8 µmol/L, respectively. Following admission, the patient received symptomatic supportive treatment and empirical anti-infection therapy with amikacin. However, his diarrhea and fever did not improve, and he experienced intermittent abdominal pain. An enhanced abdominal CT scan revealed multiple liver abscesses with gas formation and splenomegaly. Peripheral blood NGS indicated an infection with Aeromonas veronii. Immediate percutaneous drainage of the liver abscess was performed, and bacterial cultures and sensitivity tests were conducted on the aspirated fluid. As the patient had AML with agranulocytosis and Aeromonas veronii bacteremia, he was treated based on drug sensitivity results with the addition of a broad-spectrum antimicrobial drug. The treatment included a combination of ciprofloxacin and the broad-spectrum antibiotic cefoperazone-sulbactam for infection control, along with supportive measures such as blood transfusions. After stabilization, the patient received azacitidine in combination with the "Dry White Sand" regimen (interferon alpha-1B, interleukin 2, and thalidomide) for AML treatment.

Final diagnosis

AML (high-risk group) with Aeromonas veronii infection.

Results and follow-up

The patient's regimen of ciprofloxacin combined with cefoperazone-sulbactam effectively controlled the infection, as evidenced by a gradual decrease in temperature until it returned to normal, no further diarrhea, and reductions in bilirubin, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin levels compared to previous measurements. The drainage tube for the liver abscess ceased to drain fluid after 21 days, at which point it was removed. Given the patient’s diagnosis of AML with positive AML1:ETO and ASXL markers, classified as high risk, azacitidine was administered alongside the "Dry White Sand" regimen (interferon alpha-1B, interleukin-2, and thalidomide). However, the patient subsequently experienced bone marrow suppression, leading to the discontinuation of the "Dry White Sand" regimen. Repeat abdominal CT scans performed 16 and 28 days after antibiotic treatment revealed significant absorption of the liver abscess cavity compared to prior scans (Fig. 1B, C). The patient's condition improved, and treatment was deemed effective. The patient and their family requested discharge. Upon discharge, laboratory tests revealed a white blood cell count of 5.69 × 109/L, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, interleukin-6, hemoglobin, bilirubin, albumin, and uric acid levels of 102.18 mg/L, 0.3 ng/mL, 81.18 pg/mL, 91 g/L, 22.42 µmol/L, 40.2 g/L, and 452.8 µmol/L, respectively.

Discussion

This case report discusses a 23-year-old male previously diagnosed with relapsed acute AML who underwent three cycles of chemotherapy. Upon follow-up, the patient exhibited elevated blood uric acid levels and presented with fever, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting. Notably, there was a history of seafood consumption. The pathogenicity of Aeromonas veronii is multifactorial, involving various virulence factors such as exotoxins, extracellular enzymes, and adhesion molecules. These cytotoxins can lead to diarrhea and, in severe cases, hemorrhagic colitis. Studies indicate that antineoplastic drugs may induce changes in the intestinal mucosa, facilitating the translocation of colonizing bacteria from the gut to the circulatory system [7]. Therefore, patients with hematologic disorders and solid tumors, particularly those who have undergone multiple chemotherapy cycles, are at an increased risk of developing extraintestinal infections caused by Aeromonas veronii. In severe cases, this can result in septicemia, sepsis, and septic shock.

Reports indicate that Aeromonas veronii can cause extraintestinal infections, such as soft tissue infections and sepsis, in some immunocompromised patients, often associated with exposure to water or seafood consumption. For example, Charlotte L2. reported a 64-year-old Caucasian woman in 2022 with a medical history of hepatitis C-related cirrhosis, end-stage renal disease, blood dialysis, chronic anemia, and hypothyroidism. After falling into a pond and scraping her left forearm, she developed bloody blisters, along with fatigue, shortness of breath, and blurry vision three days later. Signs of septic shock subsequently appeared. Blood cultures revealed the Aeromonas veronii complex. Following symptomatic treatment, she received gentamicin, Levaquin, vancomycin, and Zosyn, leading to stabilization and discharge. Christoph Sproll [8] reported a 2022 case of a patient with oral squamous cell carcinoma who developed severe pneumonia and sepsis five days after receiving leech therapy. Microbiological examination of tracheal secretions and blood cultures revealed Aeromonas veronii, the same species isolated from the leeches used in treatment. Unfortunately, antimicrobials were not administered promptly, and the patient died from infectious shock. In recent years, extraintestinal infections of Aeromonas veronii have been increasingly reported, particularly in hematological and hepatobiliary systems. For instance, Manlio Monti [9] reported in 2022 a 64-year-old female patient with advanced pancreatic cancer and jaundice who presented with fever, abdominal pain, severe thrombocytopenia, and renal failure after percutaneous hepatic biliary drainage. Blood cultures confirmed Aeromonas veronii infection, and she was treated with levofloxacin and ceftazidime. The patient was later discharged in stable condition and continued oral antimicrobial treatment. Savino Spadaro [10] reported in 2014 a 74-year-old man with chronic lymphocytic leukemia and immune thrombocytopenia who regularly received steroids. He was admitted to intensive care with necrotizing fasciitis and multi-organ failure after being scratched by the dorsal fin spines of a black bullhead (Ameiurus melas). Blood cultures confirmed Aeromonas infection, but the patient ultimately died due to immunocompromise-induced comorbidities. In a retrospective study on Aeromonas bacteremia, Yao Sun [11] found that most patients had multiple underlying conditions, including cirrhosis in immunosuppressed states, diabetes, leukemia, and various malignancies.

The high incidence of Aeromonas veronii bacteremia in patients with cirrhosis, leukemia, and infectious diseases, along with the recurrence of bacteremia in some patients [12], underscores the need for heightened attention to food safety and wound hygiene in susceptible hosts. Given that bacterial infections are a significant cause of mortality in patients with AML, it is crucial to identify the pathogens that pose particular risks to this population.

According to the Sanford Guide to Antimicrobial Therapy [13], Aeromonas veronii is primarily found in contaminated soil, water sources, and the feces of healthy individuals. While it mainly causes human diarrhea, it can also affect the skin, joints, respiratory tract, urinary system, endocardium, peritoneum, eyes, and other body parts, potentially leading to septicemia and sepsis. Although Aeromonas veronii primarily exists in water and soil environments, it can rapidly colonize various ecological niches and hosts, demonstrating significant intestinal colonization ability in humans and animals, resulting in opportunistic infections [14, 15]. While Aeromonas veronii infections in humans are relatively rare compared to other bacterial infections, the incidence of bloodstream infections is notably higher in Asia compared to Europe [5, 16].

Reports indicate that Aeromonas veronii is sensitive to aminoglycosides, quinolones, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and amikacin. However, reports also indicate that Aeromonas ability to produce at least three types of β-lactamases, and studies by Xavier Bertran [17] and Chi-Jung Wu [12] confirmed that Aeromonas veronii tends to produce class B carbapenemases and class D penicillinases, while not producing class C cephalosporinases. Consequently, it is typically resistant to penicillin, carbapenems, and first- and second-generation cephalosporins but is sensitive to third-generation cephalosporins [1]. Sulbactam, a beta-lactamase inhibitor, irreversibly inhibits most β-lactamases produced by resistant strains. Moreover, sulbactam has been reported to have a significant synergistic effect with cefoperazone, resulting in a reduced MIC value compared to the individual components [18]. In this case, the patient presented with fever and diarrhea, raising suspicion of Aeromonas veronii infection. Initial treatment with levofloxacin was administered, and Aeromonas veronii infection was confirmed through peripheral blood NGS. Subsequently, empirical treatment with ceftazidime-sulbactam 300 mg every 8 h intravenously and amikacin 400 mg every 12 h intravenously was initiated. As the patient exhibited abnormalities in liver and kidney function, treatment was adjusted to ciprofloxacin 200 mg every 12 h intravenously, in combination with ceftazidime-sulbactam 300 mg every 8 h, following the results of liver abscess puncture fluid culture and drug sensitivity testing. The patient's clinical symptoms improved, and no more fluid was drained from the liver abscess drainage tube.

The findings highlight the importance of inquiring about medical history and classifying high-risk patients in clinical practice. For immunocompromised patients with a history of water contact, empirical antibiotic therapy should be promptly administered with full coverage for Aeromonas veronii. Additionally, clinical isolates must undergo antibiotic sensitivity testing, and antibiotics should be administered based on these results. Literature indicates that third-generation cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole are the most effective treatments and should be considered for empirical therapy [2, 19, 20]. Early clinical suspicion and treatment of suspected patients can significantly reduce incidence and mortality rates, improve patient prognosis, and lower the risk of complications and sequelae.
