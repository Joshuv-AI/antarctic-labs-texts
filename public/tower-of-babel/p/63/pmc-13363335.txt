Reassessing Ethical Considerations in Pediatric Facial Transplantation: Where Are We Now?
Authors: Matteo Laspro, Erika Thys, Thor S. Stead, Ogechukwu C. Onuh, Hailey P. Wyatt, Alexis K. Gursky, Eduardo D. Rodriguez, Laura L. Kimberly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Many strides have been made in the field of vascular composite allotransplantation, and some vascular composite allotransplantation procedures, including upper extremity and facial transplantation, are arguably nearing the standard of care for adult recipients. Although centers devoted to pediatric upper extremity transplantation have been established, very little recent scholarship has addressed the advisability of initiating pediatric facial transplantation (PFTx).Methods:This conceptual ethical analysis assesses the present risks and benefits associated with PFTx based on a review of the literature combined with our team’s clinical experience.Results:Considerations for PFTx include anticipated improvements in quality of life and social integration during a critically important formative developmental phase, ongoing risks associated with immunosuppression and known challenges with nonadherence in adolescent and young adult populations, unknowns about facial growth and continued incorporation of the allograft, the likelihood of need for retransplantation, and issues around decisional capacity and the ability to provide informed consent.Conclusions:Weighing the risks and benefits of PFTx, particularly the risks associated with long-term immunosuppression and the likely need for retransplantation, we believe that the reservations are such that pediatric patients should not be considered candidates for PFTx at this time.

Takeaways

Question: What are the risks and benefits of initiating pediatric facial transplantation, and should pediatric patients be considered candidates at this time?

Findings: Although potential benefits include improved quality of life and social integration during critical developmental years, concerns remain about long-term immunosuppression, the high nonadherence risk in adolescents, unknown effects on facial growth, the likely need for retransplantation, and challenges with informed consent and decisional capacity.

Meaning: Given the significant medical, psychosocial, and ethical reservations, pediatric patients should not be considered candidates for facial transplantation at this time.

INTRODUCTION

The field of vascular composite allotransplantation (VCA) has evolved during the past several decades, achieving successful outcomes for hand, uterus, and face transplantation (FTx).1–5 During this time, VCA in pediatric recipients (individuals younger than 18 y old) has become a reality, with 2 pediatric hand transplantation (PHTx) cases documented.6,7 Although pediatric facial transplantation (PFTx) remains hypothetical, benefits seen in adult FTx recipients may translate to children; thus, the procedure holds promise for functional and psychosocial improvements in children with severe facial injury or congenital defects. However, PFTx also confers serious risks, including acute and chronic graft rejection, the necessity for lifelong immunosuppression and attendant risks of infection, organ toxicity, higher odds of malignancy development, and graft loss.8,9 Further, recovery and follow-up are arduous, requiring patients to adhere to a complex pharmaceutical and rehabilitation regimen.

Although surgical techniques used in adult and pediatric cases may be similar, pediatric VCA poses additional considerations, including children’s ability to consent or assent to surgery and lifelong immunosuppression, anatomical growth of the transplanted graft, anticipated graft lifespan, and need for retransplantation. Moreover, psychosocial considerations change according to pediatric developmental stages. As much as these concerns have been discussed in PHTx, PFTx ethical paradigms differ in meaningful ways that merit deliberation. Indeed, early conversations in the field reflect a lack of consensus about the circumstances under which PFTx could be undertaken.10–16 This analysis revisits considerations raised nearly a decade ago and seeks to determine whether PFTx is advisable in light of recent developments in the field by evaluating its risks and benefits through the bioethical principles of autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice.17,18

POTENTIAL BENEFITS OF PFTx

Quality-of-life Improvement

Improvement in overall functional and psychosocial quality of life (QoL) is the primary goal of FTx and aligns with the principle of beneficence.18,19 Although FTx is considered life-enhancing rather than lifesaving, improvements in QoL, particularly the possibility of social reintegration, are often substantial enough that VCA has been described as a remedy for “social death.”20–23 For example, studies have demonstrated that following FTx, patients experience reduced depressive symptoms, fewer negative verbal comments, and decreased social anxiety.24 Despite these results, it is important to recognize the importance of incorporating patients’ perspectives about how to prioritize QoL outcomes, particularly given that patients’ and providers’ considerations of what aspects of QoL are important may differ.22,25,26 How pediatric recipients’ values and goals for QoL should be integrated into medical decision-making for PFTx, including the consistency or variability of their perspectives over time, is unknown. Thus, although it is uncertain whether FTx in adults will correlate directly with PFTx outcomes, data regarding pediatric facial disfigurement suggest the positive effect of facial reconstruction on mental health outcomes.27

Functional Considerations

Adults have reported a desire to regain a sense of self and bodily integrity after undergoing FTx and improving their functional outcomes.22 This is particularly true for patients with facial trauma and for those who experience difficulties with breathing, chewing, swallowing, or speaking.28 Nasal orifice reconstruction often returns the patient’s sense of both smell and taste, thereby improving QoL.28 Facial musculature restoration facilitates the return of facial expressions and speech, which are necessary for social interaction, communication, and expressing emotions.28 Restoration of the masticatory muscles and oral cavity allows for tracheal decannulation or even oral food intake, thereby mitigating malnutrition.28 Based on adult FTx recipients’ experiences, it is likely that pediatric recipients would also experience significant functional improvement from PFTx above what is currently available through standard reconstructive options. Though there are no data to directly support potential functional gains in pediatric recipients of PFTx, benefits can be extrapolated from adult FTx data as well as from similar scenarios such as pediatric facial reanimation, where reanimation procedures have been shown to provide durable functional and favorable QoL outcomes beyond 10 years.29,30

Psychosocial Considerations

Pediatric patients navigate complex developmental stages and social contexts. Children and teenagers younger than 18 years report higher rates of anxiety, body image issues, and social stigma.15 This is further compounded by visible defects or physical disabilities, particularly in obvious facial differences that may impair social interactions.28,31 The location of the facial disfigurement can determine the response others have to their appearance, as stronger reactions occur when the disfigurement is located centrally on the face.32

A study by Masnari et al33 showed that children without craniofacial defects are more likely to attribute characteristics such as being unpopular, ugly, or “bad at school” and report less willingness to befriend a child with a craniofacial difference. Data from multiple studies have shown that children with visible disfigurements are often stared at, whispered about, or subjected to bullying, which can lead to smaller peer groups, more behavioral problems, underachievement in school, and constrained career aspirations.32,34,35 Children with visible disfigurements have also been reported in previous studies to have higher levels of depression, anxiety, and social isolation than their peers.32,34,36,37

There is considerable literature describing how reconstructive surgery may mitigate the social harms of appearance-altering features such as burns, congenital malformations, or those resulting from trauma.32–36,38–40 In view of the social complexity of childhood, adolescence, and early adulthood, as well as data from adult FTx recipients who largely report significant improvement in social reintegration, the potential psychosocial benefits of PFTx are profound and could truly alter the course of a child’s life during an incredibly socially formative time.22,41

POTENTIAL RISKS OF PFTx

Immunosuppression Risks

Infection

Reports on adult VCA suggest that approximately 58% of patients have experienced opportunistic infections after surgery due to immunosuppression.42 These can be life-threatening conditions in immunosuppressed patients, as illustrated by a VCA patient who died after a multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas infection.43 Infection with such pathogens has been reported extensively in the FTx literature, and although the majority of cases are managed successfully, risks still exist.42–44 Hence, PFTx patients would likely develop an infection over the course of their lives. Even in solid organ transplantation (SOT), the incidence of infections is greater in pediatric recipients compared with their adult counterparts, as children may not have reached immunological maturity from natural exposure and, depending on the patient’s age, may not have completed their primary immunization series at the time of surgery.45 From the limited literature on pediatric VCA, an 18-month follow-up case report indicated more than 1 systemic infection in the postoperative period.46 Although none were fatal, infection has the potential to impact the integrity of the allograft. This can warrant allograft removal in cases of necrosis, which would be considerably more complicated in PFTx than in PHTx.

Malignancy

Another concern is the risk of malignancy. From the adult VCA literature, an increased lifetime incidence of certain neoplastic pathologies is well documented.47,48 For instance, although rare in the general population, posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) is a recurrent source of concern for VCA surgeons due to its aggressive and often fatal disease course. In a recent report of 48 cases of FTx, 2 (4.2%) patients died from PTLD, which is a higher rate than that observed in kidney transplantation (1.9%).47,48 From the SOT literature, rates of pediatric PTLD are as high as 20% after transplantation.49 Although the condition has not been observed in the pediatric hand transplant case performed, if pediatric VCA follows similar patterns, rates are expected to be higher, especially considering that recipients would live with an immunosuppressive regimen for a longer period of time than their adult counterparts.

Organ Damage and Potential Need for SOT

Apart from the long-term issues associated with immunosuppression, immune-modulating medications included in facial transplantation regimens may have an adverse impact on other organ systems, especially in pediatric patients where cumulative effects of medications are increased. Tacrolimus, for example, is known to be severely nephrotoxic, with patients on long-term treatment requiring constant monitoring of blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels. In fact, some adult VCA patients have developed end-stage kidney disease due to tacrolimus-based immunosuppression and required dialysis.50,51 One such patient required renal transplantation, whereas a second succumbed to immunosuppression-induced renal dysfunction.1,51 Although tacrolimus is a mainstay in VCA due to its prohealing properties and facilitation of nerve regeneration, the risks of tacrolimus in a pediatric patient are substantial, especially if kidney dysfunction does emerge.52,53

Given the toxicity and risks of immunosuppression in VCA, there has been some promising evidence regarding the minimization of systemic immunosuppression via topical application of localized immunosuppression for skin-containing allografts, as well as steroid weaning and a dual, rather than triple, maintenance immunosuppression regimen.54,55 Furthermore, significant preclinical data in animals show successful graft tolerance via chimerism, although clinical data are still lacking.56 With continued work in immunosuppression minimization and tolerance strategies, a more favorable landscape for PFTx could emerge.

Allograft Rejection and Need for Retransplantation

Adverse outcomes have been reported in approximately 29% of adult FTx recipients, including acute or chronic rejection, allograft loss, and death due to infection, malignancy, or immunosuppression noncompliance.44,57,58 These risks may be amplified in pediatric patients, whose physiological response to allotransplantation may be less predictable. Of the 2 pediatric VCA procedures reported, only 1 was performed between genetically dissimilar individuals.6 According to the only true pediatric VCA case, the patient demonstrated no evidence of chronic rejection, but over the course of his 18-month follow-up, reported 8 episodes of acute rejection.46 Although each episode was successfully treated with steroids, the frequency of rejection raises concerns that pediatric immune responses may differ from those of adults and could require distinct management strategies.

Moreover, advances in immunomodulation have improved allograft survival, including noninvasive rejection monitoring using blood- and urine-based biomarkers and reduced reliance on steroid-based regimens.59–62 Although such parameters stress the risk/benefit ratio in an adult population, for pediatric individuals, concerns regarding allograft longevity open the likelihood for retransplantation. The prospect of graft removal and subsequent retransplantation is a more complex and ethically fraught consideration in PFTx than for PHTx. Only 2 cases of facial retransplantation have been reported—that of a 52-year-old woman who experienced unsalvageable graft vasculopathy, and that of a 43-year-old man whose facial allograft had to be removed 6 months before receiving a new allograft better matched to his genetic profile.63,64 Given their longer life expectancy, pediatric recipients may face a lifelong commitment to revision surgery, rehabilitation, follow-up, and the burden associated with graft failure.

Allograft Growth

Another unknown is whether the allograft would follow the recipient’s innate developmental growth pattern after the procedure is performed. A transplanted limb that does not follow growth patterns may not serve its original purpose of restoring form and function to the recipient. In the first case of PHTx, long-term outcomes demonstrated cortical integration of the limb into the recipient’s neuronal circuitry, suggesting bodily “comprehension” of the presence of the transplanted limb.65 A subsequent 4-year follow-up study assessing rates of growth in the transplanted limb confirmed the theory that allograft growth followed the predetermined rate expected for the recipient based on his age and demographics.2

When the last ethical analysis of PFTx was published in 2016,14 data were promising for allograft growth in PHTx; however, the possibility must be acknowledged that craniofacial growth may follow different patterns in pediatric patients younger than 18 years. Studies analyzing pediatric reconstruction of the face may inform consideration of PFTx in the future. Particularly in fibular reconstruction of the mandible, cephalometric evaluations assessed whether fibular growth paralleled that of the intact contralateral mandibular segment and found no statistical differences in expansion, suggesting that local reconstruction allows for growth in pediatric surgery.66,67 Although FTx is a more extensive surgery than fibular reconstruction, nothing currently published suggests that the pediatric response to a larger graft would differ.68,69 Nonetheless, there are no clinical data to confirm allograft growth trajectories in PFTx. Questions remain regarding the potential risks of malalignment, growth disparities, and the possibility of secondary reconstruction.54

Medical Nonadherence

The transition from childhood to adolescence presents developmental challenges for medical adherence. Although parents retain decisional authority and may consent to transplantation on behalf of their children, as recipients progress toward adulthood and become increasingly independent, their adherence to immunosuppressive medication tends to decrease, potentially jeopardizing the viability of the transplanted organ.70 The reasons for decreases in adherence are multifaceted.71 From an intrapersonal standpoint, a diagnosis of depression has been associated with a lack of medical follow-up and adherence.72 From an interpersonal and familial perspective, young people experiencing conflict with care figures, as well as intraparental discord, were correlated with worse SOT outcomes.73 In pediatric populations with chronic health conditions requiring medications, rates of medical nonadherence ranged from 50% to 88%. For example, in the case of kidney transplantation, medication nonadherence was reported at 68% for people younger than 21 years old, compared with 32% for individuals older than 21 years.74–76 As children age, the combination of pressures to conform socially and establish independence from parents/guardians and other adult authority figures (such as clinicians) leaves young people more vulnerable to becoming medically nonadherent.75 This dynamic is particularly troubling in PFTx, considering documented challenges with adherence in adult FTx recipients and associated adverse outcomes.77 Social support for PFTx patients would likely require an even more conservative approach than for adult FTx recipients, with implications for equity that would be difficult to mediate.6,78,79

Informed Assent and Pediatric Decisional Capacity

Ensuring informed consent/assent is challenging in PFTx, given the potentially life-limiting nature of the procedure and its sequelae. When children lack decisional capacity, authorization rests with legal guardians, particularly in younger patients who cannot meaningfully grasp the long-term consequences of transplantation.

Even in adolescents who can participate in medical decision-making, the stakes are exceptionally high to justify substituting the judgment of a surrogate decision-maker if the patient does not yet have full decisional capacity. Although parents may act in their child’s best interest, narratives of resentment toward parental surgical decisions are well described, especially for interventions affecting bodily identity.11,80,81 For example, this has been documented in relation to intersex gender surgery at a young age.80 In PFTx, evolving current and future identity may further complicate assent.81 Most concerning is permitting surrogate decision-making for a procedure with a substantial risk of life-altering and potentially life-limiting risk.82

DONOR CONSIDERATIONS

Although a thorough discussion of donor considerations is beyond the scope of this article, the following points regarding authorization, identity concerns, and public trust in pediatric donation are discussed. Children younger than 16 years cannot legally provide premortem intent to donate, and only some states allow children aged 16–18 years to register intent with parental approval. Thus, authorization for pediatric donation generally rests with parents or legal guardians.

Experience from adult FTx suggests that parental decision-making may be influenced by misconceptions that a donor child’s appearance or identity could be transferred to the recipient. However, data demonstrate that FTx recipients neither resemble their donors nor associate their identity with donor appearance.83–85

Public attitudes further complicate donor availability. Although support for solid organ donation in the United States is generally high, hesitation toward VCA donation, including the face, has been documented.86 Although no data exist regarding public acceptance of PFTx donors, pediatric solid organ donation literature indicates that parents who decline donation cite concerns about protecting their child’s body, uncertainty regarding organ viability, and the desire to allow their child to die peacefully.87,88 In contrast, parents who consent to donation often do so to honor their child’s wishes or to make something good out of traumatic loss. These motivations may extend to PFTx but are likely compounded by the symbolic and visible nature of facial donation. Thus, the donor pool for PFTx may be smaller than for adult FTx, especially given human leukocyte antigen and skeletal maturity matching requirements, and the added risks may further limit acceptance. Future work should assess societal acceptability.89

LIMITATIONS

This is a conceptual ethical analysis that reviews the published literature and draws on our institutional experience in FTx. Given the absence of PFTx cases, our ethical assessment relies on extrapolation from adult FTx and the limited number of pediatric VCA cases. Although we do not currently recommend PFTx, the risk-benefit ratio may change with advances in immunosuppression and allograft survival. Therefore, our recommendations and conclusions should be reassessed as new evidence emerges.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Although the potential benefits of PFTx for young people merit careful consideration, at this time the risks associated with PFTx continue to be serious and potentially life-limiting. Recommendations have been made for pediatric patients to wait to undergo VCA until they are deemed mature enough to fully comprehend the risks of surgery, the immunosuppressive regimen required, and potential postoperative outcomes, including discussions about the need for retransplantation and adverse health consequences associated with immunosuppression.26 Despite advances in adult FTx over the past 2 decades, major reservations persist for pediatric patients, especially for those younger than 16 years of age, thus leading us to conclude that PFTx should not be undertaken at this time—not until the risk-benefit ratio becomes more favorable, either through advances in immunosuppression or strategies to greatly increase allograft lifespan. For these reasons, the authors do not recommend a clinical pathway for PFTx, as such guidance could be misconstrued as endorsing ethical permissibility despite persistent, unresolved risks. Even for candidates at or just above the threshold of adulthood, great care should be taken to support the patient and their family in understanding possible complications, required postsurgical immunosuppression, and the possible need for retransplantation. Potential candidates must display emotional maturity and exhibit full decisional capacity. In addition, providers should be familiar with not only the surgical techniques involved in FTx but also the specific challenges around medical adherence for very young adults and strategies to best support these recipients and their care partners.
