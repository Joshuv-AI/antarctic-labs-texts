Perioperative mortality and survival after lymph node excision in hepatocellular carcinoma: a national cancer database propensity score–matched cohort study
Authors: Huisong Lee, Kyung Uk Jung, Michael Luu, Kambiz Kosari, Nicholas Nissen, Ju Dong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe oncologic benefit of lymph node excision (LNE) during hepatectomy for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains controversial. This study examined the association between LNE, perioperative mortality, and long-term survival following curative-intent liver resection.MethodsThe National Cancer Database (2004–2021) was queried to identify patients who underwent curative hepatectomy for HCC. Patients who underwent liver transplantation, palliative surgery, or had distant metastases were excluded. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to balance patients undergoing hepatectomy with and without LNE. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with 30- and 90-day mortality. Overall survival (OS) was compared among patients according to LNE.ResultsAfter matching (n = 8,728), all PSM-matched variables achieved standardized mean differences < 0.10, confirming excellent balance. LNE was independently associated with increased perioperative mortality. Removal of 1–3 lymph nodes was associated with increased odds of 30- and 90-day mortality (OR 1.37 and 1.38, respectively), while removal of ≥ 4 nodes conferred higher risk (OR 1.64 and 1.67, respectively). Older age, greater comorbidity burden, and advanced T stage were also associated with increased postoperative mortality. In survival analyses, OS did not differ significantly between No-LNE and pN0 groups (P = 0.151), with comparable 5-year survival rates and median survival.ConclusionsIn this national cohort, LNE performed during curative hepatectomy for HCC was associated with increased postoperative mortality, without an apparent long-term survival benefit in patients with node-negative disease. These findings should be interpreted with caution given the observational design and inherent indication bias.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12957-026-04497-y.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Lymph node metastasis, Lymph node excision, Survival rate, Mortality

Introduction

Management of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has progressed across multiple treatment modalities, including surgical resection, liver transplantation, transarterial chemoembolization, radiofrequency ablation, and radiotherapy [1–5]. However, a standardized treatment approach for patients with suspected lymph node metastasis (LNM) has not been established. In the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th edition, regional LNM is classified as stage IVa irrespective of T stage, reflecting its association with adverse survival outcomes [6].

The clinical role of lymph node excision (LNE)—defined in this study as any surgical removal and pathological examination of regional lymph nodes as recorded in the NCDB, encompassing a spectrum from targeted sampling to more extensive regional dissection— remains unclear [7, 8]. Studies showing that localized LNM demonstrates different survival patterns compared with distant metastasis (M1) suggest that further examination of LNE in selected clinical scenarios is warranted [9]. At the same time, LNE may increase operative morbidity and mortality, indicating the need for outcome-based evaluation. Moreover, systemic treatment options for HCC with LNM are limited, and the therapeutic benefit of LNE beyond its role in staging remains uncertain [10–12].

This study compares short-term mortality and long-term survival between patients who underwent LNE and those who did not. The National Cancer Database (NCDB) was used to ensure an adequate sample size, as LNE is not commonly performed in HCC surgery. Because patients selected for LNE may present with more advanced disease characteristics, propensity score matching was applied to reduce measurable confounding. Additionally, we assess the prognostic significance of LNE across histological subtypes with a higher propensity for LNM, such as fibrolamellar HCC and combined HCC-cholangiocarcinoma (HCC-CCA) [13, 14].

Patients and methods

Study design and patient selection

This study utilized publicly available, deidentified NCDB data, which was exempt from institutional review board. From 2004 to 2021, a total of 348,087 cases were registered under liver neoplasms. Benign neoplasms, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, non-surgical cases, and cases with distant metastasis were excluded. Because cirrhosis is prevalent among patients with HCC, liver function may affect survival independently of tumor-related factors. Liver transplantation, which is generally performed in patients with compromised hepatic function, was excluded to reduce the influence of liver dysfunction on survival analysis. Patients with metastases beyond regional lymph nodes were also excluded to isolate the association between LNE and oncologic outcomes. After excluding liver transplantation and palliative procedures among surgically treated patients, 21,733 individuals who underwent curative-intent liver resection were included. Patient selection adhered to previously described methodology [15].

Variables analyzed

The study analyzed a range of demographic, clinical, and pathological variables, including:

Demographics: Age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, income, geographic location, and Charlson Deyo comorbidity index.

Institutional factors: Facility type.

Institutional factors: Facility type.

Clinical factors: Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels.

Clinical factors: Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels.

Pathological factors: Pathological T stage (pT stage), pathological N stage (pN stage), fibrosis, histologic grade, and histological subtypes.

Pathological factors: Pathological T stage (pT stage), pathological N stage (pN stage), fibrosis, histologic grade, and histological subtypes.

Propensity score matching

Therefore, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed based on LNE status. Of the 21,733 patients, 3,376 (15%) underwent LNE. To account for the imbalance in group size, a 3:1 nearest-neighbor matching ratio was applied. Matching variables included age, sex, race, education level, income, geographic location, treatment at an academic facility, histologic type, pT stage, and the Charlson–Deyo comorbidity index. Variables with more than 40% of missing data, such as MELD score (missing in 69%), AFP elevation (missing in 54%), and fibrosis (missing in 62%), were excluded from the propensity score model. This threshold was determined a priori. Because these variables are associated with both surgical complexity and prognosis, their exclusion represents a potential source of residual confounding, acknowledged as a key limitation of this study. Covariate balance between the No-LNE and LNE groups before and after PSM was assessed using the standardized mean difference (SMD). SMD was calculated as the absolute difference in group means (for continuous variables) or proportions (for binary variables) divided by the pooled standard deviation or pooled proportion standard deviation, respectively. An SMD < 0.10 was considered to indicate adequate covariate balance.

Statistical analysis

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause or last follow-up. Patients with unknown survival status or survival time (coded as 999 in the NCDB) were excluded from survival analyses, resulting in an analytic cohort of 8,123 patients. OS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with survival curves constructed for three groups: No-LNE, pN0, and pN1. The number of patients at risk at each time point (0, 50, 100, 150, and 200 months) was displayed beneath each survival curve. Pointwise 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Greenwood variance formula with the complementary log-log (log-log) transformation. Censoring events were indicated by tick marks on each curve. Between-group comparisons of OS were performed using the two-sided log-rank test. Subgroup analyses were performed separately for each major histologic subtype — HCC, not otherwise specified (NOS); fibrolamellar carcinoma; combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma (HCC-CCA); and clear cell type — using the same methodology. Subtypes with fewer than two patients in a given group were excluded from that comparison.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Matching quality was assessed using SMDs < 0.10 indicating adequate balance. A complete-case sensitivity analysis was performed using multivariable logistic regression restricted to patients with available AFP and fibrosis data, adjusting for these additional variables to assess robustness. Figures were generated using the matplotlib library (version 3.x) in Python. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Before PSM, significant differences were observed between the non-LNE (n = 17,894) and LNE (n = 3,376) groups across multiple demographic and clinical variables. Patients in the LNE group were younger and had different distributions of sex, comorbidity burden, race and ethnicity, treatment facility type, and geographic region. Tumor-related factors—including clinical N stage, pT stage, grade, AFP status, fibrosis, and histologic subtype—also differed, with the LNE group demonstrating higher proportions of advanced disease features. MELD scores and socioeconomic variables were largely similar between groups, although missing data were substantial for several variables.

After 3:1 PSM, 6,546 non-LNE and 2,182 LNE patients were included in the matched cohort. All 13 variables included in the matching algorithm achieved SMD < 0.10, confirming excellent covariate balance (Table 1). The largest improvement was observed for pT stage (SMD: 0.382 → 0.053) and histologic type (SMD: 0.212 → 0.013). Variables excluded from the matching model—cN stage (SMD 0.179) and fibrosis (SMD 0.199)—remained imbalanced as expected, given their intentional exclusion due to > 40% missing data.

Factors associated with 30- and 90-day mortality

Before propensity score matching, the 30- and 90-day mortality rates were 3.9% and 6.6% in the non-LNE group, respectively, compared with 5.2% and 9.7% in the LNE group. After propensity score matching, the 30- and 90-day mortality rates in the non-LNE group were 4.0% and 7.4%, respectively, whereas those in the LNE group were 5.5% and 10.2%.

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, several factors were independently associated with early mortality after hepatectomy for HCC including LNE which demonstrated a consistent and significant association with both 30-day and 90-day mortality. Compared with the non-LNE group, removal of 1–3 lymph nodes increased the odds of 30-day mortality (OR 1.369; 95% CI 1.059–1.770; P = 0.016) and 90-day mortality (OR 1.380; 95% CI 1.133–1.680; P = 0.001). Removal of ≥ 4 nodes further increased the risk (30-day: OR 1.643; 95% CI 1.088–2.479; P = 0.018; 90-day: OR 1.674; 95% CI 1.223–2.290; P = 0.001). These findings indicate a stepwise increase in short-term mortality with greater extent of nodal removal. Other variables associated with higher mortality included older age (per-year increase: OR 1.022–1.024; both P < 0.001), higher comorbidity burden (Charlson–Deyo score 3: OR 2.19–2.51; both P < 0.001), and advanced T stage (T3 and T4 showing significantly elevated odds for both 30- and 90-day mortality). Female sex and treatment at academic facilities were associated with lower mortality at both time points. Overall, LNE was one of the strongest predictors of early postoperative mortality, independent of age, comorbidities, tumor stage, and other clinical factors. (Table 2).

Overall survival

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated distinct survival patterns according to LNE status and pathologic nodal classification. The No-LNE and pN0 groups showed comparable OS, with no statistically significant difference between the two curves (log-rank, P = 0.151). The No-LNE group showed 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of 83.6%, 62.2%, and 55.9%, respectively, with a median survival of 55.9 months. Similarly, the pN0 group demonstrated 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of 80.1%, 60.0%, and 48.0%, and a median survival of 55.0 months. In contrast, patients with pN1 disease exhibited substantially poorer survival outcomes. The 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates were 57.2%, 28.3%, and 19.8%, respectively, with a median survival of 13.5 months, indicating a marked reduction in long-term survival compared with both No-LNE and pN0 groups. Overall survival in histological subtypes

These subtype-specific analyses were performed as hypothesis-generating explorations and should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample sizes, which resulted in wide confidence intervals and insufficient power for definitive conclusions. Subtype-specific Kaplan–Meier analyses demonstrated that No-LNE and pN0 groups had comparable OS across all evaluated histologic subtypes. There was no statistically significant difference in HCC, not otherwise specified (NOS), fibrolamellar type, HCC-CCA, and clear cell type (all P > 0.05). In HCC, NOS, the largest subgroup, pN1 disease was associated with significantly poorer survival compared with both No-LNE and pN0 groups, consistent with the findings in the overall cohort. A similar pattern was observed in HCC-CCA, where pN1 patients demonstrated markedly reduced long-term survival relative to the other groups. For the clear cell subtype, no pN1 cases were identified, precluding comparison involving node-positive disease, but survival curves for No-LNE and pN0 remained closely aligned. In the fibrolamellar subtype, although statistical significance was not reached due to limited sample size, the pN0 group showed a trend toward improved long-term survival compared with No-LNE, with a greater proportion of long-term survivors observed (P = 0.188).

Taken together, these analyses consistently show that omission of LNE does not result in inferior long-term survival compared with confirmed pN0 status across all major HCC histologic subtypes, while pN1 disease demonstrates distinctly worse outcomes. (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical significance of LNE in patients undergoing curative-intent hepatectomy for HCC, using the NCDB—which captures approximately 70% of newly diagnosed cancers in the United States—with PSM applied to improve baseline comparability [5]. A major finding was the strong association between LNE and increased 30- and 90-day mortality. Even limited removal of 1–3 lymph nodes increased early mortality, and removal of ≥ 4 nodes further augmented this risk. This stepwise pattern suggests that the physiological burden of nodal dissection may be associated with higher early postoperative mortality, though residual confounding from unmeasured surgical complexity cannot be excluded. These associations remained significant after multivariable adjustment; however, given the inherent limitations of observational data, a definitive causal interpretation is not supported. Other predictors of early mortality included older age, male sex, a Charlson–Deyo comorbidity index of 3, and advanced T stage, identifying patient populations in whom the risks of LNE may be particularly pronounced. Conversely, female sex and treatment at academic facilities were associated with reduced postoperative mortality, highlighting the potential protective effects of institutional expertise and perioperative care quality.

The technical complexity of LNE likely contributes to its adverse outcomes. Dissection near major vascular structures can increase operative time, blood loss, and postoperative complications, risks that are amplified in the presence of impaired liver function or hilar varices. Extensive lymphadenectomy may also compromise hepatic perfusion through unintended ligation of small arterial or portal venous branches. In a randomized controlled trial reported by Wu et al., which included a relatively small cohort of 85 patients, no significant differences in morbidity or mortality were observed between patients who underwent lymph node excision and those who did not [16]. Mazzotta et al. demonstrated, using a prospective multicenter registry, that complication rates increase proportionally with the complexity classification of liver resection; procedures requiring additional operative steps were associated with higher complication rates, suggesting that conventional complexity scores may underestimate the true operative burden when adjunctive procedures such as LNE are performed [17]. This evidence supports the view that LNE should be considered an independent contributor to surgical risk, and that complexity scoring systems for hepatectomy may benefit from incorporating nodal dissection as a distinct complexity-modifying factor. Prior studies showing worse outcomes following invasive diagnostic interventions, including preoperative biopsy, further support the concept that additional operative manipulation may be detrimental in HCC [11, 18]. Collectively, these observations support a selective rather than routine approach to LNE, particularly in patients with advanced age, significant comorbidity, or advanced tumor stage.

Long-term survival analysis demonstrated no meaningful difference between the No-LNE and pN0 groups in the overall cohort or within any histologic subtype. Median survival was nearly identical (55.9 vs. 55.0 months), and no subtype showed a survival advantage associated with LNE. These findings contrast with other gastrointestinal malignancies, where nodal staging guides adjuvant therapy and risk stratification. In HCC, where intrahepatic and hematogenous spread predominate and effective node-directed therapies are lacking, the prognostic value of confirming nodal negativity appears minimal. Earlier studies reporting poor long-term outcomes after LNE were likely influenced by selection bias, as LNE was more often performed in patients with suspicious or advanced disease [19, 20]. Moreover, prior research suggests potential survival disparities across racial groups, underscoring the need for further investigations to elucidate the underlying biological and socioeconomic determinants of these differences [21, 22]. In this study, PSM reduced measurable confounding, though residual indication bias cannot be excluded. Nonetheless, no survival advantage of LNE was observed in node-negative patients. An important caveat is the probable presence of occult nodal disease within the No-LNE cohort: patients who did not undergo LNE were not pathologically staged, and an unknown proportion may harbor undetected nodal metastases. Clinical nodal staging has high specificity but low sensitivity for pathological nodal involvement, meaning many true pN1 patients are classified as cN0. The survival equivalence between No-LNE and pN0 should therefore not be interpreted as definitive evidence that LNE provides no oncologic benefit, but rather reflects the net population-level effect in the absence of effective node-directed therapies.

Subtype analyses further reinforced these findings. In fibrolamellar carcinoma, the pN0 group showed a trend toward improved long-term survival, although statistical significance was not reached. This likely reflects the inherently indolent biology of fibrolamellar tumors rather than any therapeutic effect of nodal dissection. However, given the higher rates of lymph node metastasis reported in fibrolamellar HCC, further dedicated research is warranted [15, 23, 24]. The absence of pN1 disease in clear cell carcinoma underscores the rarity of nodal involvement in certain biologically distinct variants and suggests that LNE provides minimal diagnostic benefit in these subtypes. Together, these results underscore that routine LNE offers limited prognostic or therapeutic advantage in HCC.

Although LNE did not improve survival among node-negative patients, pN1 status remained a powerful adverse prognostic factor, with a median survival of only 13.5 months across relevant subtypes. However, because the detection of nodal metastasis does not currently influence adjuvant treatment decisions, the clinical utility of identifying pN1 disease is limited. The potential prognostic value must therefore be balanced against the increased perioperative risks associated with LNE.

In clinical practice, LNE is typically performed either as a targeted procedure for radiologically suspicious or palpably enlarged nodes, or as a component of complex hilar dissection. The NCDB does not capture preoperative imaging findings or the intraoperative rationale for LNE, precluding determination of whether LNE was performed based on clinical suspicion versus as a routine step. Our prior study using the same dataset demonstrated that clinical nodal staging (cN1) has high specificity but low sensitivity for pathological nodal involvement, consistent with findings from prior nodal staging investigations. These findings underscore the importance of selective LNE guided by tumor biology—including fibrolamellar histology, combined HCC-cholangiocarcinoma, advanced T stage, and high tumor grade—rather than routine nodal dissection in all patients.

In this study, pN stage was incorporated into the propensity score model, whereas cN stage was not included. We elected not to adjust for cN stage given its known limitations in accurately reflecting true nodal involvement in HCC. Prior investigations have demonstrated substantial discordance between clinical and pathologic nodal staging, likely attributable to the limited sensitivity and specificity of preoperative imaging modalities for detecting regional LNM [15]. Accordingly, inclusion of cN stage in the propensity score model could have introduced misclassification bias and potentially obscured the association between LNE and outcomes.

Our study spans a nearly two-decade period (2004–2021), during which perioperative care for HCC surgery has evolved considerably, including the adoption of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, improvements in patient selection criteria, and refinements in minimally invasive surgical technique. Improvements in HCC outcomes over time have been documented in large cohort studies. The indications and frequency of LNE may also have shifted over this period, and era effects therefore represent an additional source of unmeasured confounding.

The most important limitation of this study is the inherent indication bias in who undergoes LNE. Patients selected for LNE may represent a subgroup with radiologically suspicious nodes, more complex hilar anatomy, or more aggressive tumor biology which independently predict worse perioperative outcomes. Although PSM substantially reduced measurable confounding, unmeasured variables including surgeon intraoperative judgment, extent of hilar involvement, and overall procedural complexity could not be balanced. Therefore, the increased perioperative mortality in LNE patients may partly reflect unmeasured differences in baseline surgical complexity rather than a direct harmful effect of nodal dissection per se. Furthermore, key intraoperative variables unavailable in the NCDB—including extent of hepatectomy, concurrent vascular resection, estimated blood loss, intraoperative transfusion, presence of portal hypertension, use of minimally invasive approaches, and post-hepatectomy liver failure—independently predict perioperative mortality and are likely correlated with LNE performance. Additionally, variables excluded from the PSM model (fibrosis post-PSM SMD 0.199; AJCC clinical N Post-PSM SMD 0.179) remained imbalanced after matching, representing a source of residual confounding. Finally, this study was limited to overall survival and perioperative mortality; important oncologic outcomes including recurrence patterns, disease-free survival, and the influence of LNE on adjuvant treatment decision-making could not be evaluated. Additional NCDB-inherent limitations include the absence of key etiologic data such as viral hepatitis, alcohol-associated liver disease, and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, as well as the lack of BCLC staging and portal hypertension data [6, 21]. The prognostic role of racial and socioeconomic disparities, histologic grade, and MELD score—previously reported to influence HCC outcomes—could not be fully evaluated [19, 21]. Future studies should identify patient subgroups that may benefit from selective LNE while minimizing procedural risk. As systemic therapies, particularly immunotherapy, continue to evolve, the role of LNE should be reassessed within emerging therapeutic frameworks [25–27].

Conclusions

In this large national cohort of patients undergoing curative-intent hepatectomy for HCC, LNE was associated with increased 30-day and 90-day mortality and did not confer a long-term survival benefit in node-negative disease. Long-term OS was comparable between patients who did not undergo LNE and those with pathologically confirmed pN0 status across all major histologic subtypes. In contrast, pN1 disease consistently predicted poor outcomes. These findings should be interpreted with caution given the observational design, inherent indication bias, and absence of detailed surgical variables. A selective, risk-stratified approach to nodal evaluation guided by tumor biology and individual patient risk factors may be preferable to routine LNE. To move beyond associational findings, prospective studies incorporating detailed intraoperative data and comprehensive oncologic endpoints are needed to establish the causal risk-benefit profile of LNE in HCC.
