Family alliances feeding the carotenoid pathway in tomato
Authors: Manuel Rodriguez-Concepcion, Sooyeon Lim, Sun-Hwa Ha, Hiroshi Ezura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarotenoids are a family of lipophilic metabolites with essential roles in photosynthesis and photoprotection, and secondary roles as pigments providing color to non-green tissues. In addition, their cleavage generates compounds collectively known as apocarotenoids that can function as flavors, pigments, and bioactive molecules. In plants, carotenoids are synthesized and stored in plastids. Isopentenyl diphosphate and dimethylallyl diphosphate supplied by the methylerythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway are used to produce geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP), a common precursor for carotenoids and other plastidial isoprenoids such as chlorophylls, tocopherols, plastoquinone, phylloquinone, diterpenes, and gibberellins. The first committed step of the carotenoid pathway is the condensation of two GGPP molecules to form phytoene, which is later converted into the rest of the carotenoid products. In stark contrast to other plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana, tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) has gene families encoding the plastidial enzymes that catalyze the main rate-determining step of the MEP pathway [deoxyxylulose 5-phosphate synthase (DXS1 and DXS2)], the production of GGPP [GGPP synthase (GGPPS1–GGPPS3)], and its diversion to the carotenoid pathway [phytoene synthase (PSY1–PSY3)]. Here we review the latest advances in our understanding of how the different tomato paralogs of these three enzymes contribute to carotenoid biosynthesis in different plant tissues, developmental stages, and environmental conditions.Keywords: Carotenoids, deoxyxylulose 5-phosphate synthase, geranylgeranyl diphosphate synthase, phytoene synthase, tomato

Introduction

Carotenoids are photosynthetic pigments required for the assembly of the photosynthetic apparatus and for its protection against excess light (Rodriguez-Concepcion et al., 2018). Their functions in plants, however, go beyond chloroplasts and green tissues. Non-photosynthetic tissues of many plant species accumulate high levels of carotenoids in specialized plastids named chromoplasts (Sun et al., 2018). The characteristic colors of carrot roots, marigold flowers, tomato fruits, or corn seeds are provided by carotenoids. Furthermore, carotenoid cleavage leads to the production of apocarotenoids, including important bioactive molecules such as the plant abscisic acid (ABA) and strigolactone (SL) hormones (Beltran and Wurtzel, 2025). Carotenoids are in great demand by cosmetic and agro-food industries as natural pigments, and they also provide benefits for human health as biologically active molecules and precursors of retinoids, including vitamin A (Rodríguez-Concepción et al., 2018).

Plant carotenoids are plastidial isoprenoid lipids synthesized from metabolic precursors supplied by the methylerythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway (Box 1). The first and main flux-controlling step of the MEP pathway is the condensation of pyruvate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate catalyzed by deoxyxylulose-5-phosphate synthase (DXS) enzymes (Wright et al., 2014). The MEP pathway eventually produces the five-carbon (C5) unit isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) and its double-bond isomer dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP), which are the universal building blocks for all isoprenoids. Carotenoids are not directly produced from IPP and DMAPP but from C20 geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP), synthesized by GGPP synthase (GGPPS) from one DMAPP and three IPP molecules (Rodriguez-Concepcion et al., 2018). MEP-derived GGPP is also used to produce other plastidial isoprenoids such as chlorophylls, tocopherols, plastoquinone, phylloquinone, diterpenes, and gibberellins (Barja and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2021). Channeling of GGPP into the carotenoid pathway is carried out by phytoene synthase (PSY), which produces C40 phytoene from two GGPP molecules in the first committed and main rate-determining step of the carotenoid biosynthesis pathway (Zhou et al., 2022). Downstream desaturation and isomerization reactions then transform non-colored phytoene into red-colored lycopene, which is later converted into orange-colored carotenes such as β-carotene by cyclization of its linear molecule ends. Oxidation of one or the two rings produces yellow-colored xanthophylls such as lutein, zeaxanthin, violaxanthin, and neoxanthin (Rodriguez-Concepcion et al., 2018; Beltran and Wurtzel, 2025). Apocarotenoids are formed by both enzymatic and non-enzymatic (oxidative) cleavage of these carotenoids (Beltran and Wurtzel, 2025).

The model plant Arabidopsis thaliana produces all its carotenoids using single catalytically active enzymes for critical steps of the pathway: DXS (AT4G15560, CLA1), GGPPS (AT4G36810, GGPPS11), and PSY (AT5G17230). This is best illustrated by the albino phenotype resulting from the lack of carotenoids observed in Arabidopsis cla1, ggpps11, and psy single mutants (Phillips et al., 2008; Pokhilko et al., 2015; Ruiz-Sola et al., 2016). In stark contrast to this model plant, phylogenetic analyses have shown that most plant species (including crops) present a much more complex scenario of small gene families encoding DXS, GGPPS, and PSY paralogs with diversified gene expression profiles, protein partners, and enzymatic features (Walter et al., 2015; Stauder et al., 2018; Barja and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2021; de Luna-Valdez et al., 2021; Song et al., 2023). Retention of several gene copies after duplication events has indeed allowed neofunctionalization (i.e. that duplicate isoforms acquire new functions) but also subfunctionalization (i.e. divide the ancestral functions among paralogs) in DXS, GGPPS, and PSY gene families from different plant species (Walter et al., 2015; Stauder et al., 2018; Barja and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2021; de Luna-Valdez et al., 2021; Song et al., 2023).

Here, we will focus in tomato (Solanum lycopersicon), a crop with a major economic value and a diverse array of carotenoid biosynthetic enzyme paralogs. This diversity is consistent with the variety of roles of carotenoids in different tissues of the tomato plant. Besides playing roles in photosynthesis and photoprotection and serving as precursors for ABA and SL synthesis, tomato carotenoids also participate in processes that are absent in Arabidopsis. Notably, they contribute to change the color of fruits as they ripen, and participate in root mycorrhizal associations. In agreement with such diversification of their roles, the tomato genome contains several genes encoding carotenoid-related DXS, GGPPS, and PSY enzymes with different expression patterns, kinetic properties, and/or interacting partners (Zhou and Pichersky, 2020; Barja et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022; Di and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2023; Song et al., 2023; Ezquerro et al., 2023a). Here we review recent advances in our understanding of how different tomato DXS, GGPPS, and PSY paralogs contribute to the production of carotenoids in photosynthetic tissues (e.g. for photoprotection), fruits (e.g. for pigmentation), or roots (e.g. for mycorrhization).

DXS family

Plant DXS proteins belong to three distinct phylogenetic groups: class 1, 2, and 3 (de Luna-Valdez et al., 2021). Class 1 DXS enzymes usually play housekeeping roles and class 2 homologs are often required for the production of secondary (i.e. specialized) isoprenoids, whereas class 3 proteins appear to lack enzymatic activity (de Luna-Valdez et al., 2021; Di and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2023). The tomato DXS family includes one member of each group: class 1 DXS1 (Solyc01g067890), class 2 DXS2 (Solyc11g010850), and class 3 DXS3 (Solyc08g066950). Of these, DXS1 is the only one clearly contributing to carotenoid biosynthesis in green tissues and during fruit ripening (Lois et al., 2001; Paetzold et al., 2010; García-Alcázar et al., 2017). Mutant plants defective in DXS1 are indeed albino, confirming a non-redundant role for DXS2 and DXS3 (Lois et al., 2001; Paetzold et al., 2010; García-Alcázar et al., 2017). Recent results have shown that the activity and the levels of this tomato enzyme are feedback regulated by the MEP pathway products by a mechanism of allosteric regulation (Di et al., 2023) (Box 1). The proposed mechanism involves direct binding of IPP/DMAPP to the active DXS1 dimer away from the active site. The allosteric site for IPP/DMAPP binding was predicted to correspond to a region where monomers interact. Binding of IPP/DMAPP thus causes monomerization (and hence inactivation) of the enzyme. Disruption of the dimeric structure exposes hydrophobic regions of the monomers to the medium, which can eventually cause monomer aggregation if dimerization is prevented by persistent high IPP/DMAPP levels (Box 1). DXS aggregates are eventually degraded by the stromal Clp protease complex (see below) (Pulido et al., 2013; Rodriguez-Concepcion et al., 2019). In summary, this mechanism of DXS regulation supports fast and reversible responses to a sudden change in IPP/DMAPP supply (by rapidly shifting the dimer–monomer equilibrium accordingly) but also long-term responses if IPP/DMAPP abundance persists (via aggregation and eventual removal of monomers that would otherwise be available for dimerization). Although the mechanism was demonstrated for tomato DXS1 and Escherichia coli DXS, it probably applies to the regulation of most (if not all) active DXS proteins from plants and bacteria.

In contrast to the presence of high levels of DXS1 transcripts in virtually all tissues of the tomato plant, DXS2 expression is more restricted and mostly confined to roots, leaf trichomes, and flowers (Paetzold et al., 2010; Di and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2023) (Box 2). In agreement with this distribution and with the specialized role proposed for class 2 DXS enzymes, silencing of tomato DXS2 did not cause bleaching but negatively impacted the production of MEP-derived monoterpenes (Paetzold et al., 2010). Interestingly, transcripts encoding DXS1 and DXS2 are found together in some tissues, and DXS1 can co-localize and probably form heterodimers with DXS2 in plastids (Di and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2023). These results suggest that DXS1 and DXS2 might play coordinated roles in vivo. Tomato DXS3 has been confirmed to lack DXS activity (Di and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2023). It cannot be ruled out, however, that heterodimers of DXS3 with DXS1 and/or DXS2 could also play a role in regulating the overall DXS activity in at least some scenarios (de Luna-Valdez et al., 2021; Di and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2023).

GGPPS family

The tomato genome encodes five GGPPS-like enzymes and two related proteins referred to as SSU type I and II (Zhou and Pichersky, 2020). Paralogs GGPPS1 (Solyc11g011240), GGPPS2 (Solyc04g079960), and GGPPS3 (Solyc02g085700) have been shown to produce GGPP for the synthesis of carotenoids and other related plastidial isoprenoids (Zhou and Pichersky, 2020; Barja et al., 2021; Ezquerro et al., 2023b). The other two GGPPS-like proteins are encoded by genes found very close to GGPPS3 in the genome (Solyc02g085710 and Solyc02g085720). These proteins, respectively referred to as TPT1 and TPT2, are targeted to mitochondria and they appear to lack GGPPS activity (Zhou and Pichersky, 2020). Proteins SSU-I (Solyc07g064660) and SSU-II (Solyc09g008920) also lack catalytic activity on their own, but they are found in plastids where they can heterodimerize with true GGPPS enzymes to change their catalytic properties. GGPPS enzymes can form heterodimers with SSU-I proteins to produce the C10 precursor of the monoterpene geranyl diphosphate (GPP), whereas heterodimerization with SSU-II proteins enhances the production of GGPP (Zhou and Pichersky, 2020; Song et al., 2023) (Box 1).

Tomato genes encoding true GGPPS enzymes show distinct expression profiles (Box 2), suggesting subfunctionalization (Barja et al., 2021). GGPPS1 expression is very low and restricted to only some tissues such as roots and trichomes. By contrast, GGPPS3 expression is high and virtually constant, whereas expression of the GGPPS2 gene is more responsive to peak demands of GGPP for carotenoid biosynthesis (e.g. during de-etiolation and fruit ripening). Loss of GGPPS1, GGPPS2, and/or GGPPS3 activities in CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats) mutants confirmed that each of these paralogs plays different roles despite being enzymatically similar (Barja et al., 2021; Ezquerro et al., 2023b; Burbano-Erazo et al., 2025) (Box 1). GGPPS2 and GGPPS3 differentially contribute to carotenoid biosynthesis in most tissues, whereas GGPPS1 appears to be specifically involved in the production of carotenoid-derived SLs in roots and diterpenes in leaves (Barja et al., 2021; Ezquerro et al., 2023b; Burbano-Erazo et al., 2025). GGPPS3 is the main housekeeping paralog for plastidial GGPP production under normal growth conditions. Recent work suggested that GGPPS2 might be the main enzyme involved in GPP and monoterpene biosynthesis by preferentislly binding to SSU-I proteins (Pérez-Pérez et al., 2025, Preprint). However, GGPPS2 also appears to supply extra GGPP when a more active production of carotenoids is required, such as for photoprotection or fruit pigmentation (Barja et al., 2021; Burbano-Erazo et al., 2025). Loss of both GGPPS2 and GGPPS3 results in an embryo-lethal phenotype as it cannot be compensated by the remaining GGPPS1 (Barja et al., 2021).

PSY family

Three genes encoding PSY paralogs are present in the tomato genome (Cao et al., 2019; Ezquerro et al., 2023b). PSY1 (Solyc03g031860) and PSY2 (Solyc02g081330) are similar proteins with a common phylogenetic origin (Giorio et al., 2008; Cao et al., 2019), whereas PSY3 (Solyc01g005940) belongs to a different clade of more ancient origin that is only found in dicots (Stauder et al., 2018). Interestingly, PSY1 is one of the very few land plant PSY sequences lacking a neighboring aromatic–aromatic combination that is crucial for high enzymatic activity (Cao et al., 2019). Gene expression data (Box 2) suggested a role for PSY3 in roots and a differential contribution of PSY2 and PSY1 in shoot tissues (Stauder et al., 2018; Ezquerro et al., 2023b) (Box 1). PSY2 was proposed to be required for carotenoid biosynthesis in photosynthetic tissues, whereas the predominant role of PSY1 occurred in non-photosynthetic but carotenoid-rich shoot tissues such as flower petals and ripening fruit (Stauder et al., 2018; Cao et al., 2019). The presence of three PSY paralogs with differential gene expression profiles in tomato has been known for a long time, but genetic evidence of their physiological roles was only available for PSY1 until recently.

Generation of edited lines defective in PSY1 and/or PSY2 revealed that both paralogs contribute to the production of carotenoids in both chloroplasts and chromoplasts (Ezquerro et al., 2023b). Analyses of single mutants not only confirmed that PSY2 is the main isoform supporting chloroplast carotenoid production but they additionally revealed an important role for PSY1 when an extra supply of carotenoids is needed for photoprotection, such as after exposure to high light (Ezquerro et al., 2023b). Conversely, in chromoplasts of flower and fruit pericarp tissues, PSY1 was confirmed to play a predominant role while PSY2 was found to retain some activity detectable when PSY1 activity is missing (Gupta et al., 2022; Karniel et al., 2022; Ezquerro et al., 2023b). Besides confirming the subfunctionalization of tomato PSY1 and PSY2 paralogs, the analysis of edited lines demonstrated paralog-specific roles in ABA production. PSY1 was found to be responsible for the production of ABA in the fruit pericarp, whereas PSY2 was the main paralog associated with ABA synthesis in seeds, leaves, and roots (Ezquerro et al., 2023b). Double mutants defective in PSY1 and PSY2 were albino, confirming that the remaining PSY3 is unable to compensate the loss of PSY activity in shoot tissues. Indeed, other lines of evidence concluded that the main role of PSY3 is in roots, supplying phytoene to produce carotenoids and derived apocarotenoids (including SLs) associated with root mycorrhization (Stauder et al., 2018).

Interfamily coordination

Several mechanisms have been demonstrated to coordinate the levels and activity of DXS, GGPPS, and PSY paralogs to ensure that enough MEP-derived precursors are supplied and properly channeled into the carotenoid pathway when needed. Some of these mechanisms operate at the gene expression level. For example, transcription factors transducing light responses such as Long HYpocotyl 5 (HY5) and Phytochrome-Interacting Factors (PIFs) act as a activation/suppression module that directly regulates the expression of the Arabidopsis genes encoding the rate-controlling DXS and PSY enzymes (Toledo-Ortiz et al., 2014; Chenge-Espinosa et al., 2018). Recent experiments also showed that GOLDEN2-LIKE (GLK) transcription factors coordinately control carotenoid biosynthesis by directly interacting with G-BOX BINDING FACTORS (GBFs) to regulate the expression of Arabidopsis genes encoding DXS and PSY (Sun et al., 2025). Tomato homologs of HY5, PIFs, and GLKs have been found to directly bind the promoter of the PSY1 gene, and their altered expression impacts carotenoid biosynthesis in leaves and ripening fruits, as expected based on their described role in Arabidopsis (Nguyen et al., 2014; Llorente et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021). It remains unknown whether these transcription factors regulate tomato genes encoding DXS, GGPPS, and PSY paralogs either differentially or coordinatedly.

A higher level of regulation takes place at the protein level. Both DXS and PSY proteins are substrates of the stromal Clp protease, which is the main machinery for protein degradation in the plastid stroma (Rodriguez-Concepcion et al., 2019). The Clp protease complex acts in coordination with several types of chaperones for the control of proper folding (i.e. activity) and amounts of plastidial proteins. Misfolded forms of DXS are recognized by the DnaJ co-chaperone J20 and delivered to the Clp chaperone ring ClpC1 subunit for unfolding prior to degradation by the proteolytic core of the complex (Pulido et al., 2016). In the case of PSY, direct binding of the DnaJ-like protein ORANGE ensures proper folding and enzymatic activity, preventing interaction with ClpC1 for its Clp-mediated degradation (Welsch et al., 2018). The control of both DXS and PSY activities by this system is likely to ensure a proper supply of MEP-derived precursors for carotenoid biosynthesis in different plastid types. In tomato, overexpression of ORANGE stimulated carotenoid accumulation (Yazdani et al., 2019), whereas reduced Clp protease led to increased levels of both DXS and PSY enzymes in the chromoplasts of ripe fruit (D’Andrea et al., 2018). Strikingly, ORANGE promoted the differentiation of larger chromoplasts (Yazdani et al., 2019), and Clp-defective fruits showed aberrant chromoplasts (D’Andrea et al., 2018). These results suggest that ORANGE and the Clp protease also influence carotenoid storage (via plastidial ultrastructure) during tomato fruit ripening.

Another important layer of regulation at the protein level is the direct interaction of GGPPS and PSY enzymes. GGPPS paralogs from several plant species can physically interact not only with PSY but also with other enzymes catalyzing the conversion of GGPP into the first committed intermediate of their corresponding pathways (Ruiz-Sola et al., 2016; Barja and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2021; Dong et al., 2023; Ezquerro et al., 2023b). GGPPS–PSY binding is actually a requirement for efficient phytoene synthesis due to the highly amphipathic nature of GGPP (Camagna et al., 2019). In Arabidopsis, the Nudix hydrolase 23 (NUDX23) protein interacts with both GGPPS and PSY enzymes, causing their stabilization and assembly in a large protein complex (Rao et al., 2024) (Box 1). A NUDX23 homolog exists in tomato as well (Solyc03g062830), but its gene expression remains virtually unchanged, while GGPPS2 and PSY1 are up-regulated to support carotenoid biosynthesis during fruit ripening (Barja et al., 2021).

In tomato, GGPPS–PSY interaction, which probably facilitates channeling of MEP-derived GGPP into the carotenoid pathway (Ruiz-Sola et al., 2016; Camagna et al., 2019; Barja and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2021), occurs in a paralog-specific manner (Barja et al., 2021; Ezquerro et al., 2023b). Co-immunoprecipitation experiments showed that GGPPS1 only interacts with PSY3 (Ezquerro et al., 2023b), consistent with the specialized role of these two paralogs in (apo)carotenoid biosynthesis in roots (Box 1). The same experimental approach showed interaction of GGPPS2 with both PSY1 and PSY2 (but not PSY3), whereas GGPPS3 could not be immunoprecipitated with any of the three tomato PSY paralogs (Barja et al., 2021; Ezquerro et al., 2023b). The absence of direct GGPPS3–PSY interaction is quite shocking because the analysis of mutants suggested that GGPPS3 is the housekeeping supplier of GGPP for carotenoid biosynthesis in shoot tissues (Barja et al., 2021; Burbano-Erazo et al., 2025). However, in silico protein structure simulations also predict that interaction of PSY1 or PSY2 with GGPPS2 homodimers leads to formation of a channel facilitating the transport of GGPP that is absent in the case of GGPPS3 (Box 3). It was suggested that the interaction of tomato GGPPS3 with PSY paralogs might be mediated by the formation of heterodimers with GGPPS2 or with GGPPS-like SSU-II proteins (Barja et al., 2021; Barja and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2021). Bioinformatic prediction of protein–protein binding structures also supports this conclusion, as GGPPS2–GGPPS3 heterodimers could form relatively short channels with PSY1 or PSY2, potentially enabling a more efficient GGPP transfer (Box 3). Heterodimerization of GGPPS2 or GGPPS3 with SSU-II only resulted in channel formation with PSY1, with GGPPS3–SSU-II showing a much higher efficiency in GGPP transport (Box 3). Based on these simulations, it could be concluded that GGPPS2–GGPPS3 heterodimers are the most efficient to interact with PSY1 or PSY2 and produce carotenoids in all tissues under normal conditions. This model is consistent with expression data showing that genes encoding these paralogs are simultaneously expressed in most tissues, although at different levels (Box 2). However, the capacity of GGPPS2 homodimers to productively interact with PSY1 and PSY2 (Box 3) together with the results from GGPPS2-defective lines and the data showing that GGPPS2 gene expression is responsive to both developmental and environmental requirements of enhanced carotenoid production (Barja et al., 2021; Burbano-Erazo et al., 2025) support the conclusion that this paralog might be the main one channeling GGPP into the carotenoid pathway in tissues, developmental scenarios, or environmental conditions in which a fast and effective production of carotenoids is required.

Conclusion

Carotenoids are synthesized from MEP-derived GGPP, a hub intermediate for the production of many other plastidial isoprenoids essential for photosynthesis (chlorophylls, tocopherols, plastoquinone, and phylloquinones) and plant development (gibberellins). Despite tomato being well recognized as a model plant system to study the biosynthesis and regulation of carotenoids, until recently we lacked fundamental information on how different paralogs of biosynthetic enzymes contributed to supply precursors (in the case of DXS) and channel them into the carotenoid pathway (GGPPS and PSY) in different tissues of the plant and in response to developmental and environmental cues. Putative roles for these paralogs were originally deduced based on expression patterns and, more recently, from the analysis of mutants defective in individual genes. The last works on the analysis of multiple mutants and of protein–protein interactions unveil a very complex scenario of different enzyme isoform combinations contributing to carotenoid and apocarotenoid synthesis depending on the developmental stage and/or specific requirements of the tissue. Further work would be necessary to provide mechanistic detail to the formation of DXS and GGPPS homo/heterodimers and their differential interaction with other protein partners and particular downstream enzymes such as PSY paralogs. A complete picture of the system will provide valuable information to more precisely engineer tomato plants for the production of higher carotenoid levels in specific tissues (e.g. fruits) with minimal negative impact on related GGPP-derived products such as phytonutrients like tocopherols (vitamin E) or hormones like gibberellins or ABA.
