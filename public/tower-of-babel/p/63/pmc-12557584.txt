A custom-made appliance for mandibular mobilization in children with limited mouth opening
Authors: Mahdis Maleki, Sally Elshennawy, Paniz Haghighi, Taras Masnyi, Kyle Stevens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLimited mouth opening can impact oral function and hygiene, necessitating surgical intervention. Adjunctive appliance therapy has been shown to improve postoperative outcomes. At The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids), a customized Mandibular Opening Appliance (MOA) was developed to enhance jaw mobility post-surgery.MethodsA retrospective chart review was conducted for seven patients who completed MOA therapy at our orthodontic clinic between 2021 and 2024, using data from patients' charts. Maximum incisal opening (MIO) was recorded pre-surgery, post-surgery, following MOA use, and follow-up.ResultsPre-surgical MIO ranged from 5 to 17 mm, with a mean of 8.8 ± 5.2 mm. Among the five patients with good to excellent compliance, final MIO ranged from 18 to 42 mm, representing a mean increase of 23.2 ± 9.2 mm (range: 13–36 mm). In contrast, two patients with poor compliance showed only minimal improvement (2 mm and 4 mm).ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate the efficiency of the MOA in improving MIO in compliant pediatric patients. Incorporating such appliances post-surgery shows potential for improving mandibular mobility and supporting long-term outcomes. Although a larger sample size is needed to strengthen the evidence, current results remain compelling and support refining appliance protocols for this patient population.Keywords: Limited mouth opening, TMJ ankylosis, Trismus, Hypomobility, Orthodontic appliance

Introduction

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a ginglyarthrodial joint formed by the glenoid fossa of the temporal bone and the mandibular condyle.1 The TMJ plays an essential role in craniofacial development and masticatory function, and its dysfunction can have a substantial impact on a patient's quality of life. The normal mandibular range of opening spans from 34 mm to 55 mm in 5-year-old children and from 36 mm to 65 mm in 18-year-old adults.2 Limited mouth opening can result from disorders of the TMJ and associated masticatory muscles,3 and one such cause is TMJ ankylosis. Previously TMJ ankylosis has been classified into two types: true ankylosis, which involves bony or fibrous fusion within the joint itself, and false ankylosis, which is due to extra-articular factors such as muscular, ligamentous, or other soft tissue abnormalities. False ankylosis is often clinically referred to as ‘trismus’.4

A maximum incisal opening (MIO) of ≤35 mm is commonly used to define limited mandibular opening.5 This condition may result from myofascial pain dysfunction, infection, trauma, tumors, multiple medications, or psychiatric and neurologic factors.6 Iatrogenic causes, including craniotomy procedures and, less commonly, orthognathic surgery, have also been reported.6 Congenital disorders affecting the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), such as Arthrogryposis Multiplex Congenita (AMC), are associated with restricted opening, micrognathia, and weakness of the masticatory muscles.7 Coronoid process hyperplasia can similarly limit opening when an elongated coronoid impinges on the zygomatic bone.8

True TMJ ankylosis is defined as bony or fibrous adhesion of the joint components, typically presenting with facial deformity, restricted jaw opening, and absent condylar movement on the affected side.9 This condition impairs mastication, speech, and oral hygiene, and may adversely affect mandibular growth.10 Management typically involves surgical intervention, followed by rehabilitation to preserve mobility and prevent recurrence.6 Postoperative adjunctive therapies, including mobility appliances and exercises, are considered essential but remain infrequently documented.

A recent systematic review highlighted gaps in clinical practice and literature, particularly the lack of consensus on a standard appliance, as protocols vary across institutions.11 Historically, space holders were recommended after arthroplastic surgery to separate the maxilla and mandible, preventing contact of the articular surfaces, ankylosis, and relapse.12 More recently, generic jaw exercisers such as the TheraBite® Jaw Motion Rehabilitation System™, Dynasplint Trismus System® (DTS), and Orastretch® Press Device,13 along with the Spring-Bite14 and EZBite,15 have been introduced. Although readily available, these devices lack specificity and rely heavily on patient compliance and dexterity. Traditional orthodontic appliances, including acrylic bite blocks, twin blocks, and activators, have also been used postoperatively.11,16 While custom-made, these appliances are non-adjustable and do not allow incremental modifications necessary to restore optimal joint function during healing.

To address these limitations, The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids) developed the Mandibular Opening Appliance (MOA), a custom-made, adjustable intraoral device designed to improve compliance, enable controlled incremental mandibular opening, and reduce adverse effects associated with both generic and traditional appliances. By facilitating controlled postoperative opening, the MOA may reduce relapse, support oral hygiene, and enhance overall quality of life in pediatric patients following surgery. Accordingly, this retrospective study addresses the research question: Does the custom-made MOA improve MIO in pediatric patients with limited mouth opening after surgery?

Methods

A retrospective chart review was conducted on patients who have used the MOA from 2021 to 2024. All patients were initially assessed by a plastic surgeon and orthodontist prior to the surgical procedure, to determine if the appliance would be beneficial to improve mandibular opening post-surgery. Inclusion criteria included pediatric patients for whom the surgical team anticipated that surgery and physiotherapy alone would be insufficient to achieve optimal postoperative mandibular mobility. Exclusion criteria included patients in whom surgery and standard physiotherapy were expected to provide adequate mandibular function without the appliance, or those with conditions preventing safe or effective use of a custom intraoral device. Patients meeting the inclusion criteria were provided with the MOA to enhance stretching potential and maximize postoperative mandibular opening.

Physiotherapy was initiated one week after surgery to promote functional recovery and prevent joint stiffness. Patients were instructed to perform daily exercises focusing on improving jaw mobility. These included both assisted and unassisted mouth opening and closing exercises, where the mouth was held open for 2 s and repeated five times. Additionally, assisted maximum jaw excursions were performed in both open and closed positions, with ten repetitions each. Stretching exercises using tongue depressors were also prescribed, starting with 10 s holds and gradually increasing to 30 s, repeated three times. These exercises were intended to gradually increase range of motion and restore normal jaw function. Compliance with physiotherapy was monitored through in-person assessments by a physiotherapist, conducted on the same day as the follow-up appointments with the plastic surgeon. All physiotherapy sessions and progress notes were documented in Epic,17 ensuring accurate tracking of adherence and outcomes.

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from patients' electronic medical and dental records using Epic17 and axiUm18 systems. AxiUm is a dental electronic health record (EHR) system software that is used by the Department of Dentistry as part of their routine standard of care to track appointments, billing, and patient health information. Epic is a hospital-wide EHR system used to document patients' medical history and communicate/coordinate with other healthcare providers. Each record was reviewed using the patient's medical record number (MRN) to extract the following information: patient's age when they started treatment, sex, diagnosis, surgical procedure performed, surgical date, use of other adjunctive therapy, date when the appliance use started, date when appliance use stopped, frequency of follow-up visits after stopping the appliance, amount of initial maximum opening, final maximum opening, maximum opening in retention, and patient-reported compliance. All participants in the study provided complete data, with no instances of missing information or dropouts.

MIO was measured using an American Orthodontics orthodontic ruler (SKU: 853-53). Patients were seated in an upright position during each measurement. These standardized procedures were applied consistently across all time points to ensure measurement reliability and reproducibility.

Patient-reported compliance with the MOA was monitored and categorized according to daily usage. Compliance was classified as follows: excellent, when the appliance was worn full-time for 14–20 h per day; great, for 3–4 times daily, totaling 8–14 h; good, for 2–3 times daily, totaling 4–8 h; fair, for 1–2 times daily, totaling 2–4 h; and poor, when wear was infrequent, less than 1 h per day. This structured scale allowed for standardized evaluation of patient adherence and its potential impact on postoperative mandibular opening outcomes. Patients were seen for regular follow-up in the orthodontic clinic by the same orthodontist at intervals of approximately 4–6 weeks. MIO was measured at each visit as previously described, and all findings were recorded in the patient's electronic chart (Epic)17 until discontinuation of the appliance. Follow-up after appliance discontinuation was not standardized and varied vastly between patients, which is acknowledged as a study limitation.

All the patients who participated in this study signed an informed consent form. Data was collected and stored electronically on a password-protected Excel spreadsheet. Subsequently, each patient was assigned a unique study ID number, de-identifying the sample. Ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Board of SickKids (REB #1000081385, approved on February 23, 2024).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize study data, including means and standard deviations for continuous variables such as patient age and MIO range; frequencies and proportions were used to describe categorical variables such as sex. Additionally, changes in MIO were calculated by subtracting the initial and final measurements at several timepoints: initial presentation, post-surgery before appliance insertion, completion of appliance use, and at follow-up after discontinuing appliance use. Given the small sample size of this case report (n = 7), formal statistical analysis to test for significant differences was not conducted, as the study was not adequately powered to detect statistically meaningful effects.

Appliance fabrication

The MOA is fabricated as two removable components using intra-operative dental impressions or, alternatively, 3D intra-oral scans with printed models. A bite registration is taken to mount the models and to assess MIO after surgical release, as preoperative impressions are usually not feasible due to limited mouth opening.

Each component is made from cold-cure acrylic with a dual-layer Essix base, with thickness adjusted to ensure full coverage of the mandibular anterior teeth. The maxillary component consists of two parts connected by an expansion screw positioned anteriorly at first and, in some cases, repositioned to the mid-palatal region once greater mouth opening was achieved (>25 mm). The mandibular component is designed to distribute forces evenly across teeth and minimize unwanted movement.

When activated, the expansion mechanism separates the two maxillary pieces, applying pressure to the mandible and facilitating gradual increases in mouth opening. Further activation is possible by adding acrylic to the occlusal surfaces as opening improved. Illustrative appliance components are shown in Fig. 1.1–1.7. Results

All patients who were treated with the MOA were included in this study. More specifically, a total of seven patients (four females and three males) were identified as consecutive cases treated with the MOA between April 2021 and January 2024. Surgical procedures

The surgical procedures included coronoidectomy and pterygo-masseteric sling release in six cases, temporalis release in two cases, coronoidectomy with TMJ fibrous release in one case, and condylectomy with costo-chondral graft in one patient (Table 1).

Group-level outcomes

Five of the seven patients reported acceptable compliance (minimum of 4 h daily wear) with appliance wear. For these patients, the initial pre-surgical opening ranged from 5 to 17 mm (mean: 8.8 ± 5.2 mm) (Fig. 4). Table 2 summarizes the changes in MIO over each time point based on compliance. On average, final mouth opening after surgery and appliance wear (T2-T0) increased by 23.2 ± 9.2 mm (95 % CI: 12.1–34.3 mm; range: 13–36 mm). There was an average increase of 17.6 ± 8.2 mm in mouth opening solely due to the use of the appliance in compliant patients (range: 5–27 mm). Among compliant patients (n = 5) median change in MIO was 20 mm (range: 13–36 mm, compared with 3 mm, range 2–4 mm among the non-compliant patients (n = 2). Stratified comparison by compliance revealed that compliant patients achieved a mean gain of 23.2 mm (median: 20 mm), while the non-compliant patient improved by 3 mm. Although calculation of Cohen's d was not feasible due to the limited non-compliant sample size, the observed 20 mm mean difference suggests a clinically meaningful benefit of compliance. The follow-up period ranged from 5 to 18 months, during which the compliant patients showed a relapse of 5.6 ± 6.8 mm (range: 0–16 mm) in MIO.

Discussion

Comparison with previous studies

There have been various appliances suggested in the literature to increase TMJ mobility in patients with limited mouth opening; however, these appliances are not usually custom-made. The purpose of this case series was to introduce a novel mandibular opening appliance designed at SickKids Orthodontic Clinic, which has been successfully utilized to improve mandibular mobility after surgery. This appliance contributed to a substantial increase in MIO when used by patients with a range of TMJ disorders. Such appliances are designed to stretch the masticatory muscles to improve MIO.13 Common generic appliances include Therabite® (for MIO >10 mm) and Dynasplint Trismus System® (for MIO ≥9 mm). Other reported options include custom screws and lever systems like the Spring-bite and TMJ Exerciser.13

The reported increase in MIO is variable when comparing studies evaluating TMJ mobility appliances. According to systematic reviews, there was considerable heterogeneity across the types of appliances used, intervention regimens, and time frames, making direct comparisons between TMJ mobility studies challenging.11,13 Previous research has reported an increase in MIO of 3–5.2 mm with Therabite® and 1.5–6.2 mm with the DTS® appliance.19, 20, 21 In this study, the MOA achieved an average increase in MIO of 17.6 ± 8.2 mm post-surgical intervention. It has been reported that an increase of 6–28 mm using a computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) device with a power screw, with treatment duration and follow-up periods varying across cases.22

Previous studies have reported complications related to TMJ mobility appliances including osteomyelitis,23 loosened dentition,24 mandibular fracture,25 and pain.26 Consistent with the study by Melchers et al.,26 we also found pain to be a barrier to the use of the MOA for some patients. Differences in reported adverse events between our study and the referenced studies may be attributed to older age groups or more complicated medical histories of participants in these other studies.

Role of compliance

Compliance was found to have a substantial impact on the effectiveness of mandibular mobility appliances to improve MIO. Patients have different goals, physiotherapy regimens, adherence to intervention instructions, and the ability to withstand appliance-applied forces. Previous studies reported difficulties adhering to instructions when using Therabite® and DTS®,21 while multiple other studies have failed to report compliance with appliance use.13 In this study, MOA compliance was assessed as the duration and frequency of appliance use; greater compliance was linked with an increase in MIO. More specifically, the patients with the lowest compliance reported the smallest changes in MIO, with an increase in MIO of 2 mm and 4 mm, whereas the patients with the greatest compliance (8–20 h daily wear) reported the largest change in MIO of 18 mm and 26 mm, underscoring the importance of consistent appliance use in optimizing treatment outcomes.

Strengths & limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate an intraoral active appliance for TMJ rehabilitation that does not require an extraoral component, addressing a gap that was previously highlighted in systematic reviews.11,13This appliance allows for intraoral activation and precise adjustment of the acrylic to gradually increase mouth opening over time. Additionally, this design prevents unintended tooth movements throughout the treatment period.

The MOA does not require the patient or parent to hold the appliance in place and is easy for patients to insert and remove themselves. This feature can potentially enhance compliance and comfort with appliance use. The appliances were all made by the same laboratory technician and treated by the same orthodontist. Additionally, the surgeries were only performed by two surgeons, and all the patients were given the same physiotherapy exercises, ensuring consistency in surgical technique and postoperative management. Another strength of this study is the assessment of relapse, reflected by reductions in MIO at follow-up. Including these measurements enhances the reliability of long-term outcome evaluation, setting this study apart from many small series and case reports that do not report relapse.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Although comparable to other studies,27, 28, 29 the small sample size of seven subjects group substantially limits the generalizability of the findings. Further research is warranted in the future to assess the long-term impacts of the MOA in larger sample sizes. Additionally, the study was conducted at a single institution by one orthodontist, therefore the results may reflect local practices and expertise, potentially limiting their generalizability to wider clinical contexts.

Another important limitation is the absence of a control group. Comparisons with patients treated by surgery and physiotherapy without the MOA were not available therefore it is difficult to isolate the specific effects of the MOA or to assess the independent contribution of physiotherapy. While inclusion of such groups would have yielded stronger comparative data, the retrospective design of this case series did not allow for such an approach. Moreover, once the treatment showed potential benefit, withholding it from eligible patients would not have been feasible.

Furthermore, variability in diagnosis, surgical management, patient compliance, and follow-up duration introduces potential inconsistencies in outcomes. However, this variability also suggests that this appliance may be effective across a range of different patient demographics, provided that good compliance is maintained.

Because compliance was associated with increased effectiveness of the MOA, its assessment in this study warrants careful consideration. Compliance was evaluated solely through patient and parent self-reporting, which introduces the possibility of recall and reporting bias, therefore the accuracy of compliance data cannot be verified. This limitation is particularly relevant given the central role of compliance in treatment outcomes, underscoring the need for more reliable monitoring methods in future research.

As with many orthodontic devices, a challenge identified by some patients was difficulty consistently adhering to prescribed appliance wear, particularly due to constraints of attending school during daytime hours. Discomfort was also reported, particularly among non-compliant patients, however compliant patients noted a decrease in discomfort over time.

Additionally, relapse with the MOA is an important consideration in evaluating long-term outcomes. In this study, some loss of opening was noted clinically; however, because there was no standardized follow-up schedule after appliance discontinuation, relapse could not be consistently monitored. As a result, the available data are insufficient to draw definitive conclusions about the magnitude or timing of relapse. Nevertheless, reporting relapse remains important, as it highlights a key aspect of treatment assessment and underscores the need for structured follow-up in future studies. Finally, measurements of MIO were performed by a single examiner, which may increase the risk of observer bias. However, to enhance reliability, each measurement was double-checked during the appointment and standardized procedures were applied across all time points to maintain consistency.

Implications and future directions

Beyond the cohort assessed in this study, this custom-made appliance holds promise for broader clinical applications. Given that limited opening can develop following orthognathic surgery, radiotherapy, and trauma, introducing this appliance in adult populations may offer a valuable non-invasive option for maintaining or improving jaw function. Future research should focus on evaluating this appliance in larger sample sizes to enhance the robustness of findings. Larger cohorts are also needed to clarify whether the effects of the MOA differ according to underlying diagnosis or surgical procedure. Future studies with larger sample sizes may wish to conduct multivariable linear regression analyses to assess mouth opening changes, adjusting for factors such as patient age, sex, and compliance as potential covariates. Studies assessing its effectiveness in patients who do not require adjunctive surgery would provide valuable insight into its broader applicability. Moreover, this appliance may hold potential as a preventive measure for individuals at risk of limited mouth opening, such as those with head and neck cancer or burn injuries, to help maintain mandibular mobility and prevent functional limitations.

Finally, while this study demonstrated overall improvement in MIO, the heterogeneity of the cohort highlights the need for further research to explore potential diagnosis- or procedure-specific effects. Once validated in larger studies, the MOA could be integrated into standard post-surgical protocols as an adjunct to support functional rehabilitation, such as in pediatric patients with TMJ ankylosis.

Conclusion

Adjunctive appliance therapy using the MOA was effective in improving MIO following surgery, particularly in patients who adhered to the prescribed use. For pediatric patients with severe limited opening requiring surgical intervention, incorporating orthodontic appliances into their treatment plan shows potential for improving mandibular mobility. However, ongoing monitoring until growth completion is essential to ensure long-term stability and prevent relapse.
