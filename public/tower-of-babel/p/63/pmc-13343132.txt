Protocol for quantitative live-cell imaging of early GPCR trafficking using acid-stable afCFP-Venus FRET
Authors: Yu Guo, Toshimasa Takahashi, Cheng Wang, Lisha Yi, Daisuke Nawa, Nanxiang Yin, Weidong Liu, Ziwei Wang, Heedong Jeon, Akitoshi Hara, Yoichi Takami, Koichi Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol to visualize and quantify β-arrestin-dependent signaling of the angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R). We describe steps for detecting receptor-β-arrestin interactions during early trafficking events under our imaging conditions using an afCFP-Venus FRET pair. We then detail procedures for visualizing early intracellular signaling dynamics following AT1R activation and for analyzing GPCR trafficking and signaling dynamics in living cells.Subject areas: Cell Biology, Single Cell, Cell Membrane, Cell-based Assays

Before you begin

The angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) represents a prototypical G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) that undergoes β-arrestin–mediated internalization into endosomal compartments upon activation by its canonical ligand, angiotensin II (AII), or heterologous ligands.1,2,3 Tracking these receptor–β-arrestin interactions in real time is critical for understanding dynamic intracellular signaling.4

In this protocol, we describe a live-cell fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)–based method to quantify early AT1R–β-arrestin interactions. The assay employs AT1R fused to an acid-stable cyan fluorescent protein (afCFP) and β-arrestin2 fused to Venus. Compared with conventional CFP/YFP pairs, afCFP exhibits greater resistance to acidic pH,5 while Venus offers a lower pKa (∼5.8–6.0) than conventional YFP variants (e.g., EYFP, ∼6.5–6.9),6 providing more stable fluorescence behavior during early endosomal trafficking. Upon receptor activation, β-arrestin2 recruitment brings the fluorophores into close proximity, resulting in a quantifiable decrease in afCFP emission and an increase in Venus emission.7 This approach enables quantitative, high-temporal-resolution analysis of early AT1R signaling and trafficking dynamics in living cells (Figure 1).

Schematic overview of the afCFP–Venus FRET-based assay for monitoring AT1R–β-arrestin2 interaction

Schematic representation of the FRET–based assay used to visualize β-arrestin2 recruitment to the AT1R in living cells. Upon AII stimulation, AT1R undergoes conformational activation and recruits β-arrestin2 to its cytoplasmic C-terminal domain. In this system, AT1R is C-terminally fused to afCFP, while β-arrestin2 is C-terminally fused to Venus, a yellow fluorescent protein variant. Ligand-induced proximity between AT1R and β-arrestin2 brings afCFP and Venus into close spatial proximity, enabling FRET. Under our imaging conditions, the afCFP–Venus pair showed relatively stable donor fluorescence behavior during early trafficking events.

Overview

This protocol introduces a versatile live-cell imaging strategy to quantify G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) trafficking and signaling with high temporal resolution. The key innovation lies in the use of an afCFP fused to the AT1R, enabling FRET measurements during early trafficking events under our imaging conditions. Conventional CFP-based sensors often suffer from signal loss due to pH-dependent quenching during early trafficking events, limiting their applicability for studying early receptor trafficking. By contrast, the afCFP–Venus FRET pair provided relatively stable fluorescence behavior and photostability under our imaging conditions, allowing quantitative monitoring of receptor–β-arrestin interactions during early trafficking events. This protocol integrates optimized live-cell fluorescence imaging, ratiometric FRET analysis, and quantitative time-resolved measurements to capture dynamic GPCR signaling events that are otherwise difficult to resolve. In contrast to previous approaches that primarily focus on receptor activation at the plasma membrane, this method enables quantitative monitoring of early AT1R–β-arrestin2 interactions during initial trafficking events following angiotensin II stimulation. Furthermore, the workflow is compatible with standard cell culture systems and does not require genetically modified cell lines, facilitating broad adoption.

By combining fluorescent probes suitable for early trafficking analysis with a streamlined imaging and analysis pipeline, this protocol provides a powerful and accessible platform for investigating GPCR trafficking, endosomal signaling, and biased agonism in living cells. The approach is readily adaptable to other GPCRs and signaling contexts, expanding its utility for both basic and translational research. To extend this protocol to a novel GPCR of interest, researchers should verify that the C-terminal afCFP tag does not interfere with native receptor folding, ligand binding affinity, or G-protein coupling. Optimization steps may include adjusting the linker sequence and length between the receptor and the fluorophore, titrating the plasmid ratio during transfection to achieve equimolar donor/acceptor expression, and confirming the low endogenous background of the targeted GPCR in the selected host cell line. Furthermore, researchers can readily utilize this validated FRET platform to perform concentration-dependent kinetic analyses tailored to specific pharmacological research needs.

Expected outcomes

By following this protocol, CHO-K1 cells co-expressing AT1R–afCFP and β-arrestin2–Venus yield triple-channel time-lapse images with detectable donor (afCFP) and acceptor (Venus) signals and low background during early receptor trafficking. After stimulation with AII (10-6 M) a rapid increase in the baseline-normalized FRET ratio (R/R0) is typically detected within 30–60 s, consistent with β-arrestin2 recruitment to activated AT1R (Figure 3).

To accurately quantify the intermolecular FRET between AT1R-afCFP and β-arrestin2-Venus, we employ the Sensitized Emission method to rigorously correct for donor spectral bleed-through and acceptor cross-excitation. All fluorescence images are first subjected to background subtraction using Fiji. The corrected FRET intensity (FRETc) is determined using the following linear subtraction formula: FRETc = IDA - (α × IDD) - (β × IAA). In this framework, IDA represents the total signal in the FRET channel (excitation: 445 nm, emission: YFP), IDD denotes the donor intensity in the CFP channel (excitation: 445 nm, emission: CFP), and IAA signifies the acceptor intensity under direct excitation (excitation: 514 nm, emission: YFP). The correction coefficients, α and β, are calibrated using single-labeled control cells to isolate the true sensitized emission. The donor bleed-through coefficient (α) is calculated as IF/ID from cells expressing only the afCFP donor, where ID is the donor intensity in the CFP channel and IF is the leakage of CFP signal into the YFP emission window. The acceptor cross-excitation coefficient (β) is calculated as IF/IA from cells expressing only the Venus acceptor, where IA is the YFP intensity under direct 514 nm excitation and IF is the signal generated by the direct excitation of Venus by the 445 nm laser.

The final corrected FRET ratio is calculated as FRETc/IDD and subsequently normalized to the baseline mean value (R/R0). This mathematical correction ensures that the reported signals specifically represent the molecular proximity of AT1R and β-arrestin2, effectively eliminating the spectral artifacts associated with intermolecular FRET measurements. In parallel, donor and acceptor intensities (F/F0) show reciprocal changes without marked drift when imaging parameters are optimized (Figure 4A). Vehicle-treated cells show minimal changes in R/R0 and F/F0 over the 5-min acquisition (Figures 3 and 4A–4E). These datasets allow quantitative comparison of AT1R–β-arrestin2 interaction kinetics across conditions using time-course plots and summary metrics such as area under the curve (AUC) (Figure 4B), and support visualization of early AT1R trafficking during the initial post-stimulation period.

Quantification and analysis of FRET responses

Time-lapse fluorescence data were analyzed to quantify dynamic changes in ratiometric FRET signal associated with AT1R–β-arrestin2 interactions. Image stacks acquired from the CFP and YFP channels were imported into Fiji, and a single region of interest (ROI) encompassing the entire cell was manually defined in the CFP channel and applied identically to the YFP channel for all time points. Background fluorescence was measured from a cell-free region within the same field of view and subtracted from both donor and acceptor signals on a frame-by-frame basis to correct for camera offset and background noise.

To account for differences in expression levels between cells, FRET ratios were normalized to the mean baseline value obtained from the initial pre-stimulation period (typically the first 4 frames), yielding normalized ratios (R/R0). This normalization minimizes variability due to expression heterogeneity and facilitates comparison across cells and experimental conditions.

Time-resolved FRET traces were then used to quantify signaling dynamics. For each cell, the magnitude of β-arrestin recruitment was assessed by calculating the peak FRET response and the area under the curve (AUC) over the imaging period. Data from multiple cells and independent experiments were pooled for statistical analysis. Statistical comparisons between experimental groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, with significance defined as p < 0.05. Data represent mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated.

Limitations

FRET signals were quantified using a sensitized emission–based approach with correction for donor bleed-through and acceptor cross-excitation. While this method improves the accuracy of FRET measurements compared to simple intensity ratios, it may still be influenced by experimental factors such as signal-to-noise ratio, expression variability, and photobleaching. Therefore, our conclusions rely primarily on baseline-normalized dynamics (e.g., normalized FRET ratio and AUC) rather than absolute FRET values.

This assay uses heterologous overexpression in CHO-K1 cells, which may differ from native AT1R contexts (e.g., receptor density, membrane composition, scaffolding), and thus could influence β-arrestin recruitment dynamics and endosomal dwell time.

Image analysis has additional constraints: puncta quantification depends on size and circularity thresholds, which may introduce bias (e.g., missed or falsely identified structures), and the effective dynamic range of fluorescence detection remains limited. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined at the whole-cell level and were not designed to resolve subcellular FRET heterogeneity or localized signaling domains.

Photobleaching remains a potential confound, although it was minimized by optimizing laser power and imaging conditions.

Venus is more sensitive to acidic conditions (pKa ∼6.0) than afCFP, and therefore FRET signals may be attenuated in strongly acidic intracellular compartments such as late endosomes and lysosomes. Accordingly, the applicability of the afCFP–Venus FRET pair is primarily limited to early stages of receptor trafficking. Furthermore, the development of yellow fluorescent proteins with improved resistance to acidic environments would enhance the reproducibility of FRET-based analyses in intracellular compartments and represents an important direction for future studies.

Although the afCFP–Venus pair showed more stable fluorescence behavior than the conventional CFP–YFP pair under our experimental conditions, this study has certain temporal limitations. Our observation window was primarily optimized to capture the rapid, dynamic early-to-mid phase of β-arrestin2 recruitment to AT1R following AII stimulation. Given that the complete trafficking of GPCR complexes from early endosomes to lysosomes typically occurs on a longer timescale, the 4-minute imaging duration in the current protocol is insufficient to confirm the definitive arrival of the complex at the lysosomes. Future studies utilizing extended time-lapse imaging and specific lysosomal markers are required to fully characterize the long-term intracellular fate of the AT1R-β-arrestin complex.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Cells exhibit low transfection efficiency. (Related to Major step 1).

Potential solution

Ensure that the cells are at the optimal confluency (typically 60–80%) at the time of transfection. Avoid over-confluent or sparse cultures, as both reduce efficiency. Check that the plasmid DNA is of high purity (A260/A280 ∼1.8–2.0) and free of endotoxins. Optimize the ratio of DNA to lipofection reagent (e.g., Lipofectamine 3000) based on the manufacturer’s guidelines and perform small-scale optimization trials for your specific cell line.

Problem 2

Cells show high toxicity or detachment after lipofection. (Related to Major step 1).

Potential solution

Reduce the amount of lipofection reagent and/or plasmid DNA. Some cell lines are sensitive to the chemical components of transfection reagents. Additionally, consider changing the medium 4–6 hours after transfection to minimize exposure. Use serum-free medium only during the transfection complex incubation step, then replace with complete medium.

Problem 3

Receptor fluorescence is weak or uneven across cells. (Related to Major step 1).

Potential solution

This may result from inefficient plasmid delivery or suboptimal plasmid concentration. Confirm the integrity and promoter strength of the expression plasmid. Perform a side-by-side comparison of transfection conditions (e.g., varying DNA amounts or Lipofectamine ratios). Ensure that the observation is performed at the optimal time point post-transfection (e.g., 18–24 hours), as expression may be delayed or transient.

Problem 4

Reduced fluorescence intensity or uneven signal distribution during imaging. (Related to Major step 3).

Potential solution

Excessive expression of fluorescently tagged proteins can lead to aggregation or cellular stress, resulting in uneven fluorescence. Reduce the amount of plasmid DNA if necessary and ensure that cells are maintained under optimal physiological conditions. Confirm that the imaging chamber temperature is stably maintained at 37 °C, as deviations can impair protein folding and fluorescence stability.

Problem 5

Non-specific fluorescence or no puncta are observed. (Related to Major step 3).

Potential solution

This may be caused by autofluorescence or overexposure during imaging. Select cells showing proper localization and dynamic fluorescence in both channels (e.g., CFP and YFP). Adjust exposure time and laser intensity empirically. In addition, always passage cells at consistent density and avoid stress conditions that can induce background signal or reduce transgene expression.

Problem 6

Cloning of the plasmids used for FRET is unsuccessful.

Potential solution

If PCR amplification is weak or unsuccessful, optimize the annealing temperature by gradient PCR, extend the elongation time, and consider adding 2–5% DMSO for GC-rich regions. Primer design, template quality, and primer-dimer formation should also be re-evaluated.
