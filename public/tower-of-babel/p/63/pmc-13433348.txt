Adolescent locked femoral nail for recurrent femoral fractures in children with osteogenesis Imperfecta: a case report and literature review
Authors: Wenrong Yang, Jincai Liu, Zhijun Chen, Jianbin Dong, Zhicheng Zhang, Taotao Xu, Dan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a hereditary connective tissue disorder characterized by increased bone fragility and recurrent fractures. The femur is the most frequently fractured site in pediatric OI patients. Traditional internal fixations such as plates and elastic intramedullary nails carry high complication rates. Although extendable intramedullary nails represent the gold standard for children with OI, they show insufficient stability in adolescent patients with large medullary cavities and severe osteoporosis. Adolescent Locked Femoral Nail (ALFN) is a fixation system designed for adolescents, but its specific efficacy in recurrent femoral fractures in adolescent OI patients has been rarely reported.Case presentationA 13-year-old male patient with a confirmed COL1A1 pathogenic variant (Sillence type III OI) experienced four left femoral fractures within 5 years. The first two fractures were treated with plate fixation at a local primary hospital and both resulted in postoperative refracture; the third fracture was managed with plate fixation in our hospital, which also ended in refracture.For the fourth fracture, closed reduction and titanium alloy ALFN fixation were performed, combined with intravenous zoledronic acid infusion. At the 3-year follow-up, the fracture healed uneventfully without refracture; the lower limb alignment was normal, with a 0.5 cm limb-length discrepancy and satisfactory knee function.ConclusionsALFN provides central, stable intramedullary fixation, preserves bone blood supply, and matches the anatomical features of the adolescent femur. Combined with anti-osteoporotic medication, ALFN may serve as a valuable option for selected adolescent OI patients with recurrent fractures, wide medullary canals, or failure of previous fixation methods to reduce refracture risk. For adolescents with unexplained recurrent fractures, early genetic testing and individualized surgical planning are critical to improve clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a genetic connective tissue disorder mainly caused by type I collagen abnormalities, resulting in low bone mineral density, impaired bone microstructure, and markedly increased bone fragility (1–3). The femur is the most common fracture site in children with OI, and the risk is particularly high during adolescence due to active movement and open physes (4). Recurrent fractures can lead to progressive deformity, limb shortening, and poor quality of life (5).

Surgical intervention is essential for recurrent femoral fractures. Traditional plates and elastic intramedullary nails are associated with high complication rates (6). Extendable intramedullary nails (e.g., Fassier-Duval, Bailey-Dubow) are the gold-standard treatment for pediatric OI due to their ability to accommodate skeletal growth (5, 6). However, these devices often lack sufficient stability in adolescent OI patients with extremely enlarged medullary cavities and severe osteoporosis (7).

Adolescent Locked Femoral Nail (ALFN) is specifically designed for patients over 11 years old or weighing more than 50 kg (8). While rigid intramedullary fixation has been reported in adolescents with metabolic bone disease (9), the application of ALFN in adolescent OI patients with recurrent femoral fractures remains poorly documented. We present a 13-year-old OI patient with four left femoral fractures in 5 years, successfully treated with ALFN and zoledronic acid, with a 3-year follow-up.

On March 31, 2023, he fell and sustained a fourth fracture at the proximal-middle third junction of the left femur (Figures 4A,B). Preoperative evaluation revealed a wide distal femoral medullary cavity, thin cortex, and severe osteoporosis. Surgical contraindications including local infection, coagulation disorders, and neurovascular injury were excluded. The patient weighed 55 kg, aged 13 years and 9 months. Closed reduction and ALFN (titanium alloy, Φ10 × 340 mm) were performed under C-arm guidance. The entry point of ALFN was chosen on the lateral aspect of the greater trochanter, at the same level as the superior border of the piriform fossa. It is located at the intersection of the line 20 mm distal to the lesser trochanter and a trajectory with a 12° lateral offset angle relative to the medullary canal axis. This point lies on the central axis of the medullary canal on the lateral view (Figure 4C), avoiding injury to the femoral neck physis. Postoperative x-ray showed good fracture reduction and appropriate nail size (Figure 4D). Genetic testing on October 30, 2023, detected a pathogenic variant in the COL1A1 gene, confirming the diagnosis of osteogenesis imperfecta (Figure 4E).

Discussion

Pediatric osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a rare hereditary bone disease characterized by markedly increased bone fragility and recurrent fractures. The molecular mechanism is mostly attributed to abnormal type I collagen structure or synthesis (10). Genetically, COL1A1 and COL1A2 mutations are the most common causes, accounting for approximately 69% of pathogenic variants in children with recurrent fractures (11). The present patient carried a pathogenic variant in the COL1A1 gene, consistent with the severe clinical phenotype. The femur is the most frequently injured site in children with OI, particularly during infancy and puberty. Minimal trauma can induce fractures, and previous fracture is a strong risk factor for refracture (12). This patient sustained four left femoral fractures within 5 years after trivial injuries, which is highly typical of severe OI. The patient had blue sclera, no dentinogenesis imperfecta, no hearing impairment, and a negative family history. He was classified as Sillence type III OI.

Conservative treatment including casting, bracing, and traction may be considered for stable, minimally displaced fractures but is limited by complications such as joint stiffness, muscle atrophy, and further bone loss. Therefore, surgical intervention is strongly recommended for recurrent femoral shaft fractures in OI (5, 13).With the development of multidisciplinary management, surgery combined with anti-resorptive agents has become the standard strategy for OI fractures. Zoledronic acid improves bone mineral density and reduces fracture risk and has been safely used in pediatric OI patients (14). Intramedullary fixation is the preferred surgical method, whereas conventional plating is no longer recommended due to inherent disadvantages. As an eccentric fixation method, plate-screw systems require extensive exposure and periosteal stripping before fixation, which not only disrupts blood supply at the fracture site but further weakens the already fragile cortical bone, easily causing stress concentration at the fixation site and leading to complications such as implant failure and refracture. Intramedullary fixation is an important surgical technique. The core goal of surgery is to balance fracture healing, deformity correction, functional recovery, minimally invasive trauma, and refracture prevention, while taking into account the skeletal growth needs of adolescent patients (6). Elastic intramedullary nails and Kirschner wire fixation also carry high complication risks and cannot meet the long-term stable fixation needs of OI children (7).

Before the diagnosis of osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) was confirmed, the child underwent plate fixation for two femoral fractures at a local primary hospital. For the first fracture at the age of 8 years—a transverse midshaft femoral fracture in an 8-year-old child—elastic intramedullary nailing is the evidence-based first-line surgical option. Conventional plate fixation, however, is not recommended for this indication due to inherent drawbacks, including extensive periosteal stripping, disruption of bone blood supply, stress concentration, and a significantly increased risk of refracture. For the second and third fractures (at the ages of 10 and 11 years), although telescopic extendable intramedullary nailing represents the gold-standard treatment for femoral fractures in children with OI, this technique was not applicable due to the patient's narrow femoral medullary canal and extremely poor bone quality. Meanwhile, elastic intramedullary nailing was also unsuitable for this case because of the patient's insufficient bone mass and high risk of fracture displacement after reduction. At the time of the third fracture (11 years old, weighing 42 kg), although the patient weighed 42 kg, Adolescent Locked Femoral Nail (ALFN) or telescopic intramedullary nailing could have been considered. However, the treating surgeon was an adult orthopedist who did not fully recognize the patient's underlying OI, and plate fixation was therefore used again. The successful outcome of ALFN fixation in this patient strongly suggests that earlier transition from load-bearing plate fixation to load-sharing intramedullary fixation may reduce the risk of refracture in pediatric OI patients once the diagnosis is suspected. The implant selection was progressively adjusted according to the patient's increasing age, body weight, enlarging medullary canal, and skeletal maturity. In recent years, combined internal fixation with intramedullary nail and plate has emerged as a research hotspot in the management of fractures in patients with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI). For adolescent and adult OI patients, particularly those presenting with severely comminuted and long-segment fractures involving the middle and distal femoral shaft, standalone intramedullary nailing often cannot provide adequate bone purchase and anti-rotational stability, thereby necessitating adjunct plate fixation. Furthermore, combined internal fixation serves as an optimal salvage and reconstructive option for complex clinical scenarios, including previous internal fixation failure (e.g., intramedullary nail migration or breakage), fracture nonunion, and angular deformities greater than 30° that require corrective osteotomy (15).

Extendable intramedullary nails vs. ALFN

Extendable intramedullary nails (e.g., Fassier-Duval) are effective in young children with OI and can lengthen with skeletal growth (16). However, they may provide insufficient stability and anti-rotation control in adolescent OI patients with extremely wide medullary cavities and severe osteoporosis, leading to loosening and refracture (7). Adolescent Locked Femoral Nail (ALFN) is specifically designed for adolescents and provides central, locked, three-dimensional stability that better fits large canals. Rigid intramedullary nailing has been reported in adolescents with metabolic bone disease (9), but the specific application of ALFN in adolescent OI patients with recurrent fractures and failed previous fixation has rarely been reported.

ALFN is inserted through a lateral trochanteric entry point, which avoids injury to the femoral head blood supply and the proximal femoral physis (8). Closed insertion preserves periosteal blood supply and promotes faster healing. These advantages explain the uneventful union and absence of refracture during 3-year follow-up in this patient.

Synergistic effect of ALFN and zoledronic acid

Stable fixation provided by ALFN allowed early functional exercise and protected against refracture. Combined with periodic zoledronic acid infusion, bone mineral density and bone quality were improved, creating a synergistic effect that contributed to the favorable long-term outcome.

Limitations

ALFN is not suitable for patients with severe femoral bowing deformity before corrective osteotomy. Gentle manipulation is required to avoid iatrogenic fractures, which occur in approximately 4.2% of orthopedic procedures in OI patients (17). In addition, both ALFN and telescopic nailing require specialized surgical skills, limiting widespread use in primary hospitals.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that ALFN may be a useful salvage option for selected adolescent OI patients with recurrent femoral fractures, wide medullary canals, or failure of traditional fixation methods. ALFN combined with zoledronic acid provides stable fixation, promotes fracture healing, and reduces refracture risk. Early genetic testing and individualized surgical strategy are critical for improving outcomes in these patients.
