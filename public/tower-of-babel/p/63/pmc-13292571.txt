Identification of candidate antigens for a vaccine against Avibacterium paragallinarum using reverse vaccinology
Authors: M Esperanza Felici, Sofía D Castell, Guido Marconi, Federico N Ruiz Moreno, Constanza Marin, Giuliana Vedelago, Luz M Palacios, Nicolás D Dho, Guadalupe A Lopez Menichetti, María I Crespo, Gabriel Morón, Yosef D Huberman, Rodrigo Quiroga, Belkys A Maletto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfectious coryza, caused by Avibacterium paragallinarum, is a respiratory disease that results in significant economic losses for the global poultry industry. Current inactivated whole-cell vaccines often fail to provide broad protection, largely due to the extensive antigenic diversity among circulating strains. To address this limitation, we applied a reverse vaccinology strategy to identify candidate antigens for rational subunit vaccine development. Using genomic and proteomic data from 95 Av. paragallinarum strains and a subtractive genomics-immunoinformatics pipeline, we prioritized proteins predicted to be non-similar to host proteins, antigenic, surface-exposed, and conserved across strains. A curated dataset of experimentally validated bacterial antigens was incorporated as a benchmark to assess the predictive performance of the pipeline. Thirteen proteins fulfilled all selection criteria and were further characterized based on structural features, physicochemical properties, virulence association, and essentiality. Notably, homologs of ten candidates have previously conferred protective immunity in experimental subunit vaccines against other Gram-negative bacteria across diverse animal models. These findings provide a rationally prioritized set of conserved candidate antigens and a framework to guide the development of next-generation subunit vaccines against infectious coryza.Keywords: Reverse vaccinology, Infectious coryza, Avibacterium paragallinarum, Poulty respiratory infection, Vaccine

Introduction

Infectious coryza is caused by the extracellular, Gram-negative, non-motile bacterium Avibacterium paragallinarum and affects poultry production worldwide (El-Gazzar et al., 2025). It primarily involves the upper respiratory tract of chickens of all ages, with more severe symptoms observed in adult birds. The disease burden is concentrated in regions with intensive poultry production, particularly across Asia, Africa, México, and Central and South America, with more sporadic but still impactful occurrence in parts of Europe and North America (El-Gazzar et al., 2025). In laying hens, egg production may drop by up to 40%, and in broilers it may increase culling rates (Blackall and Soriano‐Vargas, 2020). Mortality is generally low, but morbidity is high, and infected birds often remain asymptomatic carriers. Complications due to stress or co-infections can exacerbate disease severity, increase mortality, and prolong recovery time (Sandoval et al., 1994; El-Gazzar et al., 2025).

The bacterium is classified into three serogroups (A, B, and C) according to Page's scheme (Page, 1962), and into nine serovars (A-1 to A-4, B-1, and C-1 to C-4) according to Kume's classification (Kume et al., 1983) with subsequent modifications (Blackall et al., 1990). Vaccination remains the primary strategy to control infectious coryza; however, the key protective antigens of Av. paragallinarum have not been fully identified. The central challenge in controlling infectious coryza is the extensive antigenic diversity of Av. paragallinarum, which limits cross-protection among strains. Commercial vaccines are based on bacterins (inactivated whole-cell bacteria) derived from international reference strains. These vaccines reduce clinical signs and limit bacterial spread (Caballero-Garcia et al., 2022; Huberman et al., 2025), but provide incomplete protection, with limited cross-protection against emerging or variant strains due to antigenic variations within each serotype (El-Gazzar et al., 2025). Furthermore, Soriano et al. reported the lack of cross-protection between serogroups and, in some cases, even between serovars in chickens vaccinated with reference bacterins (Soriano et al., 2004). Commercially available vaccines have shown inadequate protection against field isolates from Argentina (Terzolo et al., 1997; Jacobs et al., 2003), China (Sun et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2019), the United States (El-Gazzar et al., 2025; Jacobs et al., 2003), South Africa (Dungu et al., 2009), Ecuador and Zimbabwe (Jacobs et al., 2003). Although autogenous vaccines prepared from locally isolated strains can be effective (Putnam et al., 2024), their widespread use is constrained by logistical, economic and regulatory limitations.

The combination of antigenic diversity, poor cross-protection, and mismatch between commercial vaccine strains and circulating field isolates highlights the need for improved vaccines capable of addressing strain variability. This need is further exacerbated by the limitations of bacterin production, which requires culturing Av. paragallinarum, a slow-growing and fastidious organism (El-Gazzar et al., 2025), making vaccine manufacturing expensive and labor-intensive. In outbreak settings, this constraint is particularly critical, as new vaccines often need to be generated from newly isolated field strains. In contrast, recombinant protein subunit vaccines offer a simpler and potentially more economical alternative, with a well-defined composition and the possibility of incorporating multiple antigens to broaden protective coverage (Jorge and Dellagostin, 2017). However, previous attempts to develop such vaccines against infectious coryza have primarily relied on conventional antigen-screening strategies applied to geographically restricted isolates (Noro et al., 2007; Noro et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2011; Sakamoto et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2016). Consequently, much of the antigenic repertoire of Av. paragallinarum remains unexplored, limiting the identification of broadly cross-protective vaccine candidates.

Reverse vaccinology, first validated through the development of a human vaccine against Neisseria meningitidis serogroup B (MenB), leverages whole-genome sequences and bioinformatics tools to enable comprehensive antigen discovery. In contrast to conventional vaccine development, which relies on microorganism cultivation and empirical antigen selection, reverse vaccinology starts from genomic data to systematically screen the complete protein repertoire of a pathogen in silico. This strategy enables the identification of antigens that may be overlooked by classical approaches, including subdominant antigens that are not highly immunogenic during natural infection, and can also be applied to non-cultivable microorganisms. In addition, reverse vaccinology accelerates early-stage antigen identification and reduces animal use (Rappuoli, 2000, Rappuoli et al., 2024). Reverse vaccinology is now widely applied to pathogens of relevance in both human and veterinary medicine. In poultry, it has facilitated the identification of promising vaccine antigens for Pasteurella multocida, Campylobacter jejuni, Clostridium perfringens, Salmonella Pullorum, Gallibacterium anatis, avian pathogenic E. coli, Riemerella anatipestifer, and viral pathogens such as avian rotavirus, infectious bursal disease virus, and avian leukosis virus (Gloanec et al., 2025). However, to date, no reverse vaccinology study has systematically examined the antigenic diversity of Av. paragallinarum.

Here, we analyzed 95 Av. paragallinarum strains using a reverse vaccinology pipeline to identify candidate proteins for the development of a next-generation vaccine against infectious coryza. These results provide a foundation for the rational design of novel subunit vaccines with the potential to confer broad-spectrum protection against Av. paragallinarum.

Materials and methods

To identify candidate antigens we applied a reverse vaccinology pipeline (Fig. 1).

Workflow of the reverse vaccinology pipeline used to identify and characterize Av. paragallinarum candidate antigens through sequential protein elimination. Protein sequences from 95 Av. paragallinarum strains were sequentially filtered based on similarity to host proteins, antigenicity, subcellular localization, and conservation across strains. Candidate antigens were further characterized based on the predicted physicochemical properties, topology, signal peptides, and virulence and essentiality features.

Retrieval of protein and genomic sequences

Protein sequences from 94 Av. paragallinarum strains (Table S1) and the genomic sequence of M strain (GCA_020892835.1) were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information database. Protein sequences of Gallus gallus (GCF_016699485.2) and 29 additional hosts (Table S2) were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information database for host-similarity screens. The genomic sequence of GCA_020892835.1 was annotated using Rapid Annotation Transfer Tool (RATT) (Otto et al., 2011) and translated into protein sequences using SeqKit2 (Shen et al., 2024), resulting in a total of 95 proteomes for analysis.

Experimental antigens database

A curated collection of experimentally tested antigens (hereafter experimental antigens) was assembled from public sources and the literature, including the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) (Vita et al., 2025), Dataset of Bacterial Immunogens (VaxiJenDB) (Zaharieva et al., 2019), AntigenDB (Ansari et al., 2010), Protegen (Yang et al., 2011), VaxVec (Deng et al., 2015), Vaxgen (Xiang et al., 2008) and DNAVaxDB (Racz et al., 2014) and primary publications. A summary is provided in Table S3 and source databases are detailed in Table S4.

Functional classification

Proteins were assigned to functional categories using the Clusters of Orthologous Genes (COG) database (2024 update) (Galperin et al., 2025). Additionally, Position-Specific Score Matrices (PSSMs) from the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) were employed (Wang et al., 2023). Specifically, we used the COG file from the 'little_endian' subdirectory of the CDD FTP site. The protein sequences from various Av. paragallinarum strains, along with those in the experimental antigens database, were used as query sequences for Reverse Position Specific-Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (RPS-BLAST) searches against the COG PSSMs database. COG functional categories were assigned using the ‘rpsbproc’ command line utility (Yang et al., 2020) with default parameters. In cases where results yielded multiple categories, the first letter was assigned as the primary functional category. A summary of the standard abbreviations for COG functional categories is provided in Table S5.

Similarity to host proteins

We compared all Av. paragallinarum proteins against host proteomes using protein BLAST (BLASTp). An ablation analysis was performed to determine the optimal exclusion thresholds for our pipeline (Figure S1). Proteins were classified as host-similar and excluded when they had an identity > 25%, a bit score > 50, and an E-value < 1 × 10–5 (Pearson, 2013). Only the top-scoring hit was recovered for each protein query.

Antigenicity and subcellular localization

Antigenicity was predicted with VaxiJen v3.0 server. This tool predicts antigenicity without sequence alignment, based on a consensus of three machine-learning models and using the physicochemical properties of proteins (Dimitrov et al., 2020). Subcellular localization was predicted using PSORTb v3.0. For Av. paragallinarum, the PSORTb Gram-negative model and default parameters were applied. For experimental antigens, predictions were performed using the PSORTb model corresponding for each bacterial type: Gram-negative, Gram-positive, Gram-positive with outer membrane, and Gram-negative without outer membrane, as defined by PSORTb (Yu et al., 2010). Only proteins predicted as outer membrane or extracellular were retained as surface-exposed candidates.

Conservation analysis among different strains of Av. paragallinarum

Protein clustering was performed using CD-HIT with default parameters (Fu et al., 2012), and multiple sequence alignments for each cluster were generated with MAFFT v7 (Katoh and Standley, 2013).

A conservation score (CS) was calculated for each cluster using the R package BALCONY (Płuciennik et al., 2018) to assess sequence conservation. The CS is based on the Shannon normalized (Schneider) entropy calculated across the whole protein sequence. Specifically, the CS was defined as 1 - H̄, where H̄ is the mean entropy, resulting in values ranging from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating greater conservation. Proteins were considered conserved when CS > 0.80 and prevalent when they were present in at least 90% of the analyzed strains. Different threshold values were tested (summarized in Figure S2).

Physicochemical properties and topology

Theoretical molecular weight and isoelectric point were calculated with European Molecular Biology Open Software Suite (EMBOSS) (Rice et al., 2000). Signal peptides and their cleavage sites were predicted using SignalP 6.0, a machine learning model capable of predicting all five types of prokaryotic signal peptides (Teufel et al., 2022). Topology and transmembrane features (beta-sheet or alpha-helical) were predicted with DeepTMHMM (Hallgren et al., 2022). Outputs were integrated into a decision tree (Figure S3) to guide classification (lipoprotein, beta-barrel, secreted, multi-transmembrane, etc.).

Virulence factors and essential proteins

Virulence factors were identified by BLASTp comparison against the full Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) 2019 (Liu et al., 2019), using thresholds of identity > 25%, bitscore > 50, and E-value < 1 × 10⁻⁵. Proteins were considered virulence factors if they matched at least one VFDB entry meeting these criteria. When multiple hits satisfied these thresholds for a given query protein, the top-scoring hit was defined as the one with the lowest E-value; in cases of identical E-values, the hit with the highest identity and query coverage was selected. Essential proteins were identified by BLASTp against the Database of Essential Genes (DEG) 10 (Luo et al., 2014) using the same strategy.

Retrieval of reviewed homologs and literature-based evidence

All candidate antigens of Av. paragallinarum were queries against Universal Protein Knowledgebase (UniProtKB) Reference Proteomes and Swiss-Prot, using BLASTp with default parameters to retrieve the closest reviewed homologs. A systematic literature search (to July 2024) was performed using Google Scholar and targeted keywords (Table S6) to identify experimental vaccine studies relevant to homologous proteins.

Results

Selection of candidate antigens

The prioritization of candidate antigens for vaccine development can be achieved through different strategies. In this study, we applied the sequential reverse vaccinology pipeline illustrated in Fig. 1.

Sequence similarity between pathogen and host proteins can reduce vaccine effectiveness by inducing immune tolerance or, in some cases, triggering autoimmune responses (He, 2015). To identify proteins with low sequence similarity to host proteins, BLASTp comparisons were performed between protein sequences of 95 Av. paragallinarum strains and Gallus gallus. To validate the inclusion and exclusion criteria used in our pipeline, a set of 1,784 experimentally tested antigens was compiled (Table S3). As most of these antigens were originally designed to prevent diseases in non-avian hosts, BLASTp analyses were also conducted against their corresponding host proteomes (Table S2). Fig. 2A shows the percentage of Av. paragallinarum proteins and experimental antigens classified as non-similar to their respective hosts (see Materials and Methods section for further details on classification criteria). A mean of 77.42% of Av. paragallinarum proteins were classified as non-similar to chicken proteins according to the applied BLASTp thresholds. Host-similar proteins were primarily associated with COG categories related to translation, ribosomal structure, and biogenesis, as well as transport and metabolism of amino acids, nucleotides, carbohydrates and coenzymes (Fig. 2B). Among the experimental antigens, 81.39% displayed no sequence similarity to proteins from their corresponding host species (Fig. 2A). The proportions of host-similar and non-similar experimental antigens across different hosts are shown in Figure S4. Host-similar experimental antigens were mainly associated with post-translational modification, protein turnover and chaperones; translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis; energy production and conversion; and transport and metabolism of carbohydrates and lipids (Fig. 2C).

Antigenicity predictions using VaxiJen v3.0 identified a mean of 14.92% of all Av. paragallinarum proteins as antigenic. This proportion increased to 16.72% when only proteins previously classified as non-similar to chicken were considered (Fig. 3A). Most antigenic and non-host-similar Av. paragallinarum proteins were associated with cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis or did not match any COG categories (Fig. 3B). As expected, a high proportion of the experimental antigens were predicted to be antigenic: 80.10% of the total set and 82.02% of the non-host-similar subset (Fig. 3A). These antigens were distributed across nearly all COG categories (Fig. 3C).

PSORTb v3.0 was subsequently used to predict subcellular localization. Because surface-exposed proteins are commonly viable vaccine candidates against extracellular bacterial pathogens, only those predicted to be located in the outer membrane or extracellular space were retained. In Av. paragallinarum, 2.17% of all proteins were predicted as outer membrane and 0.60% as extracellular. Among proteins classified as both antigenic and non-host-similar, 13.07% were predicted as outer membrane and 2.41% as extracellular (Fig. 4A). These surface-exposed (i.e., extracellular or outer membrane) proteins were mainly associated with cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis; inorganic ion transport and metabolism; or were not assigned to any COG category. Smaller proportions were associated with intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport; mobilome (prophages, transposons); and cell motility (Fig. 4B). Experimental antigens showed a higher proportion of surface-exposed proteins than Av. paragallinarum. In the total experimental antigens set, 15.86% and 15.70% were predicted as outer membrane or extracellular, respectively. In the antigenic and non-host-similar subset, the proportions increased to 22.42% and 20.15%, respectively. Some experimental antigens were classified under the cell wall category, consistent with the inclusion of antigens derived from Gram-positive bacteria in this set (Fig. 4A). Similar to Av. paragallinarum, most experimental antigens that were antigenic, non-host-similar, and surface-exposed were associated with functional categories such as cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis; inorganic ion transport and metabolism; intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport; cell motility; or were not assigned to any COG category. Additionally, some surface-exposed experimental antigens were linked to defense mechanisms or belonged to poorly characterized COG categories (i.e., 'General function prediction only' and 'Function unknown') (Fig. 4C).

The identification of conserved proteins is critical for developing vaccines effective against multiple virulent strains, as such proteins are less likely to accumulate mutations that compromise vaccine effectiveness. Protein conservation across Av. paragallinarum strains was evaluated using normalized Shannon conservation. Proteins were considered conserved if they had CS > 0.80 and prevalent if they were present in at least 90% of the analyzed strains. A mean of 57.94% of all Av. paragallinarum proteins met both criteria. However, only 29.73% of the proteins previously classified as non-host-similar, antigenic, and surface-exposed satisfied both conditions (Fig. 5A). Proteins within this subset were primarily associated with COG categories related to cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis; transport and metabolism of inorganic ions and lipids, or not assigned to any COG category. Smaller fractions were linked to cell cycle control, cell division and chromosome partitioning, as well as intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport (Fig. 5B).

Characterization of Av. paragallinarum candidate antigens

Thirteen candidate antigens fulfilled all selection criteria (non-similar to host, antigenic, surface-exposed, and conservation across strains). Fig. 7 presents an integrated overview of their structural, physicochemical, and functional features.

(C) Molecular weight (kDa). (D) Isoelectric point. (E) Virulence factor category. (F) Essentiality. Each column represents an individual protein, and each row corresponds to a specific characteristic. Most candidate antigens were assigned to the COG category related to cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis. The remaining candidates were classified under cell cycle control, cell division, chromosome partitioning; inorganic ion transport and metabolism; intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport; lipid transport and metabolism, or remained unassigned to any COG category (Fig. 7, row A).

Given that membrane proteins often present challenges for recombinant expression and purification in E. coli due to their hydrophobicity and structural complexity, structural features relevant to protein production were analyzed. Signal peptides were predicted using SignalP 6.0, and alpha-helical and beta-sheet transmembrane regions and beta-barrel domains were identified using DeepTMHMM (workflow shown in Figure S3). As shown in Fig. 7 (row B), most candidate antigens were predicted as beta-barrel proteins, with a smaller proportion classified as lipoproteins or secreted proteins.

Low-molecular-weight proteins are generally more amenable to high-yield expression in E. coli and are more likely to retain native conformation, whereas proteins with highly basic isoelectric points often exhibit poor solubility (Mehlin et al., 2006). Using EMBOSS, the molecular weight and isoelectric point of each protein were predicted. Most candidate antigens had predicted molecular weight below 50 kDa (Fig. 7, row C), which is favorable for recombinant expression. However, most of these proteins also displayed high isoelectric point values (Fig. 7, row D), which may complicate soluble protein recovery.

Virulence-associated proteins are attractive vaccine targets because of their role in host colonization and pathogenesis. BLASTp searches against the VFDB revealed that six of the thirteen candidate antigens were virulence-associated (Fig. 7, row E). The matched virulence factors are implicated in processes such as host cell adhesion, invasion, or function as nutritional or metabolic factors for the pathogen.

Essential proteins tend to be highly conserved (Dilucca et al., 2018) and play critical roles in bacterial survival, replication, and pathogenesis (Mobegi et al., 2014) and are often less prone to antigenic variation. Essential proteins were identified through comparison against DEG. Seven candidate antigens were classified as essential (Fig. 7, row F).

Each candidate antigen was cross-referenced against the UniProtKB Reference Proteomes and Swiss-Prot databases using BLASTp. Table S7 summarizes the Av. paragallinarum candidate antigens, the closest reviewed homologs identified via the UniProt server, and their corresponding functions.

To evaluate their potential as vaccine antigens, a systematic literature review was conducted to identify proteins from other Gram-negative bacteria that show sequence similarity to the Av. paragallinarum candidate antigens and have been experimentally evaluated as subunit vaccine antigens in different animal models (Table 1). Studies assessing protective efficacy were prioritized, whereas reports based exclusively on in silico predictions were excluded.

Discussion

Whole-cell inactivated vaccines remain the only commercially available vaccine strategy to control infectious coryza, yet their effectiveness is fundamentally constrained by the extensive antigenic diversity of Av. paragallinarum. Although multivalent bacterins can reduce clinical signs, their reliance on a limited set of reference strains results in incomplete protection when circulating field isolates differ antigenically (Terzolo et al., 1997; Jacobs et al., 2003; Sun et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2019; El-Gazzar et al., 2025; Dungu et al., 2009). The expanded serovar classification scheme, which identified nine serovars (Blackall et al., 1990), further demonstrated that these correspond to distinct immunovars exhibiting partial or no cross-protection, even within the same Page’s serogroup (Soriano et al., 2004).

Recombinant protein subunit vaccines offer a rational alternative; however, previous efforts have largely focused on a limited number of antigens identified through conventional screening approaches. For instance, immunization with the fimbrial protein FlfA conferred protection against homologous challenge strains (Liu et al., 2016), while the hemagglutinin HMTp210 has been shown to induce protective responses that are largely serogroup-specific (Noro et al., 2007; Noro et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2011; Sakamoto et al., 2013). Collectively, these studies demonstrate the promise of recombinant protein vaccines but also highlight a key limitation: protection is typically restricted to antigenically similar strains, reflecting the underlying variability of target antigens.

In contrast, the present study adopts a strain-diverse perspective by applying a reverse vaccinology pipeline to 95 Av. paragallinarum strains representing diverse geographic origins. This approach enabled the identification of 13 candidate antigens with features consistent with vaccine suitability, including predicted surface exposure, conservation, and antigenicity. By prioritizing conserved proteins, our results directly address a primary shortcoming of existing vaccines: their inability to provide broad cross-protection against antigenically diverse strains.

The biological relevance of these candidates is supported by the observation that homologs of most identified proteins have been previously experimentally assessed as vaccine antigens in different animal models and bacterial systems (Table 1). Together, these findings suggest that the pipeline effectively enriches for functionally important antigens, consistent with previous applications of reverse vaccinology in both human and veterinary pathogens (Rappuoli, 2000; Rappuoli et al., 2024; Gloanec et al., 2025). At the same time, the identification of less-characterized proteins broadens the repertoire of candidate antigens in Av. paragallinarum, providing new targets for future investigation.

These findings have direct implications for the development of future vaccines against infectious coryza. Given the multifactorial nature of protective immunity against extracellular bacteria, it is unlikely that a single antigen will confer broad protection. Instead, multicomponent subunit vaccines incorporating several conserved targets may be required to achieve robust and cross-protective immunity. This strategy contrasts with earlier approaches focused on single or antigenically variable targets, which are inherently limited in their ability to overcome strain diversity (Liu et al., 2016; Noro et al., 2007; Noro et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2011; Sakamoto et al., 2013).

From a practical perspective, the transition from in silico prediction to experimental validation will require careful prioritization. While some candidates, such as lipoproteins, are generally amenable to recombinant expression in E. coli (Leng et al., 2015), others, such as beta-barrel outer membrane proteins, pose challenges related to folding and stability (Bannwarth and Schulz, 2003). Furthermore, multi-pass membrane proteins may present additional production challenges (Korepanova et al., 2005). These constraints will need to be weighed against the immunological relevance of each candidate during downstream validation studies.

Overall, this study reframes antigen discovery for infectious coryza from strain-specific characterization toward systematic, population-informed prioritization. Continuous and comprehensive surveillance of Av. paragallinarum across all geographic regions is logistically challenging and impractical. In this context, our approach for identifying conserved antigens shared across serovars, regions, and time may represent a more rational and broadly protective strategy for vaccine development. By focusing on conserved and functionally relevant targets, this work provides a framework for the rational development of next-generation vaccines aimed at overcoming the limitations of current bacterin-based strategies.

Conclusion

This study applied a reverse vaccinology framework to systematically analyze the antigenic landscape of Av. paragallinarum across a diverse set of strains. Through a multi-criteria selection strategy, 13 candidate antigens were identified that combine key features associated with vaccine potential, including conservation, predicted surface exposure, antigenicity and lack of similarity to host proteins.

The identified candidates include both well-characterized protein families with prior evidence of protective immunity in other bacterial systems and less-explored targets that expand the repertoire of candidate antigens. Together, they represent a focused and biologically informed set of proteins for future experimental evaluation.

By prioritizing conserved antigens, this work directly addresses the antigenic variability that limits the effectiveness of current vaccines. These findings provide a foundation for the development of multicomponent subunit vaccines with the potential to achieve broader and more consistent protection against infectious coryza.
