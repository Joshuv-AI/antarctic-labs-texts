Indole-3-Butyric Acid (IBA) Derivatives for Root Architecture Modulation: Potent Promoters via In Vivo Conversion to IBA and Selective Inhibitors for Lateral Root Formation
Authors: Rie Kikuchi, Takeshi Yamada, Ami Watanabe, Koyo Shibasaki, Kazuki Arai, Haruka Yamamoto, Kouta Ibe, Sentaro Okamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Indole-3-butyric acid (IBA)-derived compounds, methyl 2-hydroxy-5-(1H-indole-3-yl)­pentanoate (JAX-44) and its acid derivative (JAX-77), emerge as potent modulators of root architecture, exhibiting significant growth-promoting activity for lateral and adventitious roots. Crucially, unlike native IBA, which often inhibits primary root elongation at higher concentrations, JAX-44 and JAX-77 maintain primary root length while substantially increasing lateral root numbers, thereby optimizing the plant’s overall root system. Mass spectrometry analyses in Arabidopsis thaliana elucidated their mechanism: JAX-44 undergoes in vivo conversion to IBA via enzymatic hydrolysis to JAX-77, followed by oxidation to a keto-acid intermediate and finally YUCCA-mediated oxidative decarbonylation. This conversion process specifically requires an N-unsubstituted indole structure. Conversely, N- or O-methylation of JAX-44, yielding compounds JAX-86 and JAX-83, switches their activity from promotion to selective inhibition of lateral root formation. This inhibitory effect is attributed to the methylation preventing the necessary oxidative decarbonylation pathway. These results suggest that JAX-44 and JAX-77 are not only potentially superior plant growth regulators with enhanced flexibility and optimized root development for practical use but also valuable chemical biology tools that provide insight into auxin biology and the specific structural requirements for IBA bioactivity.

Introduction

Auxin, one of the plant hormones, plays a key role in nearly all aspects of plant development, mediating cell division, elongation, and differentiation. − It regulates embryogenesis, apical dominance, lateral root formation, gravitropic and phototropic responses, and differentiation of vascular tissues at the whole plant level. , A central task in understanding all aspects of auxin activity is to identify specific receptors and interaction partners for indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). To date, intracellular IAA receptors have been discovered that control the degradation of a subset of transcription factors, thereby regulating the transcriptional auxin response. − However, many aspects of auxin action remain to be elucidated.

Unlike IAA, IBA alone is thought to have little transcriptional regulatory activity. IBA is thought to function primarily as a transport and storage form of IAA. , IAA can be converted to IBA, and IBA is subsequently converted back to IAA by a process similar to β-oxidation in peroxisomes. Mutants defective in peroxisome biogenesis or β-oxidation are resistant to exogenously administered IBA, and the conversion of IBA to IAA is inhibited. ,− These genetic approaches suggest that the conversion of IBA to IAA may explain the unique biological activity of IBA. The conversion of IBA to IAA has been shown to be associated with undisturbed seedling development by supplying an internal active auxin pool. One phenotypic feature of many mutants unable to utilize IBA is the paucity of lateral roots. ,,− This indicates that the conversion of IBA to IAA is required for lateral root formation (LRF); thus, the spatially and temporally coordinated release of IAA is necessary for IBA bioactivity. As a practical application, IBA has been found to be important for both softwood and hardwood cuttings. − However, the field of IBA research has faced several long-standing challenges that have limited a comprehensive understanding of its role in plant development. First, the direct detection and precise tracing of endogenous IBA and its metabolites in plants have proven difficult, often complicated by its rapid conversion to IAA and various conjugates. This has created ambiguities in understanding IBA’s specific roles and movement within the plant. Second, despite IBA’s critical function as an auxin precursor, the complete network of its biosynthesis pathways remains largely unknown, and the identity of many specific transporters for its cellular uptake and peroxisomal efflux has yet to be fully elucidated.

A large number of IAA-related compounds, including IAA derivatives such as NAA (2-(naphthalen-1-yl)­acetic acid) and 2,4-D (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid), have been developed and are widely used as chemical tools, such as IAA receptor agonists and antagonists, or as inhibitors of biosynthesis and transport, in the fields of biological science and agricultural applications. On the other hand, although IBA itself is used in applied fields as mentioned above, there are very few examples of the development of IBA derivatives.

Here, we report the synthesis of several IBA-derived agents and their biological activity in Arabidopsis thaliana and rice. Among them, methyl 2-hydroxy-5-(1H-indole-3-yl)­pentanoate (JAX-44) and 2-hydroxy-5-(1H-indole-3-yl)­pentanoic acid (JAX-77) show potent promoting effects on LRF in Arabidopsis thaliana and adventitious root formation in rice. Meanwhile, N- or O-methyl analogues of JAX-44, i.e., methyl 2-hydroxy-5-(1-methyl-1H-indol-3-yl)­pentanoate (JAX-86) and methyl 5-(1H-indol-3-yl)-2-methoxypentanoate (JAX-83), selectively inhibited LRF in Arabidopsis thaliana.

Previous studies using exogenous 3H- or 13C-labeled IBA as a tracer and MS analysis to track its metabolism and transport have yielded significant results. − The JAX-44, JAX-77, and their deuterium-labeled forms provided in this study are exogenously applied agents that are converted to IBA through internal metabolism after absorption by plants. Because these compounds can mimic an endogenous excess of IBA, they can contribute to metabolic and transport analyses of IBA and its related pathways. Furthermore, JAX-86 and JAX-83, which selectively inhibit lateral root formation, are also expected to be useful for metabolic analyses related to the IBA.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Compounds 1–10 were synthesized, and their synthetic procedures and characterizations are given in the Supporting Information. IBA was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (4-Phenoxyphenyl)­boronic (PPBo) acid was purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co.

General Information for Chemical Synthesis and Analyses

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded at 600, 500, and 400 MHz for 1H and 150, 125, and 100 MHz for 13C using the JEOL JNM-ECA600, JNM-ECA500, and JNM-ECZ400 instruments, respectively, in a CDCl3 or d 6-DMSO solvent. Chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm, δ) relative to Me4Si (δ 0.00) or residual CHCl3 (δ 7.26 for 1H NMR) or CDCl3 (δ 77.0 for 13C NMR). Infrared (IR) spectra were recorded by using a JASCO FT/IR 4100 spectrometer. High-resolution mass spectra (HR-MS) were measured by using a JEOL Accu TOF T100 equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) unit. All oxygen- and moisture-sensitive reactions were performed under an Ar atmosphere. Dry solvents (THF, diethyl ether, CH2Cl2, and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF)) were purchased from Kanto Chemicals.

Bioassay

The sterilized seeds of Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia-0 were sown onto a half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) medium (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Japan), 0.8% (w/v) agar (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Japan), and 1% (w/v) sucrose (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Japan), pH 5.8. Various concentrations (1–100 μM) of IBA derivatives (compounds 1–5), dissolved in 0.1% (v/v) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), were added to the agar plates to investigate their effect on root development. Arabidopsis seedlings were grown on a vertically oriented agar medium for 6–8 days at 22 °C under continuous light (42.93 μmol/m2/s) after being kept at 4 °C for 1–3 days. The primary root elongation and lateral root formation of eight-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings were measured using National Institutes of Health (NIH) Image software. The length of the primary root (PRL) and the number of lateral roots (LRs) were counted, and the lateral root density (LRD) was calculated by dividing the LR by the PRL.

Rice (Oryza sativa cv. Haenuki) seeds were surface-sterilized with a 10% sodium hypochlorite solution for 20 min and washed five times with sterile water. The seeds were plated on a 1/2 MS medium with or without 1 (JAX-44). The rice seedlings were cultured under continuous light for 5 days. Primary root lengths were measured by using NIH Image software.

Tracking Compounds by Mass Spectrometry

Arabidopsis thaliana (Columbia-0) seeds were sterilized with 10% sodium hypochlorite solution for 10 min and then washed five times with sterile distilled water. After washing, the supernatant was removed, 0.1% agar was added, and the seeds were dispersed by pipetting. The sterilized seeds were sown on a 1/2 Murashige–Skoog (1/2 MS) agar medium. After a half-day vernalization at 4 °C to induce germination, the seeds were grown for 6 days in an artificial climate chamber (TOMY, CLE-405, illuminance: 42.93 μmol/m2/s) at 22 °C and a 24 h light period. 50 mL tubes containing 10 mL of the compound solution were prepared, and approximately 20 plants were transferred from the medium to each tube and cultured under shaking conditions for 24 h. Arabidopsis plants were collected from 50 mL tubes, blotted dry, and then placed in a 2 mL tube containing 5 mm stainless steel or zirconia beads for crushing the plant samples. Subsequently, they were quickly frozen using liquid nitrogen, and the plant samples were crushed twice per sample using a QIAGEN Tissue Lyser LT at 45 Hz for 25 s. One mL of diethyl ether or ethanol was added to the tube containing the plant sample and stirred using a vortex mixer for about 30 s. After the tube was left in a refrigerator for 16 h, the supernatant was collected with a Pasteur pipette and filtered through cotton placed in a funnel. Another 1 mL of diethyl ether or ethanol was added to the tube containing the plant sample, and the supernatant was collected and filtered. The combined supernatant was then concentrated using an evaporator. The concentrated plant sample was dissolved in methanol and filtered (ADVANTEC DISMIC-13HP disposable membrane filter unit, 0.2 μm), and the filtrate was concentrated. The resulting sample was used for mass spectrometry (JEOL Accu-TOF T100). A sample solution was introduced into the mass spectrometer at a flow rate of 50 μL/min using a pump.

Results and Discussion

IBA-derived compounds 1–5 were readily synthesized according to the procedure depicted in Scheme . Starting with IBA ethyl ester (6), it was converted to the corresponding cyanohydrin 7 via reduction and reaction with HCN. Compound 7 was treated with acid in methanol to provide methyl 2-hydroxy-5-(1H-indol-3-yl)­pentanoate (1) (JAX-44, a compound numbered as part of our auxin analogue library). The corresponding carboxylic acid 2 (JAX-77) was prepared as a HCl salt. From 2, deuterium-labeled d 8 -1 (d8-JAX-44) was prepared by the acidic atom-exchange method developed by us. Compound 1 was converted to N-methyl analogue 3 (JAX-86) via its silyl-protected derivative 8. Meanwhile, O-methylated analogue 4 (JAX-83) was obtained by methylation of N-Cbz compound 9 derived from 8 and subsequent deprotection. Oxidation of compound 9 afforded 10, which was then deprotected to produce ketone analogue 5 (JAX-80).

As shown in Figure a, IBA exhibited a significant reduction in PRL with increasing concentrations, showing nearly no growth at 10 and 30 μM. In contrast, JAX-44 and JAX-77 maintained relatively longer primary root lengths even at higher concentrations (e.g., 10 μM, 30 μM) compared to IBA, indicating a reduced inhibitory effect on primary root elongation. Regarding effects on the number of lateral roots (Figure b), IBA induced an increase in the lateral root number at low concentrations (10 nM to 1 μM), peaking around 100 nM to 300 nM, but the number significantly decreased at high concentrations (10 μM, 30 μM). Meanwhile, JAX-44 and JAX-77, in comparison to IBA, showed peaks in the lateral root number at higher concentrations (approximately 1 μM to 3 μM) and induced a greater number of lateral roots even at concentrations where IBA suppressed lateral root formation. Overall, JAX-44 and JAX-77 tended to induce a higher total number of lateral roots than IBA. Similarly, from the results shown in Figure c, IBA increased LRD at low concentrations, peaking around 100–300 nM, and then decreasing at high concentrations. JAX-44 and JAX-77 induced higher LRDs than IBA, with their peaks also observed at higher concentrations (approximately 1 μM to 3 μM), maintaining high lateral root density even in concentration ranges where IBA showed inhibition.

As a result, JAX-44 and JAX-77 allow for continued primary root development while promoting lateral branching, which can be crucial for the overall root system establishment. This is particularly evident at higher concentrations where IBA’s ability to induce lateral roots is severely diminished or completely suppressed, while JAX-44 and JAX-77 continue to promote lateral root formation. Furthermore, their peak concentrations for lateral root induction are shifted toward higher concentrations than IBA, suggesting greater flexibility in application. These results collectively suggest that JAX-44 and JAX-77 could be superior plant growth regulators compared to IBA, as they can effectively promote lateral root development while avoiding excessive primary root inhibition, thereby optimizing root architecture.

Although 1 (JAX-44) was used as its racemic mixture, enantiomers of compound 1 were separated by high-performance liquid chromatography using a chiral stationary phase column (Daicel CHIRALPAK IE, 0.45Φ × 150 mm, i-PrOH/hexane = 1/4 (v/v), 0.50 mL/min, t R = 17.1, and 18.3 min), and their activities were studied (Figure e,f). As a result, it was found that one enantiomer had a slightly higher inhibitory activity against primary root elongation at high concentrations, but there was no significant difference in activity between the enantiomers.

The effects on the lateral root primordia formation were investigated in detail (Figure ). Arabidopsis plants were grown on an agar medium containing various concentrations of 1 (JAX-44) for 4 days. The plants were then fixed with formaldehyde and made transparent using ClearSee, and the lateral root primordia were observed under a differential interference contrast microscope. The observed lateral root primordia (LRP) were classified into seven stages (I–VII), and lateral roots (LRs) were divided into two stages (VIII and IX).

JAX-44 consistently demonstrated a strong capacity to induce the formation of LRP and subsequent lateral roots across all tested concentrations, ranging from 1 to 30 μM. A notable feature is JAX-44’s effectiveness in driving the progression of LRP through advanced developmental stages and emergence. At all concentrations, particularly from 1 to 10 μM, there was a significant accumulation of LRP in later stages (IV, V, and VI), indicating successful development from initial cell divisions to the formation of fully emerged lateral roots. While the highest overall induction appears to be around 3 μM to 10 μM, even at the highest tested concentration of 30 μM, the compound still effectively promotes the formation and progression of lateral roots. This contrasts with typical auxin responses where very high concentrations often inhibit development. Unlike many plant hormones that may cause developmental arrest or abnormalities at elevated concentrations, JAX-44 maintains the capacity to facilitate the full progression of LRP into emerged lateral roots even at relatively high concentrations (e.g., 10 and 30 μM). This is reflected by the continued strong presence of LRP in stages IV, V, and VI at these concentrations. These findings highlight JAX-44’s potent and consistent ability to not only initiate lateral root development but also ensure their successful progression through all developmental stages and emergence across a broad range of concentrations.

In contrast to the observed promoting effects of JAX-44 on lateral root development and the concentration-dependent dual effects of IBA, compounds 3 (JAX-86) and 4 (JAX-83) exhibit selective inhibitory effects on root formation, specifically impacting lateral roots (Figure ). The provided data elucidate the quantitative aspects of this inhibition of both lateral root density (LRD) and primary root length (PRL). Both JAX-86 and JAX-83 demonstrate a clear dose-dependent and selective reduction in relative lateral root density, with the latter being a more potent inhibitor. For JAX-86, the concentration required to inhibit 50% of lateral root density (IC50 LRD) is 3.02 μM, while IC50 for primary root length (IC50 PRL) is 30.9 μM. For JAX-83, the IC50 LRD is 0.56 μM, and the IC50 PRL is 5.85 μM. A critical aspect of their selective nature is revealed by the calculated selectivity ratios, defined as IC50(PRL)/IC50(LRD). For JAX-86 and JAX-83, the ratios are 10.2 and 10.4, respectively. As a result, N- and O-methylation of 1 were found to switch the activity from promoting to inhibiting lateral root formation.

Next, we investigated the effect of compound 1 (JAX-44) on the growth of rice seedlings (Oryza sativa cv. Haenuki) (Figure ). In order to clarify whether the chemicals discussed here undergo structural transformation after absorption into plants, their transformation was tracked by using mass spectrometry.

Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0) was grown on an agar medium in an artificial climate chamber for 6 days and then cultured with shaking in an aqueous solution of a specified concentration of each compound for 24 h. The plants were harvested, thoroughly dried, and then quickly frozen with liquid nitrogen and crushed. The crushed plant sample was suspended in diethyl ether and left to stand in a refrigerator for 16 h, after which the supernatant was collected. Diethyl ether was added once more to the remaining plant sample, which was then stirred and left to stand, after which the supernatant was collected. The combined supernatants were filtered and concentrated. It was then dissolved in methanol, filtered, concentrated again, and subjected to mass spectrometry. Mass spectra are shown in Figure along with the intensities of each peak.

(b) After shaking in an aqueous solution of d8-JAX-44 (100 μM) for 3 h. (c) After shaking in an aqueous solution of d8-JAX-44 (30 μM) for 3 h in the absence or presence of PPBo (100 μM). (d) After shaking in an aqueous solution of d5-JAX-86 (100 μM) for 3 h.

Figure a shows the results of the experiment using hydroxy ester 1 (JAX-44), which is an LRF promoter. IBA was barely detected in the untreated control samples (DMSO). On the other hand, upon treatment with JAX-44 (100 μM, 3 h), JAX-44 was almost completely eliminated, and its carboxylic acid 2 (JAX-77) was observed, along with a significant increase in IBA. When deuterated d8-1 (d8-1:d7-1 = 2.5:1) was used, the corresponding deuterated carboxylic acid d5-2 and d5-IBA (and d4-IBA) were detected (Figure b). In other words, it was revealed that after 1 (JAX-44) was taken up into plants, rapid hydrolysis occurred and further oxidative decarbonylation (one-carbon decrement) occurred.

As described above, 1 (JAX-44) and 2 (JAX-77) exhibited potent LRF-promoting activity. In addition, it was found that keto ester derivative 5 (JAX-80) also showed weak LRF-promoting activity. Therefore, acid 2 and the corresponding keto acid are considered to be intermediates in the conversion of 1 to IBA. The structural transformation of 2 to IBA, i.e., oxidative decarbonylation, is of the same type as the conversion of indole-3-pyruvic acid (IPyA) to IAA and may be mediated by YUCCA, one of the flavin-containing monooxygenases (FMOs) that catalyzes the transformation of IPyA to IAA. − To examine this possibility, we treated Arabidopsis plants with PPBo (100 μM, 3 h), a selective YUCCA inhibitor, followed by treatment with compound 1 (30 μM, 3 h), and then performed mass spectrometry (Figure c). As a result, in the presence of PPBo, the hydrolysis of 1 to form 2 was detected, but the production of IBA was significantly reduced to about 1/6 of that in the absence of PPBo.

In contrast, experiments with N-methyl derivative 3 (d5-3 [d5-JAX-86]) (100 μM, 24 h), which is an LRF inhibitor, showed that while a large amount of 3 remained, a certain amount of the corresponding carboxylic acid was detected. However, the corresponding N-methyl IBA product was scarcely detected (Figure d). In other words, the oxidative decarbonylation reaction requires an N-unsubstituted indole structure.

Accordingly, it is highly likely that the transformation of 2 to IBA is mediated by YUCCA (and/or a similar enzyme), a conclusion also supported by the transformation’s specificity for an N–H indole structure.

Based on these results, the metabolic pathway shown in Scheme is proposed: after being taken up into the plant, compound 1 (JAX-44) is enzymatically hydrolyzed to carboxylic acid 2, which is then further oxidized to keto form 11. Compound 11 is decarboxylated by YUCCA (and/or a similar enzyme) to produce IBA. On the other hand, compounds 3 (JAX-86) and 4 (JAX-83) do not undergo this oxidative decarboxylation reaction, but remain as themselves or as their corresponding acid derivatives and exhibit inhibitory effects. We assume that they may inhibit the conversion of IBA to IAA (β-oxidation), but details must be studied further.

Transformation of 1 (JAX-44) in Arabidopsis thaliana

Many laboratories have pointed out the difficulty of direct detection and precise quantification of IBA. This has been a major obstacle to accurate studies of IBA metabolism and transport dynamics. ,, The JAX-44 and JAX-77 compounds developed in this study are converted to IBA in plants, making them exogenous reagents that can artificially and controllably increase endogenous IBA levels by using endogenous metabolic machinery. In particular, the deuterium-labeled d8-JAX-44 used in this study enables stable isotope-labeled metabolomics analysis to track the metabolic flux of IBA and its metabolites (e.g., JAX-77 and keto-acid intermediates) with greater precision than conventional tracer studies. This provides a clear target and methodology for the elucidation of the dynamics of IBA production, degradation, transport, and conjugation at the molecular level, paving the way for a more detailed understanding of the role of IBA in root development.

In conclusion, this study reports the development of several IBA-derived agents, exhibiting either potent promotion or inhibition of the activity of lateral root formation (LRF). A key finding is the proposed metabolic pathway for methyl 2-hydroxy-5-(1H-indol-3-yl)­pentanoate 1 (JAX-44) in Arabidopsis thaliana. Upon uptake, JAX-44 is enzymatically hydrolyzed to carboxylic acid 2 (JAX-77). Subsequently, JAX-77 is oxidized to a keto form, which undergoes decarboxylation, likely mediated by the YUCCA enzyme, to produce IBA. This enzymatic conversion is specific to an N-H indole structure and is significantly reduced by the YUCCA inhibitor PPBo, supporting YUCCA’s involvement.

Compounds 1 (JAX-44) and 2 (JAX-77) demonstrated potent LRF-promoting activity. Unlike IBA, which severely inhibits primary root length and suppresses lateral root formation at higher concentrations, JAX-44 and JAX-77 maintained relatively longer primary root lengths while inducing a greater number of lateral roots. They enabled continued primary root development concurrently with enhanced lateral branching, optimizing the overall root architecture. JAX-44 also consistently facilitated the progression of lateral root primordia through advanced developmental stages, culminating in their emergence, even at relatively high concentrations, at which typical auxins might cause inhibition. In rice, JAX-44 exhibited promoting effects on adventitious root formation at concentrations above 3 μM, alongside an inhibition of the primary root.

Conversely, N-methylated analogue 3 (JAX-86) and O-methylated analogue 4 (JAX-83) exhibited selective inhibitory effects on root formation, specifically impacting lateral roots. This inhibition stems from N- or O-methylation, which prevents their oxidative decarbonylation. These inhibitory compounds are hypothesized to impede the β-oxidation pathway, which is responsible for the conversion of IBA to IAA, a process critical for lateral root formation.

These findings highlight JAX-44 and JAX-77 as potentially superior plant growth regulators compared with IBA, offering greater flexibility and optimized root architecture for practical applications in agriculture and horticulture. The insights into their metabolic pathways and the inhibitory mechanisms of methylated derivatives provide valuable knowledge for understanding auxin biology and developing novel plant growth regulators. Furthermore, these reagents are exogenous agents that can increase the level of endogenous IBA in plants, distinct from the IAA-derived biosynthetic pathway. IBA is normally present in trace amounts, making it difficult to analyze its physiological functions and metabolic pathways. However, by using these compounds and their deuterium-labeled counterparts, it is possible to mimic conditions in which IBA is in excess and to perform detailed metabolomic analyses of the resulting changes in root development and of the effects of IBA and its related metabolites on other plant metabolic pathways.
