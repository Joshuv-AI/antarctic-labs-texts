Substantially Higher Vestibular Hydrops Agreement in Older Patients Assessed by Non-contrast vs. Contrast-enhanced MRI: A Preliminary Study
Authors: Shinji Naganawa, Rintaro Ito, Yutaka Kato, Masumi Kobayashi, Toshiaki Taoka, Tadao Yoshida, Michihiko Sone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis preliminary study aimed to investigate the agreement in endolymphatic hydrops (EH) grading between high-resolution non-contrast and contrast-enhanced HYDROPS (HYbriD of Reversed image Of Positive endolymph signal and native image of positive perilymph Signal) techniques in the same subjects and to clarify the effect of patient age on the concordance between the 2 methods.MethodsA retrospective study was performed on 12 patients (24 ears; age range: 24–76 years) suspected of EH, all of whom underwent both non-contrast and 4-hour post-contrast 3T MRI. EH in the cochlea and vestibule was graded by the Nakashima scale. Agreement analyses were assessed using the weighted Cohen’s kappa (κ). Statistical significance, including the difference between Younger (24–54 years) and Older (57–76 years) patient subgroups, was determined via bootstrap analysis.ResultsThe overall agreement between non-contrast and contrast-enhanced methods was fair for both the cochlea (κ = 0.343) and vestibule (κ = 0.398). Subgroup analysis revealed a significant age-related difference in vestibular agreement (P = 0.005), showing substantial agreement in the Older group (κ = 0.795) but only slight agreement in the Younger group (κ = 0.113). No significant age-related difference was found for cochlear agreement. Quantitatively, the non-contrast method demonstrated a significantly lower contrast-to-noise ratio compared to the contrast-enhanced method.ConclusionThe concordance between non-contrast and contrast-enhanced MRI for EH assessment is site- and age-dependent. The substantially higher agreement found in older patients suggests that age-related physiological changes facilitate non-contrast visualization. However, caution is advised when interpreting non-contrast findings in younger patients due to potential risks of vestibular overestimation and cochlear underestimation. Given the preliminary nature and small sample size of this study, further investigations with larger cohorts are necessary to validate these findings and the appropriateness of age-based categorization.

Introduction

The visualization of endolymphatic hydrops (EH) by MRI in Meniere’s disease has evolved significantly since the first report using intratympanic administration of gadolinium-based contrast agent (GBCA) in 2007.1 While intratympanic administration allows for clear separate visualization of fluid compartments,2 intravenous GBCA administration has become the standard due to its non-invasiveness.3,4 The HYDROPS (HYbriD of Reversed image Of Positive endolymph signal and native image of positive perilymph Signal) method, developed in 2012, enables robust EH imaging 4 hours after intravenous Gd injection.5 Since then, various improvements in sequences and post-processing have been reported to enhance image quality and shorten scan times.6–8

Recently, there has been growing interest in non-contrast MRI techniques to visualize EH without GBCA.9–23 These methods typically rely on the intrinsic T1 relaxation time differences between endolymph and perilymph. However, non-contrast imaging faces technical challenges. The signal difference between fluids is subtle, and images are prone to artifacts from B1 inhomogeneity, especially near the air and the bone.10,12,13,19–22 Consequently, the reported diagnostic accuracy of non-contrast methods has shown considerable variability in comparison to contrast-enhanced techniques, particularly in studies employing inversion recovery pulses combined with T2-preparation protocols.13,21,24–27 Our clinical experience suggests that non-contrast EH visualization might be clearer in older patients, possibly due to age-related changes in inner ear fluid composition. However, the effect of age on the concordance between non-contrast and contrast-enhanced methods remains unclear.

The purpose of this preliminary study is to investigate the agreement in EH grading between non-contrast and contrast-enhanced HYDROPS images in the same subjects and to clarify the influence of patient age. We aim to identify specific pitfalls of non-contrast imaging to establish a foundation for its future refinement.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

This retrospective study reviewed 12 patients (24 ears; age range: 24–76 years, median: 56; 3 males) suspected of having EH at our otorhinolaryngology department. All patients underwent both non-contrast and 4-hour post-contrast MRI according to our institutional protocol between October 2024 and May 2025.

MR imaging

All non-contrast and contrast-enhanced sequences were performed on the same day using a 3T MRI scanner (Skyra; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32-channel array head coil. Non-contrast imaging was performed around 11:00 A.M., followed by a single dose (0.1 mmol/kg) intravenous injection of Gadobutrol (Gadovist; Bayer Yakuhin, Osaka, Japan), and post-contrast imaging 4 hours later. The non-contrast protocol was designed to acquire a positive perilymph image (PPI) and a positive endolymph image (PEI) by modifying the inversion time (TI) of our previous non-contrast 3D-real inversion recovery protocol, referencing prior studies.7,13,21,27 Key protocol features included using a long TR of 16000 msec and setting the TI difference between PPI and PEI to 250 msec (based on previous reports), and employing an enhanced spatial resolution of 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm × 1.0 mm. The contrast-enhanced imaging protocol used was our institution’s established protocol, based on extensive experience.5 Detailed imaging parameters are presented in Table 1.

Image processing

For both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced HYDROPS, the final HYDROPS image was generated by subtracting the PEI from the PPI, allowing for negative signal values. Although non-rigid registration software was available to suppress misregistration artifacts due to patient movement, it was not utilized in this study as no displacement exceeding the radius of the semicircular canals was observed.

Qualitative evaluation

EH in the cochlea and vestibule was graded on both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced HYDROPS images using the Nakashima grading scale (0: no hydrops, 1: mild hydrops, 2: significant hydrops).28 Two experienced neuroradiologists (S.N. and T.T.) independently performed the grading.

Additionally, we assessed the visibility of endolymph in the posterior ampulla. Although not part of the Nakashima grading criteria, the posterior ampulla is known for stable recognition with contrast enhancement,29 and its visibility serves as a sensitive indicator of contrast resolution between endolymph and perilymph.

Disagreements between the 2 readers were resolved by consensus. For assessment, the left and right ears were presented separately as distinct cases. A reading interval of more than 2 months was maintained between the non-contrast and contrast-enhanced HYDROPS images.

Quantitative evaluation

For contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) measurement, circular ROIs were placed in the utricle for endolymph and the scala tympani of the cochlear basal turn for perilymph, as these were the most stable locations for ROI placement. Noise was defined as the standard deviation (SD) of a ROI placed in the white matter of the cerebellar hemisphere. ROI diameters were 1.0 mm for the utricle and the scala tympani of the basal turn, and 2 cm for the cerebellar white matter. Measurements were performed by 1 neuroradiologist (S.N.).

Statistical analysis

Inter-observer agreement was assessed using quadratically weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficients (κ) for the ordinal Nakashima grade (0, 1, 2). Agreement was interpreted as slight (0.00–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), or almost perfect (0.81–1.00). To determine if contrast enhancement improved inter-observer agreement, we compared the kappa coefficients between the contrast-enhanced (κcontrast) and non-contrast (κnon-contrast) methods. A bootstrap method with 10000 iterations was employed to estimate the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the difference in kappa values (κcontrast − κnon-contrast) for the cochlea and vestibule separately. A difference was considered statistically significant if the 95% CI did not include zero. The degree of agreement between the 2 imaging methods (non-contrast vs. contrast-enhanced) was also evaluated using the weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient (quadratic weighting). All agreement analyses were conducted separately for the cochlea and vestibule, utilizing data from both ears (24 ears in 12 patients).

To explore the effect of age, patients were divided into 2 equal groups:

Younger group: 6 patients (24–54 years old, 2 men, 12 ears)

Older group: 6 patients (57–76 years old, 1 man, 12 ears)

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare EH severity between age groups for cochlear and vestibular evaluations in both imaging methods. Furthermore, to assess systematic bias between the 2 imaging methods (i.e., whether one method consistently assigned higher hydrops grades than the other), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed. This analysis was performed for the overall cohort and stratified by age group (Younger vs. Older) for both the cochlea and vestibule. All analyses for qualitative evaluation were performed using Python (version 3.11) with pandas (version 2.2), NumPy (version 1.26), and scikit-learn (version 1.5). Weighted κ coefficients were computed using the cohen_kappa_score() function with weights =“quadratic.” Comparison of the CNR between the non-contrast HYDROPS and contrast-enhanced HYDROPS methods was performed on 24 ears. Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.11) with pandas (version 2.2) and SciPy (version 1.12.0) libraries. Normality of the differences was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since the data followed a normal distribution, a paired t-test was employed to compare the mean CNR values.

All data are presented as mean ± SD. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (2025-0162). The requirement for informed consent was waived.

Results

Qualitative analysis

Inter-observer agreement

The inter-observer reliability for grading EH varied by imaging method and anatomical site.

For the cochlea, the inter-observer agreement was moderate using the non-contrast method (κ = 0.581) and improved to almost perfect using the contrast-enhanced method (κ = 0.947). The bootstrap analysis revealed a statistically significant difference between the 2 methods (Difference = 0.366; 95% CI: 0.123 to 0.682), indicating that contrast enhancement significantly improved reliability for cochlear evaluation (P < 0.05).

For the vestibule, agreement was substantial for the non-contrast method (κ = 0.803) and almost perfect for the contrast-enhanced method (κ = 0.929). Although the kappa value was numerically higher with contrast enhancement, the difference was not statistically significant (Difference = 0.126; 95% CI: −0.062 to 0.333; P > 0.05).

The results are summarized in Table 2.

Overall agreement between methods

The grading of EH by the consensus of 2 observers is presented in Table 3. For all 24 ears combined, the weighted kappa coefficient between the non-contrast and contrast-enhanced methods was 0.343 for the cochlea and 0.398 for the vestibule, indicating fair agreement for both sites. Furthermore, bootstrap analysis showed no significant difference between cochlear and vestibular κ values (95% CI of difference: −0.43 to 0.31; P = 0.80).

Age-related subgroup analysis

To explore the effect of age, patients were divided into 2 equal groups: Younger (n = 6 patients, 12 ears, 24–54 years old) and Older (n = 6 patients, 12 ears, 57–76 years old). The weighted kappa coefficients revealed notable differences in agreement between the 2 imaging methods across the age subgroups. In the Younger group, the agreement was Fair for the cochlea (κ = 0.262) but only Slight for the vestibule (κ = 0.113). In contrast, the Older group showed Moderate agreement for the cochlea (κ = 0.478) and Substantial agreement for the vestibule (κ = 0.795). A bootstrap comparison of the κ values between the Younger and Older groups indicated no significant difference for the cochlea (95% CI: −0.75 to 0.47; P = 0.53). However, a statistically significant difference was found for the vestibule (95% CI: −1.01 to −0.24; P = 0.005), confirming a substantially higher agreement in the older patient group (Figs. 1, 2) (Table 4).

Age differences in hydrops severity

Mann–Whitney U tests showed no significant differences except a trend for contrast-enhanced cochlear hydrops (P = 0.070).

Systematic bias in hydrops grading

In the overall cohort (24 ears), the non-contrast method assigned significantly higher hydrops grades in the vestibule compared to the contrast-enhanced method (P = 0.010). Conversely, in the cochlea, the contrast-enhanced method tended to assign higher grades (P = 0.053). Subgroup analysis revealed that these discrepancies were driven primarily by the Younger group. In younger patients, the non-contrast method significantly overestimated vestibular hydrops severity compared to the contrast-enhanced method (median: 2.0 vs. 0.0; mean: 1.33 vs. 0.42; P = 0.018). In contrast, for cochlear hydrops in the Younger group, the contrast-enhanced method assigned significantly higher grades (median: 1.5 vs. 0.0; mean: 1.33 vs. 0.58; P = 0.024), suggesting that the non-contrast method underestimated cochlear hydrops.

Notably, in the Older group, no statistically significant systematic bias was observed between the 2 methods for either the cochlea (P = 1.00) or the vestibule (P = 0.317), consistent with the high kappa agreement observed in this population.

Visualization of endolymph in posterior ampulla

In contrast-enhanced HYDROPS images, the endolymph in posterior ampulla was visible in all 24 ears. In the non-contrast HYDROPS images, the endolymph in posterior ampulla was visible only in 2 ears of 2 patients.

Quantitative analysis

Comparison of CNR

The quantitative analysis results are summarized in Fig. 3. The mean CNR for the non-contrast HYDROPS method was 11.84 ± 4.44, whereas the mean CNR for the contrast-enhanced HYDROPS method was 26.18 ± 8.23. The contrast-enhanced HYDROPS method demonstrated a significantly higher CNR compared to the non-contrast method (P < 0.001). The representative images of the patients are shown in Figs. 4 and 5.

Discussion

The present preliminary study demonstrated that the concordance between non-contrast and contrast-enhanced MRI for EH assessment is significantly influenced by age. While overall agreement was fair, the Older group showed substantially higher agreement, particularly in the vestibule. Conversely, in the Younger group, the non-contrast method showed poor agreement with the contrast-enhanced method, which is considered the current clinical reference standard. Previous studies using non-contrast methods have pointed to the difficulty in depicting endolymph in non-hydrops ears21 and the prolongation of endolymph T1 value in hydrops ears.27,30 Despite no significant difference in the severity of EH between the Younger and Older groups in the present study, the agreement between the contrast-enhanced and non-contrast methods was higher in the Older group, especially in vestibule. This suggests that age has some effect on the depiction of EH with the non-contrast method.

Age-dependent agreement and physiological mechanisms

The higher agreement in older patients supports the hypothesis that age-related changes in perilymph composition enhance contrast. In humans, the permeability of the blood-perilymph barrier increases with age,31 and animal studies have shown increased protein concentration in perilymph with aging.32 Elevated protein concentration shortens the T1 relaxation time of perilymph. This likely enlarges the T1 difference between perilymph and endolymph (which generally has a longer T1), thereby improving the CNR and visualization in non-contrast inversion recovery sequences.

Mechanisms of discrepancy in younger patients

A key finding of this study is the systematic bias observed in the Younger group: overestimation of hydrops in the vestibule and underestimation in the cochlea.

For the vestibule, the overestimation is likely due to artifacts. In younger patients with lower perilymph protein levels, the T1 difference between fluids is minimal. As a result, signal suppression remains unstable, and artifacts adjacent to the bone—attributable to B1 inhomogeneity—can persist and simulate the appearance of “significant hydrops,” even when employing an adiabatic inversion recovery pulse without T2-preparation.19,21,33

For the cochlea, we observed the opposite trend (underestimation). We propose a specific hypothesis for this discrepancy: the endolymph in the cochlea may have a shorter apparent T1 relaxation time compared to that in the vestibule, similar to the endolymph in the posterior ampulla.

In our study, the posterior ampulla—a structure not included in the Nakashima classification but useful for evaluating contrast resolution—was visualized in only 2 of 24 ears on non-contrast MRI, whereas it was visible in all ears on contrast-enhanced MRI. This poor visualization suggests that the T1 value of endolymph in the posterior ampulla is not sufficiently long to create contrast against perilymph in the absence of GBCA.

It is possible that cochlear endolymph shares this characteristic of shorter apparent T1. If the T1 of cochlear endolymph is shorter than that of the vestibular endolymph, the T1 difference between cochlear endolymph and perilymph would be even smaller, making it difficult to distinguish the endolymphatic space from perilymph. This would result in the non-visualization of the endolymphatic space, leading to a grading of “none” (underestimation), even when hydrops is present.

The mechanisms by which EH can be separately recognized by contrast-enhanced and non-contrast methods are different. The contrast-enhanced method primarily depends on the difference in the permeability of the GBCA between the endolymph and perilymph. Since the contrast effect in the perilymph is also useful for diagnosing Meniere’s disease, not just EH,25,34 the contrast-enhanced method retains an advantage in providing information that cannot be obtained with the non-contrast method. Conversely, the non-contrast method relies on the difference in T1 values resulting from compositional differences between the endolymph and perilymph.

Regarding electrolytes, endolymph is known to have a high potassium concentration, while perilymph has a high sodium concentration. For non-electrolytes, protein is said to be high in perilymph and low in endolymph. In EH, changes in the composition of both endolymph and perilymph are thought to occur.35 Previous reports have shown that T1 prolongation of the endolymph is primarily observed in hydrops ears, while T1 shortening of the perilymph is not significant.27 The mechanism for endolymph T1 prolongation is unknown, but previous reports estimate the cause as an increase in potassium concentration in the endolymph21 or a decrease in oxygen partial pressure36 (as oxygen has a T1-shortening effect). However, achieving T1 prolongation sufficient for image recognition at physiological in vivo potassium concentrations is considered unlikely,37 and changes in oxygen concentration remain unconfirmed. Elucidating the detailed compositional changes in the endolymph is of paramount importance to the pathophysiology of Meniere’s disease.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size is small (n = 12), which limits the statistical power. The division into Younger and Older groups was based on the median age of this specific cohort rather than a physiological cutoff. Therefore, the results regarding the specific age at which non-contrast imaging becomes reliable should be interpreted with caution. Second, the grading was subjective, although performed by experienced neuroradiologists. Third, we used contrast-enhanced MRI as the reference standard. While it is the most reliable clinical method currently available, it is not a histological gold standard.

Despite these limitations, this study warrants further validation as it highlights critical points of caution for the non-contrast method. To establish its utility and limitations, acquiring and comparing both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced methods in the same patient is currently important. Comparing both methods enables the simultaneous observation of compositional changes in the endolymph and perilymph, GBCA transit, and potentially the function of the labyrinthine glymphatic system.31,38 This may offer a novel approach to the pathology of Meniere’s disease. If the eyes and brain are included in the imaging FOV, the ocular glymphatic system39–42 and the brain’s glymphatic system43–49 can also be evaluated simultaneously.

Conclusion

Agreement between non-contrast and contrast-enhanced MRI for EH assessment is age-dependent. The non-contrast method shows higher reliability in older patients, likely due to increased perilymph protein concentration. However, in younger patients, there is a significant discrepancy characterized by vestibular overestimation and cochlear underestimation. This may be attributed to site-specific differences in endolymph T1 values, particularly a potentially shorter T1 in the cochlea and posterior ampulla. Clinicians should be cautious when interpreting non-contrast EH findings in younger populations. Future studies involving a larger number of subjects and a more rigorous evaluation of age-related thresholds are required to confirm the generalizability of these preliminary results and the validity of the age-grouping approach.
