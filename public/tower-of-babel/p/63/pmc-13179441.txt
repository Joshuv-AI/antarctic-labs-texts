Ultrasound-Triggered Paclitaxel-Loaded PLGA Microbubbles as a Nanobiotechnology Platform for Targeted Therapy of Triple-Negative Breast Cancer
Authors: Xing Li, Weiyang Lu, Chunxin Huang, Wei Dou, Jiayi Qian, Zihe Chen, Huilin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is one of the most aggressive breast cancer subtypes, with limited therapeutic options due to the lack of specific molecular targets and frequent drug resistance. Biotechnological advances in nanocarrier systems and ultrasound-targeted drug delivery provide new opportunities for precision cancer therapy.Objectives: This study aimed to develop paclitaxel (PTX)-loaded poly (lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) lipid microbubbles and evaluate whether ultrasound-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) could enhance the therapeutic efficacy against TNBC cells.Materials and Methods: PTX-loaded microbubbles were synthesized and characterized using dynamic light scattering (particle size, zeta potential), scanning electron microscopy (morphology), and UV spectrophotometry (drug loading efficiency). In vitro anticancer efficacy was assessed through MTT and Transwell assays (cell proliferation and migration), flow cytometry and Western blotting (apoptosis), and mitochondrial damage was analyzed using membrane potential assays and transmission electron microscopy.Results: The PTX-loaded PLGA microbubbles were spherical, well-dispersed, and stable, with an average diameter of 390.5 nm, zeta potential of –22.97 mV, and a drug loading efficiency of 4.34±0.08%. Ultrasound exposure significantly enhanced the inhibitory effects of PTX-loaded microbubbles on TNBC cell proliferation and invasion, promoted apoptosis through Bax/Caspase-3 activation and Bcl-2 downregulation, and exacerbated mitochondrial damage compared with microbubbles or PTX alone.Conclusion: This study demonstrates a nanobiotechnology-based strategy in which ultrasound-triggered PLGA microbubbles serve as an effective carrier for PTX delivery. The findings highlight UTMD as a promising targeted drug delivery platform in medical biotechnology, warranting further in vivo validation for clinical translation in TNBC therapy.

1. Background

According to global cancer statistics from 2023, breast cancer ranks as the most prevalent cancer among women ( 1 ). This malignancy exhibits considerable heterogeneity, characterized by distinct histopathological and molecular traits ( 2 ). Breast cancer is classified into four subtypes based on molecular characteristics ( 3 ). Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents the most aggressive subtype, comprising approximately 15%-20% of all breast cancer cases ( 4 , 5 ). Despite advances in treatment, the absence of targeted therapeutic inhibitors means that TNBC management continues to rely predominantly on chemotherapy ( 6 ). Unfortunately, chemotherapy-related toxicity and resistance often result in relapse and metastasis, severely compromising patient outcomes ( 7 ). Therefore, discovering new, effective, and safer therapies for TNBC is an urgent priority.

Paclitaxel (PTX), a diterpenoid compound derived from the Pacific yew, primarily targets microtubules by stabilizing them to prevent depolymerization. This action induces cell cycle to halt at the G2/M phase, eventually resulting in apoptosis ( 8 ). Currently, PTX is widely utilized in the clinical treatment of various solid tumors, including lung, breast, ovarian, colorectal, and gastric cancers ( 9 , 10 ). However, the emergence of PTX resistance diminishes chemotherapy efficacy, limiting its clinical application ( 11 ). To effectively combat tumor resistance, numerous targeted drug delivery systems for PTX have been developed, including liposomes, microemulsions, nanoemulsions, and lipid microspheres ( 12 ). In recent years, advancements in ultrasound microbubble technology have revealed that ultrasound contrast agents can enhance imaging while also serving as innovative drug delivery carriers ( 13 ). Ultrasound-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) technology offers several advantages for the targeted delivery of chemotherapeutic agents. Under ultrasound influence, microbubbles rupture, generating radiation pressure, micro-jets, and shock-waves that create shear stress on cell membranes. This process forms reversible or irreversible pores (sonoporation) in vascular endothelial cells or surrounding membranes, increasing the permeability of vascular walls and enhancing drug uptake by tumor cells ( 14 , 15 ). One study demonstrated that ultrasound-mediated destruction of PTX-loaded microbubbles inhibited cervical cancer cell proliferation and induced apoptosis, indicating the potential of UTMD for precise localization and targeted drug release ( 16 ). However, the effect of ultrasound-mediated paclitaxel-loaded microbubbles on TNBC progression remains unexplored.

The polymer poly (lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) is known for its biocompatibility and biodegradability, making it widely approved for medical application ( 17 , 18 ). In this study, we developed PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles using PLGA and performed a comprehensive characterization of these constructs. We further assessed their impact on the proliferation, invasion, apoptosis, and mitochondrial membrane potential of TNBC (MDA-MB-468) cells under ultrasound treatment. These findings offer valuable insights into the advancement of novel PTX drug delivery systems targeting TNBC therapy.

2. Objectives

The present study aimed to investigate whether UTMD could enhance the cytotoxic effects of PTX on TNBC cells. Specifically, we sought to determine its influence on cell proliferation, invasion, apoptosis, and mitochondrial function. Furthermore, by characterizing the physicochemical properties of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles, this study intended to provide experimental evidence supporting the potential application of UTMD as a novel targeted drug delivery strategy for TNBC.

3. Materials and Methods

3.1. Main Reagent

PTX (Solarbio, Beijing, China), ammonium bicarbonate (NH4HCO3, Shanghai Sangon Biology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China), PLGA (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), DSPC (Avanti Polar Lipids Ltd, Birmingham, AL, USA), Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and DMEM medium (Gibco, Rockville, MD, USA), MTT kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), CCK8 kit (Bioss, Wuhan, China), Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit and AnnexinV-FITC apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), RIPA and PMSF (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), BCA kit (Xinsaimei, China), Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

3.2. Preparation of PTX-Loaded Lipid Microbubbles

PTX-loaded microbubbles were synthesized with modified double-emulsion evaporation method. Briefly, ammonium bicarbonate (NH4HCO3, 60 mg. mL-1), DSPC (2.5 mg), PLGA (50 mg), and PTX (2.0 mg) were dissolved in dichloromethane. After thorough mixing, the solution was emulsified using an ultrasonic probe (Sonics&Materials, Newtown, CT, USA) with a power of 50 W in an ice bath for 120 seconds to form the primary emulsion. Subsequently, 5 mL solution of PVA was integrated into the primary emulsion, which was then centrifuged at 50,000 rpm for 5 minutes to generate final emulsion. Following this, 10 mL deionized water was mixed into final emulsion and stirred at room temperature for 2-6 hours to allow the dichloromethane to evaporate. After removing the supernatant, the precipitate was washed with water for 5 minutes, repeated three times. Finally, the precipitate was suspended in 0.5 mL deionized water and freeze-dried for 48 hours.

3.3. Characterization of PTX-Loaded Lipid Micro-bubbles

To characterize their morphology, PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles were dispersed in PBS and analyzed with ZEISS Supra 40VP scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany). Moreover, hemocytometer was used to measure the concentration of microbubbles, and the Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK) was employed to evaluate particle size and Zeta potential. To assess drug loading, methanol was added in varying proportions to PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles to prepare sample solutions with concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 0.2 mg. mL-1. The OD values of the solutions were measured at 220 nm using UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and a standard curve was constructed from the results. The drug loading was then calculated using the calibration curve and calculated with the following formula:

Drug loading=(PTX in microbubbles/(free PTX+PTX in microbubbles))×100% .

3.4. Cell Culture

Human TNBC MDA-MB-468 cells were grown in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS. To maintain sterility, we added 80 U.mL-1 Penicillin-Streptomycin. The cells were preserved at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 environment. The medium was replaced every two days, and cells were passaged upon reaching 80% confluence.

3.5. Drug Sensitivity Assessment

The IC50 of PTX for MDA-MB-468 cells was evaluated using the CCK8 assay. A density of 7×103 cells per well was used to seed cells in 96-well plates, with six replicate wells established for each group. After cell attachment, they were treated with PTX at different concentrations (0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 ng. mL-1) for 48 hours. Two hours prior to the conclusion of the incubation, 10 μL CCK8 solution was introduced to each well. Once the incubation was complete, a microplate reader (Biotek, Biotek Winooski, VT, USA) was used to measure absorbance at 450 nm, and the IC50 was subsequently determined.

3.6. Cell Grouping

MDA-MB-468 cells were randomly allocated into six groups: control group, lipid microbubbles+ultrasound irradiation group (PLM+US), PTX-loaded lipid microbubble group (LPLM), PTX group, PTX+ultrasound irradiation group (PTX+US), and PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles + ultrasound irradiation group (LPLM+US). The cells were treated with PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles at a PTX concentration of 15.5 ng. mL-1 (as determined by CCK8 results). For the PLM+US, PTX+US, and LPLM+US groups, cells were first treated with lipid microbubbles or PTX or PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles for 30 minutes followed by ultrasound irradiation. The parameters for ultrasound irradiation included an intensity of 0.5 W/cm2 and an irradiation time of 30 seconds per well. Prior to the subsequent experiments, the cells were cultured under varying conditions for 48 hours.

3.7. MTT Assay

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the MTT assay was employed to assess cell viability. Briefly, MDA-MB-468 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate, with each well containing 5,000 cells. After incubation, 10 μL MTT solution was added to each well and left to incubate for additional 4 hours. Afterward, 150 μL DMSO was introduced to the crystals that had formed. Lastly, microplate reader (Biotek, Biotek Winooski, VT, USA) was used to measure the OD value at 570 nm.

3.8. Cell Invasion Assay

Begin by diluting Matrigel matrix glue in a serum-free culture medium at a 1:4 ratio, then evenly distribute 50 μL into Transwell chamber. Subsequently, modify the cell concentration to 2×105 cells. mL-1 and introduce 100 μL cell suspension into the upper chamber. Then, add 600 μL medium with 10% FBS to the lower chamber, ensuring the drug concentration is consistent in the upper and lower chambers. Following a 24-hour incubation, secure the invading cells on the chamber’s lower surface using 4% paraformaldehyde and stain them with crystal violet. Finally, examine the cell morphology using IX1 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

3.9. Flow Cytometer Assay

MDA-MB-468 cells were gathered and seeded into 96-well plates with 1×106 cells per well. Afterward, the cells were placed in 100 µL labeling solution and cultured at 37 °C for 15 minutes. After incubation, the cells were incubated with fluorescent solution in the dark at 4 °C for 20 minutes. Finally, fluorescence emitted by FITC at 515 nm and PI fluorescence at 560 nm were detected using a Flow Cytometer (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA).

3.10. Transmission Electron Microscopy

To fix MDA-MB-468 cells, they were left in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Following fixation removal, the cells were dehydrated with a graded ethanol series and embedded in Epon 812. The cellular ultrastructure was then examined using a Hitachi 7650 transmission electron microscopy (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

3.11. Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay

MDA-MB-468 cells (1×106) were placed in 6-well plates and incubated for at 37 °C or 24 hours. After removing the culture medium, 1 mL Tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester was introduced, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for an extra 30 minutes. Fluorescence images were captured using BZ-H4XD fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Japan).

3.12. Western Blotting

RIPA buffer was used to extract total cellular protein, and a BCA kit was employed to measure the concentrations. After transferring the proteins to a PVDF membrane, it was blocked with skimmed milk and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following washes, secondary antibodies were applied for 2 hours. Target bands were visualized using a chemiluminescent solution and detected with Prime Western Blotting Reagent (Cytiva, UK). Grey values of the bands were analyzed using ImageJ. Antibody details are listed in Table 1.

3.13. Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. A paired t-test was applied for comparisons between two groups. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was determined by a P-value under 0.05.

4. Results

4.1. Physical Characterizations of PTX-Loaded Lipid Microbubbles

As illustrated in Figures 1A-1B, these PTX-loaded microbubbles exhibited a mean particle size of 390.5 nm, along with a zeta potential of -22.97 mV. Observations obtained from transmission electron microscopy indicated that these PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles possessed a spherical shape, characterized by well-defined contours and a densely packed configuration (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the drug loading capacity of PTX in these microbubbles was determined to be 4.34±0.08%.

Physical characterizations of Paclitaxel (PTX)-loaded lipid microbubbles. A) Particle size distributions of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles. B) The zeta potential of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles. C) Electron microscopy of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles.

4.2. Ultrasound Exposure Promotes the Inhibitory Effects of PTX-Loaded Lipid Microbubbles on Cell Proliferation and Invasion

The results indicated that PTX significantly inhibited MDA-MB-468 proliferation, with an IC50 of 15.5 ng. mL-1 (Fig. 2A). Therefore, we chose the IC50 concentration for subsequent experiments. Subsequently, MTT and Transwell assays demonstrated that PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles markedly diminished proliferation and invasion capabilities of MDA-MB-468 cells relative to the Control group. Furthermore, this inhibitory effect was significantly enhanced by ultrasound exposure (Fig. 2B-2C).

Ultrasound exposure promotes the inhibitory effects of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles on MDA-MB-468 cell proliferation and invasion. A) IC50 of PTX against MDA-MB-468 cells. B) Cell viability of MDA-MB-468 cells in each group. C) Cell invasion of MDA-MB-468 cells in each group (scale bar = 50µm). *P < 0.05, ***P < 0.001. Abbreviations: PLM+US: lipid microbubbles+ultrasound irradiation group; LPLM: PTX-loaded lipid microbubble group; PTX+US: PTX+ultrasound irradiation group; LPLM+US: PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles + ultrasound irradiation group.

4.3. Ultrasound Exposure Enhances the Pro-Apoptotic Effect of PTX-Loaded Lipid Microbubbles on Cell Apoptosis

We investigated the potential of ultrasound exposure to enhance the pro-apoptotic effects of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles on MDA-MB-468 cells. According to flow cytometry results, apoptosis significantly increased in MDA-MB-468 cells in the LPLM group relative to the Control group. Moreover, ultrasound exposure further amplified apoptosis induced by PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles (Fig. 3A). Western blot analysis indicated that PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles significantly markedly increased Bax and Caspase-3 expression in MDA-MB-468 cells, while concomitantly decreasing Bcl-2 expression. This trend was notably amplified when PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles were combined with ultrasound exposure (Fig. 3B).

Ultrasound exposure enhances the pro-apoptotic effect of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles on MDA-MB-468 cells. A) Cell apoptosis of MDA-MB-468 cells in each group. B) The expression of Bax, Bcl-2 and Caspase-3 in MDA-MB-468 cells in each group. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

4.4. Ultrasound Exposure Enhances the Effect of PTX-Loaded Lipid Microbubbles on Mitochondrial Damage

Mitochondria are essential organelles within the double-membrane structure of eukaryotic cells, primarily responsible for cellular energy meta-bolism ( 19 ). Disruptions in energy metabolism are hallmark features of tumors ( 20 ). Measurement of the mitochondrial membrane potential in MDA-MB-468 cells revealed notable decrease in the LPLM group when contrasted with the Control group. Ultrasound exposure further exacerbated the decline in mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 4A). Additionally, TEM results indicated that MDA-MB-468 cell morphology in the control group appeared largely normal, with no significant mitochondrial damage observed in the LPLM group. In contrast, the LPLM+US group exhibited pronounced mitochondrial swelling and rupture of mitochondrial cristae (Fig. 4B).

5. Discussion

Chemotherapy currently plays a pivotal role in managing TNBC, with PTX recognized as one of the most effective chemotherapeutic agents ( 21 ). Nevertheless, its clinical utility is hindered by issues related to inadequate water solubility and insufficient selectivity between healthy and malignant cells ( 22 , 23 ). Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop innovative drug delivery systems to enhance clinical outcomes. In recent years, UTMD has emerged as a promising approach for targeted drug delivery. This technique utilizes microbubbles as drug carriers, with ultrasound irradiation applied to the target tissue, leading to microbubble rupture and the release of encapsulated drugs ( 24 ). Growing evidence suggests that ultrasound combined with PTX-loaded microbubbles enhances the antitumor efficacy of PTX ( 25 , 26 ). For instance, a mouse model of pancreatic cancer showed that ultrasound in conjunction with lipid microbubbles significantly improved PTX delivery, effectively inhibiting tumor growth ( 27 ). Similarly, ultrasound-mediated PTX-loaded microbubbles were reported to suppress cervical cancer cell proliferation and promote apoptosis ( 28 ). Furthermore, UTMD has been shown to increase the sensitivity of ovarian cancer cells to PTX, indicating its potential for therapeutic applications ( 29 ). Building on these findings, we hypothesize that UTMD may enhance the cytotoxic effects of paclitaxel in triple-negative breast cancer TNBC cells.

In this study, we utilized PLGA as the outer mem-brane for PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles owing to its well-established biocompatibility, controllable biodegrad-ability, and adjustable drug release kinetics. Compared with other commonly used polymers such as PLA and PCL, PLGA provides a moderate and tunable degradation rate, allowing precise control of drug release behavior. Moreover, its excellent processability enables the preparation of nanoparticles with uniform size and high encapsulation efficiency. The PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles were then physically characterized by examining their mean particle size, zeta potential, morphology, and drug loading capacity. The results indicated that the prepared microbubbles exhibited good stability and effective drug loading. Previous studies indicated that applying ultrasound pulses with an intensity of 0.5 W/cm2 for 30 seconds does not significantly inhibit cell viability and proliferation ( 30 , 31 ). Therefore, we exposed MDA-MB-468 cells to ultrasound pulses at an average power intensity of 0.5 W/cm2 for 30 seconds and the results of CC-8 assay indicated that the IC50 of PTX for these cells was 15.50 ng. mL-1. Cell proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis are commonly used indicators to evaluate tumor cell responses to treatment ( 32 ). The study revealed that PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles had impact on hindering MDA-MB-468 cell proliferation and invasion, as well as enhancing apoptosis, although these effects were moderate. Notably, following ultrasound treatment, the inhibitory effects of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles on the on the malignant behavior of MDA-MB-468 cells were significantly enhanced. Our results align with previous studies ( 27 , 28 ), indicating that ultrasound exposure significantly enhances the cytotoxic effects of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles on TNBC cells.

Mitochondria are essential for cellular energy metabolism, functioning as the cell’s “powerhouse” by generating ATP through oxidative phosphorylation to support various physiological activities ( 33 ). Research indicates that structural damage to mitochondria is closely linked to cell death in TNBC ( 34 ). Such damage may lead cytochrome c release, initiate the caspase cascade and cause apoptosis ( 35 ). Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential are recognized as important indicators of mitochondrial structural damage and functional impairment ( 36 ). In this study, we found that ultrasound exposure enhanced the inhibitory effects of PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles on mitochondrial membrane potential of MDA-MB-468 cells. Additionally, alterations in mitochondrial morphology represent specific manifestations of mitochondrial damage in TNBC. Typically, TNBC cells display mitochondrial swelling, reduced or absent cristae, and disruption of the mitochondrial network, all reflecting a loss of mitochondrial function and abnormalities in cellular energy metabolism ( 37 ). Observing these morphological changes allows for the assessment of the extent of mitochondrial damage. Our TEM results revealed that after treatment with ultrasound exposure combined with PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles, MDA-MB-468 cells exhibited significant mitochondrial swelling and cristae rupture. These findings suggest that ultrasound exposure can enhance mitochondrial damage induced by PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles.

This study demonstrated through in vitro experiments that ultrasound exposure combined with PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles enhances the inhibitory effects of PTX on TNBC cell growth, highlighting its potential clinical value in TNBC treatment. The observed inhibitory effect may be attributed to mitochondrial disruption in TNBC cells. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report exploring the use of UTMD technology for inhibiting TNBC cell growth. Nonetheless, the study has several limitations. First, only one TNBC cell line was used. Second, the molecular mechanisms underlying the combined effects of ultrasound exposure and PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles on TNBC cells remain unexplored. Finally, in vivo validation of the in vitro results was not conducted. In the further studies, we will focus on elucidating the signaling pathways activated by UTMD, such as oxidative stress, mitochondrial l dysfunctio, and downstream regulation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway. Understanding these mechanisms will help clarify how mechanical stimulation from microbubble cavitation translates into intracellular biochemical responses, thereby providing a theoretical basis for optimizing UTMD-based therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, this study revealed that combining PTX-loaded lipid microbubbles with ultrasound can reduce TNBC cell proliferation and invasion while enhancing apoptosis. Our results suggest that this approach holds promise as a new method for the clinical treatment of TNBC.
