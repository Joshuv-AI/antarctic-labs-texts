Inhalable engineered probiotic outer membrane vesicles co-expressing multiple mpox antigens induce potent specific systemic and mucosal immune responses
Authors: Xinting Fu, Wei Wang, Han Duan, Lixia Gao, Zihui Wei, Yanqiu Wu, Jiamei Wang, Fei Ma, Yunlong Zhang, Yue Jia, Qin Gu, Changting He, Yongli Wang, Xueting Liu, Duo Jiang, Zihuan Liu, Weihao Liao, Xiaomei Ma, Shuyuan Wang, Yang Lu, Chenguang Shen, Bin Li, Yuhui Liao, Weifeng Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonkeypox virus (MPXV) is transmitted through respiratory mucosa and close contact, including sexual transmission, especially among men who have sex with men (MSM). Using bioengineering, we constructed an inhaled bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) vaccine, ABM@OMV, to block MPXV transmission. This nanovaccine consists of OMVs from ΔlpxM EcN strain that co-expressing three MPXV antigens (A9R, B6R and M1R). Our results show that OMVs vector enhances antigen uptake, mediates endosomal escape, and promotes APC maturation. Due to the adjuvant activity of OMVs and the synergistic effects of multi-antigen, ABM@OMV elicits robust systemic and mucosal immunity in both respiratory and vaginal, following intranasal immunization. This is demonstrated by the induction of three high-titer antigen-specific IgG and significant levels of SIgA. Moreover, ABM@OMV with A9R elicited stronger CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses. The vaccine conferred complete protection against lethal vaccinia virus (VACV, replacing MPXV) challenge, and 100% survival. ABM@OMV is simple to produce, scalable, and easily standardized, offering a novel strategy for needle-free mucosal vaccines against emerging infectious diseases.Keywords: Mpox, Biomimetic nanoparticles, Engineered probiotic vesicles, Mucosal immunity, Multiple antigens

Introduction

Mpox is an infectious zoonotic disease resulting from infection with the monkeypox virus (MPXV), which belongs to the Orthopoxvirus genus within the Poxviridae family. MPXV is divided into two evolutionary clades, namely the Central African (Clade I) and West African (Clade II) variants, with the former demonstrating higher pathogenicity [1,2]. The transmission pathways of MPXV are complex, involving both zoonotic and human-to-human routes. Human-to-human transmission primarily occurs through direct or indirect contact with infected bodily fluids, blood, respiratory droplets, and sexual contact [3,4]. In July 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the mpox outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) [5], a designation reaffirmed in August 2024, underscoring the continued necessity for globally coordinated control efforts. Recent studies have revealed that MPXV exhibits a high rate of genetic variability, which further complicates the epidemiological profile of the virus [6]. Owing to antigenic cross-reactivity within the Orthopoxvirus genus, conventional smallpox vaccines confer approximately 85% protection against MPXV infection [7]. However, since the global discontinuation of smallpox vaccination in 1980, waning cross-protective immunity in the general population has become a major contributor to recurrent outbreaks of mpox. Among currently available preventive vaccines, ACAM2000 (a live-attenuated vaccine) poses safety concerns [8,9], while JYNNEOS (a replication-deficient vaccine) provides incomplete protection and has limited production scalability [10]. Therefore, developing novel mpox vaccines that combine strong immunogenicity, favorable safety profiles, and large-scale manufacturability represents an urgent public health priority.

Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) are nanoscale bilayer lipid vesicles with a diameter of approximately 20-200 nm that are released by Gram-negative bacteria via budding from the outer membrane [11]. Their surfaces are decorated with diverse pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) derived from the parent bacterium, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), outer membrane proteins, and lipoproteins, while their lumen encapsulates bioactive molecules including proteins, peptides, and nucleic acids [12]. Importantly, OMVs possess inherent adjuvant properties, as surface PAMPs activate Toll-like receptor signaling pathways. They are particularly effective at stimulating Th1-type cellular immunity, thereby compensating for the limitations of conventional protein subunit vaccines in this regard [13,14]. Furthermore, OMVs exhibit efficient antigen delivery and presentation capabilities [15]. By leveraging specific interactions between bacterial outer membrane components such as LPS and antigen-presenting cells, including dendritic cells and macrophages, they enable targeted antigen delivery and promote efficient internalization [16]. OMVs protect encapsulated antigens from lysosomal degradation and facilitate cytosolic antigen release through endosomal escape mediated by bacterial pore-forming proteins such as ClyA [15]. Additionally, the ability of OMVs to prolong protein residence time in the lungs stems from their nanoscale dimensions and adhesive molecules (e.g., OmpA), which collectively hinder mucosal clearance mechanisms [17]. Owing to these exceptional carrier and self-adjuvant characteristics, OMVs have been their employed in research and applications across various immunology-related fields [18].

However, naturally occurring PAMPs present on OMVs can induce excessive activation of innate immunity, which may lead to adverse effects such as sepsis, myocardial injury, and pulmonary lesions [19]. These potential risks currently limit the clinical applicability of OMVs as carriers for subunit vaccines. To improve their safety profile, this study utilized Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) as the parental strain for OMVs production. EcN is a probiotic with a long-established record of clinical safety and well-characterized immunomodulatory properties [20]. OMVs derived from EcN (EcN OMVs) not only retain the immunomodulatory features of the parent strain but are also enriched in immunostimulatory proteins such as OmpA, FliC, and FimH, enabling effective activation of both mucosal and systemic immune responses [21]. To further reduce potential toxicity, we knocked out the lpxM gene encoding Lipid A biosynthesis myristoyltransferase in EcN. The ΔlpxM mutation leads to underacylated lipid A, thereby diminishing TLR4-mediated overactivation of immune pathways and reducing the risk of systemic inflammatory reactions [22,23]. The experimental results indicate that ΔlpxM OMVs retain considerable immunostimulatory activity while exhibiting substantially reduced toxicity, highlighting their promising potential for clinical translation.

The MPXV exists in two morphogenic forms, the intracellular mature virus (IMV) and the extracellular enveloped virus (EEV). Combinatorial immunization using immunogens specific to both IMV and EEV has been shown to confer superior protection compared to vaccination with either antigen alone [24]. The IMV protein M1R (homologous to vaccinia virus (VACV) L1R) is essential for host cell entry and represents a major target for neutralizing antibodies. Similarly, the EEV protein B6R (homologous to VACV B5R) contains epitopes recognized by neutralizing antibodies that inhibit viral infection, constituting a critical element of humoral immunity [25,26]. The M1R and B6R work together to establish a robust humoral immune barrier. Notably, although M1R and B6R are highly effective at inducing high-titer neutralizing antibodies and activating CD4+ T helper responses, which enhance antibody production and promote memory CTL formation, their ability to stimulate CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses is relatively limited [25,27]. CD8+ CTLs play a vital role in clearing intracellular pathogens by directly eliminating infected cells, thereby curbing viral replication [28]. Conservation analysis based on VACV T cell epitope databases indicates that the EEV antigen A9R is enriched with high-affinity MHC-I-restricted epitopes, enabling strong CTL activation and cross-reactive immunity [29]. Predictive experimental results indicated that immunization with VACV A8R (a homolog of MPXV A9R) significantly increased the frequency of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells and effectively suppressed viral replication [30,31], supporting the inclusion of A9R as a core antigen in novel mpox vaccine designs.

Based on the natural delivery advantages of OMVs, this study engineered a multi-antigen fusion OMV-based mpox vaccine, ABM@OMV, using ΔlpxM EcN. Genetic engineering was employed to transform a plasmid co-expressing MPXV antigens A9R, B6R and M1R into ΔlpxM EcN, a strain with the lpxM gene deleted. Following induction with isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG), the antigens were anchored to ΔlpxM EcN-derived OMVs via self-assembling proteins such as Trx and ClyA, yielding the ABM@OMV vaccine self-secreted by ABMΔEcN strains (Scheme 1A). Administered intranasally, the vaccine leverages OMVs' nanoscale architecture and surface adhesion molecules to achieve efficient retention in lung and penetrate the mucus-epithelial barrier for uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [17]. Retained bacterial outer membrane components (e.g., ClyA pore-forming protein) not only mediate endosomal escape to promote cytosolic antigen release and MHC-I presentation [29], activating CD8+ T cell responses, but also upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IFN-γ), driving APC maturation (via CD80/CD86 co-stimulatory molecules) and migration to mucosal-associated lymphoid tissues.

In a mouse model demonstrated that, compared with BM@OMV (ΔEcN-derived OMVs expressing B6R and M1R), ABM@OMV induced higher titers of serum IgG neutralizing antibodies, significantly elevated mucosal IgA levels in the respiratory and genital tracts, and enhanced CD4+/CD8+ T-cell responses. ABM@OMV triggered robust mucosal immunity in pulmonary and vaginal tissues, promoting in situ generation of secretory IgA (SIgA) specific to A9R, B6R, and M1R antigens, forming a first line of defense against monkeypox virus (MPXV) invasion (Scheme 1B). Immunologically, an effective mucosal vaccine can elicit local and systemic protection and induce immune responses at distal mucosal sites via lymphocyte homing [32,33]. Thus, the present inhaled nanovaccine represents a promising strategy to prevent sexual transmission of MPXV, which is particularly relevant for controlling the ongoing monkeypox outbreak primarily spread through sexual contact. VACV challenge experiments confirmed reduced tissue damage, decreased viral loads. More importantly, following the administration of this nanovaccine, 100% of the mice survived when challenged with a lethal dose of VACV. This ΔlpxM OMVs-based platform offers several advantages, including straightforward preparation techniques, suitability for large-scale production and standardization. It provides an innovative model for developing needle-free mucosal vaccines with significant potential applications in the public health sector for monkeypox and emerging viral infectious diseases.

Results

Design, preparation, and characterization of ABM@OMV

To generate MPXV antigen-displaying OMVs, DNA fragments encoding Trx-A9R-His and ClyA-B6R-M1R-Flag were cloned into plasmid pThioHisA (Fig. 1A) and transformed into ΔlpxM E. coli Nissle 1917 (ΔEcN). A control strain expressing only the ClyA-B6R -M1R-Flag fusion (without A9R) was also generated. Following IPTG induction, expression of the recombinant antigens was confirmed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis. Western blotting using anti-His antibodies detected A9R (within the Trx-A9R-His fusion), and anti-Flag antibodies detected B6R-M1R (within the ClyA-B6R-M1R-Flag fusion), with ΔEcN serving as the negative control. As shown in Fig. 1B and C, specific bands at approximately 55 kDa (Trx-A9R-His) and 80 kDa (ClyA-B6R-M1R-Flag) were observed in the lysates of induced engineered bacteria, consistent with the expected molecular weights. High-level expression of these exogenous proteins did not significantly impair bacterial growth (Fig. S1).

ΔlpxM OMVs were subsequently purified from ΔEcN, the strain carrying the B6R-M1R plasmid, and the strain carrying the A9R-B6R-M1R plasmid using differential centrifugation and ultrafiltration (see Materials and Methods for details). The resulting OMVs preparations were designated as ΔlpxM OMVs (empty vector control), BM@OMV (carrying B6R and M1R), and ABM@OMV (carrying A9R, B6R and M1R). Quantification via BCA assay (Fig. S2) indicated that the yields of ΔlpxM OMVs, BM@OMV, and ABM@OMV were 2.53 ± 0.07, 2.22 ± 0.03, and 2.28 ± 0.03 mg per liter of bacterial culture, respectively. No bacterial growth was observed on LB agar plates after a 48-h incubation of any OMVs preparation, confirming the absence of viable cells. To ensure batch-to-batch consistency, all OMVs were characterized for size, zeta potential, and protein concentration. The detailed characterization data for three independent batches of each formulation and the quality control release specifications are provided in Tables S1 and S2. Only batches meeting the predefined release criteria were used for subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Structural analysis confirmed all OMVs types maintained similar spherical nanostructures (50-200 nm diameter) with comparable ζ-potential (Fig. 1D–F), indicating heterologous protein incorporation didn't alter vesicle physical properties. We confirmed the surface display of A9R and B6R-M1R antigens on ABM@OMVs via Western blot (Fig. 1G). Proteinase K and binding assays digestion further confirmed that the antigens are displayed on the ABM@OMV surface, not encapsulated inside (Fig. S3). The lpxM knockout significantly reduced endotoxin toxicity (Fig. 1H), while stability studies demonstrated minimal changes in protein integrity and vesicle size after 5-week storage at both 4 °C and −80 °C (Fig. 1I–K), confirming excellent formulation stability. In addition, we compared ABM@OMV with a simple formulation of free antigens mixed with ΔlpxM OMVs at matched doses. ABM@OMV showed improved stability in simulated lung fluid and increased resistance to trypsin-mediated degradation compared with the simple mixture (Fig. S4), supporting a clear formulation advantage of antigen association with ΔlpxM OMVs.

APC-mediated antigen processing and presentation drive adaptive immunity

To systematically evaluate the efficacy of ABM@OMV as a monkeypox antigen delivery platform, we used the RAW 264.7 macrophages and bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) as an in vitro model and performed multidimensional experiments to assess its APC targeting and lysosomal escape capabilities.

First, the antigen delivery process was tracked using Cy5-NHS fluorescent labeling. Samples of the free monkeypox antigen mixture (ABM) at an equidose concentration of 10 μg mL−1, empty ΔlpxM OMVs carrier, and ABM@OMV were each co-incubated with RAW 264.7 cells. Fluorescent tracking of antigen delivery revealed significantly enhanced cellular uptake of ABM@OMV compared to free antigens. Confocal microscopy (Fig. 2A) showed strong intracellular red fluorescence at 6 h in the ABM@OMV group, quantitatively confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2B–D). Uptake kinetics demonstrated time-dependent internalization that plateaued after 6 h (Fig. S5), indicating efficient OMVs-mediated antigen delivery to immune cells.

To trace the intracellular fate of ABM@OMV, lysosomal tracking and colocalization experiments (using Lysotracker Green) were performed at three key time points (Fig. 2E). At 2 h, Cy5-labeled ABM@OMV (red) showed high colocalization with lysosomes (green), indicating ABM@OMV were captured by the initial lysosome. By 4 h, colocalization had decreased and the green fluorescence of the lysosomes had weakened, suggesting reduced lysosomal membrane stability and an increase in the pH of the lysosomal lumen. At 12 h, red fluorescence was widely dispersed in the cytoplasm with minimal colocalization, whereas the free ABM group still exhibited high colocalization (Fig. S6). Notably, because Cy5-NHS can label both ABM antigens and ΔlpxM OMVs, we further verified antigen escape using antigen-specific immunofluorescence. Confocal imaging with anti-His (A9R) and anti-Flag (B6R-M1R) antibodies together with LysoTracker showed that antigen signals were highly colocalized with LysoTracker at 2-4 h, but became clearly separated at 12 h (Fig. S7A and 7B). Line-scan analyses further confirmed the reduced overlap between antigen and LysoTracker signals at 12 h (Fig. S7C and D), supporting enhanced endolysosomal/lysosomal escape and increased delivery of OMV-associated antigens into the cytosol.

ABM@OMV possesses active lysosomal escape capability, which may be attributed to the pore-forming property of ClyA that facilitates endolysosomal membrane perturbation and antigen release [34,35]. To verify that ClyA enhances lysosomal escape and cytosolic antigen delivery, we performed mechanistic assays following a published paradigm [36]. In our system, ClyA is co-induced with antigen expression; therefore, the ΔlpxM OMVs group (without antigen induction) serves as the ClyA-negative OMV control. Mechanistically, under acidic conditions mimicking the endolysosomal environment, ClyA showed a pH-dependent redistribution from the ABM@OMV pellet to the supernatant (Fig. S7E), which was further quantified by densitometry (Fig. S7F), indicating enhanced dissociation/release of ClyA at low pH. Consistently, LysoTracker fluorescence in RAW 264.7 cells decreased over time after OMV uptake, and ABM@OMV caused a significantly greater reduction than empty ΔlpxM OMVs and PBS (Fig. S7G), indicating a stronger perturbation of the endolysosomal environment. Together with the reduced antigen–LysoTracker colocalization, these data support enhanced endolysosomal escape of OMV-associated antigens.

The immune activation efficacy was further evaluated using a dual-model antigen-presenting cell system, comprising macrophage RAW 264.7 cells and bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). Many studies have shown that M1 macrophages facilitate DC maturation and enhance immunity by secreting related paracrine signals [37]. Flow cytometric analysis of co-stimulatory molecule expression revealed that treatment with ABM@OMV for 24 h significantly induced M1 polarization in RAW 264.7 cells, with a higher proportion of CD86+ cells compared to the ABM group (Fig. 2F and G; S8). Concurrently, a morphological change from round to spread shape was observed (Fig. S9), suggesting that polarization may be mediated via interaction between macrophage surface TLRs and LPS carried by OMVs. In BMDCs, ABM@OMV effectively promoted the co-expression of maturation markers CD80 and CD86, with an activation level second only to the LPS positive control group (Fig. 2H and I; S10). These results indicate that ABM@OMV significantly enhances dendritic cell maturation and potentially initiates T-cell immune responses.

Quantitative analysis of cytokines in cell supernatants by ELISA further revealed that ABM@OMV treatment significantly enhanced TNF-α secretion in both RAW 264.7 cells and BMDCs (Fig. 2J and K), indicating its ability to effectively activate innate immune responses. ABM@OMV significantly enhanced the secretion of IL-6 and IFN-γ, key cytokines for T-cell polarization and antiviral immunity, while reducing IL-10 expression compared to the ABM group. This cytokine profile suggests that OMVs alleviates IL-10-mediated suppression of dendritic cell antigen presentation, thereby promoting T-cell activation and adaptive immunity [16]. These findings collectively demonstrate the strong adjuvant potential of OMVs as an antigen delivery vehicle.

ABM@OMV exhibits prolonged lung retention and activates innate immunity

To investigate the lung retention characteristics of ABM@OMV, this study systematically compared the biodistribution profiles in mouse lungs among the free monkeypox antigen mixture (ABM), empty carrier (ΔlpxM OMVs), and ABM@OMV after intranasal administration. Healthy BALB/c mice were intranasally administered with Cy5-labeled ABM@OMV (20 μg per mouse) and euthanized at 2 h, 12 h, and 24 h post-administration for lung tissue collection. Ex vivo fluorescent imaging (Fig. 3A) and quantitative analysis (Fig. 3B) revealed that the ABM@OMV group exhibited peak lung fluorescence intensity at 2 h, with significantly higher retention than the free ABM group over the 24 h observation period, demonstrating the OMVs carrier's capacity to prolong antigen residence in the lungs.

To validate the innate immune activation efficacy of ABM@OMV in vivo, healthy female BALB/c mice (6-8 weeks old) were randomly divided into four groups and administered the following formulations: PBS control, ΔlpxM OMVs (20 μg of protein per mouse in a volume of 40 μL), ABM (5 μg each of A9R, B6R, and M1R per mouse in 40 μL), and ABM@OMV (20 μg of protein per mouse in 40 μL). Lung lymphocytes and tissue samples were collected at 24 h after the first immunization. Flow cytometry analysis (Fig. S11) was performed to assess the activation status of alveolar macrophages (AMs) and dendritic cells (DCs), while ELISA was used to quantify cytokine levels in lung tissue. The results demonstrated that the ABM@OMV group significantly induced activation of M1-type AMs, with a significantly higher proportion observed in pulmonary lymphocytes compared to the ABM group (Fig. 3C–E). This confirms the synergistic enhancement of innate immune activation by the OMVs carrier-antigen complex. Meanwhile, ABM@OMV treatment effectively promoted DC maturation, evidenced by significantly elevated proportions of mature DCs (Fig. 3D–G) and conventional dendritic cells DCs (Fig. 3E–H), suggesting enhanced DC migration and maturation. Cytokine profiling revealed markedly higher concentrations of TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IFN-β in lung tissues of the ABM@OMV group compared to other experimental groups (Fig. 3I).

Intranasal administration of ABM@OMV elicits high-level humoral and mucosal immunity in mice

Animals received two intranasal immunizations on days 0 and 15 (Fig. 4A). Neutralizing antibody responses were evaluated using the plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) with vaccinia virus (VACV, a non-pathogenic orthopoxvirus surrogate). As shown in Fig. 4B, the ABM@OMV group demonstrated the highest VACV-neutralizing titers (PRNT50 = 670.8 ± 67.6), significantly exceeding those of BM@OMV (571.2 ± 33.5) and ABM alone (176.9 ± 28.2) (P < 0.05). All vaccine formulations induced superior neutralization compared to PBS and ΔlpxM OMVs controls. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed these findings: following co-incubation of 200-fold diluted sera with 100 TCID50 VACV in Vero E6 cells, ABM@OMV and BM@OMV groups showed markedly reduced viral plaque formation compared to ABM (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometry data further supported this observation (Fig. S12).

We subsequently analyzed MPXV-specific IgG in serum and antigen-specific IgA at mucosal sites. Two weeks post-boost, all vaccinated groups exhibited elevated antigen-specific antibody levels compared to pre-boost measurements (Fig. 4D–L, S13). Serum IgG analysis revealed peak titers in the ABM@OMV group for all antigens: A9R-IgG reached 1:28,800 (16.5-fold higher than ABM's 1:1,750, P < 0.05), B6R-IgG achieved 1:74,666.7 (298.7-fold higher than ABM's 1:250, P < 0.001), and M1R-IgG reached 1:24,000 (48-fold higher than ABM's 1:500, P < 0.001), surpassing BM@OMV (which lacked A9R). These results confirm the ability of ΔlpxM OMVs to enhance antigen immunogenicity.

Mucosal SIgA analysis demonstrated superior responses in the ABM@OMV group. BALF showed significantly elevated A9R-SIgA (1:34.7), B6R-SIgA (1:69.3), and M1R-SIgA (1:37.3) compared to ABM (P < 0.001), with a 6.9-fold increase in B6R-SIgA. Vaginal mucosa exhibited the strongest SIgA responses against all three antigens in ABM@OMV, while ABM induced minimal responses and ΔlpxM OMVs showed no significant activity, indicating expanded mucosal immunity through multi-antigen co-delivery. IgG subclass analysis revealed elevated Th1-associated IgG2a and Th2-associated IgG1 titers in ABM@OMV, with an IgG2a/IgG1 ratio >1, suggesting ΔlpxM OMVs preferentially induces Th1-biased immune responses (Fig. 4M − O).

To directly compare administration routes, mice were immunized with ABM@OMV via intranasal (IN) or intramuscular (IM) delivery. Both routes induced robust antigen-specific serum IgG responses after the second immunization (Fig. S14A–C), with IN immunization achieving systemic humoral responses comparable to IM administration under the same vaccine dose and regimen. Notably, IN immunization elicited markedly higher antigen-specific SIgA levels at mucosal surfaces, as evidenced by elevated SIgA in BALF and vaginal washes compared with IM delivery (Fig. S14D–I). Together, these data substantiate the advantage of intranasal delivery for inducing mucosal immunity without compromising systemic antibody responses. In addition, at matched antigen and ΔlpxM OMVs doses, ABM@OMV induced higher antigen-specific antibody titers than a simple mixture of free antigens and ΔlpxM OMVs (Fig. S15), demonstrating that antigen association with ΔlpxM OMVs enhance humoral immunogenicity beyond co-administration.

Intranasally administered ABM@OMV elicits systemic cellular immune responses in mice

Monkeypox virus (MPXV) infection is primarily controlled by antibody-mediated mechanisms, and B cells and T cells play pivotal roles in vaccine-induced humoral immune development. Germinal center (GC) reactions are critical for generating high-titer neutralizing antibodies, while follicular helper T cells (Tfh), acting as GC regulators, are essential for sustaining long-term protective immunity.

Therefore, in our intranasal vaccination model, we assessed GC programming in the draining lymphoid compartment. OMVs are expected to engage NALT and the lower-airway immune compartment, and antigen drainage from the lung is primarily captured in the mediastinal lymph nodes (mLNs), where GC reactions are initiated and sustained. Following the experimental protocol (Fig. 4A), mLNs were collected 14 days post-boost to assess GC reactions. Flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 5A and B; S16A, S16B) revealed significantly elevated proportions of Tfh cells (CD4+CXCR5+PD-1+; Fig. 5E) and GC B cells (B220+GL7+Fas+; Fig. 5F) in ABM@OMV-vaccinated mice compared to PBS and ABM control groups. These enhanced Tfh and GC B cell responses support a lymph node correlate of affinity maturation and durable humoral immunity. To address the anatomical distribution of these responses, we additionally compared GC B and Tfh cells across mLNs, NALT, and lung tissue (Fig. S16C and S16D). GC readouts were highest in mLNs, intermediate in NALT, and lowest in lung digests. This indicates that the GC reactions occur predominantly in the mLNs.

Durability is a key consideration for vaccine translation. To evaluate antibody durability, antigen-specific serum IgG titers were additionally measured at 6 months after the first immunization. We found that antigen-specific serum IgG titers remained high at 6 months after the first immunization (Fig. S17), consistent with robust Tfh–GC responses observed in draining lymph nodes, which are well established to drive affinity maturation and to generate memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells that together contribute to sustained humoral immunity [35].

Given the importance of T cell immunity in antiviral defense, we further investigated whether ABM@OMV immunization induces MPXV-specific T cell responses. The immunization schedule (Fig. 4A) involved two doses administered two weeks apart. Spleen samples collected 2 weeks post-boost underwent flow cytometric analysis of CD3+ (Fig. S18A), CD3+CD4+, and CD3+CD8+ T cell subsets (Fig. 5C and D). Results showed that the trivalent ABM@OMV vaccine significantly enhanced T cell activation compared to BM@OMV, with 1.33-fold, 2.13-fold, and 2.43-fold increases in CD3+ (Fig. S18B), CD3+CD4+ (Fig. 5G), and CD3+CD8+ (Fig. 5H) T cell activation, respectively. Compared to the ABM group, ABM@OMV elicited 6.71-fold and 3.13-fold higher activation in CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells, underscoring the synergistic effect of antigen-carrier integration on T cell immunity.

Furthermore, ΔlpxM OMVs-based vaccines effectively stimulated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells to produce the Th1 cytokine interferon-γ (IFN-γ) (Fig. 5I; S19). While ABM@OMV induced higher proportions of IL-4-producing T cells (Th2 cytokine) compared to other groups (Fig. 5J; S19), IFN-γ+ T cells significantly outnumbered IL-4+ T cells, indicating a robust Th1-biased response. This was primarily attributed to the A9R protein, a dominant cellular immunity antigen, driving antigen-specific cytotoxic T cell responses and critical cellular immunity against intracellular pathogens. To evaluate mucosal cellular memory elicited by intranasal vaccination, we quantified tissue-resident memory T cells in mucosal tissues. Using flow cytometry, CD8+ TRM cells were defined as CD3+CD44+CD8+ CD69+CD103+. ABM@OMV immunization significantly increased the frequency of CD8+ TRM cells in both the lungs and the genital tract compared with control groups (Fig. S20), indicating the establishment of tissue-localized cellular memory at relevant mucosal sites. Together with the mucosal antibody responses, these data provide a cellular correlate that may support durable mucosal immunity and rapid on-site protection. Consistent with this, prior pertussis studies have shown that ΔlpxM OMVs-based mucosal vaccination can induce tissue-resident memory T-cell responses associated with enhanced airway protection [38,39].

ABM@OMV elicits durable protective immunity against orthopoxvirus challenge in mice

To evaluate the efficacy of ABM@OMV in protecting against mpox, mice were intranasally immunized with ABM@OMV on days 0 and 15, then challenged with a lethal dose of VACV (a surrogate pathogen with high similarity to MPXV [40]) on day 29. Body weight and survival were monitored daily for 14 days. On day 7 post-challenge, organs were collected from each group of mice for viral load analysis.

Body weight monitoring (Fig. 6B) revealed that BM@OMV and ABM@OMV immunized mice maintained stable weight (>95% initial weight) throughout the 14-day post-challenge period. In contrast, PBS and ΔlpxM OMVs control groups showed significant weight loss (>25% by day 6), while ABM group exhibited moderate loss (∼10%). Survival data (Fig. 6C) demonstrated 100% protection in ABM, BM@OMV and ABM@OMV groups, whereas the PBS and the ΔlpxM OMVs controls suffered 50% mortality, with significant differences between vaccine-treated and control groups.

To comprehensively evaluate the inhibitory effect of vaccination on viral replication in lung tissue, we performed virus titer assays, qPCR-based viral load detection, and flow cytometric analysis. As shown in Fig. 6D, the virus titer results revealed that the BM@OMV and ABM@OMV groups had very low viral titers in lung tissues compared to the PBS control group. This difference was significant (P < 0.001). Consistent with this, qPCR analysis (Fig. 6E) measured viral abundance in lung samples based on a standard curve (Fig. S21). High mean levels of viral DNA were detected in the PBS and the ΔlpxM OMVs groups (5.37 log10 copies/g and 5.25 log10 copies/g, respectively). In contrast, VACV-specific DNA was undetectable in the lungs of the ABM@OMV group (below the limit of detection, LOD), indicating near-complete suppression of viral replication in vivo (values below the LOD were plotted at the LOD for visualization).

Flow cytometry provided direct evidence supporting these findings. On day 7 post-challenge, supernatant from homogenized lung tissue was used to infect Vero E6 cells. Detection of viral antigen expression (Fig. S22A) revealed significantly weaker positive signals in the vaccine-immunized groups than in the control groups. Statistical analysis of the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) (Fig. S22B) indicated that the MFI values in the vaccine groups were highly significantly lower than those in the control groups. These results align with the virus titer and qPCR data, collectively demonstrating that vaccination significantly reduces viral load in the lungs of challenged mice and effectively curtails viral replication.

Moreover, ABM@OMV immunization provided nearly complete protection against VACV-induced pulmonary inflammation, with lung histopathology resembling healthy mice (Fig. 6F,S23). In contrast, PBS and ΔlpxM OMVs groups showed substantial inflammatory infiltration and hemorrhage. Consistent with histopathological findings, ABM@OMV significantly suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) to levels comparable to BM@OMV (Fig. S24), demonstrating its potent anti-inflammatory capacity. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of lung, spleen, and lymph node tissues from infected mice in the PBS and ΔlpxM OMVs groups revealed extensive areas with high density of VACV antigen-positive signals, indicating substantial viral loads in these organs (Fig. 6G–I). In contrast, mice immunized with ABM, BM@OMV, or ABM@OMV exhibited significantly reduced VACV antigen-positive areas across these tissues.

Vaccine efficacy comparisons demonstrated that both BM@OMV and ABM@OMV were markedly effective and comparable in inhibiting viral replication, mitigating tissue damage, and controlling inflammatory responses. However, further immune response analysis revealed that ABM@OMV induced higher levels of neutralizing antibodies (Fig. 4B) and stronger antigen-specific T-cell immune responses (Fig. 5), demonstrating a clear advantage in comprehensively and synergistically activating both humoral and cellular immunity.

In vitro and in vivo safety evaluation of ABM@OMV

Unlike live bacteria carrying toxicological risks, OMVs retain therapeutic immunogenicity whilst exhibiting superior biocompatibility. In this study, we engineered lpxM gene-knockout OMVs to significantly reduce endotoxin activity, thereby establishing a safer foundation for subsequent development.

In vitro studies using RAW 264.7 and Vero E6 cells showed that ΔlpxM OMVs and ABM@OMV exhibited negligible cytotoxicity across the tested concentrations, whereas WT OMV reduced cell viability at higher doses (Fig. 7A and B), supporting the improved in vitro safety of the engineered vesicles. In addition, red blood cells from normal BALB/c mice were used to evaluate hemolysis. With deionized water as a positive control and PBS as a negative control, ΔlpxM OMVs and ABM@OMV induced <1% hemolysis, while WT OMV showed markedly higher hemolytic activity at elevated concentrations (Fig. 7C). Together, these results indicate minimal hemolytic risk and favorable in vitro biocompatibility of ΔlpxM OMVs and ABM@OMV.

The in vivo evaluation included dynamic monitoring and endpoint detection. Real-time monitoring of the body weight and body temperature of immunized mice showed no abnormal fluctuations (Fig. 7D and E), indicating no induction of adverse stress. After the booster immunization, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) showed that after repeated administration, the tissue structure remained intact, with no pathological damage such as edema, inflammatory infiltration, or cell necrosis (Fig. 7F). Serum biochemical analysis revealed that alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (liver function indicators), creatinine (CR), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (renal function indicators) were all within the normal range (Fig. 7G–J), suggesting no liver or kidney function damage.

In addition, we performed a time-course analysis of pulmonary cytokines at 0, 12, 24, and 48 h after the first immunization. IFN-β, IFN-γ, and TNF-α increased at 12-24 h but declined markedly by 48 h (Fig. S25A–C), indicating a transient and self-limiting response.

We also conducted H&E staining of lungs from the ABM@OMV group at day 0, day 1, day 2, and day 3 after the first immunization. Lung architecture remained intact, with no obvious inflammatory cell infiltration, edema, or tissue damage (Fig. S25D), suggesting no detectable acute lung injury at the tested dose.

Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo safety evaluations confirm that the prepared ABM@OMV exhibit excellent biocompatibility. These robust findings provide strong data support for their clinical translation as novel immunological agents, such as vaccine carriers.

Discussion

In recent years, bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) have emerged as a highly promising platform for vaccine development due to their inherent adjuvant properties and efficient antigen delivery capabilities. The commercialization of OMV-based vaccines, coupled with scalable manufacturing processes, has further accelerated their application. For example, an OMV-RBD nanovaccine developed using the SpyTag/SpyCatcher system activates innate immune pathways to elicit robust humoral and cellular immune responses, effectively neutralizing SARS-CoV-2 [16]. To inhibit tumor angiogenesis, researchers have loaded full-length basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) onto OMVs. The strong adjuvant activity of OMVs elicited sustained anti-bFGF autoantibodies in mice, resulting in tumor regression [41]. Genetically engineered OMVs nanovaccine platforms allow for flexible antigen display while effectively promoting dendritic cell (DC) maturation and stimulating potent antigen-specific immunity [16].

Despite the translational challenge posed by OMVs immunogenicity, we utilized OMVs from the probiotic E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN)—which has low intrinsic toxicity—and further attenuated them by deleting the lpxM gene to reduce LPS-related toxicity while preserving immunostimulatory capacity, thereby improving biosafety [23,42]. Safety assessments confirmed that ΔlpxM OMVs exhibit excellent biocompatibility in mice, providing strong support for their clinical translation.

Using these attenuated ΔlpxM OMVs, we constructed a monkeypox virus (MPXV) nanovaccine, ABM@OMV, by co-expressing MPXV antigens A9R, B6R, and M1R on their surface. Intranasal immunization with ABM@OMV in mice induced robust systemic, mucosal, and Th1-biased cellular immunity, conferring strong protection against a vaccinia virus (VACV) challenge.

The interaction between OMVs and antigen-presenting cells (APCs) represents a fundamental mechanism underlying their immunologic advantages. OMVs exhibit pathogen-like dimensions, which facilitate efficient recognition and phagocytosis by APCs [43,44]. Confocal microscopy confirmed that antigens displayed on OMVs are efficiently co-internalized by APCs, thereby overcoming the recognition limitations often associated with poorly immunogenic or sub-micron-sized antigens [45]. In addition to protecting antigens from lysosomal degradation, OMVs utilize pore-forming proteins such as ClyA to mediate endosomal escape. This promotes the release of antigens into the cytosol and enhances MHC class I antigen presentation, leading to robust activation of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes [46]. Flow cytometry analysis further revealed that ABM@OMV significantly upregulates the expression of CD80 and CD86 in bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). This indicates that OMV-associated pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) potently activate innate immune signaling and drive dendritic cell maturation without attenuating adjuvant activity [47].

Beyond serving as self-adjuvanting carriers, OMVs are increasingly recognized as immunomodulatory nanoplatforms whose intrinsic molecular composition and vesicular architecture can program innate sensing and cytokine milieus, thereby shaping downstream Th polarization. OMVs display a complex repertoire of PAMPs (e.g., LPS/LOS, outer-membrane proteins, and lipoproteins) and encapsulate luminal biomolecules, enabling multivalent engagement of PRRs such as TLR4 and TLR2. Such PRR integration promotes APC maturation and favors Th1-skewed immune programming, as widely discussed in OMV vaccine literature [19]. Mechanistic studies further indicate that OMVs can access intracellular inflammatory pathways (including inflammasome-linked signaling), supporting cytokine remodeling beyond “general adjuvanticity” [48,49]. In our study, ABM@OMV elicited a TNF-α/IFN-γ–high profile while suppressing IL-10. Given that IL-10 is a central immunoregulatory cytokine that restrains dendritic cell maturation and antigen-presenting function, reduced IL-10 may relieve regulatory feedback and create a Th1-permissive cytokine milieu conducive to APC activation and effective T-cell priming [50,51]. Collectively, these features support an OMV-driven immunomodulatory framework in which composition-encoded innate sensing actively shapes cytokine landscapes and promotes Th1-oriented priming.

The integrated mechanisms, including pathogen-mimetic size, endosomal escape capacity, pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP)-mediated antigen-presenting cell (APC) activation, and cytokine-influenced immune polarization, highlight the unique potential of OMVs as versatile vaccine platforms capable of eliciting coordinated systemic (humoral and cellular) immune and mucosal immune responses. Given the distinct biological roles of intracellular mature virus (IMV) and extracellular enveloped virus (EEV) during MPXV infection, simultaneous targeting of multiple antigens is considered essential for comprehensive protection [52]. This study innovatively fused A9R—a dominant antigen for cell-mediated immunity—with B6R and M1R, which are dominant antigens for humoral immunity. Results show that ABM@OMV, which incorporates A9R, induced significantly stronger antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses compared to the A9R-deficient BM@OMV group. This finding is consistent with the known role of A9R in activating cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and OMVs’ ability to enhance MHC class I antigen presentation [27].

In terms of humoral immunity, ABM@OMV elicited high-titer IgG and high-titer neutralizing antibodies. This suggests that immunostimulatory molecules on the OMV surface—such as attenuated LPS and OmpA—foster a favorable microenvironment for B-cell activation and affinity maturation [53,54]. Thus, the integrated strategy harnesses the unique properties of OMVs to simultaneously engage multiple branches of the immune system: a Th1-biased cellular response mediated by A9R and MHC-I presentation, coupled with a potent humoral response supported by the inherently adjuvant surface components of OMVs.

Mucosal immunity serves as a first line of defense against viral invasion [55]. The respiratory tract represents a critical entry point for MPXV infection [56]. Intranasal ABM@OMV induced strong mucosal SIgA responses (BALF and vaginal lavage) while maintaining systemic IgG responses comparable to intramuscular immunization, highlighting the advantage of intranasal delivery for mucosal immune induction without compromising systemic humoral immunity. Beyond antibodies, intranasal ABM@OMV also increased CD8+ TRM cells in the lung and genital tract, supporting a complementary cellular layer of local immune memory at sites of exposure. Collectively, these findings underscore the suitability of OMV-based platforms for mucosal vaccination, as OMVs can enhance mucosal antigen uptake and promote coordinated immune responses across mucosal-associated lymphoid tissues (MALT) [16], which may be particularly relevant for limiting MPXV acquisition and transmission via respiratory and close-contact routes.

ABM@OMV conferred robust protection in the VACV challenge model, with immunized mice exhibiting minimal tissue pathology, undetectable viral loads, and 100% survival. This protection was mediated by the synergistic effects of high-titer neutralizing antibodies that control viremia, mucosal SIgA that blocks viral entry, and potent Th1-type cellular immunity that eliminates infected cells. Although ABM@OMV induces a stronger cellular immune response than BM@OMV, the comparable humoral immune responses and protective efficacy against VACV infection may be due to a “ceiling effect" [57], where BM@OMV-induced antibody levels are sufficient for full protection. However, the enhanced cellular immunity of ABM@OMV suggests its potential for long-term immunity, which is also supported by antibody titers measured six months later.

The ΔlpxM EcN OMVs platform demonstrates modular flexibility, enabling rapid and adaptable vaccine development against emerging pathogens by exchanging antigen sequences fused to carriers such as Trx or ClyA [58]. Compared to more complex systems like synthetic nanovesicles or multi-antigen mRNA vaccines, this probiotic-based platform supports cost-effective and scalable manufacturing. While mouse models validate its efficacy, future studies should include non-human primate evaluations to better predict human immune responses, along with long-term immune memory assessment and further clinical safety validation.

Materials and Methods

Mice

Female BALB/c mice (6-8 weeks old) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Guangdong Medical University (Guangdong, China) and housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities under controlled conditions (20-22 °C, 30-70% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle). All animal protocols were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Ningxia Medical University (approval No.: 2025-081), adhering to ethical guidelines for animal research.

Virus strains, cell lines, and bacterial culture

The vaccinia virus (VACV) Tian Tan strain and its engineered variant expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) were kindly provided by the research group of Professor Chenguang Shen. All experiments involving live vaccinia virus were conducted in a Biosafety Level 2 laboratory at Southern Medical University. The virus was amplified and titrated in African green monkey kidney (Vero E6) cells. Viral particles were isolated by subjecting infected Vero E6 cells to five cycles of freeze-thaw lysis, followed by centrifugation at 3000×g for 20 min to remove cellular debris. The supernatant containing viral particles was then concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 150,000×g for 60 min. The resulting pellet was collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent use.

The Vero E6 cell line and the murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (50 U mL−1), and streptomycin (50 μg mL−1), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

The probiotic Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 strain was acquired from Hangzhou Hongsai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Bacterial cultures were grown aerobically at 37 °C in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth with shaking at 180 rpm.

Isolation and culture of BMDCs

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were isolated from the femurs and tibiae of 8-week-old BALB/c mice. After euthanasia by cervical dislocation, mice were immersed entirely in 75% ethanol for 5 min for disinfection. The hind limbs were then removed and further sterilized in 75% ethanol for 10 min, followed by one wash with PBS. Under sterile conditions, muscle tissues attached to the bones were carefully removed using forceps, and both ends of each bone were trimmed. The bone marrow was flushed out from the bone cavity using an insulin syringe filled with RPMI 1640 medium. This flushing process was repeated twice by inverting the bones until they turned white. The flushed cell suspension was collected and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, followed by centrifugation at 300×g for 10 min to pellet the cells. The cell pellet was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer for 10 min. The reaction was stopped by adding PBS, and the cells were collected by another round of centrifugation.

The cells were resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 U mL−1 penicillin, 50 μg mL−1 streptomycin, 20 ng mL−1 GM-CSF, and 10 ng mL−1 IL-4 for culture initiation. On day 3 of culture, the non-adherent cells and supernatant were transferred to a new culture dish for continued cultivation. Half of the medium was replaced on days 5 and 7, respectively. BMDCs were harvested on day 9 for subsequent experiments.

Plasmid and bacterial strain construction

The pThioHisA plasmid was obtained from Shanghai Dibai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., which inherently contains the trx gene. Standard molecular cloning techniques were employed for plasmid and strain engineering. Vector backbones were digested with restriction enzymes, and inserts including A9R-clyA-B6R-M1R. These were inserted into the XhoⅠ and PstⅠ sites of pThioHisA via T4 DNA ligase, generating the recombinant plasmid pThioHisA-ABM.

The resulting plasmid carries A9R, B6R, and M1R: A9R with a His-tag was fused to the C-terminus of Trx, while B6R and M1R with a Flag-tag were fused to the C-terminus of ClyA. The Trx-A9R and ClyA-B6R-M1R units are connected by a ribosome binding site (RBS) to facilitate independent expression and proper localization. All constructs were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

The engineered strain was based on Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), with lpxM knocked out (ΔlpxM) via CRISPR/Cas9 by Hangzhou Hongsai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The validated plasmid was electrotransformed into competent ΔEcN bacteria to obtain ABMΔEcN. Positive clones were selected on LB agar with 50 μg mL−1 ampicillin, then cultured for plasmid extraction and sequencing. Verified strains were stored in glycerol at −80 °C.

For protein expression, ABMΔEcN was cultured in LB medium with ampicillin (50 μg mL−1) at 37 °C to OD600 ≈ 0.6-0.8, followed by induction with 1 mM IPTG at 27 °C for 14 h. The engineered strain successfully produced the target OMVs.

Preparation of ABM@OMV

OMVs from various sources were prepared using established methods. Briefly, after protein expression induction, bacterial cultures were centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove intact bacteria. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 2000×g for 10 min to pellet dead bacteria, and then at 10,000×g for 10 min to remove debris. The supernatant was preliminarily concentrated with an ultrafiltration device (Amicon® Ultra-15, 100 kDa, Merck Millipore).

The concentrate was ultracentrifuged using a CP100NX ultracentrifuge (Hitachi Koki) with a P32ST fixed-angle rotor (Hitachi Koki, Tokyo, Japan) at 125,000×g for 2 h at 4 °C to pellet vesicles. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, washed, and ultracentrifuged again to obtain purified OMVs. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS, filter-sterilized through a 0.22 μm membrane filter (Millex®-HV, Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C.

Sterility was confirmed by plating an aliquot on agar and incubating overnight at 37 °C; no colonies were observed. OMVs protein concentration was measured by BCA assay. Heterologous protein expression was confirmed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting.

Characterization and stability of ABM@OMV

The morphology, size, and surface potential of the obtained OMVs were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) [59]. For TEM analysis, samples were adsorbed onto carbon-supported films, negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate, and air-dried at room temperature. Images were acquired using a JEM-1400Flash transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400Flash, JEOL, Japan). The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the OMVs were measured using a nanoparticle size and zeta potential analyzer (Litesizer™ 500, Anton Paar, Austria). Expression of the heterologous proteins was confirmed by Western blot analysis. To evaluate stability, OMVs were stored under two conditions: at 4 °C and −80 °C. Samples were taken weekly over a period of five weeks, during which changes in particle size distribution and zeta potential were monitored.

Protein analysis of recombinant bacteria and ABM@OMV

Bacterial proteins were extracted by ultrasonic disruption (Scientz-IID homogenizer, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology, China) on ice. Settings were: 300 W power, 3 s sonication, 5 s interval, 99 cycles. OMVs samples were lysed with RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) plus PMSF, kept on ice for 10 min, and protein quantification was performed using the BCA method.

A total of 20 μg bacterial protein was separated via SDS-PAGE on a 4-20% gradient precast gel (SurePAGE™ Bis-Tris, GenScript, China). After Coomassie blue staining (P1300, Solarbio, China) and destaining, gels were imaged (Tanon MINI Space, Tanon Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). OMVs samples showed no clear bands by Coomassie blue and were skipped.

For antigen confirmation, bacterial and OMVs samples underwent Western blot. Proteins were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF, quantified using the BCA assay, separated by SDS-PAGE, and electrophoretically transferred to a PVDF membrane (Immobilon-P, Merck Millipore, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA (BSA0020, Biosharp, China), then incubated with anti-His tag antibody (1:10,000, HY-P809476X, Med ChemExpress, USA) and anti-FLAG antibody (1:10,000, HY-P80111, Med ChemExpress, USA), respectively for A9R and B6R-M1R, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were visualized with ECL to confirm antigen expression.

Quantification and standardization of antigen loading on OMVs by tag-based ELISA

BM@OMV, ABM@OMV, and control ΔlpxM OMVs were adjusted to 0.2 mg/mL and lysed 1:1 (v/v) in RIPA buffer with gentle agitation at 4 °C for 20-30 min. The lysates were centrifuged (10,00×g, 5 min) to remove large debris; however, because the target antigens were not fully soluble under these conditions, the whole lysate was used directly for ELISA quantification. In parallel, total protein concentrations of the same lysate preparations were determined by a BCA assay for normalization.

A9R-His and B6R-M1R-Flag were quantified using a tag-based ELISA with His-tag or Flag-tag standards. Briefly, serial dilutions of whole lysates and the corresponding tag standards (His-tag or Flag-tag) were added to high-binding 96-well plates (100 μL/well) and incubated at room temperature for 4 h. Plates were washed with PBST and blocked with BSA, followed by incubation with anti-His or anti-Flag primary antibodies. After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were added, and signals were developed with TMB and measured at 450 nm. Uptake and endosomal escape of OMVs in RAW 264.7 cells

Candidate vaccines (ABM, ΔlpxM OMVs, ABM@OMV) were labeled with Cy5-NHS, purified, and resuspended in PBS. RAW 264.7 cells (1 × 106/well) were cultured to 60-70% confluency and treated with labeled samples (10 μg mL−1) or PBS for 6 h. Cells were washed, scraped, and analyzed by flow cytometry using untreated cells as control. MFI and phagocytosis rate were calculated with FlowJo.

For imaging, cells were fixed, permeabilized, stained with phalloidin (actin, green, BL119A, Biosharp, China) and DAPI (nuclei, blue, C1005, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), mounted, and imaged. Cy5 signals (red) indicated vaccine localization.

To evaluate endosomal escape, cells were incubated with Cy5-ABM@OMV for 2, 4, and 24 h, washed, stained with LysoTracker Green (C1047S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and imaged. Colocalization of red (ABM@OMV) and green (lysosomes) signals was assessed.

Polarization of RAW 264.7 and maturation of BMDCs

RAW 264.7 cells and isolated BMDCs were co-cultured with various samples at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 h to evaluate their effects on cellular maturation. After stimulation, cells were collected and subjected to surface marker staining. RAW 264.7 cells were stained with a PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 antibody (12-0862-83). BMDCs were multi-color stained using an APC-eFluor™ 780-conjugated anti-mouse CD11c antibody (47-0114-82), a Super Bright™ 436-conjugated anti-mouse CD80 antibody (62-0801-82), and a PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 antibody (12-0862-83). Following staining, all cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and analyzed by flow cytometry to detect the expression levels of maturation-associated surface markers.

Mouse immunization

Female BALB/c mice aged 6-8 weeks were randomly divided into five groups and subjected to the following treatments: phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), empty ΔlpxM OMVs (20 μg of protein per mouse in 40 μL volume), a mixture of free ABM monkeypox antigens (5 μg each of A9R, B6R, and M1R per mouse in 40 μL volume), BM@OMV (carrying B6R and M1R, 20 μg of protein per mouse in 40 μL volume), and ABM@OMV (carrying A9R, B6R and M1R, 20 μg of protein per mouse in 40 μL volume). All groups were immunized via the intranasal route on day 0 and day 15. Specifically, mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital, and 40 μL of the corresponding preparation was administered intranasally (20 μL per nostril). One day before the second immunization and 14 days after immunization, serum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and vaginal wash samples were collected. On day 14 after the last immunization, a subset of mice was euthanized, and spleens, lung tissues and mediastinal lymph nodes (mLNs) were harvested for subsequent lymphocyte isolation.

Flow cytometry analysis

On day 14 post-boost immunization, mice were euthanized; lung and spleen tissues were collected for single-cell suspension preparation. Lung tissue was minced into 1 mm3 pieces and digested with 1 mg mL−1 Collagenase V at 37 °C for 10 min, then filtered through a 70 μm strainer. Spleens were homogenized in PBS, filtered, and centrifuged at 1800×g for 5 min. Cells from both tissues underwent RBC lysis, were washed twice with PBS (500×g, 5 min), and blocked with 1% BSA for 30 min. Cells were then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. The following antibodies were used: FITC anti-mouse CD3e (11-0031-85); APC-eFluor™ 780 anti-mouse CD8a (47-0081-82); APC anti-mouse CD4 (17-0042-83); PerCP-Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse IFN-γ (45-7311-82); PE-Cyanine7 anti-mouse IL-4 (25-7041-82); eFluor™ 450 anti-mouse CD62L (L-Selectin) (48-0621-82); Brilliant Violet™ 605 anti-mouse CD44 (406-0441-82); FITC anti-mouse CD45R (B220) (11-0452-82); PE anti-mouse CD95 (APO-1/Fas) (12-0951-83); eFluor™ 660 anti-mouse GL7 (50-5902-82); PE Anti-mouse CD185 (CXCR5) (12-7185-80); Super Bright™ 600 Anti-mouse CD279 (PD-1) (63-2799-42); FITC Anti-mouse F4/80 (14-4801-82); APC-eFluor™ 780 anti-mouse CD11c (47-0114-82); Super Bright™ 436 anti-mouse CD80 (B7-1) (62-0801-82); PE anti-mouse CD86 (B7-2) (12-0862-83). After staining, cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer, and data were processed with FlowJo software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Monkeypox-specific IgG, its subclasses (IgG1 and IgG2a), and secretory IgA (SIgA) antibody titers in serum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and vaginal washes were measured using an indirect ELISA. Briefly, 96-well polystyrene plates were coated with 1 μg mL−1 of recombinant A9R, B6R, or M1R protein and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After discarding the coating solution, the plates were blocked with 1% BSA for 2 h at room temperature and washed twice with PBST. Appropriately diluted samples were added to the wells and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Following four washes with PBST, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, or IgA secondary antibodies were added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The plates were washed four times with PBST, and then 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added.

Plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT)

Neutralizing antibody activity against vaccinia virus (VACV, Tian Tan strain) was assessed via a microneutralization assay [2]. Vero E6 cells were plated at 1.0 × 105 cells/well in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Serum samples were pre-diluted 20-fold, then serially diluted two-fold; 2% FBS/DMEM served as negative control. A viral suspension (100 TCID50) and 5% rabbit complement (Pel-Freez Biologicals) were added to each well. After 2 h at 37 °C, serum-virus mixtures were transferred onto cells and incubated 1 h. Supernatant was replaced with fresh 2% FBS medium, and cells cultured 4 days at 37 °C under 5% CO2.

Cytopathic effects were assessed on day 4. The neutralization titer was defined as the highest serum dilution showing plaque reduction; PRNT50 was calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.

Immunofluorescence assay

An immunofluorescence-based neutralization assay was performed using the vaccinia virus (VACV) Tian Tan strain. Vero E6 cells were seeded at 1.0 × 105 cells/well in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to adhere. Serum samples were diluted 1:200 and mixed with VACV (100 TCID50) and 5% rabbit complement (Pel-Freez Biologicals, Rogers, AR, USA). After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, serum-virus complexes were added to Vero E6 cells and incubated for 12 h. Supernatant was removed; cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (30 min, RT), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS (30 min, dark), and blocked with 10% normal goat serum (Beyotime Biotechnology) for 30 min. Cells were incubated with anti-vaccinia virus antibody (ab35219) at 4 °C for 16 h, followed by CoraLite® Plus 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (RGAM002, Proteintech) for 1 h at RT in the dark. Images were acquired with an inverted fluorescence microscope and analyzed.

VACV challenge

On day 15 post-boost, all immunized mice were challenged intranasally with VACV (1 × 106 TCID50 per mouse, 50 μL). Body weight and survival were monitored daily for 14 days. On day 7 post-challenge, mice were euthanized, and tissues (lung, spleen, axillary lymph nodes) were collected for pathological and immunological analysis.

Viral genome quantification

Viral nucleic acids were extracted from lung tissues using a Viral RNA/DNA Extraction Kit (DP315-F, TIANGEN™, Tiangen Biotech, China), and quantified using a Vaccinia Virus Probe-based qPCR Kit (XY-PCR5694, Shanghai Xuanya Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). A standard curve was generated using 10-fold serial dilutions of a positive standard (1 × 101 to 1 × 106 copies·μL−1). Sample DNA was extracted with the kit, and negative and positive preparation controls were included simultaneously. Probe-based qPCR was performed in a 20 μL reaction volume. Each run included a standard curve, no-template negative controls, and positive quality controls. After confirming that all control results met expectations, samples were quantified based on the standard curve.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

According to standard histological procedures, portions of mouse tissue were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for over 48 h and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Sections of 3 μm thickness were cut from the paraffin-embedded tissue blocks and mounted onto glass histological slides. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological observation. For immunohistochemical (IHC) detection, sections were subjected to deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval procedures, followed by blocking of nonspecific binding sites with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The sections were then incubated with a primary antibody (1:500 dilution, Anti-Vaccinia Virus antibody, ab35219), followed by incubation with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody. An alkaline phosphatase detection system combined with Fast Red chromogen was used for color development. Finally, whole-slide images were acquired using a whole-slide scanner and quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software.

Cell viability assay

Serially diluted OMVs were inoculated into 1 × 104 RAW 264.7 or Vero E6 cells and incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 h. Then, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by further incubation for 3 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (iMark™, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) to assess cell viability.

Biosafety analysis

Following two rounds of immunization, blood was collected via cardiac puncture from mice in each group. Plasma was separated by centrifuging the whole blood at 500×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA) in the plasma were measured using biochemical assays to evaluate the in vivo biosafety of each formulation. Concurrently, major organ tissues including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested from each group. These tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for histomorphological analysis. Finally, whole-slide images of the sections were acquired under bright-field using a whole-slide scanning system, and organ structures and pathological changes were evaluated.

Statistical analysis

Most quantitative data are expressed as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed unpaired t-tests, while multiple groups were compared by one-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc tests. For specific analyses, Endotoxin content was analyzed using a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test; PRNT data were analyzed with the Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunnett's multiple comparisons; survival curves were compared using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Antibody titers were log10-transformed prior to analysis to meet parametric assumptions. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Except for blinded histopathological and imaging evaluations, no formal blinding was used in other experiments, all of which were conducted under standardized conditions.
