The richness, frequency and relatedness components of mate diversity and mate portfolios
Authors: Lawrence D. Harder, Mason W. Kulbaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Sexual reproduction produces genetically diverse families, especially when individuals mate with multiple partners.During mating, an individual accumulates a mate portfolio, the diversity of which depends on the number of mates, their relative mating contributions and characteristics. Nevertheless, studies of mate diversity generally focus on mate number or correlated mating, the probability that pairs of offspring are full siblings. Furthermore, mate diversity is usually quantified for incomplete samples that underestimate diversity because rare mates are underrepresented.Here, we demonstrate the application of methods developed in the context of species diversity to quantify and compare mate diversity. These methods are based on Hill numbers, which represent the spectrum of mate diversity from mate number (richness), through proportional representation of relative mate contributions (Shannon diversity), to emphasis on dominant mates (including Simpson diversity and the inverse of correlated mating). Hill‐number estimates can also be extrapolated to a common sample coverage, enabling standardized comparisons among portfolios, populations and species.In addition to comprehensive quantification, these methods expose new perspectives on mate diversity and provide means for their future analysis.

Introduction

In addition to segregation and recombination during meiosis, mating, especially with a variety of mates, generates the genetic and phenotypic diversity of offspring that is the hallmark of sexual reproduction (Hartfield & Keightley, 2012). The contribution of mating to this diversity depends on a population's mating system, which is an emergent property of who mated with whom and how often (Harder & Barrett, 1996; Barrett & Harder, 2017; Clo et al., 2025). By mating with a particular set of individuals, each member of a population accumulates a portfolio of mates with specific identities and characteristics who contribute to its offspring according to their respective fertilization success. In turn, the population's mating system represents the aggregate characteristics of the mate portfolios of its members. In contrast to segregation and recombination, which are universal features of sexual reproduction, the contribution of mating‐system characteristics to offspring diversity varies extensively among individuals, populations and species (see Barrett, 2003; Brandeis, 2018; Burgarella et al., 2024).

The influence of mating on outcrossed offspring diversity depends on both the number of different individuals with which an individual mates (mate richness) and their relative contributions. In species with internal fertilization, such as seed plants, mate diversity arises from a sequence of intersexual encounters that bring partners together (coupling: e.g. pollination) and determine which male partners participate in fertilization (progamy: e.g. pollen germination and tube growth into ovules). Coupling establishes the maximum number of mates per individual and their initial relative contributions. Both properties can be altered by progamic processes, including differential competition between mate gametophytes or sperm and/or their interactions with the maternal reproductive tract (Marshall & Ellstrand, 1986, 1989; Bernasconi, 2003; Ruane, 2009; Tonnabel et al., 2021). The mate diversity of maternal and paternal families can also change between sibling zygotes and independent offspring (e.g. seeds, seedlings) owing to differential survival during offspring development (e.g. Michalczyk et al., 2011). To the extent that these processes occur nonrandomly, mate diversity at fertilization or offspring independence depends on the variation in mate or offspring characteristics during preceding stages and so is not a simple linear consequence of mate number during coupling.

Mate diversity and its dynamics from coupling to independent offspring have varied effects on individual fitness, with consequences for the evolution of mating traits and reproduction in general. Quantitative effects arise if mate diversity influences offspring number as a maternal and/or paternal parent. Such influences can be negative when coupling entails costs or risk (Parker & Birkhead, 2013; Sáez et al., 2014), or positive when fertilizations or fruit retention increase with additional mates (Niesenbaum, 1999; Arnqvist & Nilsson, 2000; Paschke et al., 2002; Karron et al., 2006; Tonnabel et al., 2019). Prezygotically, coupling with multiple individuals increases the chances of engaging with fertile/compatible mates and of superior offspring genomes and allows for mate choice (Jennions & Petrie, 2000; Bernasconi, 2003; Yasui & Garcia‐Gonzalez, 2016). For example, many angiosperm species have physiological self‐incompatibility systems that preclude ovule fertilization by pollen from relatives (de Nettancourt, 2001; Igic & Kohn, 2006). For such species, exporting pollen to multiple recipients and importing pollen from multiple donors increases compatible mating. Similar benefits arise postzygotically by reducing the incidence of lethal inbreeding depression, if some partners are relatives (Michalczyk et al., 2011). Together, such quantitative effects should favour traits that promote mate diversity (Michalczyk et al., 2011; Medrano et al., 2012; Parker & Birkhead, 2013). In particular, the relation of reproductive success to mate number (one facet of mate diversity), known as the Bateman gradient, plays a central role in sexual selection when it differs between female and male functions (Bateman, 1948; Andersson & Iwasa, 1996; Tonnabel et al., 2019; Lehtonen, 2022).

Mate diversity can also confer qualitative effects on parental fertility and family performance (e.g. Nora et al., 2016). In general, mating with multiple partners increases offspring heterozygosity, thereby limiting uni‐ and bi‐parental inbreeding depression of offspring performance and promoting heterosis (Lankinen & Armbruster, 2007; Costa e Silva et al., 2014). In addition, progamic pollen‐tube competition and high heterozygosity can increase seed size, which enhances subsequent seedling establishment (Marshall & Ellstrand, 1986; Llorens et al., 2013; Takeuchi & Diway, 2021). Family performance may also be enhanced if half siblings (i.e. different mothers or fathers) have contrasting prospects of survival and/or reproduction in different environmental conditions and future conditions are uncertain (Jennions & Petrie, 2000; Olofsson et al., 2009). In these circumstances, fitness varies positively with mean performance and negatively with performance variance (Gillespie, 1973, 1974; Simons, 2009, 2011; Schreiber, 2015). Consequently, mate diversity can be advantageous if it reduces the long‐term variance in offspring success more than it reduces mean success, in a manner similar to the financial benefits of a diversified investment portfolio (Leonard, 1999; Barrett & Harder, 2017).

The preceding effects and consequences of mate diversity are typically framed in reference to mate number (richness), but that measure generally overrepresents diversity. For example, a maternal mate‐portfolio to which a few males contributed disproportionately is less diverse than if all contributed equally. Similarly, a paternal portfolio that primarily includes the focal individual's female relatives is less diverse, genetically and phenotypically, than if its mates were unrelated. These possibilities reveal the need for a broader conception of mate diversity that incorporates the proportional representation of mates and their characteristics, such as relatedness. They also raise questions about whether mate richness is the most relevant aspect of diversity in all contexts (e.g. Bateman's gradient).

An expanded conception should also recognize that diversity varies among nested scales that differ in their ecological, genetic and evolutionary relevance. For example, the mate portfolios of hermaphrodites include both female and male components, which may differ considerably (Christopher et al., 2019). Both components determine the diversity of an individual's total genetic contributions to offspring and govern the nature of sexual selection on mating traits (Anthes et al., 2010) and natural selection on sex allocation (Schärer & Pen, 2013). Additional examples include the individual and aggregate contributions of a plant's fruits to its female portfolio and of the portfolios of resident individuals to the total mate diversity of a population. This hierarchical structure of mate diversity has largely been overlooked, in part because current methods of assessing aspects of mate diversity other than richness focus on population‐level estimates and do not address the partitioning of diversity among scales (e.g. Ritland, 1989, 1990).

Regardless of the conception of mate diversity adopted in a particular study, accurate estimates of diversity are necessary to analyse its fitness consequences and genetic and evolutionary implications. For many species, the number of matings per individual and mate characteristics cannot be quantified exhaustively, so diversity must be estimated based on samples of mating events or offspring genotypes. If different mates contribute unequally, incomplete samples will underestimate diversity because common mates are more likely to be detected than rare mates. Furthermore, because underestimation depends on the frequency distribution of mate contributions within mate portfolios, equal sampling of different portfolios will generally not provide comparable estimates of mate diversity. In addition to its consequences for diversity estimation, incomplete sampling also leads to biased estimates of the influences of mate diversity on other reproductive outcomes, such as fertility (Mobley & Jones, 2013; Jones, 2015). All these statistical problems call for methods that limit underestimation and provide standardized (i.e. comparable) estimates of mate diversity.

Although the perspective that diversity depends on assemblage characteristics is uncommon in mating studies, it is well developed in the context of species diversity (for an overview, see Gotelli et al., 2024). Contemporary perspectives and methods concerning species diversity developed from extensive debate, leading to a growing consensus that Hill's (1973) approach to account for variation in the relative frequencies of entities quantifies diversity comprehensively (see Jost, 2007; Ellison, 2010 and associated Forum papers; Chao et al., 2014a). Recent elaborations of this approach allow standardization of estimates among samples, partitioning of within‐ and among‐assemblage diversity and inclusion of trait characteristics (Chao et al., 2021, 2023).

Here, we introduce these perspectives and methods in the context of mate diversity and illustrate their utility for understanding mating outcomes based on sampled mate portfolios. This approach can be applied to various mating events (e.g. pollen on stigmas, fertilizations). We specifically illustrate this approach based on the identities of the parents of individual seeds from a small population of a self‐compatible hermaphroditic, bee‐pollinated plant, Delphinium glaucum S. Watson, for which we estimated individual mate portfolios and mate relatedness. This overview demonstrates that contemporary methods can be applied straightforwardly to quantify mate diversity of promiscuous species. As previous studies of mate diversity have focussed on mate number it also exposes unexplored complexity of mating systems that might profitably be investigated by considering the heterogeneity of mate contributions and mate characteristics.

Empirical methods

We illustrate the interpolation (rarefaction), extrapolation and decomposition of mate diversity for the largest genetic individual (genet 95) in a population of 100 genets of D. glaucum on Moose Mountain, AB, Canada (50°54000.500 N, 114°45017.100 W) during the 2014 flowering season. Genet 95 produced 235 flowers, all of which produced pollen that could have dispersed to flowers of other plants, and 82 of those flowers developed fruits and seeds.

Using genotyping‐by‐sequencing, we genotyped most genets in the population for 10 458 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci (Kulbaba & Harder, 2025). With these genotypes, we estimated the relatedness of the parental plants with Wang's (2007) triadic likelihood estimator (TrioML), as implemented in the coancestry software package v.1.01.10 (Wang, 2011). Thirty SNPs were also selected to develop SNP‐SCALE markers for genotyping seeds from known maternal plants (Kulbaba & Harder, 2025). Marker selection was based on gametic phase equilibrium among loci and equal allele frequencies per locus (error rate, 0.93%).

The parents of individual seeds were identified based on the genotype of their known mother and the father inferred from each seed's genotype and the genotypes of the other plants in the population. Seed paternity was inferred using colony v.2.0.7.2 (Jones & Wang, 2010; Wang, 2013). In general, 2–3 randomly selected seeds were genotyped per fruit from fruits scattered within each genet's inflorescences. The total offspring sample for all plants involved 2596 genotyped seeds, of which 803 resulted from self‐mating. Here we consider only the outcrossed seeds, of which 97.6% were sired by plants with known genotypes. Among the 100 genets that were known parents of outcrossed seeds, 85 participated as female mates and 98 participated as male mates.

We estimated mate diversity and portfolio (dis)similarity with two software packages implemented in R v.4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). To illustrate Hill numbers and diversity estimation for individual portfolios, we used the inext.3d package v.1.0.10 (Hsieh et al., 2016; Chao et al., 2021). To demonstrate the decomposition of multiple portfolios (or portfolio components), we used the inext.beta3d package v.1.0.2 (Chao et al., 2023). The R code and data for the examples illustrated in Figs 1–3 are available via Supporting Information.

Diversity of individual portfolios

We illustrate basic features of the measurement of mate diversity for a population of N reproductive individuals, of which M individuals are potential outcross mates for any focal individual through a specific sex role (female or male). For a hermaphroditic species, M = N–1, whereas for a dioecious (gonochoric) species for which N = N ♀ + N ♂, the numbers of potential mates are M ♀ = N ♂ for females and M ♂ = N ♀ for males. Successful individuals mate with a subset of the potential mates, resulting in their own mate portfolio that minimally includes the identity of each mate and the number of its contributions to the focal individual's overall mating success. Information might also be available concerning each mate's characteristics, such as traits that might have affected their mating success or the relatedness to their mates. What constitutes a mating event (hereafter an event) depends on a study's focus, but could involve pollen grains on stigmas, fertilizations, offspring parentage, etc. Generically, the proportional representation of different individuals as mates in a mate portfolio is P = {p 1, p 2, …, p M }, where ∑i=1Mpi=1. We refer to P as the mate frequency distribution or MFD.

Hill numbers

Hill (1973) recognized that many possibilities for incorporating the effect of relative frequency on diversity metrics, D, could be represented along a continuum identified previously in the context of information theory by Rényi (1961). Specifically, for parameter −∞ ≤ q ≤ ∞, (Eqn 1a) Dq=∑i=1Mpiq1/1−q q≠1 (Eqn 1b) Dq=exp−∑i=1Mpilogepi q=1. Eqn 1a indicates that q serves two roles in q D. In the summation over all mates, q weights the influences of the relative frequencies of individual mates on the frequency sum. In the exponent, 1/(1 − q), q governs the transformation of the weighted frequency sum into a count of ‘effective’ mates (explained below). If q = 1, the exponent of Eqn 1a involves division by 0, so 1 D is instead calculated as the limit of q D as q approaches 1 (Eqn 1b). Henceforth, the relation of q D to q will be referred to as the diversity profile.

For an MFD, q D typically varies negatively with increasing q from a maximum of 1/p min for q = −∞ to a minimum of 1/p max for q = ∞, where p min and p max are the relative frequencies of the least and most frequent mates, respectively (Hill, 1973; see Fig. 1d). Within this range, 1 D is a special case, because when q = 1, p i q equals p i and mates contribute to the summation in proportion to their relative frequencies. By contrast, when q < 1 or q > 1 mates contribute disproportionately to the frequency sum compared to their relative frequencies (p i q > p i or p i q < p i , respectively). For example, consider the simple two‐mate maternal portfolio with male 1 contributing four times more than male 2 (i.e. p 1 = 0.8, p 2 = 0.2). If q = 2, p 1 q = 0.64 is 16 times larger than p 2 q = 0.04, whereas if q = 0.5, p 1 q = 0.884 is only twice p 2 q = 0.447. Thus, when q > 1 q D primarily represents the number of common (dominant) mates, especially as q increases. Conversely, when q < 1 rare mates influence q D disproportionately and this influence increases with declining q. The case of q = 0 is particularly notable, as p q = 1 for all p and the exponent equals 1. Consequently, dominant and rare mates are considered equivalently, regardless of their relative contributions, so 0 D = M. Note that the emphasis on rare mates when q < 1 has implications for estimation of mate diversity. Because infrequent sampling of rare mates creates considerable uncertainty for estimates of q D, estimation of mate diversity should generally involve only q ≥ 0 (see Gotelli et al., 2024).

To understand the effect of MFD characteristics on q D, consider the diversity profiles for three cases. Each case involves 10 mates, so differences in q D arise only because of differences in their relative frequencies (Fig. 1a–c). First, consider equally frequent mates (i.e. p i = 1/M = 0.1; Fig. 1a). For this condition, the summation in Eqn 1a becomes M(1/M) q = M 1−q , so Dq=M1−q1/1−q, which equals M regardless of q. Thus, for the equal MFD, q D = 10 mates (Fig. 1d, solid line). By contrast, when mates contribute unequally, q D differs from 0 D to an extent determined by the inequality of contributions (Fig. 1d). For example, consider two MFDs, with less inequality for case 1 than for case 2 (Fig. 1b,c, respectively). In general, when q > 0 mate diversity profiles for unequal MFDs decline with increasing q, with steeper declines for the more inequitable portfolios (Fig. 1d). Thus, the extent of divergence of q D from 0 D with increasing q illustrates the effect of unevenness in mate representation on diversity as common mates increasingly dominate q D.

The preceding cases also clarify the meaning of q D as an effective mate number for q > 0. For example, consider 1 D (orange square) and 2 D (orange triangle) in Fig. 1(d) for the unequal‐2 MFD. Recall that for an equal MFD, q D is constant for all q. Therefore, 1 D = 5.10 effective mates for the unequal‐2 MFD is equivalent to the diversity of an equal MFD with 5.10 mates (Fig. 1d, black dashed line), each of which contributed to proportion 1/1 D = 0.196 of the events in the portfolio. As q = 1 in this case, this inference applies specifically when mate contributions are weighed proportionally in the calculation of q D. If instead q = 2, q D primarily reflects the smaller number of dominant mates. Specifically, 2 D is equivalent to the diversity of an equal distribution with 2.96 mates (Fig. 1d, grey dashed line), each of which contributed to proportion 1/2 D = 0.338 of the events in the portfolio.

Hill (1973) recognized that q D for three specific values of q represent familiar diversity metrics or transformations of them. For q = 0, q D = M, or mate number or mate ‘richness’. For q = 1, the term in parentheses in Eqn 1b is Shannon's (1948) entropy, a widely used diversity index. In the context of mating, entropy refers to the average uncertainty that a given mate participated in a specific mating event. Exponential transformation of this index yields the effective mate number, 1 D, referred to as ‘Shannon diversity’ (Chao et al., 2014b). Similarly, for q = 2, the weighted frequency sum is equivalently Simpson's (1949) concentration index and Ritland's (1989) index of correlated paternity (also see Starr, 1984). In the context of mating, this index represents the probability that two randomly drawn mating events involved the same mate. Inverse transformation of this index yields the effective mate number, 2 D, referred to as ‘Simpson diversity’ (Chao et al., 2014b). In recognition of Hill's (1973) unifying insight, q D is referred to as a Hill number of order q (Jost, 2006).

Estimation and comparison of mate diversity

The preceding methods would estimate the complete mate‐diversity profile for an individual effectively if all mating events were considered; however, mate sampling is often incomplete. In general, the number of mates detected in a sample of mating events increases asymptotically with the number of events sampled as additional rare mates are detected (see Fig. 2a). Consequently, sample estimates typically underestimate mate richness and provide uncertain estimates of relative mating frequencies. Incomplete sampling additionally complicates comparison of mate diversity among individuals, sex roles, populations, etc., unless estimates can be standardized effectively.

In addition to providing a point estimate of q D s , the mate identities and frequencies for a sample of s r mating events (observed sample portfolio) can include sufficient information to characterize two general associations that are useful for standardization. First, the MFD that summarizes an observed portfolio serves as a reference from which both to interpolate the relation of q D to sampling intensity from 0 to s r (rarefaction) for the entire portfolio and to extrapolate that relation for more than s r events (see Colwell et al., 2012; Chao et al., 2014b; e.g. Fig. 2a). This inferred relation can be used to estimate q D for a wide range of sampling intensities.

As an empirical example, consider estimates of maternal mate diversity for genet 95. The observations involve the paternal identity for a reference sample of s r = 126 seeds collected from 82 fruits (mean = 1.54 seeds per fruit), which represent a small fraction of the c. 2100 seeds that genet 95 produced (125 fruits, mean 16.8 seeds per fruit). Most males identified as genet 95's male mates sired only a few genotyped seeds, but one male sired 15 (see Fig. 3a, pink bars). Owing to variation in male contributions, the observed mate‐diversity profile declines strongly with increasing q (see symbols in Fig. 2a). Specifically, the observed maternal mate portfolio involved 0 D = 46 seed fathers (q = 0; male‐mate richness), whereas Shannon and Simpson diversities were equivalent to those expected for 1 D = 33.7 and 2 D = 24.6 effective mates with equal frequencies, respectively.

According to the rarefaction/extrapolation relations inferred for genet 95, estimates of the three diversity metrics and their 95% confidence intervals increase in a decelerating manner with the number of sampled seeds (Fig. 2a). The difference between the extrapolated diversity for 2s r = 252 seeds from the values for the observed sample (Fig. 2a, symbols) indicates the extent to which the sample underestimated q D for the total maternal portfolio. In particular, mate richness was subject to greater underestimation than Shannon diversity and especially Simpson diversity. Furthermore, because the rarefaction/extrapolation relation decelerates faster with increasing q, q D for different q diverge with increasing sample size. Given this dependence on sampling intensity, how can mate diversity be estimated reliably to allow comparison among portfolios?

The relation of sample coverage, C, to the MFD sampled from a portfolio, (Eqn 2) C=1−∑i=1Mpi1−pisr provides a means of addressing these problems (Chao & Jost, 2012; Chao et al., 2014b). In the context of mate diversity, coverage represents the proportion of all sampled and unsampled mating events in an assemblage (e.g. portfolio, population) that resulted from mating of a focal parent with the other adults identified in the sample. Eqn 2 refines a relation derived by Alan Turing to break the Enigma code during World War II (Good, 2000). He identified the remarkable result that the proportion of items (e.g. individual mates) that occur only once (singletons) in a sample provides enough information to approximately estimate sample coverage (i.e. C ≈ 1–n 1/s r, where n 1 is the number of singletons). Eqn 2 enhances the approximation by also accounting for the proportion of items that occur twice (doubletons).

For a given reference sample of size s r, coverage varies with the (unknown) number of mates in the complete portfolio, M, and their relative contributions, p i (see Supporting Information Notes S1), and so is based on the assemblage's characteristics, rather than the vagaries of sampling. Consequently, diversity estimation based on coverage is less biased than that based on sample size (see Chao & Jost, 2012). Mate richness, 0 D, can be extrapolated to the coverage associated with 2s r events, C(2s r ), with negligible effect on prediction bias (Chao & Jost, 2012). For Shannon and Simpson diversity, extrapolation can be extended to C = 1, allowing nearly unbiased estimation of the asymptotes of 1 D and 2 D (Chao et al., 2014b). The different limits for unbiased extrapolation for richness vs Shannon and Simpson diversities arise because the richness extrapolation estimator is only a lower bound. The relation of q D to coverage enables standardized comparison of mate diversity among k portfolios. Chao et al. (2014b) proposed that comparisons for q > 0 involve extrapolated estimates of q D for C = 1. For richness (q = 0), they recommended comparison at a base coverage of C base = maximum [C a , C b ], where C a = maximum [C(s r1), C(s r2), …, C(s rk )] and C b = minimum [C(2s r1), C(2s r2), …, C(2s rk )].

Returning to genet 95, despite representing only c. 6% of its seeds, the reference sample (s r = 126) estimated 85.8% coverage of all of genet 95's male mates (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, extrapolation to 2s r provides nearly exhaustive coverage (C[2s r] = 95.4%) and the 95% confidence interval includes 1, the maximum possible. The asymptotic estimates of 1 D and 2 D at complete coverage (C = 1; Fig. 2c) suggest that the observed reference sample underrepresented Shannon and Simpson diversity by 24.0% and 18.9%, respectively.

Relatedness diversity

The preceding examples considered diversity based solely on mate identity, but diversity can also involve mate characteristics. Of genetic relevance is the relatedness of mates (0 ≤ R ≤ 1) to each other and to the focal individual (see Ritland, 1989). Hill numbers allow estimation of relatedness diversity as a specific case of functional diversity (see Chiu & Chao, 2014; Chao et al., 2014a, 2019). In this context, mates that are less related than a threshold (i.e. R < T) are considered functionally (i.e. genetically) distinct, whereas those with R ≥ T are functionally equivalent. In practice, analysis of functional diversity considers trait dissimilarity (i.e. the ‘distance’ between entities), rather than similarly, so in the context of relatedness, the discriminating threshold is that unrelatedness, U = 1–R, is greater than τ = 1–T. Accordingly, q D R represents the effective number of groups of mates with U ≥ τ. If no specific threshold relatedness is of interest, its average influence can be assessed by considering relatedness integrated over 0 ≤ τ ≤ 1 (Chao et al., 2019).

We illustrate this approach with the maternal portfolio for genet 95, based on the unrelatedness of its male mates estimated from their genotypes for 10 458 SNPs. One male mate in the observed portfolio was not genotyped for SNPs, so the sample involved the 45 fathers of 124 seeds. The male mates were mostly unrelated to each other (median U = 1); however, 11.6% were at least second cousins (unrelatedness ≤ 0.969). As illustrated in Fig. 2(d), increasing the unrelatedness threshold from the minimum (0.425) to the maximum (1) generally reduces the estimated diversity of the observed sample. The effect of relatedness varied with q, being evident at lower thresholds and having a greater effect at higher thresholds for richness (q = 0) than for Shannon (q = 1) and especially Simpson diversity (q = 2). This pattern indicates that the dominant fathers of genet 95's seeds were less related to each other than were the rare mates.

Diversity decomposition for multiple portfolios

Often, diversity has structure, with ‘global’ and ‘local’ components. In the context of mating, related questions arise about the diversity of mate contributions among and within a set of mate portfolios. Structured diversity can be examined from three perspectives, known in the species‐diversity literature as γ, α and β diversity (Whittaker, 1972; Jost, 2007). Conceptually, γ refers to the overall diversity of mates in a pooled sample of k mate portfolios, γ = D 1 ∪ D 2 ∪ … ∪ D k . Likewise, α is the average mate diversity for individual portfolios, α=∑i=1kDi/k, or the mean effective number of mates per portfolio. The meaning and estimation of β diversity are more contentious (Chao et al., 2008; Ellison, 2010). A common conception in the context of richness is 0 β = 0 γ /0 α, which quantifies the dissimilarity among portfolios as represented by the effective number of distinct portfolios (Jost, 2007; Chiu et al., 2014). This conceptual decomposition of γ diversity into α and β components ignores differences in the total number of mating events (e.g. offspring) among portfolios. In practice, unbiased estimates of γ, α and β diversity are weighted by those differences, as described by Chiu et al. (2014).

Although β diversity measures dissimilarity for an individual set of portfolios, a transformation of it provides a more intuitive measure that can be compared among analyses. Minimum and maximum β arise in alternative extreme conditions. In the context of mate richness, if all k portfolios involve the same set of mates, 0 γ = 0 α, so 0 β min = 1, regardless of k. By contrast, when all portfolios involve completely different mates, 0 γ = k 0 α, so 0 β max = k (also see Jost, 2006; Chao et al., 2012; Chiu et al., 2014). Owing to the latter possibility, β generally varies positively with k among analyses that consider different numbers of portfolios, complicating comparison of portfolio dissimilarity. Instead, the dissimilarity index 0 δ = (0 β–0 β min)/(0 β max–0 β) = (0 β–1)/(k–1) accounts for the influence of k on 0 β and ranges from 0 (identical portfolios) to 1 (unique portfolios), regardless of k. More generally, δq=βq1−q−1k1−q−1, q≠1 δ1=logeβ1logek, q=1 (Gotelli et al., 2024; also see Chiu et al., 2014 for the derivation of 1– q δ [their C qN ]). q δ estimates the effective average proportion of unique mate ‘cohorts’ in a portfolio.

Diversity decomposition can be applied to any set of mate portfolios drawn from a common assemblage of potential mates. As examples, this approach could be used to characterize mating heterogeneity among fruits (see Notes S2), ramets or sex roles within individual genets, or among genets or reproductive seasons within populations. However, it would be less informative for comparing mate diversity among populations that are not connected by pollen dispersal, as β and δ would be 1 and 0, respectively. Here, we illustrate decomposition by considering the maternal and paternal components of genet 95′ s total mate portfolio (i.e. k = 2 sex roles). The total sample included 126 outcrossed seeds that genet 95 produced as a female parent and 86 seeds it sired as a male parent (Fig. 3a). This sample provided the 85.8% coverage of genet 95′ s male mates (Fig. 2b,c) and 93.5% coverage of its female mates (Fig. S1d).

To demonstrate basic features of diversity decomposition, we first consider the unweighted, observed mating outcomes. For both sex roles combined, the sample for genet 95 included 0 γ obs = 64 different individuals as mates. The maternal component involved 46 male mates, whereas the paternal component involved 37 female mates, so 0 α = (46 + 37)/2 = 41.5 effective mates per portfolio. Consequently, 0 β ≈ 64/41.5 = 1.54, which represents the effective number of distinct sex‐role portfolios included in plant 95's total portfolio. According to the dissimilarity index, 0 δ = 0.54, plant 95 mated with 54% more mates overall than it did through either sex role, on average.

The preceding unweighted characterization of γ, α, β and δ for an observed sample can be extended to partition diversity for any order q and can be implemented for weighted, rarefied, observed and extrapolated estimators (Jost, 2007; Chiu et al., 2014; Chiu & Chao, 2014). For such an analysis, extrapolated sample coverage for twice the observed sample, C(2s r), differs for the estimation of γ and α, being smaller for α (see Fig. S2). Therefore, estimates of mate richness for α and β should not be extrapolated beyond C(2s r) for α (Chao et al., 2023). Fig. 3 illustrates the application of these techniques for plant 95 for extrapolated estimates at > 95% α sample coverage. The (unweighted) individual diversity profiles (Fig. 3b) reveal differences in the MFDs for female and male function. Specifically, the slower decline of q D for the paternal portfolio indicates that plant 95 mated more equitably as a male among its female mates than as a female with its male mates (also see Figs 3a, S1). According to the weighted, extrapolated estimate of richness, Plant 95 mated with 0 γ ≈ 76 of the 99 other plants in the population as female or male, and with a mean of 0 α = 45 mates through its individual sex roles (Fig. 3c). q α declines less from richness to Shannon to Simpson diversity than q γ, primarily because the averaging to estimate α tempers variation of (mean) offspring per mate. The extrapolated estimates of q β and q δ indicate considerable disparity of male and female mates in Plant 95′ s overall mating portfolio (estimates close to the maximum possible in Fig. 3d,e). This disparity resulted mostly from differences in which mates were involved in the maternal and paternal portfolios, rather than from their dissimilar contributions to offspring, as the 95% confidence intervals of estimated β and δ for richness, Shannon and Simpson diversity overlap broadly.

Discussion

For polygamous species, mate number alone provides a limited perspective of mate diversity if mates contribute unequally to mate portfolios in quantity or quality. Incomplete sampling of portfolios accentuates this shortcoming as it underestimates mate number and provides uncertain estimates of relative mate contributions. Hill (1973) numbers and contemporary methods for extrapolation and decomposition (Chao et al., 2021, 2023; Gotelli et al., 2024) can be applied readily to address these problems. These methods create a tractable framework for measuring and interpreting the diversity and variation of mate portfolios within populations. They also provide standardized diversity estimates that can be compared reliably among individuals, populations, species and studies. Intriguingly, application of Hill numbers to mate diversity also raises conceptual questions concerning what aspect(s) of diversity are most relevant biologically.

Practical considerations

The methods described above can be applied quite generally. Estimation of Hill numbers (Eqn 1a, 1b) of any order (q) for observed samples requires only recognition of different mates and counts of their contributions to a portfolio. In addition, extrapolation based on samples to estimate total portfolio diversity is based on few, relatively robust statistical assumptions (see Chao et al., 2023; Notes S1). Derivation of these methods assumed random distribution and independent sampling of individual entities (i.e. mating events). However, Chao et al. (2023) demonstrated that, with independent sampling, the distribution assumption is superfluous. Thus, for example, random sampling of fruits and their seeds to identify male mates should provide reliable, unbiased estimates even if siring by individual mates is clustered within subsets of fruits. Although extrapolation is also robust against moderate nonindependence of sampling, random sampling is always desirable.

Given the increasing, but decelerating relation of diversity estimates to the number of sampled mating events (e.g. Fig. 2a; Notes S2), sampling design can influence estimation accuracy. Larger samples are generally preferable (Notes S2). However, the details of this benefit depend on the characteristics of the actual mating portfolios, including total mate number and the inequity of mate contributions, which influence sample coverage. In general, the same coverage can be attained with a smaller sample if mates contribute equally than if their contributions differ extensively. Sampling considerations are particularly relevant to studies of the global and local diversity components for multiple portfolios. For a given sampling effort (i.e. total sample size), sampling many portfolios reduces the number of samples per portfolio. Owing to this trade‐off, sampling more portfolios reduces the coverage and accuracy for estimating mean per‐portfolio (α) diversity and the effective number of portfolio cohorts (β diversity) (Notes S2).

That diversity estimates are best compared for the same sample coverage, rather than the same sample size, has implications for the distribution of sampling intensity. To attain a given sample coverage, C~, more sampling is needed for more diverse portfolios (Chao & Jost, 2012; also see Fig. S1; Notes S1). If circumstances allow identification of mates during sampling of mating events, Chao & Jost (2012) recommend that sampling stop once C~=1−f1s·s−1f1s−1f1+2f2 where s is the number of sampled events, and f 1 and f 2 are the numbers of mates represented by one and two observations, respectively. For multiple portfolios with differing mate diversity, this approach ensures the desired coverage with less sampling effort than a fixed sample size per portfolio. As an example, this approach could be adopted if mate diversity of seeds is to be estimated and more seeds have been collected than can be genotyped. Using an appropriate sampling scheme (see Notes S2), a subsample of seeds should be collected randomly from all chosen sampling units (e.g. fruits, days, plants), genotyped, and aggregate coverage estimated and compared with Chao & Jost's stopping criterion. This process would then be repeated until the criterion was satisfied.

The asymptotic increases of estimated mate diversity and sample coverage with sample size (e.g. Fig. 2a) raise questions about the comparability of published estimates of mate diversity. For example, recent studies have used estimates of effective mate number based on the inverse of Ritland's (1989) estimator for correlated mating, r p, to support arguments concerning the relation of mate diversity to outcrossing rate (Barrett & Harder, 2017), sexual selection (Dorken & Perry, 2017), the evolution of multiple mating (Pannell & Labouche, 2013), sex allocation (Burd, 2025) and sexual systems (Bawa et al., 2025). Ritland (1989, pg. 849) defined r p as ‘the proportion of full‐sibs among outcrossed sibs’ or the probability that two outcrossed offspring drawn randomly from a maternal family had the same father. This probability is equivalent to Simpson's (1949) concentration index, so 1/r p estimates Simpson diversity (2 D). The cited surveys used 2 D estimates from many studies that involved samples of different sizes that were not extrapolated to a specific coverage and so are likely not comparable. This possibility creates uncertainty about the conclusions arising from surveys based on r p. More generally, it raises concerns about the utility of sample estimates of correlated mating and 1/r p as estimators of portfolio and population mate diversity.

Several approaches could be adopted to ensure comparable estimates of mate diversity among populations and studies. Ideally, samples would be sufficiently large to support estimation of asymptotic diversity, which will often be feasible for Shannon and Simpson diversity (Chao et al., 2014b). For richness, non‐asymptotic estimates could be compared for a standard coverage. Such comparison would be facilitated by publication of diversity‐coverage profiles (e.g. Figs 2c, S1c,d, S2).

The scope of mate diversity

Like species diversity, mate diversity is complex because it depends on the number of entities and their relative abundances and characteristics. Consequently, no single value can encapsulate mate diversity. Identity‐based Hill numbers address this complexity by representing diversity as a spectrum from ignoring abundance (q = 0, mate richness) to strong emphasis on dominant mates (q ≥ 2, including Simpson diversity). This spectrum can be elaborated by accounting for mate characteristics, including their relatedness. Thus, the Hill‐number approach allows comprehensive quantification of mate diversity.

Given the breadth of possibilities, is the entire spectrum relevant in all circumstances? Consider three examples that involve different aspects of pollination and fertilization. First, pollen removal and deposition on stigmas of flowers occur primarily during the first visit to flowers of some species of papilionaceous legumes, such as Medicago, that trip explosively when visited by a pollinator with sufficient mass (e.g. Dieterich Mabin et al., 2024). In such cases, a plant's mate diversity will vary primarily with its number of tripped flowers, so mate richness could be a relevant diversity metric. Second, when ovule fertilization and zygote development occur randomly, regardless of the relative abundance of pollen received from different pollen donors, Shannon diversity should be most representative of the diversity of maternal and paternal mate portfolios. Third, if the more common donors of pollen on stigmas or sires of zygotes are represented disproportionally as seed fathers in individual fruits (i.e. positive frequency‐dependence), Simpson diversity would be more indicative of their siring prospects. Such examples illustrate that perspectives on the many aspects of mating and their consequences might benefit from consideration of the spectrum of mate diversity and its biological relevance in specific circumstances.

The spectrum of mate diversity also raises questions about the sufficiency of current perspectives on the ecology and evolution of reproduction. For example, the Bateman gradient, which is a focal concept in sexual selection (Bateman, 1948; Andersson & Iwasa, 1996; Tonnabel et al., 2019; Lehtonen, 2022), represents the relation of reproductive success to mate richness. However, if mates contribute disproportionally to mate portfolios, reproductive success, and hence sexual selection, might vary more strongly with Shannon or Simpson diversity, depending on the absence or presence of differential pollen‐tube and zygote success (see Dorken & Perry, 2017). This and other influences of the spectrum of mate diversity await exploration. Hill numbers provide a tool that could be applied effectively in this exploration.
