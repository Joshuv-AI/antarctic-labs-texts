Human rabies exposure and associated risk factors in North-West Ethiopia
Authors: Workneh Wondimagegn Azalu, Sefinew Alemu Mekonnen, Ambaye Kenubih, Araya Mengistu Kassa, Wassie Abebe Molla, Wudu Temesgen Jemberu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe study was conducted to assess community awareness of rabies, and incidence of human rabies exposure and its associated risk factors.Study designA retrospective cohort study was conducted to assess community awareness of rabies, and incidence of human rabies exposure and associated risk factors.MethodA total of 642 study participants were selected using multistage cluster sampling from seven districts in Gondar zones in northwest Ethiopia. Data was collected using a questionnaire by face-to-face interviews. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify associations between rabies exposure and potential risk factors.ResultsThe incidence of rabies exposure in the current study was estimated to be 20.95 % per year. Almost all (99.68 %) of respondents were aware of the existence of rabies, and 98.44 % of them know that rabies is a zoonotic disease. Residents of Genda Wuha (OR = 7.68) and Metema districts (OR = 7.12) had a greater risk of rabies exposure compared to those living in Debre Tabor district. Dog ownership (OR = 1.61) was greater risk for rabies exposure than non-dog ownership, larger family size (≥7 members) (OR = 2.23) had greater risk for rabies exposure.ConclusionThe study outlined that although awareness of existence of rabies and its zoonotic risk was high, taking post-exposure prophylaxis was low. Therefore, use post exposure rabies vaccine of should be promoted.

Introduction

Rabies is caused by rabies virus of the genus Lyssavirus, family Rhabdoviridae and order Mononegavirales. The virus affects the central nervous system of all mammals (Wunner, 2007; [1,2]) and it is 100 % fatal once clinical signs appear. The virus is transmitted mainly by the bite of rabid animals and can also be transmitted through direct contact of fresh wound or intact mucosal surface (eyes, nose) with infectious saliva or by licks of rabid animals [3,4]. Once the virus starts to infect, it starts to replicate inside the bitten muscle tissue, and it travels to the central nervous system [5], and results in disease development [6]. The disease leads to respiratory dysfunction, which in turn causes secondary metabolic and circulatory issues and ultimately resulting in death [7].

Rabies is a common disease causing significant social and economic problems in many countries [8]. It is mainly affecting the poorest communities in low- and middle-income countries [8,9], particularly in Africa and Asia where over 95 % of all rabies-related deaths occur. These regions continue to face significant public health threats from the disease [10,11]. In Africa alone, rabies claims the lives of approximately 24,000 people each year [12]. Several factors contribute to the occurrence of rabies in these countries [9,13] like economic and geographic factors, animal contact, lack of awareness and education, poor dog management, demographic factors and seasonal patterns. Poor management of wounds and poor adherence to post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) are also associated with a higher risk of developing human rabies [9].

Ethiopia is considered to have a significant rabies burden among African countries, with the disease being endemic and primarily affecting rural communities. Rabies outbreaks are often exacerbated by limited access to healthcare and the reliance on traditional practices for treatment. Rural areas face heightened risks due to the prevalence of stray dogs, close interactions between domestic dogs and wildlife, and low levels of awareness about rabies prevention and control measures [14,15]. A national surveillance conducted from 2007 until 2012 showed 15,178 exposures and 272 fatal cases, with more than 88 % of the exposure cases being dog bites [16]. Based on the latest report in Ethiopia, approximately 97,000 people are exposed to rabies annually with approximately 3,000 deaths [17]. However, there is lack of reliable data on the annual number of rabies cases on dog bites and risk factors for human exposure to rabies. Understanding the current situation of rabies exposure is crucial for designing impactful public health interventions. Identifying risk factors enhances community safety by reducing exposure to rabies, while understanding human behavior related to animal interactions informs prevention strategies such as promoting safe practices around animals. Accurate data on rabies exposure and risk factors enable to allocate resources effectively to control the disease [18]. This study aims to assess community awareness of rabies, the incidence of rabies exposure over the past two years, and associated factors in northwest Ethiopia.

Methods

Description of the study area

The Amhara region is located between 8°45′N and 13°45′ N latitude and 35° 46′ and 40° 25′ E longitude, covering approximately 170,000 square kilometers in the northwestern part of Ethiopia [19]. Known for its diverse topography, climate, agroecology, and animal population, the region has varying climatic conditions. Regarding the climate of the Amhara region, 31 % of the area with an altitude of 1500 m.a.s.l. is classified as Kola (hot zone), 44 % with an altitude of 1500–2500 m.a.s.l. is categorized as Woyina Dega (warm zone), and 25 % with an elevation of 2500–4620 m.a.s.l. is classified as Dega (cold zone) [20].

The mean annual temperature ranges from 15 °C to 21 °C, with temperatures exceeding 27 °C in valleys and marginal areas. Some valleys and marginal regions exhibit arid climates. The region experiences two distinct seasons: a short rainy season lasting three to four months and a long dry season of eight to nine months [21].

The total human population of the region is estimated to be around 22,877,365, with approximately 88 % residing in rural areas and the remaining 12 % in urban areas [19].

The study specifically focused on the Gondar zones (Central, West, North, and South Gondar) of the Amhara region in northwest Ethiopia. This area is bordered by Lake Tana to the south, West Gojjam, Agew Awi, East Gojjam zones, Bahir Dar city, and Benishangul-Gumuz Region to the west, Sudan to the west, Tigray Region to the north, Wag Hemra and North Wollo zone to the east, and South Wollo and Abay River to the southeast. The total estimated human population in this area is 5,464,079, with a livestock population of 5,474,545 cattle, 2,484,400 sheep, 2,461,942 goats, 44,860 horses, 847,780 donkeys, and 6,609 dogs [22,23]. The study selected seven districts (East Dembia and Tach Armachiho from Central Gondar, Genda Wuha and Metema from West Gondar, Fogera and Debre Tabor from South Gondar, and Debark from North Gondar zones) in the Amhara region for research purposes (Fig. 1). Each of these zones (Central, North, South, and West Gondar) offers a unique combination of geography, culture, economy, and history that contribute to the overall richness of the Amhara Region in Ethiopia [22].Fig. 1Map of the study area.Fig. 1

Study population

The study population consisted of human populations in the Central, West, North, and South Gondar zones of the Amhara region in northwest Ethiopia. In these Gondar zones, majority of owned and non-owned dogs roam freely. This, combined with a lack of regulations on dog breeding resulted in a large number of uncontrolled and free roaming dogs. This dog population growth increased interactions between dogs and humans, leading to risks of rabies transmission. Many of the dogs are kept primarily for guarding and herding, but they are often not confined and not vaccinated, that exacerbate the risk of rabies exposure [24].

Study design and sampling methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted to assess community awareness of rabies, and incidence of human rabies exposure and associated risk factors. A three-stage cluster sampling technique was used to select sampling units. Initially, the four zones of Gondar were purposively selected due to the common occurrence of rabies in these areas [25,26]. Factors such as the presence of Semen National Park (the area where Semen foxes and other carnivores, which are carriers of rabies, are found), potential contact with wildlife, repeated outbreaks in the zones, and a high density of poorly managed dog populations contributed to this selection. However, there was a lack of concrete information to establish a prevention and control policy. Subsequently, districts or towns representative of the zones were chosen: East Dembia and Tach Armachiho from Central, Debark from North, Metema and Genda-wuha from West, and Debre Tabor and Fogera from South Gondar zones. Finally, four kebeles (the smallest administrative units in the country's local government structure) were randomly selected from each district, totaling 28 kebeles [27,28].

The questionnaire aimed at gathering data on occurrences of rabies exposure, associated risk factors, and post-exposure treatment in the human population. This typically occurs through bites, scratches, or licks on broken skin but may also happen through direct contact with mucous membranes. The design effect (D) is calculated as a function of the cluster size and the intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC). In this study, the cluster size was set at 28 with 23 households selected for interviews per cluster through equal distribution. In multistage cluster surveys, ICC reflects variability across all sampling stages. For infectious diseases an ICC value of 0.025 is commonly used [31]. Considering these factors, D for this sample size determination was 1.67. This resulted in a final sample size of 642 households.

Data analysis

The data was entered into Microsoft Excel and then exported to Stata release 17 (Stata Corp LP, USA) for analysis. The completeness of the data was checked, followed by calculations of descriptive statistics such as percentages and rates. Incidence was estimated by the ratio of number of rabies exposures to the total households sampled per year. Risk factor analysis was conducted using binary logistic regression model. Initially, a univariable analysis was conducted. This was followed by a pairwise correlation analysis to assess collinearity among the variables that showed a P-value of 0.05 or less in the univariable analysis. Multivariable logistic regression models were then used to identify factors associated with rabies exposure. Any variables with a P-value ≤0.05 were considered significant in the final model. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was utilized to assess the goodness of fit of the model. The presence of confounding was checked by adding one variable at a time in the model-building process. Confounding is identified when the inclusion of a variable significantly alters the observed relationship between the independent and dependent variables. A change exceeding a predefined threshold or cutt-point (25 %) was considered indicative of potential confounding. Interactions were checked for variables that remained in the final model. Findings were presented with odds ratios (ORs), 95 % confidence intervals, and p-values.

Respondent's awareness and management of rabies

The incidence of rabies exposure in the current study was estimated to be 20.95 % per year. Majorities of respondents were aware of presence of rabies (99.69 %) and that it is a zoonotic disease (98.44 %), and one or more methods of its transmission (93.93 %). However, relatively small number of them (41 %) practised control measures such as vaccinating their dogs. About 22 % of exposed respondents reported having PEP. (Table 2)

Discussion

The objectives of the study were to understand awareness about rabies, estimate incidence and to identify associated risk factors to rabies exposure, in northwest Ethiopia. We found that 99.7 % of respondents were aware of existence of rabies, it is zoonotic diseas(98.4 %), and the ways of transmission(945). The level of awareness of rabies we found in the current study (99.7 %) was higher that what was reported previously (40.9 %) by Belay et al. [32] in South Wollo zone and 87.8 % reported by Hagos et al. [33] from Mekele in Ethiopioa. However, Kebede et al [34] from Jima zone of Oromia of Ethiopia and Bahiru et al. [35] from Amhara region of Ethiopia reported almost equivalent level of awareness to rabies, 95.7 % and 98.4 %, respectively.

Respondents reported that dog plays a major role in the transmission of rabies. This is consistent with the report of Beyene et al. [17] from Ethiopia. This finding is supported by the report of Hampson et al. [36] from Tanzania who found that 89 % respondents suspected rabies exposures were due to dogs. This is almost an established knowledge reported by many authors across the world ([37]; Wunner, 2007; [14]) that dog plays the major role in transmitting rabies except in latin American countries where vampire bats also responsible up to 1 % of in the transmission of rabies [38].

While respondents were aware of rabies, the disease is zoonotic, and the ways of transmission, relatively, a small portion of respondents (21.93 %) used PEPin their family members. Previously, Beyene et al. [17] reported 48.4 % of individuals who were bitten by animals received PEP which was also small relative to the risk of rabies. The findings seems contradictory that people did not take post exposure prevention while they are exposed to rabies and know the risk of the exposure. Furthermore, the survey revealed that smaller number of respondents (41.3 %) vaccinated their dogs, highlighting another challenge alongside the low use of PEP. This can be due to many factors. Lack of money in the family to cover costs of the PEP treatment can be one reason. Another can be related to their belief about the effectiveness of the PEP treatment or shortage or inaccessibility of the vaccine [8]. Hampson et al. [8] reported that low income is one of the contributing factor for the low utilization of PEP.This is supported by a recent study conducted by Mengie et al. [39] in the same study area who reported that although greatest majority of dog owners (81 %) were willing to pay for vaccine in controlling rabies, the number of respondents decreased from 81 % to 44 % when the amount of hypothetetical cost of rabies vaccine was increased. Economic constraints are a significant challenge for rabies prevention. In developing countries, the high cost of PEP and limited access to healthcare services hinder effective rabies control [40].

The annual incidence of house hold rabies exposure was estimated at 20.95 % in the current study which was high that surpasses the incidence (10.1 %) reported by Beyene et al. [17] and 12.1 % by Jemberu et al. [25] in Ethiopia. This increased incidence might be explained related to animal, environmental, and socio-economic factors. Increased dog population and pet ownership without proper vaccination, increased urbanization leading to more human-animal interaction, spillover from wild animals and failure to control rabies in wildlife reservoirs are few of animal related factors. Climate change altering animal behavior and migration, increasing contact, and conflict displacing animals and people, disrupting vaccination programs are some of the environmental factors. Moreover, it can be related to use of traditional healers instead of medical treatment after animal bites or related to the limited access to vaccines and healthcare.

Metema, Genda wuha, Tacharmachiho and Debrak districts had higher odds of rabies exposure compared to that of Debre Tabor district. This can be explained in relation to differences in the population of dogs, or due to differences in anti-rabies dog vaccination coverage. Households in these districts, except Debark, keep relatively large number of livestock It is clear that high population of dogs creat opportunity for rabies transmission [[41], [42], [43], [44]].

It is not surprising to find higher odds of rabies exposure in households who owned dogs in families. The presence of dogs in a household increase contact between potentially infected dog(s) and one or more of family memebres compared to housuseholds the did not owned dogs. For instance, a study by Chikanya et al. ( 2021) indicated that dog owners were 10 times more likely to contract rabies compared to non-dog owners. Related to this finding, Seligsohn et al. [45] and Voupawoe et al. [46] noted that family dog(s) were responsible for higher number of rabies exposure in children. Households with large family size were found to have higher odds of rabies exposure It is not surprising as lager family size increase dog-human contact which can increase exposure to rabies. Larger families often have more individuals interacting with pets or animals, which can increase the likelihood of bites from potentially rabid animals. This is particularly true if pets are not properly vaccinated or if there is a higher presence of stray animals in the vicinity [47].

Limitation of the study

While the study effectively outlines its methodology, including sampling procedure and statistical analysis as well as justify the relationship of the risk factors and bite exposures. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged to enhance transparency and reproducibility. The lack of questionnaire validation raises concerns about data reliability, as details regarding pilot testing, expert validation, or reliability assessments such as Cronbach's alpha were not provided. The study relies on respondents recalling bite exposure incidents over the past two years, which may be subject to recall bias. People may not accurately remember the details of past events, leading to underreporting or misreporting of bite incidents. The study was the cross-sectional design of the study only provides a snapshot of bite exposure and associated factors at a single point in time. It cannot establish temporal relationships or causality between risk factors and bite exposure. Addressing these limitations in future research will strengthen the accuracy, reliability, and credibility of the findings, ensuring better methodological rigor and reproducibility.

Conclusion and recommendation

The incidence of exposure to hunman rabies is within the range of incidence of rabies exposure in Ethiopia that had been reported previously. We identified few factors associated to rabies exposure, such as having a larger family size and owning a dog. Majority of respondents (93 %–99.7 %) knew the existence of rabies, its zoonotic risk and ways of transmission. However, only a very small percentage of them reported that exposed victims in their family mamebers took PEP. Therefore use of PEP should be promoted in the study population in different ways such as increasing the availablity, accessibility and affordability of post exposure rabies vaccine.
