Nonmonotonic rate-dependent adhesion of hydrogels
Authors: Dongjing He, Jiahe Huang, Yuhang Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis work reveals that hydrogel adhesion does not always increase with separation rate, as traditionally observed, but instead follows a nonmonotonic dependence governed by competing bond-formation kinetics and time-dependent bond relaxation. By combining experiments, imaging, and theory, we provide a predictive framework that explains when and why adhesion strengthens or weakens under different loading rates. This finding reshapes the fundamental understanding of soft-material interfaces and has broad implications for systems that rely on dynamic, reversible adhesion including biological tissues, drug-delivery materials, and soft robotics. The ability to accurately predict rate-dependent adhesion opens broad avenues for designing adaptive and multifunctional hydrogel-based interfaces.

Hydrogels are crosslinked polymer networks swollen in water or aqueous solutions. They are important components in both biological systems and engineering applications. Many biological components share structural and compositional features with hydrogels, from microscopic cells and extracellular matrices (1–3) to macroscopic soft tissues, such as cartilage (4, 5), cornea (6), skin (7, 8), brain (9, 10), cervix (11), etc. In engineering, the stimuli-responsive properties and biocompatibility of hydrogels have enabled broad applications in drug delivery (12–15), tissue engineering (16–18), artificial organs (19, 20), soft sensors and actuators (21, 22), microfluidic devices (23), wearable electronics (24, 25), and beyond. In both biological and technological contexts, adhesion is critical. At the microscopic level, the dynamic bonding and detachment of cell adhesion molecules govern the cell–cell and cell–extracellular matrix communication. At the tissue level, adhesion serves to maintain tissue integrity (26) and enable essential biological functions (27, 28). In engineering applications, hydrogels adhere to biological or synthetic substrates to enable functions, such as on-site drug delivery (29), tissue repair (30), and adhesive implant (31). Understanding and quantifying hydrogel adhesion are thus vital for both fundamental biology and the design of functional materials.

Although extensive work has been devoted to understanding hydrogel adhesion, current knowledge remains fragmented and incomplete, particularly regarding how adhesion evolves under dynamic loading. When a hydrogel contacts a solid surface, interfacial bonding can arise from a variety of surface interactions including van der Waals forces, hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, dynamic covalent bonds, and physical entanglements that form and break with their own intrinsic kinetic time scales (32–39). At the same time, the structural characteristics of the polymer network, such as chain length and density, introduce characteristic length scales that further influence interfacial mechanics (40–44). As a result, hydrogel adhesion is inherently governed by the interplay of both time-dependent bond kinetics and deformation length scales, implying that adhesion should vary strongly with the rate at which the interface is separated. However, despite this expectation, rate-dependent adhesion of hydrogels remains poorly understood. Existing studies report rate-enhanced adhesion in selected systems and over limited rate ranges (45–47), but they do not elucidate how adhesion evolves across multiple orders of magnitude in separation rate or how competing kinetic processes interact at the interface. As a result, a comprehensive mechanistic framework for rate-dependent hydrogel adhesion is still lacking.

To address this gap, we systematically characterized the rate-dependent adhesion of hydrogels using atomic force microscopy-based (AFM-based) indentation experiments spanning six orders of magnitude in retraction rate and performed under varying indentation depths and contact times. These measurements reveal a previously unreported nonmonotonic dependence of adhesion on retraction rate, arising from the competition between interfacial bond stress relaxation and time-dependent bond formation. To quantitatively interpret this behavior, we developed a theoretical framework that couples interfacial bonding kinetics with the elastic deformation of the hydrogel network. The model captures the full experimental trend and accurately predicts the evolution of the contact radius, which we independently validated using confocal microscopy integrated with the AFM. Together, the experiments and theory establish a unified mechanistic picture for hydrogel rate-dependent adhesion and provide design principles for dynamic and adaptive soft interfaces.

Results

Nonmonotonic Rate-Dependent Pull-Off Force Revealed by Indentation Tests.

To probe how adhesion of hydrogels depends on separation rate, we performed AFM-based indentation tests on the widely used polyacrylamide hydrogels using a 15-μm radius polystyrene sphere as the indenter (Fig. 1 A and B), with a wide range of pulling speeds. The adhesion between the hydrogel and the indenter in this study arises from nonspecific interactions, primarily including the van der Waals forces and hydrophobic effects between the hydrogel polymer chains and the polystyrene chains (48, 49). Such nonspecific, rate-dependent adhesion is broadly relevant to biological and biomedical systems. For example, metastatic cancer cells often rely on nonspecific interactions when specific adhesion pathways are downregulated, and their attachment under physiological flow is governed by contact time and separation rate (50). Hydrogel microparticles used for mucosal drug delivery also depend on nonspecific forces to achieve sustained adhesion against rapid clearance, making their performance inherently rate-sensitive (51). Similarly, microrobotic manipulators that grip soft biological targets typically rely on reversible nonspecific interactions, where controlled adhesion and release are achieved by tuning contact duration and retraction rate (52). Thus, the mechanisms identified here extend directly to diverse applications where dynamic, nonspecific adhesion plays a functional role.

As shown in Fig. 1C, the indenter was driven onto the hydrogel at a rate of 50 μm/s, to a prescribed indentation depth, held for a controlled period of time, and then retracted at rates spanning six orders of magnitude. During loading and holding, the reaction force on the indenter rose and then relaxed due to the poroelastic response of the hydrogel until reaching an equilibrium. Upon retraction, the force transitioned into tension due to adhesion, and the maximum tensile force was taken as the pull-off force (Fig. 1D). We systematically varied the indentation depth (h0 = 1, 2, 3 μm), holding time (t0 = 10, 100, 400 s), and retraction rate (1 nm/s to 10 μm/s), and extracted the pull-off force for each condition. During tests, both the indenter and the hydrogels were completely submerged in water.

Fig. 1 E and F presents the pull-off force as a function of retraction rate for varying indentation depth and holding time. It is shown that the hydrogel adhesion is strongly size-, time-, and rate-dependent. The most striking observation is the nonmonotonic dependence on retraction rates. Instead of increasing monotonically as observed in most previous studies, the pull-off force first decreases and then increases. For larger indentation depth, the pull-off force is larger. It is worth noting that based on the classic quasi-static contact adhesion theories, including the Derjaguin–Muller–Toporov (DMT) model (53), the Johnson–Kendall–Roberts (JKR) model (54), and the Maugis-Dugdale model (55), the predicted pull-off force should not change with indentation depth. It is also observed that at high retraction rates, the pull-off force increases with holding time, reflecting continued bond formation during sustained contact, while at slower retraction rates, the curves for different holding times congregate. Similar characteristics are also observed in polyacrylamide hydrogels of different compositions; see SI Appendix, Fig. S6 and section S5 for details.

Across all loading conditions, the pull-off force decreases and then increases with retraction rate, producing a distinct nonmonotonic behavior. In literature, the nonmonotonic rate-dependent adhesion has been reported in other geometries and when coupled with other physics, such as adhesive rheology or stick–slip effects in peeling tests (56–58), or bulk viscoelastic dissipation in double cantilever beams (59). However, the dissipation mechanisms and boundary value problems in these systems differ fundamentally from this study. Within the broad literature on indentation adhesion, rate-dependent adhesion is typically monotonic, with adhesive force increasing with separation rate. Fast separation leaves insufficient time for the bond to relax and therefore produces higher forces, whereas slow separation allows full relaxation and leads to a smaller force and a plateau at an extremely slow rate. However, our measurements in this work contradict this expectation. At slow retraction, the pull-off force rises again rather than keeps decreasing or reaches a plateau value. It indicates the presence of an additional time-dependent mechanism that competes with the time scale associated with the stress relaxation of the bonds.

Discussion

Here, we propose that the additional time-dependent mechanism underlying the observed behavior is the time-dependent association of interfacial bonds, whereby the number of attached bonds increases with the duration of contact. Together with the time-dependent stress relaxation of these bonds, these competing mechanisms generate the nonmonotonic rate dependence. During an indentation test, the total time available for bond formation includes loading, holding, and retraction; because loading is rapid, which takes only 0.02 to 0.06 s, bond accumulation is governed primarily by the holding period and the time during which the interface remains closed during retraction. As illustrated in Fig. 2, bonds accumulate within the contact region during the hold, and, during retraction, separation initiates at the periphery while the center part of the interface continues to form new bonds. At slow retraction rates, the interface remains closed for a longer period, enabling greater bond buildup and producing larger pull-off forces. As the retraction rate increases, this contact time shortens, reducing bond formation and lowering adhesion. At sufficiently fast retraction, the bonds cannot fully relax, causing the pull-off force to rise again, thereby producing the nonmonotonic trend. Consistent with this mechanism, Fig. 1F shows that at high retraction rates, the pull-off force increases with holding time, reflecting greater bond accumulation during longer contact.

Illustration of bond association and dissociation during the indentation test. (A) Loading: The indenter is brought into contact with the hydrogel surface. (B) Holding: Adhesive bonds accumulate within the contact region. (C) Early retraction: Adhesive bonds continue to form within the contact region, while a cohesive zone develops at the periphery. (D) Late retraction: The contact region diminishes, and the cohesive zone gradually shrinks until the adhesive bonds fully dissociate.

Theoretical Modeling Considering Kinetic Bonding Theory.

Based on the proposed mechanism, we formulate a theoretical model for the indentation test and solve the associated boundary value problem to quantify the rate-dependent pull-off force. The model couples linear elasticity of the hydrogel, interfacial cohesive stresses, and the kinetics of bond association and dissociation. In this formulation, we neglect the time-dependent bulk behavior of the polymer network (i.e., poroelasticity). This simplification is justified on both conceptual and quantitative grounds. Bulk poroelastic relaxation primarily contributes to increases in pull-off force at higher retraction speeds; however, the phenomenon central to this study is the increase in pull-off force at lower retraction speeds, which gives rise to the nonmonotonic behavior. Because poroelastic effects operate in the opposite regime, they cannot account for the low-rate strengthening that defines the nonmonotonic behavior reported here. Moreover, including poroelasticity would only slightly adjust the magnitude of the predicted force curve without altering its qualitative form. For the hydrogels examined, the poroelastic relaxation time is less than 2 s. At the low retraction rates where the characteristic rise in pull-off force occurs (<1.5 μm/s; Fig. 1 E and F), the deformation time far exceeds this relaxation time, meaning the network behaves as a fully relaxed elastic solid; hence, poroelasticity does not appreciably influence the observed rate dependence. Because we use the relaxed-state elastic modulus as input, neglecting poroelasticity may lead to a modest underestimation of the pull-off force only at the high retraction rate regime. Additionally, poroelastic relaxation reduces force by less than 30%, which is insufficient to explain the 2 to 3 times change in pull-off force across the tested rate range, which further indicates that poroelasticity is not the origin of the nonmonotonic trend. A detailed analysis of bulk relaxation effects is provided in SI Appendix, section S4. Additionally, this formulation neglects capillary force at the hydrogel surface. Prior studies have shown that for indentation of soft materials in air, the elastocapillary force and liquid meniscus bridging could affect adhesion measurements (60–66). In the experiments of this study, both the spherical indenter and the hydrogel are fully submerged in water, and the hydrogel contains a high water fraction (~96%), such that the polymer chains in both the bulk and at the surface are in a highly hydrated condition. As a result, surface effects are greatly reduced and elastocapillary forces are negligible. Moreover, because the indentation test is conducted underwater, no liquid–air meniscus forms between the two solid surfaces.

Consider a rigid spherical indenter of radius R pressed into a hydrogel modeled as an elastic half-space with shear modulus G and Poisson’s ratio ν, as shown in Fig. 3. We assume small deformation (h0≪R), and linear elasticity applies. To solve the contact adhesion problem, a constitutive description of interfacial adhesion is required. A classic approach is to describe the adhesion through adhesion energy, such as the JKR model (54) and DMT model (53). In contrast, Maugis introduced a cohesive zone description in which the adhesion is governed by a Dugdale traction–separation relation rather than an adhesion energy (55). The Maugis approach gives a more general solution in which JKR and DMT solutions are two extreme limits. In this work, we advance this cohesive zone approach by introducing bonding kinetics, resulting in a rate-dependent traction–separation relation, which is incorporated into the boundary value problem. Here, we list the key equations, and complete formulations can be found in the SI Appendix, section S1.

With the elastic solution to the boundary value problem established, we next specify the cohesive pressure pr,t, which incorporates the kinetics of bond attachment and detachment. As described earlier, the nonmonotonic rate-dependent adhesion of hydrogels arises from two coupled time-dependent mechanisms: the stress relaxation of interfacial bonds and the time-dependent association of bonds at the interface. To capture both effects, we adopt a dynamic bond model, which was originally developed for cell adhesion molecules (69–72), and later applied to synthetic systems exhibiting rate-dependent adhesion and fracture, including PDMS–glass interfaces (73), and silicon/epoxy interface (74). In our hydrogel system, adhesion is mediated by nonspecific interactions, primarily van der Waals forces and hydrophobic association between polymer chains on the hydrogel surface and those on the polystyrene probe (48, 49). Bond formation is controlled by the time required for surface polymer chains to explore the interface and establish attachment sites. These chain-scale motions, together with the intrinsic kinetic bonding reactions, give rise to the time-dependent bond association rate. Because the adhesive contacts are borne by surface polymer chains, the stress relaxation of interfacial bonds reflects the viscous relaxation of those chains under external loading. Thus, in this context, each surface polymer chain can be regarded as an effective adhesive bond, and the dynamic bond model provides a natural framework to describe both its time-dependent association kinetics and its stress relaxation behavior.

In the dynamic bond model, the event of bond attachment is described by the bond attachment probability Qr,t, which describes the probability for the bond at a certain location r to attach at time t. where kon and koff are the bond association and dissociation rates, σbr,t is the stress carried by the attached bond, and σc is the critical cohesive strength of the bond. The probability, Qr,t, varies between 0 (all bonds detached) and 1 (all bonds attached). Initially, when the interface comes into contact and Q=0, the changing rate, ∂Q/∂t, is positive, and Q starts to increase, meaning more bonds start to form. As Q increases, the dissociation term grows correspondingly, slowing down the bond attachment. Eventually, an equilibrium between association and dissociation is reached. The stress on the bond σbr,t modulates this balance: compressive stress σbr,t<0 enhances effective association, whereas tensile stress σbr,t>0 favors dissociation. Eq. 9 shows that cohesive traction depends on both the separation distance and bonding probability. As the interface separates, the separation distance increases, which raises the cohesive tensile stress and accelerates dissociation, reducing Q. At sufficiently large separation, Q→0, and all bonds detach, resulting in complete separation. This formulation, therefore, yields a nonlinear traction–separation behavior. The shape of the traction–separation curve depends on separation speed: at faster pulling speeds, bonds have less time to dissociate, so the cohesive traction is higher at the same separation distance (SI Appendix, section S2). Eqs. 7–9 collectively capture both time-dependent bond formation kinetics and the time-dependent stress-relaxation of the bond. Together, they govern the hydrogel adhesion dynamics.

Quantitative Comparison between Theory and Experiment.

With the theoretical model established, we numerically solved Eqs. 1–9 to compute the reaction force Pt as a function of indentation displacement ht during the holding and retraction period and subsequently determined the pull-off force (SI Appendix, section S1). A representative numerical force curve is shown in Fig. 4A. During retraction, the reaction force gradually decreases and becomes tensile due to adhesion, while the contact radius at gradually decreases. However, as discussed earlier, even when at reaches zero, the interface is not yet fully detached, and the tensile adhesive force continues to rise until the pull-off force is reached, after which, the tensile force decreases and eventually reaches zero. The pull-off force predicted over a wide range of retraction rates is shown in Fig. 4B. The theoretical curve captures the full nonmonotonic, rate-dependent spectrum that arises from the interplay between bond association and dissociation kinetics. At very low rates, pull-off forces converge to the same plateau because all the time-dependent processes have sufficient time to equilibrate, and the system behaves quasi-statically. As the retraction rate increases, initially the bond stiffening arising from reduced time for bond relaxation becomes pronounced, causing the pull-off force to rise. With further increase in retraction rate, however, the shortened contact time suppresses bond formation, and this effect becomes dominant, leading to a decrease in pull-off force. When the retraction rate becomes sufficiently high that the contact time available during retraction is extremely short, the bond stiffening effect again prevails, resulting in a renewed increase in pull-off force. In fact, the experimental results only capture the later portion of the trend, the “down-and-up” transition, but not the initial “plateau-and-up” regime. This limitation arises because the AFM system cannot operate drift-free at extremely slow retraction rates. The slowest feasible rate, 1 nm/s, already requires roughly 1 h of measurement time. Slower than that, the thermal drift becomes influential, preventing us from reliably probing the initial rising branch predicted by the theory.

We then used the theoretical model to extract intrinsic material parameters by fitting the six experimental pull-off curves, including three indentation depths and three holding times, using a single, unified set of adhesion parameters. The bulk properties of the hydrogel were first determined from loading and holding responses (44, 75), yielding a shear modulus G=614 Pa, and Poisson’s ratio ν=0.286 (SI Appendix, section S4). These values were then used as inputs for the adhesion model. The stretching parameters of the adhesive bond, the characteristic stress of the bond σc and the bond stiffness ξ, are primarily determined by the pull-off force spectrum at different indentation depths. From these values, the characteristic bond extension uc=σc/ξ is obtained. The fitted parameters (Table 1) give uc=176 nm. It is much larger than the mesh size of the hydrogel polymer network, which is on the order of tens of nanometers. This result suggests that the adhesive interactions are formed by dangling polymer chains at the hydrogel surface that extend far beyond the mesh size, to the sphere. This interpretation is consistent with previous observations reporting the presence of long dangling chains on gel surfaces (76–78).

Additionally, the characteristic adhesive energy can be calculated as ω=σc2/ξ=0.155 mJ/m2. The energy to break a single adhesive bond is estimated as ω/ρ, where ρ is the bond density. Treating each dangling polymer chain as one adhesive bond and considering it is tethered to a crosslinker near the hydrogel surface, the bond density is approximated as ρ≈1/d2, with d being the network mesh size. Using the affine network model (78, 79), G=kBT/d3, and the measured shear modulus G value, the mesh size is estimated as d≈25 nm at room temperature (kBT=4×10-21J). This yields a single-bond rupture energy of ∼60 kJ/mol, several times larger than typical van der Waals force or hydrophobic interaction energies (∼8 kJ/mol) (80). This discrepancy may indicate that multiple adhesion sites can be formed on one dangling polymer chain.

The kinetic properties of the bond are primarily determined from the pull-off force spectra obtained at different holding times and from the force response in the slow-rate regime. Specifically, the values of kon and koff define the position of the initial rising segment and the location of the local maximum in the pull-off force spectrum. Details on how each adhesion parameter influences the rate-dependent pull-off force curve are provided in SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3 and sections S2 and S3). Experimentally, however, even at the AFM’s slowest accessible unloading velocity, the pull-off force has not yet reached the local maximum. As a result, the obtained kon and koff values represent the largest association and dissociation rates that ensure the theoretical local maximum lies to the left of the experimentally accessible range. The characteristic kinetic time scale, given by 1/(kon+koff) is approximately 5,000 s, indicating that bond association continues to evolve over time spans of at least several thousand seconds, consistent with the observation in other polymeric systems, where adhesion is observed to increase with contact time over tens of minutes to hours (81, 82).

Role of Contact History in Governing Adhesion Dynamics.

With the physical picture established, we next examine how loading conditions, specifically the indentation depth (Fig. 4C) and holding time (Fig. 4D), shape the rate-dependent pull-off force. Classical quasi-static adhesion theories predict no dependence of pull-off force on indentation depth (53–55, 67), whereas our previous experiments revealed a pronounced size dependence in a retraction rate corresponding to the fast regime examined here (43). This kinetic framework developed in this study reconciles these observations by showing that the classic size-independent regime and the recently observed size-dependent regime emerge as opposite limits of a unified time-dependent theory here, which fully captures the behavior across the entire range of retraction rates. At very slow retraction, both bond formation and relaxation equilibrate, and the pull-off forces converge regardless of indentation depth, consistent with the quasi-static prediction. At fast retraction, the behavior reduces to that reported by Lai et al. (43): loading–unloading hysteresis unravels a transition from Hertzian to adhesive response, and the pull-off force increases with indentation depth.

Holding time also strongly influences the rate-dependent pull-off force. At high retraction rates, longer holding times increase the pull-off force because bonds accumulate during the hold. At low rates, however, the contact time during retraction dominates over the holding duration. Consequently, differences in holding time become negligible, and the curves collapse. For instance, at 2 nm/s, the retraction time (~1,000 s) far exceeds 10 to 100 s holding times, resulting in nearly identical pull-off forces. Finally, for the longest holding time tested (400 s), the nonmonotonic dip becomes less pronounced. When the holding time is large, the subsequent unloading phase becomes comparatively short even at moderate retraction rates, shifting the valley to lower rates and reducing the contrast between the peak and valley values. If the holding time were to exceed the intrinsic bonding timescale (~5,000 s), the nonmonotonicity would disappear entirely within the experimental rate window. This behavior is consistent with studies by Chaudhury and coworkers on adhesive fracture between PDMS and glass (73, 83). In their experiments, a PDMS thin film was precoated on the glass surface 3 d before testing, so the bond association process was already complete, and retraction was governed solely by stress-relaxation. Consequently, a monotonic increase in rate-dependent adhesion energy was observed. Accordingly, their dynamic bond model assumes all the available bonds are attached before retraction, predicting a monotonic rate dependence (73), consistent with the results shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2 in our work. This trend further confirms that the nonmonotonic behavior arises from the competition between bond formation and bond relaxation.

Independent Verification through Contact Radius Measurement.

The strong agreement between the measured pull-off forces and the model predictions (Fig. 4 C and D) proves that the kinetic framework accurately captures the nonmonotonic rate dependence. To further and independently validate the model, we measured the evolution of the contact radius during holding and retraction using concurrent confocal imaging through an AFM-confocal integrated system (Fig. 5A). These measurements were performed at an intermediate retraction rate of 10 nm/s, chosen because it lies in the regime where both bond-formation kinetics and bond-stress relaxation contribute appreciably to adhesion, and also because of practical limits of the confocal system. At higher speeds, the indenter moves significantly during a single scan, causing motion blur and rapid changes in contact size; at much slower speeds, the long scanning duration leads to a significant temperature rise, influencing the sample property. At 10 nm/s, the experiment cleanly resolves the contact-area evolution throughout retraction. Binary masks were generated from the raw images to quantitatively determine the contact boundary. The measured contact diameters closely match the theoretical predictions (Fig. 5B), providing independent confirmation of the proposed theory.

From the theoretical predictions on contact radius, the crack velocity, da/dt, can be calculated by the numerical differentiation, atn+1-atn/tn+1-tn, as shown in Fig. 5C. During the early stage of retraction, the crack velocity remains nearly constant, but increases rapidly as retraction proceeds. In this work, the indenter retraction rate is prescribed, and the crack velocity emerges from the solution of the boundary value problem. It is different from some prior studies that assume a constant crack velocity under constant retraction (73, 83), enabling a JKR-based formulation with a velocity-dependent adhesion energy. As indicated in Fig. 5C, for the situation of this study, this assumption is only valid during early retraction but breaks down at later stages. By simulating the full indentation-retraction process, crack velocity variations are captured without additional assumptions.

These measurements also highlight why classical adhesion theories cannot describe the intermediate-rate regime. Hertz and DMT theories, which assume nonadhesive or long-range weak cohesive interactions (53, 68), significantly underpredict the contact radius. JKR theory, widely used for soft materials, overestimates the contact radius during retraction (Fig. 5D). Lai et al. (2019) captured the transition from Hertzian to adhesive contact near the holding configuration (43) but still significantly deviated from the measurement result (Fig. 5D). Only the kinetic model formulated in this work reproduces the full contact-radius evolution, demonstrating that time-dependent bond formation and rupture are essential for describing hydrogel adhesion across a wide range of retraction rates.

Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated that hydrogel adhesion exhibits a previously unrecognized nonmonotonic dependence on retraction rate, arising from the competition between time-dependent bond formation and stress relaxation at the interface. Through AFM indentation experiments, confocal imaging, and a quantitative kinetic model, we established a unified framework that accurately captures adhesion behavior across multiple loading conditions and timescales. This mechanistic understanding clarifies discrepancies in prior hydrogel adhesion measurements and provides design principles for engineering adaptive, rate-responsive interfaces in biological, biomedical, and soft robotic systems. The results highlight the importance of kinetic processes in soft-material adhesion and open broad avenues for tuning interfacial performance through rate control.

Materials and Methods

Fluorescent Polyacrylamide Hydrogel.

Fluorescent polyacrylamide hydrogel is used as the model material in this study. To incorporate fluorescent molecules into the main polymer chains, allyl fluorescein is first synthesized. Fluorescein (3.32 g, 0.01 mol) is dissolved in anhydrous dichloromethane (DCM, 75 mL) in a 250 mL round-bottom flask, followed by the addition of potassium carbonate (K2CO3, 2.76 g, 0.02 mol). The flask is cooled in an ice bath to approximately 0 °C. A solution of allyl bromide (1.7 mL, 0.02 mol) in anhydrous DCM (10 mL) is then added dropwise. The reaction mixture is stirred at 25 °C for 12 h, after which it is poured into saturated brine, and the aqueous layer is extracted with diethyl ether (100 mL). The combined organic extracts are dried over magnesium sulfate (MgSO4), concentrated, and purified by flash chromatography on silica gel using hexane/ethyl acetate as the eluent, yielding allyl fluorescein as a yellow solid (~30% yield).

To prepare the fluorescent hydrogel, 1.25 mg of allyl fluorescein is dissolved in 2 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Subsequently, 1.25 mL of a 40 wt% acrylamide stock solution, 0.5 mL of a 2 wt% N,N′-methylenebis(acrylamide) solution, and 1.25 mL of deionized (DI) water are added to form a precursor mixture with final concentrations of 10 wt% acrylamide and 0.2 wt% N,N′-methylenebis(acrylamide). Ammonium persulfate (APS) is then added to achieve a final initiator concentration of 0.5 wt%. The precursor solution is injected into a mold consisting of two glass slides separated by a 160 µm spacer and cured at 80 °C for 6 h. After curing, the hydrogel is immersed in DI water for 1 wk to reach its fully swollen state prior to indentation testing (Fig. 1B).

AFM-Based Indentation Test.

Indentation adhesion tests were conducted using an Asylum MFP-3D Bio AFM. Hydrogel samples were removed from DI water and glued onto circular glass coverslips (35 mm diameter, 150 μm thickness), which were mounted in a closed fluid cell. An AFM cantilever with a spring constant of 8 N/m was used, with a 15 μm-radius fluorescent polystyrene sphere (Spherotech, Lake Forest, IL) attached to the tip (Fig. 1A). The fluid cell was filled with DI water, and the AFM probe was fully submerged, ensuring that both the indenter and hydrogel remained underwater throughout all tests. Indentation experiments were performed using the “indentation” mode in the Asylum AFM software, which controlled indenter displacement via a feedback loop. The reaction force and indenter displacement were recorded as functions of time. For each set of loading parameters (indentation depth, holding time, and retraction rate), measurements were repeated at least three times at different locations on the hydrogel surface.

Confocal Microscope Measurement of Contact Diameter.

During indentation, the contact radius was measured using a Nikon A1R-HD25 confocal microscopy system. Confocal scanning was performed at 488 nm for the fluorescent hydrogel and 640 nm for the fluorescent sphere. Measurements were conducted for indentation tests with a 400 s holding time and a 10 nm/s retraction rate. Throughout the holding period and retraction, z-axis cross-sectional images were acquired every 50 s. The images were processed in MATLAB using Otsu’s thresholding method to generate binary masks and identify the contact region (Fig. 5A). The spatial resolution of the confocal images was approximately 250 nm.
