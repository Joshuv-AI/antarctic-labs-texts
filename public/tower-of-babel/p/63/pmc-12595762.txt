Double-filtration plasmapheresis as an adjunct to therapy for severe early-onset maternal erythrocyte alloimmunization
Authors: Yan Lei, Yuling Liang, Xiulan Hao, Wenjian Zhu, Xuemei Zhang, Zhihua Zheng, Xiaohua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundErythrocyte alloimmunization is an important cause of hemolytic disease in fetuses and newborn (HDFN). Intrauterine blood transfusion (IUT) is the standard treatment for anemic fetuses, but there are adverse reactions, especially before 20 weeks of gestation. Here, we used double filtration plasmapheresis (DFPP) as an adjunct therapy to prolong the time to receive IUT safely. The aim of this study was to determine the safety and efficacy of DFPP for early-onset HDFN during pregnancy.MethodWe recruited pregnant women with a high risk of early-onset HDFN. The DFPP procedure was performed by a plasmapheresis machine using a plasma separator and a plasma component separator. A 5% albumin solution served as the replacement fluid. A central venous catheter was used for DFPP. The blood flow rate was 120–150 ml/min, and the plasma separation rate was 1200–1500 ml/h. The rates of discarding the plasma and replenishing the plasma were 150–200 ml/h. The plasma volume to treat each patient was calculated via the Kaplan formula. Anticoagulation was achieved with low-molecular-weight heparin. The treatment goal was to reduce the ratio of antibodies to less than 1:32 or to maintain fetal MCA-PSV < 1.5 MoM, thereby prolonging pregnancy to allow safe IUT when necessary. Epidemiological data and hematological parameters as well as side effects were evaluated before and after DFPP.ResultsA total of 6 patients, including 5 with Rh and 1 with MN-associated maternal–fetal hemolysis, were analyzed. DFPP was initiated at a median gestational age of 14 weeks (range: 12–18). Patients received a median of 8 sessions (range: 3–9) over 4 weeks (range: 2– 5), with 3000 mL (range: 2000–3250) of plasma processed per session. A total of 40 procedures were performed. Anti-D/M antibody titers decreased by a median of 50% after each session . Other proteins, such as albumins, globulins, and complement and coagulation factors, also decreased significantly after DFPP treatment, whereas white blood cells, neutrophil and hemoglobin increased after DFPP treatment. However, coagulation factor levels returned to normal levels the next day. No obvious side effects were observed during the entire treatment period. Five women successfully prolonged pregnancy until 20 weeks of gestation and underwent IUTs, whereas one woman failed at 15 weeks of gestation due to severe fetal anemia and underwent induced labor. The cohort received a total of 26 transfusions, with a median of 5 procedures (range: 1–6) per patient over a median treatment duration of 9.5 weeks (range: 1–14).ConclusionsThese favorable results indicate that DFPP is an effective and safe rescue therapy for pregnant women with maternal incompatible hemolytic anemia. However, more clinical trials with larger sample sizes, long-term follow-up, and cost‒benefit analyses are needed to better evaluate its application value and prospects.Keywords: Double filtration plasmapheresis (DFPP), Erythrocyte alloimmunization, Hemolytic disease in the fetus and newborn(HDFN), Intrauterine blood transfusion (IUT), Alloantibodies

Introduction

Severe HDFN is caused by the incompatibility of maternal-fetal erythrocyte antigens. This condition is mediated by transplacental passage of maternal IgG alloantibodies that specifically target fetal red blood cells, ultimately inducing hemolytic anemia through antibody-dependent cellular destruction. The early onset of severe HDFNs, which is defined as occurring before 24 weeks of gestation, significantly increases the risk of hydrops fetalis or perinatal death [1]. Hydrops fetalis is a fetal disorder involving either extensive subcutaneous edema (>0.5 mm skin thickness) or fluid accumulation in two or more serous cavities (e.g., ascites, pericardial/pleural effusions) detected through prenatal ultrasound [2]‌. In fetuses without hydrops, anemia can be detected by the middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity (MCA-PSV) via Doppler ultrasound, which has high sensitivity and specificity. A MCA-PSV greater than 1.5 multiples of the median (MoM) indicated severe fetal anemia and the need for intervention [3].

In China, anti-D antibodies in the Rh system are the most common cause of HDFNs (67.2%), followed by anti-M antibodies in the MNS system (14.8%) [4]. Although the routine use of anti-RhD immunoglobulins has significantly reduced RhD alloimmunization, HDFN persist due to the unavailability of anti-RhD prophylaxis in remote areas and, more critically, the current lack of approved prophylactic treatments for sensitization against non-RhD antibodies, such as anti-M, anti-Kell, or anti-E [5].

The standard treatment for HDFN is intrauterine transfusion (IUT), a highly specialized procedure that carries a risk of fetal death, requiring fetal medicine specialists with advanced interventional ultrasound expertise to avoid iatrogenic complications. The risks are highest when fetal anemia occurs before 20 weeks of gestation [6, 7]. Therefore, to improve fetal outcomes, delaying the first IUT as late as possible during pregnancy is best. Cases have reported that intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) with or without plasma exchange in early pregnancy can delay the need for IUT [8–10]. A recent study reported that nipocalimab, an anti-neonatal Fc receptor antibody that binds to FcRn, can reduce or block the passage of antibodies from the mother to the fetus, potentially delaying severe fetal anemia or IUT in high-risk early-onset HDFN pregnancies [1]. However, severe adverse events, including fetal growth restriction, premature birth, stillbirth and infection, occurred in 46% of the maternal participants [1]. Its efficacy and safety remain under evaluation in clinical trials. Despite advances in fetal medicine, standardized evidence-based guidelines for managing severe early-onset HDFN are still lacking.

The removal of maternal red blood cell antibodies can reduce the transfer of antibodies through the placenta to the fetal blood circulation, thereby reducing fetal hemolysis. Compared with plasma exchange, DFPP is a simple and easy therapeutic intervention that provides more selective removal of macromolecules through an additional filter and requires less replacement fluid. In our previous study, DFPP was successfully employed to remove pathological antibodies from patients with severe early-onset HDFN [11]. However, experience with DFPP for severe maternal erythrocyte alloimmunization is rare and is based mainly on case reports [11–13]. In the present study, the safety and efficacy of DFPP for delaying the need for IUTs in pregnant women with severe early-onset HDFN were further evaluated.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study with prospective follow-up recruited pregnant women with a high risk of HDFN treated at the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between August 2022 and May 2025. Patients with a prior history of HDFN complicated with significantly elevated maternal antibody titers against erythrocyte antigens prior to 20 weeks of gestation, indicative of a high risk of early-onset fetal anemia, were included, whereas those who underwent intravenous immunogloblin and plasma exchange before IUT during the current pregnancy were excluded from the study to specifically isolate the effect of DFPP. All participants included in this study received DFPP treatment after evaluation by a multidisciplinary team (MDT), with treatment decisions made individually based on clinical urgency, antibody titers, and gestational age rather than a prospective allocation protocol. The number of DFPP sessions for each participant was approximately twice a week or every other day, depending mainly on the antibody titer or the fetal anemia status monitored via MCA-PSV (MoM) via ultrasound Doppler. DFPP would be discontinued if the antibody titer was stably reduced to ≤ 1:32, or when fetal MCA-PSV levels remained consistently below 1.5 MoM. The final decision to pause treatment was made by the MDT following confirmation that these parameters had been met, indicating that therapy could be safely suspended. The primary endpoint was the effectiveness of DFPP, defined as the decrease in anti-D/M antibodies to less than 1:32 or the delay of IUT until after 20 weeks of gestation. The secondary endpoint was the effects of DFPP on biochemical and hematological parameters as well as adverse events during DFPP treatment.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before study inclusion.

DFPP protocols

DFPP was performed via a plasmapheresis device (Multifiltrate CiCa®, Fresenius Medical Care) with a primary plasma separator (P2, Fresenius Medical Care) and a secondary plasma component filter (Cascadeflo EC-30 W, Asahi Kasei Medical), which removes pathogenic immunoglobulins (including IgG, IgA, IgM, and IgE) through size-selective permeability, with a documented sieving coefficient of ~ 75% for IgG. ‌A double-lumen catheter was placed into the internal jugular vein from the right neck for vascular access. 5% albumin served as the replacement fluid. Due to effective clearance of coagulation factors by the secondary filter, fibrinogen levels were monitored before and after each procedure. Prophylactic fibrinogen supplementation (2 g) was administered immediately if levels dropped below 100 mg/dL. Plasma components and blood cell counts were measured before and after DFPP treatment. The procedural specifications included the following:

Blood flow rate‌: 120–150 mL/min.

‌Plasma separation rate‌ 1200–1500 mL/h.

Plasma discard rate: 150–200 ml/h.

Replacement solution: 400–500 mL of 5% albumin.

‌Anticoagulation‌: Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) 50 IU/kg.

Target processed plasma volume‌: 1–1.5 times the estimated patient plasma volume, which was calculated using the Kaplan formula: EPV (L) = 0.065 × body weight (kg) × (1 − haematocrit).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. The quantitative data are presented as the means ± standard deviations. Pre-DFPP vs. post-DFPP comparisons were assessed through paired Student’s t tests. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of maternal participants

A total of 6 patients, including 5 Rh- and 1 MN-associated maternal–fetal HDFN, were included in the study. The total number of DFPP sessions was 40, and a median of 3000 mL (range: 2000–3250) of plasma was processed per session. The median gestational age at DFPP initiation was 14 weeks (range: 12–18), with a median of 8 sessions (range: 3–9) administered over 4 weeks (range: 2–5). Effects of DFPP

Anti-D/M antibody titers decreased tremendously after DFPP therapy, with a median reduction of 50% (range: 0–75%) per session for anti-D antibodies and 50% (range: 0–50%) for the anti-M antibody (Fig. 1). Five (83.3%) women successfully prolonged pregnancy until 20 weeks of gestation and subsequently underwent IUTs. One patient (16.7%) required emergent IUT at 15 gestational weeks due to severe fetal anemia diagnosed by ultrasound with MCA-PSV 2.4 MoM and hydrops. Safety of DFPP

No serious complications, including catheter-related infection, hypotension, anaphylaxis or bleeding, were found during DFPP treatment. Maternal blood analysis revealed a significant reduction in the levels of albumin, globulin, complement proteins, and coagulation factors after DFPP treatment compared with those before DFPP. The number of blood cells, including white blood cells, neutrophil and hemoglobin, slightly increased after DFPP treatment (p < 0.05; Fig. 2). Four (67.7%) women developed acute fibrinogen reduction below 100 mg/dl after DFPP and received immediate prophylactic administration of 2 g fibrinogen to prevent bleeding. The other depleted proteins and blood cells recovered to baseline the next day without intervention.

Outcomes of subsequent IUTs

Following the final DFPP session, the first IUT was performed after a median interval of 3 weeks (range: 0–7). Rebound in antibody titers was observed prior to IUT, with a median increase of 2-fold (range: 0.5–4) for anti-D and 16-fold for anti-M compared to post-DFPP levels. The final decision to initiate IUT was made by the MDT based on a comprehensive assessment including rebound antibody titers, fetal anemia status (e.g., MCA-PSV > 1.5 MoM, hydrops fetalis), and gestational viability for safe transfusion(> 20 weeks). Subsequently, all pregnant women underwent IUTs after DFPP sessions, with a median transfusion initiation gestational age of 21 weeks (range: 15–26), 5 total transfusions (range: 1–6), and a treatment duration of 9.5 weeks (range: 1–14). Two cases (33.3%) of acute fetal distress occurred during intrauterine transfusions: one at 15 gestational weeks resulted in intrauterine fetal demise despite emergent resuscitation, whereas the other at 30 gestational weeks resulted in prompt cesarean delivery and achieved neonatal survival.

Delivery outcomes of live-born infants

Five live-born neonates were delivered at a median gestational age of 35 weeks (range: 30–38), with a median birth weight of 2.28 kg (range: 1.55–2.83) and a median hemoglobin level of 122 g/L (range: 90–138). The Apgar score at 5/10 minutes was 10 for four infants and 4 for one case. Intensive care was required for a median duration of 10 days (range: 5–46 days).

Discussion

Plasma exchange, a standard therapy for refractory immune-related diseases, faces limitations, including nonselective plasma discard requiring high-volume replacement fluids. The process carries risks of allergic reactions and viral transmission from blood products. The emerging demand for selective pathogen removal in targeted therapies drove the development of double-filtration plasmapheresis (DFPP), a semiselective modality preserving beneficial plasma components while eliminating pathogenic substances [14]. Our DFPP protocol involves the processing of a median of 3000 mL of plasma per session while requiring only 400–500 mL of 5% albumin for replenishment, without requiring fresh-frozen plasma. This approach significantly reduces the demand for specialized blood products that are often challenging to procure clinically, effectively addressing supply constraints in plasma exchange. Furthermore, avoiding the use of RhD-positive plasma as a replacement fluid may reduce the risk of delayed hemolysis in the fetus, as even minute residual RhD-positive red blood cells in plasma products could potentially exacerbate maternal alloimmunization and fetal hemolysis [15].

The results showed that DFPP treatment significantly reduced anti-D/M antibody titers, which was associated with improved clinical outcomes. Overall, in our cohort of 6 patients, 83.3% of responsive patients achieved pregnancy progression and delayed IUTs beyond 20 weeks of gestation. These results suggest that antibody titer reduction alleviates fetal hemolysis and delays the need for IUTs.

Unfortunately, case 2 failed to sustain the delivery time. This case demonstrates the challenge of managing severe early-onset HDFN associated with extremely high RhD antibody drops detected at week 12 of gestation, which may reflect cumulative antibody production that begins weeks before conception. Although DFPP reduced the antibody titer by 87.5% within two weeks, the patient developed severe fetal anemia at 15 weeks of gestation and required an emergency IUT, resulting in fetal distress and subsequent death. These results suggest that a significant increase in pathologic antibody levels combined with abnormal MCA-PSV levels in early pregnancy may be a prognostic marker for refractory early-onset HDFN. Although DFPP showed significant efficacy in reducing the antibody burden and delaying the progression of hemolysis, its therapeutic window appears to be limited to the prehemolytic phase. The temporal relationship between the initiation of DFPP and the onset of fetal anemia emphasizes that even successful antibody reduction may not prevent disease progression once the hemolysis process is established. Notably, this case confirms existing evidence that there is a high risk of fetal death (8%−24%) associated with IUT before 20 weeks of gestation [6, 16]. These findings emphasize the extreme importance of pre-pregnancy antibody monitoring and starting DFPP immediately after confirmation in high-risk patients to postpone initial IUT until after 20 weeks of gestation.

A previous study revealed that DFPP in immune-mediated nephropathies induces transient depletion of plasma components, accompanied by a 12.5% incidence of hypotension and a 20% incidence of spontaneous bleeding complications [17]. Notably, all bleeding events correlated with critically reduced post-DFPP fibrinogen levels (< 50 mg/dl). Consistent with previous studies [17, 18], the main adverse event in our prospective cohort was transient hypofibrinogenemia, and DFPP similarly reduced the concentrations of plasma protein, but no hypotension or bleeding complications were observed. This improved safety profile may be attributed to rigorous coagulation monitoring and proactive fibrinogen supplementation implemented in our protocol. These findings suggest that protocol-optimized DFPP, which involves real-time fibrinogen monitoring and targeted supplementation, achieves enhanced clinical tolerability in high-risk obstetric populations. Fibrinogen is a glycoprotein coagulation factor with a molecular weight of 320 kDa [19], and its large molecular weight makes it easily removed in large quantities during the DFPP process. Therefore, if DFPP is frequently performed in the short term, monitoring fibrinogen may be recommended to avoid the risk of bleeding. In our preliminary report, the decrease in coagulation factor levels returned to the previous level the next day [11]. However, whether prophylactic coagulation factor supplementation is effective still requires further investigation in the future.

However, our study has several limitations. The sample size is relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our results. Additionally, we lack long-term follow-up data to assess the sustained effects of DFPP. Future studies with larger sample sizes, long-term follow-up, and cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to better assess the application value and prospects of this treatment method.

In conclusion, early intervention in HDFN is the key to improving perinatal outcomes. This study pioneers the use of DFPP as a salvage therapy for severe early-onset HDFN when traditional treatment is unavailable before 20 gestational weeks, creating opportunities for subsequent IUT. The DFPP-IUT bridging protocol is a novel paradigm for early/mid-trimester pregnancy management.
