Analysis of risk factors for perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing spinal trauma surgery
Authors: Wu Ruimin, Li Yaqiu, Dilinuer Tusentuheti, Yang Sifan, Wu Ruitian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the independent risk factors for perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing spinal trauma surgery, and to provide evidence-based support for developing targeted preventive strategies.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted, including 222 patients who underwent spinal trauma surgery at a single hospital between January 2022 and December 2024. Patients were divided into a hypothermia group (n = 98) and a non-hypothermia group (n = 124) based on whether perioperative hypothermia (core body temperature < 36.0 °C) occurred. Data on patient demographics, clinical characteristics, surgical parameters, and temperature management measures were collected. Univariate analysis was first performed to compare differences between the groups. Variables with statistical significance in the univariate analysis were then included in a multivariate Logistic regression model (forward stepwise method) to identify independent risk factors. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to test the robustness of the results.ResultsMultivariate Logistic regression analysis showed that age ≥ 60 years (OR = 5.00, 95% CI: 2.24–11.16, P < 0.001), BMI ≤ 24 kg/m² (OR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.36–6.52, P = 0.006), operation time ≥ 4 h (OR = 4.37, 95% CI: 1.58–12.09, P = 0.005), intraoperative blood loss ≥ 200 mL (OR = 3.74, 95% CI: 1.45–9.66, P = 0.007), intraoperative fluid infusion ≥ 3500 mL (OR = 5.64, 95% CI: 1.99–15.98, P < 0.001), and the use of physical warming alone (compared to combined physical and pharmacological warming) (OR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.05–4.60, P = 0.036) were independently associated with perioperative hypothermia. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of these associations.ConclusionIn this retrospective cohort, advanced age, low BMI, prolonged operation time, significant intraoperative blood loss and fluid infusion, and the use of single‑method physical warming were independently associated with perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing spinal trauma surgery. These associations do not imply causality due to the observational design and potential residual confounding. Prospective studies, particularly randomized controlled trials where ethically and practically feasible, are needed to confirm causal relationships. Clinicians may use them for risk stratification but should not assume direct protective effects of specific interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-03997-8.

Introduction

Spinal trauma is a common acute and severe condition in spinal surgery, primarily involving spinal fractures and associated soft tissue injuries such as ligaments and muscles [1]. It is characterized by a complex condition with rapid progression, often requiring early surgical intervention to restore spinal stability and neurological function [2]. Perioperative hypothermia is a frequent complication in spinal surgery, with studies reporting an incidence of over 50%, particularly in prolonged and extensive open procedures [3]. The occurrence of hypothermia is influenced by multiple factors, including patient-related aspects such as age, body mass index, and basal metabolic state, as well as iatrogenic factors like operating room ambient temperature, exposed body surface area, anesthesia methods and drug selection, intraoperative fluid management, and irrigation fluid temperature [4–6].

Hypothermia is associated with coagulation dysfunction and increased intraoperative bleeding and transfusion requirements but also delay drug metabolism, prolong anesthesia recovery time, and suppress immune function [7, 8]. These effects elevate the risk of postoperative complications such as infections and cardiovascular events, ultimately impacting the patient’s recovery process and length of hospital stay. Given these adverse effects, identifying modifiable risk factors for perioperative hypothermia is clinically crucial for improving patient outcomes. With the accelerating aging of the population, the proportion of elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery is increasing annually. Due to their lower metabolic rates and diminished thermoregulatory capacity, this group is more susceptible to perioperative hypothermia, posing greater challenges for prognosis management [9].

Although the clinical impact of hypothermia on spinal surgery patients has garnered attention, high-quality evidence regarding its independent risk factors remains insufficient, particularly lacking systematic analyses and predictive models specifically for patients undergoing spinal trauma surgery [10].

Based on previous studies in general surgery and elective spine surgery, several factors have been repeatedly associated with perioperative hypothermia: advanced age impairs thermoregulatory capacity [11]; low body mass index (BMI) reduces thermal insulation [12, 13]; prolonged operation time increases cumulative heat exposure [12, 14]; substantial intraoperative blood loss and large-volume fluid resuscitation directly lower core temperature via cold infusion [12, 15]; and reliance on physical warming alone may be insufficient compared to multimodal strategies [11, 16]. Therefore, we prespecified the following hypothesis: advanced age, low BMI, prolonged operation time, substantial intraoperative blood loss, large-volume fluid infusion, and the use of physical warming alone (compared with combined physical and pharmacological warming) would be independently associated with an increased risk of perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing spinal trauma surgery.

Identifying the key influencing factors for hypothermia in this patient population and establishing a corresponding risk warning system hold significant clinical importance. This study aims to retrospectively collect and statistically analyze data, utilizing logistic regression methods to explore the relevant risk factors for perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing spinal trauma surgery. By identifying modifiable key aspects, it seeks to propose a structured, evidence-based prevention strategy, providing a reference for implementing individualized temperature management in clinical practice and improving perioperative patient outcomes.

Subjects and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Xinjiang 474 Hospital between January 2022 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the use of anonymized historical data. All surgeries were performed in operating rooms maintained at a standard ambient temperature of 21–23 °C, as per institutional protocol. However, actual intraoperative room temperature values were not systematically recorded for each patient due to the retrospective design.

After applying these criteria, a total of 222 patients were included in the final analysis. Thus, all patients included in the final analysis were normothermic at baseline (upon operating room entry).

Anesthesia and monitoring protocol

All patients received balanced anesthesia. Induction was performed with midazolam 0.03–0.05 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.3–0.5 µg/kg, propofol 1.5–2.0 mg/kg, and rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg intravenously. After endotracheal intubation, anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (MAC 1.0–1.3) in a 40% O₂/air mixture, with intermittent boluses of rocuronium and sufentanil as needed. Standard ASA monitoring included electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, non-invasive blood pressure, end-tidal CO₂, and core temperature (nasopharyngeal probe). Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) was performed in all cases using somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP) and motor evoked potentials (MEP) with a Cadwell Cascade Elite system (Cadwell Industries, USA). Depth of anesthesia was guided by bispectral index (BIS) monitoring (Medtronic, USA), targeting a BIS value of 40–60 throughout the procedure.The pharmacological intervention (compound amino acids ± glucose) was chosen to actively increase metabolic heat production via amino acid‑induced thermogenesis and glucose oxidation, thereby counteracting the downward shift of the thermoregulatory set point caused by general anesthesia .

Clinical data collection

Clinical data were collected from electronic medical records by a dedicated research nurse. The following variables were collected for each patient: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), mechanism of injury, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes), ASA grade, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative fluid infusion volume, warming methods (physical warming alone [forced‑air warming blanket or thermal blanket] versus physical warming combined with pharmacological intervention [preoperative infusion of compound amino acids 18AA 0.2–0.3 g/kg given 30 min before surgery, sometimes combined with 5% glucose solution at 37 °C]), use of warmed intravenous fluids (defined as all intravenous fluids and blood products infused through a fluid warming device set to 37 °C). It is important to note that patients in the combined physical+pharmacological warming group uniformly received warmed intravenous fluids (37 °C) as part of the institutional protocol. In contrast, the use of warmed fluids in the physical-only warming group was not mandatory and varied by anesthesia team preference. The variable “use of warmed intravenous fluids” was recorded independently and included in the multivariate regression model to adjust for its potential confounding effect. Preoperative neurological status was assessed by the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) impairment scale. Preoperative paraplegia was defined as ASIA grade A or B (complete injury or incomplete sensory injury) [17–19].

Temperature monitoring and definition of hypothermia were as follows [20, 21]. Core body temperature was continuously monitored using a nasopharyngeal temperature probe (Smiths Medical, UK; model 900/900/900) inserted through the nostril to a depth of 8–10 cm until the tip reached the posterior nasopharynx. The probe was connected to a patient monitor (Philips IntelliVue MX450, Philips Healthcare, USA). Temperature readings were recorded at three time points: (i) upon operating room entry (before anesthesia induction), (ii) 30 min after surgical incision, and (iii) at the end of surgery (5 min before leaving the operating room). These three time points were selected because the most rapid temperature decline occurs within the first hour after induction (redistribution phase), the 30‑minute measurement captures this early drop, and the end‑of‑surgery measurement reflects cumulative heat loss and the effectiveness of warming measures. Continuous monitoring between these points allowed calculation of total hypothermia duration. The same brand and model of infrared tympanic thermometer (Braun ThermoScan PRO 6000, Germany) was used for surface validation when needed. All devices were calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Perioperative hypothermia was defined as a core body temperature < 36.0 °C [20, 21]. It is important to note that the hypothermia outcome was determined solely from the continuous nasopharyngeal temperature readings. The infrared tympanic thermometer was used only for surface validation at operating room entry and exit and was not used to define hypothermia. Hence, measurement bias from combining different thermometry methods is minimal. The duration of hypothermia was defined as the total time (in minutes) from the first recorded moment when core temperature fell below 36.0 °C to the last recorded moment when it returned to ≥ 36.0 °C, extracted from continuous temperature monitoring records [11, 22].

Preoperative paraplegia [23, 24]

Refers to a severe impairment of spinal cord nerve function that existed before the start of surgery due to spinal injury or disease. This study used the ASIA classification standard for objective assessment, defining patients with preoperative neurological function classified as ASIA Grade A (complete injury) or Grade B (incomplete sensory injury) as having “preoperative paraplegia.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. Since several key variables (blood loss, fluid infusion, and operation time) were not normally distributed (P < 0.05), all continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. The distribution of these variables is visualized in Supplementary Figure S1. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages [n(%)] and compared using the χ² test or non‑parametric rank‑sum test. Variables with P < 0.1 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model using the forward stepwise (likelihood ratio) method. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. All tests were two‑sided, and a P‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Two sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) forced entry (Enter) of all variables with P < 0.1 from univariate analysis; (2) re‑running the stepwise model after excluding extreme cases with intraoperative blood loss > 800 mL.Events per variable (EPV) was calculated as the number of hypothermia events divided by the number of candidate predictors. With 98 events and 12 candidate predictors initially considered (age, BMI, male sex, ASA grade, operation time, blood loss, fluid volume, warmed fluids, warming method, hypertension, diabetes, preoperative paraplegia), the EPV was 8.2, which is acceptable. The final model included 6 variables, giving an EPV of 16.3 (≥ 10 recommended). Internal validation was performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism‑adjusted AUC. To address potential overfitting from stepwise selection, we performed ridge regression (L2-penalized logistic regression) as a sensitivity analysis. The penalty parameter λ was selected via 10-fold cross-validation using the glmnet R package, with the minimum cross-validation error (lambda.min) chosen for the final model. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals from the ridge model were compared with those from the stepwise model to assess stability. Cut‑offs for blood loss (≥ 200 mL) and fluid infusion (≥ 3500 mL) were determined by Youden’s index from ROC curves. BMI ≤ 24 kg/m² followed WHO Asian criteria. Operation time ≥ 4 h was supported by spline analysis showing an inflection point.

To further examine the association between operative time and hypothermia across the full range of included surgeries (≥ 1.5 h), we stratified patients into three categories: 1.5–<2.5 h, 2.5–<4 h, and ≥ 4 h. The incidence of hypothermia increased progressively with longer operative time: 18.2% (10/55) in the 1.5–<2.5 h stratum, 35.6% (16/45) in the 2.5–<4 h stratum, and 68.9% (72/122) in the ≥ 4 h stratum (Cochran‑Armitage trend test: Z = 5.82, P < 0.001). Supplementary analyses using continuous predictors with restricted cubic splines confirmed a linear relationship for age and a clear threshold at 4 h for operation time, justifying the dichotomization.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

To control for confounding factors, variables with P < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression model (forward stepwise method). The results (Table 2) indicated that age ≥ 60 years, BMI ≤ 24 kg/m², operation time ≥ 4 h, intraoperative blood loss ≥ 200 mL, intraoperative fluid infusion ≥ 3500 mL, and the use of physical warming alone (compared with combined physical and pharmacological warming) were independent risk factors for perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing spinal surgery. Specifically, patients aged ≥ 60 years had a 5.00‑fold higher risk of hypothermia compared with those aged < 60 years (OR = 5.00, 95% CI: 2.24–11.16, P < 0.001). Patients with BMI ≤ 24 kg/m² had a 2.98‑fold higher risk than those with BMI > 24 kg/m² (OR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.36–6.52, P = 0.006). The risk increased to 4.37‑fold in patients whose operation time was ≥ 4 h (OR = 4.37, 95% CI: 1.58–12.09, P = 0.005). Both intraoperative blood loss ≥ 200 mL (OR = 3.74, 95% CI: 1.45–9.66, P = 0.007) and intraoperative fluid infusion ≥ 3500 mL (OR = 5.64, 95% CI: 1.99–15.98, P < 0.001) were significant risk factors. Compared with patients who received combined physical and pharmacological warming, those who received physical warming alone had 2.20‑fold higher risk of hypothermia (OR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.05‑4.60, P = 0.036), after additionally adjusting for the use of warmed intravenous fluids. The latter variable did not retain statistical significance in the final stepwise model (P = 0.094). The fully adjusted model including both warming‑related variables is provided in Supplementary Table S3. The Hosmer‑Lemeshow goodness‑of‑fit test for the regression model yielded χ² = 6.98, P = 0.538, indicating adequate model fit. Regression diagnostics, including Pearson residuals, leverage (hat values), and Cook’s distance, indicated no influential outliers (all Cook’s D < 0.4). Detailed diagnostic results are provided in the Supplementary Table S5. Although “use of warmed intravenous fluids” was significantly different between groups in univariate analysis (P < 0.001), it did not remain an independent risk factor in the multivariate model (P = 0.092), likely because its effect was mediated through the combined warming protocol.

Sensitivity and interaction analyses

To explore whether fluid infusion mediates the effect of blood loss on hypothermia, we performed a Sobel test; the result was not significant (P = 0.67), suggesting these two variables act independently rather than through a mediation pathway.

To evaluate the robustness of the aforementioned multivariate analysis results, two sensitivity analyses were performed (Table 3). First, the Enter method was used to force all variables with a P < 0.1 in the univariate analysis into the model. Second, after excluding extreme cases with intraoperative blood loss > 800 mL (n = 7), the stepwise regression model was re-run. The results of both sensitivity analyses showed no substantial changes in the odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), or statistical significance of the independent risk factors. For example, the OR for age ≥ 60 years was 5.00 in the main model and 4.82 and 5.14 in the two sensitivity analyses, with overlapping confidence intervals and all remaining highly significant (P < 0.001). This indicates that the key risk factors identified in this study remained stable under different model settings and sample conditions, demonstrating good robustness of the research conclusions. Bootstrap internal validation (1,000 resamples) yielded an optimism‑adjusted AUC of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.80–0.89), confirming model stability and supporting the robustness of the findings.

To further address potential overfitting associated with stepwise model selection, we additionally performed ridge regression, a penalized regression method that shrinks coefficients toward zero to reduce variance. The results were consistent with the stepwise main model, with all risk factors remaining significant and odds ratios showing only minimal shrinkage (Table 3), indicating that our model is robust and not subject to substantial overfitting.

Regression diagnostics further supported model stability: Cook’s distance for all cases was < 0.4, and excluding extreme blood loss (> 800 mL, n = 7) did not materially change the ORs, indicating that the model was not overly influenced by outliers.

Interaction analysis

To explore potential synergistic effects, we tested interaction terms for three physiologically plausible pairs: age (≥ 60 years) × BMI (≤ 24 kg/m²), intraoperative blood loss (≥ 200 mL) × fluid infusion (≥ 3500 mL), and operation time (≥ 4 h) × warming method (physical alone vs. combined physical+pharmacological). Each interaction term was added separately to the main multivariate logistic regression model. The likelihood ratio test was used to compare models with and without the interaction term. After Bonferroni correction for three comparisons (adjusted α = 0.017), none of the interaction terms reached statistical significance: age×BMI (OR for interaction = 1.14, 95% CI: 0.68–1.92, P = 0.23), blood loss×fluid infusion (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.59–2.11, P = 0.41), operation time×warming method (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 0.71–1.85, P = 0.18).

However, the confidence intervals for all interaction terms were wide and crossed unity, suggesting that our study may have limited statistical power to detect modest interaction effects. We therefore cannot definitively conclude that these effects are strictly additive; the absence of statistically significant interactions may reflect insufficient power rather than a true absence of effect modification. The full interaction results, including likelihood ratio test statistics, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Discussion

This study, through multivariate logistic regression analysis, identified six independent risk factors for perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing spinal trauma surgery. Ranked by effect size, they are: intraoperative fluid infusion ≥ 3500 mL (OR = 5.64, 95% CI: 1.99–15.98), age ≥ 60 years (OR = 5.00, 95% CI: 2.24–11.16), operation time ≥ 4 h (OR = 4.37, 95% CI: 1.58–12.09), intraoperative blood loss ≥ 200 mL (OR = 3.74, 95% CI: 1.45–9.66), BMI ≤ 24 kg/m² (OR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.36–6.52), and the use of physical warming alone (OR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.05–4.60). These findings systematically reveal that the occurrence of hypothermia results from the interplay among patient vulnerability, surgical invasiveness, and the effectiveness of temperature management strategies, providing a critical evidence base for constructing precision-oriented temperature management protocols.

Clinical implications and physiological consequences of perioperative hypothermia

Perioperative hypothermia is not a benign event; it has well‑documented adverse effects on multiple organ systems. Coagulopathy is one of the most critical consequences, as even mild hypothermia (36.0 °C to 35.0 °C) reduces platelet aggregation and inhibits the function of coagulation factors (particularly factor XI and XII), is associated with increased intraoperative blood loss and higher transfusion rates [7, 8]. Cardiovascular morbidity also rises: hypothermia triggers sympathetic activation, increasing myocardial oxygen demand and the risk of myocardial ischemia, arrhythmias (typically ventricular ectopy), and postoperative cardiac events [9]. Immune function is compromised, with impaired neutrophil chemotaxis, oxidative killing, and reduced cytokine production, which elevates the risk of surgical site infections, pneumonia, and sepsis [7]. Pharmacologically, hypothermia slows hepatic metabolism and renal clearance of anesthetic drugs (e.g., propofol, fentanyl, midazolam, and muscle relaxants), prolonging emergence from anesthesia, delaying extubation, and increasing PACU stay [22]. Postoperative shivering, which occurs in up to 40–60% of hypothermic patients, increases oxygen consumption by up to 500%, potentially precipitating hypoxemia and acidosis in vulnerable patients [11]. Collectively, these effects translate into longer hospital stays, higher readmission rates, and increased healthcare costs [8]. Therefore, identifying and modifying risk factors for hypothermia is not only physiologically important but also clinically and economically relevant. Our study provides a stratified risk profile that enables targeted prevention.

Risk factors in context

Advanced age, as the strongest patient-related risk factor, has been extensively elucidated in terms of its underlying mechanisms. Elderly patients often exhibit a decreased basal metabolic rate, attenuated autonomic thermoregulatory function—particularly diminished vasoconstrictive responses—and reduced heat production due to decreased muscle mass [25]. The findings of this study are highly consistent with those reported by Kay et al., who observed that the incidence of hypothermia in elderly patients undergoing joint replacement surgery without pre-warming reached as high as 85.42% [26]. This suggests that age should be considered the primary indicator for preoperative temperature risk stratification. On the other hand, lower BMI (≤ 24 kg/m²) represents another key constitutional risk factor. Adipose tissue serves not only as an energy storage organ but also as an essential insulating layer. The study by Yoo et al. [27]. confirmed an inverse correlation between subcutaneous fat thickness and the rate of core temperature decline. In underweight patients, the reduced amount of insulating material allows body heat to dissipate more rapidly through radiation and convection during surgical exposure. Therefore, for elderly and low-BMI patients, preoperative assessment should extend beyond the surgery itself, categorizing them as a “high-risk for hypothermia” population and initiating a higher-level thermal protection protocol.

This study found that operative duration, blood loss, and fluid infusion volume collectively form an interrelated “cold stress triangle.” Prolonged surgery (≥ 4 h) entails extended body cavity exposure, sustained suppression of hypothalamic thermoregulatory function by anesthetic agents, and cumulative evaporative heat loss, which aligns with the conclusions of Bozkul et al. [28]. in prostate surgery. More critically, intraoperative bleeding and the infusion of large volumes of unwarmed fluids pose a direct “cold challenge.” Collins et al. [29]. explicitly noted in their review that each liter of room-temperature crystalloid or 200 mL of 4 °C stored blood transfused reduces core body temperature by approximately 0.25–0.5 °C on average [30]. The data from this study show that when the infusion volume exceeds 3500 mL, the risk of hypothermia increases sharply (OR = 5.64), strongly indicating that in complex spinal surgeries with significant bleeding or requiring massive fluid resuscitation, conventional warming measures may be insufficient to offset the resulting heat loss. Therefore, collaboration between surgeons and anesthesiologists to actively reduce bleeding through meticulous surgical techniques, the use of hemostatic materials, and blood salvage techniques, along with the mandatory warming of all intravenous fluids, blood products, and cavity irrigation fluids (recommended to 37 °C), constitutes the technical core for breaking this vicious cycle [27, 31].

A particularly insightful finding is that, compared to “physical warming combined with pharmacological intervention,” the use of “physical warming alone” was identified as an independent risk factor for hypothermia (OR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.05–4.60). This does not negate the effectiveness of physical measures such as forced-air warming blankets or thermal blankets, but rather reveals that in highly vulnerable patients undergoing major surgical trauma, relying solely on the passive strategy of “reducing heat loss” exhibits a “ceiling effect”. Research by Park et al. [4]. also supports that active metabolic intervention can effectively counteract the downward shift in the thermoregulatory set point induced by anesthesia. These results promote an evolution in the concept of temperature management from “passive warming” to “active warming.” However, the observed association between combined warming and reduced hypothermia risk must be interpreted with caution because the non‑randomized design may introduce confounding by indication (i.e., more vulnerable patients might have preferentially received the combined protocol). Although we adjusted for measured confounders, unmeasured factors (e.g., frailty, preoperative nutritional status) could have influenced both the choice of warming method and the risk of hypothermia. For patients with multiple risk factors, the optimal strategy should be a multimodal integrated warming approach: mandating fluid warming as the foundation, using an efficient physical warming system as a barrier, and, when appropriate, incorporating metabolic heat-generating interventions pre-or intraoperatively to form a comprehensive, multi-layered protective network [32].The rationale for using amino acids and glucose as a pharmacological warming adjunct is based on their thermogenic effects. Amino acids have a high thermic effect (20–30% of energy expended as heat), stimulate sympathetic activity, and enhance mitochondrial uncoupling, while glucose oxidation directly generates ATP and heat. Together, they provide endogenous heat to compensate for anesthesia‑induced impairment of central thermoregulation, which cannot be fully corrected by external physical warming alone.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. (1) Although the retrospective design was adjusted using multivariate analysis, it cannot completely eliminate residual bias caused by unknown confounding factors (such as preoperative nutritional status, thyroid function, and the use of specific vasoactive drugs). Additionally, retrospective data collection may introduce information bias. (2) Some key variables were not included in the analysis, such as the types and doses of anesthetic and muscle relaxant agents, specific parameters of the operating room environment, and patients’ preoperative anxiety status. This limits the full explanatory power of the model. (3) As a single-center study, the sample was drawn from one specialized hospital. Therefore, caution is needed when generalizing the conclusions to other institutions, especially comprehensive hospitals with different clinical practices. (4) This study primarily focused on the occurrence of hypothermia. Future research could further explore the dose-response relationship between the depth and duration of hypothermia and postoperative complications, and evaluate its impact on patients’ long-term functional recovery and quality of life. (5) The pharmacological warming intervention was always administered together with warmed intravenous fluids and forced‑air warming, forming a bundled active warming protocol. Therefore, the independent contribution of the amino acid infusion per se cannot be completely isolated from the effect of concurrent fluid warming. However, the multivariate analysis adjusted for the use of warmed fluids, and the strong physiological basis of amino acid‑induced thermogenesis supports its additional benefit.(6) Operating room ambient temperature was set at 21–23 °C per protocol but was not individually recorded for each patient. Therefore, we could not adjust for potential variations in room temperature, which may influence perioperative heat loss. Future prospective studies should include continuous OR temperature monitoring as a covariate. (7) Several potential confounders were not recorded: Anesthetic doses were standardized per protocol but not individually documented; any variation would likely be non‑differential and bias toward the null. Operating room ambient temperature (21–23 °C per protocol) and irrigation fluid temperature (≈ 21 °C, consistent across all surgeries) were not measured per patient; the former may introduce non‑differential misclassification (biasing toward the null), while the latter would not confound between‑group comparisons. Vasoactive drug use (e.g., phenylephrine, ephedrine) and preoperative nutritional status (e.g., serum albumin) were also not available; their omission could lead to residual confounding, the direction of which is uncertain. Overall, these unmeasured factors likely attenuate rather than inflate the observed associations. Future prospective studies should include these covariates. (8) Unmeasured confounders (e.g., OR temperature, irrigation fluid temperature, vasoactive drug use) are likely non‑differentially misclassified, which would bias effect estimates toward the null. Thus, the true associations may be stronger than observed. Our decision to restrict the study population to ASA I–II patients was a deliberate methodological choice to minimize confounding by severe comorbidities (e.g., cardiovascular instability, endocrinopathies, and medications affecting thermoregulation), thereby creating a more homogenous cohort and allowing clearer identification of surgery‑ and anesthesia‑related factors. However, this restriction limits the external validity of our findings. Our results are generalizable to relatively healthy patients (ASA I–II) undergoing spinal trauma surgery, but they cannot be directly extrapolated to ASA III–IV patients, who may have higher baseline risk, impaired thermoregulation, and different responses to warming interventions. In such patients, the risk of hypothermia and its associated complications may be even higher, and our proposed risk stratification may not be fully applicable. The direction of this selection bias is uncertain: it may underestimate the effects of risk factors in ASA III–IV populations because these patients have more severely impaired thermoregulation. Our single‑center design further limits generalizability; however, a comparison of our cohort with national databases (Supplementary Table S2) showed similar demographic and injury characteristics, suggesting reasonable external validity for similar trauma populations. Future studies should specifically target ASA III–IV patients to validate our findings in this high‑risk group. (9) Limited power for interaction analysis: The interaction analyses, while suggesting no significant synergistic effects, were likely underpowered due to the modest sample size (n = 222), especially for detecting interactions which typically require larger sample sizes than main effects. The wide confidence intervals for all interaction terms (Supplementary Table S1) support this concern. Therefore, the absence of significant interactions should be interpreted with caution; it does not rule out clinically meaningful effect modification that might be detected in larger cohorts. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to adequately assess potential synergistic effects among the identified risk factors.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort of ASA I–II patients undergoing spinal trauma surgery, six variables (age ≥ 60 years, BMI ≤ 24 kg/m², operation time ≥ 4 h, intraoperative blood loss ≥ 200 mL, intraoperative fluid infusion ≥ 3500 mL, and the use of physical warming alone) were independently associated with an increased risk of perioperative hypothermia. Due to the observational design, these associations do not imply causality. While active multimodal warming strategies appear promising, prospective randomized trials are needed to confirm causal relationships. Clinicians may use the identified risk factors for risk stratification but should exercise caution in attributing direct protective effects.
