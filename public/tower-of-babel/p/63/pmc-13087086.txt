Molecular phylogenetic analyses indicate recent divergence of two gryllid-specific lineages within the generalist entomopathogenic fungus Metarhizium pingshaense clade
Authors: Oumi Nishi, Kazuhiro Iiyama, Chisa Yasunaga-Aoki, Susumu Shimizu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the phylogenetic position, pathogenicity, and phenotypic variation of Metarhizium isolates from the Australian cricket Teleogryllus commodus and the Japanese cricket T. emma. Molecular phylogenetic analysis revealed that both the fungal groups formed two clades within the M. pingshaense clade of the PARB clade, a lineage composed of generalist species that infect multiple orders of insects. Virulence bioassays on insects from three orders showed that both fungal groups were cricket-specific and distinctly different from closely related isolates of the PARB clade, despite the very shallow molecular phylogenetic divergence among them. The growth rates of both fungal groups on the agar medium were relatively slow, which seemed to reflect their narrow host range. PCR of virulence genes, such as DtxS1 and Mcl1, revealed that they were conserved among generalist PARB clade members but absent in both or one of the two gryllid-specialists. These results indicate that the two gryllid-specialist lineages have recently diverged from the ancestral generalist lineage, with concomitant divergence or loss of these virulence genes. This study contributes to our understanding of the evolutionary relationships between generalist and specialist species in Metarhizium.Keywords: Clavicipitaceae, Gryllidae, Host shift, Pathogenicity, Teleogryllus

1. Introduction

Metarhizium (Hypocreales: Clavicipitaceae) is a cosmopolitan entomopathogenic fungal genus that comprises species ranging from specialists with very narrow host ranges to generalists that attack a wide range of insects. Metarhizium anisopliae sensu lato is the most widely distributed and frequently occurring generalist lineage in this genus. Molecular phylogenetic analyses have also identified several cryptic species (Bischoff et al., 2009; Kepler et al., 2014; Mongkolsamrit et al., 2020). Recent findings indicated that species belonging to the PARB clade (M. pingshaense, M. anisopliae sensu stricto, M. humberi, M. robertsii, and M. brunneum) are among the most frequently occurring generalist entomopathogenic species (Chen et al., 2018; Kepler et al., 2015; Nishi et al., 2011; Rocha et al., 2013; Steinwender et al., 2014; Wyrebek et al., 2011) (Fig. 1). According to the ARSEF strain catalog, the generalist species have been isolated from insect hosts from more than six orders (Castrillo, 2020). Phylogenetic analysis of the insect-derived M. anisopliae and M. flavoviride complex in Japan revealed that M. pingshaense is the most frequently occurring species, infecting insect belonging to six orders and 14 families (Nishi & Sato, 2017). Several isolates of this species, including M. brunneum F52 (the active ingredient of a commercial wide-spectrum mycoinsecticide) and M. robertsii ARSEF 2575 (a model generalist isolate used in many biochemical and physiological studies), infect multiple insect orders (e.g. De Azevedo et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2011).

Phylogenetic analysis of Metarhizium spp. revealed that specialist species such as M. rileyi, M. acridum, and M. majus share a common ancestor with the PARB clade and diverge earlier than the PARB clade, suggesting that their common ancestor is a specialist species (Hu et al., 2014). Several host specialist species of Metarhizium have been described; however, no specialist species have been identified within the PARB clades. Comparative genomic analyses of Metarhizium spp. have revealed that the shift from specialists to generalists is accompanied by an increase in virulence-associated genes, due to gene family expansions and horizontal gene transfers (Hu et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2019). For example, Wang et al. (2012) suggested dtxS1, an NRPS comprising the insecticidal cyclopeptide (destruxin) synthesis gene cluster in the generalist species Metarhizium, might have been acquired from a plant pathogen through interspecific horizontal gene transfer before host range expansion. These acquired genes may enable Metarhizium spp. to cause diseases in multiple insects (Hu et al., 2014).

Metarhizium spp. were frequently isolated from Australian black field crickets (Teleogryllus commodus) (AFC) in Western District of Victoria, Australia, and were genetically identical in RAPD-PCR analyses (Driver et al., 2000; Leal et al., 1994). AFC isolates are also known to be specific to Teleogryllus spp. (Driver et al., 2000; Milner et al., 1996; Reinganum et al., 1981). A comparative genomic microarray analysis by Wang et al. (2009) revealed the presence of several virulence-associated genes in the generalist species (M. robertsii), that were not detected in the genome of ARSEF 443 isolated from AFC. This suggests divergence or loss of virulence-associated genes in the AFC isolates. However, their phylogenetic placement is not well understood. Phylogenetic DNA sequence analysis of the ITS1-5.8S rDNA-ITS2 region of Metarhizium by Driver et al. (2000) revealed that the AFC isolates belonged to M. anisopliae var. anisopliae clade, which corresponds to the PARB clade (Bischoff et al., 2009). According to the ARSEF culture collection catalogue, several Metarhizium isolates from the AFC are listed as M. pingshaense, a generalist species also belonging to the PARB clade, although detailed information regarding this identification is lacking. This implies that the AFC isolates may represent specialists within the PARB clade.

A Metarhizium isolate derived from the Japanese field cricket (JFC) T. emma was morphologically identified as M. flavoviride (Kikuchi et al., 2009). Three Metarhizium isolates derived from JFCs (F500 (NBRC 112645), F672, and F676) were identified as M. pingshaense based on phylogenetic analysis of the elongation factor 1-alpha gene (Nishi & Sato, 2017), which is a widely adopted method for phylogeny-based identification of Metarhizium spp. (Bischoff et al., 2009). These isolates were closely related to the AFC isolates based on similarities in host species and phylogenetic affiliation. However, their host specificity and phylogenetic relationship with AFC isolates remain unclear.

Elucidation of the phylogenetic status and virulence of the AFC and JFC isolates is necessary to determine whether the PARB clade can also include specialist lineages. This represents an opportunity to investigate how host specificity may have evolved independently in Metarhizium. Here, we performed phylogenetic analyses of cricket isolates from Japan and Australia, characterized their morphology and cultural characteristics, assessed their host specificity by assessing virulence toward crickets and two other orders of insects, and confirmed their possession of virulence-associated genes by PCR to determine whether they are unique specialists within the largest generalist clade of Metarhizium (i.e., the PARB clade).

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Fungal isolates

The fungal isolates used in this study, including reference isolates for molecular phylogenetic analysis, are listed in Table 1. KT2 and YT2 were isolated from Japanese field crickets (T. emma) that died of green muscardine disease and were maintained separately by region of origin in the laboratory (Fig. 1). Crickets were originally collected from Kyoto and Yamagata prefectures in Japan in Sep 2012. The fungi were isolated from the surfaces of cadavers covered with conidia, and a single-spore colony was established as a strain. The Japanese soil isolates were obtained using an oatmeal-based semi-selective medium (Nishi et al., 2011). Fkk63-13t was isolated from the soil using the bait method with subterranean termites (Nishi et al., 2017). Metarhizium isolates from soil in Japan were also tested for comparison because phylogenetic analysis showed that they were closely related to the JFC and AFC isolates. ARSEF, MAFF, and NBRC strains were obtained from ARS (Ithaca, NY, USA), NARO GenBank (Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan), and NITE (Kisarazu, Chiba, Japan), respectively.

2.2. DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing.

Crude DNA samples were prepared by first scraping mycelia from a pure agar culture with micropipette tips, and then suspending in 50-100 µL of the TE buffer (10 µmol/L pH 8.0 Tris-HCl, 1 µmol/L pH 8.0 EDTA, 0.01% RNase A (w/v)). The crude DNA solutions were kept at -20 ˚C. DNA sequences of the 5’ partial region of translation elongation factor 1-α (5TEF) of AFC and JFC isolates were determined to infer their phylogenetic placements in the current taxonomy of Metarhizium. To further detail their phylogenetic placement, the DNA sequences of four intergenic loci (MzIGS2, MzIGS3, MzIGS5, and MzIGS7) described by Kepler & Rehner (2013) were determined for both the AFC and JFC isolates. PCR of these regions was performed in 10-30-µL reaction volumes comprising 10% (v/v) of crude DNA solution, 0.3 µM of each primer, and 1×KOD ONE Master Mix -Blue- (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). The primers and conditions used for amplification were adapted from Bischoff et al. (2009), Kepler & Rehner (2013), and Wang et al. (2012); the details are presented in Supplementary Table S1. The PCR products were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis in 1.5% Agarose S (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan) dissolved in TBE buffer, stained with Midori Green Xtra (Nippon Genetics Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) or GelRed (Biotium, Freemont, United States), and illuminated with a blue LED (505 nm). For DNA sequencing, the amplified products were purified using MagExtractor PCR & Gel Clean-Up (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). Nucleotide sequences of the PCR products were determined for both strands using the Sanger DNA sequencing service provided by Eurofins Genomics Co. (Tokyo, Japan) or GENEWIZ from Azenta Co. (Massachusetts, United States), and assembled using GeneStudio (GeneStudio Inc., Georgia, United States).

2.3. Phylogenetic analysis

The dataset for the phylogenetic analysis of the 5TEF region consisted of 53 sequences, including 16 newly obtained sequences and 37 reference sequences (Bischoff et al. 2009; Hu et al. 2014; Luz et al. 2019; Nishi et al. 2011). Metarhizium majus and M. guizhouense were used as the outgroups (Fig. 1). The dataset for the four IGS regions comprised 152 sequences, including 140 newly obtained sequences and 12 reference sequences from Hu et al. (2014) and Kepler & Rehner (2013). Metarhizium humberi ARSEF 549 and M. robertsii ARSEF 2575 were used as outgroups (Fig. 2). Multiple sequence alignments were conducted using MUSCLE (Edgar, 2004), available in MEGAX (Stecher et al., 2020), with the default settings. The alignment lengths of (bp) were 659 for 5TEF, 1034 for MzIGS2, 1011 for MzIGS3, 890 for MzIGS5, and 985 for MzIGS7.

Fig. 2. Maximum likelihood phylogeny of Metarhizium pingshaense inferred from a combined dataset of four intergenic regions (MzIGS2, MzIGS3, MzIGS5 and MzIGS7). Support values were obtained from 1,000 bootstrap replicates, and values > 50% are indicated above or below the branches. Thick branches indicate bootstrap percentages ≥95%. Metarhizium humberi ARSEF 549 and M. robertsii ARSEF 2575 were used as the outgroup taxa. The scale bar indicates the number of substitutions per site for each unit branch length. As shown in the legend, the fungal isolates from crickets and the isolates tested for their virulence are marked with symbols.

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the IQ-Tree web server (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at, Trifinopoulos et al., 2016). Only 5TEF was partitioned into introns and exons (codons 1, 2, and 3). Best-fitting substitution models were selected for partitioning according to BIC by IQ-TREE with free-rate heterogeneity (5TEF intron - K2P; 5TEF codon 1 and 3 - JC; 5TEF codon 2 - F81+F; MzIGS2 - K2P+G4; MzIGS3 and MzIGS5 - K2P+I; MzIGS7 - K3Pu+ F + I). Branch support was evaluated using 1000 ultra-fast bootstraps (Felsenstein, 1985; Minh et al., 2013). All other settings were set to the default settings. The resulting Newick format tree files were edited using MEGAX software.

2.4. Morphological characterization

For comparison of conidial dimensions among 14 Metarhizium isolates which included isolates from phylogenetic subgroups associated with AFC (four isolates), JFC (five isolates), and others (five isolates), conidia were collected from 14-21-d-old culture on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium composed of 39 g potato dextrose agar (Nissui, Tokyo, Japan) per 1 L of RO water, mounted on a microscope slide with a drop of sterile distilled water containing 0.05% Tween 80, observed under a microscope Nikon OPTIPHOT-2 (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with a differential interference contrast attachment, and photographed with a microscope digital camera WRAYCAM-EL510 (Wraymer Inc., Osaka, Japan). The lengths and widths of 20 randomly selected conidia in the images were measured for each isolate using ImageJ 1.53a software (National Institute of Health, USA).

The difference in size of conidia among the 14 isolates and phylogenetic subgroups associated with AFC, JFC, and others were tested by nested ANOVA with the “aov” function in the R package “stat” (Bates et al., 2015), with conidial length or width as the response variable, the phylogenetic subgroups and the isolates (nested within the phylogenetic subgroups) as the explanatory variable.

2.5. Growth characterization

Colony growth rate was measured for 14 isolates, including isolates from phylogenetic subgroups associated with AFC (four isolates), JFC (five isolates), and others (five isolates), on three types of media, based on the methodology of Parker et al. (2023). The isolates were grown on: (1) PDA; (2) Sabouraud Dextrose Yeast Extract Agar (SDYA; 20 g glucose, 10 g peptone, 10 g yeast extract, and 15 g agar in 1 L of distilled water); (3) gryllid minimal agar medium (GMA, a Czapek Dox agar medium with sucrose replaced by an equal amount of cricket powder), composed of 3.0 g NaNO3, 1.0 g KH2PO4, 0.5 g MgSO4·7H2O, 0.5 g KCl, 0.01 g FeSO4·7H2O, 15 g of agar, and 20 g Black Cricket Powder (KAKEO Inc., Tokyo, Japan), 100% cricket powder made of Gryllus bimaculatus, in 1 L of distilled water. Growth rates were examined by inoculating 1 μL of a 1 × 107 conidia/mL suspension in the center of an agar plate (12.5 mL in a 9-cm-diam Petri dish). Plates were maintained at 25 ˚C and 70% Relative humidity (RH) with a photoperiod of 24 h darkness. Colony diameters were measured at 3, 6, 9, and 12 d. Experiments were performed with three plates of each medium type.

To test for differences in growth rates of the 14 isolates, linear mixed-effect models were constructed with the “lmer” function in the R package lme4 v1.1-29 (Bates et al., 2015), with colony diameter of each plate as the response variable, the culture period and the isolate as the explanatory variable, and plates considered as random effects. Pairwise comparison of growth rate was calculated with the function “emtrends” in the R package emmeans v1.8-5 (Lenth et. al., 2023), with all post-hoc comparisons adjusted using Tukey’s HSD test.

2.6. Virulence against Japanese field crickets

To characterize the virulence of JFC isolates in the PARB clade, 11 isolates, which included isolates from phylogenetic subgroups associated with AFC (one isolate), JFC (four isolates), and others (six isolates), were tested and evaluated for virulence against JFC and mealworms based on mortality using a single concentration of inoculum. The seven soil isolates included five isolates of M. pingshaense, which were closely related to the AFC and JFC isolates, and one isolate each from M. robertsii and M. humberi, the generalist species most closely related to M. pingshaense. Among the 11 isolates, four were further tested for virulence against crickets, mealworms, and silkworm moths and evaluated for LC50 values. These four isolates included two JFC isolates and two soil isolates belonging to the same terminal node as the JFC isolates in the phylogenetic analysis of 5TEF.

JFC adults were collected in Fukuoka, Japan, from Sep to Nov in 2011 and 2020, and kept in a plastic container at 25 ˚C in the dark. Eggs laid in moistened humus soils were kept at 4˚C for two to six mo and then incubated at 25 ˚C. Nymphs hatched from the eggs were reared at 23-27 ˚C with a commercial diet for crickets (Korogi House, Fukuoka, Japan), and nymphs 1.5-2.0 cm in body length were used for the virulence bioassay. Individuals from the 2020 sample were used for virulence bioassays with NBRC 112645, and those from the 2011 sample were used for all other bioassays.

Conidia were collected from 14-21-day-old cultures on oatmeal agar (6% fine oat flour, 1.25% agar, and 0.01% chloramphenicol), suspended in sterile distilled water containing 0.05% Tween 80, and filtered through sterile cotton stuffed in a sterile syringe to eliminate hyphae. The concentration of the conidial suspension was measured with a hemocytometer and adjusted to 1.0 × 107 conidia/ml. Nymphs were kept at 4 ˚C for 30 min to 1 h prior to inoculation to slow their movement, and were moved to a 25 ˚C room immediately after inoculation. Ten nymphs were inoculated by immersing in 10 mL conidial suspension in 50 mL centrifuge tubes for 10 s, dried on a paper towel, and then transferred to separate Petri dishes (ø 9 × 1.5 cm) lined with a filter paper (ø 9 cm). Pieces of cabbage leaves were placed in Petri dishes as food for the nymphs. The dishes were placed in plastic bags (10 dishes per plastic bag), loosely tied, and incubated at 25 ˚C in the dark for 12 d. Nymph mortality was assessed every three days. Control experiments for the bioassay were performed by inoculating the nymphs with a 0.05% Tween 80 solution without any conidia. A total of 30 or 40 nymphs (10 nymphs per replicate, three trials each with one replicate for NBRC 112645 and four trials for the other isolates) were inoculated with each isolate for the 12th day mortality comparison with an inoculum of 1.0 × 107 conidia/mL.

To determine the LC50 value (conidia/ml) for the four isolates (Kgs11-1, KT2, Oit10-1, and YT2), nymphs were inoculated with 10-fold serially diluted conidial suspensions using the method described above (1.0 × 103, 1.0 × 104, 1.0 × 105, 1.0 × 106, and 1.0 × 107 conidia/mL for KT2 and YT2, and 1.0 × 107 and 1.0 × 108 conidia/ml for Kgs11-1 and Oit10-1). Control experiments for the bioassay were performed by inoculating the nymphs with a 0.05% Tween 80 solution without any conidia. A total of 20-21 nymphs (20-21 nymphs per replicate per trial) were inoculated at the inoculum level. The dose-response relationship (conidial concentration and 12th day mortality) was estimated by a generalized linear model (binomial error distribution with a logit link) with the “MASS” package in R (R Core Team, 2022). The LC50 value and standard error were calculated using the function “dose.p” in the package.

2.7. Mealworm virulence bioassays

Virulence against mealworm (Tenebrio molitor) larvae was tested using all 11 isolates used in the cricket bioassay. The mealworm larvae were obtained from a pet store. The larvae were reared on wheat bran and cabbage leaves at 23-27 ˚C. Ten larvae, 2.5-3.0 cm in body length, were inoculated with conidial suspension (1.0 × 106 conidia/ml) prepared as described above by topical application (50 µL/larva). The excess inoculum was blotted onto paper towels. The larvae were transferred to a Petri dish (ø 9 cm × 1.5 cm) lined with moistened filter paper, fed with pieces of cabbage leaves, and kept in a plastic box (25 ˚C, 100% RH, 14L:10D) for 12 d. Larval mortality was checked every three days, and dead individuals were removed from the dishes. Thirty larvae (10 larvae per replicate, three trials each with one replicate) were used to compare the mortality rates on the 12th day.

To determine the LC50 value (conidia/mL) for the four isolates (Kgs11-1, KT2, Oit10-1, and YT2), inoculation was conducted with 10-fold serially diluted conidial suspensions using the method described above (1.0 × 106, 1.0 × 107 and 1.0 × 108 conidia/mL for KT2 and YT2, and 1.0 × 103, 1.0 × 104, 1.0 × 105, 1.0 × 106, and 1.0 × 107 conidia/mL for Kgs11-1 and Oit10-1). Control experiments for the bioassay were performed by inoculating the larvae with a 0.05% Tween 80 solution without any conidia. Twenty larvae were used for each inoculum concentration (10 larvae per trial, two trials). The LC50 value for each isolate was calculated from 12th day mortality using the method described for the cricket bioassay.

2.8. Virulence bioassays in silkworms

Virulence against silkworm larvae was tested against four isolates (Kgs11-1, KT2, Oit10-1, and YT2). The eggs of the silkworm (Bombyx mori) (hybrids of Kinshu and Showa) were obtained from Ueda Sanshu (Nagano, Japan). The larvae were reared on an artificial diet for silkworms (KIT-II, Mukin-Yosan system Kenkyu-Jo, Kyoto, Japan) at 23-27 ˚C. Fourth-instar larvae (24-48 h old after molting) were used for the virulence bioassay.

Larvae were inoculated with a 10-fold serially diluted conidial suspension (1.0 × 106, 1.0 × 107 and 1.0 × 108 conidia/mL for KT2 and YT2, and 1.0 × 104, 1.0 × 105, 1.0 × 106, and 1.0 × 107 conidia/mL for Kgs11-1 and Oit10-1, 21 larvae per inoculum level for KT2, YT2, Oit10-1 and 12 larvae for Kgs11-1) on their back with ten stroke of a sterile brush per each larva, dried on a paper towel, and then transferred into a plastic container lined with moistened paper towel to expose them to saturated humidity (100% RH). Plastic containers were incubated at 25 ˚C in the dark for 24 h. Larvae were then transferred to a new plastic container without moistened paper towel (seven larvae per container for KT2, YT2, Oit10-1, and six larvae for Kgs11-1) and incubated at 25 ˚C in the dark. After the first 24 h of incubation, larvae were fed an artificial diet. Larval mortality was checked daily, and dead larvae were removed from the plastic containers. Each experiment was performed once for each isolate. The LC50 value (conidia/mL) of each isolate was calculated from the mortality of larvae 10 days after inoculation using the same method described for the cricket bioassay.

2.9. Detection of virulence genes by PCR

The conservation of three virulence genes, the NRPS gene comprising the destruxin synthesis gene cluster (DtxS1), Metarhizium collagen-like protein gene (Mcl1), and Metarhizium esterase gene (Mest1), and two possible virulence-associated genes, lanosterol synthase gene (Lss) and flavin-binding monooxygenase (Fmo), was tested using PCR for all 11 isolates used in the virulence bioassay. The latter two genes were orthologs of two virulence-associated expression sequence tags (Lss: CN808855 and Fmo: CN808854) that were found in the generalist fungus M. robertsii ARSEF 2575 but were presumed to be lost or diverged in an AFC isolate ARSEF 443 and a locust-specific M. acridum ARSEF 324 (Wang et al., 2009). Translation elongation factor 1-alpha gene (EF1α) was used as a positive control. Primers and PCR conditions are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Primers were newly designed, except for DtxS1 (Wang et al., 2012), based on DNA sequences of their orthologous regions in the genomic sequence data of ARSEF 23 (locus tag Mcl1: MAA_01665; Mest1: MAA_03283; Lss: MAA_06587; Fmo: MAA_04433 in whole-genome sequence (WGS) project ADNJ02 (ADNJ02000001:ADNJ02000090)) and confirmed their high conservation in genomic sequencing data of 12 strains from M. brunneum, M. guizhouense, M. humberi, M. majus, and M. robertsii (multiple alignments for Mcl1 and Mest1 was presented as examples in Supplementary Fig. S1). After PCR, electrophoresis was performed to check for the presence of amplified products of the expected sizes (DtxS1:400-500 bp; Mcl1:1000 bp; Mest1:800-900 bp; Lss: 900-1000 bp; and Fmo: 1000 bp).

2.10. Identification of virulence genes by BLAST search of Metarhizium spp.

To determine the presence of virulence genes within the genus Metarhizium, BLAST analysis was performed on Metarhizium species for which WGS information was publicly available. On the NCBI BLAST Webserver (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), tblastn search was performed with the amino acid sequence of each virulence gene (DtxS1: XP_007826232; Mcl1: XP_007817854; Mest1: XP_007819472; Lss: XP_007822776; Fmo: XP_007820622) as queries, and the WGS sequence of Metarhizium spp. as a database (M. acridum CQMa 102: ADNI00000000, M. album ARSEF 1914: AZHE00000000, M. brunneum ARSEF 3297: AZNG00000000, M. guizhouese ARSEF 977: AZNH00000000, M. humberi (registered as M. hybridum) ARSEF 549: AZNF00000000, M. majus ARSEF 297: AZNE00000000, M. rileyi RCEF 4871: AZHC00000000, M. robertsii ARSEF 23: ADNJ02000000).

3. Results

3.1. Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analysis of the 5TEF sequences revealed that all isolates from AFC (ARSEF 437, 439, 443, 445, and 8760) and JFC (KT2, NBRC 112645, and YT2) belonged to the M. pingshaense clade (Fig. 1). Four additional IGS regions were analyzed to verify the phylogenetic placement of the gryllid isolates in the M. pingshaense clade (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S2). In the phylogenetic tree inferred separately from the four regions, the AFC and JFC isolates were consistently placed in two mutually exclusive clades that were not sister taxa (i.e., paraphyletic taxa) (Supplementary Fig. S2). In the phylogenetic tree constructed using the combined dataset from the four regions, the AFC and JFC isolates were grouped separately from the other members (AC and JE subclades) with high bootstrap support (99% and 100%, respectively) (Fig. 2). Two soil isolates, Fkk63-13t and 4G10-2-5, were grouped into the JE subclade (Fig. 2). Due to the small differences in the DNA sequences of M. pingshaense isolates, the bootstrap support of most subclades in the M. pingshaense clade was low, except for the AC and JE subclades. Low support values made it difficult to subdivide the M. pingshaense clade based on the GCPSR.

3.2. Morphological characterization of AFC and JFC isolates

The size of the conidia of 14 M. pingshaense isolates, including the JFC and AFC isolates, was determined (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S2). The size significantly differed among the isolates (ANOVA, length: p < 2.00 × 10-16; width: p = 8.12 × 10-6). The difference in conidial size among the three groups (AC subclade, JE subclade, and others) was not significant for length but was significant for width (ANOVA, length: p = 0.0696 > 0.05; width: p = 0.0221 < 0.05). The distribution of the conidial dimensions in the three groups overlapped, and no clear boundaries were determined (Fig. 3).

Fig. 3. Comparisons of conidial dimensions of 14 isolates of Metarhizium pingshaense. The plot indicates the means (± S.E., n=20) of length and width of each fungal isolates. The sizes significantly differed among the isolates (ANOVA, length: p < 2.00 × 10-16; width: p = 8.12 × 10-6). Difference of the conidial sizes among the three groups (AC subclade, JE subclade, and others) was not significant in length, but in width (ANOVA, length: p = 0.0696 > 0.05; width: p = 0.0221 < 0.05).

3.3. Cultural characterization of AFC and JFC isolates

The colony growth rate was generally in the order M. pingshaense > AC subclade > JE subclade in all three media tested (Fig. 4). However, there were some overlaps between the JE and AC subclades for GMA and PDA media, and between the AC subclade and other M. pingshaense for SDYA media (Tukey’s HDS: p > 0.05). In the JE subclade, the colony growth rate consistently followed the order GMA > SDYA > PDA, whereas the order was SDYA > GMA > PDA in the AC subclade.

Fig. 4. Comparisons of colony growth rates of Metarhizium pingshaense isolates across different media. Estimated growth rates (mm/day) of all tested isolates of M. pingshaense in (A) PDA, (B) SDYA, or (C) GMA (Czapek-Dox Agar with sucrose replaced with cricket powder). The individual plots represent the growth rate and standard error per plate estimated by the liner mixed model. Letters indicate different statistically significant growth rates (Tukey’s HSD test, p < 0.05).

3.4. Virulence bioassays of the three insects

LC50 value of the JFC isolates (KT2 and YT2) against JFC were approximately 1.0 × 105 conidia/ml, whereas those of the two soil isolates of M. pingshaense (Kgs11-1 and Oit10-1) were around 1.0 × 108 conidia/ml or over (Fig. 5A). Conversely, the LC50 value of JFC isolates against mealworm and silkworm were approximately 1.0 × 108 conidia/ml or over, but 1.0 × 105 ~ 1.0 × 106 conidia/ml for the two soil isolates of M. pingshaense. The differences in LC50 values between the two groups were more than 100-fold in the three insect bioassays.

Fig. 5. Comparisons of virulence of Metarhizium spp. against different orders of insects presented as the median lethal concentration (LC50) (A) and mortality (B). Significant differences of the average mortality among the 11 fungal isolates were indicated by capital letters (cricket) and lower-case letters (mealworm) (Tukey’s HSD test, p < 0.05). Error bar: 95% confidential interval (A) and starndard error (B). The species are abbreviated as follows: M. humberi (Mh), M. pingshaense (Mp), and M. robertsii (Mr).

3.5. JFC virulence bioassays

Virulence bioassay results with inocula of 1.0 × 107 conidia/ml showed that the four isolates of the JE subclade (Fkk63-13t, KT2, NBRC 112645, and YT2) caused significantly higher mortality (>80%) than the AFC isolate ARSEF 443 and six soil isolates (four M. pingshaense, one M. humberi, and one M. robertsii) on the 12th day after inoculation (Fig. 5B) (Tukey's HSD, p < 0.05). The AFC isolate ARSEF 443 was moderately virulent (42.5% mortality, which was not significantly different from that of the control). The other soil isolates presented limited virulence (0-10% mortality, which was not significantly different from that of the control).

3.6. Mealworm virulence bioassay

The virulence bioassay with the inoculation of 1.0 × 106 conidia/mL against the mealworm presented opposite trends compared to that obtained with JFC bioassays (Fig. 5B). The mortality rates of three JFC isolates (KT2, NBRC 112645, and YT2), one soil isolate (Fkk63-13t), and one AFC isolate (ARSEF 443) were significantly lower than those of the other six soil isolates and did not differ significantly from the control (Tukey's HSD, p < 0.01).

3.7. Detection of virulence genes by PCR

Amplicons of Mest1 and EF1α (positive control) were detected in all the 11 isolates (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S3). In contrast, amplicons of DtxS1 and Lss were not detected in five isolates from the AC and JE subclades, but were detected in the other six isolates. Amplicons of Mcl1 were not detected in four isolates from the JE subclade but were detected in the other seven isolates. Amplicons of Fmo were not detected in four isolates from the JE subclade and one M. pingshaense isolate (Kgs14-1), but were detected in six other isolates (four M. pingshaense isolates, one M. robertsii isolate, and one M. humberi isolate). Similar results were obtained for the PCR detection of DtxS1 and Mcl1 in 24 additional Metarhizium isolates derived from various insect hosts, including three isolates from the AC subclade, an ex-holotype of M. pingshaense (CBS 257.90), 14 M. pingshaense isolates, and six M. brunneum isolates (Supplementary Fig. S4). As supporting data for the detection of DtxS1 and Mcl1, PCR was performed for the neighboring genes of each gene using the primer sets listed in Supplementary Table 2 (the gene map was based on the whole genome sequence of M. robertsii ARSEF 23, Accession No. ADNJ02000000). The results for the neighboring genes was similar to those for DtxS1 and Mcl1 (Supplementary Fig. S5).

PCR-based detection of five virulence or virulence-associated genes in Metarhizium spp. Translation elongation factor 1-alpha gene (EF1α) was used as a positive control (PC) for the PCR detection. Complete gel images of PCR detection are presented in the supplementary data (Supplementary Fig. S3).

The results of the tblastn analysis from the WGS data to estimate the presence of the five virulence genes in the eight Metarhizium spp. are illustrated in Fig. 7, along with the results of the PCR detection described above. In the tblastn analysis, orthologs of all five genes were found in the WGS of M. majus, M. guizhouense, M. brunneum, M. humberi, and M. robertsii, but not in M. rileyi and M. album. Only the Mcl1 ortholog was found in the WGS of M. acridum.

4. Discussion

Phylogenetic analyses indicated that all Metarhizium isolates from AFC and JFC belonged to the clade of the generalist species M. pingshaense (Fig. 1). Each of these isolates formed distinct lineages within the M. pingshaense clade (i.e., the AC and JE subclades) (Fig. 2), although they showed no morphological differentiation in conidial dimensions compared to other M. pingshaense (Fig. 3). The virulence of the JFC isolates was strong against JFC and weak against mealworms and silkworms compared to that of the other tested isolates (Fig. 5), suggesting high specificity of the JFC isolates against their wild host. AFC isolates have also been reported to be specific for AFC (Milner et al., 1996; Wang et al., 2009). Milner et al. (1996) reported that M. anisopliae sensu lato FI 1099 (ARSEF 445), which was isolated from AFC, was nonpathogenic to the migratory locust Locusta migratoria (Orthoptera: Acrididae) and mealworm T. molitor (Coleoptera: Tenebrionidae) at doses that resulted in a high level of AFC mortality. The present study confirmed that an AFC isolate, ARSEF 443, had intermediate virulence against JFC and was non-pathogenic against the mealworm. These results suggested the presence of at least two distinct gryllid-specific lineages in the M. pingshaense clade. These two lineages have recently diverged from ancestral generalists, as M. pingshaense is a generalist species belonging to a generalist species complex (i.e., the PARB clade). In addition, the strong support (97%) for the clade composed of AC subclade members Kgs11-1, CBS257.90, Fkk5-2, and ARSEF 8736 in the phylogeny inferred from the combined data suggests a non-sister relationship between the AC and JE subclades and the independent origin of this gryllid specificity, although this is not strictly supported by the genealogical concordance of the four phylogenetic trees individually inferred from the four IGS loci. Because the PARB clade is known to be an assemblage of generalist species, the discovery of two gryllid-specific lineages in the PARB clade suggests more complex phylogenetic relationships between generalists and specialists in the genus Metarhizium than previously assumed.

In addition to pathogenicity, there were clear differences in in vitro growth of the gryllid-specific clade members and their close relatives in M. pingshaense (Fig. 4). The tendency of the JE and AC subclades to have slower growth rates in all media appears to reflect their high nutritional requirements in accordance with their narrow host ranges, which cannot be compensated for simply by the addition of cricket powder. This is similar to the findings of St. Leger et al. (1992), who reported that the AFC isolate ARSEF 443, as well as well-characterized specialists such as M. majus ARSEF 1946 and M. acridum ARSEF 324, presented very low germination rates in yeast extract media compared to the generalist strain M. robertsii ARSEF 2575. In addition, the JE and AC subclades consistently differed in their medium preferences. The most favorable media for growth were GMA for the JE subclade and SDYA for the AC subclade. This indicates a physiological differentiation between the two gryllid-specific clades.

PCR-based detection of virulence genes also clearly distinguished gryllid-specific clade members from their close relatives in M. pingshaense. Despite the high levels of conservation of DtxS1 and Mcl1 among the PARB clade members, they were not detected in either the AC or JE subclades. Since amplification was observed in all the isolates in the positive control, the lack of amplification was most likely due to DNA sequence mutations, such as a lack or divergence in the primer annealing sites. These results suggest the divergence or loss of this gene during specialization processes. If these results reflect divergence in DtxS1 and Mcl1, variants of these genes may enhance virulence in crickets. However, if this result reflects the loss of genes, their loss may have narrowed the host range. Both neighboring genes were undetectable (Supplementary Fig. S5), we believe that these genes were likely lost in the genomes of the gryllid-specific clades. DtxS1 encodes a non-ribosomal peptide synthase involved in the biosynthesis of destruxin, a cyclic peptide known as an insecticidal toxin originally found in M. anisopliae sensu lato (Kodaira 1962). BLAST analysis in this study confirmed that orthologs of DtxS1 are present across the PARB clade and its sister clade, the MGT clade, but are absent in clades of specialist species, such as M. acridum, M. album, and M. rileyi. Similarly, Wang et al. (2012) revealed that only Metarhizium spp. belonging to the PARB and MGT clades produced this toxin, with the exception of M. majus. They speculated that this gene was acquired by a common ancestor of the PARB and MGT clades via horizontal gene transfer, and was at least partially involved in the fitness of generalist species. The lack or divergence of this gene in independently diverged gryllid specialists strongly supports its association with host-range expansion. This finding also suggests a low contribution of destruxin to virulence against the field cricket Teleogryllus spp. but not against other insects within the host range of M. pingshaense. The other virulence gene, Mcl1, was not detected in JE subclade members. Mcl1, identified in M. robertsii encodes a collagenous protective coat of hyphal bodies (Wang & St. Leger, 2006). BLASTP and TBLASTN homology searches revealed that orthologs of this gene were found in species in the PARB clade, MGT clade, and their close relative specialist M. acridum (Fig. 7), suggesting that a common ancestor of these species acquired this gene. The lack of detection of this gene in the JE subclade members suggests a low contribution of this gene to JFC infection, although gryllids have cellular immune systems similar to those of most invertebrates (Cho & Cho, 2019; Drayton & Jennions, 2011). This result also suggests a differential adaptation to Teleogryllus spp. between the AC and JE subclades, similar to the closely related lineages in the M. majus clade that have adapted to different Scarabaeidae species (Nishi et al., 2015). Mest1 was detected in all fungal isolates tested in this study. Mest1, identified in M. robertsii encodes an esterase that degrades stored lipids (Wang et al., 2011). This is in contrast to our original assumption that, since this gene was a selective virulence factor for lepidopteran larvae, it would likely have been lost in the specialization process to crickets. Lss and Fmo are considered virulence-associated genes of M. robertsii based on expression analysis (Wang et al., 2009). Genomic microarray analysis revealed the loss or divergence of these genes in the AFC isolate ARSEF 443. Their functions have not been studied using reverse genetics approaches; however, the lack or divergence of both genes in either the AC or JE subclade members suggests their association with a broad host range.

Species in the PARB clade were previously classified based on the genealogical concordance of phylogenetic species recognition (GCPSR) (Bischoff et al., 2009; Luz et al., 2019). The present study conducted a multi-locus phylogenetic analysis within the M. pingshaense clade (Fig. 2). However, the low bootstrap support of most subclades in the M. pingshaense clade, except for the AC and JE subclades, rendered subdivision of the clade based on GCPSR difficult (Supplementary Fig. S2). In contrast, the in vitro growth rate and PCR detection of virulence genes strongly support the distinction between the AC and JE subclades and other M. pingshaense clade members (Figs. 4 and 5). Future phylogenetic analyses using whole-genome sequence information may provide molecular phylogenetic evidence that the AC and JE subclades are independent of the ex-holotype of M. pingshaense. The AC and JE subclades were easily distinguished from their close relatives by the host species, slower growth rate on agar medium, and absence of some virulence genes, which will likely serve as simple criteria for the identification of species in the future.

The PARB clade includes generalist species and evolved from a specialist ancestor (Hu et al., 2014). Comparative genomic analyses of Metarhizium spp. revealed that the shift from specialist to generalist was accompanied by an increase in genome size and gene number. During this process, the number of virulence genes increases due to gene family expansion and horizontal gene transfer (Zhang et al., 2019). This study revealed the opposite direction of host shift in the M. pingshaense clade, from generalists to gryllid specialists. Based on genomic changes during the emergence of the PARB clade, this specialization process is considered to be accompanied by a loss of virulence genes. PCR-based detection of virulence genes suggested the divergence or loss of DtxS1 and Mcl1 during the specialization process, although this is merely a correlation and not necessarily a causal relationship. The loss of virulence-associated genes in specialists was also supported by genomic array analyses conducted by Wang et al. (2009). Future genome analyses of the two gryllid-specific lineages will clarify the novel evolutionary processes of host shifting.

This study revealed the recent divergence of two independent gryllid-specific lineages of Metarhizium in Australia and Japan in the clade of the generalist entomopathogenic fungus M. pingshaense. This suggests that the phylogenetic relationships among generalists and specialists are more complicated than previously assumed and that host specialization can be convergently gained from generalist ancestors. This large phenotypic variability among the isolates, with only small differences in the DNA marker regions and microscopic morphology used for species identification, raises further questions in terms of the current phylogenetic-based taxonomy of Metarhizium. Further isolation, characterization, and phylogenetic identification will expand our understanding of the diversity of specialist and generalist species in the genus Metarhizium.
