Preliminary study on the dynamic mechanical thermal analysis of polymer foams for use in impact resistant systems design
Authors: Marzena Fejdyś, Marek Klich, Stefan Cichosz, Małgorzata Kudlińska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The objective of this research was to assess the utility of dynamic mechanical thermal analysis (DMTA) in investigation of polymer-based foams for the potential use in anti-impact protection systems. Therefore, three different types of specimens were chosen (polyolefin, polyurethane, rubber) and examined with: i. amplitude (1-100 µm), ii. frequency (0.1–100 Hz), iii. temperature (-60-60°C) sweeps. These DMTA modes enabled to successfully mimic: i. deformation during the impact, ii. foam behaviour while worn (low frequencies) and hit (high frequencies), iii. summer and winter conditions. Among others, investigation of loss angle tangent (tanδ), being a ratio between loss (E’’) and storage (E’) moduli, favourably revealed an inverse relationship between the tanδ at 100 Hz and maximum impact force needed to destroy the specimen. It was also established that a compromise between elastic (E‘) and viscous (E’’) needs to be maintained to preserve shape stability while dissipating energy to achieve the high compressive strength (CS), e.g., more viscous rubber foam – CS of approx. 86 kPa (tanδ = 0.44), rigid polyolefin foam – CS at the level of 36 kPa (tanδ = 0.15). Therefore, DMTA successfully served as a complementary tool, providing mechanistic insight that supported and explained trends observed in conventional compression and impact testing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25803-w.

Introduction

The aim of this article concerns the possibility of complementary assessing the properties of polymer-based foams for the use in impact resistant systems by the means of dynamic mechanical thermal analysis (DMTA). To provide a better understanding of this problem, below, a consistent explanation of the polymer foams characteristics and the issue of counter-impact materials have been described based on the available literature data.

A polymer foam may be easily defined as a two-phase system that contains statistically distributed gas bubbles in a polymer matrix it is created of1. Owing to this specific structure, foamed polymers products possess numerous advantageous properties, e.g., low density2, good heat3 and sound insulation4. Most importantly, polymer foams exhibit an ability to absorb and dissipate energy5, which explains their wide use as cushioning materials in energy-absorbing application, e.g., protective equipment6,7. Thanks to the deformation mechanism of the cells, polymer foams can disperse the energy of impacts in such a way that maximum force in a polymer foam is well below the maximum force in an identical, non-foamed material8,9. Therefore, they are also widely employed in automotive safety10, aerospace11,12, sports equipment13,14 and packaging15. Undeniably, what offers a key advantage over traditional materials like metals is the density of polymer foams. Thus, providing protection without adding significant weight16.

Moreover, by simply adjusting some basic properties like density and pore structure, foams might be tailored for different cushioning needs, balancing energy dissipation with structural integrity17. Thus, depending on the application, foams might be engineered for specific conditions, with closed-cell types offering moisture resistance and durability18, and open-cell types providing breathability and flexibility19.

Moving on to the impact resistant systems, BS 7971:2014 is the standard outlining the safety requirements for protective clothing used in the UK and countries that adapted the standard (including Poland). It specifically focus on protective clothing for workers exposed to violent situations. The standard covers the design, performance, and testing of clothing to ensure adequate protection against impact. Yet, in terms of the mechanical strength of the developed product, the standard only specifies the testing of impact resistance with rod and blade strikers.

These tests certainly provide valuable results regarding the durability and fulfilment of the performance conditions of the energy-absorbing system tested20,21. Nevertheless, they do not enable a complete characterisation of the components used22–24. Impact testing only indicates that the requirements of the final product are met, but cannot entirely support the development process and the conscious selection of component materials.

For this reason, the authors of this article would like to propose DMTA technique as a complementary tool that could be easily used to select materials and design energy-absorbing protective systems. Although DMTA is a low-strain technique compared with the high-strains during impacts, it permits to highlight the influence of temperature, amplitude and frequency on the material properties24–27.

Nowadays, studies performed using the DMTA technique are widely employed in terms of analysing the viscoelastic properties of polymers28. However, they are seldom cross-referenced to the possibility of polymer material behaviour during impact. Some examples of the use of the DMTA technique in the investigation of polymer foams are cited below, hence, introducing the spectrum of studies performed to date.

Recently, Mohammadpour et al.29 have published an interesting article debating on lignin-based polyurethane foam (LPUF) and hydrophobic silica LPUF (SLPUF), which were synthesized using different concentrations of silica nanoparticles (SNP). The effect of SNP on the structure and properties of SLPUF samples was investigated and compared with LPUF through the scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), dynamic mechanical thermal analysis, and compressive tests. The DMTA results revealed a decrease in the glass transition temperature from 90 °C (LPUF) to around 52 °C (SLPUF samples). This means that the hydrophobic SNP might have changed the foam type from a rigid foam to soft one.

Additionally, Tu et al.30 investigated polymer foams based on ethylene-vinyl acetate (EVA) and/or modified with shear thickening gel (STG/EVA). Authors recorded the increase of compression frequency from 0.01 Hz to 150 Hz. Storage modulus (E’) of STG/EVA varied from 0.327 MPa to 1.415 MPa, while the change of E′ of EVA was from 0.153 MPa to 0.756 MPa. The difference in storage modulus between the modified and the plain foam material tended to be more distinct at higher loading frequency, which indicated that STG/EVA possessed significant strain rate sensitivity. A similar tendency could have been observed in the test results of loss modulus. Hence, authors of the research concluded that the elasticity and viscosity of EVA foam could be improved simultaneously by the incorporated STG.

Although the above articles refer to a more general analysis of the properties of polymer foams, some of the published works relate directly to protective products. An interesting study has been published by Nicotra et al.24 who analysed different protectors in a temperature range of −50 °C to + 50 °C with three frequencies: 1 Hz, 10 Hz and 50 Hz. From the DMTA data it was evident that protectors had variations in the properties considering the temperature range investigated. All the soft-shell materials have shown an increase of the elastic modulus when raising the frequency, which is more intense for temperatures above 0 °C. Moreover, a 8-fold increase could have been observed for a certain type of protector moving from 1 Hz to 50 Hz. Authors concluded the materials were soft while not being stressed with high speed or frequency, hence, resulting in a good ergonomic comfort. Contrary, when a fast stress was applied, the material behaved as a rigid material, distributing the impact over a wider surface.

However, previously mentioned team of Nicotra et al.22 gone one step further in their next article. They did not only investigate the properties of the protectors, but also ski boots. Researchers compared the DMTA-derived data with the performance of ski boots measured with the results obtained with flexural and rebound test benches. The application of different measurement techniques and the inter-correlation of the obtained test results provided many interesting observations and conclusions. In particular, DMTA indicated that polyolefin-based foams exhibited a lower storage modulus throughout the temperature range analysed with respect to thermoplastic polyurethanes, in agreement with the lower flexural stiffness observed with the flexural test bench. Similarly, recorded differences in stiffness measured with a test bench were in an agreement with DMTA-obtained values. Based on the research, the authors stated that DMTA can be used to predict the behaviour of a ski boot from a flexural and rebound point of view.

Similarly, Signetti et al.23 investigated the thermo-mechanical and impact properties of materials used for soft-shell back protectors showing their strain-rate-sensitive behaviour. Again, authors observed the viscoelastic properties dependence on the frequency of the applied stress. Interestingly, results on some commercially available back protectors have shown that some products were very sensitive to temperature, and in the real environment could have led to a significant increase of the impact force.

Another interesting investigation has been performed by Drane et al.31. Researchers detailed a novel process of characterizing a cross-linked high density polyethylene (X-HDPE) foam for its applicability within helmet liners and successfully identified the regions of changing behaviour of the nonlinear impact absorption material system based on the established dynamic moduli values and cross-reference with different techniques. Undeniably, this report indicates the important role of DMTA in assessing the stability of the properties of used polymer foams, which is crucial from an application point of view.

Concluding on the collected information, DMTA analysis offers the possibility of plotting the elastic-viscous characteristics of the tested material32. Notably, the last two studies presented show that it is possible to correlate DMTA results for small strains with those obtained using techniques on larger samples and greater strains (e.g. flexural test bench22. Consequently, this research effectively contributes to the recently published studies and further highlights the utility of DMTA technique for assessing polymer-based foams intended for use in impact protection systems.

Key material characteristics were evaluated, including: i. the foam’s structural stability during deformation (amplitude sweep; mimicking deformation during the impact), ii. its behaviour under varying external stimulus frequencies (frequency sweep; mimicking waring conditions in low frequencies range and moment of the impact with high frequencies range), iii. the temperature range in which the material can function (temperature sweep; mimicking winter/summer conditions). Then, DMTA results were cross-referenced with impact and compression data, hence, providing mechanistic insight that supported and explained trends observed in conventional compression and impact testing.

Additionally, the selected foams offer a broad range of processing techniques and material characteristics, which provide a comprehensive understanding of their suitability for various impact-restistant applications. Methods

Maximum impact force

Quantitative evaluation of the selected foams was performed based on the methodology given in the BS 7971:2014 standard. EC-SBOK1 (Energocontrol, Poland) system was used to determine impact force. In the experiment a standardized cylinder-shaped beater (80 mm in diameter and overall mass of the moving part with the beater of approx. 2506 g) hits the samples placed on the anvil. Specimens in the form of 100 × 100 mm plates and thickness of 10 mm are put on the top of cylindrical-shaped anvil (diameter of 150 mm). The path of freefalling beater released by electromagnet lock is calculated based on velocity measurements (SMM-100 device, Energocontrol, Poland) to achieve 5 J energy of impact. The impact force transmitted to the anvil was measured using piezoelectric sensing device ICP force sensor 100C20 (PCB PIEZOTRONICS, NY, USA). In total 5 samples were tested. Then, the results were averaged and a confidence level of 10% has been assumed.

Compressive strength

Stress-strain characteristics of selected materials were analysed using universal testing machine Z10 (Zwick/Roell, Germany) according to the ISO 3386-1 standard. Samples in the cubic form with a side length of 100 mm and 10 mm of thickness were compressed between flat compressing plates. The test was carried out up to 40% of sample deformation (force measurements with Xforce HP + 5 kN sensor, Zwick/Roell, Germany). In total 5 samples were tested. Then, the results were averaged and a confidence level of 10% has been assumed. Scanning electron microscopy investigation of the polymer-based foam samples has been performed using a Quanta 200 scanning electron microscope (FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) equipped with tungsten source. Pictures presented in the further part of this study exhibit a magnification of 100x.

Dynamic mechanical thermal analysis (DMTA)

A DMA 1 instrument (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) was used for dynamic mechanical thermal analysis. The apparatus was used in compression mode and the samples employed had disc shapes with a diameter of approx. 1 cm and a thickness depending on the commercially available foam specimen - from 4 mm to 9 mm. Tests were carried out in accordance with EN ISO 6721:2019 and measurements used the following three modes: (i) amplitude sweep (amplitude: 0.1–100 μm, frequency: 1 Hz, temperature: 22 °C); (ii) frequency sweep (amplitude: 10 μm, frequency: 0.01–100 Hz, temperature: 22 °C); (iii) temperature sweep (amplitude: 10 μm, frequency: 1 Hz, temperature: −60–100 °C). Based on the analyses performed, the following parameters were determined: the storage modulus (E‘), the loss modulus (E’’), and the tangent of the loss angle (tanδ). One characteristic in certain conditions per sample type was recorded.

Linear regression

The method of least squares was applied to fit the regression line. The coefficient of determination (R²) was calculated to assess the goodness of fit. Due to the limited number of measurement points, only linear regression was tested, as the full data distribution and potential outliers remain unclear. Given the small dataset, the analysis is considered preliminary and illustrative rather than conclusive for quantitative application.

Open-cell foams have a structure where the individual cells are connected, allowing air and moisture to pass through easily33. This makes them more flexible and compressible34. Contrary, closed-cell foams have a more rigid structure, possessing cells that are sealed off from each other, preventing air and moisture from penetrating35. Consequently, closed-cell foams exhibit higher durability and offer superior thermal, as well as moisture insulation36.

Moreover, comparing SEM images taken at the same magnification, it can be easily observed that foam samples based on polyolefins show the largest pore sizes, while PUR 1, PUR 2 and NBR/PVC samples are characterised by smaller ones. This may be the result of both the influence of the polymer matrix itself and the processing (physical37 or chemical38 foaming). Numerical data confirming the above observations have been presented in Table 2.

Additionally, in terms of pore size distribution (standard deviation, maximum, minimum), there is greater inhomogeneity of structure among foams based on polyolefins (PE, PE-co-EVA) than in polyurethane- (PUR 1, PUR 2) and rubber-based foams (NBR/PVC), e.g., standard deviation for PE is on the level of 342 μm, while for PUR 1 it is approx. 90 μm.

Based on the data taken above and the information presented in Table 1, it can be concluded that the various properties of the studied samples of polymer foams originate not only from their different chemical composition (polymer matrix), but also from their varied pore structure, as might be deduced from the SEM images. All these diversities should have an impact on the properties recorded during measurements using the dynamic mechanical thermal analysis (DMTA) technique, the results of which have been thoroughly discussed in the next sections of this article.

Description of polymer-based foams properties with DMTA

DMTA makes it possible to investigate the performance of the polymer foams tested by, de facto, recording the sample behaviour at the molecular level39. This is due to the fact that the parameters studied, such as the storage modulus (E’), the loss modulus (E’’) and the tangent of the loss angle (tanδ), allowing concluding on the stiffness40, elasticity of the foam41, as well as the ranges of sample’s elastic and viscous behaviour42, are also closely linked to the movements of macromolecules within the polymer matrix the foam is composed of43. Simplifying the principles of the DMTA method, it involves recording the delay in the occurrence of stress in the material relative to a given strain, and this shift in time is the result of the duration of macromolecular movements44. Hence, every parameter recorded is dependent on the molecular mobility.

The storage modulus, as the real part of the complex modulus, is indicative of the material’s ability to conserve energy during deformation and has a bearing on its stiffness45. Contrary, the loss modulus, which is the imaginary part of the complex modulus, indicates the material’s ability to damp energy46. Given the viscoelastic characteristics of polymeric materials, it can also be generally stated that the storage and loss moduli indicate polymer behaviour characteristic of, relatively, elastic and viscous bodies39,43,45,46.

Nonetheless, the interpretation of the results must consider both elastic and viscous contributions at a time. Let the following situation serve as an example. An increase in the storage modulus can be observed in the range studied. At the same time, however, without observing a change in the loss modulus, one cannot be sure of the actual behaviour of the polymer material properties in a given range. It is due to the fact that two parallel scenarios are possible: i. E’ > E’’ – elastic characteristics are dominant, ii. E’ < E’’ – viscous behaviour is apparent. Thus, it is only the tangent of the loss angle that provides the quotient of the loss modulus and the storage modulus, combining the two parameters together47. Therefore, it can indicate the dominance of one of the behaviours: elastic or viscous48.

Moving forward, the authors would like to draw attention to the possibility of translating DMTA to performance properties of polymer foams in terms of impact resistant systems22–24,30,31. As previously published studies have shown, the relationship between results recorded at low-strain (DMTA) with those recorded during tests generating higher strain is possible to be observed22–24. It is certainly not possible to directly reflect the high-strain performance characteristics with low-strain DMTA-obtained results. However, some conclusions from the results obtained can be drawn given the different modes of operation of the DMTA device: (i) strain amplitude sweep; (ii) strain frequency sweep; (iii) temperature sweep. As such, DMTA enables the identification of trends, which are directly related to impact performance, making it a useful tool for anticipating material behaviour during high-strain impacts49. Below, a short explanation of impact phenomenon has been provided.

During an impact the foam undergoes a high-strain deformation (theoretically, reproducible by strain amplitude sweep31 and the action of the external stimulus in the form of impact force is very fast (theoretically, slow and fast stimuli can be reproduced by frequency sweep24. In addition, temperature sweep can indicate the behaviour of the foam over a wide temperature range, which might allow its performance to be determined (theoretically, mimicking winter and summer. conditions50.

In the following sections, examples of DMTA characteristics of polyolefin-, polyurethane- and rubber-based commercially available foams have been presented. Their viscoelastic characteristics under conditions of varying amplitude, frequency and temperature were thoroughly described.

Amplitude sweep to investigate strain influence

The results of the analyses performed during the amplitude sweep are shown in Fig. 2. This experiment is intended to indicate possible changes in the foam structure during an impact mimicked by variations in strain31. Although low-strain results do not necessarily entirely reflect high-strain properties, some changes already apparent at this stage may possibly indicate structure alternation in the polymer material. Putting it into the other words, if the stability of the structure is disturbed already at the stage of deformation on the micrometre scale, it is not possible for the foam to remain stable during a high-force impact.

Taking a closer look at the data shown in Fig. 2 two main issues can be observed. Firstly, the curves shown do not have stable characteristics (and this stability is further differentiated by the types of foam tested). The foams were purchased as commercially available products and then tested in terms of their properties. Therefore due to, e.g., presence of the so-called ‘skin’ on the foam surface (the skin on the surface of a foam refers to the denser, often smoother outer layer that forms during the foaming process), the samples may have been subjected to microslip in the grip of the device, resulting in the observed instability of the curves.

Secondly, differences in properties can be evidenced. Each of the measurements carried out indicates whether the foam at a given strain responds in an elastic (E’ > E’’) or viscous (E” > E’) manner51. Although all analysed samples exhibit E‘ > E’’, which indicates the elastic response of the material over the entire range tested (tanδ < 1 in the whole range for all samples), the storage and loss moduli values differ between the analysed foams.

To better visualise the variations discussed, Table 3 gathering the data on storage and loss moduli, as well as tanδ at 10 µm and 80 µm, has been prepared. Therefore, it is clearly apparent that, e.g. E’ at 10 μm for PE is at the level of 0.19 MPa, while for PUR 2 specimen – approx. 2.88 MPa. This might indicate higher stiffness52 of the PUR 2 sample in comparison with the remaining specimens.

Noteworthy, the attention should be also drawn to tanδ. Although PUR 2 shows the highest value of storage modulus, it is the same sample that reveals a high contribution of loss modulus (E’’ at 10 μm of approx. 2.33 MPa) evident with high values of tanδ. Consequently, comparing the tanδ values, PUR 2 at 10 μm shows 0.81, while the remaining samples exhibit tanδ < 0.5. Similar trend is visible for PUR 1 specimen. Therefore, this demonstrates the high contribution of the viscous response of the polyurethane foams42, while comparing with polyolefin- or rubber-based specimens.

What might be easily perceived, the order of the samples is directly indicative of the polymer groups tested. The least affected by the amplitude sweep are the foam samples which polymer matrix is incapable of any interactions (PE)58 or able to form only weak physical interactions (PE-co-EVA; hydrogen bonds)59,60. Even if the hydrogen bonds present in PE-co-EVA break as a result of material deformation, their contribution is relatively small61. Contrary, greater variations in properties may be observed for foams that are based on polyurethanes. Depending on the substrates used during the synthesis of polyurethanes and their di- or multi-functionality, linear62 or branched structures of cross-linked macromolecules63 can be formed. The intricate network, during deformation of the material, can be destroyed and this may be evidenced in recorded DMTA curves64.

Finally, the greatest changes were observed for the NBR/PVC sample, which incorporates the cross-linked rubber. Thus, this sample seems to possess the most complex network structure of the samples tested65. When the intermolecular bonds break during the deformation, it causes the largest observed difference66. In addition, the material is a blend with PVC which should not participate in cross-linked structure. Yet, it may have an effect on the properties of the foam67.

Assuming that the amplitude sweep process might reproduce high-amplitude deformation during impact, this test can provide some significant and valuable information about the behaviour of polymer foam sample while deformed. As explained above, many valuable conclusions concerning polymer foams behaviour could be made based on this simple experiment.

Frequency sweep to mimic slow and fast stimuli

Moving forward, frequency sweep experiment may also be used to predict the behaviour resulting from the foam impact. Theoretically, testing in the low frequency range might correspond to a situation where there is a slow external stimulus24. This range can mimic the wearing of an impact set containing polymer foam cushioning. Consequently, the foam in low frequency range would be expected to cause comfort to the wearer when worn and, hence, should deform and arrange itself in relation to the body. This behaviour might be guaranteed with a high proportion of the material’s viscous response to the given stimuli and, hence, a significant contribution of E’’ (elevated values of tanδ are foreseen)68.

In contrast, the opposite ought to be observed for the moment of impact. Impact is a fast external stimulus, thus, it could be reproduced with high strain frequencies24. In this region, the foam should behave in an elastic way, thereby retaining its shape and protecting the user’s body. Of course, the viscous response of the material is also crucial – the residual energy from the impact, remaining after the initial and main dissipation on the shielding material, should be successfully absorbed by the foam (relatively high contribution of E’ is expected, hence lower values of tanδ)69.

Considering the data presented in Fig. 3, which reveals the results of the frequency sweep of the polymer foam samples tested, the phenomena discussed above can be observed. The E‘, E’’ and tanδ curves highlight changes in the material’s response to a changing constant strain frequency stimulus.

Then, regarding the variations in the tanδ curve, which indicates the ratio of the loss modulus to the storage modulus (e.g. the ratio of the viscous to the elastic response)64, three distinct trends can be observed: (i) evident for PE and PE-co-EVA foams – the tanδ values initially decrease in the mid-frequency range before increasing in the highest frequency range tested (approx. 100 Hz), (ii) evident for NBR/PVC foam – the curve goes up over the entire frequency range 0.1–100 Hz, (iii) evident for PUR 1 and PUR 2 polyurethane foams – tanδ exhibits high values in the low frequency range and decreases in the high frequency range.

This phenomenon might be related to the previously observed differences in SEM images of the samples investigated in this study (Fig. 1). Namely, polyolefin- and rubber-based foams exhibit: (i) wavy pore walls (fine corrugations of the cell walls visible), (ii) angular cells. Contrary, PUR-based materials are characterised by: (i) rigid pore walls, (ii) spherical cells. As it might be deduced, spherical cells can lead to the improved stress distribution within the foam during a deformation. Moreover, the rigidness of the walls is also of a high significance. When the pore walls are loose (the cell wall is not under tension), cell wall stretching can occur during pore deformation (pore walls are characterised by a certain range of mobility).

The observed differences in the behaviour of the samples might origin from the characteristics of the polymer matrices and their glass transition temperatures. Glass transition is a second-order phase transition, which means that it is not accompanied by a measurable energetic heat effect, but can be observed as a sudden change in heat capacity73–75. Depending on the position of the glass transition temperature (Tg) of the polymer, the building material of the foam may undergo a number of transformations76. Below Tg the mobility of macromolecules is extremely limited77. For this reason, the polymer loses its elasticity and becomes brittle78.

According to some literature data, glass transition temperature of PE, depending on the grade, might occur in the range from (−125 °C)-(−10 °C)73,74, while for PE-co-EVA it is from (−40 °C)−20 °C75,76. Contrary, not cross-linked NBR exhibits Tg from (−44 °C)−5 °C77–79. Furthermore, PUR might exhibit various structural differences, hence, its Tg is in a wide range from (−60 °C)−80 °C80–82.

Taking into account the glass transition temperature ranges indicated above, there is no doubt that in the case of PE-co-EVA, PUR 1 and PUR 2 samples the visible peaks are precisely glass transitions. However, regarding the PE-foam sample, the peak visible in the tanδ curve is most likely related to the increased mobility of the polymer macromolecules79 or the onset of the softening process80. Glass transition of PE should be evidenced in lower temperature ranges.

Attention should be also drawn to NBR/PVC sample which is an interesting case. Blends shall be understood in two ways, depending on their morphologies: (i) if the polymer matrices are heterogeneously mixed – two Tg temperatures should be visible81, (ii) if the polymer matrices are homogeneously mixed – one glass transition representing the synergistic effect of both polymer matrices should be evidenced82. Regarding the analysed sample, this is most likely case no. 2, as no more peaks have been evidenced in tanδ curve.

Moving forward, employing temperature sweep mode in DMTA might be helpful in determining the operating temperature range of a certain material. Considering the specimens tested, generally, it can be concluded that significant stiffening of PE, PE-co-EVA and PUR 1 foams occurs below a temperature of approx. −40°C (as indicated in the products’ data sheets). However, PUR 2 and NBR/PVC foams exhibit a significant increase in stiffness that may already be observed at temperatures of, respectively, 0 °C and − 20 °C (data not identical to information in technical sheets). Nonetheless, the designated ranges are based only on data from the DMTA and are indicated by the graphs presented in Fig. 4 (points where a plateauing of the growing tendency of E’ is visible moving towards negative temperatures). Undoubtedly, further investigation of this phenomenon would be highly advised.

Impact testing

The overarching goal of this article is to point out the validity of using the DMTA technique in the analysis and design of impact resistant systems. For this reason, the following subsection of the article will present a comparison of the results of DMTA analysis with test results from the methods indicated by the BS 7971:2014 standard.

As mentioned in the introduction section, BS 7971:2014 is the standard outlining the safety requirements for protective clothing used in the UK and countries that adapted the standard (including Poland). The standard covers the design, performance, and testing of clothing to ensure adequate protection against impact. However, in terms of the mechanical resistance of the developed product, the standard only specifies the testing of impact resistance.

By ranking the samples in order of increasing maximum impact force (MIF) presented in Table 1, a certain series might be obtained: NBR/PVC (1.55 kN) < PUR 1 (1.62 kN) < PUR 2 (1.90 kN) < PE-co-EVA (3.63 kN) < PE (6.04 kN). As previously suggested, the behaviour of the material during impact can be mimicked by sweeping the material with frequency, and more specifically, the high frequency range might be referenced to a fast and sudden external stimulus such as impact24. Hence, by ordering the specimens in ascending order of tanδ at 100 Hz (high frequency), the exact inverse of the previously observed series is achieved: PE (0.30) < PE-co-EVA (0.31) < PUR 2 (0.36) < PUR 1 (0.39) < NBR/PVC (0.62). Below is a proposed explanation of the observed relationship.

The impact test consists of hitting the specimen with a striker and causing the foam to break.

According to the observations made using DMTA technique, the specimens were ranked as follows: PE is the sample with the highest observed E’ relative to E’' (low tanδ value), while the NBR/PVC sample shows the highest observed E’' relative to E’ (higher tanδ value). Thus, samples at the beginning of the row should tend to retain their shape during impact (higher value of E’ responsible for the elastic bahviour; more force is needed to damage the specimen), while samples at the end of the row will deform more easily (higher balue of E’' responsible for viscous behaviour; less force is needed to damage the specimen).

So far, although the samples have been arranged in the correct order of value, this phenomenon has only been discussed in qualitative terms. However, to use the method quantitatively, the observed relationship must be described in such a way that the analysed values are dependent on a mathematical function. Therefore, a further analysis was carried out to verify the possibility of quantitative interpretation of the relationship described above (Fig. 6a). Yet, authors would like to emphasise that due to the small population of samples selected for the study, it is only illustrative. Moreover, in view of such a small number of samples with different characteristics and origin (PE, PUR, NBR), it is not possible to unambiguously determine the potential shape of the trend of the relationship studied. Therefore, a simple linear function was chosen for the initial evaluation of the correlation possibilities.

Undeniably, the result of the analysis performed does not indicate that the relationship being discussed can be assessed quantitatively (R2 < 0.40). As a consequence, this relationship can only be treated in a qualitative way. Nonetheless, further work on a larger sample population should be carried out in the future to unequivocally determine the quantitative feasibility of using the DMTA technique in conjunction with impact tests.

Additionally, some more analyses based on this study, depicting the possibilities of DMTA technique quantitative use not discussed in the main body of the article, have been presented in the Supplementary file and presented in Fig. S1-S3.

Compression testing

Additionally, due to the similar test set-up in case of DMTA (compression), the authors would also like to refer to the compressive strength values of the tested foams. Both DMTA in compression mode and conventional compression strength tests involve applying a compressive force to a material, allowing direct mechanical evaluation of its deformation response. While compression strength tests measure the material’s resistance to a single, often destructive load, DMTA allows for detailed analysis of viscoelastic properties under varying strain amplitudes. Despite differences in testing dynamics, theoretically, the values from DMTA might be correlated with compressive strength, offering complementary insight into stiffness and energy dissipation capacity.

Operating temperature range

Finally, a crucial aspect that can be verified using the DMTA technique is the temperature range of use. As previously shown, it is possible to observe changes in dynamic moduli values over a wide temperature range, resulting in the possibility of detecting, e.g., potential stiffening of the material at lower temperatures, identifying points of glass transition or changes in macromolecular mobility of a polymer matrix.

At lower temperatures, the foam typically exhibits stiffening, as the polymer chains become less mobile, leading to a significant increase in stiffness87. This stiffening effect is an important factor when defining the operating temperature range, as it can limit the foam’s ability to deform or absorb energy effectively at low temperatures88. As the temperature increases, the material transitions through the glass transition region, where its behaviour becomes more flexible87. The upper limit of the operating temperature range is generally identified where the foam begins to soften or degrade, losing its structural integrity89.

Consequently, the temperature range in which the foam exhibits a balance of sufficient stiffness at higher temperatures and flexibility at lower temperatures defines the material’s effective operating range. This is crucial for ensuring that the foam maintains optimal mechanical performance, even under thermal stress, across the intended application temperature range.

Possibility of analysing the complex modulus E*

The complex modulus (E*) is a key parameter for evaluating a material’s resistance to deformation under oscillatory loading. It comprises two components: the storage modulus (E′), which reflects the elastic or “stored” energy, and the loss modulus (E″), which accounts for the viscous or “dissipated” energy. Together, these components capture the viscoelastic behaviour of a material, offering insights into both its stiffness and damping characteristics across varying amplitudes, temperatures, and frequencies. Bianchi et al.90 previously employed a comprehensive analysis of the complex modulus to characterize the properties of polymer foams, demonstrating its effectiveness as a tool in materials research. Following this approach, the present work also utilized changes in E* to investigate the behaviour of polymer foams. Specifically, complex modulus E* was evaluated at an amplitude of 80 μm (amplitude sweep) and a frequency of 100 Hz (frequency sweep), and plotted against (i) average cell size, (ii) maximum impact force, and (iii) compressive stress at 40% deformation. However, attempts to establish clear correlations between these parameters and E* were not successful. Further details have been provided in the Supplementary file and presented in Fig. S4-S7.

Summary of cross-referencing DMTA data

This section has demonstrated the potential for qualitative correlation between polymer foam data obtained via the DMTA technique and compression and impact experiments. However, the possibility of relating the data in a quantitative manner remains incomplete. Although no quantitative relationships could be identified between the DMTA technique and other methods, future research in this area is highly advised. Measuring a much larger population of samples might be necessary, as can be separating the mathematical models depending on the nature of the polymer of which the foam is composed.

Conclusions

This study successfully and clearly presented the applicability of dynamic mechanical thermal (DMTA) as a complementary technique in the investigation of the polymer-based foams for the potential use in impact resistant protection systems by determining: i. structure stability during deformation (amplitude sweep), ii. behaviour of the specimen in varying frequency of external stimuli (frequency sweep), iii. temperature range of application (temperature sweep). Therefore, DMTA provided a valuable insight into the foam properties in the mimicked event of impact. The possibility of qualitative relationship of the DMTA-derived results and the compression, as well as impact resistance has been successfully indicated. Most prominent example is the fact that by putting the specimens in ascending order of tanδ at 100 Hz, the following series is achieved: PE (0.30) < PE-co-EVA (0.31) < PUR 2 (0.36) < PUR 1 (0.39) < NBR/PVC (0.62) which is directly reciprocal to ranking the samples in order of increasing maximum impact force (MIF): NBR/PVC (1.55 kN) < PUR 1 (1.62 kN) < PUR 2 (1.90 kN) < PE-co-EVA (3.63 kN) < PE (6.04 kN). However, no reliable quantitative mathematical model has been established.

Furthermore, the characteristics that the polymer foam should exhibit to perform well in energy-absorbing applications have been identified. To provide high compressive strength, significant contributions from both the storage and loss moduli are required. This ensures adequate energy dissipation while maintaining the structure’s integrity e.g., more viscous rubber foam – CS of approx. 86 kPa (tanδ = 0.44), rigid polyolefin foam – CS at the level of 36 kPa (tanδ = 0.15). Moreover, in the low frequency range, the foam should exhibit an increased proportion of loss modulus, which is responsible for viscous behaviour, to ensure deformability and a comfortable fit. Contrary, in the high-frequency range, the foam should exhibit an increased proportion of the behavioural modulus which is responsible for the elastic response to an external stimulus, e.g. MIF for NBR/PVC sample at the level of 1.55 kN with tanδ at 100 Hz of 0.62 (lower E’’ contribution), MIF for PE specimen of approx. 6.04 kN with tanδ at 100 Hz of 0.30 (higher E’ contribution). However, the foam’s performance should undoubtedly be fitted to the final application and operating conditions.

Future perspectives and outlook

While the current study demonstrates the value of dynamic mechanical thermal analysis (DMTA) as a complementary tool in the assessment of polymer-based foams for protective applications, several avenues remain open for further investigation. To transition from qualitative to more quantitative use of DMTA in the design and evaluation of energy-absorbing materials, future research should focus on expanding the dataset through: (i) testing a broader range of foam types of defined properties, (ii) by increasing sample populations. This would allow for improved statistical validation and the establishment of more robust relationships between DMTA-derived results and different techniques.

Another interesting direction is potential for adapting DMTA protocols to more closely replicate application-specific conditions, including higher strain rates, multi-axial stress states, or environmental aging effects (e.g., humidity, UV exposure). This would further bridge the gap between laboratory-scale testing and practical implementation.

Finally, continued refinement of DMTA-based protocols may lead to its establishment as a reliable pre-screening method during the early stages of material selection and design for protective equipment. As materials science progresses, the combination of advanced testing techniques and data-driven modelling might be essential in developing next-generation foams tailored for specific protective applications, including sports gear, automotive safety systems, and personal protective equipment.
