Comparative analysis of turkey biometrics in Mexican, Spanish, and Italian populations
Authors: José Ignacio Salgado Pardo, Antonio González Ariza, Juan Vicente Delgado Bermejo, Carmen Rivas López, José Manuel León Jurado, Stefano Paolo Marelli, Silvia Cerolini, Luisa Zaniboni, Alejandro Vargas Cornejo, Marina Winter Dendena, Manuel Barrientos Morales, Patricia Cervantes Acosta, Antonio Hernández Beltrán, María Esperanza Camacho Vallejo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The present work aims to define the morphological variability of turkey populations from Italy (Brianzolo and Nero d’Italia), Spain (Andalusian), and Mexico (Guajolote from Hidalgo and Veracruz). To this end, a set of 21 body measurements, 8 body indices, and 16 qualitative traits were collected from 255 adult turkeys. A Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted separately in each sex to describe the association between the morphological variables. Furthermore, a discriminant canonical analysis was conducted for each sex to determine genotype differences. Therefore, a set of 17 and 20 discriminant items were identified for males and females, respectively. These variables included body measurements, biometric indices, and qualitative traits. The cross-validation test revealed a high accuracy of the discriminant model, which was slightly higher in males (97.6 %) than in females (96.2 %). Furthermore, the Euclidean distances between the observations indicated a close relationship between the Andalusian turkey and Mexican guajolote from Hidalgo. This work provides new insights into the biometric diversity of the domestic turkey, as most of the genotypes under study had not yet been characterized in an international context. Moreover, the morphological differences between indigenous turkeys were previously thought to be unnoticeable by most researchers. This would allow breeders’ associations and public institutions to define measurement protocols, given the current lack of consistent guidelines worldwide. Furthermore, the findings of the present study seem to depict the historic expansion of the domestic turkey from Mexico to Europe through Andalusia.

Introduction

From domestication, which typically occurs at a specific date and place, species diverge over time and through migration (Sponenberg et al., 2018). Diversification of domestic species is primarily the result of accidental reproductive practices such as isolation and genetic drift (Aslam et al., 2012), as well as the exposure to new environments and human needs (Sponenberg et al., 2019). Conversely, a closer proximity to the domestication origin implies a longer reproductive exposure to wild ancestor while maintaining an environment to which the species is adapted (Aslam et al., 2012). This may explain why modern breeds withdrawing from the area of domestication are more genetically and phenotypically diverse than those populations originating from the domestication area (Sponenberg et al., 2018). However, little phenotypic variability is found in the domestic turkey compared to other poultry species (Aslam et al., 2012). This was first attached to the relatively recent domestication, which most likely occurred between 200 BC – 700 AD in the Mexican highlands (Padilla-Jacobo et al., 2018). Another possible reason is the sustained crossbreeding with wild ancestor, as evidenced by archaeological remains (Thornton and Emery, 2017). Lastly, this lack of phenotypic variety could be a consequence of the quick expansion of the species once it reached Europe (Crawford, 1992). For centuries, the Spanish fleet transported turkeys from Central America to Europe (Bartosiewicz, 2020; Crawford, 1992; De Grossi Mazzorin and Epifani, 2016). The main departure points were central Mexico (the port of Veracruz) and some of the Caribbean islands, bound for Seville (Martínez et al., 2012; Reitz et al., 2016). This is evidenced in the high genetic closeness between the indigenous Mexican and southern Spanish (Andalusian) landraces (Canales Vergara et al., 2019). Similarly, Andalusian turkeys have been closely associated with other Mediterranean populations, especially those from Italy (Canales et al., 2023). This is a consequence of the intense bird trade between the two countries, particularly involving southern Spain (Andalusia), as evidenced by historical records (Bahuchet, 2021; De Grossi Mazzorin and Epifani, 2016; Kaps, 2023). Additionally, this connection could be derived from the Spanish domination of northern Italy (Lombardy) from 1539, shortly after the first turkeys arrived in Europe (Crawford, 1992).

Several molecular genetic studies have recently addressed the genetic diversity of Mexican, Spanish, and Italian turkeys (Canales et al., 2023; Canales Vergara et al., 2019; Ferrari et al., 2024; Saleh et al., 2025; Strillacci et al., 2020). However, the species’ phenotypic diversity has barely been explored. Some morphological studies have been carried out individually for different turkey populations of Europe, Africa, and Latin America (Adeyemi and Oseni, 2018; Aubin et al., 2017; Cigarroa-Vázquez et al., 2013; Jáuregui Jiménez and Cabrera Galdámez, 2021; Kabalin et al., 2012; Laryea, 2023; Ortíz Mayorga and Osorno Mejía, 2015). Other authors have compared indigenous genotypes with international and commercial strains (Adeoye et al., 2017; Dudusola et al., 2020; Yakubu et al., 2012). However, only two studies have compared turkey populations from different, yet still geographically close, areas. One was conducted in Italy (Marelli et al., 2022) while the other was carried out in Mexico (Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022). Hence, no studies have been conducted on the morphometric diversity comparing turkey landraces worldwide. In addition, none of the aforementioned studies have followed a homogeneous protocol, as the FAO’s guidelines for the morphometric characterization of animal genetic resources do not include turkeys (FAO, 2008).

Therefore, the present work aims to describe the morphological and biometric variability in indigenous turkey populations from Mexico, Spain, and Italy. This would enable the morphological differences between landraces to be determined and distinguished. Then, these explanatory traits could be used to define the breed’s standards, as well as to establish a specific protocol for other morphometric characterization studies, since there are still no specific guidelines on the species. Lastly, determining the similarities and dissimilarities between these populations would also contribute to the understanding of the expansion of the domestic turkey from Central America to Europe through Andalusia (southern Spain).

Material and methods

Institutional animal care and use committee statement

The present research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and within the scope of European Union legislation (Directive 2010/63/EU of 22 September 2010) and its incorporation into Spanish law (Royal Decree-Law 53/2013). Animals received humane care in compliance with the national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory and farm animals in research. In accordance with Royal Decree Law 53/2013, the study protocol was submitted to the University of Córdoba's (Spain) legally constituted Ethics Committee of Animal Experimentation, which deemed the study exempt from review as it did not comply with animal experimentation legislation.

Animal origin

In the present study, 255 adult turkeys (>20 weeks of age), including five genotypes from three countries, were measured. On one hand, 166 Andalusian turkeys (82 toms and 84 hens) were measured. The birds included in the study belonged to smallholders of the Andalusian Turkey Breeders’ Association spread throughout Andalusia. Additionally, some birds belonged to the conservation nucleus of the breed of the Agropecuary Provincial Centre of the ‘Diputación de Córdoba’ as well. Therefore, the present measurement procedure sought to maximize the representativeness of the population across farms. All birds were kept in outdoor pens and raised in backyards and foraging regimes. On the other hand, the Mexican landraces studied included 31 turkeys from the state of Hidalgo (15 toms and 16 hens) and 28 turkeys from the state of Veracruz (13 toms and 15 hens). In this case, the birds belonged exclusively to smallholders, whose farms are also characterized by an outdoor rearing based on foraging and backyard management. Lastly, the Italian breeds included were the ‘Brianzolo’ (12 toms and 8 hens) and ‘Nero d’Italia’ (3 toms and 7 hens). The Italian birds belonged exclusively to the conservation nucleus of the Poultry Unit of the University of Milan’s Animal Production Center (Lodi, Italy). In this case, the flock is kept in close pens and serves as a reproductive nucleus, providing live birds to support breeders. The availability of live birds was limited by the censuses of the populations under study. Of the genotypes in the comparison, the Brianzolo and Nero d’Italia turkeys were the only ones with known risk status and proper conservancy practices (TuBAvI, 2024). According to the FAO risk status categories, the Brianzolo and Nero d’Italia turkeys are considered to be in a 'Critical' conservation state (FAO, 2025). On the other hand, although there is no official census data on the Andalusian or the two Mexican turkey breeds, they are most likely endangered.

All animals were measured following the same protocol, which was particularly designed for this experiment. This included both quantitative (body measurements and biometric indices) and qualitative (morpho-structural and cutaneous, also called ‘phaneroptic’) variables. The present measuring protocol included 21 body measurements collected from the FAO’s guidelines (FAO, 2008) and related literature on morphological turkey characterization (Adeyemi and Oseni, 2018; Cigarroa-Vázquez et al., 2013; Dudusola et al., 2020; Kabalin et al., 2012; Marelli et al., 2022; Süzer et al., 2018). Data analysis

The discriminant canonical analysis was chosen as the statistical approach to establish differences and similarities between the genotypes (categories). Data analysis was carried out through the statistical software IBM® SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) for the correlation and discriminant analyses. The software Microsoft® Excel 2018 was used for data treatment, graphics editing, and generating maps using its ‘3D Maps’ feature. Since the domestic turkey is such a dimorphic species, all the analyses were carried out separately for each sex, as indicated in the literature (Ajayi et al., 2012).

Before the discriminant canonical analysis, a correlation analysis between weight and the body measurements was carried out separately for each sex. This analysis aimed to enhance the understanding of the relationship between zoometric variables in turkeys, which is particularly important for understudied populations such as indigenous genotypes.

Subsequently, a discriminant canonical analysis was carried out separately for each sex. Since the present study aims to define differences across turkey populations, the genotype was considered as the dependent variable in the analysis. Therefore, each landrace constituted a separate category in the analysis: Andalusian, Brianzolo, Nero d’Italia, Hidalgo, and Veracrucian turkeys. On the other hand, the morphological variables (21 body measurements, 8 biometric indices, and 16 qualitative traits) performed as explanatory or independent variables. For the qualitative variables, each categorical possibility of the variable was transcribed into binary coding (yes/no).

The present analysis employed the stepwise inclusion criterion approach to select the variables that significantly contributed to the discriminant model. This procedure evaluates, in separate steps, the contribution of including or removing variables to the overall explanation of variance. The analysis stops once no greater performance is estimated by including or removing variables. This method combines Snedecor’s F and Wilk’s lambda parameters to determine the degree of contribution of each independent variable to the explanatory potential of the overall variance. In Snedecor’s F parameter, the deviation of the mean for each category is compared with the overall average for each explanatory variable. Therefore, Snedecor’s F explains group homogeneity and highlights the suitability of each variable for discriminating between groups. Higher F values would suggest that the means of the groups are significantly different and that the variable has great discriminant ability. On the other hand, Wilk’s lambda uses the sum of squares within groups with the variance in the overall observations (without groups). Therefore, reduced values of Wilk’s lambda suggest a greater proportion of variance is explained between groups and, consequently, a variable has a higher discriminant ability.

The performance of the discriminant model may be overinflated due to multicollinearity between the explanatory variables. Multicollinearity occurs when a variable’s contribution to explaining the overall variance is already covered by other variables, making it redundant. To avoid redundant variables, the stepwise procedure considered the tolerance value of each variable as an inclusion criterion for the discriminant model. With respect to a variable, tolerance can be defined as the proportion of ‘unique’ variance, in other words, that is not provided by others. It is computed according to the following formulae:Tolerance(T)=1−Ri2were Ri2 is the multiple correlation coefficient, which expresses the percentage of an item’s variability provided by the other variables. Therefore, the higher the tolerance value, the greater the contribution of ‘unique’ variance to the model.

The assumption of equal covariance matrices was tested through the Pillai’s trace criterion. The discriminant routine of the software employed includes the Box-M procedure to test this assumption. However, this method is highly sensitive to the lack of multivariate normality and unequal sample sizes. Therefore, the Pillai’s trace criterion stands as the most appropriate alternative (González Ariza et al., 2021). To compute this, the multivariate routine of the general linear model of SPSS software was carried out.

The Euclidean distances between groups’ centroids were employed to describe the clustering pattern of observations according to their genotype. The Euclidean distances were converted into a dendrogram through the underweighted pair-group method arithmetic averages (UPGMA) from the Universität Rovira i Virgili (URV), and figures were generated using the software MEGA X 10.0.5 (Institute of Molecular Evolutionary Genetics, The Pennsylvania State University, State College, PA, United States).

Finally, model reliability was tested through the cross-validation method. This method assesses the percentage of correct assignment of observations to their real categories based on their explanatory attributes. Therefore, the leave-one-out cross-validation test aims to achieve at least 25 % higher accuracy than chance. Press’s Q statistic can support the results, since it can be employed to evaluate the discriminating power of the cross-validation function (González Ariza et al., 2021). Press’s Q statistic is computed through the following formula:Press′sQ=[n−(nK)]2N(K−1)where N is the number of observations; n is the number of correctly assignations of observations; and K is the number of categories (in this case the breeds of the turkeys).

Pearson’s correlation matrices for live weight and body measurements are represented in Table 3, Table 4 for males and females, respectively. Moderate (0.300 – 0.500) to strong (>0.500) significant correlations (p < 0.01) are reported in both sexes, with females showing slightly stronger positive and negative correlations than males. Furthermore, the degree of correlation and significance between some variables was also influenced by sex. Referring to the qualitative traits, the turkeys from Andalusia, Hidalgo, and Veracruz exhibited a wide range of colors in their eyes, beak, skin, and ornamental structures. By contrast, the Italian breeds exhibited greater homogeneity with regard to qualitative variables. The Brianzolo females were the only birds in the comparison that did not exhibit minor caruncles, which was a major breed discriminator. Similarly, some Andalusian and Hidalgo hens exhibited no major caruncles, even though this trait was not significant enough to be included in the discriminant model. The Italian turkeys showed some interesting eye colors. For example, some Nero d’Italia birds had grey-blueish eyes, while some Brianzolo turkeys displayed amber (grey-yellow) eyes. The Spanish and Mexican populations showed a wide variety of feather colors, whereas the Italian turkeys had only one plumage pattern each.

Discussion

According to the FAO's population risk status grading, the Brianzolo and Nero d’Italia turkeys are classified as 'Critical' (FAO, 2025). On the other hand, the status of the Andalusian and Mexican populations is currently unknown, but it is likely to be critical as well (Arando et al., 2021; Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022). In this context, the phenotypic characterization is a crucial step in the genetic management of domestic animal populations (FAO, 2008). This may explain the growing interest in morphometric studies of several endangered turkey breeds worldwide in recent years (Adeyemi and Oseni, 2018; Jáuregui Jiménez and Cabrera Galdámez, 2021; Laryea, 2023; Marelli et al., 2022; Ríos Utrera et al., 2016). However, few studies have compared different turkey populations worldwide, and thus, there is scarce information on the species’ biometric diversity.

Examining the correlation matrices reveals a general trend of positive associations between body weight and skeletal variables in both sexes. This is consistent with previous studies (Adeoye et al., 2017; Aubin et al., 2017; Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022; Ríos Utrera et al., 2016), which suggests that these variables may contribute to the overall size of birds. Which is more, many of the aforementioned body measurements have been shown to accurately predict turkeys’ body weight in regression models (Adeoye et al., 2018). Nevertheless, a relatively low correlation was observed between weight and breast circumference in males (0.586), compared to that reported in other studies. Strong positive correlations (>0.800) have been reported in Mexican (Cigarroa-Vázquez et al., 2013; Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022), Guatemalan (Jáuregui Jiménez and Cabrera Galdámez, 2021), Nigerian (Ajayi et al., 2012), and commercial (Adeoye and Oladepo, 2018) toms. However, the moderate correlation obtained in the present study is consistent with other reports of Mexican and Cameroonian toms (Aubin et al., 2017; Ríos Utrera et al., 2016). This could be indicative of the limited genetic selection for breast development in the breeds under study. Marelli et al. (2022) came to the same conclusion regarding the Nero d’Italia and Brianzolo turkeys, which performed poorly in a commercial body condition grading based on breast development. Therefore, an increase in weight would not necessarily result in an increase in breast circumference in unselected populations. In this regard, Adeoye et al. (2018) identified body length and thigh length as the main descriptors of live weight in indigenous Nigerian turkeys, alongside breast circumference.

A strong effect of sex on the correlation matrices was also observed. For example, females tend to exhibit stronger (positive or negative) correlations than males, which is contrary to what is observed in similar studies (Ajayi et al., 2012; Jáuregui Jiménez and Cabrera Galdámez, 2021; Laryea, 2023). Furthermore, some variables were only significantly correlated with others in one sex. This applies to neck length and snood’s length and width in males, or skull and body lengths in females. Such differences could be due to the strong influence of sexual dimorphism on morphometric traits in the species (Ajayi et al., 2012; Aubin et al., 2017; Marelli et al., 2022; Ríos Utrera et al., 2016). Moreover, the influence of sex on the correlation matrix of morphological traits has also been described in the species (Estrada Mora, 2007; Ogah, 2011). The turkey is a highly dimorphic species, in which morphological traits influence male sexual success, due to hen appeal and dominance establishment (Ajayi et al., 2012). For example, the significance of snood’s variables could only have been obtained in males because of its high development in toms, as it plays a crucial sexual role (Pointer et al., 2013). On the other hand, females’ body development is conditioned to the sexual maturation age and the development of the reproductive organs, which might explain the correlations observed in some areas where males are absent (Blondel et al., 2002). This supports the suitability of conducting the analyses separately for each sex.

The Pillai’s trace criterion validated the discriminant approach, and the models in both sexes reported viability and a high accuracy of discrimination. Therefore, despite being phylogenetically close, the genotypes under study can be considered morphologically different and distinguishable (Canales et al., 2023; Ferrari et al., 2024; Saleh et al., 2025). This was achieved by combining body measurements, biometric indices, and qualitative traits, which varied significantly across genotypes. Therefore, combining these types of variables is recommended for breed traceability analyses. This is not reflected in the FAO’s guidelines (FAO, 2008) and has only been proposed in a few studies (Marelli et al., 2022; Ríos Utrera et al., 2016). Although the biometric indices were computed from body measurements that were already included in the analysis, multicollinearity was not anticipated. The stepwise inclusion procedure only collected highly explanatory variables that provided a unique, non-redundant contribution to the explanatory potential. For example, live weight, body length, breast circumference, and shank width are highly discriminant variables in turkey breed traceability analyses (Adeyemi and Oseni, 2018; Marelli et al., 2022; Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022; Yakubu et al., 2012). However, they were not included in the current models by themselves, but rather as part of biometric indices. In this regard, biometric indices provide a more comprehensive explanation of variance than isolated linear measurements, while reducing the number of variables and simplifying the model (Jáuregui Jiménez and Cabrera Galdámez, 2021).

The head region was important in the discriminant analysis because a large number of variables were found to make a substantial contribution to the model. However, head measurements are not so commonly included in morphometric studies that compare turkey genotypes (Adeyemi and Oseni, 2018; Marelli et al., 2022; Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022; Yakubu et al., 2012). In this regard, birds have the most specialized skulls among vertebrates and can exhibit a variety of shapes and sizes (Süzer et al., 2018). The head is a polymorphic region involved in birds’ adaptability, due to their implications on the seek and capture of food, vocalization, and detection of predators (González Ariza et al., 2021). The head is therefore considered a good indicator of the evolutionary history and functional morphology of populations (Korkmazcan et al., 2025).

Turkey’s snood provided important information on breed diagnosis as well, especially in males. In this regard, limited studies have included snood length or width in breed comparison, but differences have been reported between the varieties of the indigenous Nigerian turkey (Adeyemi and Oseni, 2018). However, the size of the snood should be cautiously interpreted because of its susceptibility to external influences. The snood or ‘frontal process’ of the turkey is a fleshy head ornamentation whose size changes with its distension or retraction (Buchholz et al., 2004). It plays a crucial role in sexual appeal and mating behaviour in males (Ajayi et al., 2012), and is also used as an indicator of health and animal welfare, as well as genetic resistance to disease (Buchholz et al., 2004). Furthermore, its size can be affected by fearful and stressful stimuli as well (Estrada Mora, 2007). Therefore, the observed differences in genotypes could have been partially influenced by flock effects affecting snood metrics, as well as by other genetic factors, such as tolerance of human handling.

In line with previous literature, the leg was an important region in the discriminant model (Adeyemi and Oseni, 2018; Marelli et al., 2022; Yakubu et al., 2012). This observed diversity could be due to the key role of leg conformation in adapting to grazing systems, particularly in indigenous genotypes (Marelli et al., 2022). Furthermore, many of the breeds included have historically travelled long distances from the countryside to cities to be sold at Christmas (Fernández et al., 2009; Martin, 2009). Attending to the mean values by genotypes, the Nero d’Italia and Andalusian turkeys showed the largest leg in the comparison. However, when the leg biometric indices of hens were considered, the Andalusian turkey exhibited the greatest proportional leg development. For example, the tarsus ratio combines shank depth and width, and therefore, higher values are achieved with increasing depth or decreasing width. Proportionally thinner tarsus were observed in the Veracrucian population, and proportionally thicker in Andalusian birds. Similarly, long-leggedness reflects the length of the shank proportionally to the length of the body. Results suggest proportionally longer shanks in Hidalgo and Andalusian females, while the shortest were those of the Brianzolo hens.

Body measurements related to the bird’s size contributed to the discriminant analysis as well. Variability in wingspan and neck length has been observed across turkey genotypes (Ogah, 2011; Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022), and back length in chickens (González Ariza et al., 2021). In addition, the biometric indices related to size (stokiness and body mass index) also contributed with explanatory variance in the analysis conducted in males. For example, the stockiness index describes breast development in proportion to the body length. Therefore, the Nero d’Italia, Hidalgo, and Andalusian toms exhibited a greater proportional development of the breast compared to the Brianzolo and Veracrucian birds. On the other hand, body mass index describes the weight of the bird in proportion to the length of the body. Therefore, the Andalusian toms were the most compact in the comparison, as they were the heaviest birds (8.68 kg), yet had a similar body length (± 69 cm) to the lightest toms, which were the Brianzolo (5.01 kg). Thus, the Brianzolo is the lightest and smallest while the most stylized breed in the comparison. On the other hand, the Andalusian, Nero d’Italia, and Hidalgo turkeys were the largest birds in the comparison.

The aforementioned breed differences in morphostructure derive from the complex interactions between culture, migration routes, and adaptations that each population has undergone (Ferrari et al., 2024). Every animal landrace has been exposed to a specific ecosystem, food availability, and management practices for centuries, which have influenced their body conformation and harmony (Dudusola et al., 2020; Yakubu et al., 2012). For example, the Brianzolo turkey is a genotype originating from the hilly Brianza region of the pre-Alpine Lombardy (Ferrari et al., 2024). Its reduced size and stylized body suggest its high adaptation to the mountainous landscape (Macciotta et al., 2002). Similarly, turkeys from the Mexican highlands are known to be larger and heavier than those from Yucatan (Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022). These differences were attached to the different ecosystems each population faces (semi-arid versus tropical) and to the natural barriers posed by the Sierra Madre mountains, which isolate these populations (Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022).

In both sexes, the colors of the eye, beak, and shank were the most discriminant qualitative variables. These traits are popular in morphometric studies due to their high variability across populations of many poultry species (Corbett et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2024; Perini et al., 2024), and their proven ability to discriminate between landraces (González Ariza et al., 2021; Marelli et al., 2022). The Andalusian and Mexican turkeys exhibited a wide variety of colors, similar to other Mexican (Estrada Mora, 2007; Ríos Utrera et al., 2016) and Nicaraguan (Ortíz Mayorga and Osorno Mejía, 2015) indigenous turkeys. Therefore, the discriminant performance of color traits would be reduced in these populations, so their inclusion in the breed standards would not be advisable. Conversely, the Italian landraces exhibited a homogeneous coloring, most likely because these attributes are included in their official genetic programs (TuBAvI, 2024). Furthermore, the color of the ornamental structures on the head and neck varied significantly across genotypes, despite these traits being less commonly included in breed comparison studies. In this regard, color traits vary across populations due to their implications for the population’s adaptation to a particular environment (for camouflage or thermoregulation) or due to selection processes based on the preferences of specific breeders (FAO, 2008). However, the color of the structures of the head and neck plays a communicative role and is highly influenced by external factors, similarly to the snood’s metrics (Aubin et al., 2017).

Feather colors were also discriminant in females, especially for the red and white plumages. Once again, the Italian population displayed a single plumage color each, whereas the Spanish and Mexican populations exhibited a wide variety of plumage patterns. This is consistent with the existing literature, as almost every description of indigenous Mexican turkeys reports a wide range of feather colors (Cigarroa-Vázquez et al., 2013; Estrada Mora, 2007; López-Zavala et al., 2008; Ríos Utrera et al., 2016). In this regard, using feather color as a breed classification criterion is controversial because it does not indicate genetically homogeneous clusters of turkeys (Corso et al., 2017). It is believed that the original fifteenth-century domestic turkey from Mexico had only black feathers (Crawford, 1992; Fothergill, 2014). However, the presence of white-feathered turkeys has been registered little after the arrival of the turkey in Europe (Bartosiewicz, 2020; De Grossi Mazzorin and Epifani, 2016; Fothergill, 2014). Some authors have linked greater plumage variety to enhanced genetic diversity (Ríos Utrera et al., 2016). This is consistent with the findings of Canales et al. (2023), who identified the Mexican and Andalusian populations as the most genetically diverse turkey breeds. However, different feather colouring could be more indicative of homozygosity, since feather color is determined by just a few genes that exhibit strong dominance and epistatic effects (Estrada Mora, 2007). Nevertheless, other indigenous genotypes have also been described as polychromatic in Italy (Saleh et al., 2025), Croatia (Janječić and Mužic, 2007; Kabalin et al., 2012), Cameroon (Aubin et al., 2017), Guatemala (Jáuregui Jiménez and Cabrera Galdámez, 2021), and Nicaragua (Ortíz Mayorga and Osorno Mejía, 2015).

Examining the spatial representation plots and dendrograms reveals different clustering patterns of breeds across sexes. For males, the model identifies two distinct clusters: one consisting of Italian turkeys and the other consisting of Spanish-Mexican birds. On the other hand, the female model exhibits a clustering pattern that more closely resembles the findings of molecular genetic studies. The strong association between the Andalusian and Mexican turkeys is well documented in the literature (Canales et al., 2023), as is the genetic closeness of the Nero d’Italia turkeys to indigenous Mexican landraces (Ferrari et al., 2024). Furthermore, a similar disposition pattern of the Brianzolo turkey is described in Saleh et al. (2025) as a differentiated turkey, but at the same time, close to Nero d’Italia. Additionally, the model generated for males shows a greater overlap of observations than the model for females, which displays a greater distance between the centroids. Therefore, it is suggested that the female model would better reflect the evolutionary history of the domestic turkey.

Both Italian populations are morphologically close, particularly in the male plot. These turkeys are originally from the same region in northern Italy (Lombardia), and their genetic and morphometric similarities have been previously described (Ferrari et al., 2024; Marelli et al., 2022; Saleh et al., 2025). By contrast, the Mexican populations behaved as two different populations, showing greater separation than the Italian breeds. However, according to the DAD-IS list of breeds (FAO, 2025), no separate varieties are described within the Mexican indigenous ‘Guajolote’. Nevertheless, the wide morphometric variability of the indigenous Mexican turkey is well documented, even when comparing birds from neighbouring states are compared (López-Zavala et al., 2008; Ríos Utrera et al., 2016). Furthermore, when populations from the West and East are compared, both genetic (Strillacci et al., 2020) and morphometric (Portillo-Salgado et al., 2022) differences become more evident. Therefore, further studies including birds from a wider range of regions are recommended to identify potential varieties within the indigenous Mexican guajolote.

Lastly, the Andalusian and the modern Mexican turkeys are genetically very similar to the archaeological remains of the primitive domesticated turkey (Canales Vergara et al., 2019). Despite their great geographical distance and isolation, the Andalusian and Hidalgo populations exhibited close morphometric similarities in the models generated for both sexes. It is therefore proposed that this cluster represents an ancient group of animals that exhibits a primitive morphostructure. These breeds are large in size, with legs that are highly proportional to their bodies, as described for their wild ancestors (Stover et al., 2018). Furthermore, the Andalusian and Hidalgo populations, possibly alongside Nero d’Italia turkeys, could represent the living heritage of the original Mexican turkey without the introgression of the wild eastern subspecies (M. gallopavo sylvestris), the extinction of which was questioned by Crawford (1992).

Therefore, the present study proves that combining linear measurements, biometric indices, and qualitative traits allow the biometric differentiation of genetically related populations. These studies may be conducted separately to each sex, due to the strong influence of sexual dimorphism. In one hand, the Brianzolo turkey exhibited the lightest and most stylized morphology in the comparison, while the Andalusian, Hidalgo, and Nero d’Italia turkeys were the heaviest and largest-sized birds. However, the closeness philogeny of these genotypes was also supported in the spatial representation of observations and Euclidean distances. Despite the huge effort involved in collecting measurements from critically endangered turkeys, further studies should be conducted with a larger sample size of the populations under study. Moreover, future studies should continue the biometric study of further populations from Central America and the Mediterranean Europe. In addition, further variables discovered during the measurement collection period should also be considered, such as nail color, the number of caruncles, and the implementation of colorimetric techniques for shank and eye color.
