Clinical value of C-reactive protein as a predictor of conversion of laparoscopic cholecystectomy to open surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hassan Abuali, Mohamed Mudawi, Ibrahim Fagiri, Osman S Elhassan, Abdalla O Abdalla, Ahmed Fikry Mohamed, Sagad O O Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic cholecystectomy is the gold standard for gallbladder disease, yet conversion to open surgery remains a challenge. Unplanned conversion from laparoscopic cholecystectomy to open surgery is associated with increased morbidity and potential patient safety risks. Accurate preoperative predictors may improve surgical planning and enhance patient safety. The search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. We included studies reporting preoperative CRP in converted vs. non-converted laparoscopic cholecystectomy cases. Background

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy has become the gold standard surgical treatment for symptomatic gallstone disease, offering significant advantages over traditional open surgery, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery times [1–2]. However, conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy remains necessary in a subset of patients due to severe inflammation or complications. Conversion rates vary considerably across studies, ranging from approximately 1% to 25%, depending on patient characteristics and disease severity [3–4]. Unplanned conversion to open surgery is associated with increased operative time, higher complication rates, prolonged hospital stays, and greater healthcare costs [5–6].

From a patient safety perspective, predicting difficult laparoscopic cholecystectomy has become increasingly important. Identifying patients at high risk for conversion preoperatively would enable better surgical planning, informed patient counseling, and potentially improved perioperative outcomes [4].

Various clinical and laboratory factors have been investigated as potential predictors of conversion. C-reactive protein (CRP), an acute-phase inflammatory marker synthesized by the liver in response to tissue injury and inflammation, has gained attention as a potential biomarker for predicting difficult cholecystectomy and conversion to open surgery. Elevated preoperative CRP levels reflect the degree of gallbladder inflammation and surrounding tissue involvement in acute cholecystitis. Several studies have suggested that CRP may serve as a readily available and cost-effective tool for risk stratification before laparoscopic cholecystectomy [4, 7].

Despite growing interest in CRP as a predictive marker, individual studies have reported conflicting results regarding its independent predictive value for conversion. Additionally, the studies are often limited by small sample size and single-center biases. Given these inconsistencies and the clinical importance of predicting conversion risk, a meta-analysis would establish the strength of the association and synthesize the available evidence by providing a more definitive assessment of CRP’s predictive utility. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the association between preoperative CRP levels and conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy and quantify the magnitude of this association.

Methods

Search and screening

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [8], with the protocol prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251141888). A systematic literature search was done to identify studies examining the association between preoperative CRP levels and conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy.

Three major electronic databases were searched for relevant studies published up to October 2025. We searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus using a strategy combining multiple relevant terms (Additional file 1). References of eligible articles and relevant reviews were screened manually to identify additional studies. All publications were imported into Rayyan software (QCRI, Doha, Qatar; http://rayyan.qcri.org) for duplicates deletion and titles/abstracts screening [9]. Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts, and independently assessed full-text eligibility. Data extraction was performed independently by three reviewers Disagreements between the reviewers were resolved by discussion, with a third reviewer adjudicating when consensus was not reached.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this review were: (1) studies involving patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy; (2) measurement of preoperative CRP levels; (3) documentation of conversion to open surgery; and (4) sufficient data to calculate standardized mean differences or Odds ratio (OR) per one-unit increase in the CRP level. Both acute cholecystitis and elective/symptomatic gallstone populations were included. Studies were excluded if they did not report CRP levels separately for converted and non-converted cases, or if they lacked adequate statistical information required for meta-analysis. In addition, studies were excluded if they fell into the following categories: case reports, editorials, reviews, non-English articles, and abstracts from conferences.

Quality assessment and data extraction

The methodological quality of each study was assessed using the Quality In Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool, a validated instrument designed for prognostic systematic reviews [10]. QUIPS evaluates six domains of potential bias: study participation, study attrition, prognostic factor measurement, outcome measurement, confounding, and statistical analysis and reporting. Each domain is rated as low, moderate, or high risk of bias based on a structured set of questions. Data were extracted using a standardized form that captured study key characteristics. The following information was recorded: first author name, publication year, sample size (total, conversion group, and non-conversion group), mean CRP levels with standard deviations for both groups, patient demographics, and study-specific conversion rates. When multiple estimates were reported, those adjusted for the most confounders were selected. For studies reporting medians and IQRs, means and SDs were estimated using established transformation methods by Wan et al. [11].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out by calculating the pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) and OR estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI). To compensate for heterogeneity between studies, random effect model, using DerSimonian–Laird method was applied. The I2 statistic test was used to evaluate heterogeneity among studies, which quantifies the percentage of variation in effect estimates attributable to heterogeneity rather than chance. Publication bias was assessed using Begg’s and Egger’s tests along with funnel plot asymmetry. Meta-regression analyses were done to investigate heterogeneity and assess how specific study characteristics are associated with the effect size. In addition, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted, to evaluate the robustness of the meta-analyses by assessing the influence of individual studies on the outcomes. The significance level for all statistical tests was set at 0.05. The analyses were conducted using the meta package in R version 4.4.3.

Results

Study characteristics

The schematic flow of the study identification and selection process is presented in Fig. 1. The initial electronic database search yielded a total of 475 records. Following the removal of duplicate entries, 365 studies remained. These studies underwent a thorough title and abstract screening process. During this stage, further studies were excluded due to irrelevance and full texts of the remaining records were screened (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of studies selection process

A total of 11 studies published between 2009 and 2025, including 6,275 patients from diverse geographical regions, were eligible for inclusion in this meta-analysis [7, 12–21]. The reported mean age of participants varied between 47.1 and 71.4 years. The proportion of male patients ranged widely from 18.9% to 68.4% (Table 1). All CRP measurement were done on admission (pre-operative). The included studies reported overall conversion rate from laparoscopy to open surgery during cholecystectomy, with values ranging from 4.72% to 24% (Table 2). Risk of bias was assessed across six QUIPS domains for all included studies (Fig. 2). Overall, near half of studies were rated low risk of bias. Bias due to outcome measurement and prognostic factor measurement were the strongest domains, with nearly most studies rated low risk.

Risk of bias assessment

Meta-analysis results

The primary meta-analysis included 11 studies with a total of 6,277 patients (648 in the conversion group and 5,629 in the non-conversion group). The meta-analysis model demonstrated that patients in the conversion group had significantly higher preoperative CRP levels compared to those who completed the procedure laparoscopically, with a large effect size (SMD = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.77–1.41, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). Heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 91.1%, Q = 112.22, p < 0.001). Publication bias assessment using Egger’s regression test showed no significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.24). Similarly, Begg’s rank correlation test did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07).

Forest plot of serum CRP and conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy

Several meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The results showed that the effect size was not influenced by the key studies characteristics, including sample size (coefficient = -0.0001, p = 0.59), mean age (coefficient = 0.01, p = 0.82), and sex distribution (coefficient = -1.39, p = 0.23). Conversely, the conversion rate within each study was a significant moderator (coefficient = 1.74, p = 0.006), meaning that studies with higher conversion rates demonstrated larger effect sizes.

Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall effect estimate. The pooled SMD remained stable and statistically significant (p < 0.0001) across all repetitions, ranging from 0.94 (95% CI: 0.68–1.21) to 1.18 (95% CI: 0.88–1.49), indicating that no single study disproportionately influenced the findings. Another sensitivity analysis was done by excluding those studies that required Wan et al. conversion [13–16, 18–21]. The pooled SMD was 1.059 (95% CI: 0.641–1.477), which did not differ meaningfully from the primary estimate.

Subgroup analysis by patient population type demonstrated a statistically significant between-subgroup difference (p = 0.028). The acute cholecystitis subgroup (k = 8; SMD = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.55–1.10) yielded a more precise and stable estimate, reflected in its narrow confidence interval, compared to the mixed/elective subgroup (k = 3; SMD = 1.95, 95% CI: 0.98–2.91). The markedly wider confidence interval indicates considerable uncertainty due to the small number of studies.

To further evaluate risk of conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy associated with each unit increment in CRP, we conducted a further meta-analysis on five studies that reported OR per one-unit increase in the CRP. Of the five studies contributing to the per-unit OR analysis, three reported adjusted estimates [13, 20–21] and two reported unadjusted estimates [17, 19]. The meta-analysis indicated that risk of conversion significantly increased for each one-unit increase in the CRP (OR = 1.012; 95% CI 1.005–1.018; p = 0.004) (Fig. 4). For clinical interpretability, the equivalent OR per 10 mg/L CRP increment was 1.127 (95% CI: 1.051–1.196). Heterogeneity across studies was moderate (I² = 47.2%) and the Cochran’s Q test for heterogeneity was not statistically significant (Q = 7.57, p = 0.109). Publication bias was assessed using a linear regression test for funnel plot asymmetry, which showed no significant evidence of small-study effects (p = 0.11). Similarly, the Begg’s test indicated no evidence of bias (p = 0.05).

Discussion

This meta-analysis provided evidence that preoperative CRP levels are significantly elevated in patients requiring conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy, with a large effect size. Furthermore, the meta-analysis of odds ratios revealed that each one-unit increase in CRP level was associated with a statistically significant increase in the risk of conversion. These findings remained robust across sensitivity analyses, which strengthen confidence in the validity of the results. However, the high between-study heterogeneity limits the certainty of these estimates. The clinical utility of preoperative CRP measurement extends beyond simple risk prediction. As a readily available, cost-effective, and objective inflammatory marker, CRP may aid preoperative risk stratification and surgical decision-making, particularly in the context of acute cholecystitis.

In addition, several included studies identified specific CRP cut-off values with varying discriminative power. For instance, Mok et al. [7] reported that CRP > 220 mg/L was associated with a 61.9% conversion rate and demonstrated excellent predictive performance (AUC: 0.925), while Terho et al. [18] found that CRP > 150 mg/L yielded reasonable predictive accuracy (AUC: 0.67). Notably, Utsumi et al. [19] found that the CRP-to-Albumin Ratio (CAR) demonstrated superior predictive power (AUC 0.787) compared to CRP alone, suggesting that composite inflammatory indices incorporating nutritional status may offer enhanced predictive accuracy. Bouassida et al. [13] demonstrated that CRP had superior discriminative power compared to important hematological markers such as white blood cell count and neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio. Therefore, CRP measurement can enable surgeons to better anticipate technical difficulties, allocate experienced surgical teams to high-risk cases, ensure availability of necessary equipment for potential conversion, and provide more informed patient counseling regarding realistic expectations and perioperative risks.

Regarding biological plausibility, the link between CRP and surgical difficulty is well-founded. High CRP indicates systemic inflammation that typically mirrors severe local inflammation in the gallbladder and surrounding tissues, creating operative conditions marked by edema, dense adhesions, fragile tissue planes, and distorted anatomy in Calot’s triangle. These changes impair visualization of critical biliary structures, making laparoscopic dissection more challenging and raising the risk of conversion to open surgery [13, 19]. Similarly, studies showed that high preoperative CRP levels are significantly associated with an increased risk of conversion from laparoscopic to open surgery in other surgical conditions such as acute appendicitis. The high CRP indicates severe inflammation, which causes technical difficulties such as dense adhesions, tissue necrosis, or obscured anatomy [22–23].

Although the odds ratio associated with each unit increase in CRP appears small, severe inflammation can raise CRP levels by tens or hundreds of units, making the cumulative clinical impact substantial. This is supported by our primary analysis, which demonstrated a large standardized mean difference between conversion and non-conversion groups. Therefore, the results support a strong association between elevated CRP and the likelihood of conversion. Taken together, these findings indicate that CRP may represent a clinically useful biomarker reflecting the inflammatory severity associated with technically difficult cholecystectomy.

Subgroup analysis by patient population type demonstrated that the acute cholecystitis subgroup yielded a more precise and stable estimate compared to the mixed/elective subgroup. The small number of studies in the mixed subgroup (k = 3) and the marked heterogeneity in baseline CRP levels should be noted. In mixed populations, non-converted patients often include elective cases with near-normal CRP, potentially widening the between-group difference and inflating the effect size relative to acute-only studies where elevated CRP is nearly universal across all patients regardless of conversion status.

Several limitations of the current review should be acknowledged. The limited data and variability in CRP cut-off values reported across studies limited the ability to perform further analyses evaluating the predictive performance and optimal threshold of CRP for conversion. Another limitation is the heterogeneity noted in effect sizes, which suggest that unmeasured factors may influence the CRP-conversion relationship. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s and Begg’s tests; however, with few studies included, these tests have limited statistical power to detect small-study effects, and non-significant results should not be interpreted as confirming the absence of publication bias. Furthermore, important clinical variables, including patient comorbidities and operator experience were inconsistently reported across studies and could not be formally incorporated into the heterogeneity analyses. The meta-regression analysis revealed that conversion rate was a significant moderator of effect size, indicating that studies with higher baseline conversion rates demonstrated larger effect sizes for CRP’s predictive value. However, additional potential sources of heterogeneity such as, differences in variations in the timing of CRP measurement relative to symptom onset and surgery surgical technique could not be fully accounted for in our analysis.

Future research should focus on several key priorities. Prospective studies are needed to validate CRP’s predictive value and establish cut-off values with defined sensitivity and specificity for clinical decision-making. In addition, investigations should explore whether CRP’s predictive utility varies according to the timing of measurement relative to symptom onset or surgery, potentially identifying optimal measurement windows.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that elevated preoperative CRP levels are significantly associated with increased risk of conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy. This readily available and cost-effective biomarker can aid in preoperative risk stratification and improving surgical planning to enhance surgical safety and reduce the risk of intraoperative complications. While further research is needed to establish standardized cut-off values and integrate CRP into comprehensive predictive models, its routine assessment can improve perioperative outcomes for patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy.
