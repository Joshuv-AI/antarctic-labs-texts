Prevalence of bovine tuberculosis in cattle, camels and goats and awareness among pastoral and agro-pastoral communities of the Somali region, Ethiopia
Authors: Yonas Gizaw, Gebregergs Tesfamaryam, Mulisa Megersa Kebeta, Hassan Abdi Hussein, Teka Feyera, Aydid Abdulahi, Nestanet Desalegn, Meseret Abebe, Bisrat Tesfaye, Yibelital Muhie, Rea Tschopp, Gobena Ameni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bovine tuberculosis (BTB) is an endemic disease in Ethiopia; however, data on animal TB in the Somali region remain minimal. This cross-sectional study was conducted from January 2017 to June 2018 to estimate the prevalence of BTB in cattle, camels, and goats, identify associated risk factors, and assess community awareness of the disease. A total of 982 animals were tested using Comparative Intradermal Tuberculin Test (CIDT), with results interpreted at ≥4 mm and ≥2 mm cut-off points. In addition, a semi-structured questionnaire was administered to 110 animal owners to evaluate community knowledge related to BTB. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association of risk factors with the occurrence of BTB in the study animals. At the ≥4 mm cut-off, the individual BTB prevalence was 2.7% (95% CI: 0.96–4.5%) in cattle, 1.8% (95% CI: 0.73–3.3%) in camels, and 0% in goats indicating that goats had no reactors at the standard diagnostic threshold. When applying the ≥2 mm cut-off, prevalence increased to 3.6% (95% CI: 1.6–5.7%) in cattle, 6.7% (95% CI: 4.0–9.5%) in camels, and 3.3% (95% CI: 1.4–5.3%) in goats. At the herd level, BTB prevalence in cattle, camels, and goats was 40%, 31%, and 0%, respectively, at the ≥4 mm cut-off. Camels exhibited the largest increase in herd prevalence when applying the more sensitive ≥2 mm threshold (68%). There was no statistically significant association in the prevalence of BTB with hypothesized risk factors in any of the examined livestock species. Somali pastoralists showed limited knowledge of zoonotic BTB, with only 63% having heard of bovine TB and 24% aware of animal‑to‑human transmission. The Complete absence of TB health education reported by all respondents (0%) highlights the critical need for targeted community awareness programs and integration of TB education into routine veterinary and public health services.

Introduction

Bovine tuberculosis (BTB) is a chronic respiratory infectious disease of cattle, transmitted both within and between animal species primarily through inhalation of aerosolized droplets and, to a lesser extent, through ingestion of contaminated feed and water (Michel et al., 2010). The disease is caused by Mycobacterium bovis (M. bovis), a member of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC) which encompasses a closely related group of mycobacterial species responsible for tuberculosis in mammals, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the principal agent of human tuberculosis, and M. bovis, the main cause of BTB in animals (Borham et al., 2022).

Globally, M. bovis affects a wide range of domestic and wild animal species, making it a pathogen of major veterinary and public health concern. In livestock, BTB reduces productivity through decreased milk yield, weight loss, and increased mortality, and it imposes significant economic burdens by limiting trade in animals and animal products. In wildlife, M. bovis infections have been documented in a broad range of species, including badgers in the United Kingdom and Ireland, white-tailed deer in North America, and African buffalo in sub-Saharan Africa, where they can establish persistent reservoirs that complicate eradication efforts and disrupt ecosystem dynamics (Gormley & Corner, 2018).

The zoonotic potential of M. bovis is particularly concerning for populations living at the human–animal interface, where close contact with livestock, consumption of raw animal products, and limited access to veterinary and public health services increase the risk of transmission (Palmer et al., 2012). M. bovis zoonosis has significant public health impacts in developing countries, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa. In these settings, humans and animals often share living spaces, water sources, and grazing areas, creating ideal conditions for cross-species transmission (Olea-Popelka et al., 2017). BTB has been reported from 60% of the African, 47% of the Asian, and 38% of the Latin American and Caribbean countries (Macedo Couto et al., 2019). In developing regions where milk pasteurization is uncommon, M. bovis is estimated to cause 10–15% of all human tuberculosis cases (Ayalew et al., 2023). Infected animals excrete the bacteria through various routes, including exhaled air, sputum, feces, milk, urine, reproductive discharges, and discharges from open peripheral lymph nodes. This multifaceted shedding pattern underscores the potential for BTB transmission among animals and between animals and humans (Phillips et al., 2003).

The diagnosis of BTB in live animal relies primarily on the Comparative Intradermal Tuberculin Test which is based on a delayed hypersensitivity reaction. This test helps differentiate M. bovis infection from sensitization due to exposure to environmental mycobacteria (Dejene et al., 2016).

Bovine tuberculosis is an endemic disease in Ethiopia; it causes substantial economic losses to livestock industry by affecting various animals including cattle, sheep, goats, and camels (Ayele et al., 2004). Previous studies conducted in Ethiopia using tuberculin skin test shows that the prevalence of BTB at animal level in cattle ranges between 0.8–78% in rural extensive production system that mainly keep Zebu cattle and intensive systems that keep exotic and cross breed cattle respectively (Ameni et al., 2008, 2007; Cosivi et al., 1998; Firdessa et al., 2012). However, lower prevalence of BTB has been reported from goat and camels in Ethiopia (Hiko & Agga, 2011; Mamo et al., 2011).

Somali Region is situated in the southeastern part of Ethiopia and is the second-largest region in the country. The region is characterized by vast arid and semi-arid rangeland where pastoralism and agro pastoralism are the predominant livelihoods. Cattle, camels, and goats are the primary livestock species raised in the region, and these animals play a crucial role in providing food, income, and cultural identity for the local communities (Hussein et al., 2024). The close proximity between humans and animals in many parts of the region, exacerbated during drought and dry seasons when grazing land and water sources become scarce, increases the risk of zoonotic transmission. This risk is further compounded by a lack of awareness about the zoonotic transmission of M. bovis, absence of milk pasteurization practices, and limited access to preventive measures (Esposito et al., 2023). Furthermore, effective bovine tuberculosis (BTB) control and eradication attempts have not been implemented in the country, largely due to significant economic implications and constrained diagnostic capacity (Sibhat et al., 2017). Data on the prevalence of BTB infections in the Somali region are limited. Nevertheless, there is enough evidence to suggest that BTB occurs among both livestock and human populations in the region, with prevalence reports ranging from 2% to 20% (Gumi et al., 2012a; Hussein et al., 2024; Kemal et al., 2019). Therefore, this study aimed to address the gap by investigating the prevalence of bovine tuberculosis and associated risk factors in camels, cattle, and goats, as well as assessing the awareness of the pastoral communities regarding zoonotic bovine tuberculosis.

Materials and methods

Study area description

This study was conducted in six districts across three zones of the Somali Region, namely Gursum and Kebribeyah districts in the Fafan Zone, Degahabour and Aware districts in the Jarar Zone, and Dannan and Gode districts in the Shebele Zone (Fig. 1). Agro‑pastoralism is the dominant production system in the Fafan Zone, whereas pastoralism predominates in the Jarar and Shebele Zones, although parts of Shabele supports riverine livelihood. The livelihood of the society is entirely dependent on livestock, relying on milk, meat, and the sale of animals and animal products for food and income. The study areas are characterized by arid to semi‑arid conditions with highly erratic rainfall, making them vulnerable to recurrent water and fodder shortages that often force pastoralists to undertake seasonal migrations with their livestock. The Somali Region is estimated to host approximately 40 million head of livestock, including 6034,887 cattle, 11,013,491 sheep, 16,464,505 goats, and 6489,702 camels (CSA, 2017).Fig. 1Study area map of the somali region, Ethiopia.Fig 1: dummy alt text

Study animals

The study animals included in the present study were cattle, camels, and goats above six (6) months of age of both sexes. Animals showing clinical symptoms of acute diseases, being currently treated for an acute disease, or recently vaccinated against any infectious diseases were excluded from the study due to potential immunosuppression that would interfere with the skin test. Similarly, female animals in the last trimester of pregnancy or those that had given birth within the preceding two weeks were also excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using the cluster sampling formula described by Bennett et al. (Bennett et al., 1991), considering herds as clusters. The calculation assumed an intra-class correlation coefficient (roh*) of 0.2, an expected animal-level prevalence of 2% based on (Gumi et al., 2012a), and a standard error of 0.025. Based on these parameters, the required sample size was 894 animals across 57 herds, combining all livestock species. Sampling method

A combination of probabilistic and non‑probabilistic sampling methods was used to address the challenges posed by the wide, remote, and mobile nature of pastoral production systems, where strictly random sampling is difficult to implement. Accordingly, purposive sampling was used to select two representative districts from the targeted zones, based on livestock population potential and accessibility. Within each selected district, simple random sampling was then used to select pastoral associations (PAs) or kebeles and herds from lists provided by the District Pastoral Development Offices. The study animals were selected within each herd by listing all animals aged six months or above and randomly choosing individual animals using simple random sampling. It is acknowledged that in pastoral settings some selected animals may not have been presented for testing due to the mobile nature of herds; whenever this occurred, the next eligible animal in the herd list was selected as a replacement. Herds were defined as groups of animals owned by a single household or close relatives that shared grazing and watering points and stayed together at night and during migration. For each selected animal, data on sex, age, body condition score, herd size, parity, lactation status, and reproductive status were recorded prior to administering the purified protein derivatives (PPD). Each animal received a unique temporary identification number linked to its questionnaire and CIDT records.

Body condition scoring (BCS) was conducted following species-specific guidelines described by Hamito (2009); Kashoma et al. (2011) for cattle, (Hamito, 2009) for camels and standardized criteria by Saghi et al. (2016) for goats. The assessment was based on visual inspection and palpation of key anatomical landmarks, including the ribs, vertebral column, pelvic bones, and tail head, and for camels, the hump condition. Animals were assigned a score on a five-point scale (1= emaciated to 5=Excellent body condition). To facilitate comparative analysis across species, scores were harmonized into three categories: poor (BCS 1–2), characterized by prominent skeletal structures, minimal fat reserves, and reduced muscle mass; medium (BCS 3), indicating moderate body reserves with palpable but not prominent bones; and good (BCS 4–5), defined by smooth body contours, well-covered skeletal structures, and adequate to abundant fat deposits. This classification was used to group animals according to their nutritional status during analysis.

Tuberculin skin testing

The Comparative Intradermal Tuberculin Test was performed using bovine and avian PPD supplied by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Prionics Lelystad B.V., Lelystad, Netherlands). Two injection sites were shaved on the mid-cervical region of each animal, at least 12 cm apart. Baseline skin thickness at both sites was measured using calibrated calipers. Each site received an intradermal inoculation of either 0.1 mL of bovine PPD (3000 IU/0.1 mL) or Avian PPD (2500 IU/0.1 mL). Seventy-two hours after inoculation, animals were examined for oedema, heat, pain, exudation, and necrosis at both PPD injection sites, and skin thickness at each site was re-measured. The reaction at each site was calculated as the difference between the post-inoculation and baseline measurements.

Animals were classified as bovine reactors according to two comparative thresholds: the standard Office International des Epizooties (OIE) interpretation (OIE, 2018), in which the bovine PPD reaction is required to exceed the avian reaction by ≥ 4 mm, and a severe interpretation using a ≥ 2 mm differential to capture a broader range of positive cases, as described by Ameni et al. (Ameni et al., 2008). An animal was considered positive for mycobacterial species other than the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex when the change in skin thickness was greater at the PPD-A injection site. A herd was considered positive if it had at least one tuberculin reactor animal.

Questionnaire survey

A semi-structured questionnaire was administered to 110 owners of the sampled animals, noting that most herds were jointly owned by multiple households, to assess the pastoral community's awareness of zoonotic bovine tuberculosis. The questionnaire addressed topics including knowledge of BTB and human TB, transmission routes, risk practices (raw milk and meat consumption, animal contact), housing systems, and health-seeking behavior. The questionnaire was translated into the local Somali language, pre-tested on a small sample of pastoralists not included in the main study, and administered by trained enumerators after informing the community about the study objectives. Households that expressed interest in participating were registered, and participation was entirely voluntary.

Statistical analysis

Data was entered in Microsoft Excel sheet, coded and transferred to STATA version 11 (Stata Corp., Collage station, TX) for analysis. Data obtained from the questionnaire survey were initially summarized using descriptive statistics to describe frequencies, proportions, and distributions of variables. The Pearson chi-square test was used to assess associations between categorical explanatory variables and tuberculin skin test results. To evaluate and quantify the effects of potential risk factors on tuberculin reactivity, both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed, with results expressed as odds ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was determined at a P-value <0.05.

Results

Among the 982 animals examined, 23 (2.34%) tested positive at the ≥4 mm cut-off, whereas 102 (10.39%) were positive at the more sensitive ≥2 mm cut-off. In cattle specifically, individual prevalence was 2.7% at the ≥4 mm cut-off, with a herd prevalence of 40%; when applying the ≥2 mm cut-off, individual prevalence increased to 3.6% and herd prevalence rose to 55%. In camels, individual prevalence ranged from 1.8% at the ≥4 mm cut-off to 6.7% at the ≥2 mm cut-off. At the herd level, prevalence in camels was 31% at the ≥4 mm cut-off but increased markedly to 68% at the ≥2 mm cut-off. In contrast, goats showed no individual or herd-level positivity at the ≥4 mm cut-off, which is the standard diagnostic threshold recommended by the OIE and therefore represents the more reliable estimate. Discussion

The overall apparent prevalence of bovine tuberculosis in this study was 2.34% using the standard ≥4 mm cut-off for the CIDT. This rate aligns closely with the 2% prevalence reported in Somali pastoral systems by Gumi et al. (2012a), and consistent with other arid pastoral zones across sub-Saharan Africa. This prevalence exceeded the 0.8% from the Hamer pastoral area (Rea Tschopp et al., 2010a). While remaining substantially lower than estimates from the more humid Afar and Borana (Oromia) pastoral regions of Ethiopia (Demelash et al., 2009; Gumi et al., 2011). When the more sensitive ≥2 mm cut-off was applied, the overall apparent prevalence increased. Both estimates should be interpreted in the context of their respective diagnostic thresholds, as the lower cut-off is associated with greater sensitivity but reduced specificity, partly because of potential cross-reactivity with non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) that are widespread in soil and water of arid environments (Biet & Boschiroli, 2014). These regional differences likely reflect variation in ecological conditions and livestock production systems. Compared to the arid and sparsely populated Somali region, Afar and Oromia have relatively higher humidity, greater livestock density, and more intensive agro-pastoral practices which are factors that facilitate closer animal contact and enhance M. bovis transmission (Gumi et al., 2011; Shitaye et al., 2007).

At the species level, cattle BTB prevalence was 2.7% using the ≥4 mm cut-off and 3.6% using the ≥2 mm cut-off. Which is markedly lower than the 11.6% reported in cattle from Hawassa town and its surrounding areas by Regassa et al. (2010). Similarly, camel prevalence of 1.8% for BTB is lower than the 6% observed in a previous study from eastern Ethiopia (Beyi et al., 2014). and lower than rates reported from municipal abattoirs across Ethiopia (Ameni et al., 2008; Beyi et al., 2014). The low prevalence in the present study may be attributed to the arid ecology of the Somali region, characterized by lower livestock density, traditional extensive husbandry wide seasonal dispersal of herds, and harsh environmental conditions that constrain bacterial survival and transmission (Firdessa et al., 2012). Importantly, the low but non-negligible prevalence documented here carries real socio-economic significance for pastoral households in the Somali Region, where livestock constitute the primary source of food, income, and wealth. Even modest rates of BTB-related morbidity and reduced productivity can translate into meaningful livelihood losses, reducing herd offtake value, and constraining access to markets, an impact that are disproportionately burdens the poorest and most food-insecure households (Skuce et al., 2012).

Goats showed no BTB reactors at the ≥4 mm cut-off, which is the standard threshold recommended by the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH, formerly OIE). At the ≥2 mm cut-off, an individual prevalence of 3.3% was observed; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the lower cut-off may detect cross-reactivity with environmental NTM rather than true M. bovis infection. This finding partially aligns with reports by (Rea Tschopp et al., 2010a), who found no BTB reactors in CIDT-tested goats at either the ≥4 mm or ≥2 mm cut-offs. The observed discrepancy may be attributable to variation in testing methodology, including the absence of a standardized CIDT protocol specifically validated for goats; the WOAH guidelines recommend applying the same procedures as for cattle, but many authors have used varying cut-off definitions. This methodological heterogeneity makes cross-study comparison challenging (Beyi et al., 2014; Javed et al., 2010).

A significantly higher prevalence of BTB was observed in cattle compared to camels and goats at the ≥4 mm cut-off (P = 0.014). This finding is consistent with (Rea Tschopp et al., 2010a), but contrasts with Gumi et al. (Gumi et al., 2012a), who reported no significant difference in tuberculin positivity among different animal species. Possible explanations include the physical separation of goats and camels from cattle in some herds, potentially limiting transmission, as well as the higher turnover rate of goats, which may reduce cumulative exposure.

Herd-level prevalence of BTB was 40% in cattle, 31% in camels, and 0% in goats at the ≥4 mm cut-off, broadly consistent with the 44% herd prevalence reported in cattle from pastoral areas of Afar (Mamo et al., 2013). However, these figures are higher than the 14.3% in cattle and 3.1% in camels reported by Gumi et al. (Gumi et al., 2012a), which may reflect differences in herd management, ecological conditions, and epidemiological factors.

There was no statistically significant association found between hypothesized risk factors such as study districts, herd size, production system, age, sex, body condition, seasonal migration, introduction of new purchased animals to herd, annual migration dynamics, presence of older than ten-year lactation status, and reproductive status to positivity of BTB at a ≥ 4 mm cut off. This conclusion aligns with the findings reported by (Gumi et al., 2012a; R Tschopp et al., 2010). and may be attributable to the overall low prevalence in the study area limiting statistical power, the cross-sectional study design, and the relatively uniform extensive production system across the sampled population. Longitudinal cohort designs with larger sample sizes would be better positioned to detect risk factors for BTB acquisition in this setting (Grace et al., 2012; Rea Tschopp et al., 2010b).

The questionnaire survey revealed that 63% of community members were aware of BTB, which is higher than the 45.6% reported from the Jimma zone (Fetene & Kebede, 2009), and the 31% from Gambella (Asebe & Gudina, 2018), This relatively higher awareness may reflect the longstanding exposure of Somali pastoral communities to livestock disease and the central economic importance of cattle and camels in their livelihoods. Despite this relative awareness of BTB as a disease entity, knowledge of zoonotic transmission was markedly low: only 24% of respondents knew that animals can transmit BTB to humans, and merely 10% knew that humans can transmit TB to animals. Most respondents did not identify milk or animal tissues (lymph nodes and organs) as potential sources of BTB infection. These findings are consistent with other Ethiopian studies reporting a persistent gap between livestock disease awareness and understanding of zoonotic risk (Ameni et al., 2011; Bihon et al., 2021; Sibhat et al., 2017).

Nearly all respondents (99%) reported consuming raw milk, reflecting a strong cultural preference for raw camel milk and its taste despite the associated risks of harmful bacteria. In contrast, raw meat consumption was uncommon, with 97% stating they do not eat raw meat, an outcome largely shaped by religious and cultural norms, as all participants were Muslims. This pattern differs from findings in Gambella, where only 43% of respondents reported consuming raw milk (Asebe & Gudina, 2018). aligns with previous reports of near-universal raw milk consumption among Somali pastoralists (Firdessa et al., 2012; Gumi et al., 2012b).

The questionnaire assessment provided clear evidence that both the pastoralists and agro-pastoralists in the region have insufficient knowledge about the transmission of BTB between animals and humans. Only 24% of the respondents were aware that animals can transmit BTB to humans, and merely 10% were knowledgeable about humans being capable of transmitting tuberculosis to animals. This lack of awareness is also reflected in their perception, as most of them did not consider milk and meat as potential sources of infection to humans. These findings align with a study conducted by (Bihon et al., 2021; Sibhat et al., 2022).

This study has several strengths, including its rare multi‑species assessment of BTB in the Somali Region, concurrent sampling of cattle, camels, and goats, and incorporation of community knowledge. The use of both ≥4 mm and ≥2 mm CIDT cut‑offs enhances sensitivity versus specificity interpretation and comparability, while the knowledge survey effectively assesses animal infection patterns with human exposure behaviors.

Nonetheless, important limitations exist. The cross‑sectional design limits causal inference and detection of temporal risk factors. Small sample sizes, particularly for camels and goats may have reduced power to identify associations. CIDT cannot confirm M. bovis or differentiate NTM sensitization, a notable concern in arid environments with abundant environmental mycobacteria, and the absence of slaughter or culture confirmation further constrains diagnostic certainty. Another limitation relates to the cultural relevance of certain questionnaire items. Questionnaire responses may be affected by social desirability bias, particularly for culturally sensitive topics such as offal handling or consumption. During the pilot testing, it became clear that the studied community does not consume offals and considered such questions inappropriate or irrelevant. Consequently, offal-related questionnaire items were excluded to maintain cultural appropriateness and to minimize response bias, and the findings may not be generalizable beyond the sampled pastoral communities.

Conclusion

This study documented a low but non-negligible prevalence of bovine tuberculosis in cattle (2.7%), camels (1.8%), and goats (0% at the standard ≥4 mm threshold) in the pastoral and agro-pastoral areas of the Somali Region, Ethiopia. The results suggest limited BTB transmission within the study livestock population, likely influenced by the arid ecology, low livestock density, and traditional husbandry practices of the region. However, the absence of BTB reactors in goats at the standard diagnostic threshold, and the caution warranted in interpreting the ≥2 mm results, underscore the need for species-specific diagnostic validation in small ruminants. Of particular concern is the limited awareness among Somali pastoralists of the zoonotic implications of BTB and the risks associated with consumption of raw milk and offal. Given the close human–animal relationship, near-universal raw milk consumption, detectable BTB prevalence in livestock, and complete absence of TB health education in the study communities, there is a meaningful probability of animal-to-human transmission. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted, culturally appropriate health education programs and the integration of One Health approaches into routine veterinary and public health services. Proactive collaboration between government bodies, development organizations, and communities is essential to reduce the zoonotic risk of BTB in this region. Future studies on the molecular epidemiology of tuberculosis and the zoonotic significance of M. bovis in the Somali Region are recommended to better characterize transmission dynamics and inform evidence-based control strategies.
