Human single domain antibody-based CAR-T cells targeting BAFF-R demonstrate promising preclinical activity in B-cell malignancies
Authors: Cuiling Zhang, Qian Luo, Sungui Wu, Le Xu, Qiaoe Wei, Yanying Zhang, Chaoyang Guan, Xiaoqing Dong, Yang Hong, Taochao Tan, Bing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSingle-chain antibody-based CAR-T cells targeting BAFF-R have demonstrated antitumor effects against human B-cell malignancies and can overcome CD19 antigen loss. However, CARs built on antigen-specific single-chain antibody variable fragments may have limitations, e.g., large CAR binding domain size. To improve the function of BAFF-R CAR-T cells, we designed CARs with only a fully human heavy-chain variable domain.MethodsA fully human antibody phage display library was used to select anti- BAFF- R clones using protein/cell alternate panning. The screened clones were grafted into a second-generation CAR to generate CAR-T cells. CD107a degranulation, luciferase-based cytolysis, repeat antigen stimulation, activation markers, and exhaustion markers were used to detect the function of CAR-T cells in vitro. Xenografts were established in NPG mice following intravenous injection of Jeko-1 cells. Tumor burden and survival of mice were recorded weekly.ResultsThree single-domain heavy chain antibodies, namely Clone #5, Clone #77, and Clone #80, were selected for further evaluation. Background

B-cell malignancies comprise a group of heterogeneous tumors that occur at various stages of B-cell differentiation, primarily including B-cell lymphoma and B-cell leukemia. The prognosis of these patients has significantly improved over the years due to the adoption of novel treatments; nonetheless, some patients still suffer recurrence or are refractory, facing dismal survival [1–3]. Chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapies, specifically targeting CD19, have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in treating relapsed and refractory B-cell malignancies, with an overall response rate of 53% to 83% [4–8]. Despite this, there are still a subset of patients who do not respond to CD19 CAR-T therapies or relapse after receiving them [9]. The prognosis for these patients remains bleak, compounded by the absence of consensus on optimal management strategies [10]. The failure of CD19 CAR- T therapies can be attributed to a variety of factors, one of which is the loss of the CD19 antigen [11, 12]. To overcome this limitation, it’s critical to look for novel therapeutic targets. These targets offer the potential to counter CD19 antigen escape and enable combination with CD19 in a multi-target CAR approach for improved treatment outcomes.

Currently, several targets for B-cell malignancies, such as CD20, CD22, and ROR1, are being actively explored [13]. However, few antigens fully meet the criteria for an ideal target due to persistent challenges in limiting off-target effects for safety and in overcoming antigen escape to prevent early relapse [13]. B-cell activating factor-receptor (BAFF-R), encoded by the TNFRSF13C gene, is one of the key membrane protein receptors involved in B-cell differentiation and proliferation [14]. It is expressed almost exclusively on B cells [15] and is commonly overexpressed in various B-cell malignancies [16–18], which establishes it as an attractive therapeutic target with a low risk of severe off-tumor toxicity. Critically, as BAFF-R signaling is essential for B-cell survival, its loss is less tolerable for malignant cells, thereby reducing antigen escape risk compared to targets like CD19 [19]. According to the research by Qin et al. [20], antigen-specific single-chain antibody variable fragments (scFv)-based CAR-T cells targeting BAFF-R (PMB-101) exhibited antitumor benefits against human B-cell malignancies, even when the tumor cells had lost their CD19 antigen. Similarly, the novel BAFF-R CAR-T (MC10029 CAR-T) based on scFv showed identical outcomes [21]. However, CARs constructed using scFv may lead to certain adverse effects, such as potential anti-CAR immunogenicity and protein aggregation [22]. In contrast, single-domain heavy chain exhibits lower immunogenicity in clinical applications due to its high sequence similarity to the human variable heavy chain, and its hydrophilic modifications within the framework regions circumvent aggregation issues [23]. Moreover, compared to single-domain heavy chain, the larger size of scFv makes it less spatially advantageous to contact specific small or partially concealed cell surface antigens [24]. Additionally, evidence showed that single-domain heavy chain-expressing CAR-T cells achieve functional equivalence to conventional scFv-based CAR-T cells [24]. Thus, we designed BAFF-R CARs that contained only a fully human heavy-chain variable domain (FHVH) and evaluated the performance of BAFF-R CAR-T cells. Our results showed that BAFF-R CAR-T cells based on FHVH exhibited satisfactory anti-tumor efficacy and represented a promising treatment for B-cell malignancies.

Methods

Cell lines

Human hematological tumor cell lines including Jeko-1 (mantle cell lymphoma), Nalm6 (pre-B acute lymphoblastic leukemia cell), RS4 (B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia), Molt4 (chronic B cell leukemia), CCRF-CEM (T cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia), THP1 (acute myeloid leukemia) and K562 (chronic myelogenous leukemia) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco). Mec-1 (chronic B cell leukemia) was cultured in IMDM medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS. K562-BAFF-R cells were engineered by lentiviral transduction of the BAFF-R gene into parental K562 cells, followed by antibiotic selection and single-cell cloning to establish a monoclonal line with stable, high-level BAFF-R expression as a positive control. The expression levels of BAFF-R on different hematological tumor cell lines were assessed using PE-conjugated mouse anti-human BAFF-R antibody (clone: 11C1, BioLegend) and isotype antibody (clone: MOPC-21, Biolegend). A MACSQuant Analyzer 10 flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec) was used for data acquisition, and FlowJo V10 (Tree Star) was used for data analyses and graphs. BAFF-R positive and negative cell lines were stably transfected with firefly luciferase by lentivirus.

OVCAR3 (ovarian adenocarcinoma), KATO III (gastric carcinoma), HCT-116 (colorectal carcinoma), HEPG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), MDA-MB-468 (breast adenocarcinoma), PANC-1 (pancreatic epithelioid carcinoma), A375 (melanoma cancer), 293T (embryonic kidney), and NCI-H460 (large cell lung cancer) was cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS. All cell lines (Table S1) were purchased from the American Tissue Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA) and verified before use.

Plasmid construction

A fully human antibody phage display library (IMARS, IASO Biotherapeutics) was used to select anti-BAFF-R clones using optimal protein/cell alternate panning. IMARS platform contains not only regular antibody formats like scFv but also proprietary human single-domain antibodies (in separate sub-libraries). Four rounds of bead panning were carried out using recombinant biotinylated human BAFF-R protein (BAFF-R his) (Kactus) as the target antigen and K562-BAFF-R cells as the target cells. The phage clones were first screened for their ability to bind to BAFF-R antigen via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using plates coated with BAFF-R his and streptavidin (Pierce). After incubation with anti-M13 phage mouse antibody (abcam) and anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase antibody (clone: Poly4053, Biolegend), plates were read at 450 nm by using a microplate reader (BioTek). Flow cytometry was used to evaluate their binding affinities to K562-BAFF-R and K562 cell lines after they were stained with anti-M13 phage mouse antibody and FITC anti-mouse IgG antibody (clone: Poly4060, Biolegend). The screened clones were sent for sequencing. Then, the specificity of these clones was further tested by ELISA using plates coated with BAFF-R his, human BAFF-R protein (BAFF-R Fc) (ACRO Biosystem), recombinant biotinylated human CD19 Protein (CD19 Fc) (Kactus), biotinylated human CD70 protein (CD70 his) (ACRO Biosystem) and streptavidin. Their binding affinities to BAFF-R+ and BAFF-R− cell lines were further confirmed using flow cytometry. The final screened clones were integrated into a second-generation CAR with CD8α hinge/transmembrane region, 4‐1BB costimulatory domain, and CD3ζ intracellular signaling domain. The CAR fragment was downstream attached to the truncated epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFRt) [25] through a T2A sequence, facilitating the detection of CAR-T cells.

Antibody affinity measurement

The Octet96e system (ForteBio) was used to evaluate the binding affinity of anti-BAFF-R antibodies. In brief, the anti-BAFF-R antibodies were diluted to 20 µg/ml and loaded onto the biosensors at 0.8 nM. The binding kinetics of the BAFF-R antigen were observed at various antigen concentrations ranging from 200 to 3.13 nM, following a 180-second equilibration period. Each concentration was measured for 60 s of association and 60 s of disassociation. The 1:1 binding site model (Fortebio data analysis version 11.1) was used to analyze the binding kinetics.

Generation of BAFF-R CAR-T cells

Lentivirus was packaged by the tri-plasmid system containing a transfer plasmid, GAG-POL plasmid psPAX2, and VSV-G plasmid (pMD2.G). The lentiviral supernatants were harvested and concentrated at 24 h post transfection. 293T cells and PE-labelled human BAFF-R protein (ACRO Biosystems) was used to detect viral titer via flow cytometry. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMCs) were obtained from Milestone Biotechnologies. CD3 + T cells were isolated from hPBMCs using CD3 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec) and activated with Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher). The activated T cells were cultured in OpTmizer™ CTS™ T-cell expansion basal medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS, 200 IU/ml IL-2 (SL Pharm), and 1×GluMax (Gibco). After 24 h, T cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding the BAFF-R CAR at a multiplicity of infection of 2. The duration of T-cell expansion post-transduction was about 1 week, then FITC-conjugated CD4 (clone: SK3, Biolegend), BV421-conjugated CD8 (clone: SK1, Biolegend), APC-conjugated mouse anti-human EGFR antibody (clone: AY13, Biolegend) and PE-labelled human BAFF- R protein were used to measure transduction efficiencies via flow cytometry.

CD107a degranulation assay

BAFF-R CAR-T cells or non-transduced (mock) T cells were co-cultured with different target cells at a 1:1 effector to target (E: T) ratio in a 96-well U-bottom plate. To assess degranulation, a staining solution containing PE-conjugated mouse anti-human CD107a antibody (clone H4A3, Biolegend) and monensin (Biolegend) was prepared by mixing 1.5 µL of the antibody, 0.2 µL of monensin, and 50 µL of RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. This solution was added to each well, which already contained 100 µL of target cells and 100 µL of effector cells. After 4 h of incubation, CAR-T cells were stained with BV421-conjugated mouse anti-human CD8 antibody and APC-conjugated mouse anti-human EGFR antibody. The degranulation assay was evaluated by flow cytometry.

Luciferase-based cytolysis assay

In white opaque 96-well plates, BAFF-R CAR-T cells or mock T cells were co-cultured with different target cells transfected with firefly luciferase at specified E: T ratios in triplicate. After 24 h, luciferase assay substrate was added to each well, and the fluorescence values were measured using a microplate reader.

Repeat antigen stimulation assay

BAFF-R CAR-T cells or mock T cells were co-cultured with different target cells treated with mitomycin C one day earlier at a 4:1 E: T ratio. On day 5, T cells were counted to obtain data for the first round of repeated stimulation. Fold expansion was calculated as (the number of viable CAR-T cells or mock T cells on Day 5) divided by (the number of viable CAR-T cells or mock T cells on Day 1). Repeat the above steps using the stimulated CAR-T cells and new mitomycin C-treated target cells until the number of CAR-T cells was insufficient to support the next round of experiments. Cumulative fold expansion was determined by multiplying the fold expansion of each round ([fold expansionn] × [fold expansionn+1] …).

Detection of activation and exhaustion markers

To detect activation markers, BAFF-R CAR-T cells or mock T cells were co-cultured with Jeko-1 cells at a 1:1 E: T ratio. After 24 h of incubation, cells were stained with BV510-conjugated CD4 (clone: SK3, Biolegend), BV421-conjugated CD8, APC-conjugated EGFR antibody, FITC-conjugated CD69 (clone: FN50, Biolegend) and PerCP/Cyanine5.5-conjugated CD25 (clone: BC96, Biolegend). The assay was evaluated by flow cytometry.

To detect exhaustion markers, BAFF-R CAR-T cells or mock T cells were co-cultured with mitomycin-C-treated Jeko-1 cells at a 4:1 E: T ratio. After three rounds of stimulation, cells were stained with PE-conjugated CD8 (clone: SK1, Biolegend), PE/Cyanine7-conjugated EGFR antibody (clone: AY13, Biolegend), APC-conjugated LAG3 (clone: 7H2C65, Biolegend), BV510-conjugated PD1 (clone: NAT105, Biolegend) and BV421-conjugated TIM3 (clone: F38-2E2, Biolegend). The assay was evaluated by flow cytometry.

Mouse xenograft models

NPG mice were acquired from Charles River (Beijing, China) and housed in the experimental animal center of ClinBridge Biotech (Nanjing, China) under specific pathogen-free conditions. NPG mice are highly immunodeficient mice established by backcrossing Il2rg knockout mice with NOD-scid mice. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Protocol (AP-C210507.45) approved the animal experiments.

A total of 45 female NPG mice aged 5–6 weeks were fed a vacuum-sealed, Co⁶⁰-γ-irradiated pelleted diet for 1 week. Each mouse was injected with 1 × 106 Jeko-1 cells transfected with firefly luciferase through the tail vein. Four days later, the tumor load was detected by bioluminescence imaging (PerkinElmer). To ensure a balanced average tumor burden across cohorts, 40 mice were randomly distributed into 8 groups (n = 5 per group) based on their baseline bioluminescence signals. Group assignment was performed by generating random numbers using the RAND function in Excel. Evaluators were blinded to the allocation sequence during group assignment. After one day, each group received a tail vein injection of 200 µL of either PBS, 4 × 10⁶ mock T cells, or 4 × 10⁶ CAR‑T cells, including 1499 (PMB101), 1500 (VAY736), 1533 (BAFF‑R#1), 1537 (BAFF‑R#5), 2050 (BAFF‑R#77), and 2053 (BAFF‑R#80). The PBS and MockT groups served as negative controls, the 1499 and 1500 groups as positive controls, and the remaining four groups (1533, 1537, 2050, 2053) as experimental groups.

The mice were weighed twice a week and imaged once weekly. Their blood was collected per week from the submandibular vein, and the order was randomized. CAR expression was detected using APC-conjugated mouse anti-human EGFR antibody. The living state was observed and recorded daily. Clinical observations included body weight, food intake, activity level, fur condition, and the presence of any signs of distress (e.g., lethargy, hunched posture, orbital tightening).

Animals were immediately euthanized if they exhibited signs of severe distress, which were defined as: body weight loss exceeding 20% of baseline weight, severe lethargy, or an inability to access food or water. At the end of the experiment, the mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation. Specifically, euthanasia was performed in a dedicated chamber with a gradual CO2 displacement rate of 20% of the chamber volume per minute to minimize animal distress. Euthanasia was confirmed by the cessation of respiration for at least 2 min, followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary physical method to ensure death.

Graphs and statistical analysis

Data analyses and graphs were performed using the GraphPad Prism software version 7. All data were presented as mean ± SD, except for mice body weight data and mice peripheral blood data, which were shown as mean ± SEM. Continuous data were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two independent groups, the unpaired t‑test was applied when data met normality and variance homogeneity assumptions; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. When two categorical factors were involved, two‑way ANOVA was used. Survival data were compared using the log‑rank test. A two‑sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. P-values are represented as follows: not significant (ns), *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, or ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Screening of fully human BAFF-R-specific clones and construction of BAFF-R CARs

A diagram of BAFF-R specific clone screening process was shown in Fig. 1A. The fully human phage antibody library was used for affinity panning to enrich the clones bound to the BAFF-R antigen (Table S2). A total of 182 monoclonal clones were selected for primary screening by ELISA (Figure S1A) and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis (Figure S1B), of which 93 clones that were specific and double-positive were sequenced, yielding 29 distinct monoclonal sequences. To verify whether the 29 clones had good binding specificity to a variety of BAFF-R+/- antigens and cell lines, ELISA and FACS were utilized again and 18 unique binding clones were obtained. The representative clones were shown in Fig. 1B and C and Figure S2. The 18 clones were then constructed into CAR-T cells and functionally assessed via CD107a degranulation and luciferase-based cytolysis assays. Based on these functional assays, four clones (#1, #5, #77, and #80) were ultimately identified (Figure S3). A schematic of their CAR structures was presented in Fig. 2A, with three of the clones (#5, #77, #80) being single-domain heavy chain antibodies and the remaining one (#1) being a fully human scFv. The PMB-101 CAR molecule—an scFv antibody from City of Hope’s autologous anti-BAFF-R CAR-T therapy—was used as a reference. The CAR was constructed using a second-generation design, which represents the most prevalent architecture in current clinical applications.

To further measure the three heavy chain antibodies’ affinities to the BAFF-R antigen, the Octet96e system was used. The result showed that clone #5 (KD = 1.49 × 10− 8M), clone #77 (KD = 6.54 × 10− 8M), and clone #80 (KD = 3.28 × 10− 9M) showed slightly lower but comparable binding affinity to BAFF-R protein when compared to PMB-101 (KD = 3.64 × 10− 9M) (Figure S4).

Clones with good specificity to both BAFF-R antigens and cell lines were used to construct CAR-T cells. Comparison of viral titer in each group was shown in Fig. 2B. Proportion of CD4 + and CD8 + cells was detected 7 days after viral transduction, and no significant difference was observed among the groups, indicating a balanced coexistence of these two subsets (Fig. 2C).The transduction efficiencies of CAR-T cells ranged from 20% to 50%, and there was no significant difference between the results measured by the EGFR antibody and BAFF-R protein (Fig. 2D). On day 7 after viral transduction, the proportion of CAR-T cells in CD4 + cells was significantly higher than that in CD8 + cells (Fig. 2E).

CD107a expression on the cell surface is a marker of cytotoxic CD8 + T cell degranulation, which can be used to evaluate the immune cell antitumor activity. By co-incubating BAFF-R CAR-T cells with BAFF-R +/- expressing cell lines, we found that the expression levels of CD107a in all BAFF-R CAR-T cells were significantly higher under the stimulation of BAFF-R positive target cells, and no BAFF-R CAR-T cells were found to be nonspecifically activated in response to the stimulation of BAFF- R negative cells (Fig. 3B). Representative flow cytometry plots were shown in Figure S5. In addition, to assess the safety of BAFF-R CAR- T cells, various cancer cells from different human tissues were co-cultured with BAFF-R CAR-T cells, and no obvious CD107a released in all BAFF-R CAR-T cells under the stimulation of these cancer cells, except for Nalm-6 cells (Figure S6).

To evaluate the tumor cell killing ability, BAFF-R CAR-T cells were co-cultured with BAFF-R +/- expressing target cells at different E: T ratios. The results showed that all BAFF-R CAR-T cells could selectively kill tumor cells expressing BAFF-R antigen, whereas they were not cytotoxic against the BAFF-R negative cell lines (Fig. 3C). In contrast to mock T cells, all BAFF-R CAR-T cells exhibited considerable cytotoxicity when co-cultured with Jeko-1 and Nalm6 cells, even at a very low E: T ratio (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3C). Among the four candidate CAR-T cell constructs, 1533 (BAFF-R#1) showed the strongest in vitro cytotoxicity. For the three constructs based on a single-domain heavy-chain antibody, 2053 (BAFF-R#88) exhibited lower killing capacity, while no significant difference was observed between 1537 (BAFF-R#5) and 2050 (BAFF-R#77) (Fig. 3C).

Activation, expansion, and exhaustion of BAFF-R CAR-T cells in vitro

CD69 and CD25 are early and late activation markers of T cells. By co-incubating BAFF-R CAR-T cells with Jeko-1 cells, we found that the expression levels of both CD25 and CD69 were significantly elevated on the surface of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ CAR-T cells in all groups (Fig. 4A, Figure S7), indicating effective activation of these CAR-T cells.

To evaluate the proliferation ability of BAFF-R CAR-T cells in vitro, mitomycin C-treated Jeko-1 cells were used to repeatedly stimulate CAR-T cells. The results revealed that 2050 (BAFF-R #77) and 1537 (BAFF-R #5) CAR-T cells demonstrated a notable amplification capability as the number of stimulation rounds increased, especially at the 5th round of stimulation (Fig. 4B). Clone #77 showed the strongest expansion, with average fold increases of 4.19, 23.66, 82.12, 175.49, and 563.82 from rounds 1 to 5, respectively. Its fold expansion became significantly greater than that of clone #5 as early as the third stimulation round (P < 0.05). Clone #5 itself displayed robust proliferation, with average fold increases of 4.15, 21.93, 53.05, 73.38, and 297.7 across the five rounds, and also showed a significantly higher fold expansion than the 1500 (VAY736) CAR-T cells at the third round (P < 0.05). In contrast, clone #1 exhibited insufficient proliferative capacity, with cell numbers declining to an extent that precluded continuation of the experiment beyond two rounds of stimulation. After two rounds of stimulation, exhaustion markers (LAG3, PD1, and TIM3) of T cells were detected, and we found that there was no significant difference in the expression levels of LAG3, PD1, and TIM3 on the surface of the four candidate BAFF-R CAR-T cells (Fig. 4C, Figure S8).

Antitumor activity of BAFF-R CAR-T cells in vivo

The experimental setup for the mouse xenograft models is shown in Fig. 5A. A tumor-bearing NPG mouse model was established by injecting Jeko-1 cells via the tail vein. Five days later, BAFF-R CAR- T cells were infused into each mouse. Tumor burden was monitored longitudinally using in vivo bioluminescence imaging, as shown in Fig. 5B. After 6 weeks of observation, we found that 1537 (BAFF-R #5) CAR-T cells exhibited obvious in vivo anti-tumor effects (Fig. 5C). Compared to the 2050 (BAFF-R #77) CAR-T cell treatment group, in which all mice survived till day 42, the tumor burden of mice in the 1537 (BAFF-R #5) CAR-T cell treatment group was significantly lower (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5C). On day 49, the mice in the 1537 CAR-T cell treatment group had a considerably longer survival period than those in the PBS group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

For the CAR-T treatment of B-cell malignancies, CD19 CAR-T cells are currently the most extensively studied. However, the persistent challenge of tumor recurrence drives the ongoing pursuit of novel therapeutic targets [13]. BAFF-R has emerged as a promising target [26–28]. As a member of the tumor necrosis factor family, BAFF-R plays a pivotal role in B cell survival, maturation, and differentiation through its interaction with the B-cell activating factor ligand [29]. BAFF-R signaling predominantly activates the NF-κB pathway and concurrently relieves the inhibition of spleen tyrosine kinase, thereby enhancing the activation of the B cell receptor signaling pathway [30]. Instead of CD19 CAR-T cells, targeting BAFF-R may result in less severe B cell aplasia since early-stage B cells do not express BAFF-R [28]. Currently, the BAFF-R CAR-T cells based on the scFv have demonstrated promising clinical efficacy and safety, showing ability to overcome CD19 antigen loss [31].

In recent years, nanoantibodies based on single-domain heavy chain variable antigen-binding domains have attracted great interest [32, 33]. They can overcome some limitations of monoclonal antibodies by virtue of their small size, simple structure, strong antigen-binding affinity, and extraordinary durability under severe conditions [34]. Furthermore, they are easier to modify, manufacture, and purify, making them more suitable for clinical application [35]. Currently, CAR-T cells with FHVH have shown promising antitumor activities [36, 37] and powerful clinical efficacy [38]. Therefore, we developed a novel BAFF-R CAR-T cell product that contained only a FHVH.

Based on the fully human phage antibody library, we screened three fully human single-domain heavy chain antibodies (Clone #5, Clone #77, and Clone #80) targeting BAFF- R. They all displayed the capacity to specifically bind to different BAFF-R antigens and BAFF-R positive cell lines. We then incorporated them into CAR-T cells. All three clones were efficiently expressed on the surface of T cells. In vitro experiments revealed that three FHVH-based CAR-T cells demonstrated adequate target cell-specific activation, while 1537 (BAFF-R #5) and 2050 (BAFF-R #77) CAR-T cells displayed stronger cytotoxic capacity and expansion ability. In animal studies, 1537 (BAFF-R #5) CAR-T cells exhibited enhanced anti-tumor activity and more effectively delayed tumor progression. Furthermore, these CAR-T cells maintained a stable, increasing proportion in the peripheral blood of mice. Thus, the Clone #5 was selected for further study. We subsequently developed CD19/BAFF-R dual-targeted CAR-T cells. The dual-targeted CAR-T cells were functionally compared to CD19 and BAFF-R single-targeted CAR-T cells in B-cell malignancies with loss of CD19 or BAFF-R antigens, and the result showed that 1537 (BAFF-R #5) CAR-T cells could overcome CD19 antigen escape [39]. Furthermore, we noted that the bicistronic BAFF/CD19 CAR outperformed the tandem CAR design [39]. This advantage may be attributed to the physical separation and independent expression of the two binding domains, which likely helps maintain their optimal conformations. This observation warrants further investigation.

In addition to the three FHVH-based CAR-T cells, we identified one scFv-based CAR- T cell, 1533 (BAFF-R #1), which also exhibited potent efficacy. 1499 (PMB-101) and 1500 (VAY736) CAR-T cells were initially used as positive controls. However, in this study, the 1499 (PMB-101) CAR-T cells did not achieve the expected optimal efficacy. This outcome may be attributed to factors related to the manufacturing process; for instance, we did not classify CAR-T cell subtypes as Qin et al. [16] did. Furthermore, 1500 (VAY736) CAR-T cells showed poor anti-tumor performance in vivo, suggesting that VAY736 may not be suitable for CAR-T cell construction. Compared to FHVH-based CAR-T cells, scFv-based CAR-T cells seemed to perform weaker efficacy, but the difference was not significant.

There are still some limitations in this study. While our study successfully identified BAFF-R-specific CAR-T clones through functional screening, more assays to rigorously dissect antigen-specific versus background cytotoxicity are needed to define the safety profile. We only confirmed the efficacy of 1537 (BAFF-R #5) CAR-T cells in mice modeled with Jeko-1 cells for several weeks. For other types of B-cell malignancies and longer efficacy, more experiments are needed for further confirmation. Furthermore, although no significant side effects had been observed in animal experiments, the safety of 1537(BAFF-R #5) CAR-T cells has yet to be verified due to the use of immunodeficient mice and the absence of cytokine analyses. Future studies should be conducted against more complex animal models or humanized mouse models to assess the therapeutic potential of BAFF-R CAR-T cells in a more clinically relevant context. Lastly, this study has not fully established the advantages of FHVH-based CAR-T cells over scFv-based CAR-T cells. Further experiments are required to comprehensively compare the functional properties of these two types of CAR-T cells.

Conclusions

Taken together, we developed a novel BAFF-R CAR-T cell product based on FHVH. This product demonstrates promising efficacy both in vitro and in mouse models. BAFF-R CAR-T cells based on FHVH may offer a potentially effective treatment option for patients with B-cell malignancies.
