Variations in Haematological and Biochemical Indices in Aggressive Periodontitis: A Case‐Control Study in Ghana
Authors: Moses Banyeh, Mary Naa Barkor Ayettey‐Adamafio, Oluwatomi Owopetu, Aminu Abba Yusuf, Lawal Abubakar Zubair, Pearl Amarquaye, Bamidele Tayo, Hannah Naa Gogwe Ayettey Anie, Richard Adanu, Ogedegbe Olugbenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe occurrence of aggressive periodontitis is higher in individuals of African ethnicity, displaying distinctive features such as early onset, rapid progression, and the loss of multiple teeth. Existing research suggests an association between aggressive periodontitis and variations in haematological and biochemical indices. Nevertheless, these connections have not been thoroughly explored within the Ghanaian population.MethodsIn this case‐control study, a total of 82 participants, comprising 50 cases and 32 controls, aged between 18 and 53 years, were enlisted between September and October 2023 at the Dental Clinic of the Korle‐Bu Teaching Hospital and the University of Ghana Dental School. The participants received a periodontal examination by a board‐certified periodontist. Fasting venous blood samples were collected and subjected to analysis for haematological and biochemical variables.ResultsThe findings indicated that the serum triglyceride (mmol/L) levels were elevated in individuals with aggressive periodontitis compared to controls (0.9 ± 0.5 vs 0.7 ± 0.2; t = −2.214, p = 0.03). Specifically in males, patients with aggressive periodontitis exhibited higher serum total cholesterol (mmol/L) levels (3.6 ± 0.8 vs 3.1 ± 0.6; t = −2.332, p = 0.03). Likewise, the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio was higher in male patients with aggressive periodontitis compared to controls.ConclusionThese findings suggest a potential association between aggressive periodontitis and systemic inflammation, as well as altered lipid metabolism.Keywords: aggressive periodontitis, Ghana, inflammation, neutrophils, triglycerides

Introduction

Periodontitis is a type of periodontal disease marked by bacterial infection affecting the gingival tissue and supporting structures of the teeth. Periodontitis is a persistent condition involving ongoing inflammation and loss of alveolar bone [1]. While the primary cause of periodontitis is bacterial infection, the host's response has been widely suggested as a major contributor to the observed damage [2]. According to the 2019 global burden of disease, there was a rise in age‐standardised prevalence, incidence, and daily adjusted life years from 1990 to 2019 worldwide, with Western Sub‐Saharan Africa bearing a substantial portion of the burden [3]. A prior study in Ghana noted a periodontitis prevalence as high as 46.7%, indicative of the challenges faced by low and middle‐income countries [4].

The underlying mechanisms of periodontitis remain unclear, but it is usually characterised by changes in haematological and biochemical factors. Bacterial infection of the gingival tissue triggers local and systemic inflammation, leading to the release of pro‐inflammatory substances like cytokines and acute‐phase proteins. Consequently, polymorphonuclear cells, including neutrophils, are released into the systemic circulation, resulting in neutrophilia or an elevated neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [5]. Polymorphonuclear cells exert bactericidal activity through the oxygen burst mechanism, generating free radicals. During phagocytosis, free radicals, along with proteolytic enzymes like collagenases and elastases, are released into the microenvironment and systemic circulation. These enzymes may contribute to tissue damage in periodontitis, causing erosion of the periodontium and subsequent tooth loss [6, 7].

Similarly, the excess release of free radicals and pro‐inflammatory elements into the systemic circulation has been implicated in observed endothelial dysfunction in periodontitis. Previous studies have indicated a link between periodontitis and metabolic syndrome, including dyslipidemia and diabetes mellitus. Although the mechanism is not fully understood, it is believed that the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, proteolytic enzymes, and other molecules into circulation may lead to insulin resistance and an increase in serum levels of pro‐atherogenic molecules such as LDL‐ and VLDL‐cholesterol [1, 8, 9]. Recent advancements in molecular studies, however, have highlighted the direct role of cholesterol in the pathophysiology of periodontitis [1].

Considering its pathophysiology, periodontitis can be classified as either chronic or aggressive based on the old classification system. Aggressive periodontitis (AgP) is distinguished by early onset, rapid progression, and the loss of alveolar bone around more than one tooth [10]. AgP can manifest as either localised aggressive (LAP) or generalised aggressive (GAP) periodontitis. According to a meta‐analytic study, the overall prevalence of AgP is approximately 1.6%, with a higher prevalence of 4.2% in Africa compared to 0.1% and 0.8% in Europe and North America, respectively [11].

A previous Sudanese study found that children of African ethnicity were 2.7 times more likely to develop AgP than African Arabs. Additionally, a study in the United States of America reported that the odds of LAP and GAP were approximately 15.1% and 24.6% higher among Black children than White children. It appears that individuals of African descent are disproportionately affected by AgP [2]. The present study aimed to investigate variations in haematological and biochemical variables between persons diagnosed with AgP and a generally healthy ethnic Ghanaian population.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This case‐control investigation took place between September and October 2023 at the Dental clinics affiliated with the Korle‐Bu Teaching Hospital and the Dental School of the University of Ghana. The Korle‐Bu Teaching Hospital serves as Ghana's premier hospital and primary referral tertiary‐level health institution. The University of Ghana Dental School is associated with the Korle‐Bu Teaching Hospital, where dental students and dental surgeons undergo training under Specialist and Consultant Physicians and Surgeons. The study encompassed 82 participants, of whom 50 (61.0%) were confirmed to have AgP, while the remaining 32 (39.0%) were deemed generally and periodontally healthy. Sex distribution revealed that 40 participants (AgP = 24, control = 16) were females, and 42 participants (AgP = 26, control = 16) were males. Participants with a history of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, smoking, chronic kidney disease, HIV/AIDS, or pregnancy, as well as those with untreated endodontic lesions, necrotising periodontal diseases, or teeth exhibiting developmental or acquired mucogingival deformities that could predispose to periodontitis or interfere with accurate phenotype diagnosis, were excluded. Control subjects consisted of patients attending the dental clinic for routine dental care who showed no evidence of periodontal or systemic disease based on the study's inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Periodontal Examination

Periodontitis was diagnosed by a board‐certified periodontist following the standardised procedure and classification by Armitage [12]. Recruited participants underwent a comprehensive periodontal examination (CPE), and available orthopantomograms (OPGs) were reviewed to confirm the diagnosis. Periodontal parameters (PP) for selected teeth in each of the four quadrants were recorded using individual periodontal charts. Inter‐examiner calibration and pretesting were conducted by three previously trained examiners. All PP were recorded twice independently for each quadrant of the mouth, and the results were compared using periodontal charts until at least 90% of duplicate measurements were within 1 mm. The Fédération Dentaire Internationale (FDI) tooth numbering system was used to present the data. Individual periodontal charts captured parameters for specific teeth in all four quadrants, including bleeding on probing (BOP), clinical attachment loss (CAL), probing pocket depth (PPD), missing teeth, tooth mobility grades, furcation involvement grades, gingival index (GI), and plaque index (PI). All teeth were examined for each participant. Manual instruments, rather than electronic devices, were used throughout the examination. The instruments, including dental mirrors, periodontal probes, and Naber's probes, were manufactured by Janbro International, United Kingdom. BOP was recorded as present or absent. CAL was measured from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the base of the periodontal pocket, calculated as PPD minus the CEJ–gingival margin distance in cases without recession, or as the sum of recession and PPD when recession was present. Measurements were taken using a UNC‐15 periodontal probe with 1 mm graduations at six sites per tooth (distobuccal, midbuccal, mesiobuccal, distolingual, midlingual, and mesiolingual). A probing force of 0.25 N and a 5° angulation were maintained. Missing teeth were noted on the periodontal chart. Tooth mobility was assessed using dental mirrors and graded from 0 (physiological mobility) to 3 (severe horizontal and vertical mobility). Details about the periodontal examination and periodontal indices have already been published [13].

Sample Size

A case‐control study by Cury et al. [14] showed that the mean ± SD (mg/dl) fasting lipid variables among patients with periodontitis versus controls were triglycerides (108.3 ± 11.3 vs. 115.7 ± 9.5), total cholesterol (185.9 ± 6.0 vs 195.5 ± 5.1), HDL (51.3 ± 2.1 vs 48.0 ± 1.6) and LDL (106.0 ± 4.6 vs 120.6 ± 3.9). Using 80% power, 5% alpha and a case‐control ratio of 1:2, the estimated minimum sample sizes for an independent sample t‐test (2‐tailed) were 78, 17, 18 and 9, respectively.

Sociodemographic and Blood Pressure Measurements

Data were collected using structured interviewer‐based questionnaires. The questionnaires were pretested to identify and refine ambiguous questions. The socio‐demographic and anthropometric data collected included age, sex, height, and weight. The body mass index was then calculated: BMI = [Weight(Kg)/Height(m)2]. Blood pressure was measured using a mercury sphygmomanometer on two occasions and then averaged.

Laboratory Analysis

A fasting (8–12 h) venous blood sample was collected from the participants' antecubital fossa following established phlebotomy guidelines. The blood was dispensed into K3EDTA anticoagulant and gel‐separator vacutainer tubes. Within an hour after collection, a complete blood count was conducted on the anticoagulated blood using the Integra 400 plus, Cobas haematology analyser (Roche, 124 Grenzacherstrasse, Switzerland). Additionally, the anticoagulated blood was utilised to measure glycated haemoglobin levels, adhering to both the National Glycohemoglobin Standardisation Programme (NGSP) and International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC) standards. The blood in the gel‐separator tubes underwent clotting before being centrifuged at 3000xg for 10 min to obtain serum. Subsequently, the serum samples were aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20°C until analysis. Upon thawing at room temperature and thorough homogenization, fasting lipid variables were determined from the serum samples using the DXC 700 AU automated biochemistry analyser (Beckman Coulter, Brea, 250 S Kraemer Blvd, United States). The resulting haematological and biochemical indices were then calculated based on the direct measurements as ratios.

Statistical Analysis

Collected data were input into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet before undergoing analysis using the SPSS (v27) statistical package. The normality of the data was assessed through both the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov and Shapiro‐Wilk tests. Subsequently, the data were summarised as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and frequency (per cent) for qualitative variables. Differences in data distribution between the two groups were evaluated using the unpaired t‐test for continuous variables and logistic regression for qualitative variables. All analyses were two‐tailed, with statistical significance set at a P‐value less than 0.050.

Ethical Approval

This study adhered to the guidelines for human subject research outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki, along with any subsequent amendments. Approval for the study was granted by the institutional review board of the Korle‐Bu Teaching Hospital (KBTH‐STC 00051/2023). Written informed consent was acquired from each participant. Participation in the study was voluntary, with no imposition, and it was not constrained by cultural, religious, or political identity.

Additionally, the NLR was higher in AgP than in controls (1.3 ± 0.5 vs. 1.0 ± 0.3; p = 0.02).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore disparities in haematological and biochemical variables between healthy individuals and those diagnosed with AgP. The findings indicated that individuals with AgP exhibited a higher serum triglyceride level and a lower platelet count compared to controls. Further subgroup analysis revealed a lower platelet count, specifically in female AgP patients. Additionally, among males with AgP, elevated levels of serum TCHOL and neutrophil count, and the NLR were observed in comparison to controls. Moreover, AgP was characterised by hypertension. These results underscore the potential systemic implications of AgP and emphasise the importance of considering sex‐specific variations in haematological and biochemical profiles.

The neutrophil numbers and NLR exhibited higher levels in AgP, specifically in males. These findings align with results reported in primary studies and systematic reviews [5, 6, 7, 15]. Neutrophils play a crucial role in the progression of periodontitis, as their normal homeostasis is essential for maintaining periodontal health. Typically mobilised for non‐specific inflammatory responses involving apoptotic and phagocytic actions, neutrophils in periodontitis may respond to bacterial biofilms in periodontal pockets, leading to damage in the periodontium. The dysregulation of neutrophilic action has been associated with the advancement of AgP [16]. Neutrophils engage in bactericidal activities through processes such as phagocytosis, where oxidative burst in the phagosome produces reactive oxygen species like superoxide anions. These ions are further converted into hydrogen peroxide. The release of the enzyme myeloperoxidase from azurophilic neutrophil granules catalyses the conversion of hydrogen peroxide to hypochlorous acid [6, 7]. In the pursuit of eliminating potential pathogenic agents, these free radicals, along with proteolytic enzymes like collagenase and elastase, are released into the extracellular microenvironment, causing tissue damage, endothelial injury, and oxidative stress. This cascade of events underscores the intricate role of neutrophils in the pathogenesis of AgP.

The oxidative stress initiated by neutrophils arises from the inhibition of nitrous oxide, a vasodilator, by superoxide radicals. Concurrently, the observed tissue damage results from the hydrolysis of elastin, fibronectin, collagen III, and collagen IV, facilitated by the proteolytic enzymes elastase and collagenase [15, 16]. Moreover, the pathological bone resorption observed in periodontitis is associated with neutrophil activation of receptors responding to NF‐kappa B ligand (RANKL). While the neutrophil count can serve as an indicator of systemic inflammation in AgP, the NLR is regarded as a more comprehensive marker. This is attributed to the NLR's ability to reflect both innate (characterised by neutrophils) and adaptive (associated with lymphocytes) immune pathways [15, 16]. Previous studies on periodontitis have demonstrated a positive correlation between NLR and inflammatory markers such as C‐reactive protein, along with pro‐inflammatory cytokines like TNF‐α, IL‐12, and IL‐18 [7].

The fasting serum triglyceride and TCHOL levels were higher in AgP, with the latter being higher specifically in males. Periodontitis, a complex inflammatory disease caused by microbes, may involve sex‐related differences linked to immune regulation [17, 18]. While men generally show poorer outcomes in infectious diseases, this pattern may not apply to periodontitis because it is a chronic, bacteria‐triggered condition. The roles of hormonal, genetic, and epigenetic factors in shaping immune responses to periodontitis remain unclear and likely understudied [19].

Key findings indicate that sex differences may influence the microbial and immune aspects of periodontitis. Men tend to harbour higher levels of periodontal pathogens, such as Prevotella intermedia and show a greater abundance of Bacteroides and Prevotella species in the gut compared to women [18]. Evidence also shows that microbes can interact with and even manipulate host sex hormones to enhance their survival. In animal studies, transferring male gut bacteria to females altered hormone levels, and testosterone‐mediated microbial differences were linked to immune responses and disease progression, such as in type 1 diabetes [20]. These findings suggest that microbial–hormonal interactions may play a key role in sex‐specific susceptibility to periodontitis. Inflammation and the host immune response play a central role in periodontal disease, with sex biology potentially influencing both innate and adaptive immunity and contributing to differences in disease susceptibility [21]. Sex steroids and X‐linked genes are thought to be key mechanisms affecting immune function. However, while some links between periodontitis and X‐linked genetic disorders have been reported, the existing evidence remains weak and inconclusive [19]. Overall, women appear to have a more regulated innate immune response and greater efficiency in clearing pathogens than men. Estrogen exposure enhances NK cell production of IFN‐γ, which has mixed effects in periodontitis—promoting bone resorption in vivo but inhibiting osteoclast formation in vitro. Additionally, women may generate higher levels of resolvins through increased synthesis of long‐chain n‐3 PUFAs, contributing to more effective resolution of periodontal inflammation [22].

Periodontitis, as a general phenomenon, is linked to disturbances in lipid homeostasis; however, this may not be universally observed [1, 8, 9]. The mechanism underlying the association between disturbances in lipid homeostasis and periodontitis remains unclear, but it is predominantly attributed to the release of proinflammatory cytokines by infected gingival tissue into the systemic circulation. Nevertheless, recent molecular and genetic studies have presented evidence of the direct involvement of cholesterol in the pathophysiology of periodontitis [1].

Cholesterol plays a vital role in cell membranes and serves as a precursor to numerous biosynthetic pathways, including those involved in fat‐soluble vitamins, hormones, and bile acids. The regulation of cholesterol homeostasis involves tightly controlled processes, encompassing synthesis, influx, efflux, and metabolism. Any disruption in these processes may contribute to the development of various diseases, including periodontitis [1, 23]. Cellular cholesterol metabolism involves the conversion of cholesterol to oxysterols by hydroxylases, acting as signalling molecules to regulate lipid and fat levels within cells. Oxysterols, as metabolites of cholesterol derived through oxygenation, play a role in maintaining cellular homeostasis [1]. Notably, it has been observed that elevated cholesterol levels are associated with alveolar bone loss and gingival inflammation, attributed to increased cholesterol uptake by periodontal tissue. This observation was corroborated by increased expression of proinflammatory factors associated with periodontitis when primary human gingival fibroblast cultures were directly treated with exogenous cholesterol [1].

AgP is also characterised by hypertension and low platelet count. Previous studies have shown that periodontitis is associated with an increase in blood pressure, and its treatment may reverse it, particularly in patients diagnosed with arterial hypertension [24, 25]. The key factor linking periodontitis to hypertension is the dysbiosis of the oral microbiota. Normally, commensal bacteria in the mouth, such as Staphylococcus, help convert dietary nitrates into nitric oxide, a compound with vasodilatory effects. In periodontitis, changes in the oral microbiota disrupt this nitrate/nitrite‐reducing process, impairing nitric oxide production and potentially contributing to elevated blood pressure [25]. In addition to oral microbiota dysbiosis, other proposed links between periodontitis and hypertension include the spread of local oral inflammation to the body (systemic inflammation), the host's immune response, direct effects of oral microbes on blood vessels, and impaired endothelial function [26, 27]. While this study showed that periodontitis is characterised by low platelet count, a meta‐analysis indicated the reverse [28, 29]. The difference in results may arise from the larger sample size and the aggregation of results from several primary studies in meta‐analysis.

This study contributes essential but scarce information on the connection between AgP and changes in haematological and biochemical variables within the Ghanaian population. The scope of the study extended beyond directly measured variables to encompass their derivatives and indices as well. However, the authors acknowledge that, unlike a cohort study design, a case‐control study design cannot measure change in variables, which is more reliable.

Conclusion

Aggressive periodontitis continues to pose a challenge to oral health and may contribute to alterations in haematological and biochemical variables. This study observed that, overall, neutrophil count, NLR, and triglycerides are elevated in AgP. Additionally, total cholesterol was higher in male patients with AgP. The findings suggest a potential association between AgP, systemic inflammation, elevated fasting triglyceride levels in both sexes, and increased cholesterol in males.
