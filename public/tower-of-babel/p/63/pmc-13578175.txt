ITGB5 promotes immune escape in hepatocellular carcinoma by regulating PD-L1 through SETDB1/Hippo signaling pathway
Authors: Mengmeng Wang, Li Gu, Ruijiang Zeng, Chen Zhang, Jing Zhang, Pian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe overall response rate to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in HCC patients remains low. It has been reported that ITGB5 correlate with the immune infiltration of tumors. Our previous study showed that ITGB5 reduces HCC cell sensitivity to TKIs. However, the role of ITGB5 in HCC immunity remains unclear.MethodsThe mRNA and protein expression of ITGB5, SETDB1, LATS2 and PD-L1 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells and tissues were determined by RT-qPCR, Western Blot and Immunofluorescence. PD-L1 positive cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. The relationship between ITGB5 expression and immune cell infiltration was investigated by applying TIMER database. The effects of ITGB5 on the immune response and immunotherapy of hepatocellular carcinoma were determined by constructing a spontaneous tumor model of hepatocellular carcinoma and a subcutaneous tumor model in mice.ResultsITGB5 promotes immune escape in HCC by activating PD-L1 expression, a target gene of the Hippo signaling pathway, through the SETDB1-LATS2 axis. ITGB5 reduces the susceptibility of HCC to immunotherapy. Importantly, ITGB5 knockdown enhanced the efficacy of PD-1 therapy in mice harboring HCC.ConclusionsITGB5 plays an important role in the immune response of hepatocellular carcinoma. ITGB5 may serve as a biomarker for evaluating immunotherapy efficacy and represent a promising therapeutic target for HCC treatment, either alone or in combination with anti-PD-1 antibodies.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13402-026-01240-3.Keywords: ITGB5, SETDB1, Hippo, PD-L1, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of primary liver cancer, accounting for approximately 90% of cases. It is also the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1–2]. For patients with intermediate to advanced HCC, targeted therapies such as sorafenib have improved survival, but the median survival time remains less than two years. Primary or acquired resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) results in only 30% of HCC patients responding effectively to this therapy [3]. Recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including programmed death 1 (PD-1) and PD ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors, have emerged as promising therapeutic strategies for HCC [4]. Currently, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved the combination of atezolizumab, an anti-PD-L1 antibody, and bevacizumab, an anti-VEGFA antibody, as first-line treatment options. However, the overall response rate to ICIs in HCC patients remains low, and there are no validated biomarkers to guide clinical decisions [5]. Research indicates that key genes involved in HCC progression not only serve as prognostic biomarkers for HCC but also provide potential avenues for targeted drug development [6, 7]. Thus, exploring the molecular basis of immune response and evasion in HCC is critical for identifying biomarkers that predict the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and designing combination therapies.

The Hippo signaling pathway is a crucial tumor suppressor pathway that inhibits hepatocyte proliferation and survival, and it has been found that the Hippo signaling pathway is closely related to tumor immunity [8–9]. Activation of LATS1/2 in this pathway phosphorylates and inactivates the oncogenes YAP/TAZ, which are otherwise stabilized and translocated to the nucleus, where they bind to transcription factors and regulate gene expression to promote cell proliferation and survival [10–11]. The Hippo pathway has been implicated in the regulation of PD-L1 expression in cancer cells [12]. MST1/2 and LATS1/2, upstream kinases of the Hippo pathway, inhibit PD-L1 expression, whereas TAZ and YAP enhance PD-L1 levels in breast and lung cancer cell lines [13–14]. Dysregulation of the Hippo pathway and subsequent upregulation of PD-L1 can confer resistance to immunotherapy [15–16].

SET structural domain bifurcation 1 (SETDB1) is a histone H3K9 methyltransferase that is essential for H3K9 trimethylation-mediated gene silencing, and has been found to be involved in gene silencing in a variety of cellular processes, including cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, differentiation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), cell growth and metabolism [17]. Increasing evidence suggests that SETDB1 is closely associated with tumourigenesis [18]. overexpression of SETDB1 is associated with an aggressive phenotype in cancers such as breast, oral, liver, lung and myeloid leukaemia, and its activity promotes rapid cell growth and proliferation [19–21]. In addition to its role in cancer progression and proliferation, SETDB1 is recognised as a key regulator of immune cell differentiation and function, involved in immune cell development, immune evasion and innate immune responses. Previous studies have shown that SETDB1 depletion leads to transposable element (TE) activation and triggers an interferon response that promotes NK and CD8 + T-cell activation [22]. SETDB1 regulates PD-L1 expression in colorectal cancer through a complex pathway involving FOSB, miR-22 and BATF3 [23]. SETDB1 amplification and aberrant activation have been found to be significantly associated with poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [24] and lead to HCC development, immune escape and immunotherapy resistance [25], but the exact mechanism needs to be further elucidated.

Integrins, major cell adhesion receptors for extracellular matrix components [26], are a family of transmembrane heterodimers composed of 18 α-integrins and 8 β-integrins [27]. The integrin β (ITGB) superfamily, a subset of integrins, includes eight different members in humans [28]. ITGB family members interact with the extracellular matrix and cytoskeleton to regulate various cellular processes, including proliferation, carcinogenesis, and immune responses [29]. ITGB5 is highly expressed as an oncogene in HCC cells and is known to promote cancer cell migration, invasion, and TGF-β-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [30]. Our previous study showed that ITGB5 interacts with EPS15 to prevent EGFR degradation, thereby activating the AKT-mTOR and MAPK signaling pathways, reducing HCC cell sensitivity to TKIs [31]. However, the role of ITGB5 in HCC immunity remains unclear.

In this study, we explored the role of ITGB5 in HCC immunotherapy. We found that ITGB5 promotes immune escape in HCC by activating PD-L1 expression, a target gene of the Hippo signaling pathway, through the SETDB1-LATS2 axis. Notably, ITGB5 knockdown enhanced the sensitivity of HCC cells to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, suggesting that silencing ITGB5 or combining it with PD-1 inhibitors may represent a novel therapeutic approach for HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human HCC cell lines (HepG2 and Huh7 and Hepa1-6) were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Science Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 µg/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. All cell lines were characterized by short tandem repeat sequences (STRs) DNA analysis and tested for mycoplasma contamination with the Universal Mycoplasma Detection Kit (ATCC 30–1012 K) before use and were not contaminated with mycoplasma.

Antibodies and chemicals

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium and 10% fetal bovine serum were purchased from Gibco Biotechnology Co. Ltd. RIPA Lysis Buffer (P0013B) and BCA protein assay kit (P0010) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology. Lipofectamine 2000 (11668030) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Opti-MEM (31985070) was purchased from Gibco Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Puromycin (HY-K1057) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). UNC10013(HY-173156) was purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). The antibodies used in this research were as follows Anti-ITGB5 (Proteintech Cat# 28543-1-AP WB: 1:3000); Anti-SETDB1(proteintech Cat# 11231-1-AP WB: 1:2000); anti- LATS2 (proteintech Cat# 20276-1-AP WB: 1:1000); anti- YAP1 (proteintech Cat# 13584-1-AP WB: 1:1000); anti- Phospho-YAP1(Ser397) (proteintech 29018-1-AP WB: 1:2000); anti-PD-L1(proteintech 66248-1-Ig WB: 1:4000).anti-CTGF (proteintech 25474-1-AP WB: 1:1000); anti-CYR61 (proteintech 26689-1-AP WB: 1:4000); anti-LaminB (proteintech 10494-1-AP WB: 1:3000); anti-GAPDH (proteintech 12987-1-AP WB: 1:5000).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and western blot

Tissues and cells were lysed on ice using RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime P0013B) with 1% Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (GLPBIO GK10019). Lysates were rotated at 4 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged at 13,100 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. Protein A/G agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and primary antibodies were added to the supernatant and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The beads were washed with lysis buffer on ice for five times, and then subjected to Western blot analysis following standard protocols.

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (#9109 Takara Bio, Beijing, China) and reverse transcribed to cDNA using the PrimeScriptTM RT Kit (#RR037A Takara). Real-time PCR amplification and detection were performed using TB Green™ Fast qPCR Mix (#RR430A Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences for the related genes are listed in Table S2.

Plasmids and transfection

Overexpression plasmids FLAG-ITGB5 (GV657 vector), FLAG-SETDB1 (GV657 vector), FLAG-LATS2 (GV657 vector), and FLAG-YAP1 (GV657 vector) were purchased from Shanghai Gene Chem Co. Ltd. Small hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting ITGB5, SETDB1, LATS2 and YAP1 were purchased from Shanghai Genechem Co.,Ltd. HepG2 and Huh7 cells were inoculated into 6-well plates and transfected with shRNA and overexpression plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) and Opti-MEM (Gibco, Shanghai, China). After 8 h, Opti-MEM was replaced with complete DMEM medium. After 72 h, transfected cells were screened with appropriate concentrations of puromycin (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) to construct stable expression cell lines. Sequences of relevant shRNAs are shown in Table S3.

Flow cytometry

HepG2 and Huh7 cells infected with shControl or shITGB5 were prepared and stained for flow cytometry using APC anti-human CD274 antibody (Proteintech, 66248-1-IG, 1:100) for 40 min at 4 °C. The cells were then washed three times with PBS, resuspended in 100 µl staining buffer, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

ELISA assay

The ELISA kit (#E-HSEL-M0007, Elabscience, China) was used to detect serum IFN-γ level. In brief, samples and standards were added to the sample and standard wells. The plate was covered with a membrane and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. The liquid in the wells is discarded and washed. Biotinylated antibody solution was added to each well, covered with a membrane, then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Enzyme conjugate working solution was added to each well, and incubated for 30 min. Substrate solution was added to each well, and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. Stop solution was added to each well to terminate the reaction. The OD value was immediately measured at the corresponding wavelength using an enzyme microplate reader (Thermo, USA).

Nuclear‒cytoplasmic extraction

The NE-PER™ Kit (#78833, Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) was used to isolate the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions according to the instructions of manufacturer. Proteins localized in the cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments were subsequently assessed via Western blotting.

MG132 proteasome inhibition assay

Transfect HepG2 and Huh7 cells according to the above steps. Forty-eight hours after transfection, replace the culture medium with medium containing a final concentration of 10 µM/mL MG132(S2619, Selleck) and continue culturing the cells for 6 h. Extract proteins according to the above steps and perform Western blotting analysis.

Protein half-life detection assays

HepG2 and Huh7 cells were transfected according to the aforementioned steps. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the culture medium was replaced with medium containing a final concentration of 40 µg/mL CHX(S7418, Selleck), and the cells were continued to be cultured. Protein was extracted at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h after medium replacement according to the aforementioned steps, and Western blotting was performed for detection. After exposing the Western Blotting bands, use ImageJ software to quantify the protein bands. Finally, use Prism software to plot the half-life curve of the SETDB1 protein based on the quantification results.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed using the Chromatin Extraction Kit (Abcam, USA) and ChIP Kit Magnetic - One Step (Abcam, USA). Purified DNA was analyzed by RT-qPCR. The primer sequences used for ChIP-qPCR are shown in Table S4.

Glutathione S-transferase pull-down assay

The RIPA lysate was combined with the previously indicated phosphatase and protease inhibitors to extract the total protein from the cells. Glutathione Sepharose beads were used to immobilize the GST fusion protein (GE Healthcare Life sciences, USA). Beads were cultivated in the cell lysate at 4 ◦ C for an entire night. On the next day, the beads were re-suspended in the sample buffer after being cleaned six times with a binding buffer. Following SDS-PAGE, the glues were stained for six to eight hours using Coomassie Blue Staining Solution (#G2059, Servicebio, China). Glues were photographed and utilized for follow-up analysis after they were decolorized.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

The cells subjected to different treatments were inoculated into 96-well plates containing 200 µl of medium at a density of 1100 cells per well. The absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured at specific time points. The medium was subsequently removed, and 100 µl of fresh medium containing 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent (Beo Tianmei, China) was added. The wells were incubated for 30 min in an incubator at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured via a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Generation of HCC xenografts in mice

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Shulaibao Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). The mice were randomly divided into four groups of five. Hepatocellular carcinoma cells with different treatments (5 × 10^6 in 100 µL 1 × PBS) were injected into the left abdomen of the mice, and tumor growth was monitored. After the allogeneic grafts reached 50 mm^3, mice carrying the same type of tumors were randomly divided into different groups (simple randomization) and treated with anti-PD-1 (BioXcell, clone RMP1-14) or IgG (BioXcell, clone 2A3) on days 0, 3, and 6. Tumor dimensions, including width and length, were assessed with calipers every three days. The volume of the tumors was determined using the formula (L × W^2 × 0.5). After 21 days, the mice were humanely euthanized, and the tumors were carefully dissected, weighed, and photographed. The tumors were subsequently preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for subsequent immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical staining.

Syngeneic tumor model treatment protocol

Six-week-old C57BL/6 mice bearing spontaneously developed hepatocellular carcinoma tumors were purchased from Shulaibao Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). After one week of acclimatization, different adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) purchased from Brain Case (Shenzhen, China) were injected via the tail vein. Every three days, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 300 µg of anti-PD-1 antibody or isotype antibody (Bio X Cell, West Lebanon, New Hampshire, USA). Mice were monitored daily, and observations were completed at 11 weeks. The mice were humanely euthanized, and their livers were collected for measurement, weighed, photographed, and subsequently stored in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for further experiments.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue microarray slides (D097LV01) were purchased from Zhongke Guanghua (Xi’an) Intelligent Biological Technology and stained with ITGB5 antibody (Proteintech, 1:2000), LATS2 antibody (Proteintech, 1:2000), and PD-L1 antibody (Proteintech, 1:2000) using an IHC staining kit (Bios Biological Technology, Wuhan, China).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

HepG2 cells transfected with shControl or shITGB5 were inoculated into a six-well plate with a coverslip placed in each well. After 24 h, the cells were washed three times with PBS. The cells were then fixed, permeabilized, and blocked. Following an overnight incubation with the primary antibody(anti- YAP1 (proteintech Cat# 13584-1-AP IF: 1:200), the cells were incubated with the secondary antibody, stained with DAPI for nuclear staining, and the coverslips were mounted.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis were carried out by one-sided or two-sided paired Student’s t-test for single comparison and one-way ANOVA with a post-hoc test for multiple comparisons, and P values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the values are expressed as the means ± SD.

Results

Silencing ITGB5 reactivates the LATS2-hippo signaling pathway.

To demonstrate the role of ITGB5 in hepatocellular carcinoma immunity, we performed RNA-seq analysis of ITGB5 knockdown Huh7 cells to identify differentially expressed genes (Fig. 1A). Western blot experiments confirmed successful knockdown(Fig. S1A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that ITGB5 is involved in the regulation of the Hippo, AMPK, TNF, TGF-beta, PI3K-AKT, Rap1, and other cancer-related processes (Fig. 1B), and could play a role in a variety of cancers such as colorectal, small cell lung, thyroid, pancreatic, endometrial, hepatocellular, bladder, chronic myelogenous leukaemia, prostate, gastric and other tumours (Fig. 1C). GO analysis revealed that ITGB5 could participate in biological processes such as immune response and histone modification in hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Fig. 1D, E). Since the Hippo pathway is critical in cellular immunity [32], we focused on whether ITGB5 regulates the Hippo pathway. Among these differentially expressed genes, a key component of the Hippo pathway, LATS2, emerged as a responsive gene sensitive to ITGB5 knockdown (Fig. 1F). RT-PCR and Western blot analyses showed that ITGB5 silencing significantly elevated the mRNA and protein levels of LATS2, the key component of the Hippo pathway, in hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Fig. 1G, H), whereas ITGB5 overexpression significantly downregulated LATS2 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1I, J). Immunohistochemical staining in human hepatocellular carcinoma tissues (n = 79) also demonstrated a negative correlation between ITGB5 and LATS2 expression (Fig. 1K, L). It has been shown that the Hippo signalling pathway is tumour-suppressive and that the core molecule LATS2 inhibits the transcriptional regulation of downstream genes by YAP1 in the nucleus by phosphorylating YAP1 to impede its entry into the nucleus [33, 34]. To further demonstrate whether ITGB5 could affect the nuclear translocation of YAP1, we performed Western blot experiments, which showed that ITGB5 knockdown increased the phosphorylation of its downstream YAP1 protein and decreased the YAP1 content in the nucleus (Fig. 1M). Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation and Western blot analysis were performed on ITGB5-knockdown hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines. Results demonstrated that ITGB5 knockdown increased cytoplasmic YAP protein levels while decreasing nuclear YAP protein levels (Fig. S1B). Consistent with the protein blotting results, immunofluorescence showed that the nuclear localization of YAP1 in ITGB5 knockdown cells was greatly impeded (Fig. 1N). CTGF and CYR61 are major target genes in the Hippo signaling pathway, which can be activated by YAP1 transcriptional activity in the nucleus. RT-PCR and Western blot analysis further demonstrated that the mRNA and protein levels of CTGF and CYR61 were downregulated in ITGB5-silenced hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Fig. 1O, P). In contrast, the mRNA and protein levels of CTGF and CYR61 were upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma cells with ITGB5 gene overexpression (Fig. S1C, D).Taken together, our data suggest that ITGB5 inhibits the Hippo pathway in hepatocellular carcinoma cells by downregulating LATS2 expression.

ITGB5 regulates LATS2 expression through SETDB1.

To determine the specific mechanism by which ITGB5 silencing promotes LATS2 expression and activates Hippo signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, we performed bioinformatics analysis of the TCGA database and screened out the potential transcription factors and cotranscription factors of LATS2 (Fig. 2A), and then docked the transcription factors and cotranscription factors of LATS2 with ITGB5 in computer molecular simulations, in which SETDB1 had the lowest binding energy and the SETDB1 had the strongest binding ability to ITGB5 (Fig. 2B). Our previous GOBP analysis revealed that ITGB5 is involved in the biological processes of histone modification and histone methylation in hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Fig. 1D), while SETDB1 is a specific methyltransferase involved in tumour metastasis and invasion by mediating the transcriptional silencing of autosomal genes by methylation of histone 3 lysine 9(H3K9) [35]. And we reviewed the literature and found that SETDB1 promotes HCC development, immune escape and immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance [25], so we speculated that ITGB5 regulates LATS2 expression through SETDB1. For further verification, we transfected shSETDB1 plasmid in HepG2 and Huh7 hepatocellular carcinoma cells. Western blot and RT-qPCR experiments validated that shSETDB1 and shLATS2 exhibit excellent knockdown efficiency(Fig. S2A, B). Subsequently, we performed RT-qPCR, and the results showed that the knockdown of SETDB1 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells significantly increased the mRNA level of LATS2 (Fig. 2C). Therefore, we explored the relationship between ITGB5, LATS2, and SETDB1. In ITGB5 knockdown cells, we found that SETDB1 mRNA expression was unchanged but protein levels were decreased (Fig. 2D, E). Similarly, overexpression of ITGB5 resulted in unchanged SETDB1 mRNA expression but increased protein levels (Fig. 2F, G). Knockdown of SETDB1 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells resulted in increased mRNA and protein levels of LATS2 (Fig. 2H, I), whereas overexpression of SETDB1 down-regulated mRNA and protein levels of LATS2 (Fig. 2J, K). This suggests that ITGB5 regulates SETDB1 expression at the post-transcriptional level. Further experiments showed that overexpression of SETDB1 rescued the ITGB5 silencing-induced increase in LATS2 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2L, M), and knockdown of SETDB1 rescued the ITGB5 overexpression-induced decrease in LATS2 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2N, O). In contrast, knockdown of SETDB1 did not restore the ITGB5 silencing-induced increase in LATS2 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2P, Q), and overexpression of SETDB1 did not restore the ITGB5 overexpression-induced decrease in LATS2 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2R, S). In conclusion, we demonstrated that ITGB5 regulates LATS2 expression through SETDB1(Fig. 2T).

ITGB5 transcriptionally regulates LATS2 expression by downregulating SETDB1 ubiquitination.

The above experiments have demonstrated that ITGB5 regulates SETDB1 expression, but the exact mechanism remains unclear. Next, we further validated the interaction between ITGB5 and SETDB1 in 293T tool cells and HCC cells. Indeed, ITGB5 and SETDB1 immunoprecipitated each other (Fig. 3A). In this study, ITGB5 has been found to regulate SETDB1 expression at the post-transcriptional level. Therefore, we hypothesised that ITGB5 may affect the degradation of SETDB1 protein. Subsequently, we treated HepG2 and Huh7 cells with 10 µM of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 for 6 h to block the proteasomal degradation pathway of the protein, and then assessed SETDB1 protein levels. CCK-8 assays confirmed no cytotoxicity of this treatment condition in HepG2 and Huh7 cells(Fig. S3A, B). The results showed that MG132 treatment effectively rescued the decrease in SETDB1 protein levels in ITGB5 knockdown HepG2 and Huh7 cells (Fig. 3B). Additionally, we treated HepG2 cells with cycloheximide (CHX) to block protein synthesis and then examined SETDB1 turnover. The results showed that ITGB5 knockdown significantly accelerated the degradation rate of SETDB1(Fig. 3C). Meanwhile, overexpression of ITGB5 in HepG2 cells stabilised SETDB1 protein levels under CHX treatment conditions (Fig. 3D). Since proteasomal degradation of cellular proteins is usually mediated by ubiquitination [36], we measured the ubiquitination status of SETDB1 in ITGB5 knockdown or overexpressed cells. Immunoprecipitation experiments revealed that silencing ITGB5 significantly enhanced SETDB1 ubiquitination levels (Fig. 3E). ITGB5 overexpression markedly reduced SETDB1 ubiquitination levels (Fig. 3F). To explore the specific mechanism by which ITGB5 regulates SETDB1 ubiquitination, we designed truncation mutants based on the domains of ITGB5 and SETDB1(Fig. 3G). GST pull-down experiments demonstrated that the cytoplasmic domain of ITGB5 interacts with the C-terminal domain of SETDB1 to regulate SETDB1 ubiquitination(Fig. 3H). Transcriptional silencing of euchromatin genes by methylation of H3K9, which is involved in a variety of biological processes [37, 38].Previous studies have confirmed that SETDB1 suppresses the expression of LATS2. As a histone methylation regulator, SETDB1 plays a crucial role in gene expression regulation. ChIP-seq results reveal that SETDB1 binds to the transcription start site of LATS2(Fig. 3I). Does the regulation of LATS2 by ITGB5 depend on SETDB1-mediated histone methylation? ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed that ITGB5 knockdown significantly reduced H3K9me3 levels in the LAST2 promoter region༈Fig. 3J, K༉. These findings indicate that ITGB5 enhances histone methylation modifications in the LATS2 promoter region by downregulating SETDB1 ubiquitination, leading to LATS2 transcriptional repression.

ITGB5 regulates PD-L1 expression through the hippo pathway.

Research reports indicate that YAP1 promotes PD-L1 transcription in the cell nucleus, while LATS2 increases YAP1 phosphorylation, inhibits its nuclear translocation, and affects tumor immune responses [13–14]. we speculated that ITGB5 could play a role in the immune response of HCC by affecting PD-L1 expression through LATS2. RT-PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that LATS2 silencing significantly elevated the mRNA and protein levels of PD-L1 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Fig. S4A, B), while LATS2 overexpression significantly downregulated PD-L1 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S4C, D). Knockdown of YAP1 inhibited PD-L1 mRNA and protein expression in hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Fig. S4E, F), whereas overexpression of YAP1 upregulated PD-L1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S4G, H). We next performed a correlation analysis of ITGB5 and PD-L1 expression levels using data from the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database. The results showed that ITGB5 and PD-L1 (CD274) expression were positively correlated (Fig. 4A). Flow cytometry further demonstrated that PD-L1-positive cells were significantly reduced after knockdown of ITGB5 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Fig. 4B). Additionally, RT-PCR and Western blot analyses showed that ITGB5 silencing significantly reduced PD-L1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4C, D), while ITGB5 overexpression upregulated PD-L1 expression(Fig. 4E, F). Staining in human hepatocellular carcinoma tissue microarrays (n = 79) also showed a positive correlation between ITGB5 and PD-L1 expression (Fig. 4G). Further experiments showed that knockdown of LATS2 rescued the decrease in mRNA and protein levels of PD-L1 caused by ITGB5 silencing (Fig. 4H, I), and overexpression of LATS2 restored the increase in mRNA and protein levels of PD-L1 caused by ITGB5 overexpression (Fig. 4J, K). Taken together, ITGB5 can promote PD-L1 expression by downregulating LATS2 expression. Additionally, we investigated whether ITGB5 affects other immune checkpoints also involved in HCC immune escape. Correlation analyses using GEPIA data revealed that among the immune checkpoints CTLA-4, LAG3, HAVCR2, and TIGIT, only HAVCR2 exhibited a positive correlation with ITGB5 expression (Fig. S4I). In HepG2 and Huh7 cells, ITGB5 silencing reduced the mRNA levels of CTLA-4, LAG3, HAVCR2, and TIGIT; however, at the protein level, only HAVCR2 was consistently downregulated (Fig. S4J, K). These results suggest that ITGB5 may contribute to immune escape by affecting other immune checkpoints in HCC cells, warranting further investigation. Given the central role of the PD-L1/PD-1 axis in HCC immune evasion and its established clinical relevance, we focused our subsequent experiments on evaluating the effect of ITGB5 on PD-L1 expression.

ITGB5 promotes immune escape in HCC cells through the SETDB1/LATS2/PD-L1 Axis.

RT-PCR and Western blot analyses in HepG2 and Huh7 cells showed that ITGB5 knockdown caused a decrease in PD-L1 expression, whereas overexpression of SETDB1 or silencing of LATS2 rescued the down-regulation of PD-L1 induced by ITGB5 knockdown (Fig. 5A, B). To demonstrate in vivo experiments that ITGB5 promotes hepatocellular carcinoma immune escape through the SETDB1/Hippo/PD-L1 axis. In this study, a mouse subcutaneous graft tumour model was constructed using Hepa1-6 hepatocellular carcinoma cells in C57BL/6 mice. We have validated that ITGB5 upregulates PD-L1 expression in Hepa1-6 cells (Fig. S5A-D). Animal model results showed that knockdown of ITGB5 significantly slowed down the growth of the transplanted tumour and decreased the wet weight of the tumour. On the contrary, overexpression of SETDB1 accelerated the growth of the transplanted tumour and increased the tumour weight. Notably, overexpression of SETDB1 restored the tumour growth inhibition caused by ITGB5 knockdown(Fig. 5C-E). Additionally, we constructed an in situ model of HCC with normal immune function. Immunohistochemical staining of collected tumor tissues for ITGB5 confirmed the success of the model construction (Fig. 5F). Similarly, the number and volume of tumours on the livers of mice decreased after ITGB5 knockdown, while the overexpression of SETDB1 increased the number and volume of liver tumours, and the overexpression of SETDB1 reinstated the inhibition of tumour growth caused by the ITGB5 knockdown (Fig. 5F-I). Subsequently, RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis of extracted mouse HCC tissues revealed significantly downregulated PD-L1 expression in ITGB5-deficient HCC cells(Fig. S5E, F). Changes in the number of immune cell infiltrates play an important role in the immune escape of a wide range of tumour cells, with T cells being an important part of mediating tumour immunity [39]. Previous studies have shown that PD-1 of T cells can interact with PD-L1 of tumour cells to inhibit the activation, expansion and effector functions of specific CD8 + T cells, thus helping tumour cells to evade immune destruction [40], and increased PD-L1 expression of tumour cells can inhibit the activity of CD8 + T cells, affecting the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade therapy [41]. To further visualise the effect of ITGB5 on HCC cell immune escape via SETDB1/Hippo/PD-L1 axis, the harvested hepatocellular carcinoma spontaneous tumour-forming tissues were subjected to immunofluorescence staining in this study. The results showed that ITGB5 knockdown increased the tumour infiltration of CD3 + CD4+CD8 + cells in mouse hepatocellular carcinoma, while SETDB1 overexpression decreased the tumour infiltration of CD3 + CD4+ CD8 + cells, and SETDB1 overexpression restored the increase in tumour immune cell infiltration caused by ITGB5 knockout (Fig. 5J-M). IFN-γ is a key effector molecule of the immune response. To assess immune cell function, we collected mouse serum and measured IFN-γ levels. The results showed that ITGB5 knockdown increased IFN-γ levels, whereas SETDB1 overexpression decreased IFN-γ levels. Furthermore, SETDB1 overexpression restored the increase in IFN-γ levels induced by ITGB5 knockdown(Fig. S5G). In summary, ITGB5 remodels the tumor immune microenvironment and promotes immune escape of hepatocellular carcinoma cells through the SETDB1/HIPPO/PD-L1 axis. Studies indicate that EML4-ALK gene expression is more common in younger, non-smoking NSCLC patients [42]. PD-L1 expression levels are higher in patients with squamous cell carcinoma of the lung compared to those with lung adenocarcinoma [43]. Thus, clinical and pathological characteristics correlate with the expression of certain molecules. To investigate whether pathological features in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients correlate with relevant molecular expression levels, we analyzed the relationship between Grade classification and ITGB5 and PD-L1 expression levels in human HCC tissue microarrays. Results showed that higher Grade classification correlated with increased ITGB5 and PD-L1 expression levels (Fig. S5H).

Silencing ITGB5 enhances the efficacy of Anti-PD-1 therapy in HCC Mice.

We have demonstrated that ITGB5 can regulate PD-L1 expression in HCC cells. To further explore the role of ITGB5 in HCC immunotherapy, we applied the TIMER database to investigate the relationship between ITGB5 expression and immune cell infiltration. The mRNA expression level of ITGB5 was negatively correlated with the infiltration levels of various immune cells (e.g., CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, B cells, monocytes, and natural killer cells) in HCC tissues (Fig. 6A). Mice with spontaneous hepatocellular carcinoma were treated with a PD-1 inhibitor and classified into responders and non-responders based on survival, tumor size, and tumor number. Compared to non-responders, responders exhibited significantly lower ITGB5 mRNA and protein expression in liver tumors (Fig. S6A-C), suggesting ITGB5 as a biomarker for anti-PD-1 therapy. We then assessed whether ITGB5 knockdown improves sensitivity to anti-PD-1 treatment. Hepa1-6 cells infected with shControl or shITGB5 lentivirus were subcutaneously injected into immunoreactive C57BL/6 mice, which were subsequently treated with anti-PD-1 antibody or IgG control antibody. Knockdown of ITGB5 inhibited tumor growth, whereas anti-PD-1 treatment had a limited effect on the growth of shControl tumors. Interestingly, in the ITGB5 knockdown group, anti-PD-1 treatment strongly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 6B-D). Additionally, we constructed an in situ model of HCC with normal immune function (Fig. 6E). Immunohistochemical staining of collected tumors for ITGB5 confirmed the success of model construction (Fig. 6F). Similarly, the combination of ITGB5 silencing and anti-PD-1 antibody further impaired tumor proliferation compared to anti-PD-1 treatment alone (Fig. 6F-I). Tumors were harvested for immunofluorescence staining at the end of treatment. Anti-PD-1 treatment moderately increased tumor infiltration of CD3 + CD4+ CD8 + T cells in shControl mice, whereas in the ITGB5 knockdown group, anti-PD-1 treatment significantly increased tumor infiltration of CD3 + CD4+ CD8 + T cells (Fig. 6J-M). Similarly, ITGB5 knockdown significantly increased serum IFN-γ levels in anti-PD-1-treated mice (Fig. S6D). In summary, ITGB5 silencing remodels the immunosuppressive microenvironment by downregulating PD-L1 expression, which contributes to the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. To advance the translational potential of these findings, we explored pharmacological inhibitors against ITGB5 and SETDB1. Currently, no specific ITGB5 inhibitor is available, and broad-spectrum integrin inhibitors (e.g., cilengitide) risk non-specific effects. Research on the recently developed SETDB1 inhibitor UNC10013 remains limited. In our study, UNC10013 treatment significantly downregulated PD-L1 expression in HCC cell lines HepG2 and Huh7 (Fig. S6E). In a spontaneous liver cancer mouse model, this inhibitor enhanced sensitivity to PD-1 blockade (Fig. S6F, G). These findings delineate a translational pathway and furnish a promising tool compound, yet considerable challenges remain.

Discussion

Immunotherapy has shown unique efficacy in treating a wide range of cancers, with checkpoints such as PD-1 and CTLA-4 approved for the treatment of many cancers. However, the emergence of drug resistance poses new challenges to clinical treatment. Previous studies indicate that PRDX6 promotes HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by regulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR and Notch1 signaling pathways. Knocking down PRDX6 significantly inhibits liver cancer progression, suggesting that targeting PRDX6 holds promise as a novel therapeutic target for hepatocellular carcinoma [44]. Wang et al. revealed that FIGNL1 regulates HCC formation by modulating extracellular matrix-receptor interactions through HMMR regulation. Developing inhibitors targeting the novel therapeutic target FIGNL1 may represent a unique and effective treatment strategy for HCC patients [45]. Therefore, exploring key mechanisms of tumor progression and identifying novel targets for tumor diagnosis and treatment are crucial for developing new anticancer drugs. In this study, we identified a novel mechanism regulating PD-L1, which leads to PD-L1 upregulation in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells and subsequently induces immune escape in liver cancer cells. Targeting key molecules in this pathway can significantly enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy for liver cancer.

Integrins are heterodimeric (α/β subunit) cell surface glycoproteins that bind to ECM proteins and connect via their cytoplasmic structural domains to components of the intracellular actin cytoskeleton. In addition to their structural role in bridging the ECM and intracellular proteins, they play a key role in signal transduction events. As a member of the integrin family, ITGB5 has been shown to promote cancer progression in various tumors, including gastric cancer [46], breast cancer [30], and pancreatic cancer [47]. It has also been shown to reduce sensitivity to TKIs in HCC. In the current study, we found that ITGB5 promoted tumor immune evasion by upregulating PD-L1 expression in HCC. Importantly, knockdown of ITGB5 increased the sensitivity of HCC cells to PD-1 therapy. Therefore, ITGB5 could be used as a potential biomarker for anti-PD-L1/PD-1 therapeutic efficacy, guiding clinical decision-making. Inhibiting ITGB5 expression might improve the efficiency of immune checkpoint blockade and advance cancer immunotherapy.

Epigenetic modifications lead to aberrant gene expression in cancer. SETDB1 is a histone lysine methyltransferase involved in DNA methylation and gene silencing [48]. By inhibiting oncogenes, aberrant histone methylation of H3K9 by SETDB1 leads to carcinogenesis [49, 50]. Previous studies have established that SETDB1 is highly expressed in various types of cancer and affects prognosis [51, 52], and in the present study we further report that SETDB1 is associated with drug resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma.

LATS2 is a central component of the Hippo pathway that inhibits tumor progression by phosphorylating the transcriptional cofactor YAP1, preventing its entry into the nucleus [53]. There is a close relationship between the Hippo pathway and tumor immunity. Studies have shown that the Hippo pathway regulates T-cell function [54, 55], macrophage polarization [56, 57], B-cell differentiation [58, 59], MDSC activity [60], and the dendritic cell-mediated immune response [58, 61], as well as PD-L1 expression in cancer cells [9]. The upstream kinases of the Hippo pathway, MST1/2 and LATS1/2, inhibit PD-L1 expression, whereas TAZ and YAP increase PD-L1 levels in breast and lung cancer cell lines [13]. YAP has been identified as a regulator of PD-L1 expression in human non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) [62]. In the current study, we demonstrated that ITGB5 inhibits LATS2 in HCC, thereby promoting YAP nuclear translocation and YAP-mediated PD-L1 transcription.

In this study, we report an uncharacterised role of ITGB5 in reducing tumour immunity by promoting Hippo pathway-mediated PD-L1 expression. Mechanistically, ITGB5 directly binds to SETDB1 to inhibit its ubiquitination degradation, SETDB1 transcriptionally represses LATS2 expression, and inhibition of the Hippo pathway further increases the expression of its target gene PD-L1. These findings provide a strong rationale that targeting ITGB5 restores the sensitivity of immunotherapy-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma. However, due to the complexity of the tumour microenvironment, many more factors are involved in the occurrence of immune escape, and there is still a long way to go to completely solve the challenge of immunotherapy resistance in tumours, which needs to be overcome by further research and clinical exploration.
