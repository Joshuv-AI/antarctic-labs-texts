Mandible and Temporomandibular Joint Reconstruction with Flow-through Fibula and Lateral Femoral Condyle Flaps: A Case Series
Authors: Janis Zarins, Kristians Vistarts, Atis Svare, Oskars Radzins, Kristaps Blums, Renars Deksnis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Simultaneous anatomical reconstruction of mandible and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) defects following tumor resection poses a significant challenge in achieving good healing and long-lasting functional outcomes. The authors propose a novel autologous technique using a flow-through fibula and lateral femoral condyle osteochondral flap for simultaneous mandible and TMJ reconstruction. Both flaps were harvested from the same donor leg and joined via on-table osteosynthesis and a flow-through microvascular anastomosis. Preoperative virtual planning and patient-specific cutting guides were used to optimize accuracy. This technique was applied to 4 patients with mandible tumors who underwent resection of the mandible body, ramus, condyle, and adjacent soft tissue. All patients recovered well, with functional mouth opening exceeding 3 cm and no signs of ankylosis during follow-ups. This alternative “like-for-like” autologous reconstruction method is a novel technique for large mandible and TMJ defect reconstructions, offering a durable and effective solution that enhances patient outcomes.

Takeaways

Question: How can large mandible and temporomandibular joint defects be effectively reconstructed using autologous tissue following tumor resection?

Findings: This study presents a novel autologous technique using a flow-through fibula osteocutaneous flap and a lateral femoral condyle osteochondral flap. In 4 patients, this approach resulted in functional mouth opening greater than 3 cm, no ankylosis, satisfactory aesthetics, and stable anatomical alignment up to 43 months after follow-up.

Meaning: This novel technique using a lateral femoral condyle and fibula flow-through flap allows precise mandibular and temporomandibular joint reconstruction with a vascularized cartilaginous surface.

INTRODUCTION

Reconstruction of large mandibular and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) defects after tumor resection remains challenging, as these defects compromise mastication, speech, aesthetics, and quality of life. Conventional options include vascularized and nonvascularized bone grafts, costochondral grafts, and alloplastic TMJ prostheses, with the free fibula flap considered the gold standard for mandibular reconstruction. However, each method carries limitations, including risks of ankylosis, resorption, malocclusion, trismus, hardware failure, and long-term TMJ instability, often exacerbated in oncological patients requiring adjuvant radiotherapy.1

This approach for reconstructing mandibular and TMJ defects combines a flow-through fibula osteocutaneous flap and a lateral femoral condyle (LFC) osteochondral flap, providing a “like-for-like” autologous reconstruction. By simultaneously restoring both anatomical units—the mandible and the TMJ with vascularized bone and cartilage—this technique ensures precise alignment, durability, and good functionality, while addressing extensive defects. Importantly, the use of vascularized tissues increases resilience in oncological patients requiring adjuvant radiotherapy.

Advanced 3D virtual planning was implemented using Materialise Mimics for segmentation and anatomical modeling, and Materialise 3-matic for surgical simulation and design. Patient-specific cutting guides for the mandible and fibula optimized accuracy while allowing intraoperative adjustments. The condyle was sculpted manually using a 3-dimensional–printed TMJ model as a reference to avoid damaging the periosteal blood supply.

In all cases, an Esmarch hemostatic manual tourniquet was applied to the middle third of the upper thigh to facilitate the LFC and fibula flap harvest. Flap harvest began with the fibula osteocutaneous flap, with cutting guides fixed on the fibula and osteotomies performed while dissecting the peroneal artery and veins. The LFC osteochondral flap was then harvested in a retrograde manner from the anterior pole of the LFC, a zone supplied by the superolateral geniculate artery. An oscillating saw was used to shape the LFC flap to replicate the contour of the native mandibular condyle. After tourniquet release, both flaps were left for reperfusion. Osteosynthesis of fibula fragments was done before flap separation. Immediately after harvesting both flaps, the same flushing steps were followed: following ligation of their respective vascular pedicles, the arterial lumen was gently flushed with unfractionated heparin diluted in saline (concentration ~50 IU/mL), allowing efflux through the venae comitantes. Venous pedicles were then flushed separately. All flushing was performed manually using a 10-mL syringe.

The next step was performed on a table, and a schematic drawing is represented in Figure 1. Final sculpting of the LFC flap was done, and osteosynthesis was performed in all cases using two 2.0-mm titanium mini-screws from the KLS Martin osteosynthesis system to secure it to the fibula flap, as shown for patient 3 in Figure 2. Subsequently, a flow-through end-to-end anastomosis was done between the peroneal artery to the superolateral geniculate artery and 1 vena comitans. The new mandible was positioned in the glenoid fossa and secured to the remaining mandible using osteosynthesis. After flap inset, the peroneal artery was sutured end-to-end to the superior thyroid artery and 2 venae comitantes to 2 branches of the internal jugular vein. All microvascular anastomoses were performed using 8-0 Dafilon sutures with a Zeiss S88 microscope. The oral lining defect was reconstructed using the fibula skin paddle.

All patients achieved satisfactory outcomes, with no signs of ankylosis during follow-up and functional mouth opening exceeding 30 mm. The mouth opening of patient 3 is shown in Figure 3. Tracheostomy and nasogastric tube management followed a standardized protocol. Oral rinses and speech therapy began on days 2–3, and water swallowing on days 5–6 if tolerated. Tracheostomy cuff deflation was usually initiated on day 7, and both tubes were removed within 10 days postoperatively after interdisciplinary confirmation of adequate airway control and safe swallowing. Visual analog scale scores indicated favorable functional and aesthetic outcomes with minimal donor-site morbidity. DISCUSSION

The authors present an innovative reconstructive technique for large mandible and TMJ defects in patients with oral cavity tumors. This method uses a flow-through fibula osteocutaneous flap combined with an LFC osteochondral flap, providing precise like-for-like reconstruction with good functional, aesthetic, and oncological outcomes.

Various methods exist for mandible and TMJ reconstruction, including no reconstruction, alloplastic implants, and nonvascularized or vascularized bone flaps.1 Despite possible advantages, alloplastic materials or TMJ patient-specific implants do not provide soft tissue reconstruction, requiring a microvascular flap.2 Nonvascularized bone offers biological compatibility and low donor-site morbidity, but several problems, such as resorption, fractures, ankylosis, and inadequate bone stock, can occur.3 Vascularized bone flaps are the first choice for difficult mandible reconstructions involving large bone defects, an inadequate soft tissue envelope, or cases where the recipient bed has been compromised by radiation, chronic infection, or previous surgery.4 The free fibula flap is the most commonly used for ramus–condyle area reconstruction.5,6 Ankylosis, dislocation, occlusion, and trismus are reported as the most common complications when using a fibula flap, although the occurrence rate is less compared with costochondral grafts, joint prostheses, or plate-with-condyle type reconstructions.7,8 In this study, no signs of ankylosis were observed during follow-ups (8, 26, 30, and 43 mo after surgery), and computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a good cartilaginous surface of the LFC flap with no signs of resorption. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the radiological outcome of patient 3 at 26 mo after reconstruction, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E693.)

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first report of fully autologous reconstruction of large mandible and TMJ defects after tumor surgery using microvascular fibula and LFC flow-through flaps. Recent work by Shuck et al9 demonstrated a similar reconstructive principle using the medial femoral trochlea flap with a fibula flap, providing another example of a vascularized flow-through flap for joint reconstruction. The rationale behind selecting the LFC is the biomechanical loading differences between the medial and lateral knee compartments. As reported by Devaraj et al,10 the medial tibiofemoral compartment experiences higher compressive stress and contact pressure than the lateral side during full extension and load-bearing. These findings imply that harvesting a flap from the LFC rather than from the medial side may reduce donor-site morbidity, especially in preserving long-term joint function.

This technique yielded satisfactory results for more than 8–43 months of follow-up, maintaining good bone and joint alignment. The vascularized osteochondral component of the neomandible condyle could resist resorption problems in late follow-up. Patients achieved acceptable facial symmetry, aesthetics, and functional recovery of mastication, occlusion, and speech. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays good occlusion in patient 3 at 26 mo after reconstruction, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E694.)

Like-for-like autologous reconstruction with flow-through fibula and LFC flaps is a promising alternative for large mandible and TMJ reconstructions. Further studies with a control group for other reconstructive methods should be conducted, and longer follow-ups are required to assess long-term outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

This novel technique, using an LFC and fibula flow-through flap, allows precise mandibular and TMJ reconstruction with a vascularized cartilaginous surface. It shows promising functional and aesthetic results with potentially reduced donor-site morbidity, warranting further validation through long-term follow-up and comparative studies.
