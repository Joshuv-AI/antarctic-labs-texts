Evidence of central autonomic dysfunction in orthostatic hypotension of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Laura de Lima Xavier, Eran Dayan, Nina Browner, Miriam Sklerov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Autonomic dysfunction, particularly orthostatic hypotension (OH), is common and predicts poor outcomes in Parkinson’s disease (PD). Brain contributions to autonomic failure in PD are suspected but understudied.Methods:31 PD participants underwent autonomic reflex testing and functional brain MRI. Region of interest (ROI) analyses were used to characterize intranetwork connectivity of salience network (implicated in sympathetic nervous system function) nodes. Seed-based connectivity of key Central Autonomic Network (CAN) (responsible for top-down autonomic control) ROIs was performed to confirm the specificity of these findings. Between-group differences in functional connectivity as well as associations of connectivity with clinical variables were investigated using ANOVA. All analyses were controlled for age and disease duration.Results:12/31 PD participants had OH. Functional connectivity between three clusters including the anterior cingulate cortex, bilateral anterior insula, rostral prefrontal cortex, and supramarginal gyrus were weaker in OH compared with no-OH. Blood pressure reduction measures and severity of self-completed assessment of autonomic dysfunction scores were associated with lower internode connectivity. No differences in intranetwork connectivity between PD with OH and those without were found in non-autonomic networks.Conclusion:Disrupted central sympathetic autonomic function through the salience network and the CAN is implicated in OH in PD. These findings identify potential novel targets for neuromodulation therapies in PD.

Introduction

Orthostatic hypotension (OH) is common in Parkinson’s Disease (PD) with an estimated prevalence of 30–50 % [1]. Only one third of individuals with PD and OH are symptomatic, but the presence of OH predicts poor outcomes in all affected individuals [2]. While peripheral contributions to autonomic dysfunction in PD have been highly studied [3], central nervous system involvement remains unclear.

The influence of the central nervous system on autonomic regulation is well established. Investigations dating back to 1948 showed that stimulation of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) resulted in modulation of blood pressure and heart rate [4]. Later, data from lesional and functional MRI (fMRI) studies contributed to knowledge of the Central Autonomic Network (CAN) (Supplementary Figure 1). Animal and fMRI studies of autonomic responses have revealed a widespread network involving brainstem, subcortical, and cortical structures [5–7]. This network modulates baseline autonomic function using environmental, emotional, and behavioral inputs, and coordinates responses to changing stimuli.

The anatomic distribution of the CAN and its role in adaptive regulation, especially sympathetic outflow responses, has been recognized to overlap with a cortical system which emerges during resting state fMRI called the salience network [5]. Anchored in the anterior insula and ACC, this network orchestrates behavioral and physiological responses to external and internal sensory inputs through interoception (Supplementary Figure 1) [8,9]. It has been stipulated that this topographic overlap between the two networks might represent dynamic sympathetic physiologic and behavioral response mechanisms to environmental changes.

Several structures in the CAN and salience network, particularly the ACC and insula, are affected in PD, with histopathological evidence of targeted Lewy body accumulation within these regions [10]. In the few brain imaging studies investigating autonomic function in PD, functional and white matter abnormalities have become apparent [11–14]. In light of these data, this study aims to examine the contribution of dysfunction specifically in the CAN and salience network on sympathetic dysfunction, especially OH, in PD. We hypothesize that connectivity within the salience network and across CAN nodes is affected in individuals with PD with OH compared to those without, and that changes in connectivity correlate with the degree of sympathetic autonomic dysfunction.

Methods

Participants

Recruitment goals were based on historical precedents for similar work, available funds, and practical considerations during the COVID-19 pandemic. The minimal sample size for our study was based on a power calculation derived from a previous publication that examined fMRI differences in connectivity in PD with low and high SCOPA-AUT scores. [13] This prior study reported a Cohen’s effect size of d = 1.3. To achieve a power of 0.8 and a significance level of 0.05 for a two-sample t-test, an estimated sample size of N = 10.3 per group was estimated to be required. Inclusion criteria were: Age 45–90 years; diagnosis of idiopathic Parkinson’s disease based on the UK Parkinson’s Disease Society Brain Bank clinical diagnostic criteria; Parkinson’s disease symptoms for at least 3 years; at least Hoehn-Yahr stage II; capacity to provide informed consent in English.

Exclusion criteria were: diagnosis of severe dementia; contraindications for MRI such as claustrophobia, or implanted ferromagnetic metal; severe tremor that would interfere with image quality; clinically significant conditions which may be an alternative cause of OH, such as neuropathy, renal failure, heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias, severe diabetes, or spinal cord injuries; medications that lower blood pressure or heart rate such as antihypertensive medications, diuretics, and alpha and beta-adrenergic blocking medications; known factors that may interfere with performance of autonomic testing procedures such as significant lung disease, oro-buccal weakness, and limitations of thoracic volume; or known history that may affect neuroimaging results.

At the UNC Movement Disorders clinic, patients undergo intake orthostatic vital signs testing. Due to time constraints, blood pressure and heart rate are measured after at least 1 min of seated position, and again after at least 1 min of standing. To promote inclusion of a broad range of autonomic symptoms, we recruited the first 15 eligible participants with a blood pressure reduction of at least 20/10 mmHg after 1 min of standing from seated position (ie at least transient OH), and the remainder with <20/10 mmHg blood pressure reduction, based on intake vitals. While a blood pressure reduction after 1 min of standing, without confirmation of sustained hypotension, only indicates the presence of transient OH, this method was expected to enrich recruitment for the presence of autonomic dysfunction.

Determination of OH for groupings of PD with OH and PD no-OH and data analysis were based on orthostatic vital signs recorded during tilt testing procedure as well as supportive evidence of neurogenic origin of the OH (lack of blood pressure normalization in late Phase II and lack of overshoot in Phase IV, and lack of adequate heart rate response to OH). (Fig. 1). OH was defined as a sustained blood pressure reduction of at least 20/10 mmHg in the first 3 min of head up tilt, plus both additional features supporting neurogenic orthostatic hypotension origin (lack of blood pressure normalization in late Phase II and lack of overshoot in Phase IV, AND lack of adequate heart rate response to OH [15,16], OR a sustained blood pressure reduction of at least 30/15 within 3 min with at least one additional feature supporting neurogenic orthostatic hypotension origin (lack of blood pressure normalization in late Phase II and lack of overshoot in Phase IV, OR lack of adequate heart rate response to OH). These more stringent definitions were used in hopes of improving the positive predictive value of OH diagnosis with the tilt test in the autonomic lab.

PD participants were prospectively enrolled from January 4, 2019–9/14/2021. 91 patients were pre-screened based on medications and co-morbidities, and 34 were found to be eligible and enrolled in the study. Of these, one was found illegible after enrollment due to inability to confirm absence of ferromagnetic material in the body, and two due to changes related to the COVID-19 pandemic. 31 participants completed the study. Procedures were approved by the local University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Institutional Review Board, and participants provided written informed consent.

Clinical evaluations

Participants completed the Scales for Outcomes in Parkinson’s disease – autonomic (SCOPA-AUT) [17], and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [18]. The SCOPA-AUT total scores are presented excluding sexual function subscores as 15 participants either indicated “Not applicable” or answered for both male and female questions. The Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson’s disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) [19] part III motor examination, and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment [20] were administered by trained, and certified raters. Levodopa Equivalent Daily Dose (LEDD) was calculated for each participant [21].

Autonomic testing

Using WR Medical Electronics autonomic testing equipment included: 1. Heart rate response to deep breathing, where beat to beat heart rate was recorded while participants were asked to take eight consecutive breaths over 6 s; 2. Valsalva maneuver, where beat to beat heart rate, and blood pressure measurements were taken while the participant was asked to blow into a mouthpiece attached to a manometer at a pressure of 40 mmHg for 15 s; 3. Tilt table test, where beat to beat heart rate, and blood pressure were measured while participants were in the supine position for at least 5 min, followed by 10 min of recording while participants were tilted upright to 70°. WR Testworks software was used for analysis and interpretation by one scorer (MS).

Magnetic resonance imaging acquisition

Participants underwent brain MRI on a 3 T S Prisma scanner. The protocol was based on the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI, http://www.ppmi-info.org/). This protocol is optimized for the PD population, and allows for future comparison with the PPMI data bank [22]. Structural 3D images were acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo sequence (echo time = 2.98 ms, repetition time = 2300 ms, flip angle = 9°, field of view = 256 × 256 mm, and voxel size = 1mm3). Resting-state fMRI scans were acquired using an echo-planar imaging sequence (echo time = 25 ms, repetition time = 2400 ms, flip angle = 80°, field of view = 222 × 222 mm, voxel size = 3.25 mm3, 40 axial slices in ascending order, slice thickness = 3.25 mm, and total number of scans = 210). During the resting-state fMRI scans, participants were instructed to rest quietly with their eyes open, and attempt not to fall asleep.

Imaging preprocessing

Standardized preprocessing pipelines of functional and anatomical volumes were applied using CONN toolbox, version 21.a (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/conn). In brief, preprocessing included functional data realignment to the first scan of the first session using B-spline interpolation; unwarping and susceptibility distortion correction; outlier detection and motion correction where acquisitions with displacements above 0.9 mm or global blood-oxygen level–dependent signal changes above five standard deviations were flagged, and framewise displacement at each time point was estimated to be scrubbed in the future for nuisance variable regression; functional and anatomical data normalization into standard MNI space and segmentation into grey matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid; and functional data smoothing using spatial convolution with a Gaussian kernel of 8-mm full-width half maximum. Additional denoising steps included band-pass filtering at 0.008–0.09 Hz and removal of signal associated with the motion parameters, white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and outlying functional volumes using ordinary least squares regression.

ROI–ROI (intranetwork connectivity) analysis

Region of interest (ROI) analysis was used to characterize cross-network (intranetwork) connectivity. Network analyses were based on predefined anatomical nodes available within the CONN toolbox defined from CONN’s ICA analyses of Human Connectome Project dataset (497 healthy subjects). The networks investigated and associated ROIs included the salience network represented by the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), bilateral anterior insula (AI), rostral prefrontal cortex (RPFC), and supramarginal gyrus (SMG) (Supplementary Figure 1) given evidence of associations with sympathetic nervous system function. To investigate specificity of brain network involvement in OH in PD, we also investigated the default mode network (DMN); the dorsal attention network (DAN); the fronto-parietal network (FPN); the sensorimotor network (SMN); and the visual network (VN). ROI coordinates are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Seed based connectivity

To complement the ROI-to-ROI network analysis and investigate whether connectivity of other CAN regions is affected, we conducted an exploratory seed-based connectivity analysis focusing on highly implicated CAN brain regions. Select CAN nodes, namely the bilateral hypothalamus, and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), were chosen based on prior literature demonstrating their relevance in the CAN and in PD [13]. The ACC ROI was obtained from the Harvard-Oxford cortical atlas within the CONN toolbox. For the hypothalamus a 4m spheric ROI was created using MNI coordinates extracted from previous literature [23], focusing on the medial hypothalamus due to its integrative role in autonomic responses (Supplementary Table 1). Functional connectivity between each seed and the rest of the brain was tested with the seed-to-voxel approach. Seed-to-voxel maps were computed for each subject separately.

Statistical analysis

Clinical descriptive statistics are presented with % for dichotomous variables, or mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. p values were calculated using Wilcoxon rank sum test (dichotomous variables) and Mann-Whitney test (continuous variables). Correlations between clinical variables were determined using Spearman correlation coefficients. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc. 2023. SAS/STAT® 15·3 User’s Guide. Cary, NC: SAS Institute Inc.).

Differences in strength of ROI-to-ROI connections between groups were investigated using a 2-way ANOVA and default hierarchical clustering. Cluster level inference used the functional network connectivity cluster threshold p < 0·05, cluster level p false discovery rate corrected (FDR), and connection threshold (p < 0·05, p uncorrected) to perform an F test for group differences. This method is implemented in CONN toolbox and full description can be accessed in their manual [24]. Results were corrected for age and disease duration, which was decided a priori given known associations between age and OH, and due to concerns that disease duration or overall disease severity may contribute to structural and functional brain changes in PD. Similarly, differences in strength of seed-to-voxel connections between groups as well as the effect of total SCOPA-AUT score (SCOPATOT), the cardiovascular subscore of the SCOPA-AUT (SCOPACV), and maximal systolic blood pressure fall during 10 min head up tilt (SBP fall) on connectivity were investigated using 2-way ANOVAs correcting for age and disease duration. Significant differences were set to a 2-sided voxel threshold of p < 0·001, and cluster-size false-discovery rate–corrected p ≤ ·05. The effects of clinical variables on connectivity were only performed for networks that showed differences between the groups to avoid the risk of multiple statistical testing and potential Type I errors.

Following ROI-to-ROI analysis, a subanalysis was performed to investigate correlations between abnormal internode connectivity and clinical scores. Fisher Z-scores were extracted for each subject as a measure of the strength of connectivity between nodes that showed differences in connectivity between PD participants with OH and no OH. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for z-scores and SBP fall, SCOPA total, SCOPACV and SBP fall controlling for subject age, and disease duration. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ ·05. Analysis performed in RStudio (version 2023.12.1.402).

Results

Clinical variables

31 people with PD participated in the study. Age range was 52–82 years, and 17 participants were women. 12/31 were found to have OH on tilt testing.

Clinical features of the participants are presented in Table 1. Participants in the OH group were older than those in the no-OH group (mean 71·67, SD 5·94 years in OH group; mean 63·79, SD 7·71 years in no-OH group; p = 0·005), had a more severe blood pressure reduction with tilt (mean 56·00, SD 23.60 mmHg in OH group; mean 10·58, SD 11·61 mmHg in no-OH group; p < 0·001), and had lower Valsalva ratio (mean 1·26, SD 0·17 in OH; mean 1·44, SD 0·18 in no-OH; p < 0·01). There were no differences in sex, primarily affected side of PD, disease duration, MoCA score, HADS score, SCOPA-AUT total or cardiovascular subscore, MDS-UPDRS III motor scores, LEDD, or heart rate variability with deep breathing, another measure of cardiovagal autonomic function. Of note, two participants with OH on tilt testing were taking vasopressor medications to increase blood pressure.

There was a significant correlation between MoCA and HADS-D (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, depression subscore) score (Spearman rho = −0·36, p < 0·05), between total SCOPA-AUT score and age (Spearman rho = 0·63, p < 0·001), and between orthostatic systolic blood pressure reduction and age (Spearman rho = −0·49, p < 0·01). There was a significant correlation between LEDD and disease duration (Spearman rho = 0·54, p < 0·01), MoCA (Spearman rho = −0·51, p = 0·01), HADS-D (Spearman rho = 0·55, p < 0·01), and SCOPA-AUT (Spearman rho = 0·42, p = 0·02). There were no correlations between other clinical variables (Supplementary Table 2).

ROI–ROI (internetwork connectivity) analysis

Functional connectivity between salience network ROIs was weaker in PD with OH compared to PD no-OH. Specifically, connectivity analysis with hierarchical clustering found three clusters which were weaker in OH compared with no-OH (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 3). Cluster one involved bilateral anterior insular to bilateral prefrontal cortices and anterior cingulate gyri (F(2,26) = 6·93, p = 0·02). Cluster two was rooted in the right supplementary motor area to bilateral rostral prefrontal cortices, and anterior cingulate cortices (F(2,26) = 5·72, p = 0·02). Cluster three comprised connectivity between the anterior cingulate and left rostral prefrontal cortex (F(1,27) = 7·29, p = 0·02). No differences in internetwork connectivity between PD with OH compared with PD no-OH were found in non-autonomic networks (DMN, DAN, FPN, SMN, and VN).

Connectivity values between salience network nodes that were significantly lower in PD with OH showed negative linear correlation with autonomic testing measures. Greater degrees of systolic reduction correlated with lower internode connectivity values between left rostral prefrontal cortex and right anterior insula (R2 = −0·51, p = 0·003) and right rostral prefrontal cortex with right supplementary motor area (R2 = −0·39, p = 0·02) (Fig. 3). Connectivity between the right rostral pre-frontal cortex and right supplementary motor area also showed an inverse correlation with SCOPACV scores (R2 = −0·38, p = 0·03). No correlation between SCOPATOT and internetwork connectivity measures was identified.

Seed based connectivity

No differences in seed-based connectivity from the ACC or hypothalamus were found between individuals with PD with OH and those without OH. However, functional connectivity to these regions was associated with measures of autonomic symptom burden (Supplementary Figure 2, Supplementary Table 4). SCOPATOT was associated with increased connectivity between the left medial hypothalamus and the left precuneus (t [29] = 5·87, p = 0·004) and right cerebellum VIII (t [29] = 5·07, p = 0·007), as well as between the right medial hypothalamus and ipsilateral middle temporal gyrus (t(29 = 5·56, p = 0·009). Meanwhile, SCOPACV was associated with decreased connectivity between the ACC and bilateral posterior cingulate gyrus (t [29] = −5·31, p = 0·000001), left precuneus (t [29] = −4·92, p = 0·007), and increased connectivity with the left precentral gyrus (t [29] = 5·91, p = 0·02). There was no effect of SBP fall on seed-to-voxel connectivity measures.

Discussion

This study identified altered functional connectivity exclusively within salience network nodes associated with sympathetic autonomic function in individuals with PD with OH. Furthermore, the severity of orthostatic systolic blood pressure reduction correlated with functional connectivity between structures implicated in sympathetic autonomic control. These findings support localization of PD-related autonomic dysfunction in the central nervous system, in addition to peripheral etiologies.

When compared to PD without OH, individuals with PD and OH showed disrupted connectivity within the ACC, rostral prefrontal cortex, anterior insula and supramarginal gyrus nodes of the salience network. The rostral prefrontal cortex falls within the larger area of the medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC), and within Brodmann area 10. Together with the MPFC, the anterior insula is implicated in regulation of autonomic, neuroendocrine, and behavioral processes in response to psychological, and physiologic stimuli [25–27]. In PD, structural changes to the insula have been associated with autonomic dysfunction. [12].

We additionally found that subjective measures of autonomic symptoms were associated with disrupted connectivity in key CAN regions, namely the ACC and hypothalamus. Sympathetic outflow is driven by the ACC as part of central integration of the bodily state with volitional behavioral engagement. The cardiac sympathetic index has been shown to correlate with functional activity in the ACC as well as the precuneus, bilateral insula cortex, and hypothalamus, which is in line with these findings [28].

Impaired sympathetic myocardial innervation is proposed as a primary driver of OH in PD [3]. However, many individuals with PD without OH also have significant sympathetic denervation, and severity of denervation does not correlate with clinical signs, suggesting additional mechanisms. In the current study, the degree of orthostatic SBP reduction correlated with decreased connectivity within nodes of the salience network which overlap with CAN structures, suggesting a central driver of OH in PD. This finding aligns with a previous description of anatomical overlap between sympathetic components of CAN with the salience network, as well as the putative function of the salience network in switching from low activity states to cognitive vigilant states [8]. The specificity of this finding in our cohort is supported by the absence of differences in connectivity within other functional networks, including the default mode network, which shares functional hubs with parasympathetic components of the CAN, and the sensorimotor network which is likely similarly affected in both groups.

Our study demonstrated the specificity of the associations between dysfunction in sympathetic-implicated brain networks, and physiologic evidence of sympathetic failure in PD. A previous investigation of alterations in salience network connectivity between PD with and without significant autonomic dysfunction failed to find any significant differences between groups [12]. They did find that autonomic dysfunction correlated with disrupted white matter in fronto-subcortical and posterior cortical regions, and with reduced functional connectivity to left palladium, right caudate, left insula, and right cerebellum. A possible explanation for these diverging results is that this study used the CASS, a composite score that includes cholinergic-sympathetic, parasympathetic and adrenergic/noradrenergic-sympathetic subscores. The composite score may have diluted an association between salience network connectivity and predominantly sympathetic drivers of OH. Autonomic testing in the current study revealed that parasympathetic-mediated function (heart rate responses to deep breathing and Valsalva), was mildly impaired in both groups. Meanwhile, orthostatic SBP reduction was drastically higher in PD with OH compared to PD no-OH. These findings support the presence of differing etiologies for the noradrenergic-sympathetic component of autonomic failure, which drives orthostatic SBP changes, and the vagal-mediated responses in PD.

Similarly to prior studies, the PD with OH group was significantly older, and both orthostatic BP reduction and SCOPA-AUT total scores increased with age [29], but not with disease duration or severity of motor symptoms. The effect of disease duration on OH has been inconsistent across studies with some suggesting a positive correlation [30], and others none [31]. These diverging findings suggest different patterns of progression in PD. The existence of different PD phenotypes has been previously suggested [32]. Our findings of disrupted connectivity within networks that support physiologic and behavioral adaptive responses in PD with OH further support an anatomically broad, albeit network targeted disease progression in PD.

The implications of the current work also extend practically to novel therapeutic approaches such as neuromodulation. Studies of autonomic responses to non-invasive brain stimulation are sparse and demonstrate mixed results. Systematic reviews of this literature have revealed mostly no to small effects of non-invasive brain stimulation on autonomic function, though much of this may be attributable to heterogeneity in approach, stimulation parameters, and population [33,34]. Very few have attempted stimulation in locations highly implicated in autonomic function, such as the insula [35–38]. Furthermore, most of these studies have studied the effects of non-invasive brain stimulation on heart rate and heart rate variability, which are not well established markers for sympathetic or parasympathetic dysfunction. One recent study in health controls, however, demonstrated modulation of muscle sympathetic nerve activity and heart rate, as well as significant increases in blood pressure in response to transcranial alternating current applied to the right or left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [39]. There have been no published studies investigating the effects of non-invasive brain stimulation on autonomic function in Parkinson’s disease to our knowledge. Future studies in the development of neuromodulation methods for autonomic failure in Parkinson’s disease may benefit from targeting cortical regions identified in the current study, such as the insula or medial prefrontal areas.

Limitations to this study include the possible effect of hypotension on fMRI measures, as well as possible supine hypertension during scanning as intrascan BP was not monitored. This was mitigated by assessing connectivity within other networks suggesting true network-specific changes. Additionally, the differences in connectivity we found between several network nodes, which are vascularized by different arterial systems (e.g., Anterior and Middle Cerebral Arteries), further support that our findings are more likely related to intrinsic brain function at rest rather than artifacts from altered vascular flow during scanning. In addition, LEDD doses were similar across groups, decreasing the possibility of results being influenced by medication-related hypotension. Another limitation is the possibility of long-term effects of OH resulting in structural brain changes, such as grey matter atrophy [14,40,41] and white matter small vessel disease [42,43], suggesting that long-term structural effects of OH are possible along with the functional changes reported in our study. Still, as we found significant alterations in only CAN and salience networks and other networks remained relatively spared, we suspect that these changes were less likely to have influenced our main results. Our assessment of brainstem autonomic centers was also limited due to MRI resolution. Future studies with higher resolution or neuropathology might address this gap. PD patients with dementia were excluded from this study, which may have eliminated a subtype of patients with both autonomic and cognitive impairments. While this may restrict generalizability, it likely improved this study’s ability to isolate alterations attributable to sympathetic autonomic failure. Finally, the relatively small n may be masking additional findings. More highly powered studies may further stratify our results by additional clinical and pathophysiologic features.

In conclusion, we show that central sympathetic autonomic control through CAN and salience network function is implicated in the pathophysiology of OH in PD. Understanding patterns of network connectivity alterations as they relate with autonomic dysfunction in PD can assist in predicting disease course, guide therapeutic planning, and identify new targets for precision neuromodulation.
