Frequency of complementary medicine use and attitudes among Iranian patients with chronic diseases
Authors: Akram Ghahramanian, Allehe Seyed-Rassuli, Zhila Rahimlou, Emad Behboudi, Vahid Ghaffarilaleh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The study aimed to determine the frequency of the use of complementary medicine (CM) in patients with chronic diseases and its relationship with their attitude towards CM. This cross-sectional study was conducted in Sina Hospital, a general clinical setting located in Tabriz city, Northwest Iran. A total of 400 adult patients with chronic diseases—including cardiac, rheumatologic, endocrine, and nephrological conditions—were recruited using convenience sampling from both specialized outpatient clinics and a traditional medicine unit. Data collection involved structured interviews conducted in clinical settings. Descriptive statistics and bivariate tests were performed, followed by binary logistic regression analysis to identify predictors of CM modality use. According to the result, the most commonly used complementary and alternative Medicine within the past 12 months were Herbal Medicine, Prayer Therapy, Water therapy, Cupping and Massage Therapy with 83, 36, 27, 11, and 8%, respectively. The mean scores of the patients’ attitude towards holistic health were higher than their attitude towards the validity of complementary medicine (21.37 vs. 15.88). Logistic regression analysis revealed that belief in holistic health significantly predicted lower odds of using herbal medicine (OR = 0.85, p = .005), prayer therapy (OR = 0.85, p = .018), and water therapy (OR = 0.82, p = .009). Belief in CM’s scientific validity was positively associated with prayer therapy use (OR = 1.24, p < .001). Additional predictors varied by modality: increased age and high economic status predicted greater use of prayer and water therapies, whereas gender and education levels showed mixed associations. The use of herbal medicine was common among patients with chronic diseases. Therefore, health care providers should pay attention to the use of herbal medicine by their patients considering their interactions with modern medications, efficacy, and side effects. Given the poor attitude towards the validity of CM, there is a need for community-level education on the use of CM for prescribing it by professional practitioners.

Introduction

Complementary Medicine (CM) refers to therapies and medicines that are used to help the diagnosis, treatment, and prevent diseases or disorders. The use of CM in the world is increasing1. Nowadays, besides progress in the field of medical sciences, still modern medical treatments are not easily accessible beside the side effects and their effectiveness. Therefore, patients seek complementary therapies in which more than 80% of patients in developing countries use herbal medicine2. A study on American society has shown that 29 to 42% of the population use CM either self-medication or prescription from a registered practitioner3. According to the other studies, a high number of population (70–90%) in Italy, Germany, Canada, France, Nigeria 84%, Japan and Singapore 76%, South Korea 74.8%, China 60%, and Iran 62.5% use CM including herbal medicine4.

Chronic diseases are a global and individual health issue. Chronic diseases account for seven out of 10 deaths in the U.S., leading to the death of more than 1.7 million Americans each year. Moreover, more than 75% of the $2 trillion spent on public and private healthcare in 2005 went toward chronic diseases5. While chronic diseases can be controlled but not fully treated and some of them can be life-threatening or remain over time which is difficult to manage6. Studies have shown that CM in patients with chronic diseases is also used for the reasons such as patient’s sense of responsibility in treating illness or self-care and the importance of health in life with less pain and comfortability6,7. A study in Iran has shown that participants were less familiar with CM but were expressed a great interest in becoming familiar with it, indicating that the medical society should have sufficient information about the benefits, disadvantages and side effects of using CM so they can provide or suggest a necessary treatment8.

In a recent review by Ghafari et al.9, on the herbal medicine proposed by Iranian traditional medicine, it was found that traditional medicine is well used by Iranian people before the modern health organization systems to be set up. They argued that traditional medicine is a part of the culture of the people and plays a significant role in solving health issues. On the other hand, it has been reported that the development and promotion of traditional medicine is consistent with the culture and heritage of people around the world10. To meet future expectations in the healthcare system of practitioners, it is necessary that enough knowledge to be available on CM for health care providers.

In addition to official health directives and policies issued by Iranian governmental health authorities during the recent years, aimed at promoting and regulating the safe use of complementary medicine, most patients who use CM (63.9%) state that they are seeking new therapies such as complementary medicine that could improve their health11. Furthermore, some patients complain about the side effects of modern medicines and try to use CM. According to the literature review, the friends and relatives, the healthcare staff, family members, and especially the elders beside the religious and cultural beliefs are the main sources of information on CM12.

Despite the increasing use of complementary medicine (CM) worldwide, there remains a significant gap in understanding its utilization patterns and associated attitudes among Iranian patients with chronic diseases. Previous studies have largely focused on general populations or specific therapies, but detailed data on different chronic disease groups within Iran are scarce. This lack of knowledge limits healthcare providers’ ability to effectively support patients’ use of CM in the context of chronic illness management. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by investigating the frequency of CM use and its relationship with patients’ attitudes towards CM across diverse chronic diseases. The findings are expected to inform culturally appropriate healthcare policies and educational interventions for promoting safe and informed use of CM among patients.

Materials and methods

Research participants

In this cross-sectional study, a total of 400 patients with chronic diseases —defined operationally as any physician-diagnosed condition documented in the medical record and persisting for more than six months—through convenience sampling were recruited. These disease groups were selected based on their high prevalence and burden in the patient population accessing Sina Hospital’s specialized clinics, as well as their relevance to complementary medicine use in previous Iranian and international studies. We excluded other chronic conditions not commonly managed in these clinics (e.g., respiratory diseases, cancers, neurological disorders) to maintain homogeneity in the study sample and focus on the most representative chronic disease categories for the study setting. Patients who were unwilling to participate, were younger than 18 years of age, or had a history of drug use (self-reported) were excluded.

Participants were considered CAM users if they reported using any CAM modality (e.g., Herbal Medicine, Prayer Therapy, Water Therapy, Cupping, or Massage Therapy) at least once within the past 12 months, regardless of frequency or current use status. This operational definition captures any use within a one-year period but does not distinguish habitual or ongoing use.The sample size, using Cochran formula, with considering of a 50% probability of using CM, z = 1.96, and d = 0.05, with an attrition rate of 10% was estimated, 400 patients.

Data collection

In this study, two questionnaires and a demographic information form (including age, gender, marital status, education, occupation, income, and source of information on CM) were used. Data were collected from a general hospital in Tabriz, Iran.

Complementary Medicine, as operationalized in this study, refers to therapies used in addition to conventional medical treatments for diagnosis, treatment, or prevention of diseases. It differs conceptually from CAM, which in some contexts includes therapies used as alternatives to conventional medicine. Given this distinction, the study focuses specifically on CM modalities relevant to the Iranian chronic disease population1. For measuring the frequency of CM use, a questionnaire developed by Tehrani et al.13 was used, which includes 13 questions about varioust complementary therapies. After informing the purpose of the study and obtaining informed consent form eligible patients, each patient was asked to respond to the questions of the questionnaire. The response for each question (whether she/he know this method of CM or not) included “I know this method”, “I have used this method so far”, “I used this method in the last month “, and “I used this method in the past year “. Moreover, there are two questions for evaluating the effectiveness of using CM and the side effects they experienced.

The attitude towards CM was collected by an 11-item scale, the Holistic Complementary and Alternative Medicine Questionnaire (HCAMQ) developed by Hyland and colleagues14. Six items of the HCAMQ are related to beliefs about the scientific validity of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) and five items assess the beliefs towards holistic health (HH). Responses to each item were made using a 6-point Likert format ranged from strongly agree to strongly disagree. In the subscale of CAM, the scoring was as Strongly agree = 1, agree = 2, mildly agree = 3, mildly disagree = 4, disagree = 5, and strongly disagree = 6. But the reverse scoring was used in the subscale of HH as: Strongly agree = 6, agree = 5, mildly agree = 4, mildly disagree = 3, disagree = 2, and strongly disagree = 1. The total scores of the questionnaire ranges between 11 and 66. In this study, after the translation of the questionnaire from English to Persian, the validity of translation was confirmed linguistically and culturally.

The content and face validity of the questionnaires were then evaluated with the participation of six faculty member of Nursing and Midwifery Faculty and six patients with chronic disease. The reliability of the data collection tools was evaluated through the test-retest method and the reliability for the HCAMQ and its subscales ranged from 0.77 to 0.86 and for the Tehrani questionnaire was 0.91.

After receiving approval from the ethics committee, participants were selected by convenience sampling method. After explaining the aims of the study and obtaining signed informed consent form from subjects, the patients with chronic diseases were enrolled. Questionnaires were completed by the main author in the clinics by asking the questions from subjects and explaining the questions in detail by interviewing the subjects.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS statistic for windows, version 23.0 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA) at the significance level less than 0.05. Descriptive statistics, Chi Square and t independent tests were used for analysis. Chi-square tests were used to describe differences in demographic characteristics, socioeconomic characteristics, health status, and personal health practices and to examine sex differences in CAM use. Independent t-tests were used to compare the mean of the duration of the disease and age differences in CAM use.

To extend these analyses and identify predictors of CM use, three separate binary logistic regression models were constructed—each focused on one of the most frequently reported CM modalities: herbal medicine, prayer therapy, and water therapy. The dependent variable in each model represented self-reported use of the respective therapy in the past 12 months (coded as 1 for use, 0 for non-use). Independent variables included two subscales of HCAMQ for measuring attitude toward complementary medicine including Subscale 1: Beliefs regarding the scientific validity of complementary medicine and Subscale 2: Beliefs about holistic health; age, gender, educational attainment, and economic status. Regression analyses were performed using maximum likelihood estimation. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported to indicate effect size and direction.

Results

Participant characteristics

The demographic data of the participants and the association of their information with three complementary methods (including of praying, water therapy, and herbal medicine) are shown in Table 1. Out of 400 patients interviewed in this study, with a mean age of 41.85 years, most of them (83%) were women, 43% of the subjects had academic education, and 73% of them were married. There was no significant difference between male and female subjects in the use of herbal medicine (P =.755), but men have often used the water therapy (P =.002) where women have often used the prayer therapy (P =.009). Subjects with high education level have used herbal medicine more than illiterate counterparts (P =.033). Moreover, unmarried patients were less likely to use water and praying therapies (P <.001). Subjects with poor economic status were more likely to use herbal medicine, praying and water therapy (P <.001) compared to other income status. The subjects who used water therapy and praying therapy had significantly higher mean age (P <.001).

The results showed that the subjects with smoking history were significantly used water-therapy compared to the non-smokers (P =.006). Use of CM

Table 3 shows that the therapies such as Herbal Medicine, Prayer Therapy, Water therapy, Cupping and Massage Therapy were the most popular methods of CM used by the patients within the past 12 months. The minority of the subjects used practiced yoga (4%), acupuncture (3%) and homeopathic, hypnotherapy, acupressure and traditional medicine (2%). None of the subjects used energy therapy and chiropractic. According to the results, the satisfaction for herbal medicine, cupping, praying, and water therapy was 78%, 8%, 30%, and 24%, respectively. However, 4% and 19% of the herbal and massage therapy users reported side effects.

Attitude towards CM

According to the results of HCAMQ, the mean score of the attitude towards CM (from a possible score of 11 to 66) was 37.25 ± 3.96. In terms of subscales of HCAMQ, the results showed that the mean scores of the CAM subscale (from the maximum possible score of 36) was 15.88 ± 2.93. Logistic regression results

To identify predictors of complementary medicine use, three binary logistic regression models were constructed, each focusing on one of the most frequently reported modalities: herbal medicine, prayer therapy, and water therapy. These three modalities were selected because they had the highest usage frequency among participants (Table 3) and hold greater cultural significance and wider application within the Iranian community, which prioritized them for statistical analysis. Other complementary medicine modalities were reported by fewer participants, limiting the feasibility and reliability of conducting detailed analyses on those modalities within this study. It should be noted that future research could explore these other modalities in more depth to provide a more comprehensive understanding of complementary medicine use. The independent variables comprised two subscales of the HCAMQ—beliefs regarding the scientific validity of CM and beliefs about holistic health—as well as participants’ age, gender, education, and economic status.

For the herbal medicine model, belief in holistic health significantly predicted lower odds of use (OR = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.76–0.95; p =.005). In contrast, belief in the scientific validity of CM was not statistically significant (p =.195), nor were age, gender, education levels, or economic status. However, individuals reporting high economic status demonstrated a marginal association with increased use (OR = 2.25; 95% CI: 0.99–5.12; p =.052).

In the prayer therapy model, both HCAMQ subscales were statistically significant. Greater belief in the scientific validity of CM was positively associated with use (OR = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.12–1.37; p <.001), whereas stronger belief in holistic health was inversely associated (OR = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.75–0.97; p =.018). Age was also a significant positive predictor (OR = 1.08; 95% CI: 1.05–1.10; p <.001). Male participants were substantially less likely to report using prayer therapy compared to females (OR = 0.11; 95% CI: 0.04–0.26; p <.001). Regarding education, participants with middle school (OR = 0.37; p =.019) and high school education (OR = 0.38; p =.018) had significantly lower odds of prayer therapy use compared to those who were illiterate. High economic status was positively associated with prayer therapy use (OR = 2.31; 95% CI: 1.13–4.70; p =.020).

In the water therapy model, holistic health beliefs were significantly associated with reduced odds of use (OR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.71–0.95; p =.009), and age showed a positive association (OR = 1.06; 95% CI: 1.03–1.08; p <.001). Males were more likely to report use of water therapy than females (OR = 3.03; 95% CI: 1.40–6.55; p =.005). High school education was negatively associated with water therapy use (OR = 0.38; 95% CI: 0.16–0.90; p =.028). Belief in the scientific validity of CM, other education levels, and economic status were not statistically significant predictors in this model. Full regression outputs are provided in Table 5.

Discussion

The results of this study, which aimed to determine the frequency of the use of CM in patients with chronic diseases and its relationship with the attitude towards CM, showed that the most popular methods used by participants of the study within the past 12 months were Herbal Medicine, Prayer Therapy, Water therapy, Cupping and Massage Therapy. Patients were mostly used them for prevention purposes. The patients who used herbal medicine were expressed that the remedy was useful and only 4% of the patients were experienced side effects. A study has reported that patients with chronic diseases were used at least one type of CAM, and the use of multivitamin and multimineral dietary supplements was one of the most commonly used complementary therapy. Furthermore, patients were also used herbal remedies, mind and body therapies, chiropractic, massage, diet, acupuncture, nature therapy, or a combination of these treatments15. In another study, 48.3% of patients with inflammatory bowel disease have used one type of CM over the past year, and massage and natural products were the most commonly used methods. However, 83.1% of patients were experienced positive effects of CAM and 14.4% of them were expressed negative effects16.

In a cross-sectional study on the prevalence of traditional medicine, the use of CM by patients with chronic diseases in Cambodia, Vietnam, and Thailand was 27%, 26.3%, and 23.9%, respectively. Furthermore, the use of praying, meditation and relaxation techniques were 30.1%, 13.9%, and 9.9%, respectively7. According to the findings, subjects with higher education and middle age were more users of herbal medicine, prayer, and water therapies than other subjects. Also, those with poor and higher income were more likely to use these therapies compared to middle income status. In other studies, the middle-aged women, rural areas, and high education level were the most common users of CAM17,18. In the Klein and colleagues’ study18, methods such as homeopathy, naturopathy, osteopathy, herbal medicine, and acupuncture were the most common CM. In the other study, similar to our study, women, people of middle age, and those with higher education were more likely to use CM16. In a study by Sarvesh et al., on Indian asthma patients, the results showed that the herbal medicine was the most commonly used CAM treatment and most of the users were in lower middle class19.

The results of the current study showed that women used prayer therapy more than men. Similar to our results, the result of a previous study showed that a higher percentage of women with multiple chronic conditions used CAM as compared to men20. Other studies have also shown that middle-aged women with high education level are more interested in using CM21,22.

Regarding the attitude towards CM, patients showed a positive attitude towards herbal medicine, as they believed healthy when used CM. However, they were not believed that cupping and homeopathy were necessary to be considered for their health. In addition, patients believed that consuming traditional medicines were not always beneficial for their health. Some patients expressed that praying is important in being healthy but was believed less credential to it and this is in contrary to the belief of western studies which it does not popularity in the western population23.

Based on the results, the attitude of patients toward HH subscale was better than their attitude towards the scientific validity of CAM. This result may be attributed to the participants’ experience of side effects and the fear of complications of CM. In this study, 78%, 8%, 30%, and 24% of the participants on herbal, cupping, prayer, and hydrotherapy had experienced positive effects respectively, as well as 4% and 19% of herbal and massage users reported the side effects of these methods, respectively24. Similar to this finding, another study has also reported side effects for CAM in the 18% of CM users. The authors argued that this belief may be a sign of mistrust and concern for CM and the Ministry of Health should control CM-related products25.

Contrary to expectations, our logistic regression analysis revealed that stronger holistic health beliefs were associated with lower odds of using herbal medicine (OR = 0.85, *p* = 0.005), water therapy (OR = 0.82, *p* = 0.009), and prayer therapy (OR = 0.85, *p* = 0.018). This counterintuitive result suggests that chronic disease patients—despite endorsing holistic principles—may prioritize conventional treatments for severe conditions. In contrast, belief in CAM’s scientific validity significantly predicted prayer therapy use (OR = 1.24, *p* < 0.001), highlighting divergent motivational pathways for different CAM modalities. Our findings partially conflict with Klein et al. studies linking holistic attitudes to CAM use18. This discrepancy may stem from disease-specific focus, cultural context, and methodological rigor.

A study by Barikani et al. in Iran showed that 96.4% of participants expressed a positive attitude towards CAM26 and a similar result was found in Switzerland27. A study in Malaysia has reported that 64% of patients with chronic diseases were used CAM. A similar results have been reported on patients with rheumatoid arthritis showing that the patients with rheumatoid arthritis have used CAM at least once time for relieving their symptoms28. The interest in CAM has increased during the past years and the number of the CM centers is increasing. The lower cost of CM alongside of the increasing the patients’ knowledge on CM have improved the patients’ interest on CM19. Another study from Iran in 2015 has also shown that the frequent visits to the physician on chronic diseases have influenced people in using CAM29. The results of previous studies show a lack of knowledge about CAM and its use, which could lead to inappropriate use CM among patients with chronic diseases30. Therefore, Health care policymakers need to pay attention to the wide range of potential benefits of CM interventions and to provide more information to the public so that health care providers can meet the patient’s needs and provide adequate information. Moreover, there is also a need to teach CM in nursing and medical schools. Patients must be educated about CM therapies and they should be advised to discuss all their treatment related issues with clinicians. Finally, healthcare professionals should be familiar with all aspects of using CM therapies so that they could provide proper information to their patients.

One important limitation of this study is the primary focus on three commonly used complementary medicine modalities (herbal medicine, water therapy, and prayer therapy) in the analyses of associations with attitudes toward complementary medicine, which resulted in the exclusion of other complementary therapies from the analysis. This limitation may be attributed to higher usage frequency, cultural significance, or data constraints. Future research should consider a broader range of complementary medicine approaches with larger and more diverse samples across different populations to provide a more comprehensive understanding of usage patterns and attitudes. Such studies can enhance insight into various dimensions of complementary medicine use and inform the development of more effective policies and interventions for managing chronic diseases.

Conclusion

A proportion of patients with chronic disease report utilizing CM concurrently with conventional treatments. It is inferred that the use of CM in Iran is also of interest, especially herbal medicine, prayer, and water therapy are the priorities of patients with chronic diseases. Whereas, often CAM is used to prevent or cure. But due to the poor attitude on the scientific validity of CM, which can be because of low knowledge or fear of its complications, there is a need for proper training on CM. Health professionals should discuss the use of complementary medicine with patients so that these treatments can be used in addition to conventional treatments to help patients. Patients should be encouraged to seek valid information about these therapies from their health care providers.
