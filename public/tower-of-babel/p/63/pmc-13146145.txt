Defining Obesity Under the Lancet Commission Criteria: Metabolic Syndrome–Anchored Body Fat Percentage Thresholds in Korean Adults
Authors: Han Na Jung, Yun Kyung Cho, Myung Jin Kim, Jung Yoon Moon, Eun Hee Kim, Min Jung Lee, Woo Je Lee, Hong‐Kyu Kim, Chang Hee Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThe revised Lancet Commission framework prioritizes excess adiposity over body mass index (BMI) alone for diagnosing obesity, emphasizing population‐specific body fat assessments. However, body fat percentage (BFP) thresholds reflecting adiposity‐defined obesity remain limited, particularly by sex and age in Asian populations. This study aims to compare BMI‐ and adiposity‐defined obesity classifications and derive sex‐ and age‐specific BFP thresholds in Korean adults.Materials and MethodsData from 18 254 adults who underwent routine health examinations were analysed, with BFP assessed using multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis. Adiposity‐defined obesity was classified per Lancet Commission criteria using waist circumference, waist‐to‐height ratio and BMI. BFP thresholds were derived from logistic regression as the values at which the predicted probability of metabolic syndrome matched its observed prevalence among individuals with adiposity‐defined obesity. Confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using bias‐corrected and accelerated bootstrap.ResultsAdiposity‐defined obesity was more prevalent than BMI‐defined obesity, with discordance increasing with age, particularly among women. Overall, 6.6% were classified as obese solely by adiposity‐based criteria, rising to 11.1% in men and 15.8% in women aged ≥ 65 years. Overall BFP thresholds were 27.8% (95% CI, 27.4%–28.2%) for men and 39.5% (38.9%–40.2%) for women, with minimal age variation in men but reaching 41.0% (38.8%–43.9%) among women aged ≥ 65 years.ConclusionsAdiposity‐defined obesity identifies phenotypes missed by BMI, particularly in older adults. BFP thresholds derived from adiposity‐defined obesity vary substantially by sex and age. These findings support adiposity‐centred diagnostic approaches and provide sex‐ and age‐specific BFP thresholds for Korean adults.

Introduction

Obesity is a major global public health challenge, affecting more than 1 billion people worldwide [1]. Since 1990, prevalence has increased by 155% in men and 105% in women, and more than half of the global population is projected to be overweight or obese by 2050 [1]. Body mass index (BMI) has long been the primary criterion for diagnosing obesity in clinical practice and public health surveillance [2, 3]. However, BMI is widely recognized as an imprecise proxy for adiposity [2, 4] and may misclassify individuals because it does not directly reflect excess adipose tissue, the defining feature of obesity [5].

In response to the limitations of BMI‐centred frameworks, the Lancet Commission on Obesity proposed a revised paradigm defining obesity by excess adiposity and its clinical consequences [4]. This framework incorporates anthropometric indicators, including direct assessments of body fat and underscores the need for age‐, sex‐ and ethnicity‐specific thresholds [4]. Body fat percentage (BFP) is highlighted as a central metric within this paradigm, as it directly quantifies adiposity and more accurately captures obesity‐related risk across diverse age, sex and ethnic groups than BMI [5, 6]. BFP has been shown to predict cardiometabolic risk factors and long‐term mortality more accurately than BMI, supporting its physiological relevance as a marker of adiposity‐related disease burden [6, 7]. However, robust evidence for clinically meaningful BFP thresholds remains limited, particularly in Asian populations. Available evidence is limited to a few studies in Chinese cohorts of approximately 2000–3000 participants, suggesting obesity cut‐offs of roughly 24%–26% in men and 34%–37% in women [8, 9]. In the absence of population‐specific data, BFP classification in Korea has therefore been largely informed by international cut‐offs, which may not adequately capture population‐specific risk [10, 11, 12]. Moreover, most studies focus on sex‐specific differences in BFP while largely neglecting age‐related changes in body composition [13, 14], potentially obscuring adiposity‐related risk in older adults [15, 16].

To facilitate clinical implementation of adiposity‐centred obesity definitions, BFP thresholds should be linked to clinically meaningful metabolic risk. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) serves as a practical reference, reflecting clustered adiposity‐related cardiometabolic dysfunction and defined by standardized measures routinely obtained in health examinations [17, 18]. Aligning BFP thresholds with MetS prevalence among individuals with adiposity‐defined obesity provides a clear link between the revised Lancet framework and real‐world metabolic burden [17]. This approach is particularly pertinent in Korean and other Asian populations, where cardiometabolic risk occurs at lower BMI levels and BMI‐based definitions may underestimate excess adiposity.

Therefore, this study aims to compare obesity classification patterns based on conventional BMI definitions with the Lancet Commission adiposity‐based criteria in Korean adults undergoing health examinations. Additionally, sex‐ and age‐specific BFP thresholds corresponding to adiposity‐defined obesity were derived to provide clinically relevant criteria for identifying excess adiposity in the general Korean adult population. By establishing population‐specific BFP thresholds anchored to a clinically meaningful framework, this approach seeks to reduce BMI‐related misclassification and support more precise risk stratification.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Data were reviewed from adults aged ≥ 20 years who underwent health examinations between January 2012 and December 2013 at the Health Screening and Promotion Center, Asan Medical Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea (Figure S1). Of 22 838 examinees, individuals with chronic liver disease (cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B or C), an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, overt thyroid dysfunction, a history of cardiovascular disease or malignancy, or use of systemic glucocorticoids or sex hormone replacement therapy were excluded. Participants were further excluded if data required to define obesity or MetS were missing. After exclusions, 18 254 participants were included in the final analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (No. 2020‐0343), and informed consent was waived because all data were anonymized.

Data Collection

Trained nursing staff performed standardized anthropometric measurements. Height and weight were measured in light clothing, and BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and iliac crest at end‐expiration, and waist‐to‐height ratio (WHtR) was calculated as waist circumference divided by height. Blood pressure was measured in the right arm after ≥ 5 min of seated rest using an automated sphygmomanometer (Vital Sign Monitor 300 Series, Welch Allyn, Beaverton, OR, USA). Body composition, including lean and fat mass, was assessed by direct segmental multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (MF‐BIA) (InBody 720, InBody Co. Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea). BFP was calculated as total fat mass divided by total body mass and expressed as a percentage [10, 19].

Venous blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein after an overnight fast. All analyses were performed at the central laboratory of Asan Medical Center. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) was measured using the hexokinase method, and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was determined by high‐performance liquid chromatography. Serum lipid profiles and liver enzymes were assessed by enzymatic colorimetric assays. High‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein and serum creatinine were measured by immunoturbidimetric and Jaffe methods, respectively. eGFR was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation. All assays were performed at 37°C.

Data on medical history, medication use and health‐related behaviours were obtained using standardized self‐administered questionnaires. Regular exercise was defined as meeting at least one of the following criteria: ≥ 30 min of moderate‐intensity aerobic activity on ≥ 5 days/week, ≥ 20 min of vigorous‐intensity aerobic activity on ≥ 3 days/week, or resistance exercise on ≥ 3 days/week. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes mellitus was defined as FPG level ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c level ≥ 6.5%, or current use of glucose‐lowering medication.

Definitions of Obesity and Metabolic Syndrome

BMI‐defined obesity was classified as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, in accordance with the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity guidelines [3, 20]. Adiposity‐defined obesity was determined per the Lancet Commission criteria by the presence of one or more of the following: (a) waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men or ≥ 85 cm in women with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2; (b) WHtR ≥ 0.5 with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2; (c) waist circumference and WHtR thresholds met regardless of BMI; or (d) BMI > 40 kg/m2 [3, 4, 20].

MetS was defined by the presence of ≥ 3 of the following: (a) waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men or ≥ 85 cm in women; (b) triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL or current use of lipid‐lowering medication; (c) high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) < 40 mg/dL in men or < 50 mg/dL in women, or current use of lipid‐lowering medication; (d) systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication; and (e) FPG ≥ 100 mg/dL or current use of glucose‐lowering medication [18, 21].

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were stratified by sex and age (< 40, 40–64 and ≥ 65 years) to account for differences in body fat distribution and metabolic risk. The prevalence of adiposity‐defined obesity and MetS was estimated across strata. Anthropometric, biochemical and clinical characteristics were compared between participants with and without adiposity‐defined obesity. Concordance between BMI‐ and adiposity‐defined obesity classifications was evaluated descriptively.

The association between BFP and MetS was evaluated using sex‐ and age‐stratified, unadjusted binary logistic regression models. Predicted probabilities of MetS were estimated across the continuous range of BFP values using the fitted models. BFP thresholds were defined as the BFP values at which the predicted probability of MetS equalled the observed MetS prevalence among individuals with adiposity‐defined obesity, by inverting the regression equations [17]. Ninety‐five percent confidence intervals for the estimated BFP thresholds were obtained using the bias‐corrected and accelerated bootstrap method with 1000 resamples. Anthropometric characteristics were subsequently summarized for participants classified as obese according to these BFP thresholds. To address potential conceptual overlap between adiposity‐defined obesity and MetS due to the inclusion of waist circumference, modified MetS definitions excluding waist circumference were applied in sensitivity analyses and corresponding BFP thresholds were derived. In addition, a sensitivity analysis accounting for age, current smoking, heavy drinking (≥ 30 g/day), regular exercise and menopausal status (in women) was performed using logistic regression models in which covariates were fixed at the mean value for age and the observed proportions of the binary variables to estimate covariate‐adjusted predicted probabilities.

Sex‐ and age‐stratified receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed to evaluate the ability of BFP to discriminate adiposity‐defined obesity. Finally, to describe the relationship between BMI and BFP at the individual level, scatterplots of BMI versus BFP were generated and BFP distributions were examined across BMI categories defined as underweight, normal weight, overweight and obese. All analyses were conducted using complete cases. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.01.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prevalence of Adiposity‐Defined Obesity and Metabolic Syndrome

Table S1 presents the sex‐ and age‐specific prevalence of adiposity‐defined obesity and MetS in the study population. Overall, adiposity‐defined obesity was more prevalent in men than in women (46.4% vs. 26.5%), with a similar pattern observed for MetS (36.9% vs. 20.9%). In men, adiposity‐defined obesity prevalence remained consistently high across age groups (45.1%–46.5%). MetS prevalence in men increased with age, reaching 41.1% among those aged ≥ 65 years. In women, marked age‐related differences were observed. The prevalence of adiposity‐defined obesity increased substantially with age, from 12.4% in those aged < 40 years to 47.9% in those aged ≥ 65 years. MetS prevalence also increased from 4.6% to 48.9% over the same age range.

Clinical Characteristics According to Adiposity‐Defined Obesity

Both men and women with adiposity‐defined obesity exhibited significantly higher BMI, waist circumference, WHtR and blood pressure than those without obesity (Table 1). Body composition measures, including lean mass, fat mass and BFP, were also higher in the obesity group, with a mean BFP of 25.0% (SD, 4.9) in men and 35.6% (SD, 4.7) in women. Individuals with adiposity‐defined obesity showed unfavourable metabolic profiles in both sexes, including higher glycaemic indices, adverse lipid profiles, elevated liver enzyme levels and increased inflammatory markers compared with those without obesity. Regular physical activity was less prevalent and obesity‐related comorbidities were more frequent among those with adiposity‐defined obesity in both men and women. Additionally, women in this group had a higher prevalence of menopause.

Body Mass Index and Adiposity‐Defined Obesity Classifications

In the overall population, concordance between BMI‐ and adiposity‐defined obesity classifications was 92.1%, whereas 1.4% of participants were classified as obese by BMI alone and 6.6% were exclusively by adiposity‐based criteria (Figure 1). Concordance varied by age and sex, with the highest agreement observed in adults aged < 40 years. With increasing age, the proportion of individuals identified as obese only by adiposity‐defined criteria increased, reaching 11.1% in men aged ≥ 65 years and 15.8% in women of the same age group (Figure 1).

Prevalence of Metabolic Syndrome Among Individuals With Adiposity‐Defined Obesity

Among individuals with adiposity‐defined obesity, overall MetS prevalence was 59.7% in men and 52.2% in women. In men, age‐stratified prevalence was 54.3% among those aged < 40 years, 59.6% in those aged 40–64 years and increased to 64.8% among those aged ≥ 65 years. In women, MetS prevalence was lower in younger individuals (37.0% in those aged < 40 years) but increased substantially with age, reaching 48.4% in those aged 40–64 years and 71.7% in those aged ≥ 65 years, indicating a pronounced age‐related gradient.

Body Fat Percentage Thresholds Based on Metabolic Syndrome Prevalence

Sex‐specific predicted probability curves for MetS were estimated across the continuous BFP range (Figure 2). In both sexes, the predicted probability of MetS increased progressively with increasing BFP; however, at comparable BFP levels, women consistently exhibited a lower predicted MetS probability than men. Based on these curves, BFP thresholds corresponding to adiposity‐defined obesity were identified at the points where the predicted probabilities matched the observed MetS prevalence in the adiposity‐defined obesity group.

Sex‐specific body fat percentage thresholds for metabolic syndrome based on predicted probability curves. Solid (men) and dashed (women) curves show predicted probabilities of MetS according to BFP, estimated using sex‐stratified logistic regression models. First, the prevalence of MetS in individuals with adiposity‐defined obesity was plotted as a horizontal reference probability on the y‐axis. Second, the BFP at which each sex‐specific predicted probability curve intersected this reference probability was determined. Finally, these intersection points were projected onto the x‐axis to define sex‐specific BFP thresholds for obesity. BFP, body fat percentage; MetS, metabolic syndrome.

Table 2 summarizes the resulting sex‐ and age‐specific BFP thresholds. The overall BFP threshold was 27.8% for men and 39.5% for women. A significant age‐by‐sex interaction was observed (p < 0.001), with minimal variation across age groups in men, whereas a higher threshold was observed among women aged ≥ 65 years (41.0%). In the sensitivity analyses, similar BFP thresholds were observed when alternative MetS definitions excluding waist circumference were applied (Table S2) and when key covariates were accounted for (Table S3), indicating consistent results across sensitivity analyses. Sex‐ and age‐stratified ROC analyses demonstrated good discriminatory ability of BFP for adiposity‐defined obesity (Table S4).

Anthropometric Characteristics Among Individuals With Body Fat Percentage‐Defined Obesity

Among individuals with BFP‐defined obesity using sex‐specific thresholds, mean BMI, waist circumference and WHtR were 28.3 kg/m2 (SD, 3.1), 97.6 cm (SD, 7.6) and 0.58 (SD, 0.04) in men and 28.8 kg/m2 (SD, 3.2), 93.5 cm (SD, 7.7) and 0.60 (SD, 0.05) in women (Table 3). Among men and women with obesity defined by sex‐ and age‐specific BFP thresholds, BMI and waist circumference were highest in those aged < 40 years and generally declined with age, except for waist circumference among women aged ≥ 65 years. In contrast, WHtR remained generally consistent across age groups in both sexes.

Distribution of Body Fat Percentage Across the Body Mass Index Spectrum

The relationship between BMI and BFP at the individual level is illustrated in Figure S2. Overall, BFP tended to increase with increasing BMI in both sexes. However, a wide vertical dispersion of BFP values was observed at any given BMI, including at the BMI‐defined obesity threshold. In addition, markedly different BFP distributions were observed between men and women, with generally lower BFP values in men and higher BFP values in women at the same BMI level. Figure S3 illustrates the variability in the BFP distribution within each BMI category, with substantial overlap of BFP values across BMI groups.

Discussion

In Korean adults undergoing health examinations, obesity prevalence and classification differed substantially when applying the revised Lancet Commission criteria based on adiposity compared with conventional BMI‐based definitions, with marked variation by sex and age. Adiposity‐defined obesity was more prevalent than BMI‐defined obesity, with increasing discrepancy by age, particularly among women. Using MetS prevalence as an external clinical anchor, we derived population‐specific BFP thresholds for adiposity‐defined obesity. These thresholds differed markedly by sex, with overall BFP thresholds of 27.8% in men and 39.5% in women. Age‐related variation was minimal in men but apparent in older women. In addition, BFP varied widely across BMI levels, indicating heterogeneous adiposity at similar BMI. These findings support adiposity‐defined obesity as a complement to BMI and provide sex‐ and age‐specific BFP thresholds to address a critical evidence gap.

Although BMI is widely used for screening, accumulating evidence shows that it is a crude proxy for adiposity, offering limited and inconsistent information on fat distribution [17, 22]. Large‐scale body composition analyses reveal that the BMI–BFP association is nonlinear and weaker in the BMI range of 20–25 kg/m2, which includes much of the general population [22]. Integrative multi‐omic studies show metabolic heterogeneity within identical BMI categories, underscoring that BMI alone cannot distinguish biologically distinct obesity phenotypes [23]. Our findings support an adiposity approach, as adiposity‐centred measures capture individuals missed by BMI‐based definitions. In this context, BFP is a biologically grounded metric that directly quantifies the fat driving obesity‐related pathology [24]. Recent advances in body composition assessment, including MF‐BIA, have improved the practicality of BFP measurement for broader clinical and epidemiologic use. Among individuals with BFP‐defined obesity, WHtR remained stable across age groups in both sexes, whereas BMI showed clear age‐related differences (Table 3). This suggests that BFP‐defined obesity reflects a consistent level of central adiposity across adulthood, reinforcing its clinical relevance beyond age‐related variation in body size.

Differences in body fat composition between Asian and Western populations provide important context for interpreting BFP‐based obesity thresholds. At a given BMI, Asian individuals have higher total and visceral adiposity, contributing to greater cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI and BFP levels [12, 25]. Consistent with this pattern, the BFP thresholds identified in our study (27.8% in men and 39.5% in women) were lower than those reported in a recent NHANES‐based analysis conducted in a predominantly Western cohort with limited Asian representation (7.8%) [17]. That study proposed obesity thresholds of 30% for men and 42% for women, based on MetS prevalence using BMI‐defined obesity as the reference [17]. Differences in BFP thresholds between studies should be interpreted in light of methodological factors, including reliance on BMI‐defined rather than adiposity‐defined obesity and the younger age distribution of the NHANES cohort [17]. These findings suggest that BFP thresholds from Western populations may overestimate appropriate cut‐offs for Asian adults, especially regarding obesity‐related metabolic risk.

Although the revised Lancet Commission framework recommends ethnicity‐specific cut‐off, empirically derived BFP thresholds remain largely undefined across populations [4]. In Korea, BFP‐based obesity definitions still rely on international criteria, ≥ 25% in men and ≥ 35% in women [10, 26, 27]. One Korean study proposed alternative BFP thresholds for normal‐weight obesity to detect ≥ 1 cardiovascular risk factor (≥ 25.7% in men, ≥ 36.0% in women) [13, 20]. However, these values were derived from individuals with normal BMI, limiting generalizability across the full BMI spectrum. Against this backdrop, our study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of BFP thresholds for adiposity‐defined obesity in Korean adults.

Age‐related changes in body composition further undermine the validity of BMI as a marker of adiposity. With aging, declining fat‐free mass and increasing fat mass lead BMI to underestimate true adiposity [15, 16]. At a given BMI, BFP increases with age by approximately 1.1%–1.4% per decade [22]. Consistent with this concept, discordance between BMI‐ and adiposity‐defined obesity was modest overall (approximately 8%) but increased substantially with age in our study. Approximately one in nine men and one in six women aged ≥ 65 years with excess adiposity were misclassified as non‐obese by BMI, demonstrating that BMI‐based criteria may fail to identify obesity in older adults. Meanwhile, women aged ≥ 65 years required higher BFP thresholds to define obesity. Similar sex‐specific patterns of aging‐related body composition change have been reported previously [22, 28]. Women exhibit steeper age‐related increases in BFP, likely reflecting cumulative postmenopausal hormonal changes [22, 28]. Moreover, our data showed that BFP distributions at the same BMI level differed substantially by sex. Because BMI‐defined classification uses a fixed threshold, it may overestimate adiposity in men while underestimating adiposity in women. Taken together, these findings illustrate an important limitation of BMI as a proxy for adiposity and support incorporating sex‐ and age‐specific BFP thresholds, especially in older adults and women, where BMI is least informative for identifying excess adiposity.

Importantly, the Lancet Commission framework distinguishes preclinical from clinical obesity, defining clinical obesity by organ dysfunction or impaired daily function rather than metabolic abnormalities alone [4]. Obesity‐related illness may involve multiple organ systems, including cardiovascular, respiratory and musculoskeletal systems [4]. In this context, individuals may meet criteria for clinical obesity even in the absence of MetS. Because this study anchored BFP thresholds to MetS as a measurable outcome, the findings primarily reflect the metabolic dimension of excess adiposity. It is therefore possible that BFP thresholds derived using alternative clinical anchors could differ from those observed in this study. Future studies incorporating broader obesity‐related clinical manifestations are required to refine adiposity‐based thresholds within the Lancet Commission framework.

This study provides timely empirical validation of the revised Lancet Commission framework in an Asian population and is, to our knowledge, the first to derive sex‐ and age‐specific BFP thresholds for adiposity‐defined obesity in Korean adults. By anchoring BFP thresholds to adiposity‐defined obesity rather than BMI, our findings provide population‐relevant criteria that may improve obesity classification across adulthood. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross‐sectional design prevents causal inference between obesity and MetS. Additionally, single‐centre recruitment from a voluntary health screening programme may limit generalizability. As such programmes may attract relatively health‐conscious individuals, selection bias cannot be excluded and the male‐predominant composition of our study population may have influenced the observed distributions. Furthermore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to other Asian populations. However, because the study population consisted of individuals undergoing routine health screening rather than patients with overt disease, our findings may be applicable to the general adult population. In addition, the large sample size enabled robust sex‐ and age‐stratified analyses. Nevertheless, external validation in independent cohorts is warranted to confirm the generalizability of the proposed BFP thresholds across different populations. Second, the age distribution was skewed toward middle‐aged adults. Nevertheless, analyses were conducted overall and within predefined age strata, revealing age‐specific patterns in obesity misclassification and BFP thresholds. Lastly, BFP was measured using MF‐BIA rather than dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry (DXA) or other imaging methods. BFP estimation by MF‐BIA may be influenced by hydration status, recent food intake, or muscle mass and may underestimate BFP by approximately 4% compared with DXA [29]. Nevertheless, MF‐BIA has demonstrated excellent measurement reliability, with repeated BFP estimates differing by less than 0.2% [30], and provides a standardized and widely applicable approach for large‐scale epidemiologic studies [31].

In conclusion, obesity classification based on adiposity‐defined criteria reveals substantial misclassification when relying on BMI, especially among older adults and women in Korea. Sex‐ and age‐specific BFP thresholds from adiposity‐defined obesity differ substantially from conventional cut‐offs and those proposed in mainly Western populations. These findings support adiposity‐centred diagnostic approaches and provide population‐specific BFP thresholds to improve identification of excess adiposity across adulthood in Asian populations.
