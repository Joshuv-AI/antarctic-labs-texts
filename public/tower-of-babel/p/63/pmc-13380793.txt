Cortical brain entropy architecture reveals multidimensional structure of schizophrenia
Authors: Qiang Li, Vince D. Calhoun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySchizophrenia is a multidimensional psychiatric disorder lacking a unifying systems-level framework. We introduce a cortical brain entropy architecture that characterizes the spatial and hierarchical organization of functional entropy across the cerebral cortex. In large-scale neuroimaging data, this architecture differentiates schizophrenia from health and captures multidimensional network variation. Entropy alterations follow structured cortical organization, revealing coordinated disruptions within association networks. These findings identify cortical brain entropy architecture as a compact systems-level marker of schizophrenia heterogeneity and establish a quantitative link between macroscale cortical entropy and clinical symptom dimensions.

Why it matters

Cortical brain entropy provides a systems-level perspective on human brain hierarchical organization and its alterations in schizophrenia. Here, we introduce a cortical brain entropy architecture that characterizes the spatial and hierarchical organization of entropy across the cortex, differentiates individuals with schizophrenia from healthy controls, and captures network-level variation as a compact marker of neurobiological heterogeneity.

Introduction

The heterogeneity of schizophrenia (SCZ) points to large-scale network disorganization rather than isolated focal dysfunction, suggesting that the disorder emerges from disrupted coordination across distributed brain systems.1,2,3,4,5 These network-level abnormalities have been linked to altered predictive coding6,7 and related theoretical frameworks, which propose impaired information processing and disrupted hierarchical communication across brain systems.8

Entropy provides a way to quantify brain activity as a proxy for the level of disorder or unpredictability within neural systems.9 When entropy increases, the system becomes more disorganized, which can disrupt structure and coherent function.10 Conversely, when entropy decreases, the system becomes more ordered and structured, promoting stability and efficient processing. However, too little entropy can make the system overly rigid, reducing flexibility and adaptability. In short, healthy brain function tends to balance both near a critical point,11 maintaining enough order for stability while preserving enough variability for flexibility and responsiveness. Disruptions to this balance can lead to impaired function, either through excessive rigidity or excessive randomness.

To capture these complex patterns, we introduce functional cortical brain entropy, a measure quantifying the spatial and hierarchical organization of information processing across the cerebral cortex. We hypothesize that this cortical brain entropy architecture reflects the hierarchical organization of functional structures and provides a compact, systems-level representation of multidimensional disease variation in SCZ.

Materials and methods

Participants

A group of 1,110 participants was selected from the Bipolar and Schizophrenia Network for Intermediate Phenotypes consortium.12,13 This included 640 healthy controls (NC) and 470 individuals diagnosed with SCZ. The mean age of the SCZ group was 36.40 years (standard deviation [SD] = 12.13), while the mean age of the NC group was 35.81 years (SD = 12.28). In the SCZ group, there were 168 males and 302 females. In the NC group, there were 379 males and 261 females. An initial analysis of the dataset showed notable differences between groups in terms of age and sex, as determined by two-sample t tests for all pairwise comparisons. Specifically, the comparison between the SCZ and NC groups revealed a significant difference in age (page = 0.01) and a highly significant difference in sex distribution (psex < 0.001). Data preprocessing

We applied a standard preprocessing pipeline to our resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) data to ensure both data quality and analytic reliability. The process started with rigorous quality control to retain only the high-quality datasets. Each participant’s rsfMRI data were processed using a standardized preprocessing pipeline, including slice-timing correction, rigid-body motion correction, and distortion correction. The first five volumes of each scan were discarded to allow for signal stabilization. Head motion was assessed using framewise displacement (FD), and volumes with FD greater than 0.25 mm were identified for scrubbing. Nuisance regression was performed to remove nonneuronal signals, including motion parameters, white matter, cerebrospinal fluid signals, and other nuisance covariates. The functional time series were temporally filtered using a band-pass filter (0.01–0.08 Hz) to retain low-frequency resting-state fluctuations. The preprocessed data were then aligned to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, resampled to isotropic voxels of 3 mm3, and spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 6 mm.

Cortical entropy was subsequently calculated from the preprocessed functional data after temporal filtering and spatial smoothing to ensure that entropy measures reflected standardized spatial and temporal characteristics of the resting-state signal.

Estimating cortical brain entropy through SampEn metrics

Sample entropy (SampEn) measures the unpredictability of a time series by estimating the likelihood that sequences similar over m points remain similar at the next point.14 It is defined as the negative logarithm of this probability, using a tolerance r to determine similarity. The tolerance is typically set relative to signal variability (e.g., r = 0.2 × SD(X)). Unlike related metrics, SampEn excludes self-comparisons, thereby reducing bias and improving stability for short time series. In this study, the parameters m = 3 and r = 0.3 were adopted based on previous work.15

SampEn is nonnegative by definition, as it is based on the logarithm of a conditional probability. For each voxel in the rsfMRI data, the time series can be expressed as a sequence of measurements x(i), where i indexes discrete time points and x(i) is the corresponding signal amplitude. For a time series of length T, a template vector of length l is constructed asXl(i)={xi,xi+1,…,xi+l−1},

which represents a short segment of the signal and is used to probe its temporal structure.

To quantify similarity between two vectors Xl(i) and Xl(j), with i ≠ j, we use a distance function D[Xl(i),Xl(j)] and a predefined tolerance r. Using this threshold, defineNl(r)=#{(i,j):D[Xl(i),Xl(j)]<r},i≠j,

as the total number of vector pairs of length l whose distance is less than r. Likewise, for vectors of length l + 1,Nl+1(r)=#{(i,j):D[Xl+1(i),Xl+1(j)]<r},i≠j,

denotes the count of matching pairs at the extended template length. SampEn is then defined asSampEn(l,r)=−ln(Nl+1(r)Nl(r)).

In this formulation, the ratio Nl+1(r)Nl(r) estimates the conditional likelihood that two sequences that are similar at length l remain similar at length l + 1. Taking the negative logarithm yields a nonnegative measure of temporal irregularity: smaller values of SampEn indicate more predictable, regular time series, whereas larger values reflect increased complexity and reduced predictability.

Cortical brain entropy distribution across resting-state networks

Entropy maps were summarized within seven canonical resting-state networks (RSNs), including the medial visual, sensorimotor, dorsal attention, ventral attention, frontoparietal, default mode, and subcortical networks. For each participant, mean entropy values were extracted from each network using a predefined RSN atlas.16,17,18 The atlas was resampled to match the dimensions of the subject-specific entropy map. Entropy values were then normalized within each subject to facilitate comparison across networks. Group-level entropy distributions were summarized using the mean and SD of the normalized entropy values for each network.

Spatial correlation analysis between cortical entropy, myelin, and cortical gradient

To investigate the spatial associations between cortical entropy and cortical organizational features, the cortical entropy map was analyzed together with cortical myelin content and the principal cortical gradient. The HCP-MMP (Human Connectome Project Multi-Modal Parcellation) atlas19 was aligned to the cortical entropy map space, and parcel-wise mean entropy values were calculated by averaging voxel-level entropy values within each cortical parcel. The resulting cortical entropy values were extracted across the 180 cortical regions defined by the HCP-MMP atlas. Spatial correlations were then calculated between parcel-wise cortical entropy values and corresponding regional measures of myelin content and the principal cortical gradient.

Statistical analysis

To quantify the differences in brain entropy between NC and SCZ, voxel-wise Cohen’s d effect-size maps were computed. This procedure was applied across all voxels to produce spatial maps representing the extent and direction of entropy alterations between groups. Positive values of d indicate relatively higher entropy in one group compared to the other. These effect-size maps provide a detailed spatial characterization of intrinsic brain activity complexity across psychosis-spectrum conditions, highlighting the regions most prominently affected in each group comparison.

Associations between brain entropy and symptom severity

To examine how brain entropy relates to clinical symptoms, we conducted voxel-level analyses correlating entropy measures with scores on the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). This included the positive, negative, and general psychopathology subscales, each treated as separate predictors. Correlations were computed independently within SCZ cohorts across all voxels within a predefined brain mask. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Voxels surviving FDR correction were interpreted as exhibiting significant associations between brain entropy and symptom severity.

Results

The default mode network has a low-entropy core maintaining structured and predictable neural activity patterns

Brain entropy reflects the variability and complexity of neural activity, providing insight into the organization of cortical structures. As illustrated in Figure 1B, we employed a fine-grained 24-network parcellation scheme20 to precisely localize specific brain regions and networks and examine their entropic distribution across the cortex. Our analysis revealed that frontal areas exhibited low entropy, reflecting highly regular and predictable activity patterns. Likewise, the anterior temporal cortex and orbitofrontal regions, which cluster within the executive control network, also exhibited very low entropy values, indicative of tightly structured neural dynamics. Importantly, regions exhibiting low entropy largely overlapped with the classical default mode network (DMN), including the medial prefrontal and anterior temporal cortices, as illustrated in Figure 1C. This pattern indicates that, although the DMN dominates spontaneous activity during rest, its temporal dynamics are highly constrained and structured, leading to consistently low brain entropy values. From an information-theoretic perspective, low entropy reflects reduced unpredictability and high regularity, suggesting that DMN regions operate in a regime optimized for maintaining stable, coherent patterns of neural activity. Neurophysiologically, this stability may support the continuous maintenance of internal cognitive states by minimizing random fluctuations and preserving coordinated activity across distributed cortical regions. These findings demonstrate that, even while certain networks dominate spontaneous activity at rest, the brain simultaneously maintains highly ordered and energetically efficient patterns. Consequently, the cortical brain entropy architecture provides a quantitative framework linking resting-state dynamics, network organization, and functional stability.

Entropic structure of large-scale brain networks associated with SCZ

In Figure 1D, effect-size cortical entropy maps are illustrated, showing region-specific alterations in brain entropy in individuals with SCZ.

Our analyses revealed significant changes in multiple large-scale networks, including the DMN, visual network, executive control networks, attention-perception network and mirror-neuron system, and auditory networks. Crucially, the DMN and executive control networks exhibited widespread increases in entropy, reflecting more irregular and unpredictable neural activity patterns, whereas primary sensory regions, including the visual and auditory networks, showed more localized entropy changes. The attention-perception networks and mirror-neuron system displayed moderate entropy alterations across association cortices. Overall, these results indicate that brain entropy changes in SCZ are not uniform but instead vary systematically across different networks.

From a neurophysics perspective, the structured, regulated, and predictable neural activity patterns were disrupted in SCZ across these networks, spanning from low-order (primary visual and sensorimotor) to high-order (executive control, mirror-neuron system, and DMN) networks. This disruption resulted in neural activity becoming more irregular, unstructured, and unpredictable.

Hierarchical cortical entropy architecture is associated with myelin content and the principal cortical gradient

To investigate the hierarchical organization of cortical entropy, we examined its spatial correspondence with cortical myelin content and the principal cortical gradient using HCP-MMP parcellation.19

Spatial correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between cortical entropy and myelin content (r = 0.26, ∗∗∗p = 0.0005), as shown in Figure 2B. This finding indicates that cortical regions with higher myelin content tended to exhibit higher entropy values, suggesting a potential relationship between cortical entropy and underlying cortical microstructural organization. We further assessed the relationship between cortical entropy and the principal cortical gradient, which captures the transition from primary sensory-motor regions to transmodal association cortices. Cortical entropy showed a significant negative correlation with the principal cortical gradient (r = −0.16, ∗p = 0.0326), as presented in Figure 2C. This finding suggests that cortical regions located toward the transmodal end of the cortical hierarchy tend to exhibit lower entropy values, indicating a potential relationship between cortical entropy and the macroscale organization of cortical hierarchy.

Together, these results indicate that the spatial distribution of cortical entropy is more strongly associated with cortical myelination than with the principal cortical gradient, highlighting a potential link between entropy and cortical microstructural architecture.

Entropic structure alterations associated with clinical symptoms in SCZ

The unstructured and irregular neural activity within affected brain networks in SCZ was also associated with clinical symptoms. Strong correlations were observed between cortical entropy and clinical symptoms (Figure 3A). Positive symptoms, present in 63% of patients,21 were associated with increased entropy primarily in the DMN, auditory network, and executive control networks. Negative symptoms, present in 74% of patients, also showed significant correlations with entropy in overlapping regions, with the strongest effects observed in the DMN and executive control networks (Figure 3B). Significant correlations were also found between cortical entropy and general symptom scores (67% of patients), predominantly located in the DMN and executive control networks (Figure 3C). Discussion

In the present study, we introduced a cortical brain entropy framework to characterize brain function and SCZ from a neurophysics perspective. Cortical entropy captured the multidimensional structure of neural activity, revealing that specific networks in SCZ exhibit unstructured, irregular, and unpredictable activity. These entropy alterations were systematically associated with clinical symptoms, offering novel insights into SCZ from a neurophysiological perspective. Furthermore, we mapped cortical entropy onto a highly detailed brain network template, enabling precise localization of networks showing entropy alterations compared to healthy individuals.

In the next step, we aim to examine the temporal dynamics of cortical entropy and functional connectivity to better understand their relationship with symptom progression and to identify potential entropy-based biomarkers in SCZ.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the selection of parameters for estimating SampEn may influence the results. Although the parameters used in this study were selected based on previous studies and established practices,15 different parameter settings may affect entropy estimation and its sensitivity. Future work should further investigate parameter optimization and sensitivity analysis to improve the robustness and reproducibility of SampEn-based measures. Second, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to assess longitudinal changes in brain dynamics or determine causal relationships between altered SampEn patterns and clinical progression. Future studies incorporating longitudinal data are needed to investigate the temporal evolution of these alterations and their potential predictive value. Third, the potential effects of medication status were not fully considered, which may influence brain functional dynamics and entropy measures. Future studies should account for medication exposure, dosage, and treatment effects to better characterize the relationship between SampEn changes and disease-related network alterations. Finally, this study primarily focused on cortical entropy alterations at the macroscopic level and did not investigate their potential relationships with cellular- or molecular-level mechanisms. Previous studies22 have suggested that brain entropy is not randomly distributed across the cortex but is associated with specific patterns of cellular and molecular organization, which may reflect underlying neurobiological processes. Future research integrating multimodal approaches, such as molecular imaging, transcriptomic data, or cellular-level measurements, could help elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying cortical entropy changes and their relationship with neural organization.
