Outcome measurement in GNAO1 ‐related disorder: Balancing global severity and domain‐specific change
Authors: Jana Domínguez‐Carral, Juan Darío Ortigoza‐Escobar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This commentary is on the original article by Heideman et al. on pages 1316–1324 of this issue.

Recent efforts to standardize data collection in rare neurodevelopmental disorders are critical to advancing both clinical care and research. In this context, the development of consensus‐based domains and assessment instruments for a GNAO1‐related disorder (GNAO1‐RD) registry represents an important step toward harmonized longitudinal data and future trial readiness. 1 GNAO1‐RD is characterized by marked phenotypic heterogeneity, including movement disorders, epilepsy, and neurodevelopmental delay, which complicates both clinical monitoring and outcome selection. 2 , 3 The use of structured frameworks, such as the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), provides a valuable foundation for capturing multidimensional disease burden. However, the translation of consensus domains into meaningful and sensitive outcome measures remains a key challenge.

A central issue in this field is how best to balance global disease severity metrics with domain‐specific assessments. Composite severity scores offer clear advantages: they are feasible in routine clinical settings, allow standardized comparisons across cohorts, and provide an integrative view of disease burden. In GNAO1‐RD, global severity instruments have been designed to reflect the most clinically relevant domains, including movement disorders, epilepsy, developmental impairment, and associated complications.

At the same time, the multidimensional nature of GNAO1‐RD and fluctuations primarily driven by dyskinetic crises raise important questions regarding sensitivity to change. 4 , 5 Experience from longitudinal cohorts suggests that global severity scores tend to remain stable over time at the group level, which may reflect either true disease stability or limited responsiveness of the measure to subtle clinical changes. In contrast, domain‐specific instruments—particularly those targeting movement disorders—may detect progression or treatment effects not captured by composite measures. This divergence highlights a fundamental methodological consideration: global and domain‐specific tools are not interchangeable, but complementary.

This distinction has direct implications for registry design and future interventional studies. Registries aiming to describe natural history and overall disease burden may appropriately rely on global severity measures as anchor outcomes. However, when evaluating targeted interventions—such as treatments for dystonia or epilepsy—more granular, domain‐specific instruments are likely required to detect clinically meaningful change. The consensus process underscores this need, as agreement on domains was stronger than on specific instruments, reflecting ongoing uncertainty in outcome selection.

Another important implication relates to underrepresented domains. Areas such as social–emotional functioning, communication, and caregiver burden were identified as relevant but remain insufficiently studied and lack validated instruments in this population. Addressing these gaps will be essential to ensure that registries capture not only neurological symptoms but also broader aspects of functioning and quality of life.

Overall, current evidence does not support the conclusion that global severity scores are inherently insensitive to change; rather, it suggests that their performance depends on their intended use. 2 When interpreted as integrative measures of disease burden and used alongside targeted assessments, they can play a central role in standardized longitudinal data collection. Future work should focus on validating outcome measures across disease severities, aligning registry data with trial endpoints, and ensuring that selected instruments are both feasible and sensitive to clinically meaningful change. International collaboration will be essential to achieve these goals in such an ultra‐rare and heterogeneous disorder.
