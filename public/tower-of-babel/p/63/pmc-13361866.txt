Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation alleviates chronic heart failure by targeting cathepsin K/p53-mediated ferroptosis
Authors: Lulu Zhao, Yanzhou Lu, Yuchi Hu, Songyuan Dai, Xing Wang, Xi Zhai, Lijin Pu, Baotong Hua, Jing Wang, Ling Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure remains a major global health challenge, with ferroptosis implicated in its pathogenesis. Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) has shown therapeutic potential for heart failure, but its regulatory mechanism underlying myocardial ferroptosis remains unclear.MethodsIsoproterenol-induced chronic heart failure (CHF) rats received taVNS for 2 weeks. Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography, while myocardial pathology was analyzed using histological staining. Ferroptosis was evaluated via flow cytometry, transmission electron microscopy, and detection of ferroptosis markers. In vitro, Ang II-stimulated H9C2 cells were treated with Ach or subjected to cathepsin K knockdown.ResultsThe taVNS treatment improved LVEF and reduced LVIDd/LVIDs in CHF rats, accompanied by attenuated hypertrophy and fibrosis as well as decreased NT-proBNP, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels. Additionally, taVNS suppressed ferroptosis in cardiac tissues of CHF rats, as evidenced by reduced ROS and Fe²⁺ levels, decreased iron deposition, and upregulated SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression. However, the mAChR and α7-nAChR antagonists (atropine and MLA) abolished these benefits of taVNS. Moreover, taVNS inhibited cathepsin K/p53 pathway to suppress ferroptosis in CHF rat cardiac tissues, rescuing SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression and reducing ROS and Fe²⁺ levels. Acetylcholine replicated taVNS effects in vitro, reversing Erastin-induced ferroptosis and suppressing cathepsin K/p53 pathway.ConclusionThe taVNS treatment modulated cholinergic receptor activation to suppress cathepsin K/p53-mediated ferroptosis, ameliorating cardiac dysfunction in CHF. These findings revealed the specific mechanism by which taVNS exerted cardioprotection via cholinergic receptors, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for CHF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00811-6.

Introduction

Heart failure continues to pose a major global health burden, characterized by progressive cardiac dysfunction and poor clinical outcomes [1]. Although the pathophysiology of heart failure is multifactorial, increasing evidence implicates cardiomyocyte death as a critical contributor to disease progression [2]. Among various forms of regulated cell death, ferroptosis, an iron-dependent process driven by lipid peroxidation, has recently emerged as a key player in cardiac pathology [3, 4]. In the context of heart failure, excessive iron accumulation and oxidative stress render cardiomyocytes particularly vulnerable to ferroptosis, thereby further exacerbating cardiac dysfunction [5]. These observations highlight the urgent need for developing therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis in heart failure.

Recent studies have identified cathepsin K, a lysosomal cysteine protease, as a potential modulator of cardiac function. Elevated cathepsin K expression has been consistently observed in failing hearts [6], and its increased secretion contributes to cardiac structural abnormalities [7]. Notably, genetic ablation of cathepsin K attenuates doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity by improving cardiac energy metabolism and suppressing NF-κB-mediated apoptosis [8]. A study by Xu et al. further demonstrates that cathepsin K inhibition regulates p53 stabilization to enhance oxaliplatin-induced apoptosis [9]. It is well established that p53 triggers ferroptosis by downregulating SLC7A11 and inhibiting GPX4 activity [10, 11]. Taken together, these findings suggest the existence of a potential link between cathepsin K and the regulation of ferroptosis, although this relationship has not yet been explored in heart failure.

The autonomic nervous system, particularly vagal tone, plays a crucial role in maintaining cardiac homeostasis [12, 13]. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) has demonstrated cardioprotective effects in various cardiac pathologies, including heart failure [14, 15]. These benefits are partly mediated through the release of acetylcholine (ACh) [16], which activates both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors [17, 18]. Transcutaneous auricular VNS (taVNS), a non-invasive approach to vagal modulation [19], has shown promise in improving cardiac function in patients with heart failure [20, 21]. Furthermore, VNS has been demonstrated to exert cardioprotective effects against doxorubicin-induced cardiomyocyte death by activating mAChR and nAChR [22]. More recently, studies have reported that VNS can suppress oxidative stress and ferroptosis by activating the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis, thereby alleviating myocardial injury following hepatic ischemia-reperfusion in rats [23]. However, the impact of taVNS on ferroptosis in the context of heart failure, as well as the underlying mechanisms, remains poorly understood.

In this study, we demonstrate for the first time, using both in vivo and in vitro models, that taVNS alleviates ferroptosis in myocardial tissues of rats with chronic heart failure (CHF) by inhibiting the cathepsin K/p53 pathway, consequently improving cardiac function. Moreover, the cardioprotective effects of taVNS are mediated through cholinergic receptor activation. Our findings not only elucidate a previously unrecognized mechanism underlying taVNS-mediated cardioprotection but also identify the cathepsin K/p53/SLC7A11/GPX4 axis as a potential therapeutic target for CHF.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (aged 6–8 weeks) obtained from Spiff Biotechnology (Beijing, China) were housed in specific pathogen‑free conditions, keeping constant temperature (21–26℃) and relative humidity (40–70%). Throughout the study period, rats had ad libitum access to food and purified water in individually ventilated cages systems. After one‑week acclimation period, experiments were conducted following approval by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University. All animal procedures were performed according to the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 and experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University.

CHF rat model

CHF models were induced in SD rats via daily intraperitoneal injections of isoproterenol (ISO, 10 mg/kg) for two weeks, whereas sham-operated controls (Sham) received equivalent saline injections. Following CHF induction, taVNS was administered for two weeks under light isoflurane anesthesia (0.5–1% O₂) using custom-made bilateral magnetic electrodes (2 × 0.5 × 0.05 cm) attached to the auricular conchae. The stimulation parameters were as follows: 2/15 Hz alternating frequency, 2 ms square pulses at 2 mA delivered for 30 min/day (8:00–11:00 AM). Conduction efficacy was confirmed by auricular vibration, with saline-moistened cotton swabs enhancing contact. Rats in the Sham and CHF groups underwent the same electrode attachment procedure but did not receive electrical stimulation. Four weeks prior to CHF induction, AAV vectors carrying either a cathepsin K overexpression construct (cathepsin K-OE) or a negative control (NC-OE) were administered via tail vein injection.

To systematically evaluate the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of taVNS in CHF, SD rats were randomized into three experimental cohorts. The first cohort (for taVNS efficacy evaluation) included the Sham, CHF, and taVNS groups. The second cohort (for verification of cholinergic receptor dependency) included the Sham, CHF, taVNS, taVNS+atropine, and taVNS+methyllycaconitine (MLA) groups. The third cohort (for investigating ferroptosis regulation mechanism) included the taVNS, taVNS + NC-OE, taVNS+cathepsin K-OE, taVNS+cathepsin K-OE+DMSO, and taVNS+cathepsin K-OE + PFT-α groups.

Cell culture and treatment

The rat cardiomyocyte cell line H9c2 (Procell, Wuhan, Hubei, China) was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. For experimental treatments, cells were seeded in appropriate culture dishes and treated as follows: (1) Ang II (1 µM, 48 h) to induce cardiac injury; (2) Ach (50 µM, 48 h) to assess cholinergic protection; (3) Erastin (2.5 µM, 48 h) to induce ferroptosis; or (4) cathepsin K knockdown (shRNA transfection, 48 h) to investigate its role in ferroptosis regulation. All experiments were conducted with appropriate controls (including untreated cells and scrambled shRNA) and were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Cardiac function assessment

Transthoracic echocardiography was utilized to evaluate rat cardiac function. After 2 weeks of treatment, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed in the supine position on a heated platform with their limbs fixed. Chest hair was removed using depilatory cream and an electric shaver to ensure clear acoustic windows. Echocardiographic measurements were performed using a small animal ultrasound imaging system. Two-dimensional and M-mode images were acquired from the parasternal long-axis view to assess left ventricular (LV) parameters, including LV ejection fraction (LVEF), LV fractional shortening (LVFS), LV end-diastolic diameter (LVIDd), and LV end-systolic diameter (LVIDs). All measurements were performed by an investigator who was blinded to the experimental groups.

Histopathological assessment of myocardial tissues

Following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, heart tissues were transversely sectioned and subjected to comprehensive histopathological evaluation. H&E staining was performed to assess general myocardial architecture, with representative whole-heart images captured using a digital camera system. Cardiomyocyte hypertrophy was assessed using WGA-AF488 staining (#MP6329, Maokang Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) under fluorescence microscopy. Myocardial fibrosis was evaluated using Masson’s trichrome staining kit (#G1346, Solarbio, Beijing, China) followed by differentiation in 1% acetic acid, under which collagen deposition appeared blue and cardiomyocytes red. Stained sections were imaged under a microscope, with ImageJ software used for quantitative analysis. All histopathological assessments were performed in a double-blinded manner. Images were coded by an independent third party, and evaluations were conducted by a pathologist blinded to group allocation. Statistical analysis was completed prior to unblinding.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Following the manufacturers’ protocols, cardiac tissue levels of NT-proBNP, TNF-α, IL-6, mAChR, and α7-nAChR were quantified using commercial ELISA kits, including those for NT-proBNP (#E-EL-R3023, Elabscience, Wuhan, Hubei, China), TNF-α (#E-EL-R2856, Elabscience), IL-6 (#E-EL-R0015, Elabscience), mAChR (#CB10321-Ra, COIBO, Shanghai, China) and α7-nAChR (#BY-ER335167, Byabscience, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Briefly, myocardial homogenates (1 g tissue per 9 mL PBS) were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. For NT-proBNP, TNF-α, and IL-6 detection, 100 µL of sample or standard was added to pre-coated wells and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min, followed by incubation with biotinylated detection antibody (60 min) and then HRP conjugation (30 min). For mAChR and α7-nAChR assessment, plates were coated overnight with capture antibodies prior to sample incubation. After color development and reaction termination, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (#Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labelling (TUNEL) assay

Paraffin-embedded cardiac tissue sections were deparaffinized by immersion in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. After PBS washing, sections were encircled with a hydrophobic barrier, treated with 20 µg/mL Proteinase K (37 °C, 20 min), and then permeabilized (room temperature, 20 min). Follow the instructions of the TUNEL kit (#G15011, Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China), sample sections were labeled using a TUNEL reaction mix at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. TUNEL-positive cells (green fluorescence) and total nuclei (blue, DAPI) were visualized under a fluorescence microscope. Apoptotic index was calculated as the percentage of TUNEL-positive nuclei per field.

Oxidative stress and iron deposition analysis in cardiac tissues

To evaluate oxidative stress in cardiac tissues, we performed assays for reactive oxygen species (ROS), Malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione homeostasis. Tissue ROS levels were measured using ROS Detection Kit from BestBio (#BB-470516, Shanghai, China), with myocardial tissue sections incubated with DHE probe working solution at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark, followed by fluorescence measurement. MDA level was determined using the MDA Content Assay Kit (#BC0025, Solarbio). Glutathione homeostasis was assessed by measuring both reduced (GSH) and oxidized (GSSG) glutathione levels using commercial kits for GSH (#BC1175, Solarbio) and GSSG (#BC1185, Solarbio).

For histological evaluation of iron deposition, tissue sections were incubated with Perls’ working solution (Prussian Blue Staining Kit, #G1424, Solarbio) for 20 min at room temperature. Subsequently, counterstaining was performed with eosin for 20 s. Iron deposits were identified as blue granules under light microscopy.

Cellular ferroptosis assessment

In H9C2 cardiomyocytes, intracellular ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) levels were determined using Fe²⁺ Content Assay Kit (#BC5415, Solarbio) with absorbance measurement at 593 nm. In parallel, MDA and GSH/GSSG levels in H9C2 cardiomyocytes were measured using MDA Assay Kit (#S0131S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (#S0053, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Cardiac tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Following antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked for 15 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against SLC7A11 (#A03036-2, 1:200; Boster, California, USA), GPX4 (#A25009, 1:200; ABclonal, Wuhan, Hubei, China), cathepsin K (#ab187647, 1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), or p53 (#10442-1-AP, 1:200; Proteintech). Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (#PV-6000, Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. DAB was used as the chromogen for visualization, and hematoxylin was applied for nuclear counterstaining. Stained sections were then imaged under a light microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cardiac tissues or H9C2 cells using TRIzol reagent (#G3013, Servicebio) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After assessing RNA concentration and purity, cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (#RR037Q, Takara, Tokyo, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (#RR820Q, Takara) on a Real-Time PCR System (#SLAN-96 S, Hongshi Medical Technology, Shanghai, China). Each reaction was performed in triplicate, and the data were analyzed using 2−∆∆CT method. The primers used for qRT-PCR were listed in Table 1.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Protein extracts from cardiac tissues or H9C2 cells were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were quantified using BCA kit (Biosharp, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies anti-cathepsin K (#PB9856, 1:1000; Boster), anti-p53 (#PB9008, 1:1000; Boster), anti-SLC7A11 (#BM5318, 1:1000; Boster), anti-GPX4 (#BM5231, 1:1000; Boster), anti-IL-1β (#ab283818, 1:1000; Abcam), and anti-GAPDH (#ab8245, 1:1000; Abcam). Membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies anti-mouse IgG (#ab6789, 1:5000; Abcam), anti-rabbit IgG (#ab6721, 1:5000; Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a gel imaging analysis system (SINSAGE, Beijing, China).

Flow cytometry analysis of cell apoptosis

Following treatment, cells were collected using 0.25% trypsin (without EDTA), washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in Binding Buffer. Cells were then stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC (#BMS500FI-100, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min in the dark, followed by 10 µL PI (20 µg/mL) to distinguish apoptotic (Annexin V+) and necrotic (PI+) cells. Samples were analyzed immediately using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Mitochondrial morphology was assessed by TEM analysis. Cells were trypsinized, gently pelleted, and fixed with pre-warmed EM fixative (#G1102, Servicebio). Fixed cell pellets were stored at 4 °C and transported on ice for further processing, which included osmium tetroxide post-fixation, dehydration, and epoxy resin embedding. Ultrathin sections were then stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, then imaged using a TEM.

Statistical analysis

Each assay was performed for 3 times. All analyses were performed in a blinded manner. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Two-tailed Student’s t test were used for comparing two variables. One-way ANOVA test was used for multiple variable comparison. P < 0.05 was considered as a significant difference.

Results

The taVNS treatment improved cardiac function in CHF rats

To investigate the therapeutic potential of taVNS, we established a CHF rat model using ISO induction. Compared with the Sham group, ISO-treated rats exhibited significant cardiac dysfunction, as evidenced by reduced LVEF and LVFS and increased LVIDd and LVIDs. Notably, taVNS treatment ameliorated these abnormalities in LVEF, LVFS, and LVIDs in CHF rats (Fig. 1A). HE staining, WGA staining, and Masson staining collectively confirmed that ISO induced myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis in rat hearts, which were attenuated by taVNS therapy (Fig. 1B-D). ELISA analysis revealed elevated levels of the heart failure marker NT-proBNP as well as the inflammatory factors (TNF-α and IL-6) in CHF rat cardiac tissues, accompanied by decreased expression of the cholinergic receptors mAChR and α7-nAChR. Importantly, taVNS treatment reversed the changes in these indicators (Fig. 1E-F). Thus, these results indicated that taVNS effectively improved cardiac function, alleviated myocardial injury and inflammation, and modulated cholinergic receptor expression in CHF rats.

The taVNS treatment ameliorated cardiac function in CHF rats via activating mAChR or α7-nAChR

To validate the role of cholinergic receptors in CHF, we administered atropine (mAChR inhibitor) or MLA (α7-nAChR antagonist) concurrently with taVNS treatment in CHF rats. Both atropine and MLA effectively blocked the taVNS-mediated increases in LVEF and LVFS, as well as the reduction in LVIDs (Fig. 2A). WGA and Masson staining further demonstrated that atropine or MLA significantly diminished the cardioprotective effects of taVNS, exacerbating myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis (Fig. 2B-D). Furthermore, in taVNS-treated CHF rats, administration of atropine or MLA led to a rebound of NT-proBNP, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels (Fig. 2E-F) and suppressed mAChR and α7-nAChR expression (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these results confirmed that the cardioprotective effects of taVNS depended on the activation of mAChR or α7-nAChR.

The taVNS treatment attenuated ferroptosis in cardiac tissues of CHF rats via cholinergic receptor activation

The number of TUNEL-positive cells in cardiac tissues of CHF rats was significantly higher than that in the Sham group, indicating increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis, which was reduced by taVNS intervention (Fig. 3A). ROS fluorescence intensity was markedly enhanced in the CHF group (Fig. 3B), accompanied by elevated MDA levels and a reduced GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 3C-D), all of which were reversed by taVNS treatment. Prussian blue staining further confirmed a significant increase in iron deposition in the myocardium of CHF rats, which was effectively alleviated by taVNS (Fig. 3E). IHC and WB analyses revealed marked downregulation of the ferroptosis-related proteins SLC7A11 and GPX4 in CHF rats, which were restored by taVNS intervention (Fig. 3F-G). Notably, co-administration of atropine or MLA significantly reversed the protective effects of taVNS, as evidenced by increased TUNEL-positive cells, elevated ROS and MDA levels, reduced GSH/GSSG ratio, enhanced iron deposition, and downregulated SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression compared with the taVNS group (Fig. 3A-G). These findings suggested that ferroptosis was elevated in CHF rat cardiac tissues, which was suppressed by taVNS treatment in a cholinergic receptor-dependent manner.

The taVNS treatment reduced myocardial ferroptosis in CHF rats by inhibiting cathepsin K/p53 pathway

qRT-PCR and WB analyses showed that cathepsin K expression was upregulated in the cardiac tissues of CHF rats, which was normalized by taVNS (Fig. 4A-B). To investigate the role of the cathepsin K/p53 pathway in taVNS-mediated regulation of ferroptosis, cathepsin K knockdown was performed in Ang II-stimulated H9C2 cells (Fig. S2A-B). These in vitro experiments demonstrated that Ang II stimulation upregulated cathepsin K and p53 expression while downregulating SLC7A11 and GPX4 in H9C2 cells, effects were counteracted by cathepsin K knockdown (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometry and TEM analysis revealed that Ang II stimulation increased apoptosis and induced ferroptosis-related mitochondrial morphological changes, including mitochondrial swelling, cristae disappearance and vacuolization. Importantly, these abnormalities were significantly ameliorated by cathepsin K knockdown (Fig. 4D-E). Additionally, cathepsin K knockdown markedly inhibited Ang II-induced increases in Fe²⁺ content and MDA levels in H9C2 cells, as well as the decrease in the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 4F-G). Taken together, these results indicated that taVNS might regulate myocardial ferroptosis through modulating cathepsin K.

WB and IHC analyses showed that myocardial tissues from the taVNS+Cathepsin K-OE group exhibited significantly higher cathepsin K and p53 protein expression compared with the taVNS + NC-OE group, along with reduced SLC7A11 and GPX4 levels. Of note, these changes were rescued by co-treatment with the p53 inhibitor PFT-α (Fig. 5A-B). TUNEL staining further confirmed that cathepsin K overexpression increased apoptosis in taVNS-treated CHF rats, which was partially counteracted by PFT-α intervention (Fig. 5C-D). Moreover, relative to the taVNS + NC-OE group, the taVNS+Cathepsin K-OE group showed elevated MDA and ROS levels and a reduced GSH/GSSG ratio, all of which were nearly restored to the levels observed in the taVNS-alone group following PFT-α co-treatment (Fig. 5E-F). Prussian blue staining revealed that cathepsin K overexpression significantly increased iron deposition in the myocardium of taVNS-treated CHF rats, which was markedly alleviated by PFT-α (Fig. 5G). Overall, these findings revealed that taVNS alleviated myocardial ferroptosis via cathepsin K/p53 pathway, thereby improving cardiac function in CHF rats.

Ach inhibited cathepsin K/p53 pathway and ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes, mimicking taVNS effects

In Ang II-stimulated H9C2 cells, Ang II stimulation significantly increased the expression of fibrosis markers (Col I and Col III) and the pro-inflammatory factor IL-1β (Fig. S1A-B). Moreover, Ach treatment effectively mitigated both apoptosis and mitochondrial morphological alterations induced by Ang II (Fig. S1C-D). In Ang II-stimulated H9C2 cells, Ach treatment improved ferroptosis-related indices, as reflected by reduced Fe²⁺ content (Fig. S1E), upregulated SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression (Fig. S1F), suppressed MDA level, and increased GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. S1G). Taken together, these results indicated that Ach treatment alone was sufficient to attenuate Ang II-induced cardiomyocyte injury. Consistently, qRT-PCR and WB analyses further revealed that Ach treatment inhibited cathepsin K expression in Ang II-stimulated H9C2 cells (Fig. S1H-I). Importantly, these effects were highly consistent with the in vivo results obtained with taVNS, indicating that Ach might exert cardioprotective effects through the same cathepsin K/p53 pathway.

In Ang II-pretreated H9C2 cells, the ferroptosis inducer Erastin significantly increased cathepsin K and p53 expression while reducing SLC7A11 and GPX4 levels, all of which were effectively neutralized by Ach treatment (Fig. 6A). Flow cytometry and TEM analyses further demonstrated that Ach intervention alleviated Erastin-induced apoptosis and the ferroptosis-related mitochondrial morphological changes (Fig. 6B-C). Additionally, Ach treatment significantly suppressed the Erastin-induced increases in Fe²⁺ content and MDA levels, as well as the decrease in the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 6D-E). Collectively, these results implied that Ach recapitulated the effects of taVNS by inhibiting the cathepsin K/p53 pathway and suppressing cardiomyocyte ferroptosis. In summary, taVNS modulated cholinergic receptor activation to suppress cathepsin K/p53-mediated ferroptosis, thereby ameliorating cardiac dysfunction in CHF.

Discussion

This study systematically elucidates for the first time that taVNS improves cardiac function in CHF by inhibiting the cathepsin K/p53 pathway-mediated myocardial ferroptosis. Our key findings demonstrated that taVNS exerted cardioprotective effects in a manner dependent on activation of cholinergic receptors including mAChR and α7-nAChR, as evidenced by the reversal of its protective effects on cardiac function, inflammation, and ferroptosis following administration of atropine or MLA. In addition, the crucial regulatory role of the cathepsin K/p53 signaling pathway in myocardial ferroptosis under CHF was also revealed in the present study. Furthermore, acetylcholine, which serves as the primary neurotransmitter of the vagus nerve, was shown to effectively mimic the cardioprotective effects of taVNS in vitro. These discoveries not only provide new insights into autonomic regulation in CHF but also establish a theoretical foundation for the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis.

Ferroptosis, defined as an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, has been increasingly recognized as a key player in the pathogenesis of heart failure [4]. In line with previous reports [24], our investigation revealed significant iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and GSH/GSSG ratio imbalance in myocardial tissues of CHF rats, accompanied by downregulation of the key ferroptosis regulatory proteins SLC7A11 and GPX4. Consistent with clinical observations documenting a strong correlation between elevated cathepsin K levels and CHF progression [25], we consistently observed this phenomenon in both our in vivo and in vitro CHF models. Notably, the present study provides the first mechanistic evidence that cathepsin K promoted myocardial ferroptosis in CHF rats through activation of p53. Specifically, we identified p53 as a key mediator linking cathepsin K to myocardial ferroptosis in CHF, which aligns with established mechanisms whereby p53 suppresses SLC7A11/GPX4 to trigger ferroptosis [10]. This finding offers new perspectives for understanding the relationship between cathepsin K and iron-dependent cell death in CHF pathogenesis. It is particularly noteworthy that cathepsin K may play divergent roles under different pathological conditions. For instance, whereas it exhibits cardioprotective properties in doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity [8], cathepsin K conversely exacerbates cardiac remodeling in obesity-related and pressure overload-induced dysfunction [26, 27]. In our CHF model, cathepsin K promoted myocardial ferroptosis and consequently impaired cardiac function. These divergent effects likely reflect disease-specific pathophysiological contexts and microenvironmental variations, thereby underscoring the complex regulatory networks governing cathepsin K’s pleiotropic actions in cardiac disease.

As a non-invasive neuromodulation technique, the cardioprotective effects of taVNS have been well documented in both preclinical and clinical studies [20, 21]. Consistent with these reports, our study demonstrated that taVNS mediated its cardioprotection through activation of cholinergic receptor (mAChR and α7-nAChR). While further confirming previous observations by Huo et al. regarding cholinergic mediation [20], we significantly extend these findings by identifying the cathepsin K/p53/SLC7A11/GPX4 axis as a downstream effector pathway of taVNS. Specifically, we confirmed that taVNS potently suppressed myocardial ferroptosis and restored SLC7A11/GPX4 expression by inhibiting cathepsin K/p53 signaling. This discovery also echoes recent reports showing that VNS modulates the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis [23], while uniquely positioning cathepsin K/p53 as an upstream regulation of this axis. Ach signaling is recognized as a major regulator of cardiac remodeling and plays a critical role in restoring sympathovagal balance to alleviate cardiac dysfunction [28, 29]. Importantly, we revealed that Ach could mimic the cardioprotective effects of taVNS by inhibiting the cathepsin K/p53 signaling pathway and suppressing ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes in our CHF models. Additionally, previous studies have reported that VNS attenuates endotoxin-induced systemic inflammatory responses [30]. Our experimental data further showed that taVNS suppressed TNF-α and IL-6 levels in myocardial tissues of CHF rats through mAChR/α7-nAChR activation, which aligns with research showing that α7-nAChR inhibits cytokine synthesis via the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway [31, 32]. Moreover, although taVNS may influence hemodynamic parameters, accumulating evidence suggests its cardioprotective effects are primarily mediated through anti-inflammatory and autonomic regulation rather than acute hemodynamic changes, as low-intensity taVNS exerts minimal effects on baseline blood pressure while mitigating sympathetic overactivation and systemic inflammation in heart failure [33, 34]. These results indicate that the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway may represent another mechanism through which taVNS exerts cardioprotective effects in CHF.

A phase II study by Gaetano et al. reports that VNS is safe and well-tolerated in CHF patients while also improving life quality and left ventricular function [35]. Moreover, clinical data from epilepsy, depression, pain and other indications have further confirmed the safety and tolerability of taVNS [36]. Given its non-invasive nature and convenience, taVNS holds significant promise for the long-term clinical management of CHF patients. Furthermore, our findings suggested that the cathepsin K/p53 axis could serve as a potential therapeutic target, providing direction for the development of novel CHF treatments. Clinical trials of odanacatib, a cathepsin K inhibitor, have been completed on osteoporosis treatment [37], whereas p53-targeting therapeutics such as APR-246 are currently undergoing clinical evaluation for oncological indications [38]. These drugs could potentially be repurposed for CHF clinical trials.

Although the therapeutic mechanism proposed in this study for CHF is innovative and clinically translatable, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the ISO-induced CHF model reproduces key features of human CHF, validation in pressure overload or ischemia models would further enhance the generalizability of our conclusions [39, 40]. Second, heart rate and blood pressure were not measured in the current study, as our primary focus was on myocardial remodeling and inflammatory pathways. Future studies incorporating hemodynamic monitoring would help further delineate the contributions of direct anti-ferroptotic effects versus potential hemodynamic influences of taVNS. Third, the specific intermediate mechanisms by which cholinergic receptor activation downregulates cathepsin K remain to be further explored. Existing studies suggest that NF-κB acts as an upstream regulator of cathepsin K [41], and the anti-inflammatory effects of taVNS may be achieved through NF-κB inhibition [42]. Future studies should verify whether taVNS suppresses cathepsin K via the Ac1h-NF-κB axis, which would help further elucidate the mechanism by which taVNS alleviates CHF. Fourth, cathepsin K promotes collagen degradation in fibroblasts, participates in inflammatory responses in macrophages, and affects apoptosis in cardiomyocytes, all of which can influence cardiac function [43]. Although our current investigation primarily focused on cardiomyocytes, future studies for examining taVNS effects in other cell types such as fibroblasts and immune cells would be beneficial. In summary, this study systematically reveals a novel mechanism by which taVNS inhibits myocardial ferroptosis and improves cardiac function through the cholinergic receptor-cathepsin K/p53 signaling axis. Despite some limitations, these findings indicate the promise of noninvasive neuromodulation combined with targeted ferroptosis therapy as a potential breakthrough solution to CHF.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the cardioprotective effects of taVNS in CHF and their underlying mechanisms. Our findings demonstrated that taVNS not only reduced myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis, and inflammation via activating cholinergic receptors (mAChR and α7-nAChR) but also attenuated cardiac tissue ferroptosis by inhibiting cathepsin K/p53 pathway. Importantly, Ach was shown to inhibit the cathepsin K/p53 pathway and suppress cardiomyocyte ferroptosis, recapitulating taVNS effects. Therefore, taVNS improved cardiac function in CHF by modulating cholinergic receptor activation, which subsequently inhibited cathepsin K/p53-mediated ferroptosis. Collectively, these findings establish taVNS as a promising non-pharmacological intervention for CHF and identify the cathepsin K/p53/ferroptosis axis as a novel therapeutic target.
