Global, regional, and national burden of heart failure and its risk factors between 1990 and 2021 and projections to 2050: an analysis of the global burden of disease study
Authors: Shaohua Yan, Peizhi Deng, Ke Chai, Shengfeng Wang, Kai Hu, Jiefu Yang, Hua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAt present, there is a lack of recent heart failure (HF) epidemiological data up to 2021. This study aims to assess the HF burden and its risk factors from 1990 to 2021 and project trends to 2050.Methods and resultsWe derived HF prevalence and years lived with disability (YLDs) from Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, performing subgroup analyses by sex, age, sociodemographic index (SDI), and regions. Health inequalities were measured using the Inequality Slope Index and Concentration Index. Additionally, we made a decomposition analysis of the HF burden and forecasted its impact by 2050. The global age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) and age-standardized YLD rate (ASYR) of HF were 676.68 (95% UI: 598.68–776.84) and 64.7 (95% UI: 44.2–89.47) per 100 000 people and increased by 5.5% and 5.9% from 1990 to 2021, respectively. The ASPR and ASYR were higher in regions in the relatively higher SDI quintile, while the HF burden in regions in the lower SDI quintile is increasing. Ischaemic heart disease accounted for the highest ASPR of HF globally [244.02 (95% UI: 179.09–322.13)], followed by hypertensive heart disease [148.32 (95% UI: 117.32–186.28)]. The ASPR and ASYR of HF are projected to rise from 2022 to 2050, with males dominating.ConclusionThe escalating HF burden represents a critical public health challenge, necessitating immediate intervention. Policymakers must devise precise strategies aimed at curbing and preventing the HF burden.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents a complex clinical syndrome featuring a variety of symptoms and indicators.1 Heart failure stands as the primary reason for hospitalizations among the elderly, distinguished by its recurring and distressing symptoms along with complications and comorbidities that lead to a continuous decline in functional capacity and quality of life.2 Due to the chronic characteristics of HF, the economic burden on the global healthcare infrastructure and financial landscape is considerably and dynamically heavy.3

Previous comprehensive studies have presented the epidemiology of HF over the preceding decade. In 2012, the United States witnessed a 2.4% prevalence of HF, with estimates anticipating an escalation to 3.0% by the year 2030.4 Previous study showed post-discharge mortality of patients with HF was 2.4% at 30 days, 13.7% at 1 year, and 28.2% at 3 years based on registry data from China.5 The age-standardized annual incidence of HF in Australia was 0.348%.6 The scope of the above studies was predominantly confined to populations within a single nation or specific geographic area.

In addition, previous studies elucidating the global epidemiological landscape of HF have provided valuable insights into the epidemiology of HF up to 2019.2 These studies have highlighted the increasing HF prevalence and significant variations across regions and income levels. However, they have not extended their analysis to include more recent data or provide projections for the future, which is crucial for planning and resource allocation amid the ongoing global health challenges. This study seeks to address the research gap by utilizing the latest Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data from 1990 to 2021 and by providing HF burden forecasts up to 2050.

Methods

Data acquisition and disease model

The GBD 2021 report includes data on 204 countries and regions from 1990 to 2021, categorized by age, sex, and geographical location.7 A Bayesian meta-regression disease model (DisMod-MR 2.1) was deployed to gauge the prevalence and years lived with disability (YLDs) rates of HF, synthesizing data from population surveys, cohort studies, registries, administrative health records, and detailed registry and cohort study microdata.8

Definition of heart failure and attributable causes

Heart failure has been clinically identified and determined in research through the application of organized, symptom-guided standards akin to those outlined by the Framingham criteria or the European Society of Cardiology's characterization of congestive HF, which can be found in a previous study.9,10 In summary, HF diagnosis by Framingham standards necessitates meeting two major or one major plus two minor criteria. The European Society of Cardiology requires evident cardiac-related signs and symptoms due to structural or functional heart issues. Additional information on the foundational data sources applied for HF estimations can be accessed through the website of the GBD 2021 Data Input Sources Tool.

The aetiological factors (with corresponding ICD codes detailed in Supplementary material online, Table S1) for HF were identified through a literature review and consultation with domain experts.

Joinpoint regression analysis

The temporal trends in HF epidemiology were examined through the application of the Joinpoint regression analysis model.11 This model is adept at identifying significant changes in trends over time within a time-series dataset, thereby offering insights into the progression of prevalence or YLDs. Furthermore, for a comprehensive view of the overall data set trajectory, the average annual percent change (AAPC) was determined. Statistically, an annual percent change (APC) or AAPC with a 95% CI above zero suggests an increasing trend, while one with a 95% CI below zero indicates a decreasing trend. A 95% CI that includes zero implies no significant trend change.

Sociodemographic index and decomposition methodology

The research employed the sociodemographic index (SDI), a metric that assesses a region's sociodemographic development based on income, education, and fertility levels.12 Based on varying SDI levels, regions are categorized into five distinct groups: low SDI, low-middle SDI, middle SDI, high-middle SDI, and high SDI. We also utilized decomposition analysis to break down the prevalence and YLDs of HF into components influenced by age distribution, population growth, and shifts in disease epidemiology.13

Cross-country inequality analysis

To determine the extent of disparity in HF burden linked to SDI among nations, we used the slope index of inequality and the Concentration Index.14 The Slope Index of Inequality was calculated through regression analysis, with the prevalence and YLDs of HF across all age groups in each country plotted against a sociodemographic development scale. This scale was based on the SDI, using the median position of the population's cumulative distribution within each class stratified by SDI. The health inequality's Concentration Index was ascertained through the alignment of a Lorenz curve with the progressive distribution of population health data, stratified by the SDI, along with the condition's prevalence and YLDs. Moreover, the numerical integration of the area under the curve was performed.15

Projection analysis

The Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model was engaged to forecast the expected numbers and rates of HF prevalence and YLDs over the span from 2022 to 2050. Essentially, the age-period-cohort model, a logarithmic linear Poisson model, assumes that the combined effects of age, period, and cohort are represented through a Poisson distribution (see Supplementary Methods). The prevalence and YLDs of HF for the year 2021, both in absolute numbers and age-standardized rates, were established as a reference. For the BAPC model, a 1% yearly rise was set as a conservative estimate, while a 1% annual decline was regarded as an optimistic projection.

Statistical analysis

We determined the prevalence and YLD counts, age-standardized rates, and percentage changes with 95% uncertainty intervals to measure the HF burden across various ages, sexes, years, and geographic locations. Data variables were presented as numbers, percentages, and ratios. Pearson correlation was applied to determine the linkage between the burden of HF and associated comorbidities. Analyses and visualizations were performed using R version 4.3.3, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Results

Global HF burden

Until 2021, the global cases of prevalence and YLDs of HF were 55.50 million (95% UI: 49.00–63.84) and 5.31million (95% UI: 3.63–7.36), respectively and had increased by 118.2% and 118.6% since 1990. The global age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) and age-standardized YLD rate (ASYR) were 676.68 (95% UI: 598.68–776.84) and 64.7 (95% UI: 44.2–89.47) per 100 000 people and increased by 5.5% and 5.9%, respectively, during the period 1990–2021 (Tables 1 and 2, Supplementary material online, Figure S1). In 2021, the global ASPR of males is higher than that of females [760.78 (95% UI: 673.19–874.71) and 604 (95% UI: 534.95–692.29) per 100 000 people, respectively]. The global ASYR per 100 000 people was 72.68 (95% UI: 49.58–100.54) for males and 57.78 (95% UI: 39.43–79.62) for females (Tables 1 and 2, Supplementary material online, Figure S1). By age group, the ASPR rises with age for both sexes. The HF cases reach a summit between 70 and 74 years in both sexes, with females outnumbering males in HF cases at ages 80 and above (Tables 1 and 2, Figure 1).

Regional HF burden

Regarding GBD regions, East Asia, South Asia, and Western Europe were the top three regions for the largest numbers of prevalent and YLD cases in 2021. High-income North America was the region with the highest ASPR [882.03 (95% UI: 821.29–953.3)] and ASYR [82.36 (95% UI: 56.36–113.54)] in 2021 (Tables 1 and 2). According to SDI regions, countries in high SDI regions had the highest ASPR [730.02 (95% UI: 660.87–811.4) per 100 000], while countries in low-middle SDI regions had the lowest estimates [621.04 (95% UI: 544.54–717.29)] in 2021. Countries in high SDI regions had the highest ASYR [69.05 (95% UI: 47.29–95.26)] per 100 000, while countries in low-middle SDI regions had the lowest estimates [58.51 (95% UI: 40.05–81.08)] (Tables 1 and 2).

National HF burden

In 2021, the national ASPR of HF ranged from 322.43 to 1085.7 cases per 100 000 people. France [1085.7 (95% UI: 920.21–1284.19)], Sweden [1060.21 (95% UI: 936.14–1219.74)], and Poland [1025.57 (95% UI: 957.48–1100.75)] had the highest ASPR of HF. In 2021, the national ASYR of HF ranged from 668.5 to 922.09 cases per 100 000 people. France [103.29 (95% UI: 67.32–143.42)], Sweden [99.87 (95% UI: 66.88–137.67)], and Poland [96.71 (95% UI: 66.12–134.5)] had the highest ASYR of HF. Among 204 countries, the largest increase in ASPR for HF between 1990 and 2021 was seen in Bhutan (12.8) and Spain (12.8), while Austria had the largest decrease (−25.6). Similarly, Bhutan had the largest increase in ASYR (13.0), and Austria had the largest decrease in ASYR (−26.0) (see Supplementary material online, Tables S2 and S3, Supplementary material online, Figure S2).

Joinpoint analysis

The results of the Joinpoint regression analysis showed the ASPR of HF globally had an AAPC of 0.17 during 1990–2021. The turning point occurred in 2018; a decreasing trend was seen in 2018–2021 (APC 2018–2021 = −0.13). Similarly, for the global ASYR, there is an overall increasing trend between 1990 and 2021 (AAPC = 0.18), followed by a decreasing trend between 2018 and 2021 (APC 2018–2021 = −0.11) (Figure 2). For both males and females, there was an overall increase in ASPR. Similar trends were observed in ASPR for males and females (Figure 2). For 5 SDI regions, there was an overall increase for both ASPR and ASYR of HF. The ASPR and ASYR showed a stable, increasing trend in the low and low-middle SDI regions (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3). Notably, during 2019–2021, we observed a decreasing trend only in High SDI regions for ASPR and ASYR. Similar trends were observed in males and females in 5 SDI regions (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3).

Decomposition analysis

For global prevalence and YLDs, the contributions of epidemiological change, population growth and population aging all increased, mainly caused by population growth, leading to 52.09% and 52.00% increases in HF prevalence and YLDs burden in the past 32 years, respectively (Figure 3, Supplementary material online, Table S4). The contribution of aging to prevalence was most pronounced in high SDI (60.35%), high-middle SDI (59.79%), and middle SDI regions (51.74%). The contribution of Population growth to prevalence was most pronounced in middle SDI (64.6%) and low-middle SDI regions (92.48%). Most of the increase in HF YLDs driven by aging was in high SDI (60.11%) and high-middle SDI quintiles (59.59%); middle SDI (51.23%), while the pronounced contribution was population growth in low-middle and low SDI quintiles (Figure 3, Supplementary material online, Table S4). Similar trends of prevalence and YLDs were found for males and females (Figure 3).

Cross-country inequality analysis

In the HF burden assay, in 1990, the slope index of inequality for prevalence and YLDs were 320 and 29, respectively, and in 2021, these values were 718 and 66 (Figure 4, Supplementary material online, Figure S4). This indicates that compared to the countries in the lowest SDI regions in 1990, those in the highest SDI regions had prevalence and YLDs that exceeded the values of 320 and 29, respectively (per 100 000 people) and that these gaps became 718 and 66 (per 100 000 people) in 2021. Between 1990 and 2021, the concentration index for prevalence and YLDs has shown an increasing trend (Figure 4, Supplementary material online, Figure S4).

Causes of HF

In 2021, among all the underlying causes analysed, ischaemic heart disease (IHD) accounted for the highest ASPR of HF globally [244.02 (95% UI: 179.09–322.13)], followed by hypertensive heart disease (HHD) [148.32 (95% UI: 117.32–186.28)], other cardiomyopathy [53.35 (95% UI: 43.65–63.75)], congenital heart anomalies (CHA) [45.33 (95% UI: 37.15–55.17)], and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [43.24 (95% UI: 32.08–57.68)] (see Supplementary material online, Table S5).

The proportion of age-standardized prevalence rate of HF due to each cause varied widely by age group in 2021 (Figure 5). In children and adolescents aged <20 years, CHA has become the predominant cause of HF. In adults aged over 35 years, ICD accounted for the most among all causes of HF, followed by HHD. The effects of COPD on HF were mainly concentrated in adults aged ≥60 years. The proportions of ASPR of HF due to non-rheumatic degenerative mitral valve disease (NDMVD) and non-rheumatic calcific aortic valve disease (NCAVD) increased with age. They ranked third and fourth in the elderly aged ≥90 years.

The proportion of age-standardized prevalence rate of heart failure by cause and age group, 2021.

Supplementary material online, Figure S5 shows the sexual and regional variations in the proportion of ASPR of HF due to each cause. IHD, HHD, and other cardiomyopathy ranked in the top three for the cause of HF in 2021 and remained unchanged compared with 1990 and 2010 (see Supplementary material online, Figure S6). Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease emerged as a significant contributor to HF, particularly in middle or lower SDI regions. In low SDI regions, HHD accounted for most of the HF causes.

Projected global burden of HF

According to the prediction, the future ASPR of HF in the world will be expected to reach 743.65 per 100 000 people, with males for 800.00 per 100 000 people and females for 625.20 per 100 000 people in 2050. Also, we projected that ASYR would continue to increase from 2021 to 2050, reaching 72.22 per 100 000 people, with 77.80 per 100 000 people for men and 60.58 per 100 000 for women, respectively, in 2050 (Figure 6, Supplementary material online, Table S6).

Discussion

This study provides an extensive overview of HF disease burden and trends at the global, regional, and national levels based on sex and age by analysing GBD data from 1990 to 2021. Our study confirmed that HF is prevalent mainly in economically developed regions, with a pronounced impact on male populations and those in middle and elder age groups. The overall burden is projected to continue worsening, with the ASPR and ASYR expected to rise from 2022 to 2050.

The escalating global burden of HF is inextricably tied to two interrelated phenomena: population aging and advances in managing acute and chronic diseases. First, aging populations worldwide directly amplify HF risk through age-related myocardial remodelling, arterial stiffness, and cumulative exposure to comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes.16-18 Our decomposition analysis revealed that population aging accounted for 60.35% of HF burden growth in high SDI regions. Second, while modern therapies for acute coronary syndrome, cancer, and diabetes have markedly reduced short-term mortality, they paradoxically expand the pool of individuals at risk for HF.19-21 These dynamics are particularly pronounced in high-SDI nations, where aging cohorts and advanced care coexist, but are increasingly observed in low and middle-SDI regions undergoing epidemiological transition.

Heart failure exhibits significant epidemiological variation across different regions and nations. High-income regions in North America report the highest ASPR and ASYR of HF, with Central Europe ranking second. The Heart Failure Association ATLAS project reported that HF prevalence among 13 European countries was at a median of 17 per 1000 people, with figures from Greece and Spain at 12 or lower and Lithuania and Germany above 30 per 1000 in 2019.22 Among a cohort of 2.3 million people aged 65 and older, as the overall prevalence edged up from 16.2% to 17.2% over 9 years, even the incidence of HF cases dropped by 32%.23 Furthermore, our investigation found that the ASPR of HF in regions with medium to high SDI are notably higher than those in low-middle and low SDI areas. The same trend was found for the ASYR. In addition, the results showed the largest increase of ASPR and ASYR was seen in lower-SDI areas. Meanwhile, the decomposition analysis showed aging was the main contributor to HF burden growth in high-level SDI regions, while population growth was more pronounced in low-level SDI regions.

This could be well explained. Regions in the higher SDI regions, which often correspond to more developed areas with greater access to healthcare and healthier lifestyles, exhibit higher rates of HF. This may be due to improved diagnostics and surveillance, leading to higher ascertainment of HF cases and an aging population that is more susceptible to HF. Conversely, the lower HF prevalence in underdeveloped countries stems from a confluence of underdiagnosis, competing mortality, and distinct aetiological profiles. First, diagnostic gaps in low SDI regions, including limited access to echocardiography, biomarkers, and specialized care, result in lower diagnoses.24 Second, competing mortality from communicable diseases (e.g. HIV, tuberculosis), maternal-neonatal conditions, injuries, and untreated non-communicable diseases (e.g. low rates of cancer survival) drastically reduces survival to ages where HF typically manifests.8,25,26 Third, epidemiological differences show that IHD, the dominant HF cause in high SDI regions, is less prevalent in low SDI areas.27,28 The increasing burden in lower SDI regions underscores the need for targeted interventions, including improved primary prevention, enhanced healthcare infrastructure, and tailored public health strategies.

The observed higher HF prevalence in men compared with women can be attributed to several factors.29 Wei et al.30 similarly reported higher ASPR in males than females using data up to 2019, which aligns with our 2021 results and shows the persistent sex disparities. Men demonstrate a higher prevalence of HF, potentially due to increased cardiac mass and contractility, which may predispose to conditions like myocardial infarction.31,32 They also face heightened exposure to risk factors like smoking and sedentary behaviour.33,34 Conversely, oestrogen's cardioprotective effects in women, including reduced collagen production and myocardial fibrosis, contrast with men's increased fibrosis under stress, contributing to sex disparities in HF incidence.35

The prevalence of HF rises with advancing age, showing a significant surge in those over 65. A meta-analysis based on the community indicated that the median HF prevalence in individuals aged 60 and above was 11.8%, with a range of 4.7% to 13.3%.36 In Denmark, the typical age of patients upon receiving an HF diagnosis is between 76 and 77 years, with the bulk of these diagnoses occurring between the ages of 66 and 85.37 This demographic shift corresponds with the expanding elderly population, necessitating an integrated HF management approach that encompasses geriatric care. By 2030, projections indicate that the number of elderly individuals will exceed 1 billion.38 Although the advancement of medical interventions has succeeded in deferring the onset of HF and enhancing survival rates among affected individuals, there is a burgeoning demand for the integration of geriatric medicine into the management paradigms of HF.

The current study provides a comprehensive analysis of the underlying causes of HF in 2021, revealing IHD as the leading cause, which is consistent with previous studies.39 Zhang et al.40 found increasing ASPR of IHD-induced HF globally from 1990 to 2021, particularly in low SDI regions, which echoes our findings and emphasizes the need for effective prevention strategies. The ASPR of HF due to various causes showed substantial variation across different age groups. In children and adolescents under 20 years, CHA was the predominant cause, emphasizing the importance of congenital heart defect interventions and the long-term follow-up of these patients to prevent HF.41 COPD predominantly affected adults aged 60 years and above, highlighting the interplay between respiratory diseases and HF, particularly in older populations. This calls for integrated care approaches addressing respiratory and cardiovascular health in older people.42

Furthermore, the aetiology spectrum of HF varied widely across different SDI regions. In low SDI regions, HHD was the leading cause of HF, likely due to the higher prevalence of uncontrolled hypertension and limited access to healthcare.43 Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease was a significant contributor to HF, particularly in middle or lower SDI regions. This may be attributed to higher rates of smoking and exposure to indoor and outdoor air pollution, which are prevalent in these regions.44 Our study provides data concerning the HF-related aetiology spectrum for different regions, enabling public health policymakers to develop more effective prevention and treatment strategies.

We predict the ASPR and ASYR of HF will increase from 2022 to 2050, suggesting a significant disease burden and emerging challenges in HF management. Given the projected increasing burden of HF worldwide and the limitations of current treatments, it is imperative to develop effective prevention and management plans. Accordingly, nations globally must enhance their healthcare systems, make full use of the opportunities brought by demographic changes, pay particular attention to population aging and its medical resource allocation, and provide self-management methods based on evidence-based medical evidence for HF patients in a timely manner.

Limitations

The research has limitations, including notable heterogeneity due to varying cultural, ethnic, and geographic factors across included studies. Additionally, this analysis did not differentiate between HF types, such as those with preserved or reduced ejection fractions, due to insufficient data in the GBD 2021 dataset. Moreover, since HF is viewed as an outcome of multiple aetiologies within the GBD framework, this analysis focuses on HF prevalence, not incidence, due to the unavailability of mortality data, which limits the ability to determine if the rise in prevalence is from higher incidence or lower mortality.

Conclusion

The study from 1990 to 2021 indicated that the HF burden varies significantly by sex, SDI regions, and location and is projected to worsen with aging and growing populations. This insight can inform policymakers in developing cost-effective strategies to address the escalating healthcare needs.
