Associations of carotid flow velocity with cerebral perfusion and cerebral small vessel disease: a community-based prospective study
Authors: Peipei Yang, Xiaoshuai Li, Yuqing Huang, Xinyu Zhao, Ying Hui, Shouling Wu, Zhenchang Wang, Xian-Quan Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo explore the associations of carotid artery hemodynamic parameters with cerebral perfusion and cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) in a healthy population.MethodsA total of 654 participants from the Kailuan community were included after those with incomplete data or carotid stenosis ≥ 50% were excluded. Carotid ultrasound was used to measure the peak carotid flow velocity (PSV), end-diastolic velocity (EDV), resistance index (RI), pulsatility index (PI) and carotid intima–media thickness (IMT) of the common carotid artery (CCA) and internal carotid artery (ICA). MRI was performed to assess cerebral perfusion and CSVD features, including white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunes (LA), cerebral microbleeds (CMB), and enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS). Multivariate regression models were used to analyze the associations of carotid hemodynamics with cerebral perfusion and CSVD.ResultsCCA–EDV was positively associated with whole-brain perfusion (β = 0.14, p = 0.017) and regional perfusion in gray matter (GM) (β = 0.16, p = 0.012), frontal (β = 0.15, p = 0.014), parietal (β = 0.19, p = 0.006), temporal (β = 0.14, p = 0.020), and hippocampus (β = 0.12, p = 0.034), but no significant associations were found between CCA–EDV and white matter (WM) perfusion. A higher CCA–EDV was associated with a lower risk of LA (OR = 0.93, p = 0.029). The CCA–PSV was negatively correlated with the total CSVD score (OR = 0.99, p = 0.047). No significant associations were detected between other carotid hemodynamic parameters and LA, CMB, EPVS, WMH, or total CSVD score.ConclusionsCarotid artery hemodynamic parameters, especially the CCA–EDV, are closely related to cerebral perfusion and the development of CSVD. Reductions in total and regional cerebral perfusion, along with an increased LA burden, were associated with decreased CCA–EDV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03238-3.

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a chronic progressive condition involving the small arteries, arterioles, capillaries, and venules within the brain [1]. CSVD is a potentially devastating disease that requires proactive prevention and improved treatment [2]. Studies have indicated that CSVD accounts for approximately 25% of ischemic strokes and is a significant contributor to vascular cognitive impairment (VCI) and dementia [3]. Typical imaging features of CSVD include white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunes (LA), cerebral microbleeds (CMB), and enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS) [4, 5]. CSVD is associated with cognitive impairment and carries a high risk of vascular events, which may lead to permanent disability or death [3, 6].

Abnormal cerebral perfusion is one of the critical pathophysiological mechanisms of CSVD [7–9]. Chronic hypoperfusion leads to white matter lesions and lacunar infarcts, particularly in deep white matter and subcortical regions [10]. Studies have demonstrated that cerebral blood flow (CBF) is significantly reduced in the normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) of patients with CSVD, suggesting that hypoperfusion may be an important early feature of the disease [11]. Carotid artery flow is an essential indicator for assessing whole cerebral perfusion, as it can reflect the overall blood supply to the brain. Studies have indicated that low carotid artery flow velocity is associated with a greater risk of cerebrovascular diseases further improving the prediction of stroke incidence [12, 13]. In addition, reduced carotid flow velocity may suggest increased intracranial vascular resistance [12]. However, complex pathophysiological interactions exist between carotid hemodynamic changes, cerebral perfusion abnormalities, and CSVD. Previous studies have focused primarily on the impact of pathological conditions such as hypertension and carotid stenosis in the elderly population on CSVD [13, 14]. The association of carotid hemodynamic parameters with cerebral perfusion levels and CSVD in healthy individuals without carotid stenosis has not been systematically investigated. In the general population, subtle abnormalities in carotid artery blood flow and cerebral perfusion may indicate early indicators of CSVD.

This study explored the relationships among carotid artery blood flow velocity, cerebral perfusion, and CSVD in the Kailuan cohort. By investigating these early changes, we can gain a deeper understanding of the pathophysiology of CSVD, which contributes to the early diagnosis of CSVD and facilitates the provision of personalized preventive measures for high-risk populations.

Materials and methods

Study population

All participants in this study were recruited from the Kailuan community population. The participants voluntarily consented to undergo clinical examinations, laboratory tests, questionnaire assessments, carotid ultrasonography, and brain MRI scans between January 2023 and May 2024. A total of 1028 participants were ultimately enrolled in the study. Participants who met any of the following criteria were excluded from this study: (1) incomplete brain MRI data and carotid ultrasound data and (2) common carotid artery (CCA) and internal carotid artery (ICA) stenosis exceeding 50%. This study analyzed 654 subjects after excluding participants with missing data (n = 371) and stenosis exceeding 50% in either the ICA or the CCA (n = 3) (Fig. 1). The institutional ethics committee approved the study protocol, and all the participants provided informed consent.Fig. 1Flow chart of participant inclusion in this study

Flow chart of participant inclusion in this study

Covariates

Demographic information, lifestyle habits, smoking status, drinking status, and medical history of all participants were collected via standardized questionnaires. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated via anthropometric measurements, including body weight and height. All baseline blood samples were collected in the morning following a minimum of 12 h of overnight fasting. The serum levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured via an automatic analyzer. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels were measured in plasma samples. Blood pressure was measured 3 times at 1–2-min intervals and was averaged for analysis.

Carotid hemodynamics measurements

The carotid arteries were examined via an ultrasound scanner (VINNO Technology (Suzhou) Co., Ltd.) equipped with a high-resolution broadband line array transducer (X4-12 L, 6.5–18 MHz). All ultrasound examinations were performed in the supine position by an experienced technician. The IMT was measured longitudinally three times in the right and left common carotid arteries 1 cm proximal to the carotid bulb in B-mode [15]. The blood flow velocity in the CCA and ICA was determined by pulsed wave Doppler during at least three cardiac cycles. The peak carotid flow velocity (PSV), end-diastolic velocity (EDV), automatically calculated resistance index (RI) and pulsatility index (PI) were recorded for each artery.

MRI data acquisition and processing

All participants were examined with 3.0 T MRI (Discovery MR750W, General Electric, USA), a protocol including a 3D T1-brain volume (BRAVO) sequence, 3D T2-weighted sequence, sagittal 3D T2 FLAIR, diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) sequence, and 3D pseudo-continuous ASL sequence. The detailed parameters have been described in previous studies [14]. Cerebral blood flow (CBF) maps were automatically postprocessed on a GE AW 4.6 workstation via 3D ASL Functool software. The CBF maps were co-registered into MNI space. The absolute CBF values were measured in global regions (i.e., the whole brain, gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM)) and regions associated with cognitive function (i.e., frontal, parietal, temporal, and hippocampus).

In addition, total intracranial volume (TIV) as a potential covariate was estimated via statistical parametric mapping (SPM12) to correct for the effects of individual head size during statistical analysis. According to the neuroimaging guidelines and reporting standards for cerebrovascular changes, the neuroimaging markers of CSVD, including LA, CMB, EPVS, and WMH, were analyzed (Fig. 2). The MRI imaging evaluation was conducted with the STRIVE imaging study standards for CSVD [16]. All images were evaluated independently by two experienced neuroradiologists. In cases of disagreements over image interpretation, a consensus was achieved through consultation and discussion with a senior physician. The total burden of CSVD was rated on a scale from 0 to 4 by evaluating the presence of each of the four MRI features [13, 17]. The presence of LA (1 point) was defined as ≥ 1 lacune. The presence of CMB (1 point) was defined as the presence of any CMB. The presence of EPVS (1 point) was defined as moderate to severe enlargement in the basal ganglia (grades 2–4). The presence of WMH (1 point) was defined as confluent deep WMHs (Fazekas scores 2–3) or irregular periventricular WMHs (Fazekas score 3). The participants were classified into two groups on the basis of the total burden of CSVD: 0–1 point counts as mild groups (CSVD-m) and 2–4 points counts as moderate to severe groups (CSVD-s) [18].Fig. 2Representative images of CSVD in MRI. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with SAS software (version 9.4). The baseline characteristics of the participants are presented as the mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. p value < 0.05 were considered significant. In this study, three models were constructed to control for possible confounders. Model 1 was adjusted for sex and age; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, smoking and drinking history, and physical activity; Model 3 was developed based on Model 2 and further adjusted for BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), FBG, TG, TC, LDL-C and/or TIV. A generalized linear regression model was used to analyze the relationships between carotid hemodynamic indices and cerebral perfusion; a binary logistic regression model was used to analyze the relationships with CSVD. The main results are based on Model 3.

Results

Patient characteristics

The demographic characteristics of all the participants are shown in Table 1. Among the total sample (n = 654), 296 (45.26%) were men. The mean age of the participants was 55.28 ± 11.04 years. The average ± SD. velocities of the CCA–PSV and CCA–EDV were 81.70 ± 23.10 cm/s and 16.91 ± 5.58 cm/s, respectively. The average ± SD. velocities of the ICA–PSV and ICA–EDV were 72.50 ± 21.97 cm/s and 19.52 ± 7.72 cm/s, respectively. The mean RI values in the CCA and ICA were 0.73 ± 0.06 and 0.63 ± 0.10, respectively; and the mean PI values were 1.65 ± 0.50 and 1.20 ± 0.44, respectively. The prevalence rates of LA, CMB, EPVS, and WMH were 13.46%, 25.59%, 58.72%, and 21.41%, respectively. A total of 69.11% of the participants were in the CSVD-m group, whereas 30.89% were in the CSVD-s group. Associations between carotid hemodynamic indices and brain perfusion

Among all carotid artery hemodynamic parameters, CCA–EDV was particularly significantly associated with cerebral perfusion in both the incompletely adjusted models (Model 1 and Model 2) and the fully adjusted model (Model 3) (Fig. 3 and Tables S1, S2). Associations of carotid hemodynamic indices with LA, CMB, EPVS, WMH and the CSVD score

The results from the two incompletely adjusted models (Model 1 and Model 2) indicate that CCA–EDV was significantly associated with LA, EPVS, and the CSVD score; CCA–PSV showed a considerable association with WMH and the CSVD score; and ICA–EDV was significantly linked to LA and EPVS (Table S3). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), these associations were attenuated; however, the associations between CCA–EDV and LA (OR [95% CI] 0.93 [0.87, 0.99], p = 0.029), as well as between CCA–PSV and the CSVD score (OR [95% CI] 0.99 [0.98, 1.00], p = 0.047), remained statistically significant (Table 4). Discussion

CSVD is a common, chronic, and progressive cerebrovascular disorder [5]. Carotid hemodynamic parameters, including the IMT, blood flow velocity, and RI, are closely associated with the occurrence and progression of CSVD [19, 20]. These parameters serve as important indicators for evaluating the functional status of cerebrovascular health. This study revealed a significant positive correlation between the CCA–EDV and both total cerebral perfusion and perfusion in various brain regions, suggesting that the CCA–EDV may be an important predictor of cerebral perfusion and CSVD.

Our research comprehensively investigated the relationships between carotid hemodynamics and cerebral perfusion, as well as early changes in CSVD, in healthy individuals without carotid artery stenosis. We found that the CCA–EDV was positively associated with perfusion levels in total and various brain regions, including the frontal lobe, parietal lobe, temporal lobe, and hippocampus. These findings suggest that the CCA–EDV may serve as an important hemodynamic indicator reflecting the state of cerebral microcirculation in individuals without obvious large vessel disease. Notably, the CCA–EDV was independently associated with the occurrence of LA but was not significantly associated with CMB, WMH, EPVS, or the overall CSVD burden score. In addition, the CCA–PSV was significantly negatively correlated with the overall CSVD burden score. These findings indicate that the CCA–EDV and CCA–PSV may be sensitive indicators of early CSVD changes. Furthermore, statistical analysis revealed that other carotid hemodynamic parameters, including the CCA–PI, CCA–RI, ICA–PSV, ICA–EDV, ICA–PI, ICA–RI, and CCA–IMT, were not significantly associated with imaging markers of CSVD or its total burden score (all p > 0.05).

CCA–EDV and cerebral perfusion

The CCA is crucial for supplying blood to the brain, which delivers essential oxygen and nutrients to cerebral tissues [21]. This blood supply is vital for maintaining brain function, supporting metabolic processes, and ensuring cerebral perfusion [22]. This study revealed that among all carotid hemodynamic parameters, only the CCA–EDV had a significant positive correlation with both global and regional cerebral perfusion, which was confirmed through a multivariable model that adjusted for factors, such as age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, SBP, FBG, LDL-C, TG, TC, and TIV.

As noted previously, in a healthy cerebrocardiovascular system, vascular resistance remains at a low level. This low-resistance state ensures continuous and smooth arterial blood flow during both systole and diastole, maintaining normal physiological activities in the brain. However, when distal vascular resistance gradually increases or blood flow obstruction occurs, hemodynamics are significantly affected. Among the hemodynamic parameters, the EDV is more sensitive to changes in resistance than the PSV is. The EDV operates under relatively low arterial blood pressure to overcome resistance [20]. Research using resting-state magnetoencephalography has shown that a low EDV in the CCA is associated with reduced brain activity and cognitive impairment resulting from hypoperfusion [21]. A study in community populations revealed that the EDV indicates the ability to supply blood to the brain during diastole and is more sensitive than the RI and PSV in predicting ischemic stroke [12]. Chung et al. reported that the CCA–EDV, an indicator of cerebral arterial resistance reflecting cerebral atherosclerosis, can serve as a surrogate marker for early elastic arteriosclerosis [23]. In this study, the results revealed a strong correlation between the CCA–EDV and cerebral perfusion in cognitively related brain regions, suggesting that the CCA–EDV could serve as a valuable indicator for predicting cerebral perfusion and cognitive function in healthy individuals.

Carotid hemodynamics and CSVD

In addition, we further investigated the relationships between carotid hemodynamic parameters and CSVD. In a multivariable model (Model 3), the CCA–EDV was associated only with the occurrence of LA and not with WMH, EPVS, CMB, or the overall CSVD burden score. A community-based follow-up study from Taiwan revealed that the risk of stroke decreases as EDV increases, which aligns with our findings [12]. These findings suggest that a greater EDV might reflect better cerebrovascular perfusion and lower vascular resistance. However, another study focusing on an Asian population revealed a significant correlation between low EDV and the severity of WMH volumes, LA, CMB, and the CSVD score [13]. The discrepancy could stem from the relatively younger average age of the participants in this study (55 years, ranging from 47 to 64 years), as age is a significant factor influencing carotid hemodynamics [24]. In addition, the viscoelastic properties and hemodynamic parameters of the carotid artery, such as shear force and flow velocity, may not yet have reached significantly abnormal thresholds in younger populations, thereby weakening the association between the EDV and other CSVD markers [25]. Consequently, age-related vascular physiological changes could be one of the primary reasons for the inconsistent research findings. Future studies should include participants from different age groups to more comprehensively evaluate the predictive value of the EDV for CSVD and its interaction with age. Another possible reason is that the fluctuation range of the CCA–EDV in the study population may not have reached the critical value that triggers WMH or CMB, whereas the LA, as an ischemic lesion, is more sensitive to hypoperfusion [26, 27]. The low load of risk factors and the heterogeneity of pathological mechanisms in healthy individuals may result in insignificant associations with other CSVD markers [20].

Our findings indicate a significant negative correlation between the CCA–PSV and the CSVD score. The CCA–PSV is an important indicator of the hemodynamic status of the carotid artery. A decrease in the CCA–PSV may indicate a reduction in blood flow velocity or an increase in vascular resistance. Such hemodynamic changes could lead to inadequate perfusion of the cerebral microcirculation, potentially resulting in cerebral small vessel pathologies [28, 29]. Interestingly, the CCA–PSV correlated well with the total CSVD score but not with the cerebral perfusion parameters. The likely reason is the strong autoregulatory ability of the brain, which maintains a relatively stable perfusion level through vasodilatation or vasoconstriction mechanisms when carotid hemodynamics change [30]. Furthermore, these results suggest that the CCA–EDV could reflect cerebral blood flow perfusion more sensitively than the PSV.

Carotid atherosclerosis is a recognized risk factor for stroke, CMBs and WMHs [31]. Studies have indicated that the presence of carotid plaques can lead to a decrease in both the CCA–PSV and the CCA–EDV while simultaneously increasing the RI and the PI [32, 33]. These studies demonstrated a correlation between IMT and CSVD burden, with a particularly pronounced effect on the increased burden of WMH among individuals over the age of 70 years [34–36]. Carotid plaques are associated with an increased risk of CSVD, which is particularly correlated with a higher incidence of lacunar cerebral infarction [37]. However, in this study, participants with carotid artery stenosis greater than 50% were excluded, as our objective was to investigate factors related to CSVD in healthy individuals. Few studies have explored the associations between carotid blood flow parameters and neuroimaging findings of CSVD in healthy populations without carotid stenosis. Moreover, we evaluated the effects of carotid hemodynamic changes on whole cerebral perfusion and perfusion in local brain areas associated with cognitive function. These findings may support further exploration of neurovascular mechanisms related to cognitive decline and early intervention strategies. The strengths of this study include the use of a large community-based sample, enabling access to detailed data from the same group of individuals over different periods, laying the foundation for subsequent longitudinal studies.

Limitations

Although the study results revealed a significant association among carotid hemodynamics, cerebral perfusion, and CSVD, several limitations still need to be addressed. First, this study employs a cross-sectional design and is based solely on a Chinese community-based cohort, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic populations. Second, the sample in this study represents a relatively healthy community-dwelling population. As a result, fewer positive events were observed during the study, potentially weakening the associations between carotid ultrasound parameters and both cerebral perfusion and CSVD. Third, the total CSVD burden score was categorized into two groups based on a cutoff value of 1 point in this study, which consistent with previous studies [38, 39]. It is comparable and practical, but may overlook subtle, progressive pathological changes. Future research requires more data or multicenter studies to compensate for the deficiencies of single data sets and improve the reliability and generalizability of the results.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings suggest potential associations between carotid artery hemodynamic parameters (particularly CCA–EDV) and CSVD in healthy individuals, highlighting their research significance for cerebral perfusion and small vessel disease pathogenesis. However, prospective longitudinal studies are essential to further explore the causal relationship, which may yield more sensitive hemodynamic markers for early diagnosis of CSVD, ultimately informing individualized treatment strategies.
