Case Report: Personalizing Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation Therapy for Treatment-Resistant Depression for a Patient with Complex Medical History
Authors: Julia C. Schmidt, Rachel Davis, Erica Rapp, Micol Rothman, Elizabeth Fenstermacher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Patients presenting with treatment-resistant depression (TRD) often have complex medical comorbidities that can limit the use of new and novel treatments. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is FDA-approved and, overall, has a low adverse side-effect profile. Consensus guidelines, however, are broad and lack specificity around managing individual patient risk. Therefore, individual clinicians must make significant determinations about candidacy and risk management in medically complex patient presentations. This case report details a 49-year-old male with a 10-year history of major depressive disorder with significant medical comorbidity (chronic pain, post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia secondary to hypoparathyroidism, and basal ganglia calcifications). We illustrate how interdisciplinary care coordination can allow for safe and effective use of TMS in a medically complex patient with severe TRD. This case highlights the importance of personalized treatment planning to expand access to neuromodulation.

CASE PRESENTATION

Mr. A was a 49-year-old cisgender man referred for transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to treat refractory major depressive disorder (MDD). At intake, he relayed a 10-year history of recurrent severe depressive episodes with persistent depressive symptomatology between episodes. His symptoms included pervasive low mood, fatigue, amotivation, low appetite, difficulty concentrating, and daily passive suicidal ideation. Mr. A was able to fully and frankly discuss his internal state and denied acute safety risk, including adamant repudiation of active suicidal ideation. Mr. A had no history of suicide attempts or psychiatric hospitalizations. He also described a constant state of “low panic” and “fight or flight” punctuated by a history of panic attacks. Despite lacking a formal anxiety diagnosis, his symptoms met criteria for generalized anxiety disorder according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition.1 His anxiety symptoms manifested as excessive rumination, insomnia, weight loss, muscle tightness, hyperventilation, and sense of doom. Upon initial interview, Mr. A reported extensive early trauma, including physical, emotional, and verbal abuse, characterized by neglect, refusal of needs, limited support, high expressed emotion, and physical discipline. While he denied flashbacks or nightmares, he retained vivid memories. He was engaged in psychotherapy with a stable therapist and engaged in daily mindfulness practice. Mr. A also reported an extensive medical history: Graves’ disease status post thyroidectomy, post-surgical parathyroid hormone (PTH) insufficiency with difficulty maintaining normocalcemia, basal ganglia calcifications, and chronic back and neck pain following a disabling work-related injury. Additionally, his history was also significant for hypomagnesemia, hypokalemia, chronic kidney disease stage II, chronic migraines, and neuropathic pain.

At the time of presentation, Mr. A had not fully responded to four prior antidepressant medication trials across distinct classes (bupropion, escitalopram, duloxetine, and esketamine), as well as multiple augmentation strategies from five different medication classes targeting comorbid anxiety, insomnia, and chronic pain (temazepam, zolpidem, gabapentin, tramadol, and botulinum toxin). (See Table 1.) His response to medical marijuana for intractable headaches and depression was also muted. Despite these interventions, his depression remained severe (Patient Health Questionnaire [PHQ9] rating of 26 and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale [HAMD-17] score of 36).

TREATMENT COURSE

Mr. A’s psychiatric treatment history and refractory depressive symptoms made him a reasonable candidate for TMS therapy for treatment-resistant MDD. He was in regular therapy and on a complex medication regimen (Appendix 1). Mr. A endorsed discouragement and skepticism that any treatment would bring relief to his persistent depressive symptoms, but agreed to trialing TMS treatment.

Mr. A’s initial safety screen (Appendix 2) identified recurrent hypocalcemia and stable basal ganglia calcifications as relative contraindications. He had no history of seizures. Given the theoretical risk, we consulted his endocrinologist and neurologist to coordinate care. Review of Mr. A’s imaging confirmed his basal ganglia calcifications were long-standing, radiographically stable, and located outside the TMS targeted area. Due to rapid attenuation of the TMS electromagnetic field, these calcifications were not thought to increase his seizure risk during TMS. Consequently, Mr. A’s baseline risk was thought to be comparable to the general population. Due to his significant difficulty maintaining normocalcemia, however, a modified TMS protocol was agreed upon and implemented. This treatment included weekly lab monitoring of electrolyte levels in addition to our clinic’s standard daily safety questions (Appendix 3).

Our laboratory’s reference range for serum calcium levels defines normocalcemic ranges as 8.6–10.3mg/dL. In the setting of chronic hypoparathyroidism, however, the target range of 8.0–9.0 mg/dL is generally accepted to minimize symptoms and end-organ complications. Therefore, to ensure both safety and therapeutic efficacy, we adopted this latter range as our goal; the range constituted a safe level to perform TMS. As Mr. A was scheduled for treatment initiation, his screening baseline laboratories were below his individual reference range of 8.0mg/dL. Consequently, treatment was postponed for one week pending repeat laboratories, which again demonstrated subtarget calcium levels. In his third week, his serum calcium testing was 8.2mg/dL after his endocrinologist recommended starting low dose (0.25 mcg) calcitriol. With his calcium stabilized within range, TMS treatment began.

TMS treatment was delivered using the BrainsWay H1 Coil and the FDA-approved protocol2 for MDD. This included cortical mapping and weekly redetermination of motor thresholds to minimize seizure risk consistent with our clinic’s standard protocol. A plan for a total of 30 TMS treatment sessions was developed using the H1 Coil and the FDA-approved standard protocol for depression (55 trains, utilizing 18 Hz stimulation frequency, each train 2 seconds in duration [36 pulses], with 20-second intertrain intervals; each treatment delivered a total of 1,980 pulses). (See Figure 1.) The treatment protocol was scheduled for five days a week, except observed holidays or periods when Mr. A’s calcium was outside of safe range. Blood calcium and depressive symptom scores were monitored throughout treatment. (See Table 2.)

Following his initial TMS session, Mr. A reported immediate improvement in mood and energy, accompanied by a renewed sense of hope. Although placebic response and positive expectancy could confound early results, these improvements persisted and compounded over the first two weeks of treatment. Mr. A continued to report sustained and cumulative advancement, including increased energy, reduction of persistent negative emotions, decreased anxious rumination, and significant reduction in suicidal ideation.

In the third week of treatment, Mr. A’s depressive symptoms worsened (PHQ9: 18), coinciding with a significant social stressor—a custodial disagreement and his son’s allegations of abuse by the child’s biological mother. This transient symptom exacerbation may have also been related to a pattern of mid-course symptom intensification, anecdotally described by patients as the TMS dip. Despite relative worsening, Mr. A did continue to report benefit relative to baseline, including increased motivation and energy, stable resolution of suicidal ideation, and unanticipated relief of chronic neuropathic back pain.

Throughout the second half of his treatment course, we observed euthymic affect. Mr. A also continued to report stable increased motivation, energy, and mood, as well as chronic pain abatement. He did still report persistent concentration difficulties and irregular sleep patterns. Mr. A also began to express worry about the durability of improved symptoms.

In the final weeks of treatment, Mr. A reported muscular stiffness consistent with prior hypocalcemic episodes. An additional laboratory draw demonstrated a decline of serum calcium from 8.8 to 8.4 mg/dL over a few days, yet treatment continued as levels were above the 8.0 mg/dL threshold. Over the next several days, however, Mr. A reported increased spasticity of his paraspinal muscles and repeat weekly laboratory testing showed serum calcium levels of 7.7 mg/dL—below the established treatment target. We paused TMS treatment for one week. Despite disappointment with the unplanned interruption, Mr. A continued to report sustained improvement in mood, motivation, and future orientation.

One week later, we retested calcium levels and they were within the established treatment range. Mr. A’s TMS treatment restarted and he completed the remainder for a full course of 30 total sessions.

End of Treatment Course

At Mr. A’s final visit, he described feeling “everything was totally different” and expressed ongoing improved energy, mood, motivation, and engagement in preferred activities, with complete stable resolution of suicidal ideation. His PHQ9 improved significantly, decreasing from 26/27 at baseline to 7/27 post-treatment, with a nadir of 3/27 prior to a significant and ongoing social stressor. Likewise, his HAMD-17 scores improved from an initial rating of 36 to a final rating of 5 on the last treatment day.

Around three months post-TMS completion, Mr. A reached out to the clinic again in appreciation. He reported some symptom recurrence (sleep disruption and anxious rumination) in the context of social stressors. He also reported, however, being able to navigate these challenges in a much healthier way and without significant mood deterioration. He continued to endorse a “completely new way of being” with sustained, durable, and dramatic improvement of mood and anxiety symptoms.

Erica Rapp, MD (Consultation-Liaison Psychiatry/Question 1)

Treatment-resistant unipolar depression affects 1.1% of the United States (US) population, with an annual financial burden of $43.8 billion.3 There is no universally accepted definition of TRD, although a number of staging models have been proposed based on the number and nature of treatments attempted.4 One common clinical definition is insufficient response to two or more antidepressants at adequate dose and duration.4

In registry data, studies of TRD’s prevalence and financial effects have used definitions ranging from failure to respond to a single antidepressant trial to the more complex, involving sequential or concurrent prescriptions for multiple psychotropic medications within a two-year time frame.3,5,6 Notably, six weeks of treatment with the minimum starting dose listed in the American Psychiatric Association’s Practice Guidelines for the Treatment of Patients with Major Depressive Disorder was considered an adequate trial.3,6,7

Definitions of TRD used in TMS literature typically involve a measure of depression severity and antidepressant response failure or intolerance. A score of 20 or higher on the HAMD-17 is a common measure.8,9 The Clinical Global Impression-Severity Scale (minimum score 4) and Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (minimum score 20) have also been used.8,10 The most stringent definition of treatment failure involves poor response to at least two antidepressant medications for at least six weeks each.8,9 Some definitions specify that these medicines be from different classes.8 Many studies only require failure of one antidepressant however.10 Intolerability is classified as discontinuation of at least two and up to four antidepressants due to adverse effects.8,10

Rachel Davis, MD (Interventional/Psychiatry/Question 2)

Options for treating refractory depression include adding psychotherapy, increasing antidepressant dose, switching to another antidepressant, adding a second antidepressant, augmenting with another pharmacologic agent, neuromodulation procedures, and novel or experimental therapies.

Psychotherapy

A recent Cochrane review concluded that there is moderate-quality evidence to support adding psychotherapy to antidepressant treatment for those with TRD, with beneficial short-term, medium-term, and long-term effects.11 This review did not compare effectiveness of the four different psychotherapy types evaluated: cognitive behavioral therapy, dialectical behavioral therapy, interpersonal therapy, and intensive short-term dynamic therapy.

Pharmacotherapy

We should consider switching antidepressants after a first failed trial (either due to lack of initial positive response at two to four weeks or intolerability) in those with less severe symptoms where it is feasible to wait for a response or if the patient expresses such a preference.12 When changing from SSRI/SNRI to tricyclic antidepressants (TCA), response rates range from 44–73%.13 When changing from a TCA to a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAO-I), studies have demonstrated response rates up to 60%.13 In the Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR*D) clinical trial, the MAO-I tranylcypromine was compared to a combination of mirtazapine and venlafaxine. There was no significant difference in response rates between the two groups.13

With no or inadequate response to an antidepressant, there is robust evidence for augmenting an antidepressant with an atypical antipsychotic.12 A systematic review and economic evaluation, however, found lithium to be more clinically effective and economically favorable; lithium also has well-documented protective effects against suicide.14,15 The Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments guidelines, Level 1 provides evidence for augmentation with aripiprazole, quetiapine, and risperidone (first-line), as well as brexpiprazole and olanzapine (second-line), and ziprasidone (Level 3, third-line).12 Other pharmacologic options for enhancement include bupropion, lithium, mirtazapine, modafinil, triiodothyronine (Level-2 evidence, second-line), and stimulants (Level-3 evidence, third-line).12 Intravenous ketamine is experimental but has Level-1 evidence for use with TRD.12 Likewise, intranasal esketamine was approved by the FDA in 2019 for TRD.16 Other experimental or novel pharmacologic strategies include psilocybin, buprenorphine, scopolamine, and anti-inflammatories in individuals with inflammation markers.13

Neuromodulation

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), a noninvasive neuromodulatory technique, is now a first-line recommendation for patients with MDD who have not responded to at least one antidepressant (Level-1 evidence for acute efficacy and Level-3 evidence for maintenance efficacy).17

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is generally a second-line treatment for MDD but may be a first-line option for patients with TRD, acute suicidal ideation, or psychotic features (Level 1 evidence). ECT may also benefit those with repeat medication intolerance, catatonic features, prior favorable ECT response, rapidly declining physical health, or pregnancy plus the aforementioned features (Level-3 evidence).17 Individuals who have not responded to ECT are unlikely to respond to rTMS.17 Third-line neurostimulation treatments include vagus nerve stimulation (Level-3 evidence—acute, Level-2 evidence—maintenance) and transcranial direct current stimulation (Level 2 evidence—acute, Level-3 evidence—maintenance).17 Experimental treatments include deep brain stimulation (Level-3 evidence) and magnetic seizure therapy (Level-3 evidence).17

Elizabeth Fenstermacher, MD (Interventional Psychiatry/Question 3)

The decision to refer a patient to TMS, like all medical and psychiatric treatments, is based on the anticipated risk-to-benefit ratio of the procedure. Is the treatment more likely to help than harm the patient? At the time of TMS referral, patients have frequently been prescribed several classes of antidepressant medication at adequate doses and duration with either partial or nonresponse over years to decades. Based on STAR*D trial findings, we know that for every subsequent medication trial there is diminishing likelihood that further medication trials will yield significant improvements.18 Mr. A demonstrated clear evidence of treatment resistance (failure of > two antidepressant medication trials [four total] and severe ongoing symptomatology [HAMD-17 and PHQ9 both in severe range]) with psychotherapy. TMS has demonstrated a significantly greater likelihood of treatment response and remission in the TRD setting, although not all patients respond. Three major areas generally predict TMS response: (1) patient-related factors, (2) illness-related factors, and (3) TMS procedure.19

Patient-related factors include age, gender, genetic influences, hormones, underlying personality factors, and underlying neurobiological connectivity.19–25 Older age has repeatedly shown decreased TMS response; there is possible diminished response for patients > 45 and significant decline and efficacy in patients > 65 years.19,20 Conversely, female gender has demonstrated a positive predictive effect on TMS treatment response.19,20 Additional patient-related factors include acceptance of treatment and ability to adhere to the recommended treatment protocol, which can significantly predict overall treatment response.26 Another patient-related factor, albeit a modifiable one, that can influence rTMS response is coadministration of certain medication classes. Coadministration of stimulant and bupropion correlate positively with rTMS treatment outcomes;27,28 whereas coadministration of benzodiazepine, antipsychotic, and anticonvulsant medications appear to predict negative response to TMS.27,28 Prior positive response to neuromodulation, including both rTMS and ECT, are both predictive of positive response to current rTMS treatment.20 Another modifiable factor, employment, has also demonstrated positive correlation with likely rTMS treatment response.29

Illness-related factors include characteristics of the depressive episode (duration, severity, core symptoms) and comorbidity.20,30 Patients with a shorter depression duration (< five months) have demonstrated more favorable TMS response.20,31 Recently, refractoriness, or number of treatment failures, has shown negative correlation with treatment response; though, this correlation is likely related to duration and may be a proxy for such.28 Patients with comorbid anxiety or posttraumatic stress disorder diagnoses may demonstrate greater response to TMS.30,32 The presence of agitation (as measured on the HAMD-17, Item 9) has positively correlated with TMS response.20

Finally, the TMS protocol nature, including treatment target (brain region), method of targeting (i.e., neuronavigation), frequency (high, low, theta burst), session number, device, state dependence (i.e., with provocation, mindfulness), and coadministration of therapy all appear to contribute to TMS response.19,26,31,33 High pulse numbers per session and high number of sessions have both demonstrated positive correlations with treatment response.26,31,33 It is hypothesized that TMS state dependence differentially activates task-induced connectivity and influences TMS response.34 Delivery of TMS along with psychotherapy is also highly recommended and predictive of positive response.35

Mr. A had patient-level factors that may predict both positive and negative responses to rTMS. Positive predictive factors included his history of early-childhood trauma, comorbid anxiety, and positive response (3) on HAMD-17 psychomotor agitation Question 9. Mr. A did have individual-level factors that increased his risk of treatment failure, including skepticism, age > 45 yo (49 yo), length of persistent depressive symptoms, severity at time of presentation, and disability status. During treatment, Mr. A’s shift toward positive expectancy and compliance were predictive of positive outcomes; though, the necessary treatment interruptions for safety and psychosocial stressors threatened success.

For TMS, the overall risk-to-benefit ratio for the majority patients is favorable with low likelihood of harm. Typical side effects include localized pain at the stimulation site, discomfort (~39% of patients), and headaches (~28% of patients).36 More serious, though rare, adverse events include risk of inducing hypomania or mania and risk of self-limited seizure.37 Contraindications that increase a patient’s risk of side effects include known neurologic tumors, brain injury (including ischemic stroke or hemorrhagic insult), intracranial hypertension, personal or family history of epilepsy, presence of a pacemaker, cardiac or cerebral stimulators, metal fragments in the eyes, and ferromagnetic metallic implants within 12 inches of the coil stimulator.20,37 Other risk factors that reduce seizure threshold and increase risk include sleep disturbance, substance use, medication changes, electrolyte imbalance, and metabolic disorders.37 With hypocalcemia, the inverse of neuronal excitability to blood calcium levels drives this relationship.38 Low serum calcium levels increase excitability of the central nervous system, lowering seizure threshold and, thus, increasing seizure risk.39 There is also a single case report of a woman who experienced retinal detachment potentially related to TMS-evoked eye movements; she was highly predisposed with premorbid retinal pathology.40

Although TMS has a favorable safety profile, the most serious identified risk is self-limited seizure; the highest seizure risk reported is 0.06%.37,41 The potential efficacy and relatively benign side-effect profile of rTMS made this the preferred modality for Mr. A’s depression, despite the need to mitigate increased seizure risk related to recurrent hypocalcemic episodes. Accordingly, optimizing and maintaining serum calcium within the accepted target range was imperative to minimize seizure risk and safely proceed with TMS treatment.

While not a traditional risk, there are two primary community barriers to treatment: (1) cost (which is prohibitive for most patients without insurance support) and (2) scheduling (the recommended rTMS treatment schedule for MDD includes five sessions/week for six weeks). There are significant variations in coverage; Medicare varies by region and private insurers differ in plan type. Most insurers also require several medication trials (typically at least two and sometimes as many as four) without demonstrated benefit or adverse effects before approving TMS for TRD.42

Significant limitations persist in our capacity to predict whether a particular patient will respond to TMS treatment. Most of the current TMS literature is based on small cohort studies, with significant methodological differences. Results and directionality of response also frequently diverge. While large registry database studies are being employed to strengthen our understanding of these factors, they are correlational and retrospective.31 Very recently, larger prospective randomized controlled trials, such as Theta Burst vs. High-Frequency Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation in Depression (THREE-D), are gaining traction. Hopefully, they will facilitate better understanding of the factors that predispose a patient to positive TMS treatment response.29

Micol Rothman, MD (Endocrinology/Question 4)

Hypoparathyroidism is an uncommon endocrine disorder where parathyroid gland failure or removal leads to hypocalcemia. In normal physiology, PTH acts on the skeleton to mobilize stored calcium. PTH also acts on the kidney level to increase synthesis of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, which facilitates gastrointestinal calcium absorption. Additionally, PTH promotes phosphaturia and tubular reabsorption of calcium. Thus, PTH deficit or absence interferes with maintenance of normal serum calcium and promotes hyperphosphatemia and hypercalciuria.43 There are both genetic and acquired cases of hypoparathyroidism with acquired, particularly post-surgical, hypoparathyroidism as the most common etiology. In fact, Graves’ disease, the catalyst for Mr. A’s thyroidectomy, is considered a high-risk condition for developing post-surgical hypoparathyroidism;44 the increased bone turnover induced by the hyperthyroid state and the increased vascularity of a Graves’ gland make parathyroid injury more likely.44

Symptoms of hypocalcemia include neuromuscular manifestations, such as muscle spasm, numbness, and tingling in the extremities and the perioral region.44 On physical exam, a Chvostek’s sign, in which tapping the facial nerve elicits a facial muscle twitch, may be visible. This response, however, is not as sensitive or specific as the Trousseau’s sign, whereby inflating a blood pressure cuff leads to spasm.43 More serious presentations of hypocalcemia can include seizures or arrhythmias. Up to half of patients with hypoparathyroidism have basal ganglia calcifications with concern that phosphorous elevations may play a role in their development.43 Without the presence of PTH to keep urinary calcium levels low, excess calcium is excreted and may lead to renal stones. Bone turnover is reduced, but bone density is generally preserved without elevated fracture risk. Many patients complain about poor quality of life. Likewise, 36-Item Short Form Health Survey data shows that patients with hypoparathyroidism report more quality-of-life concerns than controls.43

Until recently, we were unable to replace the missing PTH itself, and a piecemeal approach was taken. Such an approach entails a high pill burden for the patient with multiple daily calcium tablets and activated 1-25 vitamin D (calcitriol). We also often employed thiazide diuretics to keep urinary calcium down.45 The goal has been to maintain serum calcium in the lower part of the reference range.45 Over the last 10 years, however, studies showed that replacing PTH with 1–84 recombinant human parathyroid hormone (rhPTH) allowed patients to maintain normal serum calcium with reduced hypercalciuria and decreased calcitriol and overall pill burden.45 This protocol was approved for use in 2015.45 Due to Mr. A’s frequent difficulties with hypocalcemia, basal ganglia calcifications, and quality-of-life symptoms, we started him on 1–84 rhPTH.45 In 2019, however, his specific protocol was pulled from the US market. This created severe challenges for Mr. A. His calcium again destabilized and he underwent emergency room visits and a hospitalization for hypocalcemia. Due to the severity of his case, we were able to obtain PTH replacement via a compassionate use program. In 2024, we provided him with early access to palopegteriparatide, a synthetic version of PTH, which is now FDA approved for hypoparathyroidism. Since that time, his serum calcium has largely maintained goal levels, with urinary calcium at < 300 mg daily and no kidney stones.

CONCLUSION

Overall, Mr. A ended up having a significant response to rTMS treatment. A 50% or greater reduction in HAMD-17 scores is the gold standard for a clinically meaningful response to rTMS.20 Mr. A achieved an 86% symptom reduction, meeting criteria for remission (0–7 HAMD-17 is considered within normal limits). His case illustrates the importance of considering patient-level risk factors and balancing the risks of harm versus benefit when offering a patient rTMS. Our team’s efforts and active electrolyte monitoring during treatment successfully mitigated safety concerns. We also ameliorated patient-level factors (skepticism about treatment) to achieve the best possible treatment outcome.

Although Mr. A’s recurrent, severe hypocalcemic episodes could have posed a barrier to TMS, close interdisciplinary collaboration and laboratory monitoring allowed us to safely deliver a full treatment course. Mr. A’s marked clinical improvement—from prior TRD to remission—underscores both the efficacy of TMS in complex medical cases and the value of patient-tailored safety protocols. This case highlights best practices for delivering safe TMS treatment to patients with significant electrolyte disturbances, demonstrating that, with appropriate precautions and interdisciplinary coordination, even theoretically high-risk patients can achieve very meaningful, positive, and durable benefits.
