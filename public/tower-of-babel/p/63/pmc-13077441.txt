ABSTRACT
Key Points•NUTM1 rearrangement in infant leukemia leads to unique transcriptomic and epigenetic landscapes, defining a distinct leukemia subtype.•BRD9-NUTM1 drives transformation into B-lineage–committed ALL, while also enhancing sensitivity to chemotherapy.

Introduction

NUTM1-rearranged (NUTM1-r) neoplasms comprise a diverse group of malignancies characterized by fusions involving the NUTM1 gene. NUTM1 is located on chromosome 15q14 and encodes a protein containing a highly conserved acidic domain (AD1) capable of binding and activating the histone acetyltransferase p300.1,2 Originally identified in NUT midline carcinoma (NMC), a rare and aggressive form of squamous cell carcinoma, one of the most aggressive human solid malignancies, NUTM1 fusions have since been discovered in various other cancers, including hematologic malignancies.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Among these, NUTM1-r B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (NUTM1-r B-ALL) has emerged as a distinct entity, particularly in infant and pediatric populations.3,4,8 In recent years, NUTM1-r B-ALL has gained increasing attention due to its unique molecular profile and clinical characteristics. This subtype accounts for 21.7% of KMT2A (MLL1)-germ line (KMT2A-g, KMT2A-wildtype) infant B-ALL cases, with an estimated frequency of 3% to 5% in this age group, compared with 0.4% to 0.9% in older children.3 The spectrum of NUTM1 fusion partners in B-ALL differs from those observed in other NUTM1-r neoplasms, reflecting the distinct biology of this leukemia subtype. Various 5′ partner genes have been identified, including BRD9, IKZF1, CUX1, AFF1, ZNF618, SLC12A6, and ACIN1,3,8,9 with BRD9, a bromodomain-containing protein, being the most frequent partner, accounting for ∼26% of infant NUTM1-r B-ALL cases.3 Although NUTM1-r has been associated with increased NUTM1 expression, HOXA cluster upregulation, and downregulation of NOTCH and Hedgehog pathways,3,9, 10, 11, 12 the mechanisms underlying leukemic transformation remain uncharacterized.

Bromodomain proteins function as epigenetic readers that recognize acetylated histone tails to regulate transcription.13 Approximately 60 known human bromodomains are divided into 8 subfamilies based on structural conservation.14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 The best-characterized bromodomain and extraterminal domain family includes BRD2, BRD3, BRD4, and BRDT, whereas family IV contains BRD9 among its 7 members.13 Recent studies, including ours, have demonstrated that BRD9 is a unique member of the newly identified ATP-dependent chromatin remodeler complexes, SWI/SNF complexes22,23 specifically the noncanonical BRG1/BRM-associated factors complex (ncBAF, also known as GBAF).24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 Although the genomic region is rarely mutated in human cancer, BRD9 mRNA levels were found to be post-transcriptionally regulated at the stage of pre-mRNA splicing in cancers harboring mutations in splicing factors,22,23 suggesting previously unrecognized roles of ncBAF in cancer development. Using a newly generated Brd9 conditional knockout murine model, we previously identified that BRD9-depleted hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) undergo both quantitative and qualitative alterations in cell fate, leading to lineage skewing and changes in self-renewal capacity.23 Consistent with these findings, we discovered that BRD9 is essential for the development and maintenance of acute myeloid leukemia, as its loss significantly promotes myeloid differentiation with open chromatin at CTCF sites.23 These data elucidate a mechanistic basis for BRD9/ncBAF in hematopoiesis, highlighting its role in the pathologic perturbation of hematologic malignancies.

Of interest, a series of studies demonstrated that BRD9-NUTM1 fusion, where functional domains such as bromodomain and DUF (domain of unknown function) remain, recurrently occurs in infant B-ALL.4,33,34 NMC is driven by NUTM1 fusion oncoproteins, including BRD4-NUTM1, BRD3-NUTM1, and NSD3-NUTM1, which block differentiation and promote tumor growth.7,35, 36, 37, 38 The NUTM1 portion recruits p300 to activate its histone acetyltransferase activity, resulting in NUTM1-r protein occupancy of 100 to 200 extremely broad, cell-type–specific hyperacetylated genomic domains.39, 40, 41, 42 Although these results indicate the roles of bromodomain family proteins in NUTM1-r malignancies, the functional aspects of the BRD9-NUTM1 fusion in infant B-ALL remain to be elucidated. Furthermore, there has been no report on how the BRD9-NUTM1–fused ncBAF chromatin remodeling complex regulates chromatin or how these mechanistic insights can be translated into therapeutic implications.

In this study, we describe previously unknown characteristics of infant NUTM1-r B-ALL through comprehensive analyses of primary patient sequencing and clinical data, murine models of B-ALL driven by BRD9-NUTM1, and the epigenetic status of transformed hematopoietic cells. We demonstrate that NUTM1-r leukemia constitutes a distinct subgroup in DNA methylation and mRNA expression profiles and that BRD9-NUTM1 functions as a driver event for transformation with lineage restriction. Furthermore, we elucidate the therapeutic vulnerability of leukemias harboring BRD9-NUTM1, comparing them with poor-prognostic KMT2A-rearranged (KMT2A-r) leukemias.

Methods

Patients and samples

This study comprised 8 newly diagnosed infants (aged <12 months) with KMT2A-germ line B-ALL. The patients were enrolled in the MLL10 (n = 4)43 or MLL96/98 (n = 4)44 trials and were selected based on sample availability. Diagnostic bone marrow (BM) or peripheral blood samples were procured from the Japan Children's Cancer Group, Japanese Pediatric Leukemia/Lymphoma Study Group, and The University of Tokyo Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents and/or legal guardians in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was approved by the Human Genome, Gene Analysis Research Ethics Committee of The University of Tokyo (G0948-[19]), the Ethics Committee of Kyoto University Graduate School and Faculty of Medicine (G-1030-8), the Review Board of Tokyo Medical and Dental University (G2000-193 and G2000-103), and the Review Board of Japan Pediatric Leukemia Study Group (JPLSG) (041). Further clinical information is provided in supplemental Tables 1-5, available on the Blood website.

Animals and ethical approval

All animals were housed at The University of Osaka (Japan) and the Foundation for Biomedical Research and Innovation (Japan) using a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle at an ambient temperature of 21.5 ± 1°C and 30% to 70% humidity. All animal procedures were followed by the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees. All mouse experiments were performed according to a protocol approved by the Animal Experiments Committee of the Graduate School of medicine at The University of Osaka (24-055-006) and the Foundation for Biomedical Research and Innovation (22-10) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Results

NUTM1-r leukemia is a distinct subgroup in infant leukemia

To characterize NUTM1-r leukemia, we procured diagnostic samples from 8 infants with KMT2A-germ line (KMT2A-g, KMT2A-wildtype) leukemia enrolled in the MLL1043 and MLL96/98 trials.44 RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) identified 4 patients (50%) harboring NUTM1-r, involving BRD9, AFF1, and CHD9 fusion partners (supplemental Figure 1A; supplemental Tables 1-3). Next, to characterize the mutational profile, targeted deep sequencing with a custom panel for infant ALL was used,45 which revealed RAS pathway mutations in 2 of 4 patients with NUTM1-r (50%) (supplemental Table 4). In 1 case (MLL_10_039), for which a paired normal DNA sample was available, whole-exome sequencing revealed a low mutational burden with 3 nonsilent mutations throughout the exome, comparable to KMT2A-r infant leukemia (supplemental Table 4).4,45 A sequence-based copy number analysis identified an average of 0.5 copy number alterations in infants with NUTM1-r, confirming minimal genomic alterations (supplemental Table 5). To further characterize the NUTM1-r leukemia within the broader spectrum of pediatric leukemias, we used previously published RNA-seq data sets on pediatric and infant B-ALL4,46 and performed gene expression analysis. Hierarchical clustering and uniform manifold approximation and projection identified NUTM1-r leukemia as a distinct transcriptomic subgroup (Figure 1A; supplemental Figure 1B). Analysis of differentially expressed genes in the 4 infants with NUTM1-r and 61 with KMT2A-r revealed low CD34 and FLT3 expression and high NUTM1 expression (Figure 1B). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of B-lineage progenitor and NF-κB signaling pathway signatures, concomitant with downregulation of BM CD34+ cell- and neutrophil-associated signatures in NUTM1-r samples (Figure 1C; supplemental Figure 1C). To systematically characterize the differentiation status of patients with NUTM1-r, we performed Gene Set Variation Analysis on an expanded comprehensive cohort comprising 15 patients with NUTM1-r, including 2 AFF1, 1 BRD9, and 1 CHD9 infants from our cohorts, 2 St Jude infants (1 BRD9 and 1 ACIN1), 9 pediatric St Jude patients (1 BRD9, 3 CUX1, 2 ACIN1, 1 IKZF1, 1 SLC12A6, and 1 ZNF618), and 61 KMT2A-r infant/pediatric patient RNA-seq data sets10 using the Human Cell Atlas data set as reference.47 NUTM1-r ALL consistently demonstrated diminished Lymphoid-Primed Multipotent Progenitor signatures with robust pre–B-cell signatures, indicating a more committed B-lineage phenotype (Figure 1D). In addition, cell-type deconvolution analysis of 2046 patients with B-ALL48 revealed that NUTM1-r cases had significantly lower HSC/multipotent progenitor (MPP) abundance scores with higher B-progenitor scores, supporting the conclusion that NUTM1 rearrangements drive B-lineage commitment across age groups (Figure 1E; supplemental Figure 1D). Furthermore, flow cytometric immunophenotyping corroborated the B-lineage–committed nature of NUTM1-r leukemias, with positive CD10 expression (61%-69%) and negative CD34 expression (1.7%-5.5%) (supplemental Table 2).

To gain a deeper understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of NUTM1-r leukemia, we also performed DNA methylation array analysis on our cohort of KMT2A-g infants (n = 8) and compared them with previously published methylation array data sets on pediatric and infant B-ALL.45,49 Notably, infants with NUTM1-r clustered as a distinct population in the uniform manifold approximation and projection, indicating their unique DNA methylation profile within B-ALL (Figure 1F; supplemental Figure 1E). Differential methylation analysis revealed that NUTM1-r induced genome-wide hypomethylation rather than hypermethylation (Figure 1G). Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis of hypomethylated regions in NUTM1-r indicated the terms associated with transcriptional machinery, for instance, regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, transcription regulatory region nucleic acid binding, and RNA polymerase II cis-regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding (supplemental Figure 1F). In addition, the hypermethylated sites indicated the significant enrichment of GO terms regarding transcriptional regulation, including structural constituent of chromatin and immune response pathways (cytokine receptor binding and immune receptor activity) (supplemental Figure 1G). Through Locus Overlap Analysis (Figure 1H; supplemental Figure 1H), we observed enrichment of hypomethylation at binding sites for key B-cell regulators EBF1, MEF2C, and EP300 and at DNase hypersensitive sites (Figure 1H). Notably, MEF2C and EP300 binding sites were also enriched in hypermethylated regions, suggesting locus-specific methylation regulation (supplemental Figure 1H). Further integrative analysis of mRNA expression with DNA methylation demonstrated additional insights into the epigenome-driven differential gene expression (Figure 1I; supplemental Figure 2A). Notably, TBC1D12 and PHYHD1, a potential oncogene, exhibited hypomethylation, correlating with high expression levels (Figure 1J-K; supplemental Figure 2B-C).50 Conversely, LGALS1 demonstrated hypermethylation in both the promoter and TSS regions, leading to its reduced expression in NUTM1-r leukemias (Figure 1J-K). Consistent with the molecular findings, the patients with NUTM1-r (n = 4) demonstrated a favorable prognosis (supplemental Figure 2D-F).34 These comprehensive analyses revealed the unique genetic and epigenetic signatures of NUTM1-r leukemia. B-cell lineage commitment was uniformly observed across all NUTM1-r samples regardless of age or fusion partner, demonstrating the universal role of NUTM1-r in driving B-cell lineage specification.

Biological effects of NUTM1 rearrangement

BRD9 is now recognized as one of the most recurrent fusion partners in NUTM1-r leukemia, as demonstrated by Boer et al in KMT2A-g Interfant-enrolled infants with B-ALL.34 Other bromodomain-containing proteins, such as BRD3 and BRD4, have been identified as NUTM1 fusion partners in NMC.51 Our previous studies elucidated BRD9's pivotal roles in normal and malignant hematopoiesis, particularly in HSC fate specification and B-cell maturation, with loss leading to myeloid lineage skewing.23 Reported BRD9-NUTM1 fusions preserve the functional domains essential for ncBAF formation and cancer pathogenesis, including the BRD9 bromodomain and DUF domain, along with the NUTM1 NUT domain,4,22 suggesting their essential roles in the disease pathogenesis. To investigate how NUTM1-r affects transcription and chromatin states during B-cell lineage specification, we generated patient-derived BRD9-NUTM1 and AFF1-NUTM1 complementary DNA (cDNA) (supplemental Figure 1A). Biochemical analysis confirmed that BRD9-NUTM1 forms aberrant ncBAF complexes (supplemental Figure 3A-B). Using induced leukocyte stem (iLS) cells,52 immortalized lineage-negative (Lin−: B220−/CD3−/Ter-119−/CD11b−/Gr-1−) ScaI+c-Kit+ cells by stem cell factor (SCF)/IL-7/Flt3L and coculture with OP-9 feeder cells, we conducted B-lineage differentiation assay (Figure 2A). This is performed by manipulating the expression of an ID3-ER (estrogen receptor) fusion protein, whose nuclear translocation is induced by 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT).52 In the presence of 4-OHT, ID3-ER represses E2A activity and B-cell lineage development, arresting iLS cells at the MPP stage and retaining the potential for differentiation into T, B, or myeloid lineages. On withdrawal of 4-OHT, E2A reactivation occurs, promoting B-cell differentiation of iLS cells. To explore the impact of NUTM1-r on B-lineage development, we established iLS cells transduced with either an empty vector, BRD9-NUTM1, or AFF1-NUTM1 cDNA. Flow cytometry and transcriptional profiling reveal that BRD9-NUTM1 promotes early B-lineage differentiation and directs the transcriptional landscape toward B-cell programs. At day 6, BRD9-NUTM1–transduced iLS cells exhibit increased B progenitor differentiation, whereas AFF1-NUTM1 remained comparable to control (Figure 2B; supplemental Figure 3C). At the transcriptional level, BRD9-NUTM1 and AFF1-NUTM1 iLS-derived cells display distinct profiles from controls by RNA-seq (Figure 2C-D; supplemental Figure 3D). GSEA at day 6 reveals enrichment of B-cell–related pathways in the NUTM1-r groups (BRD9-NUTM1 and AFF1-NUTM1) (Figure 2E). By day 14, these B-cell–related pathways remain enriched in the NUTM1-r group (supplemental Figure 3E). Moreover, BRD9-NUTM1–transduced 32Dcl3 cells exhibit growth advantages even under low interleukin-3 (IL-3), consistent with the enhanced proliferation of murine hematopoietic cells (Figure 2F-G). Human cord blood CD34+ cells transduced with BRD9-NUTM1, ACIN1-NUTM1, or AFF1-NUTM1 under B-cell differentiation conditions promoted early lymphoid progenitor (ELP) differentiation, recapitulating murine findings (Figure 2H). BRD9-NUTM1 and ACIN1-NUTM1 also induced robust CD10 expression, indicating enhanced B-lymphoid commitment, whereas AFF1-NUTM1 did not (Figure 2I). These findings underscore the direct involvement of BRD9-NUTM1 in B-lineage commitment in vitro.

BRD9-NUTM1 is a driver event in leukemia development

Current analysis and previous reports have highlighted the unique molecular characteristics of NUTM1-r infant leukemias. However, the role of NUTM1-r in leukemia initiation and maintenance in vivo remains unexplored. To assess the leukemogenic potential of BRD9-NUTM1 in HSCs, we transduced NUTM1-r cDNAs (BRD9-NUTM1, AFF1-NUTM1, or ACIN1-NUTM1) into Lin− BM mononuclear cells, followed by transplantation into lethally irradiated wild-type recipients (Figure 3A). The BRD9-NUTM1 group exhibited significantly shorter survival than controls (Figure 3B; supplemental Figure 4A). BRD9-NUTM1 mice developed leukemia with lymphoblastic morphology, severe macrocytic anemia, leukocytosis, and splenomegaly (Figure 3C-D). Histologic and morphologic analyses revealed hypercellular BM and disruption of normal spleen architecture, with infiltration by immature leukemic cells (Figure 3E). Protein expression of BRD9-NUTM1 was confirmed in both the BM and spleen of leukemic mice (Figure 3F). The leukemic cells from BM or spleen expressing BRD9-NUTM1 displayed a B-cell progenitor, prepro–B-like phenotype, characterized by B220+, CD43+, CD19−, and IgM−, without T-cell (CD3), myeloid cell (Gr-1), and erythroid cell (Ter-119) markers (Figure 3G; supplemental Figure 4C). Immunoglobulin heavy chain rearrangement was confirmed, supporting a B-cell lineage signature (supplemental Figure 4D). Concordantly, recipient mice transplanted with ACIN1-NUTM1–transduced cells exhibited a B-lineage–committed leukemic phenotype, whereas AFF1-NUTM1 recipients did not develop leukemia (supplemental Figure 4A-B). This immunophenotype closely recapitulates human NUTM1-r infant B-ALL, underscoring the model's relevance to the clinical entity. These findings demonstrate that the single transduction of BRD9-NUTM1 serves as a driver event sufficient to induce leukemia, mimicking the characteristics of human NUTM1-r B-ALL.

BRD9-NUTM1 immortalizes hematopoietic cells both in vitro and in vivo

To evaluate the leukemic stem cell potential of the BRD9-NUTM1 mouse model, we conducted serial transplantation from primary BRD9-NUTM1 leukemic mice (Figures 3A and 4A). Notably, neoplastic cells expressing BRD9-NUTM1 recurrently induced leukemia and accelerated mortality (P < .0001) within 3 weeks, manifesting as leukocytosis, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and splenomegaly (Figure 4B-D). The transplantable BRD9-NUTM1 leukemic cells demonstrated the rearrangement of immunoglobulin heavy chain, consistent with the B-lineage identity observed in primary leukemic cells (Figure 4E; supplemental Figure 4D). Furthermore, colony formation assays and liquid culture experiments demonstrated that BRD9-NUTM1–expressing leukemic cells immortalized pre–B colonies in IL-7–supplemented semisolid media and maintained continuous proliferation in liquid culture supplemented with IL-7, FLT3L, and SCF (Figure 4F-G). Such immortalization capacity persisted even under myeloid-supporting conditions (IL-3, IL-6, SCF, and erythropoietin) (supplemental Figure 4E). The resulting colonies lacked T-cell (CD3), myeloid (Gr-1), and erythroid (Ter-119) markers while expressing B-lineage markers B220 and CD43 (supplemental Figure 4F), demonstrating that BRD9-NUTM1 enforces stable B-lineage commitment. To elucidate the functional domains critical for this transforming activity, we analyzed domain-deletion mutants. The BRD9 bromodomain is dispensable for leukemogenesis, whereas the DUF domain (required for ncBAF complex formation) and the NUTM1 MIN domain (essential for p300 recruitment) were required (Figure 4H-I; supplemental Figure 4G).22,36 These results demonstrate that BRD9-NUTM1 expression initiates leukemogenesis and confers leukemic stem cell properties through aberrant ncBAF complex assembly and p300-mediated transcriptional activation.

Comprehensive evaluation of dysregulated chromatin and transcription state in BRD9-NUTM1 leukemia

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which BRD9-NUTM1 fusion impairs differentiation and promotes leukemogenesis, we compared transcriptional profiles of the BRD9-NUTM1 leukemia model with KMT2A-r (MLL-r) mouse leukemia models (KMT2A-MLLT3 [MLL-AF9], KMT2A-MLLT1 [MLL-ENL]) and wild-type BM cells (control). The leukemic cells from each model exhibited distinct transcriptional programs based on the clustering and principal component analysis (Figure 5A). Pathway analysis revealed upregulation of B-cell–related signaling (NCOR and BCL6 signaling) and NF-κB signaling, mirroring observations in patients (Figures 1C,H and 5B). GSEA demonstrated positive correlations between BRD9-NUTM1 and B-cell–related signatures and leukemic transformation-related signatures, including HOXA9-MEIS1 early progenitor hematopoiesis (supplemental Figure 5A). Consistent with findings in human infant ALL, BRD9-NUTM1 upregulated NF-κB signaling (Figure 5C; supplemental Figure 5A). Notably, we observed significant downregulation of Cd34, in line with the B-cell lineage–committed phenotype observed in patients (Figure 1B; supplemental Figure 5B).53 We performed DNA methylation analysis in our murine BRD9-NUTM1 model using the mouse EPIC array to assess its relevance to human NUTM1-r leukemia (Figure 1F-H; supplemental Figures 1E-H and 5C-G). Overall, the methylation profiles clustered by group, with BRD9-NUTM1 samples revealing distinct hypomethylation patterns at binding regions of key B-cell differentiation genes, including STAT5/6, IRF8, and CEBPβ.54 In addition, leukemia-associated genes Hoxa9 and Meis1 demonstrated decreased methylation compared with controls. Importantly, expression-methylation correlations for Lgals1 and Phyhd1 were consistent with those observed in human NUTM1-r samples, indicating concordant epigenetic regulation across species (supplemental Figure 5C-G). To correlate gene expression alterations with BRD9-NUTM1–related chromatin dynamics, we performed genome-wide open chromatin mapping by assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) in BM cells and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) for H3K27ac in purified c-Kit+ BRD9-NUTM1–transduced cells. ATAC-seq revealed unique open chromatin regions in BRD9-NUTM1 leukemia compared with normal BM and other leukemias (Figure 5D-F; supplemental Figures 5H and 6A). Although BRD9-NUTM1–specific accessible genomic regions were few, with most changes (96.9% of peaks in the BRD9-NUTM1 group) reflecting quantitative differences at shared chromatin regions, the overall accessibility profile resembled that of KMT2A-r leukemias (supplemental Figure 6A) while preserving distinct B-lineage features. The BRD9-NUTM1 group exhibited significant enrichment of B-cell–related motifs, including KLF3, PBX3, ATF2, and BATF, in addition to Hoxa9 and Meis1 motifs (Figure 5E-F; supplemental Figure 5H), when compared with normal BM or KMT2A-r leukemia, in accordance with our patient data (Figure 1H). ChIP-seq for H3K27ac, associated with the higher activation of transcription, revealed global hyperacetylation in BRD9-NUTM1 cells (Figure 5G).2 BRD9-NUTM1 ChIP-seq by using HA-tag antibody revealed enrichment of YY1 binding motifs, involved in p300 recruitment55 (supplemental Figure 6B). Integrated analysis across transcriptome, ATAC-seq, and H3K27ac profiles identified coordinately regulated genes with increased H3K27ac, chromatin accessibility, and transcriptional output, particularly for genes adjacent to promoters and enhancers (supplemental Figure 6C-E) in BRD9-NUTM1 cells. Pathway analysis corroborated enrichment for B-cell differentiation (Pax5), p300 recruitment (YY1), and stemness (SOX2) programs (supplemental Figure 6F). Notable examples included the Jag1, critical for B-cell differentiation, and the Cxcl14, which mediates B-cell inflammatory responses (supplemental Figure 6G). These results indicate that BRD9-NUTM1 promotes chromatin accessibility and active transcription, consistent with DNA methylation patterns in patients with NUTM1-r. Collectively, our BRD9-NUTM1 mouse model recapitulates human NUTM1-r leukemia, providing a platform for mechanistic investigation and therapeutic development.

BRD9-NUTM1 leukemia is sensitive to the inhibition of nucleic acid synthesis

To elucidate the dependency of NUTM1-r leukemia on cancer-associated pathways, we conducted a comprehensive drug screen using 340 anticancer compounds on K562 human leukemia cells with inducible BRD9-NUTM1 expression and murine leukemia cells (BRD9-NUTM1 and MLL-AF9) (Figure 6A; supplemental Table 6). Both BRD9-NUTM1–expressing K562 cells and mouse leukemia cells exhibited marked sensitivity to nucleic acid synthesis inhibitors, including α-amanitin, doxorubicin, daunorubicin, actinomycin D, and azacytidine, indicating a strong dependency on DNA/RNA synthesis processes, such as replication, repair, and transcription; in contrast, they exhibited resistance to microtubule polymerization inhibitors, specifically nocodazole and vinblastine sulfate (Figure 6B). We assessed the response of BRD9-NUTM1 leukemia to cytarabine (Ara-C) treatment, a key pyrimidine analog of the therapeutic regimen for infant patients with B-ALL, including those on the Interfant-06 protocol.56 As previously reported, BRD9-NUTM1 leukemia exhibited a robust response to cytarabine therapy in vivo, in contrast to the relative resistance observed in KMT2A-r leukemia (Figure 6C). Limiting dilution assays further validated the efficacy of the treatment, demonstrating a significantly lower frequency of leukemic stem cells in the BRD9-NUTM1 model compared with the KMT2A-r model (Figure 6D). These results suggest that the favorable treatment response of NUTM1-r leukemia reflects transcriptional dependence and enhanced chemosensitivity of leukemic stem cells, revealing molecular vulnerabilities that can inform therapeutic optimization and guide clinical management.

Discussion

Our study presents several novel findings regarding NUTM1-r leukemia, specifically focusing on BRD9-NUTM1 fusion in infant B-ALL. Notably, we identified that NUTM1-r leukemia constitutes a distinct subgroup with unique transcriptional and epigenetic profiles, as evidenced by RNA-seq and DNA methylation analyses. This subgroup is characterized by overexpression of the normally silent NUTM1 gene, low CD34 expression, and high expression of B-cell development-related genes, such as EBF1 and TCF3 along with genome-wide hypomethylation, particularly in regions related to B-cell differentiation, such as EBF1 and BCL6. These findings corroborate previous studies highlighting the unique molecular characteristics of NUTM1-r malignancies, particularly in infants, where the incidence of NUTM1-r B-ALL is notably higher compared with children.4,33,35 Although ectopic expression of NUTM1 in hematopoietic cells may have some roles, the identification of BRD9 as a recurrent fusion partner in NUTM1-r leukemia is particularly significant, underscoring the role of bromodomain-containing proteins in the pathogenesis of B-ALL, similarly to BRD4, BRD3, and NSD3, in NMC and other solid tumors. In addition, the observed upregulation of B-cell–related signatures in NUTM1-r cases is consistent with previous findings that have suggested a role for these fusions in lineage specification and differentiation blockage. Our study adds to the growing body of evidence by demonstrating that BRD9-NUTM1 functions as a driver event in leukemogenesis, promoting lineage restriction and transformation.

The BRD9-NUTM1 fusion protein, similar to other bromodomain and extraterminal domain family fusions, likely exerts its oncogenic effects through chromatin modification and transcriptional regulation. Bromodomains function as epigenetic readers, recognizing acetylated lysine residues on histone tails, thereby influencing gene expression.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 In our study, BRD9-NUTM1 fusion led to significant chromatin remodeling, as evidenced by distinct transcriptional profiles and unique open chromatin regions identified through ATAC-seq. The motif analysis revealed that BRD9-NUTM1 fusion significantly alters chromatin accessibility patterns, particularly enriching for binding sites of key B-cell–related transcription factors, including KLF3, PBX3, ATF2, and BATF, and for the stemness-related HOXA9-MEIS1 program. This unique chromatin landscape orchestrated by BRD9-NUTM1 thus appears to simultaneously promote B-lineage specification and maintain a stem-like state. The fusion protein retains functional domains from both BRD9 and NUTM1, suggesting that these domains play critical roles in the oncogenic process, likely through interactions with chromatin remodelers and transcriptional machinery. Previous studies demonstrated that BRD4-NUTM1 drives hyperacetylated chromatin domain formation at oncogenic loci, promoting transcriptional activation in NMC.42,57 Concurrently, the fusion protein represses differentiation-associated genes by sequestering p300 away from these loci, thereby impeding cellular differentiation.1,40,58 Our findings suggest that BRD9-NUTM1 operates through a similar mechanism, influencing chromatin accessibility, particularly at enhancers marked by H3K27ac. This shared mode of action among NUTM1 fusions likely contributes to the differentiation arrest and oncogenic transformation observed in NUTM1-r leukemia. Integrated analysis of transcriptomic, ATAC-seq, and H3K27ac revealed gene clusters with coordinated increases in chromatin accessibility, active histone marks, and transcription (supplemental Figure 6B-F). Although the lack of fusion junction-specific antibodies currently limits comprehensive target mapping, our data of BRD9-NUTM1 ChIP-seq using HA-tag antibody provide valuable insights into BRD9-NUTM1's direct transcriptional targets and distinct mechanistic features (supplemental Figure 6B). Notably, the cell-type specificity of BRD9-NUTM1 in leukemia vs BRD4-NUTM1 in NMC indicates that each bromodomain partner dictates context-dependent cell fate specification and cancer type.34,39

The impact of NUTM1-r, particularly the BRD9-NUTM1 fusion, on the stemness and transformation potential of leukemic cells is a crucial aspect of our experimental findings. We observed that BRD9-NUTM1 promotes the B-lineage commitment while conferring properties associated with leukemic stem cells. This is distinct from the characteristics of high-risk KMT2A-r infant B-ALL, which is uniquely characterized by early developmental, hematopoietic, and lymphoid progenitor signatures, indicative of an early origin before hematopoietic specification.45,59,60 The dual role is evidenced by the ability of BRD9-NUTM1–expressing cells to immortalize hematopoietic cells in vitro and induce leukemia in vivo, as demonstrated in our murine models. Despite limited transduction efficiency and extended latency period due to the large BRD9-NUTM1 construct (∼5000 base pairs) approaching retroviral vector packaging capacity, consistent leukemia development from minimal transduced cells underscored the robust transforming potential of BRD9-NUTM1. Other NUTM1 fusions demonstrated similar effects, as ACIN1-NUTM1 also exhibited a B-lineage–committed leukemic phenotype resembling BRD9-NUTM1. This cross-fusion consistency strengthens our findings, which were validated by our CD34+ cord blood experiments. All 3 NUTM1 fusions (BRD9-NUTM1, AFF1-NUTM1, and ACIN1-NUTM1) promoted ELPs, and BRD9-NUTM1 and ACIN1-NUTM1 also enhanced CD10 expression, indicating more B-lymphoid progression, but AFF1-NUTM1 did not. Furthermore, AFF1-NUTM1 recipients did not develop leukemia within the experimental timeframe, even though iLS differentiation assays revealed capacity for B-lineage commitment based on transcriptional analysis. This discrepancy aligns with clinical data indicating that patients with AFF1-NUTM1 often harbor concurrent mutations, particularly in NRAS (supplemental Table 4), suggesting that additional genetic events may be requisite for AFF1-NUTM1–driven leukemogenesis. Nevertheless, comprehensive mutational profiling in expanded patient cohorts is warranted to define co-occurrence patterns and their functional significance. The transformed cells exhibit a B-lineage–committed (prepro–B-like) phenotype, further emphasizing the role of this fusion in aberrant differentiation and leukemic transformation. These experimental observations align with molecular evidence indicating that NUTM1-r leukemia originates from lineage-committed precursors rather than MPPs. Transcriptional profiling revealed that NUTM1-r cases exhibit significantly reduced Lymphoid-Primed Multipotent Progenitor signatures, accompanied by robust pre–B-cell signatures, in contrast to KMT2A-r cases (Figure 1D-E; supplemental Figure 1D). This is further supported by surface antigen profiles revealing low CD34 and positive CD10 (membrane metalloendopeptidase) expression (supplemental Table 2), characteristic of committed pre–B cells. Collectively, these findings suggest a distinction that helps explain the unique biology of NUTM1-r leukemia, including its cell of origin, generally favorable prognosis, and specific therapeutic sensitivities (supplemental Figure 2D-F).

Our findings highlight high cytarabine sensitivity of NUTM1-r leukemic stem cells, suggesting that BRD9-NUTM1 drives leukemogenesis without sustaining typical stem cell dormancy. The murine BRD9-NUTM1 model revealed enhanced RNA polymerase II programs while suppressing stemness-associated cell cycle regulators (Cdkn1a, Cdkn1c, and Cdkn2a) (supplemental Figure 5B).61, 62, 63 These active transcriptional states represent potential mechanisms underlying therapeutic vulnerability, explaining enhanced leukemic stem cell clearance and favorable clinical responses in NUTM1-r leukemias. To elucidate the molecular basis of these vulnerabilities, we highlighted LGALS1 and PHYHD1 as representative genes demonstrating inverse expression-methylation relationships in patients with NUTM1-r (Figure 1I-K; supplemental Figures 2A-C and 5F-G). LGALS1 (Galectin-1), linked to leukemic stem cell biology and relapse risk,64,65 demonstrated reduced expression concurrent with hypermethylation, which may contribute to therapeutic vulnerability. Conversely, PHYHD1, a 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase,66 displayed higher expression with lower methylation, potentially promoting leukemogenesis through altered α-ketoglutarate metabolism. These coordinated methylation and expression changes indicate NUTM1-driven reprogramming that activates B-lineage commitment while compromising the maintenance of leukemic stem cells. Clinically, the favorable outcomes found in NUTM1-r infant ALL support consideration of treatment de-escalation strategies similar to those being explored for other favorable-risk ALL subtypes (eg, ETV6-RUNX1, hyperdiploid).67 The demonstrated chemotherapy sensitivity supports consideration of reduced-intensity treatment approaches in this leukemia subset. Nevertheless, clinical heterogeneity, including high-risk NUTM1-r cases,33,34 necessitates precise risk stratification and comprehensive evaluation in larger patient cohort to refine therapeutic strategies for NUTM1-r leukemias.

Overall, our study provides comprehensive insights into the distinct molecular and biological features of BRD9-NUTM1 fusion in NUTM1-r leukemia. These findings not only enhance our understanding of NUTM1-r leukemia but also reveal potential therapeutic vulnerabilities that extend beyond hematologic malignancies. The distinct properties of BRD9-NUTM1, including its influence on chromatin accessibility and its modulation of leukemic stem cell characteristics, highlight promising targets for therapeutic intervention. Moreover, our research underscores the potential for improving patient outcomes by leveraging the chemosensitivity associated with this fusion, paving the way for novel treatment strategies.
