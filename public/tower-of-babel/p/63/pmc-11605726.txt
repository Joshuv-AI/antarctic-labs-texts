Differences in linear and angular measurement of the lower facial third in lateral cephalometry and lateral photometry: A comparative study
Authors: Nani Sesqui, Mochamad Fahlevi Rizal, Margaretha Suharsini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn clinical orthodontic practice, to evaluate aesthetics in dentistry, lateral cephalometry and lateral photometry are used to measure and assess craniofacial morphology. In contrast to cephalometry, photometry has no radiation hazard and is less expensive. There has been a paradigm shift in contemporary orthodontics that now emphasizes soft tissue aesthetics, rather than depending entirely on hard tissue evaluation from lateral cephalometry. Therefore, the key question is whether lateral photometry can be a substitute for conventional cephalometry for diagnostics, prognostic analysis, and treatment planning.ObjectiveTo differentiate between lateral cephalometry and lateral photometry measurement in children with stage 2 and 3 vertebrae maturation in the Deutero Malay population.Materials and MethodsA standardized protocol was followed for all lateral cephalograms and photographs from 38 subjects who met the inclusion criteria. Three parameters focusing on the lower third craniofacial measurement were investigated: two linear parameters (mandibular ramus length and mandibular length) and one angular parameter (gonial angle). These parameters were measured using Webceph software. All parameters were digitized on both cephalograms and photographs and were compared using an independent sample t-test.ResultsWhen comparing the angular cephalometric and photometric variables, we found that the mandible length (Go-Me) and gonial angle (∠Go) in the cephalometry measurements had an insignificant difference compared to the photometric measurements, while the ramus length (Co-Go) in the cephalometry measurement had a significant difference compared to the photometric measurements.ConclusionAlthough we cannot rule out cephalometry as the primary record in orthodontics, lateral photometry assessment can be used as an alternative candidate for measuring mandibular body measurements and gonial angle in stage 2 and 3 cervical maturation in the Deutero Malay race.Keywords: Photometry, Cephalometry, Ramus height, Mandibular length, Gonial angle measurements

Introduction

Lateral cephalometry is a standard method that plays the biggest role in studies regarding change and development, determining skeletal and dental craniofacial morphology characteristics, treatment planning, monitoring treatment stages, and final treatment evaluation (Ferrario et al., 1993, Zhang et al., 2007). Lateral cephalometry captures three-dimensional craniofacial tissues in two-dimensional images. Therefore, even with the correct positional technique, it is quite difficult to produce a clear image that enables one to attain the correct measurements, which is why capturing more than one image from different angles is often necessary (Proffit, 2018). Lateral cephalometry has weaknesses, such as radiation and sensitivity, so it requires special equipment. Such equipment includes a head holder for accurate results and different protective gear to shield patients from radiation, especially children and teenagers (Stupar et al., 2018). This increases the risk of radiation, so it is necessary to look for other techniques that minimize this risk (Claus et al., 2012, Gomes et al., 2013, Khan et al., 2018). Lateral cephalometry is also expensive (Khan et al., 2018), so it would be very helpful if there were techniques with a lower cost and greater simplicity that could be used to assess craniofacial morphology (Devereux et al., 2011). There has been the development of a new paradigm for determining treatment outcomes: soft tissue evaluation. This paradigm states that the goals of modern orthodontic and orthognathic treatment are also based on the evaluation of facial soft tissues, not solely on the relationship between the teeth and jaw. This paradigm places greater emphasis on clinical facial evaluation than assessment using study models and radiographs (Proffit, 2018).

Constantly developing photometric techniques have made it possible to obtain the highest photo quality with minimal distortion (Gautam et al., 2019). One paper stated that lateral photometry can be used as a low-cost, noninvasive analysis alternative (Khan et al., 2018). The use of this technique is based on previous research, which states that the appearance of structures is related to the hard tissue morphology below them; therefore, the use of lateral photometry can be developed to assess craniofacial anatomical characteristics (Zhang et al., 2007). Lateral photometry has been used in adult and pediatric patients. Quantitative analysis of soft tissue profiles is widely used to measure the influence of various classes of malocclusion, and it is also used to measure the influence of orthodontic treatment on aesthetics (Lai et al., 2000).

The aim of this study is to differentiate the measurement between lateral cephalometry and lateral photometry in children with stage 2 and 3 vertebrae maturation in the Deutero Malay population.

Materials and methods

Subject selection

The sample of this comparative analytic study with a cross-sectional design was obtained through purposive sampling with a minimum sample size of 34, with the following criteria: children of the Deutero Malay race with stage 2 or 3 cervical vertebra maturation, no congenital anomalies, moderate BMI, and an angle class I first molar relationship who were indicated to require interceptive orthodontic treatment. Those excluded had facial asymmetry and had experienced craniofacial trauma.

The minimum research sample size required was 34 people, which was calculated using the g*power application to test paired data. The power was chosen to be 0.9, the significance level to be 0.05, and the effect size to be 0.8. The study subjects were 38 children who needed orthodontic treatment. They were selected according to the inclusion criteria, and their parents were asked to provide informed consent regarding their willingness to participate in the study.

Methods

The subjects who were selected had lateral cephalometric radiographs taken in the natural head position (NHP) under conditions of centric occlusion and relaxed lips. On the same day as the lateral cephalometry, they were directed to have lateral photometry taken using a Canon D90 Kit DSLR camera positioned at the subject’s head level at a distance of 50 cm, with the face, ears, and neck clearly visible, hair tucked behind the ears, and accessories such as glasses and jewelry temporarily removed. The upper and lower teeth were in a state of centric occlusion, the lips were relaxed, and a marker was placed on the face in the form of surgical tape after landmarks had been identified through palpation. This tape was attached to the tragus (Tr) and gonion (Go) landmarks. Pink 0.5 mm Styrofoam was attached with the help of double-sided tape to the menton (Me) landmark (Fig. 1).

Positioning of a subject’s photometry acquisition. Tr: tragus, Go: gonion.

For each patient, dental floss was used to mark a line from the tragus to the orbitale that represented the frankfort horizontal plane, indicating the achievement of an NHP. The patient stood with an NHP in front of a 30 × 90 cm mirror. A vertical scale in the form of a stadiometer (Seca) was placed along the midsagittal plane of the face to facilitate measurement calibration.

The patient was instructed to move their head up and down by slowly reducing the amplitude until they felt relaxed. This position was then confirmed through a grid visible on the camera. Photographs were taken three times, and the best one was used. The criteria for the best image was that it was shadow free and had all parts of the face clearly visible, with the eyes straight ahead and the dental floss aligned with the grid on the camera.

After obtaining the photographic results from both methods, measurements were taken using the Webceph application. Lateral cephalometry and photometry were arranged in such a way as to make the same comparison through the superimpose technique found in the application. After superimposing, the reference points were determined in the cephalometry application (Co, Tr', Go, Go', Me, Me'), as shown in Fig. 2. In cephalometry, the ramus length was measured, with the mandibular ramus length measured from Co (condylon) to Go; the mandibular length measured from Go to Me; and the gonion angle formed from Tr (tragion)–Go–Me in photometry. In photometry, measurements were taken from the length of the mandibular ramus measured from Tr' to Go' in photometry; the length of the mandible measured from Go' to Me' in cephalometry; and the angle of the mandible formed from Tr'–Go'–Me'. The data were then analyzed using SPSS 20.

Results

The research subjects in this study are described in Section 2.1. An interoperator reliability test was carried out using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) statistical test. The operators in this study were two pediatric dentistry residents who measured the X-rays and photometry. The test results showed the ICC values to be 1.0 (excellent) for the Co–Go distance measurement, 0.97 (excellent) for Tr'–Go', 1.0 (excellent) for the Go–Me distance measurement, 0.98 (excellent) for Tr'–Go', 0.99 (excellent) for the Go angle, and 0.95 (excellent) for the Go' angle. In conclusion, the interoperator reliability in the measurement of the Co–Go distance, Go–Me distance, and Go angle using cephalometric and photometric methods had very good agreement.

A descriptive analysis of lateral cephalometric radiographs and photometry measurements showed the results of all measurements with lateral cephalometric radiography and photometry. The mean Co–Go distance was 48.52 mm, with a standard deviation of 3.57. Normality testing using the Shapiro–Wilk test showed a p-value of 0.911 (normally distributed) (i.e., p > 0.05). The Tr'–Go' distance had a mean of 50.59, with a standard deviation of 3.72. Normality testing using the Shapiro–Wilk test showed a p-value of 0.501 (normally distributed) (i.e., p > 0.05).

For the Go–Me distance, the mean value was 56.95 mm, with a standard deviation of 5.79. Normality testing using the Shapiro–Wilk test showed a p-value of 0.249 (normally distributed) (i.e., p > 0.05). The Go'–Me' distance had a mean of 59.88 mm, with a standard deviation of 7.48. Normality testing using the Shapiro–Wilk test showed a p-value of 0.432 (normally distributed) (i.e., p > 0.05).

For ∠Go, the mean value was 124.32°, with a standard deviation of 4.92. Normality testing with the Shapiro–Wilk test showed a p-value of 0.145 (normally distributed) (i.e., p > 0.05), while ∠Go' had a mean of 124.76°, with a standard deviation of 4.52. Normality testing with the Shapiro–Wilk test showed a p-value of 0.655 (normally distributed) (i.e., p > 0.05). The distribution of data for Co–Go, Tr'–Go', Go–Me, Go'–Me' distance measurements, as well as ∠Go and ∠Go', showed a normal distribution, so further statistical analysis using an independent t-test was conducted.

The measurements in Table 1 show mean difference tests on measurements of Co–Go distance, Go–Me distance, and Go angle with lateral cephalometry and photometry. The independent t-test conducted on Co–Go showed a p-value of 0.016, which means there was a significant difference between cephalometric radiographs and photometry in the Co–Go distance. The independent t-test conducted at the Go–Me distance showed that there was no significant difference between cephalometric radiographs and photometry, with a p-value of 0.060. For the Go angle, the independent sample t-test showed a p-value of 0.681, which means there was a nonsignificant difference between cephalometric radiography and photometry.

Discussion

Changes in the field of orthodontics have shifted the old paradigm, which prioritizes the ideal relationship of hard tissue to achieve an ideal craniofacial morphology, and lateral photometry is expected to provide another option. With the new paradigm of orthodontic treatment, the evaluation of soft tissue structures is currently carried out first in determining a treatment plan and can be done using lateral photometry (Banerjee et al., 2019, Proffit, 2018). The use of photometry that follows the standardization of lateral photometry techniques has several advantages, one of which is the absence of pressure applied to the surface of the face, which can cause errors. In addition, the time taken for a photograph is quite short, and photometric images facilitate the illustration process compared to the use of cephalometry. The advantage of photometry is that measurements can be repeated to obtain longitudinal data (Mehta et al., 2017).

Although there is a standard for taking lateral photometry, there are still potential errors due to the distance from the lens to the subject, which can affect measurements, especially linear measurements. Measurement errors can also be caused by head posture errors. Setting the head position can be done in various ways, one of which is using the NHP, which is a standard position that can be achieved by asking the subject to stand in an upright position with their eyes looking at an image parallel to them in a mirror reflection. Determination of the NHP is very important because even the slightest deviation of the NHP will significantly affect the position of the landmark and affect the measurement results (Mehta et al., 2017). In this study, an NHP was carried out by the attachment of dental floss, which was then aligned to the horizontal grid position on the camera.

The results of this study showed that the mean differences between the Go and the G' angle (p = 0.681) and Go–Me and Go'–Me' (p = 0.060) were statistically nonsignificant, while Co–Go and Tr'–Go' (p = 0.016) showed a significant difference.

The results of this study are in accordance with previous studies that have proven that the gonion angle in photometry is not significantly different from the gonion angle in cephalometry (Gupta et al., 2022). This proves that the gonion angle in soft tissue can be used as an alternative to measuring the gonial angle in children through photometric measurements.

Taking two-dimensional photographs of craniofacial structures that are not on the mid-sagittal line causes distortion, which, in turn, can affect growth evaluation. This can have an impact on the measurement of the mandibular ramus length represented by the Co–Go point. In this study, the distance between Co–Go and Tr'–Go' had a significant difference (p < 0.016), which is different from previous research that states that there is no significant difference between the two. This can occur because lateral cephalometry is produced from flattening 3D objects, which are then projected and distorted at each point in proportion, influenced by the distance from the midsagittal (Maspero et al., 2020). The length of the ramus in the cephalometry measured on Co–Go can be distorted. The large difference between measurements can be caused by the thickness of the soft tissue covering the hard tissue landmarks, causing imprecise landmark marking (Aksu et al., 2010).

The measurement of mandibular length in this study was carried out by measuring from the Go point to the Me point. The measurement results showed that there was no significant difference between the measurement of mandibular length in cephalometry and mandibular length in photometry, with a p-value of 0.060. This is also different from a previous study conducted on adult patients in India, which showed significant differences between the two measurement methods (Mehta et al., 2017). Craniofacial soft tissue analysis in children is different from adult patients because growth and development are still ongoing, and differences in dimensions and sizes can cause distortion in morphological estimation. The configuration of facial soft tissue is highly variable and is not always easily described by a specific formula. Although some parts of the soft tissue represent the underlying hard tissue, some soft tissue structures are strongly influenced by the length, thickness, and functional aspects of the soft tissue, such as the strength of the tissue pull, so that the position of the soft tissue is very independent (Kasai, 1998). This aligns with research that states that 50 % of the variability of soft tissue is related to the underlying hard tissue and that the remaining 50 % depends on the variation in the soft tissue itself (Halazonetis, 2007).

Conclusion

In the comparison of linear measurements between cephalometry and photometry, the mandibular length in soft tissue and gonial angle can be used as an alternative in measuring the mandible in children, where there is no statistically significant difference between cephalometry and photometry. Linear measurement of the length of the ramus produced significant differences, so it cannot be used as an alternative to cephalometric measurements. This may have happened because large differences between measurements can be caused by the thickness of the soft tissue covering the hard tissue landmarks, causing imprecise landmark marking. However, soft tissue variability is not only related to the underlying hard tissue but also depends on variations in the soft tissue itself.

Photometry cannot yet be fully utilized for everything. The use of cephalometry is still required to provide important data in the field of orthodontics. Lateral photometry can be used to supplement data needs in large-scale epidemiological studies when no cephalometric method is available.

This research could be the first step in using photometry as an alternative facial measurement to replace cephalometry in Deutero Malay children. However, it was carried out on children who had malocclusion and needed orthodontic treatment, so it cannot be used as a standard for assessing measurements in Deutero Malay children. Therefore, more research needs to be conducted in the future.
