Spatial and Temporal Trends of Ambient PM2.5 and Source-Apportioned Black Carbon in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Kyan K. Shlipak, Tesfaye Mamo, L. Drew Hill, Asmamaw Abera, Jeff Blair, Araya Asfaw, David J. Diner, Sina Hasheminassab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ambient fine particulate matter pollution (PM2.5) is the leading global environmental risk factor for human health. This study leverages three years (April 2022-March 2025) of continuous, in situ ambient PM2.5 and black carbon (BC) measurements from the Multi-Angle Imager for Aerosols (MAIA) investigation’s surface monitoring network to characterize particulate air pollution in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Continuous PM2.5 data were collected from 10 PurpleAir sensors deployed across Addis Ababa and calibrated using collocated Beta Attenuation Monitors at two contrasting U.S. Embassy sites (Central and Jacros). Continuous BC data were collected with AethLabs MA350 microAethalometers operating at these two sites. During the study period, the average PM2.5 concentration across all 10 sites was 30 μg/m3, and the average BC concentrations were 3.7 μg/m3 and 7.7 μg/m3 at Central and Jacros, respectively. Both PM2.5 and BC concentrations exhibited strong diurnal and seasonal variation, with peaks during morning traffic and nighttime inversion, especially during the wet season. PM2.5 levels were highest at sites west of the city center and exceeded the WHO guideline for daily PM2.5 of 15 μg/m3 on 99% of days. The Aethalometer Model for source apportionment estimated that, on average, 94% of total BC in Addis Ababa was attributable to fossil fuel combustion, while 6% was associated with biomass burning. Directional and trajectory analyses indicate distinct local and regional sources of biomass and fossil fuel BC, including potential long-range transport of shipping emissions from the Gulf of Aden. This study presents, to our knowledge, the first long-term, multisite characterization of continuous PM2.5 and source-apportioned BC in Ethiopia. Findings from this study establish a pollution baseline for this MAIA target area ahead of the satellite’s launch, improving understanding of particulate pollution levels and sources and supporting future assessment of air quality trends in this region.

Introduction

Air pollution is a significant global hazard, posing major public health and environmental risks while contributing to millions of premature deaths each year. In particular, exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) has been extensively and robustly linked to adverse health outcomes, including respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, as well as increased mortality. The 2025 State of Global Air Report estimates that, globally, ∼4.9 million annual deaths are attributable to ambient PM2.5 exposuremore than any other category of pollution. Furthermore, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that ∼91% of deaths from exposure to outdoor air pollution occur in low and middle-income countries. Among the various components of PM2.5, black carbon (BC) has been consistently linked to adverse health effects, and is also estimated to be elevated in the Global South.

The African continent faces disproportionately high levels of air pollution. Here, over one million annual deaths are associated with air pollution, surpassing HIV, Tuberculosis (TB), and malaria as the leading environmental cause of death. , Due to increasing populations, fuel consumption, and motor vehicle usage, ambient air pollution exposures are projected to increase through the 21st century. , However, efforts to conduct the requisite air quality assessments to develop air pollution mitigation plans in African cities have thus far been largely limited to small-scale measurements, primarily using ground-based in situ observations.

Satellite remote sensing offers a powerful way to help address these gaps by providing broad, spatially continuous observations of atmospheric aerosols that complement and extend the limited coverage of ground-based monitoring networks. The Multi-Angle Imager for Aerosols (MAIA) investigation, jointly implemented by NASA and the Italian Space Agency, aims to assess the health effects of exposure to different types of ambient particulate matter (aerosol mixtures containing particles of different sizes, shapes, and chemical compositions). MAIA focuses on 12 Primary Target Areas (PTA) worldwide, where satellite observations will be integrated with surface measurements and chemical transport model (CTM) outputs within a geostatistical framework to produce 1 km resolution maps of ground-level PM2.5, PM10, and speciated PM2.5 components (including sulfate, nitrate, organic carbon, elemental carbon, and dust). Each PTA spans approximately 360 × 480 km, and there are two PTAs in Africa: one centered over Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, and one over Johannesburg, South Africa.

Due to the absence of adequate surface monitoring resources in the Addis Ababa PTA, the MAIA project has deployed a diverse network of ground-based instruments, including low-cost continuous PM sensors, microAethalometers, and filter-based samplers for PM2.5 chemical characterization. Together, these measurements enable a prelaunch assessment of the spatial and temporal variability of ambient PM2.5, as well as its composition and dominant sources across the region.

Previous studies have used in situ measurements and chemical transport models to assess air quality in Addis Ababa. Tefera et al. (2020) conducted filter tape measurements of PM2.5 mass and composition between 2015 and 2016, measuring annual-average PM2.5 mass concentrations of 53.8 μg/m3 and finding the dominant chemical component to be organic matter (44.5%). Kumie et al. (2021) used continuous PM2.5 measurements from three BAM-1020 monitors between 2017–2020, finding annual-average concentrations of 42.4 μg/m3, 34.7 μg/m3, and 24.2 μg/m3 at the three sites. Guttikunda et al. (2025) developed an emissions inventory and used the Weather Research Forecasting Comprehensive Air Quality Model with Extensions model (WRF-CAMx) CTM to model PM2.5 source contributions across Addis Ababa. They found the transport, residential, and industrial sectors to contribute 81% of total primary PM2.5 in the greater Addis Ababa airshed and modeled PM2.5 concentrations to be highest in the Nifas Silk-Lafto district, slightly south and west of the city center. Ararsa et al. (2025) used AeroQual Series 500 instruments to measure PM and gaseous pollutants at 66 sites across the Akaki Kality Sub-City, Addis Ababa for two months during the 2024 wet and dry seasons, reporting average PM2.5 concentrations of 60.4 ± 100 μg/m3 and 46.6 ± 7.6 μg/m3 for the dry and wet seasons, respectively. Getachew et al. (2025) used two BAM PM2.5 monitors and the BenMAP-CE model to estimate the health and economic impacts of PM2.5 in Addis Ababa, attributing ∼1466 premature deaths in 2019 to exposures to an average annual PM2.5 concentration of 32.8 μg/m3.

Most existing in situ studies in Addis Ababa have been limited to short-term deployments of PM2.5 sensors and have typically not conducted continuous, real-time measurements. This study seeks to address this gap by leveraging the first long-term, continuous, multisite measurements of PM2.5 and source-apportioned BC in Addis Ababa to identify spatial and temporal trends, high pollution events, and directional patterns in pollution transport and source influence.

Methods

This study integrates multiyear ground-based air quality measurements, meteorological observations, and trajectory analyses to characterize the spatial and temporal variability of PM2.5 and BC across Addis Ababa. A network of low-cost and research-grade instruments were deployed at ten sites representing diverse land-use types, with data subjected to standardized quality control, calibration, and source-apportionment procedures. These measurements were combined with reanalysis meteorology and statistical spatial analyses to assess pollution patterns, source influences, and intersite heterogeneity.

Monitoring Sites

Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, lies ∼1000 km north of the equator and has a subtropical highland climate at an elevation of 2355 m (7726 ft) above sea level. Addis Ababa covers an area of 527 km2 and has an estimated population of 5.96 million people, a 4.4% increase from 2024, making it one of the densest and fastest growing urban centers in Africa.

The ten sampling sites were in a variety of commercial, residential, school, industrial, and green spaces (S.I. Table S1), providing broad spatial coverage and capturing some of the variability in Addis Ababa’s local emissions and activities (Figure ). Sites were also selected for consistent wall power, stable Internet access, ease of maintenance, and security.

Meteorology

To describe the local meteorology in Addis Ababa, this study uses ground observation data from a weather station at the Addis Ababa International Airport, which is run by the Ethiopian National Meteorology Agency (NMA) and was accessed via Meteostat. The fifth-generation atmospheric reanalysis from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ERA5) was employed to provide planetary boundary layer height (BLH) data for Addis Ababa.

May was the warmest month in our study period, with an average temperature of 19.0 °C, while December was the coolest, averaging 16.2 °C (S.I. Figures S1 and S2). During the study period, typical diurnal temperatures ranged between 10 and 25 °C though temperatures can drop to below 5 °C during nights in January and February (S.I. Figure S1). December was the driest month, receiving just 3.2 mm of rain during the three-year study duration, while July was the wettest, receiving 986.1 total millimeters of rainfall (S.I. Figure S3).

The NMA uses three season definitions to describe precipitation: Bega (October-January), the driest season; Belg (February-May), a transitional season with moderate precipitation; and Kiremt (June-September), the main rainy season with the highest amount of precipitation. During Kiremt, Addis Ababa sees westerly winds, whereas the city has predominantly easterly winds during the rest of the year (S.I. Figure S4). Wind speeds and planetary BLHs also decrease quite notably during the wet Kiremt season, and the dip in BLH shifts toward earlier in the afternoon (S.I. Figures S1 and S5).

Instrumentation and Study Period

The MAIA investigation installed PurpleAir sensors at ten sites across Addis Ababa (Figure ). Two of these sensors are colocated with Beta Attenuation Monitors (BAM; Federal Equivalent Method, FEM) at the U.S. Embassy (Central) and U.S. Embassy warehouse (Jacros) sites (Figure ). At these two sites, AethLabs microAethalometers (MA350) have also been deployed to measure BC. In addition, the MAIA investigation installed and continues to operate filter-based samplers at four sites across Addis Ababa and its vicinity (not shown here) to collect time-integrated PM2.5 samples for subsequent chemical analysis using X-ray fluorescence (XRF), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and ion chromatography (IC). These measurements are ongoing and are expected to continue through the end of the MAIA investigation. However, this study focuses on the first three years of continuous PurpleAir and microAethalometer measurements, which occurred from April 1, 2022, to March 31, 2025.

PurpleAir

The PurpleAir sensors (PA-II-SD model) used in this study contain two PMS5003 sensors (Plantower, Beijing, China), which measure the scattering of light at a red wavelength (∼680 nm) to estimate the mass concentration of PM2.5. , They are also equipped with a pressure, temperature, and humidity sensor (BME280).

The PurpleAir sensor is designed to measure PM2.5 concentrations within a range of 0 to 500 μg/m3 and can operate under ambient temperatures from −10 to 60 °C with relative humidity levels spanning 0 to 99%. Hourly data were downloaded from the PurpleAir API for each site. Additionally, due to periodic Wi-Fi outages at each site, data derived from the API was supplemented by data manually downloaded from the local storage on each device.

Beta Attenuation Monitor (BAM)

The MetOne Beta Attenuation Monitor 1020 (BAM) is a widely used monitor of hourly PM2.5 concentrations that has been designated by the U.S. EPA as a Federal Equivalent Method (FEM). The BAM operates by measuring the difference in the attenuation of a beta ray with a particle deposit on a glass fiber filter tape over time. BAMs were located at the Central and Jacros sites as part of the U.S. Department of State air quality monitoring program (DOSAir). These sites used FEM-designated instrumentation and established operating procedures intended to maintain regulatory-quality hourly PM2.5 measurements. Routine operation includes periodic flow checks, filter tape replacement, instrument diagnostics, and scheduled maintenance. ,, For this analysis, BAM data were further screened as described in Section Data were downloaded from the AirNow API for the duration of the 3-year study period.

microAethalometer

The AethLabs microAethalometer model 350 (MA350) is a research-grade aethalometer commonly used for short- and long-term BC measurements. MA350 data were collected continuously from April 1, 2022, to March 31, 2025, at a 60 s resolution and resampled to an hourly resolution. MA350s measure light transmission through a particle deposit on a filter-tape at five wavelengths from the ultraviolet (375 nm) to near-infrared (880 nm) to determine BC mass concentrations, and have been robustly evaluated in intercomparison studies. − Because the performance of filter-photometers has been shown to decrease under significant changes in temperature or relative humidity, − MA350s used in this study were equipped with custom air conditioned enclosures, which included a Wi-Fi-enabled system to transfer real-time data to a server. The MA350 units were operated without size-selective inlets, and the reported values should be interpreted as ambient BC in the sampled aerosol stream rather than as size-fractionated BC. MA350s are also capable of extended battery-powered operation, well-suited for Addis Ababa, where frequent power outages during Kiremt pose a monitor deployment challenge.

MA350s use the DualSpot algorithm to correct for the loading effect, in which apparent absorption decreases as the particle loading increases. Furthermore, using measurements taken at the near-infrared and blue (470 nm) or ultraviolet wavelengths, BC mass concentrations can be apportioned to fossil fuel or biomass sources using the Aethalometer Model for source apportionment (S.I. Section 4). This source apportionment model relies upon an assignment of an absorption Ångström Exponent (AAE), which describes how aerosol light absorption varies with wavelength, for fossil fuel and biomass sources (S.I. Figure S6). Here, an AAE of 1.0 was used for fossil fuel sources and 2.0 for biomass sources, consistent with commonly applied values in the Aethalometer Model, especially in the absence of region-specific measurements constraining AAEs. − However, laboratory studies have found AAEs may vary from 0.6 to 1.3 for fossil fuel sources , and 1.0 to 5.0 for residential biomass combustion. ,

In addition to the two sites in Addis Ababa (Central and Jacros), the MAIA project installed MA350 microAethalometers in Beijing, China; Pasadena, California; and New Delhi, India. Measurements in Beijing were discontinued in November 2024 following its redesignation as a MAIA Secondary Target Area (STA). BC measurements from the New Delhi, Pasadena, and Beijing sites were included for comparison with those in Addis Ababa and other MAIA target areas, as discussed in Section . Data from MA350 units were downloaded from the AethLabs API and supplemented by data manually downloaded from the local storage on each device.

Data Processing and Quality Control

PurpleAir

Data cleaning and processing followed established protocols described in the literature. , The data were filtered for hourly temperature measurements between −17.8 and 48.9 °C (0 °F–120 °F), relative humidity measurements between 0% and 100%, and raw PM2.5 measurements (cf_1) between 0 and 500 μg/m3. Data were also filtered for PM2.5 mismatch errors, defined as instances where the difference between the two raw PMS5003 PM2.5 readings (i.e., channels a and b) exceeded both 5 μg/m3 and 70% of their mean value. Additionally, some devices exhibited a behavior in which relative humidity measurements would remain at 100% for extended periods. For this reason, data were also filtered for periods with over 12 h of continuous 100% relative humidity measurements.

Data completion rates after data processing and quality control, defined as the total number of hours with measurements divided by the total number of hours of sensor deployment, ranged from 58.1% at the AAEPGDC site to 93.7% at the Central site (S.I. Table S2). Across the 10 sites, the average data completion rate was 75.9%. For further details on the data cleaning and processing, see the Supporting Information (Section ; S.I. Table S3).

Beta Attenuation Monitor

BAMs data were filtered for raw concentration values reported equal to −999 and 985, which correspond to error status codes, for negative raw concentration values, and for reported maintenance periods (S.I. Table S4). The BAMs at the Central and Jacros sites had data retrieval rates of 68.1% and 87.6% during the study period, respectively (S.I. Table S2).

microAethalometer

MicroAethalometer data were cleaned by removing status errors (S.I. Table S5), flow deviations from the set point, irregular flow ratios, rapid temperature change, device leaks, irregular loading compensation, and periods of enhanced noise (S.I. Table S6). The Central and Jacros sites had 93.9% and 93.9% data completion rates, respectively (S.I. Table S2). See the Supporting Information (Section 2) for a more detailed description of data cleaning.

BC contributions from biomass burning and fossil fuel combustion were quantified using the Aethalometer source apportionment model. Source apportionment estimates were produced in postprocessing using an approach designed to mimic the now-integrated microAeth firmware approach. A detailed description of the model and its uncertainty is provided in the Supporting Information (Sections 3 and 4).

PurpleAir Calibration

Extensive prior studies have reported that while PurpleAir sensors generally correlate well with reference instruments, their measurements can be systematically biased due to meteorological influences and variations in aerosol composition. Therefore, a local empirical calibration for the PurpleAir sensors was derived using measurements from two PurpleAir units colocated with BAM monitors at the Jacros and Central sites. A multiple linear regression model (MLR) was trained using cleaned hourly raw PM2.5 (calculated as the mean of pm_cf_1 from channels a and b), along with corresponding temperature and humidity measurements. Data from both colocated sites were combined to develop a single calibration model, which was then applied to all PurpleAir monitoring sites across Addis Ababa.

To reduce the impact of outliers on the training of the MLR model, an initial MLR model was first trained using all data points, then high-influence points having a Cook’s Distance greater than 50/n were omitted, where n is the total number of data points, leading to the removal of 111 of 31,409 total data points. Spatial Trend Assessment

Pollution Directionality

The OpenAir R package was used with wind data obtained from the Addis Ababa International Airport to assess the directionality of source-apportioned BC at the Jacros site. This analysis was only conducted at the Jacros site, not the Central site, due to the former’s proximity to the airport meteorological station. The PolarPlot function was used to construct bivariate polar plots showing average pollutant concentration by wind speed and direction.

Back trajectories were computed using the Hybrid Single-Particle Lagrangian Integrated Trajectory (HYSPLIT) model developed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. HYSPLIT combines Lagrangian air-mass tracking with an Eulerian gridded framework to simulate trajectories driven by advection and dispersion processes, including their backward-time consistency. , Back trajectories were calculated for a fixed receptor location (Addis Ababa) at a receptor height of 10 m above ground level. For each day during our study period, 96 h backward trajectories were initialized 4 times per day. The HYSPLIT model was run using the National Center for Environmental Prediction (NCEP)/National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR) Reanalysis data set, a global, three-dimensional atmospheric data set (2.5° × 2.5° horizontal resolution, 6 h temporal resolution).

The Potential Source Contribution Function (PSCF) approach was applied to identify likely upwind source regions by integrating HYSPLIT back-trajectory simulations with pollutant measurements at the Jacros and Central sites. − The PSCF produces a probability field describing the source strength of a grid cell, which can be interpreted as the probability of air parcels originating from that grid cell exceeding a certain pollution threshold, defined in this study as the 75th percentile.

Coefficient of Divergence

Following the methods of Daher et al. (2013), the spatial variation of PM2.5 across Addis Ababa was determined using the Coefficient of Divergence (COD). CODs were calculated between the temporally aligned, hourly mass concentrations measured at paired PurpleAir sampling sites (eq ). CODs provide information on the degree of uniformity of the pollution measured between pairs of sites. A value of 0 indicates that concentrations are identical at both sites and a COD of 1 implies that concentrations are highly disparate over time. Generally, a COD below 0.2 suggests two sites are homogeneous, and a COD > 0.2 suggests two sites have substantial heterogeneity. PurpleAir Calibration

As shown in S.I. Figure S7a, raw PurpleAir PM2.5 measurements were strongly correlated with collocated BAM observations (R 2 ∼ 0.75) but exhibited notable humidity-dependent bias, tending to overestimate PM2.5 at higher humidity and underestimate it at lower humidity.

To account for this effect, hourly PurpleAir PM2.5 data were calibrated to BAM PM2.5 data at the Central and Jacros sites with the first order MLR model shown in eq , trained on pooled data from both sites, with raw PurpleAir PM2.5, relative humidity, and temperature data as predictors (Table ). The temperature and relative humidity were statistically significant predictors of BAM PM2.5 when combined with PurpleAir PM2.5 measurements (P < 0.001). Applying the MLR calibration substantially reduced the humidity-related bias in PurpleAir PM2.5 measurements, decreasing the RMSE by 47% compared to the raw data (S.I. Figure S7b).

Coefficient of Divergence

The COD was used to assess intersite variations in PM2.5 levels. The overall average pairwise COD across all sites was 0.23. The Black Lion Hospital, AASTU, ALERT Hospital, and Reppie Waste sites had the highest average COD at 0.25, and the ECA site had the lowest average COD at 0.20 (S.I. Figure S9). Notably, each site had an average pairwise COD above the cited threshold of 0.2, indicating substantial heterogeneities in PM2.5 trends at the 10 different sites across Addis Ababa. The Black Lion Hospital and AASTU sites had the highest pairwise COD of 0.34, and the Jacros and AAEPGDC sites had the lowest pairwise COD at 0.14 (S.I. Table S9).

Pollution levels at sites can be assumed to be driven by meteorology and emissions patterns. The AASTU site is geographically the farthest site from the others, which likely contributes to the higher COD. In contrast, the two hospital sites and the Reppie Waste site are located near other PurpleAir sampling sites (Figure ). The high average CODs at these three sites may be attributed to different drivers of high and lower PM periods: hospitals and waste sites may experience irregular emissions peaks compared to typical, background evening and morning traffic emissions and daytime industrial emissions.

Temporal Trends of Ambient PM2.5

During the study period, daily averaged PM2.5 concentrations across all PurpleAir sites exceeded the WHO 24 h guideline of 15 μg/m3 on 98.7% of days (Figure ), which is intended to be the 99th percentile of daily concentrations. Annual average concentrations surpassed the WHO annual guideline of 5 μg/m3 by at least a factor of 4 each year at each site.

Daily averaged calibrated PM2.5 mass concentrations across all PurpleAir sites during the study period. The dashed red line represents the World Health Organization’s daily PM2.5 guideline.

The WHO also provides interim targets to facilitate air pollution reduction efforts in countries that substantially exceed guideline levels. During each year of the three-year study period, annual average concentrations across PurpleAir sites met interim target 1 (35 μg/m3) but exceeded interim target 2 (25 μg/m3). Daily average PM2.5 concentrations exceeded the interim target 1 of 75 μg/m3 on only 5 days, corresponding to 0.45% of study days, and exceeded the interim target 2 of 50 μg/m3 on 44 days, corresponding to 4% of study days.

Monthly Diurnal Trends of Ambient PM2.5

PM2.5 exhibits strong diurnal and seasonal variability in Addis Ababa. Average diurnal PM2.5 trends show a distinct morning peak that appears consistently across all months, likely driven by increased emissions during morning rush hours, combined with a low BLH that limits the vertical dispersion of pollution (Figure ; S.I. Figure S10). PM2.5 levels then decrease during the middle of the day (9 am −3 pm), as the BLH and wind speed rise (S.I. Figure S1), leading to vertical and horizontal dispersion of pollution. A secondary peak occurs at around 7 pm, growing steadily from February and reaching its highest levels in September. During Kiremt (June–September), this evening peak exceeds the morning peak in magnitude, driving average PM2.5 levels to be substantially higher during this period than during the rest of the year (Figure ). Average monthly PM2.5 levels across all sites peaked in September at 40.02 μg/m3, excluding the Meskel holiday, and were the lowest during February at 24.52 μg/m3. Beyond meteorological drivers, emissions from evening cooking, biomass burning, and power outage-related fuel combustion likely contribute to the persistence of the evening peak across Kiremt months, as discussed further in Section .

Diurnal trends were similar across all sites except Skylight Hotel, which exhibited a clear visual trend of relatively lower PM2.5 concentrations during the morning and evening peaks but higher concentrations mid-day (S.I. Figure S11). Thus, the Skylight Hotel site, which is located near Addis Ababa International Airport has a higher median and lower standard deviation than sites with comparable average PM2.5 levels (Table ).

Diurnal PM2.5 levels were comparable for each day of the week from Monday through Saturday; however, Sunday showed a similar morning peak but significantly lower PM2.5 concentrations during the evening, especially during Kiremt (S.I. Figures S12–S14). Much of Addis Ababa operates on a six-day work week, likely contributing to the lack of evening peak seen in the diurnal profile on Sunday, especially during the wet season, when the lower BLH amplifies the response of ambient concentrations to changes in emissions.

Spatial and Temporal Trends of Ambient BC in Addis Ababa

Summary Statistics

Mean BC concentrations measured were 7.67 μg/m3 at Jacros and 3.73 μg/m3 at the Central site, with maximum measured hourly concentrations of 113.50 μg/m3 and 56.63 μg/m3, respectively. BC levels at Jacros were not only higher, but also more variable, with a standard deviation of 7.27 μg/m3 compared to 4.00 μg/m3 at the Central site.

Monthly Diurnal Trends of Source-Apportioned Ambient BC

BC concentrations at both U.S. Embassy sites showed strong diurnal and seasonal variations (Figure ), closely resembling the previously discussed trends in PM2.5 (Figure ; S.I. Figure S15). At both the Central and Jacros sites, a large peak in BC is seen during the morning, followed by a second evening peak, especially during Kiremt. Fossil fuel source-apportioned BC (BCff) accounts for 94% of total BC, with diurnal profiles closely matching those of total BC. Biomass burning BC (BCbb) exhibited similar evening and morning spikes, especially at Jacros, but of a lower magnitude than BCff. Average total BC and BCff levels were highest during June and July at both sites (S.I. Figure S16), and lowest during October at Central and February at Jacros, whereas BCbb peaked in June and September at both sites.

Monthly diurnal average mass concentrations of total black carbon (BC), fossil fuel black carbon (BCff), and biomass burning black carbon (BCbb) at the Central and Jacros sites. Error bars indicate the 95% Confidence Interval. Hidar Sitaten and Meskel festival days were excluded to facilitate the assessment of long-term seasonal trends.

BC and BCff diurnal profiles were consistent by day-of-week from Monday through Saturday at both sites, but levels were substantially lower on Sunday. BCbb levels, on the other hand, were unaffected on the weekends and approximately uniform on each day of the week (S.I. Figures S17 and S18).

Interestingly, though BC and BCff profiles at the two sites follow similar diurnal and seasonal patterns, a “lag” is visible between them. The morning peak at Jacros occurs 1–2 h earlier in the day than at Central. In the evening, however, the peaks are more-or-less aligned. Because the sites are not positioned along a common upwind–downwind axis, this offset likely reflects differences in local emission timing and source proximity rather than aerosol transport between sites. Jacros is more strongly influenced by near-road traffic (and potentially early morning freight activity), which can produce an earlier and sharper morning maximum. In the evening, emissions and the transition to more stable conditions tend to occur on a more uniform citywide schedule, which can lead to more aligned peaks across sites. The lack of a “lag” in diurnal BCbb profiles between the two sites further indicates that it is caused by local biomass burning rather than traffic.

Episodic High-Pollution Events in Addis Ababa

Continuous, high-time-resolution measurements of PM2.5 and BC allowed for the identification and assessment of high-pollution episodes in Addis Ababa. During the study period, two major recurring episodic high pollution events were identified: the Meskel and Hidar Sitaten festivals. Meskel is an annual celebration occurring on the 17th of Meskerem in the Ethiopian calendar (September 26th or 27th in the Gregorian calendar). It is celebrated by the Ethiopian Orthodox Church to commemorate the fourth century discovery of the True Cross by Saint Helena, and includes the lighting of the Demera, large wooden bonfires, in public areas across the city. , Both the Jacros and Central sites see extreme BC signals above the average September diurnal profile during each year sampled (Figure ). Hourly BC concentrations surpassed 100 μg/m3 and 50 μg/m3 at Jacros and Central in 2024, with approximately 50% of the BC signal apportioned to BCbb. Consequently, the 2024 Meskel event yielded the highest hourly and daily average BCbb observed during the entire study period, followed by the 2023 Meskel event.

Total black carbon (BC), fossil fuel black carbon (BCff), and biomass burning black carbon (BCbb) concentrations at the Jacros and Central sites during the Meskel (September 26th or 27th) and Hidar Sitaten (November 21st or 22nd) festivals from 2022 to 2024. Concentrations are compared to monthly mean diurnal profiles with 95% confidence interval. The Meskel concentrations are shown from noon on Meskel to noon the next day because festivities typically take place in the late evening.

Hidar Sitaten is an annual holiday occurring on the 12th of Hidar in the Ethiopian calendar, (November 21st or November 22nd). Hidar Sitaten commemorates the deadly Spanish Flu in Ethiopia through the widespread burning of trash and refuse, but has been known to cause air quality hazards in Addis Ababa. During the 2022 and 2024 Hidar Sitaten festivals, peaks in hourly BC at Jacros and Central of approximately 70 and 30 μg/m3 were measured, respectively. Unlike Meskel, a smaller fraction of the total BC measured was apportioned to biomass burning, likely reflecting the change in pollution source from wood to garbage with a lower proportion of combustible organics. Additionally, 2023 BC concentrations do not significantly differ from typical November levels. Wind speeds, wind directions, BLHs, and precipitation were similar during the 2023 Hidar Sitaten to the 2022 and 2024 events. Because the open burning conducted on Hidar Sitaten is organized by government and nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), the reduction in air pollution in 2023 may reflect reduced organizational activity.

A clear signal is also visible in PM2.5 readings at Jacros and Central during Meskel and Hidar Sitaten. During each year from 2022 to 2024, hourly PM2.5 levels during Meskel surpassed 200 μg/m3 at both sites, including over 400 μg/m3 in 2024. The secondary formation and aging of the Meskel plume may have led to similar PM2.5 concentrations despite lower levels of primary emissions, as indicated by a lower BC fraction of total PM2.5. Despite the elevated BC concentrations at Jacros compared to Central, PM2.5 concentrations were similar at both sites on Meskel. PM2.5 levels were also substantially elevated on Hidar Sitaten, with peaks ranging from 150 to 400 μg/m3 between the two sites during 2022 and 2024. However, PM2.5 trends more closely match BC trends during Hidar Sitaten, possibly reflecting the enhanced spatial distribution of burning sites during Hidar Sitaten than during Meskel.

Source-Apportioned BC Directionality

Local sources of air pollution at the Jacros site were assessed using wind speed and direction data from the nearby Addis Ababa International Airport meteorological station to generate polar plots for both BCbb and BCff. The directionality of source-apportioned BC at the Jacros site was distinct for BCbb and BCff. The polar plot (Figure ) indicates that BCff predominantly originates from north and northwest of the sampling site. In contrast, BCbb shows elevated concentrations associated with easterly winds. The area north of and immediately surrounding Jacros is known to be highly industrial, whereas the areas west of Jacros are predominantly residential and commercial neighborhoods. The polar plots imply that the majority of BCbb is sourced from local residential biomass burning, rather than industrial burning, whereas industrial sites and main roads are likely the main source of BCff in the area.

HYSPLIT simulations indicate that air parcels reaching Addis Ababa during Bega predominantly originate from the east and northeast. These transport pathways are consistent with winter monsoon circulation, which drives air masses from northeast to southwest during this period. Consequently, most PSCF grid cells in Figure are located east of the city. Both BC and PM2.5 exhibit an eastward extension in their PSCF fields toward the Gulf of Aden. This signal is more pronounced at the Central site, whereas it is less distinct at the Jacros site, likely due to the stronger influence of local emissions at the more polluted location, which may mask regional transport signatures. The observed eastward PSCF enhancement suggests a potential contribution from sources associated with the Gulf of Aden, a major international shipping corridor. ,

The BCbb PSCF at the Central site shows comparatively stronger weighting toward the Gulf of Aden and weaker weighting from nearby source regions than the BC, BCff, and PM2.5 results. In this study, the Aethalometer source apportionment model attributes BC with an AAE of 2.0 to biomass burning. However, heavy fuel oils commonly used in maritime shipping have also been reported to exhibit AAEs near 2.0 (±0.1), suggesting the BCbb signal associated with the Gulf of Aden may reflect contributions from shipping emissions. Although Addis Ababa is located far inland and at an elevation exceeding 2000 m, similar indications of shipping-related contributions have been reported in recent source apportionment analyses of PM2.5 chemical composition for the region, further corroborating the potential impact of long-range transport of shipping emissions on BCbb in Addis Ababa. It should be noted that this attribution remains indicative rather than conclusive, as the PSCF analysis identifies source regions associated with elevated concentrations but does not directly identify emission sources. The shipping signal is therefore interpreted as a plausible contribution supported by transport patterns, AAE values consistent with heavy fuel oil combustion, and prior literature, rather than a definitive source attribution.

Comparison of BC Levels Across MAIA Target Areas

To contextualize the BC levels measured at our Addis Ababa sites, we compared the three-year average concentrations from this study with available measurements from other MAIA target areas. This comparison draws on published studies reporting ambient BC in those regions and, where available, incorporated MAIA’s own MA350 measurements (Pasadena, U.S.; Beijing, China; and New Delhi, India). It should be noted that most referenced measurements were collected at limited, centrally located sites within each target area and therefore may not represent spatially averaged BC levels across the entire region. In addition, although studies with relatively recent and year-long data sets were prioritized to capture seasonal variability, not all studies met these criteria. As such, this comparison provides a broad contextual overview rather than an exhaustive or systematic review.

Addis Ababa had the fifth highest average ambient BC concentration of the 34 target areas assessed, behind only Dhaka, New Delhi, Lagos, and Karachi (Figure ). Seven of the eight target areas with the highest measured BC levels are located in low or lower middle income nations, in line with recent studies. , Notably, Addis Ababa had higher BC levels than each site assessed in Europe and the Western Hemisphere (S.I. Figure S21), exemplifying the high degree of pollution in Addis Ababa, particularly compared to higher income countries.

Discussion

The three-year, multisite, hourly measurements of ambient PM2.5 and source-apportioned BC presented here provide valuable insight into the spatial and temporal patterns of particulate pollution in Addis Ababa and establish an important baseline in preparation for the MAIA satellite investigation.

This study finds that meteorology impacts both seasonal and diurnal pollution trends. During the day, the BLH heightens, increasing vertical convection and diluting ground-level pollution. Later, during the evening, the boundary layer decreases, reducing vertical convection and increasing ground-level PM2.5 (S.I. Figure S22). During Kiremt, the boundary layer is substantially lower during the day, with an average peak height of 1460 m, compared to heights of 2060 m during Belg and 1930 m during Bega. Wind speeds are also decreased, leading the average Kiremt ventilation coefficient, defined as the BLH multiplied by the wind speed, to be 51% lower than during the rest of the year, reducing the dilution of air pollutants. Meteorological impacts of pollution are also visible on a more granular scale. Subseasonal variations in BLH showed inverse relationships with pollution levels, where short-term periods with elevated BLHs coincide with decreases in pollution (S.I. Figure S23).

Emissions likely also contribute to both seasonal and diurnal trends. Traffic activity is highest in the morning and evening, aligning with the bimodal diurnal profile measured. Furthermore, the elevated precipitation during the wet season is known to cause power outages, leading to more localized biomass and fossil fuel burning activity, contributing to the Kiremt evening peaks.

To provide additional insight into the relative contribution of primary combustion emissions, this study assesses trends in the BC fraction of PM2.5. Because BC is only formed as a primary emission during the combustion of fuels, higher BC fractions of PM2.5 indicate emissions activity, rather than increases in ambient pollution driven by meteorology or secondary chemistry. BC comprised 24.2% of PM2.5 on average at Jacros and 17.4% at Central, highlighting Jacros’s closer proximity to pollution sources, and the BC/PM2.5 ratio peaked diurnally from 6 to 7 am at both sites during each month of the year (S.I. Figure S24). The BC/PM2.5 ratio was 7.5% lower on Sundays at Central and 16.2% lower at Jacros than the rest of the week (S.I. Figure S25), further indicating that emission related activities are lower on Sunday. The monthly average BC/PM2.5 ratio was highest at Central during Kiremt, peaking at 23.2% during July (S.I. Figure S26), suggesting elevated Kiremt pollution at the Central site to be due to a combination of increased emissions activity and changes in BLH height. However, the BC/PM2.5 ratio at Jacros was 25.8% during Bega and 25.9% during Kiremt (S.I. Figure S27), indicating the increased pollution during Kiremt (Figures and ) to be primarily driven by meteorology rather than emissions.

The BC/PM2.5 ratio of 24.2% found in this study at the Jacros site closely matches the filter-based average EC/PM2.5 ratio of 25.4% measured by Tefera et al. (2020) in central Addis Ababa from 2015 to 2016. Absolute PM2.5 concentrations were 30% lower in the present study: mean PM2.5 at Black Lion Hospital was 37.3 μg/m3, compared with 53.8 μg/m3 measured by Tefera et al. at a site approximately 300 m away. However, the Tefera et al. site was at the Arat Kilo campus and was closer to traffic than the Black Lion Hospital site of this study, likely contributing to the difference in measured PM2.5. Our measurements are consistent with those of Kumie et al. (2021), who conducted continuous PM2.5 monitoring from 2017–2020 at two comparable locations. At the Central site, mean PM2.5 was 23.1 μg/m3 in this study compared with 24.2 μg/m3 reported by Kumie et al., while at the ALERT Hospital–adjacent school site (∼1 km from our ALERT site), corresponding values were 34.8 and 34.7 μg/m3, respectively, suggesting pollution levels in Addis Ababa are not substantially decreasing.

The annual Meskel and Hidar Sitaten holidays produce severe air quality events, with hourly peaks exceeding 200 μg/m3 of PM2.5 and 30 μg/m3 of BC at the Jacros site (Figure ), and posing a short-term health hazard to the millions of residents in Addis Ababa. Targeted strategies to reduce the air quality impacts of these events could help mitigate exposures. Furthermore, the identification of these events is just one of many applications of the ground-monitoring data from the MAIA investigation. Near real-time hourly and subhourly measurements of PM components enable the identification of short-term health hazards that may be overlooked in time-integrated filter-based measurements.

As with most urban monitoring studies, interpretation of spatial variability and source contributions benefits from several contextual considerations. First, while the network of ten PurpleAir sensors and two microAethalometers may not provide complete spatial coverage of the entire metropolitan area, it captures key temporal trends and meaningful contrasts across sites. Spatial trends measured in this study (Figure ) align closely with the CTM results of Guttikunda et al. (2025), who similarly found average PM2.5 concentrations to be highest south and west of the city center, indicating that the surface PM monitoring network represents the main spatial gradients in PM across Addis Ababa. One potential cause of the elevated particulate pollution in these areas is the Reppie solid waste disposal and incineration site. Second, PurpleAir measurements at each of the 10 sites were calibrated using a single correction algorithm trained with collocated BAM and PurpleAir data from two reference sites. This approach follows established practice for calibration of low-cost PM sensors, , and by incorporating data from two contrasting environments, we aimed to account for local differences in aerosol properties and humidity responses that could otherwise affect site-to-site comparability. , Nevertheless, residual differences in site-specific aerosol properties may introduce additional uncertainty into the calibrated PM2.5 estimates at individual sites. , The calibration model achieved a test-set hourly RMSE of 8.21 μg/m3, a 47% reduction from the precalibration RMSE of 15.21 μg/m3, reflecting a substantial improvement relative to the uncalibrated data and a reduction in the humidity-dependent bias visible in S.I. Figure S7. However, small, non-noise residual errors likely remain after calibration. Therefore, small differences among sites, months, or hours should be considered relative to the residual calibration uncertainty. The larger spatial and temporal patterns emphasized in the manuscript, including persistent differences between central/western and peripheral sites and repeated seasonal/diurnal behavior, are less likely to be driven by residual calibration uncertainty because they are consistent across extended periods and supported by the COD analysis and meteorological context.

The separation of BC into fossil fuel and biomass burning fractions relies on assumed AAE values and standard Aethalometer Model formulations. Given the diversity of combustion sources in Addis Ababa, some mixing of source signatures is expected, which may introduce uncertainty into the two-component attribution. The average BC fraction apportioned to biomass burning is just 5.8%, substantially lower than the 18.3% of PM2.5 mass apportioned to biomass burning by Tefera et al. (2021). However, this discrepancy may reflect fundamental differences in the quantities being apportioned: aethalometer-based methods quantify only the BC component of biomass burning, while filter-based PM2.5 source apportionment includes associated organic aerosol and other nonabsorbing particulate species. Additional details on model assumptions and associated uncertainties are provided in the Supporting Information (Section 4).

This study demonstrates the value of long-term, continuous, multisite monitoring for assessing air quality trends. These data sets capture seasonal, spatial, and diurnal patterns, as well as short-term pollution events that lower time-resolution observations may miss. Multiple monitoring sites reveal spatial variability across the city, and pairing air quality with meteorological data helps attribute pollution to wind patterns and broader atmospheric influences. Together, these integrated approaches can support air quality management decisions and increase public awareness.

Although in situ measurements provide essential ground truth for characterizing local air quality, integrating these observations with satellite remote sensing and chemical transport modeling offers a more complete and spatially continuous understanding of pollution patterns than any single approach alone. This integrated framework is central to the MAIA investigation, which will combine surface measurements with satellite-derived aerosol properties and modeled concentrations to estimate near-surface PM2.5 and its chemical components across a set of globally distributed target areas. The ground-based data set presented here establishes a critical prelaunch baseline for the Addis Ababa PTA and will serve as ground truth for MAIA’s generation of gap-filled maps of PM2.5 composition throughout the region.
