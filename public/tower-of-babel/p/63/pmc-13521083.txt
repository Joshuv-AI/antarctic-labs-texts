Clinical utility of 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid gene polymerase chain reaction versus tissue culture in shoulder periprosthetic joint infections
Authors: Collin N Nguyen, Jack H Drake, Mark M Mounajjed, Eric J Cotter, Brian F Grogan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiagnosing shoulder periprosthetic joint infection remains challenging, particularly in the setting of indolent organisms. Broad-range 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid (RNA) gene polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with sequencing has been proposed as an adjunct to culture; however, its diagnostic performance in revision shoulder arthroplasty using periprosthetic tissue remains incompletely defined.MethodsSingle center retrospective study of revision shoulder arthroplasty cases in which both intraoperative periprosthetic tissue cultures and broad-range 16S rRNA gene PCR with sequencing were obtained which is routine at the study center. Five deep periprosthetic tissue specimens from the glenoid and/or humeral bone–implant interfaces were cultured aerobically and anaerobically for a minimum of 14 days. For molecular testing, 5 tissue specimens were pooled and homogenized prior to automated DNA extraction, real-time PCR amplification, sequencing, and organism identification using SmartGene IDNS. The microbiologic reference standard for infection was defined as classified by the 2018 International Consensus Meeting (ICM). Diagnostic performance of 16S PCR was calculated relative to this reference. Agreement between 16S PCR and culture was assessed using Cohen κ.ResultsFifty-nine revision cases were included; 18 met the 2018 ICM criteria for infection and 41 were classified as not infected. Relative to the ICM-confirmed causative organism, 16S PCR yielded 0 true positives, 18 false negatives, 6 false positives, and 35 true negatives. Sensitivity was 0.0%, specificity was 85.4%, positive predictive value was not calculable (no true positives identified), negative predictive value was 66.0%, and overall accuracy was 59.3%. Overall agreement was 66.1% with only slight chance-corrected agreement (Cohen κ = 0.087).ConclusionIn this shoulder-specific cohort, 16S rRNA gene PCR demonstrated low observed sensitivity, poor organism-level concordance with tissue culture, and limited clinical utility. Routine use of 16S PCR in revision shoulder arthroplasty cannot be supported based on the findings of the present study. Its role may be limited to select cases with high clinical suspicion and negative bacterial cultures, though further prospective study is needed.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Revision shoulder arthroplasty, 16S rRNA PCR, Molecular diagnostics, Tissue culture, Cutibacterium acnes, Diagnostic accuracy

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is among the most consequential complications following primary shoulder arthroplasty. Current studies estimate that the incidence of PJI following primary shoulder arthroplasty is approximately 1-2.5%.6,17 As the volume of shoulder arthroplasty continues to rise, the burden of shoulder PJI is expected to increase, with significant implications for patient health care costs, morbidity, and quality of life.9,11,21 Patients frequently require prolonged antibiotic treatment following revision surgery for PJI, which underscores the importance of accurate diagnosis and timely treatment of periprosthetic infection.11

However, the diagnosis of shoulder PJI is challenging compared to hip and knee PJI due to less virulent and indolent organisms commonly inciting shoulder PJI such as Cutibacterium acnes.12 Unlike in the knee and hip, evidence for shoulder arthrocentesis remains limited, with reported sensitivities ranging from 0 to 33% in the literature.4,18,20 Thus, perioperative tissue biopsy and culture currently remain the gold standard for detecting PJI and identifying the causative microorganism; however, they are limited by long incubation requirements, the presence of biofilm-producing bacteria, and lower bacterial loads.17

A unique challenge in the diagnosis and management of shoulder PJI comes in the form of single positive culture results in patients with a low pre-operative index of suspicion for infection. C acnes has historically been considered a contaminant, a byproduct of the significant colonization of the skin around the shoulder by the bacteria.15 Thus, distinguishing a true positive from a contaminant in this setting is a frequently faced dilemma. It also remains a topic of debate whether 1 or even 2 positive cultures of C acnes is indicative of a true infection without overt symptoms.2,3,16,19

In response to these challenges, new techniques utilizing molecular diagnostic methods have emerged as potential adjuncts to guide the diagnosis of shoulder PJI.5 The 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid (rRNA) gene is a highly conserved region of the bacterial genome containing hypervariable sequences that enable genus- and species-level identification across a broad range of organisms. Broad-range polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of these hypervariable regions, followed by sequencing and database comparison, allows for organism identification independent of culture conditions, making it particularly appealing in settings of low bacterial load, prior antibiotic exposure, or biofilm-producing organisms where conventional culture may fail. One such adjunct is 16S rRNA gene PCR; however, current studies are typically comprised of mixed-joint cohorts with limited reporting of shoulder-specific outcomes.1,7,8,10 The results to date demonstrate 16S PCR has high specificity and moderate sensitivity for detecting PJI, with 1 mixed joint meta-analysis reporting pooled specificity and sensitivities of 94% and 80%, respectively, making it potentially useful in culture-negative infections or cases with a single positive culture result.13

Diagnostic algorithms for PJI, such as the 2018 International Consensus Meeting (ICM) criteria, do not formally incorporate molecular results, leading to inconsistent protocols and interpretations across institutions.14 Furthermore, no large-scale retrospective studies have evaluated the performance of 16S PCR compared to intraoperative tissue culture specifically in shoulder arthroplasty cohorts. This paucity of investigation highlights the need for joint-specific evaluations of molecular diagnostics. A retrospective study comparing 16S PCR to intraoperative tissue culture, using standardized PJI definitions, will help clarify the accuracy of 16S PCR, and its role in culture-negative infections. Thus, this study's purpose was to assess the contribution of 16S rRNA gene PCR testing in the diagnosis of shoulder PJI.

Materials and methods

Patients

A retrospective review of consecutive patients who underwent revision shoulder arthroplasty was conducted at a single academic center from September 2018 to September 2025. The study was approved by the University of Wisconsin-Madison Institutional Review Board (IRB#: 2025-1302). Inclusion criteria were patient age of at least eighteen years at the time of revision shoulder arthroplasty and revision shoulder arthroplasty surgery wherein tissue biopsy and 16S rRNA PCR testing were performed. Exclusion criteria were patients who had received systemic antibiotics within the month leading up to revision or already had an antibiotic spacer in place at the time of revision. During the early portion of the study period, 16S rRNA PCR was not yet routinely ordered by the operating surgeons; cases lacking paired 16S and culture data from this era were excluded. For all remaining cases, both intraoperative tissue culture and 16S rRNA PCR were obtained concurrently as part of routine practice.

Demographics

Patient demographics such as age, gender, clinical indication, index arthroplasty details, revision arthroplasty details, and antibiotic exposure before revision were collected via chart review. Pre-operative suspicion for PJI was determined by the treating surgeon based on clinical presentation, imaging, and laboratory findings. Culture and 16S rRNA PCR data were collected and included the organism isolated, the number of positive samples, and the time to positivity for each case.

Definition of periprosthetic joint infection

PJI versus aseptic revision status was classified according to the 2018 ICM shoulder PJI criteria, which served as the reference standard for infection status throughout this study. For evaluation of 16S rRNA PCR, the causative organism confirmed at the time of an ICM-defined infection served as the reference for organism identification.

Culture and molecular techniques

Tissue samples were taken at the time of revision shoulder arthroplasty from the deep shoulder joint from bone-prosthetic interface at the glenoid and/or humeral implant after changing gloves, utilizing sterile technique, and with fresh instruments. Five samples were cultured in both aerobic and anaerobic environments for a minimum of 14 days to maximize the detection of C acnes. Periprosthetic tissue specimens were processed using institutional microbiology protocols under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. For aerobic culture, specimens were plated onto chocolate agar and blood agar, and inoculated into thioglycolate broth as liquid enrichment medium. For anaerobic culture, specimens were plated onto reducible blood agar and inoculated into chopped meat carbohydrate broth. For 16S rRNA PCR analysis, 5 tissue samples were pooled and homogenized prior to PCR processing. Bacterial DNA from biological samples was isolated by automated nucleic acid extraction using Maxwell RSC (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) procedures. Real-time PCR amplification and subsequent DNA sequencing targeting 16S rRNA gene sequences were performed. DNA sequences generated from the PCR products of the 16S rRNA gene were compared to known bacterial 16S sequences within the SmartGene IDNS (SmartGene North American Operations, Raleigh, NC, USA) to provide genus and, whenever possible, species identification. The 16S rRNA sequencing assay used at the study institution is a qualitative, Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments-certified and College of American Pathologists accredited method that reports genus- or species-level identification only when sequence homology is at least 98% with reference entries in the SmartGene database. In validation studies, the assay demonstrated an analytical sensitivity of approximately 104 bacterial cells and 100% concordance with reference-laboratory and culture identifications, exceeding the predefined ≥95% correlation criterion.

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. The diagnostic performance of 16S rRNA PCR (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value [PPV], negative predictive value [NPV], and accuracy) was assessed using the 2018 ICM criteria for infection status and the corresponding culture-confirmed causative organism as the reference standard. A 16S PCR result was classified as a true positive only if it identified the reference organism in an ICM-confirmed infection; a non-concordant result in an infected case (16S-negative or organism-discordant) was classified as a false negative. A 16S PCR-positive result in a non-infected case was classified as a false positive, and a negative result in a non-infected case as a true negative. Confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated and agreement between binary 16S PCR and culture results was assessed using Cohen κ.

Results

Of the initial 82 patients that underwent revision surgery, 59 patients were included in the final analysis. Patients were excluded for the following reasons: 12 had insufficient 16S data and 12 had been on antibiotics within the month leading up to revision arthroplasty. In total, 18 of the 59 patients (30.5%) met the 2018 ICM criteria for infection, and shoulder PJI was ruled out in the remaining 41. Among the 41 cases classified as not infected, none underwent re-revision surgery or received a subsequent diagnosis of PJI within 1 year of the index revision procedure. The mean age of infected and not infected cases was 64.1 ± 12.6 years and 67.0 ± 8.5 years, respectively. The mean time between index surgery and revision for infected and not infected cases was 53.5 ± 52.1 months and 55.7 ± 50.5 months, respectively. The average body mass index for infected and not infected cases was 30.2 ± 3.8 kg/m2 and 31.0 ± 6.7 kg/m2, respectively. Demographics and indications are presented in Table I.

Culture results

Of the 59 patients included, 18 (30.5%) met criteria for PJI and 41 were considered not infected. Culture positivity, defined as ≥2 positive concordant cultures, was observed in all 18 PJI cases. Of the PJI cases, the most common isolated bacterium was C acnes, which was identified in all 18 PJI cases. Four of these cases were polymicrobial, with a second organism co-isolated alongside C acnes (Streptococcus agalactiae, Staphylococcus epidermidis, coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, and Staphylococcus pasteuri, respectively). Single positive cultures were identified in 8 cases that did not meet the criteria for PJI: C acnes (3), Staphylococcus capitis (1), Streptococcus parasanguinis (1), S epidermidis (1), gram-negative rods (1), and Micrococcus species (1). These cases were considered contaminants at the time of revision as they did not meet the 2018 ICM shoulder PJI criteria. 16S results

Testing for 16S rRNA PCR identified bacteria in 4 patients who met 2018 ICM criteria for PJI. In all 4 cases, the organisms identified by 16S PCR were discordant with tissue culture, which grew C acnes; 16S PCR instead identified Micrococcus species, S capitis, Streptococcus mitis, and an organism most closely homologous to Granulicatella adiacens, respectively. All 4 16S PCR results were interpreted as contaminants and did not impact management.

An additional 6 cases demonstrated 16S PCR positivity in patients who did not meet ICM criteria for PJI. In these cases, 16S PCR identified Clostridium saudiense, Burkholderia cepacia complex, Staphylococcus warneri, Corynebacterium species, and organisms most closely homologous to Corynebacterium/Micrococcus and Lawsonella/S capitis. In all cases, when a single positive paired culture was available, they were negative or discordant. In all cases, the 16S PCR result did not influence surgeon or infectious disease diagnosis or management and the organisms were considered contaminants. In 14 PJI cases diagnosed with ≥2 concordant C acnes cultures, 16S rRNA PCR failed to detect any bacteria. Among the 8 single positive intraoperative cultures, only 1 also demonstrated positive 16S rRNA PCR, and that organism was discordant with culture; both were interpreted as contaminants. Diagnostic accuracy of 16S ribosomal RNA PCR

The sensitivity and specificity of 16S rRNA PCR, using the 2018 ICM criteria for infection status and the corresponding culture-confirmed organism as the reference standard, were 0.0% (95% CI, 0.0%–18.5%) and 85.4% (95% CI, 70.8%–94.4%), respectively. The PPV was not calculable (no true positives identified), and the NPV was 66.0% (95% CI, 51.7%–78.5%). The overall accuracy was 59.3% (95% CI, 45.7%–71.9%). Diagnostic accuracy results are outlined in Table III. No 16S PCR result identified the causative organism in any of the 18 confirmed infections. Cohen κ for agreement between 16S PCR and culture was 0.087 (95% CI, −0.239–0.412).

Discussion

In our cohort, 16S rRNA PCR demonstrated poor observed organism-level sensitivity relative to the reference standard, with a sensitivity of 0.0% (95% CI, 0.0%–18.5%). It showed higher specificity (85.4%; 95% CI, 70.8%–94.4%), suggesting limited utility as a primary organism-identification test. In this series, 16S PCR results did not alter surgical planning at revision or antibiotic management in any case.

The results of this study stand in contrast to a recent review by Olearo et al,13 which systematically evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of 16S PCR, wherein they reported a pooled sensitivity of 78% for conventional PCR and 83% for real-time PCR, with specificities for both ranging from 94 to 95%. However, they lacked the granularity of data to stratify analysis by prosthesis type, thus lacking shoulder-specific data, which is a key limitation that they point out.13 Of note, meta-analysis estimates of 16S PCR diagnostic accuracy in this case reflects PJI detection, whereas our analysis evaluates organism-level concordance using tissue culture as the organism reference; therefore, direct comparison of sensitivity and specificity is limited.

In a mixed-joint arthroplasty cohort, Marín et al reported higher diagnostic performance for universal 16S rRNA PCR in the diagnosis of PJI, with per-sample sensitivity of 67.1%, specificity of 97.8%, PPV of 94.3%, and NPV of 84.8%. However, direct comparison with the present study is limited because their cohort was not shoulder specific (including only 1 shoulder infection case) and was derived from a mixed-joint PJI diagnostic framework, differing from our shoulder specific organism concordance framework.

In contrast, our study demonstrated poor concordance between 16S PCR and tissue culture, with no 16S-positive result demonstrating organism-level concordance with culture across the entire cohort. After accounting for the high proportion of concordant negative results, Cohen κ demonstrated poor agreement (κ = 0.087). Taken together, these findings suggest that, in this shoulder arthroplasty revision cohort, 16S PCR had limited utility for organism identification despite a higher specificity.

In the current study, positive 16S PCR results were uncommon and, when present, did not show consistent concordance with bacterial culture data. Specifically, in cases with single positive bacterial cultures, 16S PCR did not influence clinical decision-making. It also failed to identify any culture-negative PJIs. Culture-negative PJI was defined as meeting 2018 ICM criteria in the absence of positive tissue cultures. No such cases were identified in the present cohort. In the current study, 16S PCR did not change the management of PJI in any case, including surgical planning or antibiotic management. Despite this, it may still play a role in providing evidence against active infection, although clinical and surgical data should also be considered. Therefore, although the potential added clinical utility and faster turnaround of 16S rRNA PCR are theoretically appealing, these advantages must be weighed against the added cost to patients and the added burden of false-positive results, including unnecessary additional testing or procedures, and the anxiety experienced by the patient when receiving these results. At our institution, the cost for 16S rRNA PCR is $250 per test. While our findings raise questions about the added value of routine 16S PCR given its limited diagnostic yield, this figure is institution-specific and cannot be generalized to other centers or health systems. Despite this, these findings support further study into the cost-effectiveness of 16S PCR in shoulder surgery specifically.

Despite limited utility as a routine adjunct, there remain select clinical scenarios in which 16S rRNA PCR may still warrant consideration. These include cases with high pre-operative clinical suspicion for PJI in the setting of negative or equivocal intraoperative cultures, or a discordant clinical picture characterized by elevated inflammatory markers in the absence of culture positivity. In these settings, the relatively high NPV of 16S PCR may help provide supplementary evidence against active infection, though its poor PPV limits its ability to confirm it. As such, 16S PCR results should inform, though not replace, clinical and surgical judgment.

The lower observed sensitivity, discordance, and limited impact on decision-making of 16S rRNA PCR in the current study may be due to the technical limitations of 16S rRNA gene sequencing at lower bacterial loads, a common finding in low-virulence, chronic shoulder PJI. In our study, we present 18 (30.5%) PJI cases, all of which are supported by ≥ 2 positive cultures. However, only 4 of these 18 cases demonstrated positive 16S PCR results, none of which showed concordance at the bacterial level.

This finding is consistent with results from the mixed-joint cohort study by Bémer et al,1 who observed reduced sensitivity of 16S rRNA PCR for low-virulence organisms commonly found in shoulder PJI, including C acnes and coagulase-negative staphylococci (11% and 72%, respectively). While shoulder cases comprised a small subset of the study population (4%), these findings outline the potential limitations of 16S PCR specifically for organisms commonly implicated in shoulder PJI.

Our study is not without limitations. As with all retrospective studies, there are inherent risks of selection bias. Second, due to a relatively small sample size, statistical power is limited, and sub-group analysis is not possible. Third, as the current literature surrounding shoulder PJI is heterogeneous and a current topic of debate, the determination of false positives and true infections is based on clinical judgment of the respective surgeon, which introduces misclassification bias. Additionally, this study took place at a single academic institution with unique microbiological protocols regarding culture and 16S PCR methods. As a result, the generalizability of results may be limited. Additionally, patients excluded for recent antibiotic exposure may differ in disease spectrum from those analyzed, and the potential for spectrum bias cannot be fully excluded. The 16S assay used at the study institution is analytically validated (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments certified and College of American Pathologists accredited), with a reported detection threshold of approximately 104 bacterial cells. The low observed sensitivity is therefore potentially influenced by the characteristically low bacterial burden of shoulder PJI, which may fall near or below this threshold for organisms such as C acnes. In addition, the standard practice of pooling and homogenizing all 5 tissue specimens, as adopted at our institution to reduce testing costs while maintaining diagnostic yield, may also affect the effective bacterial load per reaction, though the effect of dilution cannot be determined from our data. Beyond low bacterial load and specimen pooling, the observed PCR performance may also relate to the specific DNA extraction method, the sensitivity of the broad-range primers used, particularly when considering the necessity of using tissue samples versus synovial fluid samples in the setting of prevalent “dry taps” in the shoulder when compared to other joints; these technical factors could not be assessed in the present retrospective design and warrant evaluation in future work.

Conclusion

In this shoulder-specific cohort, 16S rRNA gene PCR demonstrated low observed sensitivity, poor organism-level concordance with tissue culture, and limited clinical utility. Routine use of 16S PCR in revision shoulder arthroplasty cannot be supported based on the findings of the present study. In concordance with the Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines,10 its role may be limited to select cases with high clinical suspicion and negative bacterial cultures, though further prospective study is needed.
