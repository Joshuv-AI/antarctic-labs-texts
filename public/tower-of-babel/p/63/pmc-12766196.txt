Artificial intelligence-integrated wearable biomedical devices for cancer management
Authors: Penghua Zhai, Weixin Xu, Guifang Duan, Yukun Wu, Mingxin Qi, Lingqian Chang, Wei Mu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer remains the leading cause of death globally. Early diagnosis and intervention play deterministic roles in improving clinical prognosis. Traditional cancer management heavily depends on central hospital-based imaging and invasive diagnostics, which are intermittent and costly. Moreover, these strategies show limitations to patient compliance and real-time diagnosis. The emergence of wearable biomedical devices (WBDs) has offered a compelling alternative, enabling continuous, non-invasive in situ monitoring of bio-signals and real-time tissue imaging in daily settings. In particular, these devices have recently been integrated with therapeutic modules and artificial intelligence (AI) and have been adapted to closed-loop interventions, allowing for precise, on-demand drug delivery and localized therapy. In this review, we provide an overview of AI-integrated WBDs with their applications in cancer screening, diagnosis, and therapy. To solve the remaining issues of inaccurate screening, delayed intervention and severe side effects, the innovation of WBDs mainly includes conformable wearing structures, adhesive materials and integrated sensors/drug delivery modules. The integration of AI into WBDs has demonstrated high performance in improving signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and real-time data processing, which significantly enhance the capabilities in long-term monitoring, and patient-specific bio-signal variations. The last session provides future directions for AI-integrated WBDs, focusing on improving SNR, reducing false positives caused by high sensitivity, and addressing patient data privacy concerns during AI training.Keywords: Cancer management, Wearable biomedical devices, Artificial intelligence, Early diagnosis, Drug delivery

Introduction

Cancer remains the world-leading cause of death.1, 2, 3 Clinically, multiple factors including timely screening, accurate diagnosis, and effective medical intervention play critical roles in improving patient prognosis and 5-year survival rates.4,5 However, traditional cancer management strategies rely heavily on hospital-based imaging and invasive diagnostics, which are intermittent, costly, and centralized, leading to unequal access and poor patient compliance.6, 7, 8 On the one hand, current monitoring strategies are constrained to scheduled hospital visits, providing limited access to real-time changes in bio-signals, which may lead to the delayed diagnosis and therapy decisions.6 On the other hand, current drug-based therapies relying on oral or injectable methods are prolonged and systemic, which lack spatial and temporal precision, making it difficult to achieve targeted, on-demand treatment at the tumor site.7,9

In recent years, wearable biomedical devices (WBDs) have brought new prospects to cancer management by enabling continuous, non-invasive in situ monitoring of bio-signals and real-time tissue imaging in daily settings, owing to their miniaturization, long-term energy supply, high sensitivity, and conformal wearing.10, 11, 12, 13 Based on the target analyte, WBDs can be divided into wearable biomedical sensor and wearable biomedical imager. The former achieves high-sensitivity detection and real-time response through advancements in highly sensitive sensors, such as wearable electrochemical sensors, bringing new prospects for early screening and diagnosis.14, 15, 16 Furthermore, when integrated with therapeutic modules for on-demand drug delivery and localized therapy methods, these devices enable minimally invasive or even non-invasive closed-loop interventions, exemplified by wearable microneedle patches.17,18 The latter, leveraging advancements in adhesive materials and conformal wearable structural design, enables tissue imaging of patients without temporal or spatial constraints, bringing new prospects for real-time and long-term monitoring.19, 20, 21 Building on these advances, recent developments in power supply technology, flexible electronics, and bio-adhesion technology, such as self-powered technology, micro-electromechanical systems, and hydrogel, have significantly advanced the durability and comfort of WBDs, further boosting their effectiveness in cancer management.22, 23, 24

With the rapid advancement of WBDs in real-time monitoring and timely intervention, significant challenges have emerged that limit their clinical effectiveness. Signal quality issues arise from improper device placement and environmental interference, while clinical reliability is compromised by the inherent complexity and variability of bio-signals, suboptimal detection thresholds, and substantial inter-individual biophysical differences.11,25,26 To address these challenges, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) based artificial intelligence (AI) have emerged as a transformative solution for enhancing the performance of WBDs across multiple dimensions.27, 28, 29, 30 First, AI based denoising post-processing methods improves the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of WBDs, effectively resolving signal quality issues stemming from improper device placement and environmental interference, and suboptimal.31,32 Second, AI’s advanced pattern recognition ability tackles the complexity of biological data patterns, delivering real-time and reliable clinical insights that are accessible to patients without specialized medical knowledge.33,34 Additionally, AI enhances the efficiency and availability of patient-physician communication and remote collaboration through precise data analysis and interpretable outputs.24,34,35 Consequently, AI-integrated WBDs provide more accurate, real-time, and user-friendly solutions for cancer management.28,36

In this review, we provide a timely overview of the advances of WBDs for their applications in cancer management (Fig. 1). Firstly, we introduce fundamentals of WBDs for cancer management, including the non-invasive in situ monitoring of bio-signals and real-time tissue imaging. Then, we summarize the function of AI in WBDs. The AI-integrated WBDs demonstrates high performance in improving real-time data processing and closed-loop interventions. In addition, we review the applications of WBDs in cancer screening, diagnosis, and therapy respectively. To achieve the early diagnosis and intervention, WBDs innovate in conformable wearing structures, adhesive materials and integrated sensors/drug delivery modules. Finally, we summarize the current features of WBDs and challenges, as well as their future development directions in improving SNR, reducing false positives caused by high sensitivity, and addressing patient data privacy concerns during AI training are also prospected.

Fundamentals of WBDs for cancer management

WBDs typically measure biophysical or biochemical parameters via skins, and convert them into bio-signals or images for further analysis.13,37 With the unique advantages such as miniaturization, long-term energy supply, high sensitivity and conformal wearing, WBDs enable minimally invasive or even non-invasive continuous monitoring and timely therapeutic intervention for tumors.38

Based on the type of analytes, WBDs can be categorized into two types: wearable biomedical sensor (WBS) and wearable biomedical imager (WBI). WBS is designed to measure bio-signals, enabling the continuous and real-time monitoring of tumor-related indicators, such as pH levels, chemical concentrations, and specific biomarkers.39,40 WBI provides structural or functional visualization of tumors by converting bio-signals into images, such as wearable ultrasound imager (WUI).23,41 Both WBS and WBI generate continuous and real-time data, which facilitates the cancer management.

Wearable biomedical sensor

According to the working principle of the sensor, WBS can be primarily divided into two types: biophysical sensor and biochemical sensor.36,42 Biophysical sensor enables the monitoring of bio-signals associated with vital human activities, including impedance, body temperature, and blood oxygen concentration. The fundamental principle is to convert these various biophysical parameters into measurable and processable signals, such as electricity, magnetism, and temperature. For example, wearable piezoelectric sensor converts its proportional deformation caused by tumor volume changes into corresponding alterations in electrical impedance, thereby determining the optimal timing of therapy and evaluating the therapeutic effect.43 Additionally, wearable breast monitoring patch utilizes two biometric patches to record and analyze thermodynamic metabolic data from breast skin surface, thereby determining whether malignant tumor exist in breast.44 Therefore, biophysical sensor offers the benefits of real-time, continuous, and non-invasive monitoring, holding immense promise for early diagnosis, and evaluation of therapeutic effect.

Biochemical sensors are designed to identify specific chemicals or biomolecules in body fluids, including pH levels, cell-free DNA (cfDNA), and tyrosinase (TYR).45,46 The principle involves translating specific interactions between the target molecule and the sensing interface into measurable signals, such as electrical signals, optical signals and mass changes. For instance, a stretchable wearable microneedle patch that minimally penetrates the skin enables in situ detection of TYR, facilitating early discrimination of melanoma from benign moles and healthy skin, while also allowing dynamic monitoring of TYR levels before and during anti-PD-1 therapy to support prognostic evaluation and treatment response assessment.47 In addition, based on the synergistic effect of CRISPR-activated graphene bio-interfaces, the patch extracts and monitors cfDNA in vivo over extended periods, showing its potential in early tumor screening.48 Therefore, biochemical sensor offers high sensitivity, selectivity, and real-time monitoring of biomarkers in body fluids, providing a new means for early screening and therapy of cancer.

WBS can not only enable early diagnosis through real-time, continuous, and highly sensitive monitoring, but also enhances therapeutic effect by facilitating in situ and non-invasive therapy and drug delivery.

Wearable biomedical imager

Imaging, as one of the most intuitive and widely used tools in clinical practice, provides clear visualization of the anatomic structure or metabolic level of a patient.49,50 Compared with traditional in-hospital imaging equipment, WBI enables long-term tracking of tumor progression and therapeutic response, owing to its advantage of being continuous and non-invasive wearable.19,41 Among various WBI techniques, two commonly adopted modalities are WUI 41,50 and wearable electromagnetic imager (WEI).51,52

WUI has attracted substantial research interest due to its ability to provide real-time anatomical information by leveraging the distinct reflections of ultrasound waves across different tissue types.20,41 Compared with traditional ultrasound imager, the difference of WUI mainly comes from the mechanical properties of their probes. WUI is mainly divided into stretchable imager and bio-adhesion imager. The former places significant emphasis on the fabrication of stretchable probes, which improves the wearing comfort.21,23 Stretchable probes are typically fabricated by structured stretchable conductors, filler-based stretchable conductors, or liquid metals.21 The latter mainly focuses on the adhesion materials between the ultrasound transducer and the skin.53 Bio-adhesive WUI enhances both the long-term monitoring capacity and resolution.54 By miniaturizing and flexing traditional imager, WUI improves the long-term monitoring capabilities, making it more suitable for cancer management.

In addition, WEI serves as another effective modality for real-time and continuous tumor imaging by detecting variations in tissue dielectric properties.52,55 WEI encompasses techniques such as wearable electrical impedance tomography (EIT) and wearable microwave imaging. Typically, EIT measures voltage distributions resulting from low-frequency current injection and reconstructs conductivity or impedance maps.56 In contrast, microwave imaging estimates the spatial distribution of dielectric constant and conductivity by analyzing the propagation and scattering of microwave electromagnetic waves.52 As a non-invasive and cost-effective imaging technique, WEI is suitable for real-time monitoring, particularly in regions with limited medical resources.

WBI not only streamlines medical procedures but also mitigates the risk of performance variability associated with physician inexperience. In addition, it enables the transition in health monitoring from “short-term” to “long-term,” and from “hospitalized” to “home-based.”

Integration of AI techniques in WBDs

WBDs produce diverse types of data, ranging from bio-signals to medical images. To derive clinically meaningful insights from these heterogeneous data, advanced data processing and analytical techniques are crucial. AI techniques play an important role in this process by enabling the signal denoising, identification of complex patterns, and prediction of future outcomes, thereby supporting more informed and personalized treatment decisions.

Depending on the clinical objective, AI models are generally categorized into classification models and risk prediction models. Classification models focus on analyzing the current state of the disease, such as identifying tumor presence, malignancy differentiation, and metastasis. In contrast, risk prediction models are designed to estimate future clinical outcomes, such as the likelihood of therapy-related complications, postoperative outcomes, or mortality risks. These tasks are supported by a broad range of ML techniques, ranging from classical shallow models such as decision tree, support vector machines (SVMs) and random forests (RF), to DL algorithms such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and Transformer-based architectures. Varying by data type, different pipelines are employed. For bio-signals obtained by WBDs, preprocessing typically involves denoising. Subsequent analysis including feature extraction and classification, can either follow a stepwise pipeline, as in classical shallow models, or adopt an end-to-end processing approach, as seen with 1D CNNs. For image-based data from WBIs, 2D/3D CNNs or Transformer-based models are preferred to capture high dimension spatial features. Nevertheless, the development of generalized AI models requires large-scale, high-quality datasets for practical training and rigorous real-world validation. Overcoming these challenges is critical to fully realizing the potential of AI-integrated WBDs in advancing cancer care.

Application of WBDs in cancer screening

Working pipeline

Early cancer screening is critical for improving prognosis and reducing mortality. It primarily targets asymptomatic individuals and is conducted on a population basis, aiming to identify suspected malignancies or high-risk lesions at an early stage. By detecting disease before clinical symptoms arise, screening promotes earlier diagnosis and facilitates more effective therapy, ultimately leading to improved health outcomes at the population level. However, traditional screening methods usually depend on intermittent sampling and bulky equipment, limiting continuous and real-time monitoring. Recent advancements in WBDs enabled non-invasive, in vivo detection of cancer-related biomarkers, providing opportunities for continuous monitoring. WBDs specifically designed for cancer screening typically operate by collecting bio-fluids through non-invasive interfaces, which contain biomarkers indicating tumor activity or inflammation. Integrated with flexible electronics, these devices utilize electrochemical, optical, or mechanical transduction methods to convert molecular interactions into real-time digital signals. Equipped with advanced data processing systems, often bolstered by wireless modules, they transmit the collected data to external devices like smartphones or cloud servers, enabling real-time analysis and detection of cancer-related abnormalities for subsequent clinical intervention. The early screening strategy makes a paradigm shift from traditional, laboratory-based testing to real-time, continuous, and personalized cancer surveillance.58,59 Some recent progresses in the application of WBDs in cancer screening are shown in Fig. 3. Current wearable screening strategies primarily focus on the detection of both DNA-related and non-DNA-related biomarkers. Specifically, DNA-related biomarkers, including Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) DNA, cfDNA, and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), provide high specificity for cancer screening but may not be detectable in all early-stage patients.60 Alternatively, non-DNA-related biomarkers, including circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and skin-surface enzymes, can provide insights into early biochemical and biophysical changes associated with tumor initiation or host responses.14,47 These non-invasive biomarkers are ideally suited for continuous tracking and immediate feedback, serving as a powerful complement to molecular diagnostics in wearable cancer screening devices. In addition, some tumor imaging- based methods have also been employed for early cancer screening, such as imaging of tissue temperature changes and abnormal microwave reflections.

In summary, wearable cancer screening devices offer an innovative solution that overcomes numerous limitations of traditional methods. Leveraging advanced bio-sensing and data transmission technologies, they facilitate continuous, non-invasive, and real-time monitoring of various tumor-related biomarkers. These devices possess significant potential to improve the accuracy of early cancer screening and effectiveness of personalized surveillance. However, challenges like sensor stability, limited clinical operational duration, data security concerns, and reliable power supply systems are still need to be addressed before widespread clinical adoption. When integrated with AI, WBDs can potentially achieve enhanced real-time data interpretation and personalized risk assessment, further improving early screening and patient-specific surveillance.

DNA-related biomarker detection

The integration of wearable technologies with bio-sensing systems has opened new avenues for minimally invasive or even non-invasive, real-time monitoring of tumor biomarkers.

For identification of tumor-related bio-signals, Yang et al. reported an integrated wearable microneedle patch to in situ screen nasopharyngeal tumor.62 The patch combined facile microneedle patch sampling in situ with point-of-care testing (POCT) electrochemical flexible microfluidic detection. The patch could capture and identify EBV cfDNA from interstitial fluid (ISF) around 15 minutes with a maximum capture efficiency of 93.6%. To improve the capture efficiency of cfDNA, Yang et al. proposed engineered wearable epidermal system to screen nasopharyngeal tumor. The system could rapidly capture and sense cfDNA by combining reverse iontophoresis and microneedles.61 Owing to a dual-extraction effect demonstrated by reverse iontophoresis and microneedles, the system successfully isolated the cfDNA from ISF within 10 minutes with a maximum capture efficiency of 95.4%. To improve the detection capability of macromolecule biomarkers, Yang et al. developed a wearable microneedle patch based on the synergetic effect of CRISPR-Cas9 and graphene biointerfaces to screen suspected nasopharyngeal tumors earlier.48 The patch employed conductive microneedles and reverse iontophoresis to efficient extraction cfDNA and real-time monitor concentration changes. Experimental results showed that the patch could retain high sensitivity for up to 10 days under high-interference conditions. CtDNA is another promising biomarker for early cancer screening. Fang et al. developed a wearable self-healing bioelectronic patch (iMethy) to achieve early cancer screen by dynamic monitoring methylated ctDNA.63 The iMethy combined transdermal ISF extraction based on an osmosis hydrogel bridge and field effect transistor (FET) –based biosensing. Experimental results showed that iMethy could detect ctDNA as low as 10−16 M in cellular models.

In conclusion, integrating advanced WBS with minimally invasive approaches significantly enhances real-time monitoring of DNA-related biomarkers. These devices demonstrate high sensitivity, rapid detection, and long-term stability, validating their potential for cancer screening. While these devices still face several challenges, such as limited biomarker specificity in complex conditions and potential skin irritation from long-term wear. With further development and validation, they could enable decentralized, patient-friendly, and precise tumor monitoring outside clinical settings.

Non-DNA-related biomarker detection

Beyond DNA-related biomarkers, a diverse array of non-DNA biomarkers such as CTCs, proteins, enzymes, and volatile compounds also offers valuable insights into early tumor development and progression. Recent advances in wearable technologies have enabled real-time, non-invasive detection of these biomarkers, expanding cancer screening possibilities.

For identification of tumor-related bio-signals, Liu et al. designed an innovative saliva-amassing low-dosed risk-factor alerted detecting (SALAD) system.67 The system employed an efficient liquid amassing (LA) module to collect and immobilize intraoral biomarkers in dynamic and complex saliva environments. It could achieve high sensitivity in situ detection under UV light.

For carcinoma in situ screening, to distinguish melanoma and normal tissues, Ciui et al. designed a minimally invasive microneedle biosensor to dynamically detect the TYR concentration signals.14 The system was based on a bandage electrochemical sensor, combining a catechol (CAT)-containing agarose gel, covering the three-electrode system, integrated to small flexible electronics. It could complete this task under good sensitivity with linear dependence (slope of 4.6 ± 0.3 µA mg−1 mL−1 TYR) and a negligible intercept (0.07 ± 0.09 µA). Additionally, for distinguishing melanoma and normal tissues, Huang et al. developed a flexible surface enhanced Raman scattering (SERS)-based microneedle patch to dynamically detect the TYR concentration signals.47 This platform incorporated a wearable microneedle (MN) patch and trimetallic Au@Ag-Pt nanoparticles (NPs). It could successfully apply to discriminate the melanoma subjects from skin moles and normal ones, as well as screen potential melanoma from lactate dehydrogenase negative patients. Moreover, to extract important biomarker associated with lung cancer, Maier et al. developed a paper-based electrochemical wearable sensor.64 This system could monitor exhaled H2O2 in artificial breath calibration-free and continuously. By modifying the chemistry of the sensing electrodes, the system can be extended to continuous monitoring of other analytes in exhaled breath. To monitor breast cancer biomarkers of high importance, Dervisevic et al. introduced an electrochemical WBS to detect ErbB2 concentration signals.18 This system employed high-density gold-coated silicon microneedle arrays as both a biomarker extraction platform and an electrochemical transducer. With high sensitivity and expansive detection range, it stood out as a highly promising candidate for early breast cancer screening. To quantification of protein biomarkers in blood at picomolar-level sensitivity requires, Tu et al. designed a real-time electrochemical detection of the inflammatory biomarker C-reactive protein (CRP) in sweat.65 This device integrated iontophoretic sweat extraction, microfluidic channels for sweat sampling and for reagent routing and replacement, and a graphene-based sensor array. It could quantify CRP, as well as ionic strength, pH and temperature. For continuous monitoring of circulating cells, Jang et al. developed a fluorescence-based WBS, CircTrek.68 This system resulted in a compact size of 44 mm × 39 mm × 15.5 mm. It held the potential to revolutionize early cancer screening and offered a dynamic assessment of therapeutic responses. To achieve real-time monitoring of cytokines in vivo, Xu et al. developed a functional carbon nanotube biointerface-based wearable microneedle patches to detect cytokines concentration signals.66 This system could remain stability for 5 days with high sensitivity and can detect cytokine surges within 1-4 hours.

In brief, the integration of WBDs with non-DNA biomarker detection has revolutionized cancer screening. These devices demonstrate remarkable adaptability in capturing early tumor-related signals from various bio-fluids or tissues. Breakthroughs in electrochemical sensing, microfluidics, and wireless data transmission have paved the way for real-time, non-invasive, and user-friendly detection strategies with high sensitivity and specificity. With continued innovation and clinical validation, these technologies hold great promise for enabling earlier diagnosis and more personalized cancer management.

Tumor imaging

The WBI has opened new possibilities for non-invasive and real-time tumor visualization, facilitating the continuous monitoring of structural and functional changes associated with early-stage tumors.

For distinguishing breast tumor and normal tissues, Porter et al. developed a microwave-based WBI to imaging breast tissue.52 The device used a multi-static time-domain pulse radar by embedding 16 flexible antennas in the bar to quantify and image the microwave fluctuation levels caused by tissue changes. During a 28-day volunteer test, the device showed superior image quality and signal acquisition capabilities, demonstrating its potential for personalized screening.

Overall, the WBI offers a promising approach for non-invasive, real-time, and continuous tumor imaging. Although these devices suffer from limitations such as limited spatial resolution and motion artifacts, their potential for decentralized screening is enhanced by combining highly sensitive sensors, and AI-powered denoising technologies.

Transforming cancer screening with AI-integrated WBDs

Signals from WBDs are often corrupted by noise from motion artifacts, environmental interference, and suboptimal device placement. To address these challenges, AI-based denoising techniques have been increasingly adopted to enhance signal quality. For example, Wang et al. introduced a high SNR detection method for multiple key biomarkers in tears based on a well-trained multi-channel convolutional recurrent neural network using only minute amounts of tears (∼ 20 μL), demonstrating the powerful potential of integrating biosensors and DL algorithms.32 Likewise, Nagar et al. proposed a fractional 1D CNN autoencoder for 1-dimension signal denoising compressing trainable parameters via randomized singular value decomposition to suit portable low-energy devices, outperforming conventional denoting methods.70 These studies highlight that AI-powered denoising can significantly enhance SNR in WBDs, thereby improving sensitivity and real-time monitoring capabilities. When integrated with lightweight networks, WBSs can achieve continuous and high-quality signal acquisition. WBDs can achieve continuous and high-quality signal acquisition, supporting applications such as cfDNA detection and tumor microenvironment monitoring.

Furthermore, AI models are employed to interpret the data generated by WBDs to improve screening accuracy, enable early screening, and support personalized monitoring. By integrating WBDs with AI, these devices overcome obstacles like low sensitivity and the absence of continuous assessment. Compared to traditional screening methods, AI-integrated WBDs provide more accurate, real-time, and user-friendly solutions. For example, Zhong et al. developed a AI-based wearable system for breast health monitoring to detect the presence or absence of breast tissue abnormalites.69 This system combined wearable hardware with a mobile-based infrared imaging setup and employed pre-trained ResNet architectures for robust feature extraction and classification, demonstrating optimal performance in both binary and multi-class breast cancer detection tasks. These results highlighted the potential of AI-integrated wearable thermal imaging as an accessible tool to support pre- and intra-operative decision-making.

Taken together, AI-integrated WBDs bring substantial benefits to cancer screening by achieving higher SNR, enhancing accuracy, enabling continuous monitoring, and supporting user-friendly designs. However, it is necessary to address issues such as potential data privacy and reliability across populations.

Application of WBDs in cancer diagnosis

Working pipeline

Unlike cancer screening, which aims to detect cancer in asymptomatic individuals, diagnosis is initiated after patients present clinical signs or symptoms suggestive of disease. It is a symptom-driven and individualized process involving targeted medical evaluations to confirm the presence, type, and stage of cancer, thereby enabling accurate characterization of the condition and guiding appropriate therapy planning. Therefore, WBDs designed for cancer diagnosis primarily focus on identifying abnormal bio-signals and imaging features. WBDs for cancer diagnosis integrates advanced bio-sensors and real-time data processing methods to detect and analyze cancer-related anomalies, and potentially provide real-time tumor imaging. The working principle unfolds as follows: Firstly, WBDs are equipped with non-invasive sensors to continuously monitoring body parameters. Secondly, sensors transmit the gathered data to a processor or a linked mobile device for initial preprocessing. Thirdly, the WBDs analyze the preprocessed data and seek out abnormal patterns or deviations that could indicate tumor or cancer-related metabolic changes. Finally, once detecting potential cancer-related anomalies, the device promptly alerts the users and healthcare providers in real time. This facilitates timely intervention and continuous health monitoring while obviating the invasive procedures and frequent hospital assessments.

In short, WBDs for cancer diagnosis leverage real-time bio-signal monitoring and imaging technologies to enable timely detection of cancer-related abnormalities, offering potential advantages over traditional clinical approaches. WBDs offer continuous, non-invasive, and user-friendly monitoring, facilitating timely diagnosis and medical intervention. Combining with AI, these devices can improve diagnostic accuracy through advanced feature extraction, pattern recognition, and data-driven decision support, helping to reduce false positives and enhance clinical reliability (Table 2).

WBS based signal detection

For cancer diagnosis, WBS plays a crucial role in early tumors detection with non-invasiveness and high sensitivity. They focus on capturing dynamic bio-signals, such as temperature, electromagnetic characteristics, and molecular biomarkers, which are associated with tumor development. Recent advancements have enabled integration into daily clothing, allowing real-time and user-friendly cancer monitoring.

For detection of bio-signals related to tumor diagnosis, Kim et al. developed an in vivo indwelling intravascular aphaeretic CTC isolation system to continuously collect CTCs directly from a peripheral vein.71 The system consisted of four major parts: a micro-controller, peristaltic pump, heparin injector, and a CTC capture module that contains a microfluidic CTC capture chip. It could overcome the limitation of small sample volumes and better capture tumor heterogeneity. To detect potential early indicator for intraoral cancers, Royea et al. developed the Cyrcadia Breast Monitor (CBM) to diagnosis breast cancer eralier.44 The CBM continuously recorded skin temperature rhythm data through two bio-sensing patches and uses time-series analysis to identify abnormalities. It effectively distinguished benign and malignant tumors, showing its promise for continuous monitoring. Similarly, to differentiate between malignant and benign breast tumors, Elsheakh et al. developed a smart bra, which used a flexible microwave textile antenna sensor for breast cancer diagnosis.73 Its lightweight and comfortable design made it highly suitable for home use, providing a user-friendly solution for long-term breast health surveillance. Moreover, to investigate which type of polarization was most effective for breast cancer, Elnaggar et al. introduced a dual-polarization bra-tenna sensor operating from 2 to 12 GHz frequency for early breast cancer diagnosis.72 The sensor was tailored to contours of human body and has excellent wear resistance and continuous detection stability. Experimental results showed that it could identify early tumors as small as 10 mm in diameter.

Taken together, WBS that relies on signal detection offer an innovative, non-invasive, continuous, and convenient means of cancer diagnosis. By capturing dynamic bio-signals intricately linked to tumor presence or progression, WBS achieves earlier and more sensitive tumor diagnosis. Although several limitations currently exist like relatively low diagnostic specificity and quality issues due to improper placement and environmental interference. By integrating these sensors into smart bra systems, WBS is evolving toward being lightweight, comfortable, and user-friendly.

WBI based tumor imaging

For tumor imaging diagnosis, WBI has already been applied to various types of cancer. For the diagnosis of breast tumors, Du et al. developed a conformable ultrasound breast patch (cUSBr-Patch) for whole-breast imaging.50 The system adopted a honeycomb-shaped patch integrated with a phased array and guided by an easy-to-operate tracker to achieve large-area, deep scanning and multi-angle breast imaging. Experimental results demonstrated that the system exhibited sufficient contrast resolution (∼3 dB) and axial/lateral resolutions of 0.25/1.0 mm at a depth of 30 mm, enabling the detection and characterization of small lesions (∼0.3 cm) in the breast. Besides, to diagnosis limbs-related tumors, Yang et al. introduced a triboelectric impedance tomography (TIT) system for limbs imaging and detecting changes in tissue conductivity.51 By obtaining impedance information from different soft tissues, the TIT system enabled time-resolved tomography of human limbs. This system achieved time-resolved tomography of soft tissues in human limbs and can be applied to multiple tumor-related diagnostic functions, such as detecting abnormal tissue masses and assessing tumor boundaries in limb-associated cancer.

In short, WBI, such as WUI and EIT, offers promising solutions for non-invasive, real-time tumor monitoring. WBI not only improves diagnostic sensitivity, but also increases patient comfort. Their capability to visualize internal tissue alterations outside of clinical settings facilitates earlier diagnosis and enables more frequent monitoring. However, WBI faces challenges in enhancing imaging depth and resolution, as well as in minimizing motion artifacts during routine daily activities. With continued technological innovation like integrating with AI, WBI is expected to further advance toward more accurate, accessible, and patient-friendly tumor imaging.

Advancing cancer diagnosis through Al-integrated WBDs

The integration of AI with WBDs primarily involves analyzing the data collected by these devices, including sensor signals and medical images, to enhance the efficiency and accuracy of cancer diagnosis, particularly at early stages. This includes tasks such as data interpretation, pattern recognition, and abnormality detection.

Recent studies have demonstrated that combining AI algorithms with continuous physiological signals can enable effective classification of breast tumors, offering advantages in sensitivity, comfort, and accessibility over conventional screening methods. For instance, Royea et al. proposed to use 24-hour continuous skin temperature signals collected by a thermal-based WBS to extract temperature-related features that quantify the variability in the 16 sensor temperature profile recordings, then adopt ML models such as decision tree, SVMs, RF, back-propagation neural network with ensemble methods for benign and malignant breast tumors classification.44 Besides, to improve the accuracy in early detection of breast cancer, Elnaggar et al. developed an ML-based AI model (including SVMs and decision tree) trained on tumor-related features extracted from microwave-based WBS signals. By capturing tumor-related subtle variations in the signal patterns, the system achieved breast tumor classification accuracy of up to 98%.72 Furthermore, Elsheakh et al. proposed a microwave-based WBS, which was antenna-based and using only textiles, for breast monitoring and screening.73 By combining microwave sensing data with ML, the system enables fully automated classification of breast tumors. Additionally, to address the high false negative rate of existing breast cancer detection methods, Banu et al. developed an electrochemical-based WBS to collect signals. They then applied a DL model based on the Swin Transformer architecture, which excelled at capturing complex signal patterns, combined with a mantis search algorithm for optimized model parameter tuning, to accurately distinguish between healthy and cancerous conditions and further categorize different stages of breast cancer.74

Medical imaging integrated into wearable devices serves as a critical on-demand diagnostic tool, enabling real-time visualization of dynamic physiological changes or localized tissue states. However, accurate interpretation of such wearable-acquired images often hinges on specialized analysis of motion-artifact-prone or low-resolution data, which can introduce variability and delays in assessment. AI has shown strong potential in this domain by enhancing the accuracy of wearable imaging analysis and reducing reliance on professional expertise for real-time interpretation. In the context of wearable cancer diagnostics, AI further enhances performance by automatically recognizing lesions, extracting relevant features, identifying tumor-related patterns, and classifying lesions, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and efficiency compared to traditional methods. For example, to recognize the region of interest, Tong et al. proposed to utilize deep learning-based image recognition program, the segment anything model (SAM), which comprised a Vision Transformer–based image encoder, a flexible prompt encoder, and a Transformer-based mask decoder. Compared to manual annotation, this approach significantly enhanced automation and accuracy in detecting low-concentration targets within WBI-integrated systems. It could eventually achieve ultra-low limit of detection (0.5 pM) by personal smartphones.75 Therefore, it demonstrated that AI can improve tumor detection in WBIs. Compared with traditional methods, AI enhances image analysis in wearable cancer diagnostics by automatically extracting relevant features, recognizing tumor-related patterns, classifying lesions, and improving diagnostic accuracy and efficiency.

In brief, the AI-integrated WBDs are revolutionizing cancer diagnosis. By introducing AI, WBDs not only significantly improve the accuracy of diagnosis, but also break through the technical bottleneck of imaging in complex anatomical regions, and enhance personalized diagnosis capabilities. However, it is important to address challenges such as privacy protection, high costs, and insufficient clinical validation.

Therapeutic effect monitoring

The development of personalized cancer therapy plans demands precise risk stratification and timely biophysical insights.96 Traditional monitoring approaches often rely on sporadic clinical observations and static risk models, potentially overlooking dynamic responses. Recent advances in WBDs enable continuously monitoring of bio-signals, such as physical activity and heart rate during pre- and post-operative phases.

To evaluate the effects of a wearable device intervention on the recovery, Lee et al. developed a mechanical-based WBS to dynamically monitor changes in cardiopulmonary function, physical activity, and health-related quality of life (HRQOL).81 Experimental results showed that lung cancer surgery patients in a perioperative exercise program with wearable activity monitoring had better postoperative physical activity and patient-reported outcomes than those receiving standard care. Moreover, to classify the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) during therapy, Ito et al. proposed a mechanical-based WBS to dynamically monitor sum and mean of metabolic equivalent tasks, walked distance, and step count, thereby enhancing personalized therapy planning.82 These devices enabled clinicians to make well-informed decisions, optimize surgical planning, and enhance therapy outcomes.97 Additionally, for clinical deterioration detection in post-abdominal cancer surgery patients, Stam et al. proposed a mechanical-based WBS to dynamically monitor heart rate and respiratory rate.83 Therefore, this system could detect deteriorations without requiring manual spot check measurements.

To predict the drug efficacy, Abramson et al. introduced an electrochemical-based WBS to dynamically monitor tumor volumes.84 This system was a commercially scalable wearable electronic strain sensor designed to automate in vivo testing of cancer therapeutics by continuously monitoring micrometer-scale changes in subcutaneously implanted tumors in real time. In vivo experiments demonstrated that the sensor successfully identified volume changes of tumor after drug therapy, and its recognition accuracy was verified by histological analysis.

Moreover, to guarantee the stability of the detection response along the entire proton therapy irradiation session, Fratelli et al. developed an optical-based WBS to dynamically monitor the radiation during therapy, which offering a tissue-equivalent and versatile solution for in situ radiation tracking in cancer therapy.86 This system was designed through a proper functionalization of the polysiloxane-based scintillator, enabling it to effectively target and detect different kinds of ionizing radiation. This work demonstrated how organic indirect detectors can be considered a universal radiation detecting platform able to monitor in real time and in situ the dose absorbed by patients during cancer treatments under different kinds of radiation. For tumor progression monitoring, Siboro et al. introduced a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU)-hafnium oxide (HfO2) film to dynamically monitor electrical impedance in thermoplastic polyurethane films.79 The system was designed as a TPU film embedded with HfO2 nanoparticles (HfO2 NPs), functioning as a dielectric elastomer (DE) strain sensor via electrical impedance changes. It could monitor tumor growth and regression by detecting electrical impedance variations.

To summarize, WBDs play a vital role in personalized therapeutic effect monitoring, demonstrating extensive clinical applicability across multiple domains including preoperative activity monitoring, end-of-life care, and radiotherapy dose tracking. However, current WBDs still face several limitations, such as sensor calibration issues in real-world conditions, limited battery life for long-term monitoring, and data privacy concerns. Therefore, by improving the capability of real-time data collection and analysis, WBDs not only improve the scientific nature of clinical decision-making, but also enhance self-management capabilities of patients.

On-demand drug delivery

WBDs are increasingly integrated with drug delivery modules to achieve precise, controlled, and responsive cancer therapy. These devices can monitor biophysical signals or tumor microenvironmental changes, subsequently initiating stimulus-responsive on-demand drug delivery mechanisms.

For improving drug delivery efficiency, Xue et al. developed the flexible ultrasonic microneedle patch (wf-UMP) as a convenient, efficient and minimally invasive cancer therapy platform.17 The wf-UMP combined acoustic emission, acoustic coupling, hydrogel elastomers and piezoelectric NPs to enable painless drug delivery, ROS generation and anticancer therapy. Experimental results showed that the wf-UMP significantly inhibited tumor growth and recurrence by inducing tumor cell apoptosis, increasing oxidative stress, regulating immune cell proliferation and combining with anti-PD1 immunotherapy, providing a novel non-invasive on-demand drug delivery method for cancer therapy. Moreover, for advancing deep-seated tumor therapy, Wang et al. proposed a self-powered microneedle patch with an integrated flexible triboelectric nanogenerator (F-TENG).90 Fabricated from water-soluble materials and pH-responsive NPs, it harvested biomechanical motion to provide energy for drug release in acidic tumor tissues. By leveraging the strengths of both microneedles and F-TENG, it enabled efficient deep drug delivery.

Taken together, WBDs enable precise, responsive, and on-demand drug delivery by sensing biophysical signals and tumor microenvironment changes. They offer minimally invasive and timely therapy with enhanced targeting and reduced side effects, showing great potential in tumor therapy. However, there are some challenges in bio-compatibility and user comfort concerns.

On-site physical therapy

In recent years, on-site physical therapy approaches for tumor have attracted widespread attention for their non-invasiveness, controllability and strong targeting. They regulate the tumor microenvironment through external physical effects, effectively killing tumor cells without damaging normal cells.

For realizing tumor treatment in daily life, Li et al. introduced a magnetic and temperature based WBS for personalized breast cancer therapy.88 This platform utilized TAT-modified ferroelectric BiFeO3 (TAT-BFO) NPs, which were magnetically guided to tumor sites and subsequently activated by thermal fluctuations to release reactive oxygen species (ROS). It was demonstrated to inhibit tumor growth and prevent tumor recurrence significantly. To realize the cancer therapy that capable of producing multiple responses to light and electricity without interfering with daily activities, Ju et al. introduced skin-mountable electrostimulation-augmented photothermal patch (eT-patch) comprising transparent ionic gel with MXene (Ti3C2Tx) doping for skin tumor therapy.80 The designed eT-patch had superior photothermal and electrical characteristics owing to ionic gels doped with MXene which provided high photothermal conversion efficiency and electrical conductivity as a medium. Under hydrophilic polyethersulfone (PES) treatment, it can synergistically trigger cancer cell apoptosis and pyroptosis, which together lead to the death of melanoma cells. To address issues that the miniaturization of ultrasonic probe, system integration of device, and the strategy of continuous treatment are, Zou et al. proposed the multiplexed ultrasonic patch array (CWUS Patch) for continuous sonodynamic therapy in breast cancer.78 The system enabled dynamic control of the ultrasound patches and allows real-time adjustments to optimize their performance in various applications. It can precisely regulate the activation of ROS generation and dynamic adjustment of therapy strategies. For improving the ROS generation ability is highly desirable for cancer therapy, Yao et al. introduced a wearable self-driven catalytic therapy system (TENG-CatSystem).95 The TENG-CatSystem was mainly composed of three elements: a human self-driven triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) as the electric field stimulation, a nanozyme comprising a 1D ferriporphyrin covalent organic framework coated on a carbon nanotube (COF-CNT) to generate ROS, and a COF-CNT-embedded conductive hydrogel that can be injected into the tumor tissues to increase local accumulation of COF-CNT and decrease the electrical impedances of tissues. It enhanced cancer therapy with high ROS efficiency and enables self-driven at-home treatment.

To eliminate CTCs in the blood, Li et al. proposed a wearable flexible devices for CTC capture and death.91 The flexible device used a magnetic origami membrane to capture Fe3O4@Au NPs bound CTCs and an integrated AlGaAs micro-LED for localized 48°C photothermal stimulation, killing CTCs within 10 minutes with more than 72% capture efficiency. For imaging-guided photothermal tumor therapy, Hao et al. introduced a spatiotemporal proximity-enhanced biocatalytic cascade system is introduced by encasing enzymes within metal-organic frameworks (MOFs).92 By successfully integrating the MOFs into wearable patch, the patch achieved non-invasive photoacoustic imaging guided photothermal tumor therapy. For avoiding the risk of drug-related side effects, Li et al. developed a pulsed direct-current (DC) generated from a small size fabric DC triboelectric nanogenerator (DC-TENG).80 This system was generated by coupling triboelectrification effect and electrostatic breakdown effect. It demonstrated excellent tumor suppressive ability and significantly prolongs the survival period of the 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. To improve the success ratio of macrophage-based adoptive cell therapy, Xue et al. introduced an adoptive immunotherapy strategy combining engineered macrophages with a wearable warming device.95 This device was adaptable for human patients and can be remotely controlled by a smartphone. It can provide precise and controllable thermal stimulation to the tumor microenvironment, enhancing local immune therapy responses while avoiding systemic side effects. To realize conformable tumor treatment, Sun et al. developed a flexible skin patch fabricated through a facile preparation method.89 The developed skin patch would provide a promising noninvasive approach for tumor therapies by direct tumor destruction and maintenance of the antitumor immune response. For addressing the problem that the absence of an efficient transdermal delivery modality, Yang et al. developed a stretchable electronic patch equipped with porous microneedles.92 This patch incorporated an MXene heater that maintains stable temperatures when subjected to tensile deformations. It demonstrated substantial efficacy in inhibiting the growth of subcutaneous tumors.

Overall, WBDs are increasingly recognized as a transformative paradigm in localized tumor therapy. These devices, based on external physical stimulation and combined with flexible design and intelligent feedback mechanism, achieve precise regulation of tumors and personalized therapy response. Compared with traditional methods, these WBDs have the advantages of non-invasiveness, highly controllable, and real-time. However, challenges still exist in terms of limited penetration depth, bio-compatibility, and complex integration of multifunctional components.

Enhancing cancer therapy via AI-integrated WBDs

AI-integrated WBDs are showing great potential for cancer therapy, particularly in postoperative complication prediction, biochemical indicator monitoring, and mortality risk assessment. AI-integrated WBDs enhance the dynamic therapy and precise response in individual risk assessment and real-time monitoring. Compared with traditional methods, AI-integrated WBDs achieve higher prediction accuracy, more timely risk identification, and stronger individualized intervention, bringing a more intelligent and precise improvements to cancer therapy.

For bio-signal based monitoring, in view of the complexity of human sweat, which makes the interpretation of surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) spectra complicated, Chen et al. introduced a hydrogel based WBS integrated with SERS and ML, such as light gradient boosting machine, gaussian naive bayes, linear discriminant analysis, RF, and SVMs.24 By training on features extracted from the SERS spectra, it could achieve 89.7% lung cancer progression classification accuracy. Besides, to evaluate the utility of wearable telemonitoring technologies to predict therapy outcomes using patient activity metrics and ML, Cos et al. developed a ML based mechanical-based WBS to process preoperative activity data together with clinical features.34 They applied feature engineering techniques to extract statistical, semantic, and biobehavioral rhythmic features, respectively. For time-series features, they utilized a developed feature extraction component to transform time-series data streams into circadian behavioral rhythm-related features, such as stability, variability, and amplitude. To avoid overfitting, they performed various ML models, including RF, gradient boosted trees (GBT), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), SVMs, and logistic regression (LR), to predict outcomes. Considering that existing survival prediction tools have difficulty identifying end-of-life processes and emergencies in advanced cancer patients, Liu et al. used ML to train on wearable-collected whole-body biophysical data from a mechanical-based WBS to predict 7-day mortality.35 The system extracted demographic features and wearable device features, and achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.96 and identified key predictors. Additionally, considering that existing methods for predicting heart failure progression have limited accuracy and mostly rely on invasive methods, Stehlik et al. proposed an electrical-based WBS, the chest-mounted wearable patch, combined with ML to predict heart failure readmission in cancer patients.85 By continuously monitoring physiological signals, the system predicted readmissions on average 6.5 days in advance, achieving 88% sensitivity and 85% specificity.

Besides, for cancer treatment-related cardiotoxicity, hidden symptoms, poor outpatient detection, inaccurate self-reporting, and physician overload often delay recognition and intervention, CardioAI, a multimodal AI system based on large language model, was developed.33 It integrated wearable biophysical data (e.g., heart rate and chest motion) and voice-collected symptom reports collected from an optical, chemical and temperature based WBS, enabling personalized and continuous monitoring beyond clinical settings. To identify complications in hematologic malignancies, Jacobsen et al. proposed a DL based SCC-Score system that processes whole body data from WBDs, including vital signs and physical activity.57 In the system, they trained a self-supervised contrastive learning objective to extract time series features that are typical in regular periods.

Taken together, these studies demonstrate the growing influence of AI-integrated WBDs in cancer therapy. These devices support early intervention and optimize therapeutic decisions by offering real-time and individualized insights into bio-signal changes, thereby improving overall patient outcomes. It should be noted that these AI algorithms rely on high-quality and diverse data. In addition, the generalization ability and clinical validation of these AI algorithms still need to be improved.

Summary

With their characteristics of miniaturization, long-term energy supply, high sensitivity, and conformal wearing, WBDs overcome the limitations of traditional cancer screening, diagnosis, and therapy strategies that rely on operational experience, bulky equipment, systemic drug circulation, and uneven distribution of medical resources. Furthermore, AI has been widely integrated into WBDs to address the problem of complex and substantial signals generated by real-time and long-term monitoring as well as patient-specific bio-signal variations. Although WBDs have made tremendous progress in cancer management, there are still some challenges to fully realize the potential of WBDs, such as limited SNR caused by improper device placement and environmental interference, patient data privacy concerns during AI training, and false positives caused by high sensitivity.

Improving the limited SNR of WBDs

Excellent SNR is the basis for ensuring the availability of WBDs. The SNR of WBDs is closely tied to sensor performance, adhesion, body movement and sweating.54,98, 99, 100, 101 The primary challenge to improve SNR lies in enhancing sensing and sampling performance to achieve stable continuous measurements.102,103 A development direction is the fabrication of advanced sensing and sampling units, such as wearable nano-sensors.104,105 Furthermore, optimizing both power management modules and data processing algorithms can also improve sensor performance, such as self-power technology and AI technology.106, 107, 108 In addition, the reduction of adhesion between WBDs and the skin can result in severe signal distortion and noise, which is particularly evident in wearable ultrasound patches. Therefore, the development researches may focus on bio-adhesive materials and adaptive structural designs to enhance continuous monitoring with high SNR, such as hydrogel and wearable bioelectronic mask.102,109 Overall, the signal quality of WBDs will be further improved in the future by optimizing sensor performance, adhesion, and data processing algorithms.

Protecting medical data privacy

The privacy protection of medical data from WBDs, including biophysical parameters and genetic information, faces potential challenges.110,111 Information leakage often occurs during communication as well as during AI training and inference, leading to eavesdropping, hijacking, and masquerading attacks. To mitigate these risks, medical data is commonly anonymized or pseudonymized on the source device before being transmitted to servers. However, it has been proven that anonymization is inadequate to effectively defend against re-identification attacks.112 To meet the challenge, on the one hand, it is imperative to strengthen the implementation and advancement of full-link encryption protocols, which should be further enhanced to mitigate the risk of data interception during transmission. On the other hand, the development of distributed AI processing architectures like edge computing to analyze signals locally at the device to avoid uploading raw data to the cloud. Therefore, WBDs will be able to furnish patients with more reliable and secure clinical insights in the future by improving the protection of medical data.

Reducing false positives caused by high sensitivity

The false positive refers to the detection of false positive results in negative individuals, leading to unnecessary further intervention.113,114 While WBDs have significantly enhanced the cancer detection rates with their high sensitivity, this elevated rate is partly due to its low-threshold alarm settings and algorithm bias.114 To solve this problem, on the one hand, it is crucial to develop WBDs with adaptive threshold adjustment capabilities tailored to the unique biophysical characteristics of individual patients.115,116 On the other hand, to achieve accurate and reliable responses to abnormal signals, AI models can be developed based on extensive data derived from WBDs.24,117 Overall, tackling the issue of false positives in WBDs is crucial for curbing unnecessary medical interventions and achieving proactive medicine.

We believe that with the development of AI-integrated WBDs, their application in cancer screening, diagnosis, and therapy will achieve further breakthroughs.
