Chloroplast and Mitochondrial Genomes of the Lichen-Symbiotic Green Alga Trebouxia Illuminate Evolutionary Relationships and Climate Associations and Yield New Phylogenetic Markers
Authors: Edgar L Y Wong, Jürgen Otte, Imke Schmitt, Daniel Sloan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe green-algal genus Trebouxia (Trebouxiophyceae, Chlorophyta) is the most common photosynthetic symbiont of lichens, displaying high phylogenetic diversity, and worldwide distribution across all climate zones. These single-celled terrestrial algae are valuable systems to study diversification, environmental adaptation and species interactions, yet genomic resources remain limited. We present over 30 new chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of Trebouxia species, extracted from PacBio metagenomes of diverse Umbilicaria lichens from multiple climate zones. The genomes represent previously identified operational taxonomic units (OTUs) Trebouxia jamesii (A03), T. sp. (A04), T. incrustata (A06), T. vagua (A10), T. sp. (S02), T. sp. (S03), T. sp. (S04), T. suecica (S05), T. sp. (S08), T. angustilobata (S09), T. simplex (S10), T. sp. (S20) and T. barrenoae (S28); a newly designated OTU T. sp. (A57), and several Single-Occurrence Sequences (SOS) from clades A, I, and S. Up to four Trebouxia OTUs were found within a single thallus. Organelle genomes vary considerably in size and structure. The consensus phylogenies from chloroplast (77 genes) and mitochondrial (32 genes) genes are largely congruent with the nuclear ITS tree, differing mainly in the derived clade S sections. All genes are under purifying selection, with mitochondrial genes exhibiting higher nucleotide diversity and hence phylogenetic resolution than chloroplast genes. Certain gene and protein features correlate with temperature variability, and some (such as GC content, arginine, and valine content) mirror findings in mycobiont nuclear genomes from the same samples and highlight shared signatures of environmental adaptation. We designed primers for new, variable phylogenetic markers, including chloroplast genes ftsH and rpoC1, and mitochondrial genes ATP1, ATP6, and ND6. Overall, this study advances our understanding of organelle genome evolution in Trebouxia and provides valuable resources for future ecological and evolutionary research.Keywords: adaptation, organelle, photobiont, phylogenomics, selection, symbiont

The green algal genus Trebouxia is the most diverse lichen photobiont, yet its evolution and phylogenetic diversification remain poorly understood due to the limited availability of genetic markers and genomes. We address this gap by generating over 30 complete chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes, and developing novel phylogenetic markers, substantially enhancing the support of the phylogenetic tree, and enriching the genomic resources for this key symbiotic partner. We also revealed associations between climatic variables and gene/protein features, providing insights into the evolution of Trebouxia, and pointing out parallels in genome evolution in the associated lichen mycobionts.

Introduction

Trebouxia is the most common (Muggia et al. 2020; Moya et al. 2021; Tagirdzhanova et al. 2026) and one of the largest genera of lichen photobionts with 187 identified operational taxonomic units (OTUs) to date (https://trebouxia.net/, accessed January 07, 2026). Trebouxia is an important study subject not only for symbiosis biology, but also for research on environmental adaptation, as its members can be found worldwide and in most terrestrial climate zones. The genus features only around 30 formally described species and a huge, incompletely explored phylogenetic diversity (Muggia et al. 2020). Trebouxia species have been documented to associate with more than 7,000 fungal species (Nelsen et al. 2021). They also persist in the environment as free-living organisms (Veselá et al. 2024).

Genetic and morphological analyses have identified five major phylogenetic clades (A, C, D, I, and S) within Trebouxia, primarily using the nuclear ITS rDNA (hereafter nrITS), chloroplast rbcL, and mitochondrial COX2 genes (Muggia et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2020) (Table 1), alongside chloroplast ultrastructure (Bordenave et al. 2022). While the five major clades are consistently recovered, their branch support is sometimes weak (60% to 85%), and many relationships within clades remain unresolved due to numerous poorly supported nodes (Muggia et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2020; Kantnerová and Škaloud 2025).

The Trebouxia research community uses alphanumeric codes—each starting with the clade letter, eg “S09”—as unique identifiers for species or operational taxonomic units (OTUs) within these five clades. The alphanumeric codes represent putative species and are assigned based on a curated nrITS reference data base (https://trebouxia.net/). This procedure is followed because most Trebouxia species are known only from their nrITS sequences, and the absence of pure cultures prevents formal species descriptions (Muggia et al. 2020).

Phylogenetic markers currently used in Trebouxia phylogenetics and species delimitation have several limitations. The widely used nrITS region is challenging to align, particularly among distantly related species (Medeiros et al. 2021). Length polymorphisms, insertions, deletions, and high sequence variability lead to ambiguous alignment regions, which must be excluded from the analysis. Other markers, such as rbcL, are highly conserved within Trebouxia, providing limited phylogenetic resolution (Muggia et al. 2014; Chiva et al. 2025).

Further complications arise when a single lichen individual contains multiple Trebouxia species, a common phenomenon in many lichens, termed “photobiont plurality” (Dědková et al. 2025). If multiple phylogenetic markers are amplified from such individuals, the resulting sequences might stem from different Trebouxia species, confounding phylogenetic estimates. The use of multi-copy markers (such as nrITS) further intensifies this issue as it is difficult to distinguish whether multiple unique nrITS sequences from the same lichen thallus stem from different Trebouxia species or merely different copies of the locus. These obstacles point to the need for new markers that are highly variable, single copy, easy to align, and easy to amplify across distantly related taxa. Markers with these properties are single-copy protein-coding genes, which could potentially provide higher phylogenetic support and higher resolution to differentiate species, OTUs, or haplotypes and detect multiple taxa within the same thallus.

Despite challenges in resolving phylogenetic relationships, recent advances have been made to shed light on genome content and the evolutionary history of Trebouxia. The first published Trebouxia genomes include the nuclear genome of a strain isolated from Usnea hakonensis (Kono et al. 2017); mitochondrial (Martínez-Alberola et al. 2019), chloroplast (Martínez-Alberola et al. 2020), and nuclear genomes (Barreno et al. 2022) of the culture of Trebouxia lynnae (A39); and all three genomes from a metagenomic sample of Umbilicaria pustulata (Tzovaras et al. 2020). More recently, the following genomes were published: a chromosome-level nuclear genome from clade A using Nanopore PromethION (Tagirdzhanova et al. 2026) and six high-quality nuclear genomes, generated using PacBio from clades A and S (Poquita-Du et al. 2024; Puginier et al. 2024). Using these and other green algal genomes, Puginier et al. (2024) discovered a carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme), glycoside hydrolase 8 (GH8), which horizontally transferred from mycobionts to Trebouxia. This CAZyme GH8 was proposed to have a key role in symbiotic interactions, as Trebouxia may use it to degrade the polysaccharides in the cell walls of mycobionts (Puginier et al. 2024). Other horizontal gene transfers have also been documented. For example, three genes were reported to have transferred from fungi to Trebouxia, likely before the origin of lichenization (tellurite-resistance dicarboxylate transporter family protein, class-1 nitrilase/cyanide hydratase, and oxidoreductase/retinol dehydrogenase) (Beck et al. 2015).

Trebouxia's worldwide distribution in highly divergent habitats suggests the presence of environmental specialization within the genus. Indeed, some species prefer cold and wet, others warm and dry climate niches, and some appear to be climate generalists (Rolshausen et al. 2018). Along elevation gradients we often observe turnovers in Trebouxia communities (Vargas Castillo and Beck 2012; Dal Grande et al. 2018; Rolshausen et al. 2020; Kosecka et al. 2022). Sometimes, certain specialist species are restricted to specific elevation ranges, while generalists are found across an elevational spectrum (Dal Grande et al. 2017; Medeiros et al. 2021). It has been hypothesized that habitat preferences of Trebouxia species facilitate the evolution (such as niche expansion) of lichen-forming fungi (Fernández-Mendoza et al. 2011; Rolshausen et al. 2018, 2020). Several studies have focused on local adaptation and habitat preferences of the genus, which are thought to play a significant role in partner selection among symbionts (eg Yahr et al. 2006; Peksa and Škaloud 2011; Leavitt et al. 2015; Nelsen et al. 2021). For instance, some Trebouxia species display preferences for temperature and irradiance (Del Campo et al. 2010b; Casano et al. 2011), and clade C species prefer hot and wet climates (Nelsen et al. 2021). Macroclimatic variables, such as annual precipitation, are key determinants of species ranges (Werth and Sork 2014; Lutsak et al. 2016; Dal Grande et al. 2017; Singh et al. 2017; Medeiros et al. 2021; Nelsen et al. 2021). Distinct bioclimatic profiles have also been observed for species occupying humid temperate and polar regions (Lutsak et al. 2016) and Mediterranean biomes (Muggia et al. 2010). While some species have restricted ranges, many inhabit multiple biomes or climate zones (Dal Grande et al. 2017; Rolshausen et al. 2020; Medeiros et al. 2021; Wong et al. 2024). In addition, substrate properties, including chemistry (such as rock pH) and the identity of phorophyte species, have been reported to influence Trebouxia occurrence (Werth and Sork 2014; Peksa et al. 2022), although phorophyte species do not appear to affect mycobiont occurrence (Werth and Sork 2014).

On top of habitat specialization, Trebouxia exhibits variable selectivity in mycobiont associations. Some mycobiont species associate exclusively with a single Trebouxia species (eg Rolshausen et al. 2020; this study), whereas others associate with many different Trebouxia species, eg certain species of Parmelia (Ossowska et al. 2024), Umbilicaria (Sadowska-Deś et al. 2014; this study), Cetraria (Fernández-Mendoza et al. 2011), Tephromela (Muggia et al. 2008), Evernia (Piercey-Normore 2006), Protoparmelia (Singh et al. 2017), Rhizoplaca (Leavitt et al. 2016), Lecanora (Blaha et al. 2006), or Ramalina (Casano et al. 2011). This flexibility in association and the wide distribution of certain Trebouxia species represent a crucial adaptive strategy for expanding the ecological niches of their fungal hosts (Casano et al. 2011; Muggia et al. 2014).

In this study, we present and analyze more than 30 Trebouxia chloroplast (cp) and mitochondrial (mt) genomes. We used the two publicly available cp and mt genomes—T. lynnae (A39) (Martínez-Alberola et al. 2019, 2020) and T. sp. ex. U. pustulata (Tzovaras et al. 2020)—as references to extract Trebouxia cp and mt genomes from PacBio metagenomes of field collected lichens from the genus Umbilicaria (Wong et al. 2025). Analyses of the nuclear genomes of the mycobionts of these Umbilicaria metagenomes have already been published, revealing certain genomic features that correlate with bioclimatic variables (Wong et al. 2025). Here, we aim to (i) elucidate the phylogenetic relationships of Trebouxia using cp and mt genes; (ii) assess the suitability of cp and mt genes for taxonomic classification of Trebouxia, compared to the nrITS marker; and (iii) understand genomic differences linked to climatic parameters.

Results

In this study, we investigated the organelle genomes of Trebouxia photobionts extracted from lichen metagenomes. We report new organelle genomes of Trebouxia species belonging to clades A and S and—for the first time—clade I.

Phylogenetic Relationships Among Trebouxia Taxa

A single chloroplast and a single mitochondrial genome, each with the full set of expected genes, were retrieved bioinformatically from most analyzed lichen metagenomes. The only exceptions were as follows: (i). T. sp. (Clade A) ex. U. freyi 2329, where the only contig with the full set of expected genes exceeded 1.3 Mb in length, thus deemed impossible to be the chloroplast genome but rather part of the nuclear genome (eg nuclear-embedded chloroplast sequences); (ii) T. suecica (S05) ex. U. crustulosa 2886 and ex. U. polyphylla 2894, in which no contigs with the expected gene set were detected; and (iii) T. sp. ex. U. crustulosa, in which four chloroplast and four mitochondrial genomes were extracted. Chloroplast genome sizes range from 170,614b to 431,263, and mitochondrial genome sizes range from 54,043b to 190,644b (Table S1, Fig. S1).

nrITS-based tree reconstructions revealed that eight genomes belong to clade A OTUs and 20 genomes belong to clade S OTUs (Fig. 1). Among clade A samples, there is one sample clustering with each of T. sp. (A04), T. incrustata (A06) and T. vagua (A10); and two samples cluster with T. jamesii (A03). One sample does not cluster with any known OTUs but have close matches (>99.5%) with two NCBI GenBank sequences (MW695120 and MW695186). With these three occurrences, a new OTU, A57, is designated to this taxon. The remaining two samples are considered Single-Occurrence Sequences (SOS), as their nrITS sequences did not match any NCBI GenBank sequences, and form an independent cluster in the tree (Fig. 1, Fig. S1). Only with detection of matching sequences in future studies would they be formally recognized as a new OTU. Among clade S samples, there is one sample clustering with each of T. sp. (S03), T. sp. (S04), T. simplex (S10) and T. sp. (S20); two samples cluster with each of T. sp. (S08) and T. barrenoae (S28); three samples cluster with T. sp. (S02); four samples cluster with each of T. suecica (S05) and T. angustilobata (S09); and one is treated as a SOS (Fig. 1, Fig. S1). Among metagenomes that did not yield any detected algal nrITS sequences, most cluster within the aforementioned OTUs, hence they are assigned as such. The only exception is T. sp. ex. U. phaea 1112, whose phylogenetic position is outside of clade A and S in both organelle consensus trees. This sample was collected in a population where other individuals contain Trebouxia OTUs from clade I (unpublished); hence this sample was determined to belong to clade I.

Nucleotide Diversity in Organelle Genes of Trebouxia and Phylogenetic Markers

Almost all mitochondrial genes have higher nucleotide diversity () in both clades A and S than the chloroplast genes and the most-commonly used marker—nrITS (see Introduction) (Fig. 3, Table S2 for gene length, number of polymorphic sites in samples, and π in each clade). Chloroplast genes commonly used in Trebouxia phylogenetic studies, including psbA, psbJ, and rbcL, have relatively low π compared to other genes in our dataset (Fig. 3). Based on the OTU assignments in this study and individual gene trees (see Data Availability), many genes can resolve phylogenetic relationships among the samples in this study, especially those genes with higher π than the nrITS locus (Fig. 3, Table S2).

Regarding the newly designed primers for five selected markers, all primer pairs worked on the 13 test samples (Fig. S9). All five loci (in almost all specimens) could be amplified in a single round of PCR at 50 °C (mt ATP6) or 52 °C (all other genes) annealing temperature. In the five single gene trees and the tree based on concatenated sequences, the three Trebouxia clades (A, I, and S) were well-resolved, with 80% to 98% bootstrap support (Fig. S9). The position of the three clades relative to one another varied, and the position of certain OTUs also varied within their respective clades (Fig. S9).

Genome Assembly and Genome Features

Although genome sizes vary greatly among samples, they are largely consistent within OTUs (Table S1, Fig. S2). This is most apparent in T. jamesii (A03), T. suecica (S05), T. angustilobata (S09) and T. sp. (S20). Among both chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes, T. simplex (S10) had the smallest genomes, followed by one unknown OTU extracted from U. crustulosa 2888. The largest chloroplast genome belongs to T. incrustata (A06), followed by one T. sp. (S02) genome ex. U. deusta 2335. The largest mitochondrial genome belongs to a clade I genome ex. U. phaea 1112, followed by T. sp. (S04). Some frameshifts in the genomes detected by MitoHiF also appear to be specific to OTUs studied—psbA in cp genomes of T. jamesii (A03) and orf107 in mt genomes of T. angustilobata (S09) (Table S1, Fig. S2). There is little synteny among the organelle genomes (Fig. S3 to S4). There are rearrangements even among genomes of the same OTU (Fig. S3 to S4).

Comparing overall repeat contents in organelle genomes, there are no clear patterns in the average number of different types of repeats (Table S3 to S5, Fig. S5). Clade A chloroplast genomes appear to have fewer tandem repeats per base than clade S genomes. Clade S mitochondrial genomes appear to have fewer microsatellites than clade A genomes, with mitochondrial genomes within T. jamesii (A03) having noticeably fewer microsatellites and tandem repeats than the rest of the genomes. Comparing specific repeat types and lengths (Table S3 to S5, Fig. S6 to S7), clade A genomes appear to have shorter microsatellites than clade S genomes in both chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes (Figs. S6a, S7a). Regarding other types of repeats, chloroplast genomes do not show obvious clade-related patterns (Fig. S6b, c); whereas in mitochondrial genomes, certain forward or palindromic repeats of certain size ranges seem to be more abundant in certain OTUs (Fig. S7c). We detected inverted repeats in five chloroplast genomes (Fig. S8) (excluding the previously published T. lynnae [A39]). These include one genome each from T. jamesii (A03), T. sp. (S02), T. sp. (S08), T. angustilobata (S09) and T. simplex (S10). There is no species or clade-specific inverted repeat. Only one sample has inverted repeats containing the rbcL gene (T. angustilobata ex. U. pustulata C0004 [S09]), which was reported in T. lynnae (A39) (Martínez-Alberola et al. 2020).

No inter-organelle gene transfers have been detected among the genomes studied.

Gene Loss in Organelle Genomes

As reported in Trebouxia before (Martínez-Alberola et al. 2020; Tzovaras et al. 2020), the chloroplast rps4 gene is absent from all Trebouxia chloroplast genomes in this study. The mitochondrial gene rpl14 is only present in the outgroup Symbiochloris sp. SG-2018, but absent from all Trebouxia genomes in this study, and other outgroups Myrmecia israelensis UTEX:1181 and Lobosphaera incisa SAG2468.

Selective Pressure and Features in Organelle Genes

All organelle genes have dN/dS less than 0.5 (Fig. S10a), suggesting that all genes are under strong purifying selection. In general, mitochondrial genes have more synonymous and non-synonymous mutations than chloroplast genes (Fig. S10b, c).

Similarity of Trebouxia genomes was assessed using a correspondence analysis of relative synonymous codon usage in the samples. Among chloroplast genes, all clade A genomes clustered together and are separate from clade S genomes (Fig. S11a). However, certain clade S samples were separated from the main cluster, especially on the second dimension, which accounts for 34.7% of variance (Fig. S11a). Among mitochondrial genes, all genomes of T. jamesii (A03) and two other genomes of clade A were separated from other clade A samples, whereas one genome of T. simplex (S10) was separated from remaining clade S samples (Fig. S11b).

Many chloroplast genes exhibited strong codon bias (effective number of codons [ENC] ≤ 35) in different genomes, but this does not appear to have a species-specific pattern (Fig. S12). Several chloroplast genes have strong codon bias in all genomes. These include atpB, atpH, psbD, and rbcL (Fig. S12). The only mitochondrial gene with strong codon bias is ATP9, and all mitochondrial genomes exhibited strong codon bias in this (and only this) gene.

Climate-Related Gene and Protein Features

To identify any climate-related evolutionary patterns, we checked for Pearson's correlations between temperature-associated bioclimatic variables and certain gene and protein features in all Trebouxia samples with bioclimatic data, including frequencies of nucleotides, codons and amino acids, GC content, GC or AT skew, and ENC. Before testing for correlation, collinear bioclimatic variables were grouped into PCs using PCA. This resulted in three PCs that explained at least 5% of variances, which were then used in correlation analyses (PCA results in Table S6 to S7). The first three PCs accounted for 61.3%, 24.1%, and 8.7% of variance, respectively (total 94.1%), in the chloroplast dataset as well as 59.3%, 25.4%, and 9.2% of variance, respectively (total 93.9%), in the mitochondrial dataset. Major contributors (>10%) to PC1 include four variables that describe temperature means or extrema: BIO1 (annual mean temperature), BIO5 (maximum temperature of warmest month), BIO10 (mean temperature of warmest quarter), and BIO11 (mean temperature of coldest quarter). Major contributors to PC2 include four variables that describe temperature variability or freezing probability: BIO2 (mean diurnal range), BIO4 (temperature seasonality), BIO6 (minimum temperature of coldest month), and BIO7 (temperature annual range). Major contributors to PC3 include four variables that describe temperature variability or mean: BIO3 (isothermality—BIO2/BIO7), BIO4 (temperature seasonality), and BIO8 (mean temperature of wettest quarter). The major contributors to PC1 and PC2 in this study are identical to the same PCs in Wong et al. (2025), which investigated the correlation between bioclimatic variables and mycobiont genomes.

The only correlation between PC1 (BIO1, BIO5, BIO10, BIO11) and gene or protein features is with ENC in chloroplast genes (which also correlated with PC2 [BIO2, BIO4, BIO6, BIO7]), as well as ENC and GC content of the first two codon positions (GC12) in mitochondrial genes (Fig. 4, Table S8, Fig. S13 to S14). Numerous other correlations in either type of organelle genes were between PC2 and PC3 (BIO3, BIO4, BIO8) (Fig. 4, Table S8, Fig. S13 to S13). Among these correlations with PC2 and PC3 are those involving arginine, valine, and GC12, which were reported for the mycobiont of the same lichen individuals in Wong et al. (2025) (Fig. 4).

Phylogenetic signal had been detected in climate-related evolutionary patterns in Trebouxia (Peksa and Škaloud 2011; Nelsen et al. 2022). Here, significant phylogenetic signal (Pagel's λ = 0.64 to 1.11) was also detected in the residuals of nearly all correlations, except for chloroplast asparagine percentage-PC3 and mitochondrial GC12-PC1 (Table S9), indicating strong phylogenetic conservation of codon usage and amino acid composition in both organelle genomes. However, after correcting for phylogenetic non-independence using phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS), significant between-species relationships were still detected for nearly half of the correlations (Table S9). Within-species effects were largely absent, with the only exception of mitochondrial GC12 (Table S9).

Discussion

Coexistence of Multiple Taxa Within a Single Thallus or Heteroplasmy?

It is not uncommon for multiple photobiont taxa to be detected in a single lichen individual (Del Campo et al. 2010a; Casano et al. 2011; Muggia et al. 2013, 2014; Catalá et al. 2016; Molins et al. 2018; Paul et al. 2018; Dědková et al. 2025). In one of the lichen metagenomes presented here (U. crustulosa 2888), four chloroplast and four mitochondrial Trebouxia genomes were extracted, which have different OTUs hence different phylogenetic positions. Coupled with the fact that we detected three distinct nrITS sequences in this sample (one from clade A and two from clade S) that matched the phylogenetic positions in both chloroplast and mitochondrial phylogenies, we conclude that there are indeed four distinct Trebouxia taxa in this sample.

Heteroplasmy (the presence of multiple genome variations within a single individual) is not uncommon in organisms such as plants (Wheeler et al. 2022; Khachaturyan et al. 2024) and can lead to heteroplasmic sorting (Schaffner and Patel 2022). However, this is not possible in this case, as nuclear genomes (where the nrITS is located) do not exhibit heteroplasmy. It is also unlikely that heteroplasmic organelles would be so strongly diverged that they belong to different phylogenetic clades. However, different copies of the nrITS region can diverge over time.

Phylogenetically Informative Markers for Trebouxia

Apart from the nrITS marker, there is currently a lack of highly variable loci that reliably resolve shallow branches and species-level relationships in Trebouxia phylogenies or that could act as barcodes in metabarcoding and eDNA studies. Although other loci have been used (Table 1 in Introduction), they all either lack variability, have limited available reference sequences (compared to nrITS), or provide limited phylogenetic resolution (such as cp rbcL; difficulties in aligning nrITS sequences and lack of support for major clades in the nrITS tree) (Muggia et al. 2014; Medeiros et al. 2021; this study). Most individual gene trees in this study (Supplementary material) showed congruence at the species level, although not at the sub-species level, which is consistent with previous studies (Del Campo et al. 2010b). This suggests that many organelle genes could be suitable for phylogenetic studies in Trebouxia. Among all genes analyzed, the five markers with newly designed primers in this study all provided high support for clades A, I, and S. All of them showed higher nucleotide diversity than nrITS, and four of them yielded phylogenetic trees with more highly supported nodes than the nrITS tree, suggesting that they are phylogenetically informative for both clade delimitation and can be used in conjunction with nrITS for species delimitation in the future.

Some genes, such as chloroplast ATP synthase subunit B and H (atpB, atpH), photosystem II D2 protein (psbD), ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase large subunit (rbcL), and mitochondrial ATP synthase subunit 9 (ATP9), showed strong codon bias (ENC ≤ 35), low nucleotide diversity, and low dN/dS, indicating they are highly conserved. We do not recommend these genes for phylogenetic studies and species assignment in Trebouxia. Overall, all organelle genes studied here have dN/dS < 1, similar to previous studies on other Chlorophyta (Hu et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022, 2023).

Lack of Synteny and Diagnostic Genome Architectural Features Within Species

The analyzed Trebouxia organelle genomes are highly similar in gene content but differ considerably in genome architecture. While some species exhibit consistent genome features, such as consistent genome size and specific frameshifts (orf107) in T. angustilobata (S09), most species show variable features at both the species and clade levels. There is generally little synteny, especially among chloroplast genomes, and high variation in genome size, frameshifts, repeat contents, abundance of inverted repeats, and codon usage. These echo other studies that also demonstrated variable genome structure in chloroplast genomes of Trebouxiophyceae and other orders in Chlorophyta (Pombert et al. 2006; Letsch and Lewis 2012; Turmel et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2025). No diagnostic features for clade A or S were observed in this study. Together with the observed incongruence in the mitochondrial phylogeny, a multi-marker approach along with hard-to-obtain morphological data (Muggia et al. 2020) may be necessary for resolving species relationships accurately.

Signatures of Climate Adaptation—The Importance of Environmental Variability

Identifying genomic signatures linked to climate contributes to our understanding of evolutionary mechanisms that underlie environmental adaptation and enables predictions of species responses to climate change (Waldvogel et al. 2020). We have previously studied climatic footprints in the nuclear genomes of lichen-forming fungi, which are strongly influenced by temperature variability and freezing probability (Wong et al. 2025). In the present study, we used the same metagenomes to trace climatic signatures in organelle genomes of Trebouxia symbionts. Similar to the genomes of their mycobiont hosts, Trebouxia genomes from the same lichen thalli show correlations with bioclimatic variables. Many genomic features in Trebouxia were correlated with bioclimatic PCs (groups of correlated bioclimatic variables), and significant relationships persisted after phylogenetic correction, indicating that these associations reflect genuine evolutionary responses to climate rather than artifacts of shared ancestry. Notably, arginine and valine content (or codons encoding these amino acids) correlated with bioclimatic PCs in both mycobiont nuclear genes (Wong et al. 2025) and Trebouxia organelle genes (this study). It has been shown that the genome-wide frequencies of amino acids may change in response to abiotic environmental parameters (Shen et al. 2024). For example, valine, a hydrophobic amino acid, may become more frequent in environments with low water content, likely leading to optimized protein structures under conditions of low hydration (Paul et al. 2008; Shen et al. 2024). The specific mechanisms underlying climate adaptation in lichen photobionts and mycobionts through variations in genome-wide frequencies of arginine and valine still need to be explored. Here we observe that overall arginine content is correlated with PC2 (BIO2 mean diurnal temperature range, BIO4 temperature seasonality, BIO6 minimum temperature of coldest month, BIO7 temperature annual range) in Trebouxia mitochondrial genes, but the direction of this effect differed from that in mycobiont nuclear genes (Wong et al. 2025). Valine content (overall or specific codon) correlated with PC2 also in mycobiont nuclear and Trebouxia chloroplast genes, but with PC3 (BIO3 isothermality, BIO4 temperature seasonality, BIO8 mean temperature of wettest quarter) only in Trebouxia chloroplast genes. These findings suggest that while similar bioclimatic variables may influence genome evolution all lichen symbionts, the effects differ by symbiont and organelle. This aligns with previous studies in other organisms, which have found no universal genomic signature of climatic conditions (eg Basak and Ghosh 2005; Lobry and Necşulea 2006).

GC content has been hypothesized to reflect temperature affinity (Saunders et al. 2003; Basak and Ghosh 2005; Lobry and Necşulea 2006; Liu et al. 2020) or stress (Tyagi et al. 2023). In our studies GC12 correlated with PC2 (BIO2 mean diurnal temperature range, BIO4 temperature seasonality, BIO6 minimum temperature of coldest month, BIO7 temperature annual range) in mycobiont nuclear genes, but PC1 (BIO1 annual mean temperature, BIO5 maximum temperature of warmest month, BIO10 mean temperature of warmest quarter, BIO11 mean temperature of coldest quarter) in Trebouxia mitochondrial genes. Unlike the earlier studies, which focused on temperature means or extrema, our results show that GC content can be affected by both temperature means or extremes (PC1—BIO1, BIO5, BIO10, BIO11), and temperature variability (PC2—BIO2, BIO4, BIO6, BIO7).

As with mycobiont nuclear genes (Wong et al. 2025), most correlations in Trebouxia organelle genes were linked to temperature variability rather than means or extremes. This underscores the importance of considering climate variability in ecological and evolutionary studies, as well as in conservation management. Populations with broader niche breadths are more likely to be buffered from climate change (Sheth and Angert 2014; Ikeda et al. 2017), and organisms exposed to varying degrees of environmental variability also exhibited heritable environmental tolerance and physiological flexibility (Colinet et al. 2015; Dowd et al. 2015; Woods et al. 2015; Diamond 2017; Dillon and Lozier 2019; Pincebourde and Casas 2019).

Summary

The genus Trebouxia is a hallmark taxon to study symbiosis, environmental adaptation, speciation, and diversification. This study marks a significant advance in our understanding of genomic diversity within Trebouxia, by presenting 32 chloroplast and 31 mitochondrial genomes. Our analyses demonstrate that many organelle genes offer higher variation and phylogenetic resolution than traditionally used phylogenetic markers. Like previous studies, we advocate for the use of more markers where feasible, to increase phylogenetic resolution. Importantly, we show that Trebouxia genomes, like those of their mycobiont hosts, exhibit signatures of adaptation to temperature variability rather than to temperature means or extremes. These observed correlations between genomic features (such as GC content, arginine, and valine content) and bioclimatic variables underline the dynamic responses of lichen symbionts and their organelles to surrounding environments. These findings also emphasize the importance of accounting for environmental variability in studies of adaptation, evolution, and conservation. By greatly expanding the genomic toolkit for Trebouxia, our findings enable more in-depth phylogenetic research and provide valuable insights into the genomic underpinnings of species resilience to global environmental changes.

A total of 34 chloroplast and 33 mitochondrial genomes of Trebouxia were analyzed in this study. Organelle Genome Assembly

All newly presented genomes were sequenced with PacBio, using either CLR or CCS technology, and subsequently assembled using flye v2.9.1 (Kolmogorov et al. 2020) (see Wong et al. 2025 for details). We used MitoHiFi v3.2.2 (Uliano-Silva et al. 2023) run via Singularity to extract chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes from the assembled contigs of lichen metagenomes or Trebouxia genomes and to annotate the genomes. The chloroplast genome of T. lynnae (A39) (GenBank accession: MK643158.1) and mitochondrial genome of Trebouxia sp. A1-2 (GenBank accession: MN642622.1) were used as reference genomes. The organism parameter (-a option) was set to plant. Genome size allowed (with regard to reference genome size) (−max-read-length) was set to 1.5. Minimum percentage match to reference (-p) was set to 65. MitoHiFi did not circularize any organelle genomes. Circlator v1.5.5 (Hunt et al. 2015) was then used to circularize the genomes using default settings and the lichen metagenome assemblies. In each metagenome, we typically detected one Trebouxia chloroplast and one Trebouxia mitochondrial genome, each in full length and with the expected gene set. An exception was the U. crustulosa TBG2888 metagenome, in which we detected four distinct Trebouxia chloroplast and four distinct Trebouxia mitochondrial genomes, all with the full expected gene set. All eight Trebouxia organelle genomes derived from U. crustulosa TBG2888 are included in subsequent analyses. Although not all genomes are circularized, they all contain the core set of genes, and phylogenies reconstructed from extracted organelle genes are largely congruent with the nrITS phylogeny. Hence, they are all used in subsequent analyses. Linear diagrams of all genomes were visualized using OGDRAW (assessed through https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html). Synteny among organelle genomes were assessed using MCScanX (Wang et al. 2012). Inverted repeats in the chloroplast genomes were detected using IR+ (assessed through https://irscope.shinyapps.io/IRplus/) (Díez Menendez et al. 2023).

Repeat contents in the organelle genomes were annotated using the following tools: MISA-web (assessed through http://misaweb.ipk-gatersleben.de/) (Beier et al. 2017), REPuter (assessed through https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/) (Kurtz et al. 2001), and Tandem Repeats Finder (assessed through https://tandem.bu.edu/trf) (Benson 1999). Potential inter-organelle gene transfers are assessed by aligning the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of each sample to one another using the LASTZ plugin (Harris 2007) in Geneious Prime v2024.0.7 (https://www.geneious.com). Sequence matches less than 95% or 100b in length were disregarded.

Phylogenetic Tree Reconstruction

Nuclear ITS rDNA Tree

To determine the species of Trebouxia genomes extracted, we extracted nrITS sequences of all genomes (except for T. lynnae [A39]) using ITSx (Bengtsson-Palme et al. 2013) using default settings. All sequences which were assigned to Chlorophyta, reference sequences of all recognized Trebouxia operational taxonomic units (OTUs) (accessed from https://trebouxia.net/, 10th April 2026), T. lynnae (A39) (GenBank accession: KU716051), and sequence of the outgroup Asterochloris glomerata (GenBank accession: PP834554.1) were then aligned to using MAFFT v7.525 (Katoh and Standley 2013) with the flag –adjustdirection. Ambiguous alignments were removed using trimAL v1.5.0 (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009) using the flag -automated1. A phylogenetic tree was built using IQ-TREE2 v2.6 (Minh et al. 2020) with the following settings: collapse near-zero branches (-czb), minimum split support threshold (-minsup) set to 0.5, multiple substitution models (-m) set to “TEST”, bootstrap replicates (-bb) set to 1000, and SH-like approximate likelihood ratio test replicates (-alrt) set to 1000. We assigned each taxon an alphanumeric code, based on the position of the nrITS sequence in the phylogenetic tree. Specimens that formed a supported monophyletic clade with known OTUs were assigned the same clade name and number, those that did not cluster with known OTUs were not assigned a number, and those that cluster with more than one known OTUs were treated as ambiguous.

Consensus Chloroplast and Mitochondrial Trees

Following organelle genome assembly and annotations, sequences of all organelle genes of all Trebouxia specimens (both newly-generated and publicly available on GenBank) were compiled. Gene sequences from all publicly available organelle genomes of species in the order Trebouxiales were extracted and used as outgroups, except Asterochloris sp. strain CNOR1 (mitochondria: OZ017764) and Asterochloris sp. (chloroplast: OP696657, mitochondria: OP696658), as no annotations are available. The outgroups used in this study include species that are lichen photobionts: Myrmecia israelensis strain UTEX:1181 (GenBank accessions—chloroplast: NC_025525, mitochondria: PP746563), Symbiochloris handae strain SAG:2150 (chloroplast: NC_025524), Symbiochloris sp. strain SG-2018 (chloroplast: NC_065389, mitochondria: NC_066038), as well as free-living species: Ettlia pseudoalveolaris strain UTEX:975 (chloroplast: NC_025532), Lobosphaera incisa (chloroplast: KM821265), L. incisa strain SAG 2007 (chloroplast: NC_025533), and L. incisa strain SAG 2468 (mitochondrial: NC_027060). All Trebouxia and outgroup gene sequences (except for tRNA genes) were aligned, trimmed of ambiguous alignments, and used to reconstruct gene trees following the same procedures outlined for the nrITS phylogeny. Subsequently, all gene tree files of each organelle were concatenated and used to reconstruct consensus species trees using default settings of ASTRAL v5.7.8 (Mirarab et al. 2014), with S. handae as outgroup.

For the chloroplast psbA gene, two samples were excluded from all analyses: T. sp. (S02) ex. U. deusta 2335 and ex. U. spodochroa 2324. This is because they have insufficiently annotated sequences in the selected gene region, where all other samples had annotated sequences. The mitochondrial COX1 gene was also excluded from the analyses due to high variability of the aligned sequences, likely caused by abundant introns.

Nucleotide Diversity and Selective Pressure

Nucleotide diversity of organelle genes and the nrITS region of Trebouxia samples within clade A and clade S was estimated using ProSeq4 (Filatov 2024), excluding all sites with missing data for organelle genes and ambiguous sites for nrITS (after trimal step). To assess the selective pressure on organelle genes, the number of non-synonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) mutations were estimated. Amino acid alignments created above were used to create codon alignments using PAL2NAL v14 (assessed through https://github.com/liaochenlanruo/PAL2NAL) (Suyama et al. 2006). The resulting codon alignments were then used to estimate dN and dS in each sample using SNAP v2.1.1 (assessed through https://www.hiv.lanl.gov/content/sequence/SNAP/SNAP.html) (Korber 2000). dN/dS was subsequently estimated for each sample and averaged across samples to obtain an overall gene estimate for our sample set.

Correlations Between Gene or Protein Features and Bioclimatic Variables

We studied the potential influence of climatic variables on gene and protein features focusing on three aspects: codon usage, amino acid usage, and GC content. All tRNA genes are excluded in the following analyses.

Protein translations of all organelle genomes were extracted from the GenBank files (output from MitoHiFi) using the package gbseqextractor (part of the MitoZ package) (Meng et al. 2019) or the package GffRead for samples with only gff3 files (Pertea and Pertea 2020). The package BioKIT (Steenwyk et al. 2022) was used to estimate frequencies of nucleotide bases in the gene sequences and amino acids in the protein translations (both using the character_frequency option), relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) (rscu option), and GC content for each of the three codon positions (gc1, gc2, and gc3 option). Correspondence analysis using RSCU of all samples was carried out using R (R Core Team 2021). Effective number of codons (ENC) of each gene was estimated and plotted against GC3 using the R package vhcub (Anwar et al. 2019). The relative contributions of mutations and natural selection to genes were then assessed, by comparing the resulting ENC-GC3 plot against the curve of expected codon usage that is based on the formula: ENC = 2 + GC3 + 29 / (GC32 + (1 − GC3)2. AT skew was estimated by (A% − T%) / (A% + T%), whereas GC skew was estimated by (G% − C%) / (G% + C%).

Eleven bioclimatic variables associated with temperature (BIO1 to BIO11) were extracted for each newly presented sample from WorldClim v2.1 (with a spatial resolution of 30 s/∼1 km2), which is a database of climatic data collected between 1970 and 2000 (Fick and Hijmans 2017) (same data as Wong et al. 2025; see this publication for reasons of excluding precipitation-associated variables). As many of these bioclimatic variables are correlated, a principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out to group correlated variables to the same principal component (PC). This is to avoid elevating the number of potential correlations with (seemingly) independent bioclimatic variables that actually describe similar climatic patterns. PCs that explain at least 5% of variance of data were retained (PC1 to PC3 in this case, see Results). For each selected PC, bioclimatic variables that contributed more than 10% were considered important factors. For PC1, these variables are BIO1, BIO5, BIO10, and BIO11. For PC2, these variables are BIO2, BIO4, BIO6, and BIO7. For PC3, these variables are BIO3, BIO4, and BIO8. Please refer to Results for details. Correlation between these three PCs and GC content (GC12 or GC3), amino acid usage, codon usage, or ENC, were tested. P-values were corrected for false discovery rate (FDR) for analysis with amino acid usage (N = 20) and codon usage (N = 60) separately. P-values below 0.05 were considered significant. For significant correlations, relationship between bioclimatic variables with >10% contribution to that PC and the target gene or protein feature was visualized as point graphs.

Phylogenetic signal in each gene or protein feature–PC correlation was quantified by estimating Pagel's λ on the residuals of ordinary least squares (OLS) regression between the feature and species mean of PCs using the phylosig() function in the R package phytools (Revell 2024). Pagel's λ ranges from 0 (no phylogenetic signal) to 1 (strong phylogenetic signal), with values exceeding 1 indicating greater phylogenetic clustering than predicted by Brownian motion alone. A likelihood ratio test against the null hypothesis of λ = 0 was used to assess whether phylogenetic signal was significant. Ultrametric trees used in the analysis was generated using the make_ultrametric.py script from OrthoFinder v2.5.5 (Emms and Kelly 2019), and minor deviations due to floating point rounding were corrected using force.ultrametric() in phytools.

To further assess the relationship between genomic features and climatic variables (PCs) while accounting for phylogenetic non-independence, we used phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) implemented in the nlme package in R (Pinheiro and Bates 2026), with a Brownian motion correlation structure (corBrownian) derived from the chloroplast or mitochondrial consensus trees. Since each sample was represented as a unique tip in the phylogeny, the phylogenetic covariance matrix encoded both between- and within-species relationships directly. To separate macro- and micro-evolutionary signals, each PC has a between-species component (species mean PC score, spec_mean_PC) and a within-species component (individual deviation from species mean, within_spec_PC). Both components were included as predictors in each PGLS model: gene/protein feature ∼ spec_mean_PC + within_spec_PC, allowing between- and within-species effects to be estimated independently.
