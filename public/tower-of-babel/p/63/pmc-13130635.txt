A metabolite-augmented FIB-4 machine learning panel achieves superior liver fibrosis staging in chronic liver disease
Authors: Yuanyuan Chen, Tianbiao Yang, Tianlu Chen, Huanming Xiao, Kejun Zhou, Keke Ding, Quan Liu, Mengci Li, Xufei Peng, Tao Sun, Xiaoning Wang, Ping Liu, Xin Deng, Zhenhua Zhang, Ka Zhang, Xianzhang Huang, Xiaoling Chi, Alice Pik-Shan Kong, Vincent Wai-Sun Wong, Wei Jia, Guoxiang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAccurate, non-invasive liver fibrosis detection is essential for chronic liver disease management, particularly with rising metabolic dysfunction-associated liver disease (MASLD) and chronic hepatitis B (CHB). While the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index is widely used, its performance for advanced fibrosis is limited. We develop Met-FIB using metabolomics and machine learning, integrating FIB-4 parameters (age, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and platelet count) with tyrosine and taurocholic acid identified in a CHB discovery cohort (n = 3,251). Validation includes one CHB cohort (n = 729) and two MASLD cohorts (n = 149, n = 155). Met-FIB outperforms FIB-4, FibroScan, and other serum markers across all fibrosis stages. In CHB, Met-FIB achieves 96.3% rule-out sensitivity and 85.4% rule-in specificity for significant fibrosis, with rule-in specificity reaching 98.6% and 98.8% for advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis. In MASLD, corresponding values are 93.9% and 90.2% for significant fibrosis, with >97.9% specificity for late-stage disease. Met-FIB demonstrates clinical utility for non-invasive fibrosis staging across diverse etiologies.Keywords: liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, enhanced FIB-4 panel, metabolomics, machine learning, tyrosine, taurocholic acid, chronic hepatitis B, metabolic dysfunction-associated liver disease

Introduction

Liver fibrosis, the progressive accumulation of extracellular matrix in response to chronic liver injury, represents a critical pathological pathway leading to cirrhosis and liver failure.1 Globally, the burden of liver fibrosis is increasing due to rising incidence of metabolic dysfunction-associated liver disease (MASLD),2 which affects approximately 38% of the global population and has become the most common chronic liver disease worldwide.3 The metabolic perturbations in MASLD create a pro-fibrogenic microenvironment that accelerates progression from steatosis to fibrosis.4 Liver fibrosis is particularly prevalent in China, where chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection affects approximately 87 million individuals—nearly one-third of the global HBV-infected population.5 Early and accurate detection of liver fibrosis is essential for timely intervention and prevention of irreversible disease progression.

While liver biopsy remains the gold standard for fibrosis assessment, its invasive nature, sampling variability, and inter-observer disagreement limit routine clinical application.6,7 Consequently, non-invasive diagnostic approaches have gained prominence, with the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index becoming the most widely adopted serum-based score.8 FIB-4, which integrates age, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and platelet count, has demonstrated certain utility across diverse liver disease etiologies.9,10 However, its performance remains suboptimal, particularly in intermediate fibrosis stages and for rule-in decisions—a critical diagnostic threshold that requires high specificity in order for clinicians to confirm advanced disease and provide necessary patient intervention.11,12 Building on our previous work developing a gradient boosting algorithm using clinical parameters that outperformed conventional scores in Chinese chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients,13 we hypothesized that incorporating metabolic biomarkers could further enhance diagnostic precision.

Metabolomics approaches have identified circulating biomarkers that reflect disease severity and progression in chronic liver disease.14,15,16,17,18 Our previous metabolomic profiling identified taurocholic acid (TCA) and tyrosine (Tyr) as particularly promising biomarkers, demonstrating strong associations with fibrosis progression in CHB patients independent of FIB-4 components.14 We hypothesized that integrating these metabolic biomarkers with FIB-4’s established clinical parameters could enhance diagnostic precision across the fibrosis spectrum by using Tabular Prior-data Fitted Network (TabPFN), a transformer-based approach particularly suited for small-to-moderate sample sizes that delivers robust performance without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning.19

Here, we report the development and validation of an enhanced FIB-4 panel (Met-FIB)—a six-marker diagnostic tool that augments FIB-4’s four components (age, AST, ALT, platelet count) with two metabolomic biomarkers (TCA and Tyr). Through systematic evaluation across 4,284 participants in four cohorts, including a prospective multicenter study, we demonstrate superior diagnostic performance compared to FIB-4 and other conventional non-invasive markers including AST-to-platelet ratio index (APRI), AST/ALT ratio, NAFLD Fibrosis Score (NFS), and BMI-AST/ALT Ratio-Diabetes (BARD) score across both MASLD and CHB patients, with particular improvements in rule-in specificity for advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis.

Results

The 3D partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) score plot of the discovery group demonstrated distinct clustering among controls and different fibrosis stages (Figure 2A). Identification of metabolite markers

A total of 265 metabolites in Cohort 1 was measured using our high-throughput metabolite array technology.20 This platform features absolute quantification with rigorous standardization and reproducibility. Metabolites were subjected to univariate analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test due to the non-normal distribution of the data. This analysis identified 39 differential metabolites, from which we cross-compared the top 20 metabolites derived from the intersection of Elastic Net and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) rankings to select the final metabolite markers (Figures 2B and 2C). Elastic Net and XGBoost leverage regularization techniques and ensemble learning methods, respectively, to effectively address multicollinearity and capture non-linear relationships, thereby facilitating feature selection.21,22 This comprehensive screening ultimately selected Tyr and TCA as the final diagnostic biomarkers.

To evaluate the independent associations between metabolite levels and liver fibrosis stages, we performed multivariable logistic regression analyses comparing the highest vs. lowest quartiles of metabolite concentrations, adjusting for potential confounders (Cohort 1: age, gender, BMI; Cohort 2: age, gender; Cohort 3: age, gender, BMI, diabetes status; Cohort 4: age, gender, BMI, diabetes status, and medications). Both metabolites demonstrated significant associations across fibrosis stages in CHB cohorts. In Cohort 1, Tyr odds ratios (ORs) ranged from 1.86 to 3.56 (all p < 0.001) and TCA from 9.50 to 15.64 (all p < 0.001) across fibrosis stages, with similar patterns observed in Cohort 2 (ORs ranged from 5.96 to 20.28 for Tyr and 8.44 to 32.98 for TCA across fibrosis stages, all p < 0.001). Significant associations were also observed in MASLD patients for specific fibrosis stages. In Cohort 3, TCA showed significant associations with significant fibrosis (OR = 3.21, p < 0.01) and cirrhosis (OR = 5.22, p < 0.05), while Tyr was associated with advanced fibrosis (OR = 1.25, p < 0.05). In Cohort 4, TCA demonstrated strong associations across all fibrosis stages (ORs: 5.51–19.16, p < 0.05 to p < 0.01) and Tyr was significantly associated with significant fibrosis (OR = 5.16, p < 0.01) (Table S3).

Development of Met-FIB and model selection strategy

Building on the validated utility of FIB-4 as a non-invasive marker for liver fibrosis, we developed Met-FIB, a comprehensive six-marker diagnostic panel by integrating FIB-4’s four components (AST, ALT, platelet count, and age) with two metabolite markers (Tyr and TCA), to enhance the precision and accuracy of fibrosis staging beyond a traditional approach.

To ensure robust model selection, we systematically compared five algorithms: TabPFN, Gradient Boosting, Random Forest, Categorical Boosting, and Logistic Regression in all validation cohorts, with results shown in Table S4. These algorithms were selected for their capability in binary classification tasks, which aligns with our objective of distinguishing fibrosis stages. TabPFN utilizes a transformer-based architecture with attention mechanisms to capture complex feature interactions through pre-learned priors.19 Gradient Boosting, Random Forest, and Categorical Boosting function as ensemble methods, leveraging sequential optimization, bootstrap aggregation, and ordered target encoding, respectively, to capture non-linear dependencies, while Logistic Regression serves as the linear baseline.22,23,24 While TabPFN performed inference using priors learned from millions of synthetic datasets, the other models were optimized using GridSearch Cross-Validation (GridSearchCV). Optimal hyperparameters are detailed in STAR Methods. As shown in Table S4, TabPFN consistently outperformed the other ensemble algorithms in all fibrosis staging tasks. All models were evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation in Cohort 1 and validated in Cohorts 2–4. In Cohort 1, Met-FIB demonstrated strong performance across all models (Figure S1).

TabPFN was selected as the optimal classifier in our study, exhibiting a superior balance of performance and translational feasibility. As shown in Figure 3, TabPFN achieved the closest proximity to the optimal performance zone (sensitivity and specificity ≥0.7). At Youden Index cutoffs, TabPFN demonstrated an optimal trade-off between sensitivity and specificity for advanced fibrosis (sensitivity: 0.745, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.627–0.855; specificity: 0.774 [0.728–0.823]) and maintained robust detection for cirrhosis (sensitivity: 0.857 [0.700–1.000]; specificity: 0.788 [0.747–0.829]) (Table S5). Shapley Additive Explanation analysis confirmed biological coherence, revealing that Tyr and TCA collectively contributed 24% to model predictions, comparable to FIB-4 components (76.0%). Notably, Tyr ranked among the top three predictors for significant fibrosis (20% importance), supporting the clinical value of integrating metabolic signatures with standard parameters.

Validation of Met-FIB performance

In Cohort 2, the biomarker patterns observed in the discovery cohort were successfully replicated (Table 1). Tyr concentrations increased from 71.02 μM in controls to 136.09 μM in cirrhosis patients (p < 0.001), while TCA levels rose from 7.72 ng/mL to 481.95 ng/mL (p < 0.001). As shown in Figure 4, Met-FIB demonstrated robust performance across different clinical scenarios. For distinguishing CHB patients from controls (model 1), Met-FIB achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.928 (95% CI: 0.909–0.945) and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) of 0.932 (95% CI: 0.912–0.949), with both AUROC and AUPRC significantly higher than individual biomarkers and FIB-4 alone (p < 0.001). For detecting significant fibrosis vs. early-stage fibrosis (model 2), Met-FIB demonstrated an AUROC of 0.669 (95% CI: 0.613–0.726) and AUPRC of 0.639 (95% CI: 0.565–0.713). Met-FIB achieved significantly higher precision performance compared to FIB-4 (AUPRC: 0.552, p = 0.033).

For advanced fibrosis identification (model 3), Met-FIB achieved an AUROC of 0.770 (95% CI: 0.688–0.851) and AUPRC of 0.599 (95% CI: 0.471–0.706), with significantly superior precision performance compared to individual biomarkers (AUPRC: 0.424, p = 0.046). Cirrhosis detection (model 4) showed an AUROC of 0.897 (95% CI: 0.836–0.952) and AUPRC of 0.524 (95% CI: 311–0.729), with Met-FIB demonstrating significantly higher precision performance than individual biomarkers (AUPRC: 0.239, p = 0.009). The enhanced AUPRC of Met-FIB across multiple fibrosis stage differentiation tasks indicates improved capability for identifying true-positive cases while minimizing false-positives, a characteristic relevant for clinical implementation.

To assess the impact of diabetes mellitus (DM) on Tyr and Met-FIB performance, subgroup analyses were performed according to diabetic status across the three validation cohorts (Cohorts 2–4). DM was defined according to international standards as meeting any of the following criteria: HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, or 2-h oral glucose tolerance test ≥11.1 mmol/L.25 As shown in Figure S2, we observed no statistically significant differences in Tyr levels between non-diabetic and diabetic fibrosis patients in any of the three cohorts (Cohort 2: p = 0.647; Cohort 3: p = 0.291; Cohort 4: p = 0.515). Furthermore, principal-component analysis score plots revealed a substantial overlap in the Met-FIB profiles across all three cohorts, indicating that Tyr and Met-FIB reflect hepatic pathology rather than capturing metabolic characteristics driven by DM.

Rule-in and rule-out performance vs. FIB-4 and FibroScan

Rule-out and rule-in thresholds were established for Met-FIB to optimize clinical decision-making across the spectrum of liver fibrosis stages. Comprehensive performance metrics, along with cutoff values and 95% confidence intervals, are detailed in Table S6. As visually shown in Figure 5, the diagnostic performance of Met-FIB is compared with FIB-4 and FibroScan. Rule-out sensitivity, rule-in specificity, and gray zone proportions are presented for each diagnostic endpoint. For significant fibrosis detection, Met-FIB showed strong rule-out performance in Cohort 2 with sensitivity of 96.3% (95% CI: 91.6%–98.4%) and rule-in specificity of 85.4% (95% CI: 80.0%–89.5%). In comparison, FIB-4 showed markedly lower rule-in specificity of 44.3%. In Cohort 3, Met-FIB maintained rule-out sensitivity of 93.9% (95% CI: 87.3%–97.2%) and rule-in specificity of 90.2% (95% CI: 79.0%–95.7%), outperforming FIB-4 in both metrics (rule-out sensitivity 84.7%, 95% CI: 76.3%–90.5%; rule-in specificity 64.7%, 95% CI: 51.0%–76.4%).

For advanced fibrosis detection, Met-FIB achieved excellent rule-in specificity across all validation cohorts: 98.6% (95% CI: 96.5%–99.5%) in Cohort 2, 100.0% (95% CI: 96.8%–100.0%) in Cohort 3, and 98.0% (95% CI: 93.1%–99.5%) in Cohort 4. Met-FIB consistently outperformed FIB-4 in rule-out sensitivity: 74.5% (95% CI: 61.7%–84.2%) vs. 60.0% (95% CI: 46.8%–71.9%) in Cohort 2, 35.5% (95% CI: 21.1%–53.1%) vs. 16.1% (95% CI: 7.1%–32.6%) in Cohort 3, and 61.8% (95% CI: 48.6%–73.5%) vs. 18.2% (95% CI: 10.2%–30.3%) in Cohort 4, with corresponding superior negative predictive value (NPV) in Cohort 2 (93.5%, 95% CI: 89.4%–96.1% vs. 90.8%, 95% CI: 86.4%–93.8%) and Cohort 4 (74.4%, 95% CI: 64.0%–82.6% vs. 69.0%, 95% CI: 61.0%–75.9%). Met-FIB also achieved superior positive predictive value (PPV) in Cohort 2 (84.6% vs. 66.7%, 95% CI: 45.4%–82.8%). Notably, while FIB-4 achieved 100% specificity and rule-in PPV in Cohorts 3 and 4, only 5 and 6 samples, respectively, met the rule-in criteria (≥3.25), limiting interpretation. FibroScan showed intermediate performance in Cohort 4 with sensitivity of 66.4% (95% CI: 57.0%–74.6%) and specificity of 84.8% (95% CI: 78.5%–89.5%). Detailed comparative performance metrics are presented in Figure S3.

For cirrhosis detection, Met-FIB demonstrated superior rule-in performance across cohorts. In Cohort 2, Met-FIB achieved specificity of 98.8% (95% CI: 96.9%–99.5%) with PPV of 66.7% (95% CI: 39.1%–86.2%), outperforming FIB-4 (specificity 96.3%, 95% CI: 93.7%–97.9%; PPV 42.9%, 95% CI: 24.5%–63.5%). In Cohort 3, Met-FIB maintained perfect specificity of 100.0% (95% CI: 97.3%–100.0%) and PPV of 100.0% (95% CI: 20.7%–100.0%), compared to FIB-4 (specificity 99.3%, 95% CI: 96.1%–99.9%; PPV 80.0%, 95% CI: 37.6%–96.4%). For rule-out performance in Cohort 4, Met-FIB showed higher sensitivity of 52.9% (95% CI: 31.0%–73.8%) vs. FIB-4’s 41.2% (95% CI: 21.6%–64.0%). FibroScan in Cohort 4 (cutoff ≥13.6 kPa) had 69.8% sensitivity and 87.7% specificity, showing lower specificity than Met-FIB.

Rule-in and rule-out performance vs. APRI, AST/ALT, NFS, and BARD score

Performance comparisons included only conventional markers with clinically established cutoffs; where stage-specific cutoffs were unavailable, cutoffs from earlier fibrosis stages were carried forward for reference. The comparative performance of Met-FIB against various conventional scores is illustrated in Figure 5 and detailed in Table S6. For significant fibrosis detection, Met-FIB consistently demonstrated superior rule-out sensitivity compared to APRI across all cohorts (90.1% vs. 88.1% in Cohort 1; 96.3% vs. 70.4% in Cohort 2; 93.9% vs. 50.0% in Cohort 3), which is particularly valuable for early-stage screening where sensitivity is paramount. In Cohort 3, NFS showed lower rule-out sensitivity (84.7% vs. 93.9%) with a substantially larger gray zone compared to Met-FIB.

For advanced fibrosis detection, APRI underperformed Met-FIB in all metrics including rule-out sensitivity, rule-in specificity, NPV, and PPV across Cohorts 1–4. Although APRI achieved 100% rule-in specificity in Cohort 4, this was based on only 2 samples meeting the threshold (≥1.5), limiting clinical interpretation. Met-FIB outperformed AST/ALT ratio in both rule-out sensitivity and rule-in specificity across all Cohorts 1–4, with particularly notable differences in rule-in specificity in CHB-etiology cohorts (97.3% vs. 49.0% in Cohort 1; 98.6% vs. 76.0% in Cohort 2), which is critical for late-stage diagnosis where high specificity is essential. In MASLD-etiology cohorts, Met-FIB demonstrated markedly superior rule-in specificity compared to BARD score: 100.0% vs. 50.8% in Cohort 3 and 98.0% vs. 57.0% in Cohort 4. While Met-FIB’s rule-in specificity was comparable to that of NFS, Met-FIB demonstrated a substantially smaller gray zone (18.1% vs. 47.7% in Cohort 3).

For cirrhosis detection, APRI and AST/ALT ratio showed lower performance than Met-FIB in all metrics across CHB cohorts. Notably, Met-FIB achieved substantially small gray zones. In MASLD Cohort 3, APRI, AST/ALT, NFS, and BARD score all exhibited lower rule-in specificity than Met-FIB, with their gray zones considerably exceeding that of Met-FIB (6.0%), particularly NFS with a gray zone of 47.7%. In Cohort 4, Met-FIB achieved exceptional rule-in specificity, outperforming AST/ALT, NFS, and BARD score.

Discussion

We developed Met-FIB, a six-marker panel augmenting FIB-4 with Tyr and TCA, to improve staging of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis. In the discovery phase, Met-FIB established robust clinical thresholds with high AUROC and AUPRC values. In the testing phase, which was conducted as a multicenter study, while the AUROC values showed improvement over FIB-4, the AUPRC metrics demonstrated statistically significant superiority. Unlike AUROC, which can overstate performance when one class is rare, AUPRC better reflects a model’s ability to correctly identify cases that are less common, such as cases of cirrhosis.26

The utilization of TabPFN represents a significant methodological advancement. This transformer-based model demonstrated superior balanced performance compared to four conventional machine learning approaches that underwent extensive hyperparameter optimization. While Logistic Regression exhibited competitive performance as a robust linear baseline, TabPFN was ultimately selected due to its balanced performance in validation Cohort 2 and its distinctive capability in capturing complex, non-linear feature-feature interactions. Furthermore, its transformer architecture enables effective pattern recognition without dataset-specific optimization, thereby offering enhanced translational feasibility for clinical practices.

Met-FIB demonstrated robust diagnostic performance across the spectrum of liver fibrosis progression. While detecting significant fibrosis remains a universal challenge often associated with indeterminate results, Met-FIB achieved high rule-out sensitivity (90.1%–96.3%) and rule-in specificity (85.4%–90.2%) for patients falling within the determinable range. However, Met-FIB’s distinct clinical utility is most evident in advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis. For these late-stage conditions, Met-FIB achieved specificities of 97.3%–100.0% with substantially higher PPVs than FIB-4. This enhanced rule-in capability reflects Met-FIB’s particular strength in confirming late-stage disease, where accurate staging is critical for clinical decision-making and where metabolic changes become more pronounced. Notably, in MASLD cohorts where FIB-4 performance deteriorated with critically low sensitivities (16.1%–18.2%), Met-FIB maintained a 2- to-3-fold higher sensitivity while preserving its hallmark high specificity. This balance suggests that Met-FIB is not only a precise rule-in tool but also safer than FIB-4 by reducing missed diagnoses.

Beyond outperforming FIB-4, Met-FIB consistently demonstrated superior rule-in specificity compared to conventional serum markers including APRI, AST/ALT ratio, NFS, and BARD score for late-stage fibrosis detection. While NFS has been widely validated in MASLD populations, Met-FIB demonstrated important advantages in our MASLD cohorts 3 and 4. For advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis, Met-FIB achieved comparable or superior rule-in specificity to NFS while substantially reducing the gray zone. In Cohort 3, Met-FIB’s gray zone was only 18.1% vs. 47.7% for advanced fibrosis and 6.0% vs. 47.7% for cirrhosis. Similar reductions were observed in Cohort 4, allowing more patients to be definitively classified without elastography or biopsy. Compared to BARD score, Met-FIB achieved approximately 2-fold higher rule-in specificity for advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis, reducing false-positive referrals and unnecessary invasive procedures. This combination of high specificity and narrow indeterminate range makes Met-FIB particularly suitable for risk stratification in MASLD populations.

The comparison between Met-FIB and FibroScan in the MASLD cohort (Cohort 4) revealed complementary diagnostic characteristics. Met-FIB demonstrated superior rule-in performance with higher specificities for both advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis detection, indicating that while elastography is an effective screening tool, Met-FIB serves as a superior “gatekeeper” to minimize false-positives generated by elastography. For early-stage disease detection, particularly distinguishing controls from early fibrosis, Met-FIB may also offer advantages as metabolic alterations can precede structural changes detectable by elastography. Additionally, the low rule-in PPV (40.0%) for cirrhosis detection with Met-FIB reflects the small sample size of only 17 cirrhotic patients in this cohort, creating statistical instability in PPV calculations that may lack statistical significance due to insufficient positive cases.

Our selected biomarkers hold biological significance beyond mere statistical correlation, reflecting key pathophysiological processes in liver disease progression. Tyr, present as L-tyrosine, is a semiessential aromatic amino acid essential for synthesizing thyroid hormones, catecholamines, melanin, and tyramine.27,28,29 In normal conditions, Tyr is metabolized in the liver through a pathway initiated by tyrosine aminotransferase in the cytosol.30 However, in chronic liver disease such as cirrhosis, the reduced activity of tyrosine aminotransferase results in hepatic accumulation of Tyr, leading to elevated blood levels.31,32 On the other hand, TCA, a primary bile acid, has been shown to accelerate liver cirrhosis progression by activating hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) by upregulating TLR4 and activating the S1PR2/p38 MAPK/YAP signaling pathways.33,34 Our previous research has also confirmed TCA’s role in stimulating HSC proliferation and activation via TGR5 and downstream signaling pathways like ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK.17 The increasing levels of these metabolite markers across fibrosis stages reflect the cumulative liver injury and the cycle of inflammation, fibrogenesis, and metabolic dysfunction characteristic of liver disease progression.

We previously demonstrated in two independent cohorts (n = 1,396) analyzing 98 metabolites that serum metabolites Tyr, TCA, valine, and linoelaidic acid are significantly associated with fibrosis severity and exhibit stratification capabilities for assessing chronic liver disease progression.14 The clinical application of Tyr and TCA measurements is supported by the recently validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry-based in vitro diagnostic kit approved by the National Medical Products Administration of China. This kit enables simultaneous quantification of Tyr and TCA with strong analytical performance, including robust linearity, high precision, and long-term stability suitable for routine clinical laboratory use.35 This study provides a reliable foundation for integrating these metabolic biomarkers with FIB-4 in clinical practice.

The rationale, and likely the success of augmenting FIB-4 with metabolomic biomarkers, stems from their ability to capture distinct yet complementary aspects of liver pathology. While FIB-4’s clinical parameters serve as surrogate markers reflecting liver inflammation and synthetic function, they do not directly capture the fibrotic burden or underlying metabolic changes. TCA and Tyr, by contrast, provide direct mechanistic insights into fibrogenesis pathways and hepatic metabolic dysfunction. While Tyr is known to be elevated in insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes,36,37 our findings show comparable Tyr levels and overlapping Met-FIB profiles between diabetic and non-diabetic fibrosis patients, indicating that Tyr and Met-FIB reflect the severity of hepatic fibrosis rather than being confounded by concurrent glycemic status. By integrating these metabolite markers with FIB-4’s established clinical parameters, our enhanced panel bridges the gap between indirect markers and direct metabolic assessment, explaining its superior performance particularly in late-stage disease detection where metabolic dysfunction becomes more pronounced. In conclusion, our enhanced FIB-4 panel demonstrates the clinical utility of integrating metabolic biomarkers with established clinical parameters for improved non-invasive fibrosis staging across diverse chronic liver disease etiologies.

This study has several strengths. First, the utilization of TabPFN as our machine learning framework represents a methodological advancement. Second, our systematic biomarker screening utilized a high-throughput metabolite array technology to assess 265 metabolites in a large discovery cohort (n = 3,251). This platform provides precise absolute quantification through strict standardization and reproducibility, delivering reliable data quality for large-scale clinical analyses. Based on this precise quantitative profiling, we selected TCA and Tyr as candidates. This aligns with our previous research,14 which validates both the biological significance and the robustness of our selection methodology. Third, the study’s validation across two distinct etiologies, CHB and MASLD-related fibrosis/cirrhosis, demonstrates Met-FIB’s broad clinical applicability.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations of our study. First, the exclusion of controls with metabolic abnormalities in the testing phase created an idealized reference group, which may lead to an overestimation of diagnostic performance compared to real-world populations where metabolic comorbidities are prevalent. Second, Met-FIB exhibited gray zones in detecting significant fibrosis, a common challenge with dual-threshold methods, especially in distinguishing S1 from S2. However, gray zones were smaller for late-stage detection, likely due to more distinct metabolic changes. Third, FibroScan data were only available for Cohort 4, limiting comprehensive elastography-based comparisons across all cohorts. Fourth, all cohorts consisted of Asian populations with CHB and MASLD. External validation in non-Asian populations and other liver disease etiologies such as hepatitis C virus or autoimmune hepatitis is needed to assess generalizability. Last, as a cross-sectional study, longitudinal research is needed to confirm the reproducibility and predictive utility of our models.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and cohorts

This study included a discovery cohort and three independent validation cohorts, using both prospective and retrospective designs.

Discovery cohort (Cohort 1) aimed at identifying serum metabolite markers and establishing a predictive model, comprising 3,251 participants (1,436 CHB patients and 1,815 controls). All CHB patients underwent liver biopsy. Recruitment took place between April 2013 and June 2015 at Shuguang Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, and between December 2016 and December 2017 at Xiamen Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of Shuguang Hospital (approval number: 2012-206-22-01) and was endorsed by the ethics committee of Xiamen Hospital.

Participants aged 18–75 years were recruited into control and case groups. Case group inclusion required biopsy-confirmed hepatic fibrosis and a confirmed CHB diagnosis, defined either a documented history of hepatitis B or HBsAg positivity for over six months, with current HBsAg and/or HBV DNA positivity. Exclusion criteria comprised other hepatitis virus co-infections or antibodies, non-CHB-related fibrosis or cirrhosis, other chronic diseases, active malignancy, severe cardiovascular, pulmonary, renal, endocrine or hematopoietic system disorders, prior liver transplantation, history of psychiatric medication use or substance addiction, alcohol consumption within the preceding six months, antibiotic or probiotic/prebiotic use within six weeks, pregnancy or lactation, or inability to obtain blood samples. For the control group, inclusion required no alcohol consumption, normal liver biochemistry, negative HBsAg and anti-HCV antibodies, and normal ultrasound without hepatic steatosis. Exclusion included blood sample unavailability, significant cardiopulmonary, renal, gastrointestinal or hepatic disease, acute or chronic diseases, active malignancy, or prescription medication use within 2 months. All participants provided written informed consent.

Validation cohort (Cohort 2) was a prospective, multicentered study for model validation involving 729 participants (347 CHB patients and 382 controls). All CHB patients underwent liver biopsy. Clinical indications for liver biopsy included non-invasive tests suggestive of significant liver fibrosis and diagnostic uncertainty in CHB patients. The study was conducted following the Technical Guidelines for Clinical Trials of In Vitro Diagnostic Reagents from the National Medical Products Administration (No. 16, 2014). It followed the same inclusion/exclusion criteria as the Cohort 1 and was conducted at Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine, the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, and the Second Hospital of Anhui Medical University between July 24, 2020, and October 30, 2022. The study received approval from the respective ethical review boards (approval numbers: YW2020-103(F1), 2020-031-02, and DF2020-132-01). All participants provided written informed consent.

Validation cohort (Cohort 3) comprised 149 MASLD patients from Zhongshan Hospital Affiliated to Fudan University. The study was approved by the hospital’s Research Ethics Committee (approval no. B2013-132). All patients underwent liver biopsy. Subjects aged 18–75 years were recruited. Inclusion criteria included biopsy-confirmed hepatic fibrosis with the presence of hepatic steatosis affecting ≥5% of hepatocytes, having one or more cardiometabolic risk factors. Exclusion criteria included significant alcohol consumption (defined as ≥210 g/week for men and ≥140 g/week for women) and diagnosis of other secondary causes of hepatic fat accumulation or other liver diseases. Participants were excluded from the study if they were unable to provide blood samples for laboratory testing, had a history of psychotropic drug use or substance addiction that could interfere with the study, or were pregnant or lactating. All participants provided written informed consent.

Validation cohort (Cohort 4) comprised 155 MASLD patients from Prince of Wales Hospital. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval no. 2006.439-T). All patients underwent liver biopsy. Subjects aged 18 years or above were recruited. Inclusion criteria included clinical indications for liver biopsy (such as non-invasive tests suggestive of significant liver fibrosis, diagnostic uncertainty, and screening for clinical trials), liver biopsy demonstrating steatosis affecting ≥5% of hepatocytes, presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, and provision of informed written consent. Exclusion criteria included positive hepatitis B surface antigen or anti-hepatitis C virus antibody, clinical, laboratory or histological features of other liver diseases, alcohol consumption ≥30 g/day in men and ≥20 g/day in women, past or current history of hepatocellular carcinoma or hepatic decompensation, and history of other malignancies unless in complete remission for ≥5 years.

The study enrolled 4,284 participants across four cohorts. Cohort 1 included 1,815 controls (1,336 male/479 female, median age 34 years) and 1,436 CHB patients (925 male/333 female with gender data available for 3,073 patients in Cohort 1, median age ranging from 31 years in S0-S1 to 50 years in S4 cirrhosis). Cohort 2 comprised 382 controls (147 male/235 female, median age 39 years) and 347 CHB patients (142 male/205 female, median age 37–42 years across fibrosis stages). In Cohort 3, the 149 MASLD patients ranged from S0-S1 (30 male/21 female, median age 35 years) to cirrhosis (5 male/3 female, median age 59 years). In Cohort 4, the 155 MASLD patients ranged from S0-S1 (35 male/24 female, median age 53 years) to cirrhosis (7 male/10 female, median age 62 years).

Method details

Liver biopsy and histological assessment

For all cohorts, patients underwent an ultrasound-guided percutaneous liver biopsy within one week of enrollment. Biopsy specimens were required to be at least 15 mm long and contain at least 6 portal tracts. Liver tissue samples were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and stained with hematoxylin/eosin and Masson’s trichrome. Histological evaluation was performed according to Scheuer’s classification for all cohorts except Cohort 4, with fibrosis staged from S0 to S4,38 while Cohort 4 was evaluated using the Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis Clinical Research Network System with fibrosis staged from F0 to F4.39 Both systems use equivalent criteria for advanced fibrosis (i.e., bridging fibrosis) and cirrhosis.

Transient elastography

For Cohort 4, liver stiffness measurements were also obtained using transient elastography (FibroScan, Echosens, Paris, France) within one week of enrollment. Measurements were performed using the M probe (or XL probe for patients with BMI >30 kg/m2) with at least 10 valid measurements obtained. Results were expressed in kilopascals (kPa), and examinations were considered reliable when the interquartile range was <30% of the median value and the success rate was ≥60%. Established cut-off values were applied to categorize fibrosis stages: 8.2 kPa for significant fibrosis, 9.7 kPa for advanced fibrosis, and 13.6 kPa for cirrhosis.40

Metabolite measurement and analysis

For Cohorts 1, 3, 4, serum metabolites were analyzed using the Q300 Metabolite Assay Kit (Human Metabolomics Institute, Inc., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) based on the method previously published with modifications20 on an ultra-performance liquid chromatography triple quadrupole mass spectrometry system (UPLC‒MS/MS, ACQUITY I-S, Waters, Milford, MA, USA). For Cohorts 2, serum levels of Tyr and TCA were measured by UPLC‒MS/MS using the NMPA of China-approved L-Tyrosine and Taurocholic Acid Assay Kit (approval No. 20232401415, Human Metabolomics Institute, Inc., Shenzhen, China). A 10 μL serum sample was mixed with 190 μL of isotope-labeled internal standards (L-tyrosine-d2 and taurocholic acid-d4) (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc., Andover, MA, USA). Following oscillation and centrifugation, an aliquot of 60 μL supernatant was transferred to a fresh 96-well plate and mixed with 60 μL of acetonitrile/methanol (70/30, v/v). All samples and standards were subjected to oscillation and centrifugation prior to instrumental analysis to ensure homogeneity. A 5 μL aliquot of sample was injected into UPLC‒MS/MS with an ACQUITY CSH Fluoro-Phenyl column (1.7 μm, 50 mm × 2.1 mm internal dimensions) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The entire UPLC‒MS/MS system was controlled using MassLynx 4.1.

Feature selection

Metabolites were measured experimentally and subjected to univariate analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test. This analysis aimed to differentiate CHB patients from healthy controls, significant fibrosis (S2–S4) from S0-S1 fibrosis, advanced fibrosis (S3–S4) from S0-S2 fibrosis, and cirrhosis (S4) from S0–S3 fibrosis. Metabolites with a p-value <0.001 were identified as significantly different. These metabolites were further analyzed using ElasticNet and XGBoost to identify discriminative features for the four classifications. ElasticNet was configured with a regularization strength (alpha) of 0.4 and an L1 ratio of 0.7, while XGBoost utilized 400 estimators. Metabolites were ranked based on ElasticNet coefficients and XGBoost feature average information gain. The final panel comprised top-ranking variables consistently selected across both methods for all four classification models.

Model development and training

Following feature selection, Cohort 1 data were processed for model training. Multiple machine learning algorithms were evaluated based on average AUROC performance in validation cohorts 2–4, including Gradient Boosting, Random Forest, Categorical Boosting, Logistic regression, and TabPFN—a transformer-based architecture pretrained on tabular data for rapid and accurate predictions with limited training samples. To maximize model performance, hyperparameters for each model (except TabPFN, which is a pre-trained model) were optimized using GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation on the training cohort 1. The optimal hyperparameters were configured as follows: Gradient Boosting (n_estimators = 200, learning_rate = 0.05, max_depth = 5, min_samples_split = 2, min_samples_leaf = 1), Random Forest (n_estimators = 100, max_depth = 15, max_features = 'sqrt', min_samples_split = 5, min_samples_leaf = 2), Categorical Boosting (iterations = 200, depth = 4, learning_rate = 0.05, l2_leaf_reg = 5), Logistic Regression (C = 0.1, penalty = 'l2', solver = 'lbfgs', max_iter = 1000), and TabPFN (device = 'cpu', ignore_pretraining_limits = True), with random_state = 42 for reproducibility.

Diagnostic threshold determination

Met-FIB established dual thresholds optimized from the discovery cohort: lower cutoffs prioritized >85% sensitivity for reliable rule-out, while upper cutoffs emphasized >90% specificity for confident rule-in diagnoses. For FIB-4, APRI, and AST/ALT ratio, disease-specific cutoffs for CHB and MASLD were prioritized when available, with original cutoffs applied when disease-specific values were unavailable.41,42,43,44 NFS and BARD score, which were originally developed for MASLD, were evaluated using their established cutoffs.45,46,47 Where stage-specific cutoffs were unavailable, cutoffs from earlier fibrosis stages were carried forward for reference.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.10 software. TabPFN was trained on an NVIDIA RTX 5880 Ada GPU with 48GB of VRAM. The TabPFN method was implemented in Python using PyTorch. The TabPFN repository can be accessed at https://github.com/PriorLabs/TabPFN. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were described using means with standard deviations (SDs), or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables were described using frequencies. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

To address potential confounding factors, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the independent association between metabolites and liver fibrosis stages, adjusting for key covariates including age, gender, BMI, diabetes status, and medications. Metabolite concentrations were categorized into quartiles, and comparisons were made between the highest quartile (Q4) versus the lowest quartile (Q1) as the reference group. ORs with 95% confidence intervals were calculated.

Diagnostic performance was quantified using ROC and PR curves, with calculation of areas under the ROC curve and area under the precision-recall curve for each model. 95% CI were obtained using 1000 bootstrap samples. Differences in diagnostic performance were assessed using the DeLong test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Results were stratified into three diagnostic zones: rule-out sensitivity and NPV, rule-in specificity and PPV, and intermediate zones.
