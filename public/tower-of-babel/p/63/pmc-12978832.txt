Lung and Venous Excess Ultrasound Correlation With Intracardiac Pressures Before and After Decongestion in Critically Ill Patients With Acute Decompensated Heart Failure
Authors: Jose Victor Jimenez, Sukrit Narula, Santiago Callegari, Eduardo R. Argaiz, Edward W. Chen, Roberto Lapetina Arroyo, Jennifer Miao, Zachary Feldman, Tariq Ali, Joseph H. Donroe, P. Elliott Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Venous congestion contributes to multiple organ dysfunction and mortality in critically ill patients. The venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) and lung ultrasound scores have been shown to reflect cardiac filling pressures, but their ability to track hemodynamic changes during decongestion in critically ill patients remains unclear.OBJECTIVES:To determine the correlation of lung ultrasound and VExUS scores with intracardiac filling pressures before and after decongestion in critically ill patients with acute decompensated heart failure.DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS:We conducted a prospective single-center observational pilot study of adults admitted to the cardiac intensive care and who underwent pulmonary artery catheter placement for hemodynamic-guided decongestion. We performed paired measurements of VExUS grade, simplified lung ultrasound (sLUS) scores, and calculated the renal venous stasis index (RVSI) before and after decongestion and assessed for changes and correlation with right atrial pressure (RAP) and pulmonary artery occlusion pressure (PAOP).MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:Twenty patients underwent 40-paired assessments. After decongestion, a significant reduction in RAP (–9.2 mm Hg; 95% CI, –12.4 to –5.9 mm Hg; p ≤ 0.0001) and PAOP (–12.8 mm Hg; 95% CI, –16.6 to –9.1 mm Hg; p ≤ 0.0001) mirrored reductions in VExUS grades (–1.3; 95% CI, –1.8 to –0.7; p = 0.0002), portal vein pulsatility fraction (–19.9%; 95% CI, –34.1 to –5.6; p = 0.0075), RVSI (–0.19; 95% CI, –0.38 to 0; p = 0.04), and sLUS (–11 points; 95% CI, –14.2 to –8.5; p ≤ 0.0001). VExUS grades and portal vein pulsatility fraction correlated with RAP both before (p = 0.02 and p ≤ 0.003) and after (p = 0.01 and p = 0.001) decongestion, but neither correlated with PAOP. sLUS correlated with PAOP at baseline (ρ = 0.82; p < 0.001) and following decongestion (ρ = 0.59; p = 0.05), while showing no significant correlation with RAP pre-decongestion.CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:In critically ill patients with acute decompensated heart failure, we observed changes in VExUS and sLUS scores with decongestion which mirrored changes in intracardiac pressures. The portal vein pulsatility fraction had the higher reproducibility of the VExUS subcomponents. These findings support the role of VExUS and sLUS in hemodynamic monitoring of decongestion in critically ill patients with acute decompensated heart failure.

KEY POINTS

Question: Can the venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) and simplified lung ultrasound (sLUS) scores detect changes in intracardiac filling pressures before and after volume removal in critically ill patients with volume overload?

Findings: VExUS and sLUS mirrored right atrial and pulmonary artery occlusion pressures, before and after decongestion.

Meaning: VExUS and sLUS have a potential role in the hemodynamic monitoring of decongestion in critically ill patients with acute decompensated heart failure.

Venous congestion is a key contributor to multiple organ dysfunction and increased mortality in critically ill patients (1, 2). Organ dysfunction arises from the retrograde transmission of elevated right atrial pressure (RAP) to encapsulated intra-abdominal organs, reducing venous outflow gradients, promoting interstitial edema, and impairing perfusion (3). Similarly, elevated left ventricular (LV) filling pressures contribute to pulmonary vascular congestion, capillary leak, and increased extravascular lung water, leading to impaired gas exchange and decreased lung compliance.

Accurate assessment of volume status is crucial in managing critically ill patients. However, bedside examination is often unreliable (4), as hemodynamic congestion precedes overt clinical signs (5) and most of the cardiovascular circuit is inaccessible to direct examination (6). Pulmonary artery catheterization (PAC) is considered the gold standard for measuring intracardiac pressures and its use in patients with mixed and cardiogenic shock is associated with improved outcomes (7, 8). However, the use of PAC is associated with increased costs and risks in retrospective studies, and its use has decreased with substantial practice variability across institutions (9).

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) offers a noninvasive assessment of congestion and its effects on target organs. Lung ultrasound (LUS) detects extravascular lung water secondary to elevated LV filling pressures in the setting of preserved pulmonary capillary permeability (10). In RCTs, LUS-guided decongestion has been shown to reduce hospitalizations associated with volume overload (11, 12) and to optimize ultrafiltration in end-stage renal disease (ESRD) (13). The venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) score integrates inferior vena cava (IVC) diameter measurement with Doppler evaluation of the hepatic veins (HVs), portal vein (PV), and intrarenal renal veins (RVs) to detect altered venous flow (14). VExUS strongly correlates with RAP and pulmonary artery occlusion pressure (PAOP) obtained through invasive hemodynamic assessments (15). However, there is limited data on critically ill patients.

While evidence supports the use of VExUS and LUS as surrogate markers of congestion and elevated filling pressures, most reports validate these tools as static measurements (15, 16). It is unknown whether VExUS and LUS reflect dynamic changes in intracardiac pressures in critically ill patients, a population who often have complex and fluctuating hemodynamics. We thus conducted a prospective study to evaluate whether VExUS and LUS reflect changes in intracardiac filling pressures before and after decongestion in critically ill patients with acute decompensated heart failure. We hypothesized that paired assessments (before and after decongestion) will correspond to invasively measured intracardiac pressure changes.

METHODS

Study Design and Population

We conducted a prospective, single-center observational study of adult patients admitted to the cardiac ICU (CICU) at Yale New Haven Hospital who underwent PAC placement for hemodynamic monitoring between September 2023 and December 2024. On July 25, 2025, the Yale University Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the protocol “POCUS in the Assessment of Congestion” (IRB No. 2000035525). The procedures of the protocol followed the ethical standards of the IRB and the Helsinki Declaration.

The primary objective of this study was to assess whether VExUS, its subcomponents and the simplified LUS (sLUS) correlated with intracardiac pressures (RAP and PAOP) before and after decongestive therapy in critically ill patients with acute decompensated heart failure. We hypothesized that sonographic markers of venous congestion will mirror invasively measured intracardiac filling pressures at baseline and following decongestion. The secondary objective was the assessment of interobserver agreement for POCUS-derived scoring systems in critically ill patients. Our sample size represents a convenient sample similar in the scope of other physiologic studies.

We included adult patients (≥ 18 yr) admitted to the CICU for decongestive therapy who had a PAC placed for advanced hemodynamic monitoring per the discretion of the treating team. We excluded patients with conditions that confound VExUS grading, including known precapillary pulmonary hypertension, pericardial effusion, advanced liver disease (Child-Pugh B or higher), liver transplantation, known HV or PV thrombosis, and ESRD requiring hemodialysis (14). Pregnant patients and individuals from vulnerable populations, such as those who were incarcerated, were also excluded. Additional exclusions included poor acoustic windows that precluded adequate image acquisition and patients not in sinus rhythm during ultrasonographic examination. Although tricuspid regurgitation (TR) upgrades VExUS scoring by altering HV flows, recent evidence demonstrates the utility of portal vein pulsatility fraction (PVPF) changes in patients with severe TR and venous congestion (17); therefore, this population was not excluded from our study. In our hospital, cardiac surgical patients are not managed in the CICU; therefore, this population was not included in the study.

Study Protocol

Assessment of the VExUS and sLUS score (18) was performed within 6 hours of PAC placement in all enrolled patients. PVPF and renal venous stasis index (RVSI) were calculated from PV and RV Doppler waveforms. Hemodynamic data, including RAP and PAOP (or pulmonary artery diastolic pressure [PAD] if PAOP was unavailable), were recorded immediately before the initial POCUS assessment. Baseline clinical characteristics were collected.

Decongestion was achieved through diuretics and/or ultrafiltration with renal replacement therapy. Although standardized protocols for loop diuretic up-titration and initiation of sequential nephron blockade were recommended (19), the decision to initiate and titrate decongestive therapy, as well as the assessment of tolerance to fluid removal, was left to the treating clinical team in accordance with standard clinical practice.

Patients were monitored daily for PAC-derived hemodynamic parameters, fluid balance, and interventions administered as part of routine clinical care. We performed a repeated POCUS assessment before PAC removal, after decongestion was deemed clinically satisfactory by the primary treating team or until PAOP less than or equal to 15 mm Hg was achieved (e-Fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B609). Patients were followed until hospital discharge. Clinical congestion scores were assessed at baseline and before PAC removal.

POCUS Assessment of Congestion

We performed all POCUS assessments using a Philips Sparq Ultrasound System (Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA) with a phased array probe of 2.5–5.0 MHz by two POCUS trained internal medicine residents (J.V.J., E.W.C.) with over 100 supervised scans of experience, who performed each ultrasonographic examination but were not involved in the clinical care of the participants. VExUS images were interpreted offline by investigators (J.H.D., P.E.M.) with advanced POCUS training who had no direct communication with the sonographers and were blinded to the clinical data. Each study was interpreted independently by both investigators.

Each POCUS examination included two components: VExUS and the sLUS. We obtained the VExUS score by measuring IVC diameter in the subcostal view, 2 cm from the right atrial junction (20). If the IVC diameter exceeded 2.1 cm, we proceeded with Doppler interrogation of the HV, PV, and RV following established protocols (21). We categorized Doppler waveforms as normal, mildly abnormal, or severely abnormal. For PV categorization we calculated the PVPF using the formula: PVPF = (Vmax–Vmin)/Vmax, where Vmax and Vmin represent the peak and trough velocities during the cardiac cycle. To increase the sensitivity of the measurements, we calculated the RVSI using the following formula: RVSI = duration of absent flow in RV/total duration of cardiac cycle (22). For the PVPF and RVSI, we obtained three measurements over three separate cardiac cycles, the average was recorded. The final VExUS score was computed per established protocols (21) and graded as an ordinal variable ranging from grade 0 to 3.

We performed the sLUS with standardized settings (23). With the patient in a supine position, we scanned four parasternal zones: intercostal spaces between the third–fourth and sixth–seventh ribs bilaterally, between the parasternal and midclavicular lines. We counted single and confluent B-lines per intercostal space and calculated the total sLUS as a continuous variable, as previously described (18).

Invasive Hemodynamic Measurements

Right heart catheterization was performed via the internal jugular or femoral vein. The mean end-expiratory PAOP was determined from waveform analysis of three representative tracings. Adequate catheter placement was confirmed by demonstrating a stable, reproducible, nondamped PAOP waveform with values less than the PAD, and by radiographic confirmation of the catheter tip position.

RAP and pulmonary pressures were obtained via direct measurements of the proximal and distal port, respectively (24). The PAC was removed at the discretion of the CICU team, when decongestion was deemed clinically successful, or when a PAOP less than 15 mm Hg was achieved. PAD was used as a surrogate for PAOP if PAOP waveforms were unobtainable after balloon occlusion provided baseline PAD was within 3 mm Hg of baseline PAOP.

Clinical and Demographic Data

We extracted clinical data, laboratories, echocardiograms, urine output, fluid balance, and therapeutic interventions from electronic medical records. For each patient, we recorded the clinical data closest to PAC placement within 48 hours of enrollment. We calculated a validated clinical congestion score (25) incorporating peripheral edema, pleural effusion, and ascites at initial and final assessments.

Statistical Analysis

We reported continuous variables as mean and sd or median and interquartile range (IQR) for those with non-normal distribution as determined by the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies. To assess for interobserver variability of POCUS interpretation we calculated Cohen’s Kappa. We computed bivariate analysis of continuous variables through paired Student t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for those with non-normal distribution. For comparisons of intracardiac filling pressures across VExUS grades, we used the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn test for pairwise comparisons corrected with Bonferroni equation. We used the Spearman correlation coefficient to assess the correlation between intracardiac pressures (RAP and PAOP) and VExUS scores, PVPF, RVSI, sLUS pre- and post-decongestion, and the delta of both variables. We performed statistical analyses using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) and Stata, Version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). We considered a two-tailed p value of less than 0.05 statistically significant.

RESULTS

During the study period, we enrolled 20 patients and performed 40-paired VExUS and sLUS examinations (e-Fig. 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B609). Baseline characteristics are shown in Tables 1 and 2 and Supplemental Table 1 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B609). Mean RAP and PAOP at baseline were 16 ± 5 and 27 ± 6 mm Hg, respectively. In three instances, PAOP was unable to be obtained. Median VExUS grade was 3 (IQR, 2–3), median PVPF 52% (IQR, 38–61%), median RVSI 0.3 (IQR, 0.04–0.7), mean sLUS 18 ± 5, and mean congestion score 4 ± 2. Two patients received positive pressure ventilation throughout the study period, but positive end-expiratory pressure remained unchanged throughout the study. All patients received diuretics; two underwent ultrafiltration. Eleven received inotropes, 13 vasopressors, five mechanical circulatory support, and one a systemic vasodilator. Mean net fluid balance was –6.2 ± 5 L (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective study of critically ill patients with acute decompensated heart failure and venous congestion, we demonstrated that VExUS and sLUS correlate with changes in intracardiac filling pressures during decongestion, paralleling reductions in RAP and PAOP, respectively. VExUS and PVPF, correlated with RAP, whereas sLUS correlated with PAOP. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the correlation between hemodynamic changes measured by PAC with changes in VExUS and sLUS during decongestion in decompensated critically ill patients with acute decompensated heart failure.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated associations between elevated VExUS grades with adverse renal outcomes (16, 26–30) and correlation with intracardiac filling pressures, specifically RAP (15, 31). The role of VExUS as a dynamic, trackable marker has been recently explored by Husain-Syed et al (22), who demonstrated that changes in renal Doppler flows (within 90 d) improved renal risk stratification in patients with heart failure. Acute changes in VExUS grades have been observed in patients with ESRD following hemodialysis, directly demonstrating that VExUS components are modifiable through volume removal (32–34). Our study expands on the literature by demonstrating that the VExUS score correlates with dynamic changes in right-sided pressures in critically ill patients, a population in which dynamic measurements are needed to track rapid hemodynamic changes that guide resuscitation and decongestion (35). Additionally, a recent study demonstrated the association between improvement in VExUS scores and decreased in-hospital mortality in patients with decompensated heart failure, supporting the role of POCUS in guiding decongestion (36).

Consistent with prior studies, sLUS correlated with PAOP and captured left-sided pressure changes during decongestion (10). While LUS-guided diuresis and ultrafiltration has been shown to improve outcomes in the outpatient setting, its utility in critically ill patients is less clear. Our study expands these findings further supporting sLUS as a sensitive tool for detecting changes in LV filling pressures in decompensated cardiac patients. However, the correlation between sLUS and PAOP should not be extrapolated to patients with increased extravascular lung water secondary to increased capillary permeability (such as those with acute respiratory distress syndrome or pneumonia) in whom higher sLUS likely reflect alveolar damage rather than elevated filling pressures.

Although PVPF and RVSI exhibited dynamic changes throughout the study, only PVPF consistently correlated with RAP after decongestion. These findings align with previous reports where PVPF was associated with a cumulative fluid balance and response to diuretics in a general ICU population but did not correlate with other markers of venous congestion (37, 38). Similarly, Alday-Ramírez et al (17) studied patients with venous congestion and severe TR, demonstrating that PVPF and RVSI are severely altered before decongestion, but only PVPF tracks volume removal. The lack of correlation between RVSI and intracardiac pressures may reflect our limited sample size or that the RVSI, as a semiquantitative index of renal venous congestion, does not necessarily change in a linear manner with absolute changes in intracardiac pressures. Additionally, organ specific decongestion might lag improvements of intracardiac pressures.

In patients undergoing hemodialysis, PVPF was the most sensitive marker of decongestion (33). Notably, improvements in PVPF can occur within seconds following decongestion (39), which allows for rapid assessment of decongestion. Thus, our findings reinforce the notion that among POCUS markers of right-sided congestion, PV Doppler could be the best suited for real-time monitoring of volume status. Furthermore, its high interobserver reliability underscores its practicality for data acquisition which could make it an ideal marker of congestion in clinical trials and potentially, clinical practice.

Beyond decompensated heart failure, the conceptual framework of using VExUS and sLUS to guide decongestion may have broader relevance across ICU populations. In distributive or mixed shock, VExUS and sLUS could offer an objective method to identify patients with venous congestion in whom volume offload would be preferred over fluid resuscitation (35). Although our study was not designed to evaluate these populations, our findings support the physiologic premise that ultrasound markers track dynamic changes in intracardiac pressures and may therefore complement clinical assessment when assessing volume intolerance. Future studies should test whether POCUS-guided decongestion improves patient-centered outcomes, optimizes fluid stewardship, or reduces reliance on invasive hemodynamic monitoring in diverse critical care settings.

Our study has multiple limitations. First, the small sample, strict inclusion criteria and single-center design limits statistical power, generalizability, and ability to distinguish the role of VExUS and sLUS in different cardiac phenotypes (left vs. right ventricular dysfunction). Second, the population studied is highly selective for patients with severe volume overload secondary to acute decompensated heart failure, the role of VExUS and sLUS in populations with milder degree of volume overload and outside this population (e.g., in septic patients undergoing deresuscitation) is unclear (40). Additionally, our findings may not be generalizable to mechanically ventilated patients. Third, lack of electrocardiogram gating during Doppler acquisition may reduce waveform interpretation reliability specifically of the HV (41), although PV Doppler reproducibility remained excellent. Fourth, most of our cohort had some degree of TR, which intrinsically upgrade HV flows in VExUS grading. However, PVPF appears to be less influenced by TR and has been shown to track volume status, even in severe cases (17). Fifth, because the inclusion criteria required elevated intracardiac pressures, sonographers were aware of the expected direction of change between scans, which may have introduced measurement bias. Sixth, our cohort allowed for different management strategies for decongestion (diuretics, renal replacement therapy, mechanical circulatory support, and systemic vasodilators). While this enhances generalizability, such heterogeneity increases the potential for data outliers. Last, our study did not assess echocardiography and markers of diastolic function, which can aid in the global assessment of congestion. Future studies incorporating echocardiographic markers may elucidate their role in the assessment of decongestion in critically ill patients.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study demonstrates that VExUS, particularly PV Doppler, correlates with RAP and sLUS correlates with PAOP reliably detecting decongestion in critically ill patients with acute decompensated heart failure. Among VExUS components, PV Doppler showed the highest interoperator reproducibility, underscoring its potential as a practical tool for real-time bedside monitoring. Our findings support a complementary role for VExUS and sLUS in assessing decongestion in critically ill patients with volume overload due to acute decompensated heart failure. Larger prospective studies are warranted to determine whether tailoring decongestive therapy to these ultrasound markers can improve clinical outcomes.
