Open Distal Phalanx Tuft Fractures in Fingertip Injuries: A Retrospective Cohort Study Comparing Kirschner Wire Fixation Versus Conservative Management
Authors: Islam Koriem, Khalid Nabil Youssef, Hamdy Alnaggar, Amr Nabil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeOpen tuft fractures of the distal phalanx are frequently associated with soft-tissue and nail-bed injuries, yet the optimal management—particularly the role of Kirschner wire (K-wire) fixation—remains controversial. This study aimed to compare clinical and functional outcomes between K-wire fixation and conservative management in patients with open distal phalanx tuft fractures.MethodsA retrospective comparative cohort study was conducted involving 50 adult patients with open tuft fractures of the distal phalanx. Patients were divided into two groups: K-wire fixation (n = 25) and conservative management (n = 25). Outcomes assessed at a minimum of 6 months included radiological union, distal interphalangeal joint range of motion, pinch strength, pain using the visual analog scale, patient satisfaction, and complication rates.ResultsUnion was achieved in 94% of the fixation group versus 76% of the conservative group. Time to union was shorter with fixation (5.2 ± 1.1 vs 6.8 ± 1.6 weeks). At final follow-up, the fixation group demonstrated significantly better distal interphalangeal flexion range of motion (65° ± 8° vs 54° ± 10°), greater pinch strength (88% ± 6% vs 74% ± 8% of contralateral side), and lower visual analog scale pain scores (1.4 ± 0.8 vs 2.8 ± 1.2). Despite these differences, patient satisfaction was only slightly higher in the fixation group (84% vs 76%). Complications were low in both groups, with conservative management showing higher rates of delayed union and residual tenderness.ConclusionsK-wire fixation may offer more reliable union and functional recovery in open tuft fractures with displaced or unstable fragments and significant nail-bed injury. However, given the retrospective design and sample size, treatment decisions should be individualized and tailored according to the type and severity of injury and patient involvement in decision making.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic III.

Fingertip injuries are among the most common traumatic conditions affecting the hand and frequently involve the distal phalanx, which is vulnerable due to its exposed anatomical position and minimal soft-tissue coverage.1,2 Tuft fractures represent a significant portion of these injuries and are often associated with soft-tissue and nail-bed damage.3,4

While closed and nondisplaced distal phalanx fractures generally respond well to conservative treatment,1 the optimal management of open or displaced tuft fractures remains controversial. Conservative treatment in the form of soft-tissue management only without bone fixation in such cases may lead to nail deformity, prolonged tenderness, or delayed union.3,5

Surgical stabilization using K-wire is widely practiced because of its simplicity, reliability, and cost-effectiveness. Previous studies have shown that K-wire fixation provides stable alignment, facilitates soft-tissue healing, and improves functional outcomes.6, 7, 8 Senesi et al6 demonstrated that stable fixation methods, including K-wire or needle fixation, support earlier functional recovery.

However, despite extensive literature on distal phalanx fractures, few studies focus specifically on open tuft fractures associated with fingertip injuries.

Therefore, this study sought to compare K-wire fixation versus conservative management in open tuft fractures of the distal phalanx. The objective was to evaluate union rate, final range of motion (ROM), pain, complications, and patient satisfaction to determine whether fixation provides a significant clinical advantage.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This study is a retrospective comparative cohort study that aimed to compare clinical and functional outcomes between K-wire fixation and conservative management in patients with open tuft fractures of the distal phalanx associated with soft-tissue injuries.

Study duration

The study included patients treated between January 2024 and March 2025. Patients were followed for a minimum of 6 months after intervention to assess union rates, functional recovery, and complications.

Study population

All patients treated at our institution who met the inclusion criteria during the study period were enrolled.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria consisted of adult patients (≥18 years) with radiologically confirmed open distal phalanx tuft fractures presenting within 3 weeks of injury, fracture displacement greater than 1 mm, and amenable to fixation (involvement of more than 20% of the distal phalanx tuft), and preserved digital perfusion as evidenced by normal capillary refill.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria included pathological fractures due to tumor, infection, or metabolic bone disease; a history of prior surgery or fracture involving the same digit; chronic fractures older than 3 weeks or established nonunion; pediatric patients younger than 18 years; fractures with small fragments not amenable to fixation (involvement of less than 20% of the distal phalanx tuft); and polytraumatized patients with major associated injuries that could affect hand function or outcome.

Sample size and sampling method

A total of 50 patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria were included using a consecutive sampling method. All eligible cases presenting within the study period were reviewed and analyzed.

Treatment allocation was not randomized. The choice between K-wire fixation and conservative management was based on surgeon preference and intraoperative fracture characteristics, including fragment stability, degree of displacement, and extent of soft-tissue injury. Patient preference did not influence treatment selection.

Patients were divided into two groups:1.Group A (K-wire fixation group): 25 patients.2.Group B (conservative management group): 25 patients.

Data collection

Data were obtained retrospectively from hospital records and operative notes. Collected information included the following:

Patient demographics (age, sex [refers to biological attributes based on physical and physiological characteristics], and dominant hand), mechanism and side of injury, radiological findings and fracture classification, type of management (K-wire fixation or soft-tissue repair), intraoperative details when applicable, and postoperative or follow-up findings were collected. Follow-up data included fracture union, pain, ROM, and complications such as infection or delayed union. Functional outcomes were assessed by measuring the final ROM of the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint, pinch strength, pain severity using the visual analog scale, and clinical and radiographic evidence of union. Radiological union was defined as the presence of bridging trabeculae and cortical continuity on at least two orthogonal radiographic views, in conjunction with the absence of local tenderness at the fracture site on clinical examination.

Surgical technique

For the surgical group, a soft-tissue-first technique was employed:

After thorough irrigation and debridement, skin closure was initiated at the lateral nail folds to realign the distal fragment using strong, simple skin sutures. The remaining skin wounds were then appropriately sutured. This step provided a well-realigned and stable distal part, helping to resist rotational forces that may occur during K-wire insertion, particularly in loosely attached distal fragments. Subsequently, meticulous nail-bed repair was performed using fine absorbable sutures. Restoration of the soft-tissue anatomy frequently resulted in spontaneous fracture alignment. A 0.9–1.1 mm K-wire was then inserted longitudinally under fluoroscopic guidance to stabilize the fracture; in most cases, a DIP-transfixing wire was used and was not buried beneath the skin for easy removal later (Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3).Figure 132-year-old male patient, left hand injury. Conservative management

The conservative group received proper wound care, nail-bed repair, and immobilization with a fingertip protective splint for 4–6 weeks, and gradual mobilization after soft-tissue healing. Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee. Informed consent was obtained from all participants for inclusion and publication of data.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared using the independent Student t test, while nonparametric data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Results for primary outcomes are presented with 95% confidence intervals. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariate analysis was not performed because of the limited sample size.

Because of the retrospective nature of the study, a priori sample size calculation was not performed. A post-hoc power analysis based on the primary outcome (union rate) demonstrated a statistical power of approximately 70% at an alpha level of 0.05 for detecting the observed difference between groups.

Complications

The overall rate of complications was low in both groups. Superficial infection occurred in three patients in group A and two patients in group B; all cases were managed successfully with local wound care and oral antibiotics, and no patient required additional intervention. Nail deformity was observed in six patients in group A and four patients in group B. It appeared to correlate more closely with the severity of the initial nail-bed injury rather than the method of fracture fixation. Delayed union or residual tenderness occurred in one patient in group A and five patients in group B. No cases of wire migration, wire breakage, tendon injury, neurovascular compromise, or osteomyelitis were observed.

Discussion

Open tuft fractures present a unique treatment challenge because of the combination of bony injury, soft-tissue disruption, and frequent nail-bed involvement.3,4 The present study compared K-wire fixation with conservative management in these injuries and demonstrated clear objective advantages for K-wire fixation, although subjective patient satisfaction remained similar between groups.

Recent evidence on distal phalanx fractures supports the nuanced role of both conservative and surgical management. Large epidemiological data indicate that while the majority of phalangeal fractures—including those of the distal phalanx—are treated nonsurgically, surgical intervention with K-wire fixation is frequently used in selected cases with significant displacement or instability, reinforcing the importance of individualized treatment decisions based on fracture characteristics rather than demographic factors alone.7 Retrospective comparative studies suggest that different percutaneous fixation techniques, including variations in K-wire configuration, may influence time to union and clinical outcomes. However, overall union rates are often comparable.8 Additionally, combined nail-bed repair and fracture management yield high union and patient satisfaction rates in injuries involving the nail complex, though definitive recommendations for fixation versus conservative treatment remain inconclusive.9 Systematic reviews of open distal phalanx fractures emphasize the importance of early surgical debridement and appropriate antibiotic administration in minimizing complications, highlighting the need for standardized treatment protocols.10

The higher union rate in the fixation group supports the importance of providing mechanical stability in distal phalanx fractures. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that rigid stabilization reduces micromotion, accelerates bone healing, and minimizes the risk of delayed union or persistent tenderness.11,12 Al-Qattan11 and Hay and Tay12 both reported improved radiological healing and fewer complications when fixation was used instead of splinting alone.

Functional outcomes in our study also favored K-wire fixation. Patients treated surgically achieved better DIP joint motion and significantly greater pinch strength, which can be attributed to anatomical realignment and early stabilization of the fracture. These results parallel those of Senesi et al,6 who showed stable fixation—whether by K-wires or needles—supports early rehabilitation and more efficient recovery of hand function. Improved biomechanics and reduced discomfort during movements likely contributed to the superior functional performance observed in the fixation group.

Pain outcomes also followed this trend, with significantly lower visual analog scale scores in the fixation group. Nevertheless, despite better healing and function, final patient satisfaction showed only a slight and statistically nonsignificant difference between groups. Several factors may explain this: patients often judge satisfaction based on return to daily activity rather than detailed performance; conservative treatment still provides acceptable comfort for many individuals; and temporary discomfort associated with wires or postoperative care may influence perceived satisfaction. These observations align with typical outcomes in distal phalanx injuries.1,5

Complication rates in both groups were low, reinforcing the safety of K-wire fixation when proper debridement, sterile technique, and follow-up care are used. Nail deformity, delayed healing, and residual tenderness occurred more frequently with conservative treatment, in agreement with previous studies emphasizing that unstable or open fractures are prone to poorer outcomes when treated without stabilization.5,13,14 No major complications such as osteomyelitis or wire migration occurred in the fixation group, consistent with literature reporting that distal phalanx fixation is generally safe when infection control is adequate.4,14

When compared with available literature, the findings of this study strongly support surgical stabilization for open or displaced tuft fractures. Earlier work by Mahajan et al,3 Senesi et al,6 and Al-Qattan11 all highlight the benefits of fixation in improving alignment, providing stability, and protecting soft-tissue repair. Conservative treatment remains appropriate for nondisplaced fractures but may be insufficient when significant displacement or nail-bed injury is present.

Clinically, the results suggest that K-wire fixation should be considered the preferred treatment for open tuft fractures with unstable or displaced fragments. Although patient satisfaction was similar between groups, the objectively superior outcomes—higher union rate, better ROM, reduced pain, and fewer complications—favor surgical management for achieving reliable fingertip recovery.11,12,14 Shared decision making remains important, as both treatment strategies can produce acceptable subjective outcomes, but fixation provides a more predictable course of healing.

This study is limited by its retrospective nature and modest sample size. Larger prospective studies with long-term follow-up would help confirm these findings and assess tactile sensitivity, fine motor performance, and long-term nail appearance. The absence of a priori power calculation may increase the risk of type II error, particularly for secondary outcomes, and this should be considered when interpreting the results. Also, the minimum follow-up duration of 6 months may be insufficient to detect late complications such as cold intolerance, chronic nail deformity, or post-traumatic arthritis, and cost-effectiveness could not be reliably assessed because of incomplete retrospective documentation. These patient-centered outcomes represent important areas for future prospective research. As treatment allocation was based on surgeon judgment rather than randomization, selection bias and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded.

Despite the limitations of this study, we could conclude that K-wire fixation may offer more reliable union and functional recovery in open tuft fractures with displaced or unstable fragments. However, given that patient satisfaction was similar between groups, treatment decisions should be individualized, and shared decision making remains essential.
