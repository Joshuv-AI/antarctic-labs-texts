Highlight: Segmental duplications at the center of regulatory innovation
Authors: Pedro Andrade
Published: 2026

Regulatory variation is widely recognized as a potent force for phenotypic evolution. Promoters, enhancers, and other non-coding sequences fine-tune gene expression throughout development, often acting in concert to drive organism function (Wray et al. 2003; Hill et al. 2021). In other words, many genes and their regulatory elements participate in networks with shared expression patterns, ie they are co-regulated. How these gene expression networks form remains a mystery—as does much of regulatory evolution. “We have largely understood how DNA codes for proteins since the 1960s,” says Craig Lowe, professor at Duke University, countering that “however, we still do not have a good theoretical understanding of how DNA encodes regulatory elements and assigns them to genes.”

Segmental duplications (SDs) make particularly good candidates for generating such clusters of jointly regulated genes. SDs are large (over 1 kb), recently duplicated genomic sections that have an important role in the evolution of genome structure (Vollger et al. 2022). Duplication of protein-coding genes is an established source of phenotypic innovation due to dosage effects or functional divergence between duplicated gene copies. But can duplications also explain how gene regulatory networks evolve?

In a new article in Molecular Biology and Evolution (Weaver and Lowe 2026), Lowe and graduate student Seth Weaver hypothesized that segmental duplications act as a mutational mechanism that builds networks of similar regulatory elements in the human genome.

In their study, the authors first combined sequence alignment and open chromatin data to identify 1,336 putative clusters of regulatory elements. They then asked whether these clusters were preferentially located in SDs. The results supported the hypothesis: their sets of putative regulatory elements were up to 8-fold more common in segmental duplications compared to the rest of the genome. Strikingly, 974 of the 1,336 clusters had at least three-quarters of their constituent regulatory elements in SDs. The conclusion seems clear: SDs and regulatory element families are strongly associated in the human genome.

If SDs recurrently shuffle regulatory elements in the genome, do duplicated elements serve any function once placed at their new genomic loci? Weaver and Lowe (2026) combined in vitro quantification of enhancer activity (STARR-seq) and transcription repression (CRISPRi) to find (as expected) cases of duplicated regulators affecting either the original gene copy or its duplicated coding sequence (Fig. 1a). However, they also detected complex network rewiring events, including regulators that switch to a different target gene (Fig. 1c), or isolated “orphan” regulators that gain function if placed near a novel target gene (Fig. 1d).

However, moving regulators into new genomic regions also generates novel gene-regulator interactions through b) changes in the behavior of the regulator (promoter-enhancer flexibility; c), rewiring to a new gene network; or d) the rescue of a previously inactive regulator. Importantly, they also found that promoters, which control transcription of adjacent genes, can act as distal enhancers when placed in a new genomic location (Fig. 1b). In other words, the specific role of any regulatory element depends highly on its genomic context. To Lowe, this fluidity between promoters and enhancers was the biggest surprise from their work. He hypothesizes that “promoters and enhancers are not distinct functional categories, and not many mutations are needed to convert a DNA sequence between acting as proximal or distal regulators of transcription.”

Previous studies have shown that SDs are frequently located in telomeric regions of the genome (eg Vollger et al. 2022). Weaver and Lowe (2026) show how this association drives the evolution of regulatory networks. The authors detail a compelling example: in early great apes, two specific clusters of regulatory elements (which they called regulatory families 4 and 5) were ancestrally located at a conserved autosomal primate locus; segmental duplications then transferred them into telomeric regions before gorillas, humans, and chimpanzees evolved. Once in the telomeres of these three species, these regulators underwent further rounds of duplication, both within telomeres and into new genomic loci, where they established novel regulatory interactions involving genes linked to tumor suppression.

Eloquently, Lowe likens telomeres’ role to that of a magician's cauldron: “An initial segmental duplication places the regulatory element in the cauldron, segmental duplications at the telomeres continuously mix the cauldron, and then segmental duplications are also responsible for inserting parts of the mixture back into more stable and gene-rich areas of the genome.” But just as mixing ingredients in a cauldron adds up to more than a sum of the ingredients, so does rewiring regulatory elements multiply downstream phenotypic effects, ie it leads to pleiotropy. Lowe adds that “a single segmental duplication is likely to both expand that individual's gene set, as well as rewire several regulatory interactions at the site of insertion, setting the stage for both advantageous and deleterious consequences.”

Weaver and Lowe (2026) unveil how a single duplication event can simultaneously rewire the regulatory landscape of multiple genes. But how widespread is duplication-driven regulatory rewiring as a mechanism for evolutionary change? Lowe concludes that “we do not yet know how common it is for segmental duplications to rewire gene regulatory interactions between species or between individuals within a species; however, based on the glimpses of it in this work and other publications, we think it is common for segmental duplications to contribute gene regulatory innovations.”
