Healthcare professionals’ needs when providing perinatal bereavement care: A qualitative study
Authors: Giti Atashsokhan, Maryam Farjamfar, Ahmad Khosravi, Mahboobe Taher, Khadige Abadian, Afsaneh Keramat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Caring for and supporting bereaved parents with fetal loss requires preparation, training, and attention to a set of needs of health workers to provide optimal services. This is although it has been less addressed in the educational curriculum and in-service training. This study aims to investigate the experiences of Iranian healthcare professionals regarding the needs related to the care of bereaved parents with perinatal loss.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This research was a qualitative study conducted in Shahroud City, Iran, to explore healthcare professionals’ needs from June 2021 to December 2022. Semi-structured in-depth interviews on purposive sample of 14 participants were conducted. The participants were care providers who worked in OB-GYN ward, general midwifery clinics, and a private office. The interviews were recorded verbatim, transcribed, and analyzed using an inductive content analysis. MAXQDA software version 10 was used for data analysis. Granheim and Lundman’s approach was used to analyze the data.RESULTS:Two major themes, four categories, and nine subcategories emerged. The themes included the “existence of protective laws and policies” and “skill and training needs.” Protective laws and policies included “Government Rules and guidelines” and “organizational Rules protecting the individual.” Skills and training needs consisted of two categories: “communication skills” and “Self-Care needs.”CONCLUSIONS:Educational policies, development of guidelines for the care of bereaved mothers based on culture, training programs for dealing with bereaved parents, providing self-care programs for employees, and improving the physical and emotional conditions of the workplace were among the needs expressed by the participants. Meeting the needs of healthcare professionals is effective in job satisfaction, feeling of value, and high quality of services for bereaved parents.

Introduction

Perinatal loss is a painful experience with physical, psychological, and social consequences in families.[1] The worldwide perinatal loss rate is estimated at around 2.7 million deaths per years.[2] The loss of an infant through stillbirth, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or early neonatal death is recognized as a traumatic life event.[3] Supporting and caring for bereaved parents in mourning is very important for parent’s mental health.[4]

Although many studies show that the appropriate skills of healthcare professionals such as communication skills have a positive effect on the long-term well-being of parents, this is not the only skill and need for care providers. In fact, perinatal bereavement care (PBC) has different dimensions. PBC refers to the comprehensive and integrative components of care in the physical, psychological, emotional, and spiritual domains following a perinatal loss.[5]

Many service providers state that they are not adequately prepared for the psychological care of parents.[67]

Healthcare professionals are often exposed to occupational stress from an imbalance between skills and commitments, organizational failures, or lack of support.[8] On the other hand, taking care of a grieving family imposes a great emotional load on employees’ morale, performing other duties in the workplace, and even communicating with colleagues.[9] dealing with emotions of guilt, rage, grief,[10] frustration, a sense of personal failure, feelings of inadequacy,[11] burnout syndrome, and professional inefficiency.[912]

Guidelines for PBC provide recommendations for maternity services. However, these guidelines and protocols generally describe professional duties in parents’ caring and very brief recommendations for organizational support to staff.[1314] There are no such guidelines in many countries, such as Iran, Spain, etc.[15] Literature reviews showed that few studies have been conducted on the needs of professionals when providing PBC. McNamara et al.[16] identified that 82% of healthcare providers (HCPs) received no training in dealing with intrapartum death, while 94% had no education on self-care strategies. They showed that maternity hospitals need to improve their support structures for HCPs following perinatal death. Steen’s research mentions debriefing with colleagues in difficult working conditions as a professional need.[17] Some researchers also stated the time and space for mourning as the needs of care providers.[1819]

Jonas Simpson’s findings explained that supporting colleagues and providing authentic, compassionate, and high-quality care are the priorities of the staff.[20]

Experiences of midwives showed that support in the work environment leads to resilience in stressful events of maternal or fetal death, and understanding support needs will improve the quality of care[21]; experiences are affected by the context in which people work and live and affect all human affairs.[22] Therefore, the needs of employees should be considered based on the cultural context, education, and management structures of the workplace. In Iran, dealing with the experience of fetal loss care and the type of care required have not been foreseen in the educational curriculum, and it has been paid less at in-service training.

Due to the existing gaps, this qualitative research was conducted for the first time. Therefore, this research aimed to explore the needs of healthcare professionals providing care for perinatal bereaved mothers/parents.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This research was a qualitative study. The conventional content analysis method was used. Content analysis is a research technique for making replicable and valid inferences from data in their context to provide knowledge and insight, increase the researcher’s understanding of the phenomenon, and provide a practical guide for action[15]. The study settings were hospitals, health centers, and Clinics in Shahroud City, Iran.

Study participants and sampling

This qualitative research study was conducted with 14 health professionals (June 2021 to December 2022) from four midwifery clinics, two obstetrics offices, one psychiatric and psychological counseling clinic, and one maternity ward at teaching hospitals.

Purposive sampling with maximum variation was performed. Participants were Persian-speaking health professionals with 5 years of clinical work experience and rich experiences caring for, counseling, or treating bereaved mothers. After explaining the study’s objectives and obtaining informed consent, the sampling continued to obtain deeper and richer data and stopped when no new data was obtained. The participants were 12 women and 2 men, with 5-30 years of work experience.

Data collection tool and technique

Fourteen in-depth individual face-to-face interviews using a semi-structured interview guide were conducted. Each interview lasted 30 to 60 min (median: 45 min). One interview was repeated for further description and disambiguation. The interviews were carried out at their workplace. All interviews were conducted by the first author and recorded by voice recorder with participants’ permission. Using constant comparison was performed for data analysis based on the Graneheim and Lundman approach. Data analysis was conducted concurrently with the data collection phase and was aided by MAXQDA software. Following “Elo and Kynga,”[23] in the preparation phase, each interview was reviewed several times to make sense of the data and for an in-depth understanding of the text. Then, meaningful sentences were selected as the units of analysis. In the inductive approach, the organization phase includes open coding, creating categories, and abstraction.[23]

Open coding was performed by reading the interviews and writing notes in the margins to describe the content more clearly. After several discussions, the lists of categories were grouped by collapsing those similar or dissimilar into broader higher-order categories.[2425]

Categories emerged through comparing and merging the subcategories. During this phase, research team meetings were held to discuss the findings (codes and categories) until saturation. As stated by Morse, “as the phenomenon becomes stronger, more evident, more consistent, more cohesive, and more mature, research becomes saturated, and the researcher becomes certain.”[26] Data saturation was obtained after 14 interviews. After analyzing all the data, two main themes were extracted.

For the rigor of research data, The data were validated based on the four criteria of Lincoln and Guba.[27] The following steps were followed to ensure credibility: prolonged engagement in the field from June 2021 to December 2022 and prolonged time spent with the participants. Maximum variation was used based on work experience, positions, settings, and person triangulation. Checking the findings by the research team and member checks were also performed during the data collection. For dependability and conformability of the data, the interviews were audited by experts as external checks.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval of the study was obtained from the ethics committee of Shahroud University of Medical Sciences (Code of Ethics IR.SHMU.REC.1400.011). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The interviews were recorded with the permission of the participants. They were assured of anonymity, privacy, and confidentiality of their responses. They were free to decline or withdraw participation.

Existence of protective laws and policies

In the experience of the participants, the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MoHME) should consider the ratio of HCPs to clients in the laws and policies, organizational charts, and hiring a sufficient number of employees. This sensitive care requires adequate personnel involved in the care.

One staff (no. 6) described: “When the number of shift personnel is small… you can’t spend time on these issues [PBC]… In fact, from the beginning of the shift, we dedicate our energy to mothers in labor, postpartum mothers, or to the admission and prioritize them… The shift manager reprimands her personnel for high-risk cases, but she never blames the staff for not speaking empathetically to the grieving mothers or not listening to them.”

Another staff (no. 3) stated: “We have few personnel. If I want to sympathize with these mothers and spend time with them, my colleague may say that all the work of the ward has been entrusted to them. I think that if they increase the number of shift personnel, we can pay attention to the psychological care of patients [bereaved mothers]…”

Government rules and guidelines

One of the most important statements of the participants was the absence of a national guidelines or protocols for PBC; thus, only the treatment of the body is provided to these mothers. Psychological advice to bereaved mothers is based on the personal experiences of the staff, and unfortunately, from the point of view of psychologists, in some cases, it is even unscientific and sad.

“Elaboration of clinical guidelines and protocols for dealing with/psychological care of perinatal loss and bereaved mothers are a necessity.” (no. 1.2.4.3.12.5.10.8.14, 9, 13)

“We have no guidelines… My colleagues and I are really stressed in this situation…. I don’t know what to do about showing a fetus or a stillborn child. Our doctor says to pay attention to the mother’s history of mental disorders and then decide whether to show the baby or not. Some faculty colleagues say that we should pay attention to the wishes of the mother, but I don’t know if showing the child is the right thing according to our culture.” (no. 2.3.4.6)

Organizational rules protecting the individual

According to studies, the organization’s support for the personnel is effective in work commitment and increasing motivation in performing tasks. The increasing number of complaints from HCPs will make organizations support them with awareness of laws, legal advice, etc.

“A system whose personnel have high-risk work must support them when there is a possibility of a complaint. The system should implement judicial training for them. What should they do if they are summoned to court? What are their rights? When stillbirth occurs and the patient complains, as happened to me… the system does not provide any legal advice. You must be responsible yourself. I asked colleagues who had similar problems about what I had to do… This lack of support is really discouraging” (no. 7).

The manager considering the personnel’s experience when delegating the care needed by the grieving mother is a form of organizational support. An experienced care provider should be assigned to the care of high-risk patients.

“A grieving mother told me, ‘In the previous shift, a young girl was taking care of me. She didn’t answer my questions and left the room early… I thought she was running away from me!’ When I (a midwife with 17 years of experience) asked my colleague (with 2 years of experience) the reason for this action, she said that she was very affected by the death of a 34-week-old baby and that she could not communicate with the mother out of sadness.” (no. 3)

Training and Skill Needs

Although many skills are taught in medical and paramedical schools, some skills, such as communication skills, require hands-on training and practice. Dealing with death, sympathizing with the bereaved family, and how to deliver bad news are among the communication skills that must be learned in practice. This is despite the fact that it has been briefly addressed in some curricula and service providers express who are not prepared for this situation. They admit that they experience stress, sadness, and despair when faced with the death of a fetus or patient, and dealing with these events repeatedly is exhausting.

Communication skills

All the participants stated that they had no courses for dealing with bereaved persons or giving bad news in their educational curriculum. Effective communication skills should be learned in medical schools. Attitudes and abilities should start from hospital internships and be completed in retraining. But, students have not learned how to deal with bereaved mothers in their training. Poor communication and conversation with the mother can be very destructive. Many HCPs thought they were providing empathy correctly, but they used wrong sentences and inappropriate words.

“Did you have a ruptured amniotic sac since the morning? The child has slowed down since last night! ”You’ve come late…’ Unfortunately, with this inappropriate communication, we implicitly instill in the mother ‘If you had come earlier, this would not have happened. So you are to blame for the death of your fetus.’ (no. 2.4)

“You should thank God. What would you do if the child was born with a defect? It’s all right… You’re young… You can get pregnant in three months.” (no. 3)

Appropriate verbal communication and the use of nonverbal behavior by the HCPs can be a big step in delivering the bad news and preparing bereaved mothers. The need for practical workshops about bereavement and loss, learning how to break bad news, and the exercise of empathy in practice were the most important statements of the participants.

“A mother/parent has lost a child. You want to give them this news… I think it is very painful and the situation is difficult… very difficult! You must have the skill to be able to empathize with them and tell them that you understand how hard it is.” (no. 1.2.10.12.14.9)

Self-care needs

Generally, after a traumatic experience, talking to a colleague is one of the strategies that employees use to take care of themselves. Furthermore, receiving help from a private counselor and going to relaxation classes were mentioned.

“When I share a tragic work experience with my colleague, I am released from tension. I couldn’t discuss the work problem at home, but I could share it with my colleagues. This way, I could continue my work… Sometimes I want to cry, but professional rules don’t allow it.. Last week, a mother who had IVF gave birth to twins. A few moments later, they died.” (no. 2.7)

Increasing the psychological well-being of employees is very important. Organizations should provide self-care program for employees. Self-care programs help individuals to protect themselves from tension, improve mental health, and better solve organizational challenges. “Repeated dealing with abortion and stillbirth is terrible… quite terrible. It is exhausting… After a while, I had regular encounters with these mothers. I had to go to an anxiety management class. When you deal with these mothers, you experience grief… I am much better now” (no. 6.12)

Discussion

This research is the first context-based study on healthcare Professionals’ Needs in Iran. According to the informants, one of their professional needs was having sufficient personnel and the hiring of skilled and qualified staff to improve the service. In line with Cho et al.[28] adequate staff are required to reduce missed care and enhance the quality of care. The informants’ experiences suggest that legal gaps in legislation and support policies may lead to hiring incompetent HCPs. As stated by Mosadeghrad, the quantity and quality of HCPs affect the quality of service. Healthcare managers must have distinctive approaches to attracting and retaining qualified staff capable of providing the highest quality of service. Current recruitment policies and practices in Iran should be reviewed to support managers in identifying and recruiting the most suitable person to provide quality services.[29]

Providing a suitable environment for these mothers has been one of the professional needs and concerns of the personnel. According to the Perinatal Society of Australia and New Zealand Clinical Practice Guideline, providing a quiet and private space that is separate from pregnant mothers and newborn babies is among the recommendations. However, the existing guidelines in Iran are only for the physical care of mothers after abortion and stillbirth. In the absence of guidelines, using care providers’ experiences and paying attention to their needs can be effective in improving the performance of their clinical care, and it was one of the goals of this research. However, Australia, Canada, England, and Ireland have valuable guidelines and recommend complete care in various aspects based on documentation.[3031]

Research has shown that parents’ experience of fetal loss is influenced by the HCP’s ability to provide bereavement care[6]; still, many staff mentioned that they were not prepared by the educational curriculum or clinical course to deal with bereaved parents.[32]

Healthcare workers confronted with bereaved families need training, support, and culture- and context-specific care guidelines.[33] Wool and Catlin described the most important education and training components as communication with women and families.[34] Some studies have shown that training workshops,[35] and using workbooks for teaching bereavement care to students increase confidence,[36] self-awareness, and self-compassion and support their knowledge in providing service.[35] Leitao et al.,[37] organized a training workshop with participant-centered teaching methods for maternity staff and showed that they positively commented on the content of the workshop. Similarly, a scoping review was conducted by Qian et al.,[32] about the effects of the PBC education program on knowledge, confidence, and satisfaction. Our findings showed that the needs of health service providers are not limited to the curriculum; rather, students should be trained in practical skills through internships in the clinical environment. In the postemployment period, they should update their knowledge and awareness of their communication skills; deficiencies through continuing education, practical courses, and repeated workshops with more practical content which also have promotion incentives.

Another important subject for healthcare professionals is self-care. Caring for bereaved parents imposes additional stress on healthcare personnel that must be considered.[38] Researchers reported that HCPs are vulnerable to compassion fatigue and need to take care of themselves.[39] Andrews et al.[40] found that self-care may increase the personnel’s ability to manage emotions and prevent burnout and compassion fatigue. They also mentioned that self-care should be considered from the beginning of the education. Our results agree with those of “Verdon” and Dementing,” which highlight that providing team meetings by managers can help employees express their emotions.[41] Providing psychological access to clinical psychologists by managers,[42] improving interactions with colleagues through individual training,[43] and using stress management interventions can be effective for self-empowerment[44]

Limitation and recommendation

This research is one of the few context-based studies on healthcare Professionals’ Needs in Iran and may not reflect the needs of service providers in other communities. It was a novelty and strength in our study. Also, the results of this research will be used to design and implement the bereavement care program in this setting. Another limitation in some settings was the fear of the manager’s view of the care providers. Stereotypical thinking about the current routines made the HCPs think that fulfilling their needs was out of reach, and they considered it useless to talk about it. Friendly interaction and ensuring confidentiality, using motivational methods in the interview, and reassuring the participants helped the researchers overcome these obstacles.

Conclusion

Our results showed that the service providers’ needs were diverse. Some of these needs, such as developing national guidelines for bereavement care, employing qualified and sufficient staff, and providing training on dealing with bereavement in the curriculum of medical and paramedic sciences, are related to the policies and laws of the MoHME. Some other needs could be met through internal management of the organizations, such as internal supportive rules, continuous in-service training, self-care programs, debriefing after perinatal loss, and holding meetings with employees to relieve their tensions. To improve the quality of services and gain job satisfaction and a sense of worth, it is necessary to meet the needs of service providers.
