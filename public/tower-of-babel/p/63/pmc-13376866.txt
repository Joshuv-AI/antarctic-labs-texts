Spatial distribution characteristics and protection strategies of National intangible cultural heritage in the Pearl river basin
Authors: Ying Wang, Rong Yu, Jiaying Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile the spatial analysis of Intangible Cultural Heritage (ICH) is well-established at national and provincial scales, cross-jurisdictional river basins remain a critical research gap. This study investigates the spatial heterogeneity of 289 national-level ICH items within China’s Pearl River Basin to inform regional conservation planning. Employing Kernel Density Estimation and the Geographical Detector, the analysis identifies distribution patterns and quantifies the influence of key geographical and socioeconomic factors. Results reveal a pronounced upstream-downstream gradient, with ICH concentration culminating in Guangdong Province (52.2%), and a significantly clustered distribution forming primary agglomerations in the Pearl River Delta and Chaoshan regions. The Geodetector analysis indicates that topography is the dominant factor shaping the spatial pattern (= 0.27), while its interaction with transportation exerts the strongest synergistic effect ( = 0.393). These findings suggest that the geography of national-level ICH is shaped more by foundational, path-dependent landscape features than by contemporary macro-economic indicators. Consequently, effective conservation necessitates a differentiated framework that focuses on protecting cultural originality in upstream “source” areas while leveraging the industrial capacity of downstream “sink” areas for sustainable development.Keywords: Intangible cultural heritage, Spatial distribution, Influencing factors, Cultural IP, Pearl river basinSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

ICH refers to artistic performances, folkloric activities, craftsmanship, traditional knowledge, and associated artifacts and spaces transmitted across generations by diverse ethnic groups. This encompasses traditional oral literature, fine arts, calligraphy, music, dance, and technical arts1,2. Characterized by ethnic distinctiveness, dynamic evolution, and intergenerational continuity, ICH embodies the unique cultural essence of various ethnic groups3,4. Its protection is not merely a matter of preservation but is increasingly recognized as a vital component of global sustainable development. Safeguarding ICH directly contributes to the United Nations’ 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, particularly Target 11.4 of SDG 11 (“strengthen efforts to protect and safeguard the world’s cultural and natural heritage”) and Target 8.9 of SDG 8 (“promote sustainable tourism that creates jobs and promotes local culture and products”) 5. In this context, China’s national policy has explicitly mandated strengthened ICH preservation within major strategic initiatives6.

The Pearl River Basin, situated in southern China, spans Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi, and Guangdong provinces. More than just a hydrological feature, this study conceptualizes the basin as a dynamic river-basin cultural system. Historically, it has served as a vital cultural corridor for exchange between the ethnically diverse highlands of the interior and the coastal centers of commerce, nurturing a pluralistic ecosystem inhabited by numerous ethnic minorities including the Zhuang, Miao, and Yao7,8. Similarly, Li et al. (2024) highlighted that the distribution of ethnic minority cultural heritage in southwestern China exhibits polarization characteristics, with high-density clusters predominantly concentrated in the Yunnan-Guizhou-Guangxi border region, where spatial differentiation is significantly influenced by the proportion of mountainous terrain, ethnic population distribution, and urbanization rate9. This suggests that the current study should focus on the preservation patterns of ICH and policy adaptability for diverse ethnic groups within the basin. Huang et al. (2025) further indicated that the digital conservation practices of waterside settlements in the Jiangnan region demonstrate that integrating Geographic Information System (GIS) and 3D modeling technologies to construct dynamic cultural landscape databases can significantly enhance heritage monitoring efficiency and public engagement10, thereby providing critical technical insights for this study to explore ICH conservation strategies at the watershed scale. The basin demonstrates representative ICH density and distinctive spatial distribution patterns. Amid rapid socioeconomic development, the region exhibits pronounced intercultural convergence of multi-ethnic ICH traditions. Therefore, investigating the spatial distribution of ICH in a critical region like the Pearl River Basin, analyzing its evolutionary patterns, and proposing targeted conservation strategies are essential for promoting both regional cultural transmission and sustainable development.

International scholarship demonstrates earlier engagement with spatial analysis of ICH, building upon a rich tradition in cultural geography of understanding the interplay between culture and place. Foundational scholarship has long established that cultural landscapes are products of complex human-environment interactions11, that the distribution of cultural phenomena reflects underlying social and historical processes12, and that their diffusion through space follows observable patterns13. Modern pioneering studies now apply quantitative tools like remote sensing and GIS to investigate these patterns, focusing on digital management, distribution analysis, and disaster vulnerability14–17.Domestic academic inquiries primarily focus on two dimensions: First, spatial distribution characteristics of ICH projects are analyzed through kernel density estimation and related geostatistical methods, elucidating regional distribution patterns and their determinants. At the national scale, studies have revealed that ICH is significantly concentrated in southeastern China, particularly in the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei and Yangtze River Delta regions. Natural resource endowments and socio-economic factors are identified as key spatial drivers in these patterns. However, the explanatory power of these factors varies across studies. Research consistently shows a strong affinity for regions with favorable climatic conditions and deep historical-cultural legacies18,19.

Within the Yellow River Basin, socioeconomic factors emerge as the primary determinant of ICH distribution patterns. The quantitative and spatial dimensions of ICH demonstrate significant correlations with regional economic development levels and transmission feasibility, while confronting challenges including intense developmental pressures and insufficient interpretation of contemporary values20,21. In the Yangtze River Basin, the Yangtze River Delta, Southeast Guizhou, and the Hubei-Hunan-Guizhou-Chongqing border region constitute core ICH clusters. Developmental disparities persist among ten ICH categories, particularly evident in traditional sports, theater arts, and medicinal practices22,23, with parallel investigations conducted in other regions24–26.

To conceptualize these spatial dynamics, this study adapts the “source-sink” framework from landscape ecology. In its original context, a “source” landscape generates a surplus (e.g., of a species or pollutant), while a “sink” landscape experiences a net accumulation. We translate this metaphor to cultural geography to model the flow and concentration of ICH. Within our framework, cultural “source” regions are defined not by surplus production, but by high cultural originality and authenticity. These correspond to the upstream, ethnically diverse areas of Yunnan and Guizhou, which serve as crucibles for unique traditions preserved in relative isolation. Conversely, cultural “sink” regions are areas of high cultural accumulation and density, typically the downstream economic and population centers like the Pearl River Delta. These “sinks” attract, concentrate, and often reinterpret various cultural forms, which are then supported by robust economic infrastructure and practiced by larger populations, leading to the high-density clusters observed in our data. This framework allows us to analyze the spatial dynamics of heritage not as a static pattern, but as a process of flow and concentration shaped by the basin’s distinct geographical and socioeconomic gradients.

The second research strand examines ICH preservation and development under the multifaceted interplay of natural resources and socioeconomic elements. Representative studies employ correlation analyses to quantify differential explanatory power of economic development indices and demographic distributions on ICH spatial configurations27–29. ICH spatial manifestation exhibits marked regional heterogeneity shaped by conjoint influences from socioeconomic conditions, physiographic characteristics, and cultural contexts. Moreover, distinct ICH typologies exhibit pronounced disparities in spatial clustering intensity, generating empirical foundations for deciphering distribution mechanisms and formulating preservation strategies.

From a spatial analytical perspective, three research gaps are apparent. First, current scholarship predominantly operates at national or provincial scales, with limited cross-jurisdictional investigations of major river basins like the Pearl River. Second, the thematic focus has been on identifying spatial patterns rather than analyzing the synergistic relationship between ICH transmission and regional socioeconomic systems. Finally, actionable strategic proposals derived from spatial analytics remain underdeveloped in the academic discourse. This study aims to address these gaps.

Notably, the international academic community has intensified its exploration of the spatial distribution of cultural resources and their impact mechanisms in recent years. Recent scholarship, for instance, has provided comprehensive reviews of global research on rural settlement patterns, highlighting a shift from descriptive analysis toward sustainable development and identifying key research frontiers30. Methodologically, studies are increasingly employing systematic quantitative classification to interpret the complex relationship between landscape patterns and settlement forms within specific basins31. And Zhang et al. (2025) employed a Geographical Detector to reveal the close correlation between the distribution of traditional villages and ICH in the Lingnan region, emphasizing the driving role of socioeconomic factors in cultural agglomeration, thereby providing methodological references for regional collaborative conservation32. While these advances are significant, cross-jurisdictional studies of major, culturally diverse river basins like the Pearl River remain a critical research gap that this paper aims to address.

This study establishes a comprehensive research framework to systematically analyze the spatial dynamics of ICH in the Pearl River Basin. The framework proceeds through three logical stages. First, we conduct data acquisition and processing, compiling a geospatial database of 289 national-level ICH items. Second, we perform a multi-method spatial analysis, using the Nearest Neighbor Index (NNI) and Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) to identify spatial distribution patterns, and employing the Geographical Detector model to diagnose the influence of key driving factors. This stage moves from identifying correlational patterns to exploring underlying spatial heterogeneity, drawing on approaches from recent advanced computational studies33,34. Finally, based on these empirical findings, we move to the formulation of strategies, proposing differentiated and actionable conservation pathways tailored to the basin’s unique cultural geography. This integrated framework ensures that our policy recommendations are directly grounded in robust spatial evidence.

The remainder of this paper is organized to follow this framework. Section 2 details the data and analytical methods. Section 3 presents the empirical results of the spatial analysis. Section 4 provides an in-depth discussion, interpreting the meaning and mechanisms behind these findings. Building on this analysis, Sect. 5 outlines our differentiated conservation strategies. The paper concludes with an overview of the study’s limitations and key contributions.

Data and methods

Data sources

The datasets utilized in this study were obtained from authoritative domestic repositories. The ICH data comprises the complete inventory of national-level items announced by the State Council in five official batches between 2006 and 2021, sourced from the China Intangible Cultural Heritage Digital Museum (http://www.ihchina.cn/). This timeframe was selected as it represents the most comprehensive and up-to-date official dataset available at the time of analysis, allowing for a robust cross-sectional study of the established heritage landscape. The socioeconomic data for influencing factors, including GDP and population grids, are for the year 2020, providing a stable and recent economic snapshot that aligns with the finalization of the ICH inventory. Hydrological and administrative boundary data for the Pearl River Basin were acquired from the National Platform for Common GeoSpatial Information Services (Tianditu; <https://www.tianditu.gov.cn/%3E) managed by the Institute of Geographic Sciences and Natural Resources Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Tianditu is the official, public, and free geospatial service platform managed by the Ministry of Natural Resources of China. A total of 289 national-level ICH vector datasets within the Pearl River Basin were processed and analyzed using GeoScene Pro 4.0, a professional geographic information system (GIS) platform. Methodology

To investigate the spatial distribution of ICH and its underlying determinants, this study employs a multi-method analytical approach. The selection of influencing factors is grounded in established research, which indicates that the formation and transmission of cultural heritage are shaped by a combination of foundational physical geography and evolving socioeconomic conditions. We therefore selected five key indicators across these two dimensions: Topography and Hydrography represent the stable natural environment that provides the initial context for cultural development, while Transportation, GDP, and Population serve as proxies for the human activities, economic vitality, and social structures that influence the contemporary vitality and concentration of ICH.

Results and analysis

Geospatial distribution characteristics

Structural typology analysis

As shown in Table 1, the ICH distribution in the Pearl River Basin exhibits a clear “source - sink” pattern. From Yunnan and Guizhou in the upper reaches to Guangxi in the middle reaches and Guangdong in the lower reaches, the number of ICH items increases progressively, accounting for 6.9%, 18.0%, 21.1%, and 52.2% of the basin’s total, respectively. Among the 10 ICH categories, Guangdong ranks first in nine categories (excluding Folk Literature), highlighting its role as a cultural hub where music, dance, and drama flourish due to its large population. Guangdong’s strong economy provides ample funding for ICH protection, creating a positive feedback loop where the growth of the heritage industry, in turn, supports the local economy. In contrast, the diverse natural geography of Yunnan, Guizhou, and Guangxi has given rise to unique ethnic Folk Literature, Traditional Music, and Traditional Dance, with Folk Literature being particularly prominent and showing great potential for ICH development. Notably, Guizhou and Guangxi have 11 and 9 items of Traditional Music, respectively, and both have 9 items of Traditional Dance, indicating a mutually reinforcing relationship between these two categories. Traditional Music’s prosperity has also driven the development of Traditional Dance in these regions. Among the 10 ICH categories, Traditional Dance is the most numerous, accounting for 16.9%, while Traditional Sports, Games, and Acrobatics make up only 2.8%, suggesting that dance is a primary means of cultural expression in the region.

To statistically validate the spatial distribution patterns of ICH in the Pearl River Basin, the Nearest Neighbor Index (NNI) method was employed. The analysis tests the null hypothesis that the ICH items are distributed randomly. A z-score outside the range of -1.96 to + 1.96 for a < 0.05 indicates that the null hypothesis can be rejected, confirming a significant pattern of clustering or dispersion. Kernel density analysis

Having established with the NNI analysis that the overall distribution of ICH is statistically clustered, Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) was then employed to identify the specific geographic locations of these significant agglomerations—the effective “hot spots” of cultural heritage in the basin (Fig. 1). The analysis reveals a pronounced clustering pattern, with two primary clusters emerging in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao region and the Chaoshan region, in addition to four secondary clusters located in Guiyang City (Guizhou Province), Nanning City (Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region), Kunming City (Yunnan Province), and the eastern border regions between Guizhou and Guangxi. Notably, the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao area serves as the core distribution zone of Lingnan culture, characterized by exceptionally dense ICH resources. The Chaoshan region, recognized as one of Guangdong’s three major cultural systems, preserves distinct vestiges of ancient Central Plains civilization. As provincial capitals, Guiyang, Nanning, and Kunming exhibit significant cultural agglomeration effects within their respective administrative boundaries. The eastern Guizhou-Guangxi border region represents a unique zone of multi-ethnic cultural convergence; on the Guizhou side, this area encompasses the Qiandongnan Miao and Dong Autonomous Prefecture and the Qiannan Buyi and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, while the Guangxi portion includes the Huanjiang Maonan Autonomous County, Luocheng Mulam Autonomous County, Rongshui Miao Autonomous County, and Sanjiang Dong Autonomous County. This ethnically diverse landscape provides a geographically and culturally privileged environment conducive to the emergence and flourishing of ICH practices.

Based on the kernel density distribution maps of different categories (see Fig. 2), the analysis demonstrates pronounced ICH agglomeration effects in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao region and Chaoshan area, a spatial pattern intrinsically linked to their profound cultural legacies. Furthermore, Traditional Music, Traditional Dance, Traditional Quyi (Chinese storytelling arts), Traditional Craftsmanship, and Folk Customs exhibit clustered distributions in Guiyang City and the eastern Guizhou-Guangxi border area. Notably, Folk Literature shows concentrated patterns in Kunming City and a belt-shaped zone spanning Rongjiang County (Guizhou) to Hechi and Baise (Guangxi), which correlates strongly with the dense distribution of ethnic autonomous counties and townships inhabited by Zhuang, Miao, and Dong ethnic groups in these regions.

Analysis of factors associated with spatial distribution

To quantitatively diagnose the driving forces behind the spatial patterns of ICH, we employed the Geographical Detector model. The results, presented in Table 3, reveal a complex interplay between foundational environmental factors and contemporary socioeconomic conditions. Overall, the analysis indicates that the Physical Geography dimension (Topography, =0.27; Hydrography, =0.11) exerts a much stronger influence than the Socioeconomic dimension (Transportation, q = 0.08; Population, =0.02; GDP, =0.01). Notably, the statistical insignificance of broad indicators like GDP and Population is a critical finding. It suggests that the spatial logic of national-level ICH is not dictated by sheer economic mass or population density at a macro scale, but is more deeply rooted in the enduring influence of the physical landscape and historical patterns of connectivity.

Influence of topography and geomorphology

The Pearl River Basin features the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau to the northwest, the Nanling Mountains to the north, and the southeastern hills to the east, forming a terrain that is high in the east, north, and west and low in the south. According to the spatial analysis results in Fig. 4, among the 289 national-level ICH items in the Pearl River Basin, 40 (13.84%) are located in medium and medium-high altitude areas, while 249 (86.16%) are in low-altitude areas. This indicates that high altitude significantly constrains the distribution of ICH, with the majority concentrated in low-altitude regions.

From a topographic perspective, 88 ICH items (30.45%) are situated in mountainous and loess ridge areas, with 69 of these (78.41%) located in low-altitude regions with small or medium undulations. Flat, open, and low-altitude areas in the Pearl River Basin therefore promote the human interaction and cultural exchange necessary for ICH dissemination. In contrast, medium-high altitude areas with steep slopes are relatively isolated, hindering the flow of cultural resources. These regions have poorer ecological conditions for human habitation and lower population density, resulting in limited ICH exchange and a smaller overall number of ICH items.

However, on the other hand, this isolation has, to some extent, protected local cultures from external influences, preserving the authenticity of indigenous cultural heritage. See Fig. 4 for details.

Influence of proximity to water systems

Settling near water sources is a common pattern for human survival and development, as abundant water resources provide the essential material foundation for socio-cultural progress. By conducting a buffer analysis of first- to fifth-level rivers in the Pearl River Basin (note: there are no second-level rivers in the basin), it was found that among the 289 national-level ICH items in the Pearl River Basin, 199 (68.86%) are located within a 10-kilometer buffer zone of water systems, with 125 of these situated within the 1–5 km buffer zone.

This significant spatial distribution pattern is closely related to the ecosystem services provided by rivers. River buffer zones are relatively flat and fertile, offering rich natural conditions for human cultivation. Additionally, river navigation serves as a means of communication and connection, facilitating the exchange and integration of ICH. It can be said that rivers and their adjacent areas act as channels and spaces for nurturing ICH, and the proximity to water systems has a clear influence on the distribution of ICH. Influence of proximity to transportation networks

Transportation infrastructure in the Pearl River Basin profoundly shapes the lifestyles, production methods, and regional distribution of its inhabitants. By conducting a buffer analysis of major transportation routes (railways, national highways, and expressways) in the Pearl River Basin, it was found that among the 289 national-level ICH items in the region, 154 (53.29%) are located within 500 m of transportation routes, 61 (21.11%) are within 500–1000 m, 61 (21.11%) are within 1000–2000 m, 10 (3.46%) are within 2000–5000 m, and 3 (1.04%) are located beyond 5000 m. For the analysis, continuous independent variables (Elevation, Distance to Hydrography, GDP, Population, and Distance to Transportation) were discretized into categorical strata. We employed the Natural Breaks (Jenks) optimization method for this classification. This method is ideal for identifying natural groupings inherent in the data as it minimizes variance within each class while maximizing the variance between classes, thus ensuring a statistically robust stratification for detecting spatial heterogeneity.

From a historical perspective, the Pearl River Basin has experienced continuous development and utilization activities from the Qin and Han dynasties to the Ming and Qing dynasties. Leveraging its hydrological systems, topography, and geomorphology, major transportation corridors such as the Ancient Qin-Han Road, the Miaojiang Ancient Road, and the Hanjiang Ancient Post Road were established. Within the basin, additional corridors, including the Xijiang Ancient Post Road, the Dongjiang-Hanjiang Ancient Post Road, and the Chaohui Ancient Post Road, were also formed. These key transportation routes significantly contributed to the economic and social prosperity of the regions they traversed, providing a robust material foundation for the emergence and expansion of national-level ICH. To analyze this relationship, the historical corridors were mapped schematically based on established historical geography, and their paths were digitized as line features in GeoScene Pro 4.0. This visualization (presented in Fig. 7) allows for a qualitative assessment of the spatial correlation between these historical arteries and the contemporary distribution of ICH clusters.

Influence of gross domestic product (GDP)

By overlaying the 2020 GDP per square kilometer data, captured as raster data with a spatial resolution of 1 × 1, with the distribution of 289 national-level ICH items in the Pearl River Basin, it is evident that regions with higher economic output are primarily concentrated in the Pearl River Delta urban agglomeration and provincial capital/metropolitan areas. A striking result from our analysis is the low explanatory power of macro-level socioeconomic indicators. Both GDP ( =0.01) and Population ( =0.02) were found to be statistically insignificant drivers of the overall ICH distribution pattern. This does not imply that economic and social contexts are irrelevant; rather, it suggests that their influence is more complex than these broad variables can capture. For instance, while the high concentration of ICH in the economically dominant Pearl River Delta (Fig. 8) appears contradictory, our model indicates that this is not a direct function of GDP itself. The GDP data in this map are classified using the Natural Breaks (Jenks) method, which identifies natural groupings inherent in the data to best represent the skewed distribution of economic activity within the basin. Instead, the Delta’s economic strength likely provides the fertile ground—funding, infrastructure, and markets—that allows heritage traditions to thrive and agglomerate, an effect that is captured more strongly through the interaction with transportation and geography. The insignificance of these factors highlights a key limitation of basin-scale analysis and points toward the need for future research using more refined, culturally-specific indicators, as discussed in the following section.

Analysis of the interaction of influencing factors

The interaction between different factors affecting the spatial distribution of ICH in the Pearl River Basin was analyzed using a Geographical Detector to identify the interaction between two different factors on ICH spatial distribution. The factor values of the two factors were first calculated, followed by the value when the two factors interact. By comparing the three different values, the interaction type between the two factors was determined. The interaction results are presented in Table 4. The interaction between different factors was classified as Dual-Factor Enhancement (DFE) or Nonlinear Enhancement (NLE). DFE indicates that the combined influence of the two factors is greater than their respective influences, suggesting a synergistic effect. NLE indicates that the combined influence of the two factors is greater than their respective influences or the sum of their influences, suggesting that the influence of the two factors is not only additive but also has a non-linear enhancement effect, which is usually greater than DFE. Through calculation, topography showed a Dual-Factor Enhancement with economic development and population, and a Nonlinear Enhancement with transportation; hydrography showed a Dual-Factor Enhancement with economic development and population, and a Nonlinear Enhancement with transportation. The interaction between topography and transportation was the strongest (0.393), indicating that suitable physical geography and convenient transportation have a synergistic effect on the distribution of ICH in the Pearl River Basin, indicating that their combined effect is greater than the sum of their individual effects, a phenomenon known as synergistic enhancement. This powerful synergy is logical: topography dictates the path of least resistance for movement, meaning historical transportation corridors naturally formed along river valleys and through mountain passes. These corridors became the primary arteries for trade, migration, and cultural exchange, channeling cultural practices and innovations towards accessible population centers. Therefore, the combination of a favorable landscape and an established transportation network creates a powerful feedback loop that is far more influential in concentrating ICH than either factor in isolation.

Discussion

The cultural “source-sink” gradient: A framework for understanding ICH dynamics

The most prominent finding of this study is the pronounced “source-sink” phenomenon, characterized by an incremental accumulation of ICH from the upstream headwaters to the downstream delta. This spatial gradient validates the analytical framework we adapted from landscape ecology. The upstream provinces of Yunnan and Guizhou, rich in unique ethnic traditions but with lower overall ICH counts, function as cultural “sources.” Their complex topography and relative isolation have historically served as crucibles for cultural originality, preserving distinct practices that form the bedrock of the basin’s heritage diversity.

Conversely, the Pearl River Delta in Guangdong acts as a powerful cultural “sink.” Its role as a long-standing economic and demographic center has created a gravitational pull, attracting and concentrating a high volume and diversity of ICH. This is not merely a passive accumulation; the region’s robust economic foundation provides the necessary resources—funding, markets, and audiences—to sustain and a high degree of living vitality for a wide array of cultural forms, from traditional craftsmanship to Cantonese opera, transforming them into high-value cultural assets. This dynamic confirms that the “source-sink” model is a valuable heuristic for understanding the net results of cultural flow and concentration in a major river basin. While a micro-level analysis of individual inheritor movements would be required to map these flows directly, our framework provides a robust macro-level interpretation of the static patterns that result from these complex, long-term dynamics.

Unpacking the driving forces: the primacy of landscape and the paradox of economic growth

Our Geodetector analysis provides a deeper understanding of the mechanisms behind this spatial structure. It is important to note that the -statistic is a measure of spatial stratified heterogeneity, quantifying how well the stratification of a factor explains the spatial pattern of ICH, rather than a regression coefficient that controls for spatial autocorrelation. The dominance of physical geography—specifically Topography ( = 0.27)—as the leading single factor is therefore significant, suggesting that the foundational template for ICH distribution is strongly linked to the landscape itself. Mountainous terrain in the upstream regions has historically fostered cultural isolation and differentiation, while the open plains and river networks downstream have facilitated cultural exchange and fusion.

However, the interaction analysis reveals a more nuanced story. The powerful synergistic effect between Topography and Transportation (=0.393) demonstrates that landscape provides the potential, but connectivity actualizes it. Our use of Euclidean distance as a metric is designed to capture this long-term, generalized accessibility, reflecting how historical transportation corridors, shaped by topography, have acted as the primary arteries for the flow of people, ideas, and cultural practices over centuries. These corridors channeled heritage resources towards the economic centers in the delta. While a more granular, network-based travel-time analysis would be essential for studying contemporary tourism flows, our approach is well-suited to revealing the foundational, path-dependent relationship between landscape and cultural concentration.

Perhaps the most insightful finding is the statistical insignificance of macro-level socioeconomic indicators like GDP ( =0.01) and Population ( =0.02) as direct drivers. This apparent paradox does not negate the importance of economic context. Rather, it suggests that the spatial logic of deeply rooted, national-level ICH is not determined by contemporary economic mass or population size, especially when measured with coarse proxies like gridded GDP. Instead, its distribution appears to be a legacy of long-term historical and geographical path dependencies. The economic prosperity of a region like the Pearl River Delta is therefore better understood as an enabler that sustains and amplifies existing cultural concentrations, rather than a direct cause of their initial formation. This distinction is critical for formulating effective and historically informed conservation policies.

Our findings regarding the agglomeration of ICH towards the economically developed Pearl River Delta offer a novel perspective when contrasted with recent studies in other major basins. For instance, research in the Yellow River Basin has largely focused on the coupling coordination between high-quality economic development and ecological protection37. Unlike those works, which emphasize the trade-offs and synergies within the ecological-economic nexus, our study delineates a ‘cultural-economic’ interaction where heritage resources actively migrate towards and flourish within economic ‘sinks.’ This pattern mirrors the dynamics of supply chain resilience observed in industrial sectors, where resources flow towards nodes with robust infrastructure and market capacity38. Just as resilient supply chains rely on diversified sourcing and strong logistical hubs, the sustainability of ICH in the Pearl River Basin depends on the ‘upstream source’ diversity and the ‘downstream sink’ industrial capacity, suggesting that cultural heritage management can borrow analytical frameworks from economic geography and supply chain dynamics.

Limitations and future work

While this study provides a systematic analysis of the spatial patterns of national-level ICH in the Pearl River Basin, it is important to acknowledge several inherent limitations. These boundaries, in turn, illuminate a clear and promising agenda for future research, which can be organized around three key themes: scope, variables, and methodology.

First, the study’s analytical scope is defined by its scale and temporality. Our focus on national-level ICH ensures a high standard of data authority and allows for a macro-level understanding of the basin’s most prominent cultural assets. However, this approach necessarily omits the vast and diverse landscape of provincial and municipal heritage. Similarly, by analyzing the data at a specific point in time, we provide a valuable static snapshot of the current distribution but cannot capture the dynamic processes of cultural evolution over time. Future research should therefore adopt multi-scalar and longitudinal approaches to build a more comprehensive and dynamic understanding of the region’s heritage systems.

Second, the analysis is shaped by the nature of the influencing factors and the scale at which they were examined. The indicators used for economic development and population, while effective at a macro scale, serve as broad proxies for more complex socioeconomic processes. Furthermore, by operating at the provincial level, our factor analysis is well-suited to capture macro-regional dynamics but is limited by a small sample size, which can reduce statistical power. Future research should build upon our findings by conducting more granular analysis at the municipal or even county level. This would not only provide a larger sample size for more robust statistical modeling but would also allow for the incorporation of more nuanced and culturally-grounded indicators. For instance, a “cultural niche width” index, combining fine-grained ethnic population data with ICH richness, could offer a powerful variable for explaining heritage vitality.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge the methodological scope of our analysis. The Geographical Detector is a powerful tool for identifying spatial associations, but it does not inherently establish causality. Therefore, while our findings reveal strong associations, these should be interpreted as significant correlations rather than direct causal relationships. Future work could advance this inquiry by employing more sophisticated analytical techniques. For instance, local indicators of spatial association (LISA), such as Getis-Ord Gi*, could be used to statistically test the significance of individual hot spots, while causal inference methods may offer a robust framework for moving beyond correlation to a clearer understanding of the causal mechanisms driving ICH distribution33.

Differentiated conservation strategies

The preceding discussion has established the complex spatial logic of ICH in the Pearl River Basin, highlighting the foundational role of geography and the enabling context of socioeconomic development. These insights demand a move away from a one-size-fits-all approach toward a differentiated conservation framework.Based on the above analysis, the spatial distribution of ICH in the Pearl River Basin exhibits three key characteristics: First, in terms of the quantity of ICH, a distinct “source-sink” phenomenon is observed, with ICH density increasing from the upstream regions of Yunnan, Guizhou, and Guangxi to the downstream Pearl River Delta, where Guangdong Province alone accounts for 52.2% of the total ICH in the basin. Second, regarding the spatial aggregation of ICH categories, among the 10 categories of ICH, indigenous forms such as folk literature, traditional music, and traditional performing arts are predominantly located in the midstream and upstream regions, while the other six categories exhibit significant high-density clustering in the Pearl River Delta, a pattern further supported by the overall kernel density distribution of 298 ICH sites, confirming the Pearl River Delta as the primary aggregation area for ICH in the basin and a focal point for ICH protection, inheritance, and industrial development. Third, in terms of the influence of factors on ICH distribution, single-factor effects show that the spatial distribution of ICH is primarily constrained by natural geographical elements such as topography and hydrological systems, while dual-factor interactions reveal that the combination of favorable natural conditions and convenient transportation plays a critical role in shaping the distribution of ICH, with the anticipation that indigenous ICH in regions experiencing rapid socio-economic development will have broader prospects for inheritance and development. Based on the spatial analysis of ICH distribution and its influencing factors, this study proposes a differentiated conservation framework. The strategies are tailored to the unique cultural-ecological characteristics of the upstream “source” regions (Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi) and the downstream “sink” region (Guangdong). The goal is to move beyond generalized recommendations toward an actionable roadmap for policymakers, balancing heritage preservation with sustainable regional development. Leveraging natural and cultural endowments to cultivate ICH cultural IP

The spatial distribution of ICH in the Pearl River Basin is shaped by its Physical Geography : the northwestern Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau, northern Nanling Mountains, and eastern Southeast Hills create a terrain descending from east, north, and west to south, which fundamentally influences ICH categories and characteristics. Yunnan, Guizhou, and Guangxi dominate in Folk Literature (82.35%), Traditional Music (60.00%), and Folk Customs (57.63%), collectively accounting for 82.35%, 60.00%, and 57.63% of these categories basin-wide. As China’s historic ethnic enclave, these upstream/midstream regions host Tibetan, Zhuang, Yao, Miao, and other minorities, resulting in ICH with distinct ethnic-regional signatures. In contrast, the downstream Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area excels in 9 categories (e.g., Traditional Fine Arts, Traditional Dance, Traditional Drama, Traditional Craftsmanship), except Folk Literature. To amplify regional ICH influence, upstream areas should leverage their natural/cultural assets (e.g., mountain landscapes, ethnic diversity) to systematically refine ethnic ICH essences, synergizing ecotourism and cultural tourism to cultivate ICH cultural IPs. Meanwhile, the downstream Peart River Delta—with its dominant ICH diversity and mature cultural industries—must demonstrate Socioeconomic leadership to drive upstream-midstream collaboration, fostering basin-wide ICH IP industrialization. Developing basin-representative ICH IPs requires converting latent ICH resources into high-recognition, high-monetization cultural symbols (e.g., Dong, Zhuang, Yi, Cantonese, Hakka traditions), constructing IP-centric industrial chains to establish new economic growth poles.

Leveraging regional economic advantages to strengthen the foundation of ICH industries

Based on the spatial overlay analysis of 2020 GDP per km² and 289 national-level ICH items in the Pearl River Basin, regions with stronger economic aggregates—primarily the Pearl River Delta (PRD) urban agglomeration and provincial capitals—constitute the main Aggregated (Agg.) zones for ICH. Enhanced socioeconomic inputs exhibit significant Dual-Factor Enhancement (DFE) effects on ICH productivity. However, outside the PRD and capital regions, most areas demonstrate low ICH tourism revenue despite rich ethnic ICH resources: examples include Tiandong County, Guangxi (representative Zhuang ICH: Miluotuo epic, Zhuang Lion Dance); Rongjiang and Congjiang Counties, Guizhou (Dong ICH: Zhulang Niangmei opera, Dong Pipa songs, Dong timber architecture techniques); and Honghe Hani and Yi Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan (Yi ICH: Hani Four-Season Production Ballad, Yi Cigarette Box Dance). Current development models fail to fully transform these cultural assets into industrial economic advantages. In contrast, Foshan City within the PRD (15 national-level ICH items spanning Traditional Music [Shifan Music, Bayin Luogu], Traditional Dance [Dragon Dance, Guangdong Lion Dance], Traditional Drama [Cantonese Opera], Traditional Quyi [Dragon Boat Ballads], Traditional Fine Arts [Festival Lanterns, Lion Head Craftsmanship], Traditional Craftsmanship [Shiwan Ceramics, Xiangyunsha Silk Dyeing], Folk Customs [Mid-Autumn Qiushe Festival, Ancestral Temple Fair], and Traditional Sports [Wing Chun]) exemplifies efficient ICH preservation empowered by robust economic foundations. To foster synergistic interactions between ICH conservation and industrial development, three policy pathways are proposed: First, strengthen legal frameworks through systematic implementation of national regulations including the Law of the People’s Republic of China on ICH (2011), Traditional Craft Revitalization Plan, ICH Conservation Special Program (2015–2019), and provincial-level ordinances in Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou, Yunnan, and Fujian. Second, refine sector-specific policies by cataloguing regional ICH projects and inheritors, establishing ecological conservation zones, formulating phased action plans, and integrating ICH preservation with rural revitalization. Third, drive industrial innovation through “ICH+” convergence models (e.g., ICH + Tourism, ICH + Education, ICH + Creative Industries, ICH + Live Streaming), strategically incorporating ICH industries into regional economic development blueprints as green transition catalysts for new quality productive forces. Collaborative advancement of these frameworks will structurally enhance the basin’s ICH system via historical narrative reconstruction, landmark infrastructure development, and diversified cultural carrier creation.

Leveraging water and land transportation advantages to cultivate ICH cultural industry belts

Analysis reveals that the interaction between topography and transportation factors has the strongest synergistic effect on the spatial distribution of ICH in the Pearl River Basin. Historically, transportation corridors such as the Xijiang Ancient Post Road, the Dongjiang-Hanjiang Ancient Post Road, and the Chaohui Ancient Post Road were formed along the basin’s water systems, playing a pivotal role in facilitating cross-regional cultural exchanges among diverse ethnic groups. For example, the Xijiang Ancient Post Road connected Yunnan and Guizhou in the north, traversed Guangxi and Guangdong, and extended to Hong Kong and Macao in the south, serving as the birthplace of multiculturalism and Cantonese culture. The Dongjiang-Hanjiang Ancient Post Road runs through the core areas of Dongjiang Hakka culture, Meizhou Hakka culture, and Cantonese culture, while the Chaohui Ancient Post Road is a convergence point of Cantonese, Hakka, and Chaoshan cultures. In recent years, modern transportation infrastructure such as the Chaohui Expressway, Hanjiang Expressway, Xijiang Highway, and Dongjiang Highway has been constructed along these ancient post roads, providing a solid foundation for cultivating cross-regional ICH cultural industry belts. Generally, an industry belt relies on geographic location and natural resources, supported by linear infrastructure such as transportation arteries, and centers around core cities as growth poles to form an industrial system. Given the abundant ICH resources in the Pearl River Basin, it is recommended to develop a comprehensive basin-wide plan. By leveraging the rich ICH resources along historical post roads such as the Xijiang Ancient Post Road, the Dongjiang-Hanjiang Ancient Post Road, and the Chaohui Ancient Post Road, ICH industry nodes can be established through various forms such as ICH workshops and inheritor studios. These nodes, connected by transportation arteries, can form cultural corridors, with the Pearl River Delta urban agglomeration and provincial capitals serving as ICH industry hubs, ultimately creating a point-line-plane cultural industry belt. In practical implementation, the following measures are essential: first, weaken administrative boundaries and focus on cultural commonalities to promote regional collaborative development and construct cultural corridors; second, emphasize internal diversity in ICH industry belts, such as using the Dongjiang-Hanjiang Ancient Post Road to build a cultural corridor centered on ethnic, Cantonese, and Hakka cultures, creating differentiated scale effects and avoiding homogenized malignant competition among ICH industry belts.

Conclusions

The Pearl River Basin exhibits a distinct “source-sink” phenomenon in the spatial distribution of ICH, characterized by an incremental accumulation from upstream to downstream. The total quantities of ICH show a clustered (aggregated) pattern, increasing from Yunnan (6.9%) and Guizhou (18.0%) to Guangxi (21.1%) and culminating in Guangdong (52.2%). Among the ten ICH categories, Guangdong dominates top rankings in nine domains (excluding Folk Literature), including Traditional Fine Arts, Traditional Music, Traditional Dance, Traditional Drama, Traditional Quyi (Chinese storytelling arts), Traditional Sports, Games, and Acrobatics, Traditional Craftsmanship, Traditional Medicine, and Folk Customs.

The spatial distribution of ICH in the Pearl River Basin shows a clear agglomeration pattern, forming two major agglomeration areas in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area and the Chaoshan region, and four minor agglomeration areas in Guiyang of Guizhou Province, Nanning of Guangxi, Kunming of Yunnan, and the eastern boundary of Guizhou and Guangxi. This finding aligns with Shen et al. (2024) comprehensive analysis of the spatial distribution of China’s national cultural heritage, which confirms that the southeastern region has become a cultural resource aggregation zone due to its favorable climate and profound historical-cultural endowments39. However, the present study further reveals the hierarchical gradation characteristics of the ‘source-sink’ phenomenon within the basin. This dynamic, where the natural environment provides a foundational template for early settlement while cultural and economic factors later drive expansion and agglomeration, resonates with recent findings on the evolution of waterside settlements in other major Chinese basins, such as the Nanxi River30. Our identification of the Pearl River Delta as a primary ‘sink’ or accumulation zone, driven by socioeconomic factors, is consistent with research in other regions that highlights the powerful role of economic vitality and transportation corridors in shaping cultural landscapes.

Physical Geography and Socioeconomic factors exert differential influences on the spatial distribution of ICH in the Pearl River Basin. In single-factor analysis, Topography was identified as the dominant factor (=0.27), followed sequentially by Hydrography (0.11), Transportation (0.08, Population (0.02), and Economic Development (0.01), indicating that ICH distribution is predominantly constrained by Physical Geography. Among dual-factor interactions, the Topography-Transportation interaction exhibited the strongest Dual-Factor Enhancement (DFE) (=0.393), demonstrating that optimal Topography combined with Transportation accessibility plays a primary synergistic role in amplifying ICH spatial agglomeration. Wu et al. (2024) demonstrated that activating cultural heritage IPs in the Yunnan-Guizhou-Guangxi Karst Region through multimedia dissemination and digital exhibition technologies is an effective strategy to overcome bottlenecks in cultural industry development in economically lagging areas40. Therefore, it is recommended to strengthen cross-platform collaborative development of ICH resources within the basin and establish the cultural brand “Digital Pearl River.” Additionally, Zhang et al. (2024) revealed that the spatial reconstruction of historical transportation corridors along the Grand Canal significantly enhances the regional connectivity of cultural resources41, indicating the necessity to prioritize the planning of cultural-tourism integrated projects for corridors such as the Xi River Ancient Post Road.

The conservation and inheritance of ICH in the Pearl River Basin necessitate dual strategies: preserving the pristine ICH ecology in the scenic mountainous regions of Yunnan, Guizhou, and Guangxi, while reinforcing top-level planning through ICH distribution drivers. Anchored by industrial hubs within the Pearl River Delta urban agglomeration and provincial capitals, strategic priorities include cultivating ICH Intellectual Property (IP), expanding the ICH industry chain, and establishing cultural industrial belts. This integrated approach achieves synergistic equilibrium—development through preservation while ensuring preservation through developmental practices.
