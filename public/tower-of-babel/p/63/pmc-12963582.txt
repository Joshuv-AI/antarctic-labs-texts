Nintedanib enhances the antitumor efficacy of pd-1 blockade, potentially through inhibition of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts
Authors: Ryo Suzuki, Satoshi Watanabe, Kunihiro Shono, Takaaki Masuda, Kensuke Yanai, Ryo Yamazaki, Yumi Ando, Tomoya Wakabayashi, Susumu Tanaka, Tomoki Sekiya, Kohei Kushiro, Naohiro Yanagimura, Miyuki Sato, Tomohiro Tanaka, Koichiro Nozaki, Yu Saida, Satoshi Hokari, Masashi Arita, Riuko Ohashi, Kenjiro Shima, Yosuke Kimura, Nobumasa Aoki, Yasuyoshi Ohshima, Toshiyuki Koya, Toshiaki Kikuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) inhibitors have shown promising and durable responses in patients with several types of cancer, many patients show resistance to PD-1 inhibitors. Recent evidence has demonstrated that immunosuppressive cells are induced in tumor microenvironment and inhibit the anti-tumor effects of anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (αPD-1 mAb). To investigate whether nintedanib-a multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting vascular endothelial growth factor receptor, fibroblast growth factor receptor, and platelet-derived growth factor receptor-suppresses immunosuppressive cells and enhances the anti-tumor effects of αPD-1 mAb in preclinical models, flowcytometry, immunohistochemistry, and RNA sequencing of tumor-tissue, tumor-draining lymph nodes, spleens were conducted. RNA sequencing of murine tumor tissues revealed that nintedanib decreased the gene signatures related to myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). Flow cytometry showed that nintedanib significantly decreased the MDSC and CAF percentage in tumor-bearing hosts and increased IFN-ϒ+CD4+ and CD8+ T cells infiltrating into tumors. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated that nintedanib treatment significantly increased the number of CD8+ T cells in the internal area of the tumor. Adding nintedanib to anti-PD-1 mAb therapy significantly inhibited in vivo tumor progression. These results indicate that nintedanib suppresses MDSCs and CAFs by inhibiting VEGFR-, PDGFR-, and FGFR-mediated signaling, thereby increasing effector T-cell infiltration into tumors and enhancing the anti-tumor effects of αPD-1 mAb therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04350-x.Keywords: Nintedanib, Cancer-associated fibroblast , Myeloid derived suppressor cells, PD-1

Introduction

Programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) play important roles in anti-tumor immunity. PD-L1 on cancer and antigen-presenting cells binds to PD-1 on activated effector T cells, inhibiting the cytotoxicity of T cells [1]. PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors reactivate exhausted tumor-specific T cells, enabling them to attack tumor cells [2]. In previous phase III trials, anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (αPD-1 mAb) therapy improved the survival of patients with various types of cancer than conventional chemotherapy [3–7]. Although αPD-1 mAb therapy produces durable responses in some patients with cancer, all patients with malignancies have not benefited from this therapy because of other mechanisms of immune escape, for example, immunosuppression by myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [8, 9]. For αPD-1 mAbs to be effective, tumor-specific effector T cells must be induced, infiltrated into tumor tissues, and suppressed through the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. Several studies have shown that MDSCs and CAFs inhibit effector T-cell induction and infiltration [10–12]. MDSCs suppress the priming of effector T cells in tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) and decrease their percentage [10]. CAFs interfere with T-cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment, forming immune-excluded/desert tumors [13]. These immunosuppressive cells are induced by cytokines secreted from various cells in the TME [14–16]. MDSCs are promoted by cytokines including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), IL-1β, IL-4, IL-10, IL-13, IL-17, and IL-28 [17, 18]. CAFs are also induced and activated by cytokines, including VEGF, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), IL-1, and IL-6 [19]. The blockade of these cytokines and cytokine receptors may suppress MDSC and CAF induction and function, and augment the anti-tumor effects of αPD-1 mAbs.

Nintedanib is a multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor for VEGFR1/2/3, FGFR1/2/3, and PDGFRɑ/ϐ, and has been approved in many countries for the treatment of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [20]. It has also been investigated for treating patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and nintedanib plus docetaxel combination therapy significantly improved progression-free survival compared with placebo plus docetaxel [21]. Nintedanib has been approved in Europe for treating lung adenocarcinoma after first-line therapy. It has been reported that MDSCs express VEGFR, and CAFs express FGFRF and PDGFR, and acquire immunosuppressive properties through downstream signaling via these receptors [19, 22]. Although many antiangiogenic agents have been approved, nintedanib is distinct from other VEGF inhibitors in that it concomitantly inhibits FGFR and PDGFR signaling. VEGF, PDGF, and FGF are key cytokines involved in the induction and activation of MDSCs and CAFs, both of which contribute to immunosuppression within the TME. We therefore hypothesized that blockade of VEGF-, PDGF-, and FGF-mediated signaling pathways by nintedanib would suppress MDSCs and CAFs and thereby enhance antitumor immune responses. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the effects of nintedanib on anti-tumor immunity, MDSCs, and CAFs in murine tumor models. We further examined whether nintedanib augments the antitumor efficacy of αPD-1mAb therapy. Through the combination of PD-1 blockade with simultaneous inhibition of VEGFR, FGFR, and PDGFR by nintedanib, this study aimed to relieve multiple layers of immunosuppression in tumor-bearing hosts, including those mediated by MDSCs and CAFs.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Niigata University (SA01485). Female C57BL/6 J (B6) and female BALB/cA (BALB/c) mice were purchased from CLEA Laboratory (Tokyo, Japan). C57BL/6-Tg (CAG-EGFP) C14-Y01-FM131Osb (GFP) mice were obtained from the Brain Research Institute of Niigata University and we signed a material transfer agreement with Osaka University. All the mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment and used between 8 and 12 weeks of age. Mice were euthanized humanely once tumors reached the size specified by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Niigata University.

Tumors

CT26 cells derived from colon adenocarcinoma in BALB/c mice and MC38 cells (catalog no. ENH204-FP), which was obtained from Kerafast derived from colon adenocarcinoma in B6 mice were cultured in complete medium (CM) consisting of RPMI 1640 medium (catalog no. 11875093, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fatal bovine serum (catalog no. A5670701, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids (catalog no. 11140050, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (catalog no. 11360070, Thermo Fisher Scientific), antibiotic mixture (100 units/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin, catalog no. 15140122, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 28.6 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (catalog no. 21985023, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cells were suspended in 50 μL Hanks balanced salt solution (HBSS, catalog no. 14170112, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for subcutaneous inoculation.

Single-cell suspension preparation

The harvested spleens were crushed in CM using the flat end of plunger, filtered through 200 μm filter, and centrifuged at 300 × g for 8 min. For red blood cell lysis, centrifuged cells were treated with 1–2 mL ACK buffer consisting of 155 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, and 0.5 mM EDTA-2Na and incubated for 1 min on ice. To deactivate the ACK buffer, the cell suspensions were added with 30–50 mL HBSS and centrifuged at 300 × g for 8 min. The harvested tumors were minced using scissors in CM and digested with a mixture of 0.1% collagenase (catalog number. C5138, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.01% DNase (catalog no. DN25, Sigma-Aldrich), and 2.5 U/mL hyaluronidase (catalog no. H6254, Sigma-Aldrich) for 2–3 h at around 25 °C. Following tumor digestion, the single-cell suspensions were filtered through 200 μm filter and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. To obtain TDLNs, BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1.5 × 106 CT26 cells in the bilateral flanks. The TDLNs were harvested on day 12, torn using a needle, crushed using the flat end of plunger in CM, filtered through a 200 μm filter, and centrifuged at 300 × g for 8 min. The TDLN cells were then activated with an immobilized anti-CD3 mAb (145-2C11) in 24-well plates for two days and expanded in CM containing 16 U/mL murine IL-2 (catalog no. 402-ML, R&D Systems) for three days.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1.5 × 106 CT26 cells along the midline of the abdomen, intraperitoneally treated with 10 mg/kg αPD-1 mAbs (RMP1-14, catalog no. BE0146, Bio X cell) on day 5, and orally treated with 50 mg/kg nintedanib (catalog no. CS-0104, ChemScene) from days 5 to 11; the tumor tissues were harvested on day 12 for RNA extraction, library construction, and data processing, which were performed at the Beijing Genomics Institute (Beijing, China). RNA data were sequenced using DNBSEQ-G400RS based on the mouse reference genome (GRCm38). Mouse gene readouts were converted to human gene readouts using the Mouse Genome Informatics database (http://www.informatics.jax.org/). Genes with less than 1 transcript per million (TPM) were excluded from the analysis. In terms of volcano plot, a significant fold change was defined as > 2 or < 0.5. GSEA was performed as described by Subramanian et al. [23].

Flowcytometry

Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated mAbs against CD11b (M1/70, catalog no. 561688) and CD45.1 (A20, catalog no. 553776); phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated mAbs against Gr-1 (RB6-8C5, catalog no. 553128) and IFN-γ (XMG1.2, catalog no. 554412); PE-Cy7-conjugated mAbs against CD4 (RM4-5, catalog no. 552775), CD8 (53–6.7, catalog no. 552877), CD45 (30-F11, catalog no. 552848), CD90.2 (Thy-1.2, catalog no. 561642), and Ly-6C (AL-21, catalog no. 560593); allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated mAbs against CD45.2 (104, catalog no. 558702), Ly-6G (1A8, catalog no. 560599), and isotype-matched mAbs were purchased from BD Biosciences. The PE–anti-forkhead box P3 (Foxp3) antibody (FJK-16 s, catalog no. 14–5773) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. APC–anti-podoplanin (PDPN) antibody (8.1.1, catalog no. 127407) was purchased from BioLegend. After treatment with an FcR blocking Ab (2.4G2, catalog no. 553142, BD bioscience), single-cell suspensions were labeled according to the manufacturer’s protocol and analyzed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer and FlowJo software (BD Biosciences). Intracellular IFN-γ was stained using Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (catalog no. 554714, BD Biosciences). Briefly, T cells were stimulated with or without CT26 tumor cells prepared from solid tumor tissues at a 1:1 ratio. In some experiments, MDSCs were further co-cultured with T cells at a 1:1 ratio. Brefeldin A at a concentration of 10 μg/ml (catalog no. B6542, Sigma-Aldrich) was added at 6 h, and the cells were harvested at 24 h. The cells were then pretreated with FcR-blocking Ab before being stained for 30 min with Cy-conjugated anti-CD4 or anti-CD8 mAb. Washed cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with Fixation/Permeabilization Kit, and incubated for 40 min with PE-conjugated IFN-γ. Intracellular Foxp3 was stained using Anti-Mouse/Rat Foxp3 Staining Set PE (catalog no. 72–5775-40, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunogenic cell death (ICD) assessment

A total of 7.5 × 105 CT26 tumor cells were cultured in CM on a 60 mm dish for 12–24 h. When cells reached 50% confluency, 1 μM nintedanib or 25 μM doxorubicin (catalog no. 44513, Sigma-Aldrich) were added into medium. Six hours later, the tumor cells were further cultured without drugs for 24 h and the tumor cells and supernatants were harvested. The high-mobility group box-1 (HMGB-1) protein concentrations in the supernatants were assessed using the HMGB-1 ELISA Kit Exp (catalog no. 326078738, Shino-Test Corporation), following the manufacturer’s protocols. Calreticulin (CALR) expression on the cell surface was assessed using flow cytometry. After exposure to nintedanib or doxorubicin, the tumor cells were stained with anti-CALR antibodies (catalog no. ab2907, Abcam), anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (catalog no. A-11008, Invitrogen), and 7-AAD (catalog no. 559925, BD Biosciences). The percentage of CALR+7-AAD− cells were calculated.

MDSC assessment

The CT26 tumor-bearing mice were treated with nintedanib from days 5 to 39 and αPD-1 mAbs on days 5, 12, 19, 26, and 33. The spleens and tumors were harvested and subjected to flow cytometry on day 40. MDSCs were classified into two types according to the surface marker expression pattern: polymorphonuclear-MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs, CD11b+Gr-1+Ly-6G+ cells) and monocytic-MDSCs (M-MDSCs, CD11b+Gr-1+Ly-6C+ cells) and evaluated the percentage of total MDSCs, PMN-MDSCs, and M-MDSCs in live cells. To assess the suppressive effect of nintedanib on MDSCs, BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated with CT26 tumor cells and treated with nintedanib from days 26 to 38. The spleens were harvested on day 40. After preparing single-cell suspensions from the spleens, MDSCs were isolated using a Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cell Isolation Kit (catalog no. 130–094–538, Miltenyi Biotec). Then, 1.0 × 106 activated TDLN cells were co-cultured with 1.0 × 106 CT26 cells in the presence or absence of 1.0 × 106 MDSCs for 24 h. IFN-γ production from these TDLN cells were assessed.

CAF assessment

GFP mice were inoculated with 1.5 × 106 MC38 tumor cells and treated with nintedanib from days 5 to 20 and αPD-1 mAbs on days 7 and 14. The tumors were harvested and subjected to flow cytometry on day 21. As previously described, the CAFs were defined as αSMA+PDPN+GFP+CD45− or αSMA+CD90+GFP+CD45− cells [24].

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte assessment

BALB/c or B6 mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1.5 × 106 CT26 or MC38 tumor cells and treated with nintedanib from days 5 to 14. Tumors were harvested and subjected to flow cytometry on day 19. The percentage of IFN-γ+ cells in CD4+ or CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) was evaluated using flowcytometry.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The CT26 tu mor-bearing BALB/c mice were treated with nintedanib from days 0 to 18 and αPD-1 mAbs on days 10 and 15, and tumors were harvested on day 19. The harvested tumor tissues were soaked in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 12 − 24 h and preserved in 70% ethanol. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue (FFPE) sections of 4-μm thickness were deparaffinized and incubated in an antigen retrieval solution at pH 9.0 (catalog no. 415201, Nichirei Biosciences, Inc.) for 15 min at 121 °C. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited using 3% H2O2–methanol for 15 min and the sections were blocked with 10% normal goat serum. As the first step of staining, the sections were stained by overnight incubating with anti-CD4 (catalog no. 413951, clone 4B12, Nichirei Corporation) and anti-CD8 (catalog no. 413201, clone C8/144B, Nichirei Corporation) antibodies at 4 °C. In the second step, a Histofine Simple Stain MAX-PO (multi) kit (catalog no. 414191, Nichirei Corporation) was used for 30 min. The sections were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) at each step. For visualization, the antigen–antibody complex was reacted with 3,3-diaminobenzidine using a Histofine DAB substrate kit (catalog no. 725191, Nichirei Corporation), and nuclear sections were stained with hematoxylin. To assess lymphocytic infiltration, we defined the marginal area as the outer part of the tumor tissue, including the capsule and muscle, and the internal area as a more central position than the marginal area. The number of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells was counted at 0.04 mm2 magnification in six different regions of the tumor, and the average was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Tumor measurement

BALB/c mice bearing CT26 tumors or B6 mice bearing MC38 tumors were treated with nintedanib (50 mg/kg, orally) from days 0 to 18, and with αPD-1 mAbs (10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) on days 10 and 15. Tumor size was measured in two perpendicular dimensions three times per week using digital calipers, and the tumor area (mm2) was recorded.

Statistical analysis

The significance of differences between the two groups was analyzed using Student’s t-test. The significance of differences between three or more groups was analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Tukey–Kramer method. All statistical analyses were performed using the JMP 16 software (SAS Institute Inc.). A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered significant. Unless otherwise specified, all experiments were conducted using 3 to 5 mice per group and were repeated at least twice.

Results

Adding nintedanib to αPD-1 mAbs reduces gene signatures related to MDSCs and CAFs

First, we used RNA-seq to comprehensively evaluate whether the addition of nintedanib to αPD-1 mAb therapy inhibits gene expression related to immunosuppression, including suppressive cytokines in the TME. CT26 tumors were harvested from BALB/c mice treated with αPD-1 mAbs with or without nintedanib, and bulk RNA-seq was performed on these tumors. RNA-seq data quality was assessed using FastQC. The average sequencing depth was approximately 41 million clean reads per sample. The total mapping ratios ranged from 92.34 to 94.85%, and the uniquely mapped read ratios ranged from 80.05 to 84.24%, indicating high-quality RNA-seq data. Gene expression values were normalized prior to downstream analyses. In addition, Pearson correlation coefficients among biological replicates were high (r = 0.963), demonstrating robust reproducibility of the RNA-seq data. Figure 1A demonstrates the volcano plot of gene expression comparing nintedanib plus αPD-1 mAb combination therapy with αPD-1 mAb monotherapy. The addition of nintedanib to αPD-1 mAb therapy significantly decreased (< 0.5-fold change) the expression of 295 genes including Fap, Acta2, Postn, Flt1, Flt4, and Cxcl9, whereas 7 genes were upregulated (> twofold change).

Hallmark gene set analysis indicated that compared with tumors from mice treated with αPD-1 mAb monotherapy, those from mice receiving nintedanib and αPD-1 mAbs had decreased epithelial–mesenchymal transition, angiogenesis, IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, inflammatory response, as well as coagulation (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). An analysis of gene set related to signaling pathways revealed that VEGF, PDGF, and FGF pathways were significantly suppressed by adding nintedanib to αPD-1 mAbs (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S1B). In addition, for the MDSC gene set, we used 85 genes based on a previous report by Angelva et al., and for the CAF gene set, we used 165 genes derived from the report by Kim et al., which were applied to the GSEA [25, 26]. GSEA showed that gene signatures related to MDSCs and CAFs in tumors from mice treated with nintedanib significantly suppressed (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. S1B, C and Supplementary Table 1).

Nintedanib suppresses MDSC induction and function

Previous studies have demonstrated that immunosuppressive cytokines, including VEGF, induce MDSCs and suppress anti-tumor immunity [17, 18]. To investigate whether nintedanib inhibits MDSC induction and function, we assessed MDSCs in the spleens and tumors derived from BALB/c mice inoculated with CT26 cells and treated with nintedanib, αPD-1 mAbs or both. Figure 2A shows the gating strategies used to identify PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs. Nintedanib significantly reduced both the percentage and the absolute number of MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs in the spleens and tumor tissues, with or without αPD-1 mAb therapy (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Figs. S2, S3).

We have previously shown that TDLNs play a pivotal role in initiating tumor-specific effector T cells [10]. TDLN cells secrete IFN-γ upon specific-tumor stimulation after anti-CD3/IL-2 activation in vitro. To evaluate whether nintedanib suppresses MDSC function, we isolated MDSCs from the spleens of CT26-bearing BALB/c mice treated or untreated with nintedanib. The viability of MSDCs were approximately 90% after isolation in both groups. These MDSCs were co-cultured with activated TDLN and CT26 tumor cells in vitro. Intracellular cytokine staining of IFN-γ revealed that MDSCs from nintedanib untreated mice significantly reduced the percentage of IFN-γ+ cells in CD4+ cells or CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2C). In contrast, MDSCs from nintedanib treated mice did not inhibit IFN-γ production from both CD4+ to CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2C). Representative gating strategy used to CD4+IFN-γ+ cells and CD8+IFN-γ+ cells was shown in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Nintedanib reduced CAF percentage

Since FGF and PDGF promote CAFs, we investigated whether nintedanib also suppressed CAF induction by blocking the FGF and PDGF signaling pathways [27, 28]. We harvested GFP-negative MC38 tumors from GFP mice treated with nintedanib monotherapy, αPD-1 mAb monotherapy, or both. CAFs were defined by two surface protein expression pattens: αSMA+PDPN+ cells and αSMA+CD90+ cells (Fig. 3A). Nintedanib significantly reduced the percentage and the number of CAFs among CD45−GFP+ cells (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S5). We also assessed whether nintedanib inhibits the induction of regulatory T cells (Tregs). BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated with CT26 tumor cells and treated with nintedanib monotherapy, αPD-1 mAb monotherapy, or both. The spleens and tumors were harvested, and the percentage of CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs among CD4+ cells was analyzed using flow cytometry. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S6A, B, the addition of nintedanib did not decrease the percentage of CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs in CD4+ cells in the spleen and tumors.

Nintedanib increases the density of TILs

αPD-1 mAbs exhibit anti-tumor effects by inhibiting PD-1 and PD-L1 binding at tumor sites and reactivating TILs [29]. The density of CD8+ T cells at the tumor margin and inside tumors predicts αPD-1 mAb efficacy [30]. In addition, IFN-γ-related gene signatures in the TME correlate with response to αPD-1 mAb therapy [31]. Since MDSCs inhibited IFN-γ secretion from CD4+ to CD8+ cells, and nintedanib suppressed MDSC induction and function (Fig. 2A, B), we assessed whether nintedanib increases IFN-γ production from TILs. CT26 and MC38 tumors from mice treated with or without nintedanib were freshly harvested, and their single-cell suspensions were stained with IFN-γ intracellularly. As shown in Fig. 4A, nintedanib significantly increased the percentage of IFN-γ+ cells in CD4+ and CD8+ TILs. Furthermore, the proportion of tumor-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells on day 40 were evaluated by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. S7). The addition of nintedanib significantly increased the proportions of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.

CAFs inhibit lymphocyte infiltration into the tumor and suppress anti-tumor immunity [32]. To evaluate whether nintedanib increased TIL, we harvested CT26 tumors from BALB/c mice treated with nintedanib and analyzed the density of CD4+ and CD8+ TILs in two tumor compartments: the internal and marginal areas. Figure 4B shows the representative IHC images. As shown in Fig. 4C, nintedanib significantly increased the density of CD4+ TILs in marginal areas and CD8+ TILs in marginal and internal areas.

Nintedanib does not induce ICD

Certain cytotoxic anti-cancer agents induce ICD and augment anti-tumor immune responses [33]. Recently, Liu et al. showed that crizotinib, a molecular therapeutic agent for echinoderm microtubule-associated protein-like 4-anaplastic lymphoma kinase and c-ros oncogene 1 positive lung cancer, suppressed tumor growth by not only blocking the signaling pathway, but also ICD [34]. The findings that nintedanib increased IFN-γ+ cells in CD4+ and CD8+ TILs prompted us to assess whether nintedanib induces ICD and enhances anti-tumor immunity. CT26 tumor cells were exposed to nintedanib, doxorubicin (positive control) [35] or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, negative control), and the tumor cells and supernatants were harvested for flow cytometry and ELISA. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S8A, HMGB-1 concentrations were not significantly different between tumor cell cultures exposed to nintedanib and those exposed to DMSO. Similarly, the percentage of CALR-positive CT26 tumor cells did not increase after exposure to nintedanib compared to DMSO (Supplementary Fig. S8B).

Nintedanib augments anti-tumor effects of αPD-1 mAb

We next assessed whether nintedanib enhances anti-tumor effects of αPD-1 mAb therapy. CT26 and MC38 tumor cells are sensitive to anti-PD-1 antibodies [36, 37]. In this study, to investigate the additive effect of nintedanib on anti-PD-1 therapy, the initiation of anti-PD-1 treatment was delayed after tumor inoculation. BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated with CT26 tumor cells along the midline of the abdomen and treated with nintedanib and αPD-1 mAbs. Figure 5A shows the curves of CT26 skin tumor growth. The addition of nintedanib to αPD-1 mAbs significantly suppressed skin tumor progression compared with nintedanib alone or αPD-1 mAb therapy alone. To investigate this enhancement of anti-tumor effect in another tumor, B6 mice were subcutaneously inoculated with MC38 cells and treated with nintedanib and αPD-1 mAbs. As shown in Fig. 5B, the addition of nintedanib significantly inhibited skin tumor growth of MC38 compared with αPD-1 mAb therapy alone.

Discussion

Although PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors play an important role in treating various cancer types, some patients exhibit primary or acquired resistance against them. For PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors to have anti-tumor effects, effector T cells must be induced, infiltrate tumors, and suppressed by PD-1/PD-L1 interactions. However, tumor cells can develop through various mechanisms to escape immune cell attacks and acquire advantageous traits for survival and progression through various genetic mutations. The activation of oncogenic pathways leads to the acquisition of traits such as enhanced proliferative signals and immortality. Additionally, the activation of oncogenic pathways suppresses anti-tumor immune responses, enabling tumor cells to evade immune system attacks [38]. Tumor cells secrete various cytokines and induce immune suppressor cells, including MDSCs, CAFs, and Tregs, through the activation of oncogenic pathways [14–16, 39].

In this study, we demonstrated that nintedanib decreased MDSCs and CAFs and enhanced the anti-tumor effects of αPD-1 mAb therapy. RNA-seq and GSEA revealed that adding nintedanib to αPD-1 mAb therapy significantly decreased gene signatures related to MDSCs and CAFs (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1). The RNA sequencing performed in this study was bulk sequencing of tumor tissues; therefore, it is possible that nintedanib inhibited cytokines critical for the induction of MDSCs and CAFs that are produced by vascular endothelial cells and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that nintedanib inhibited MDSC induction, attenuated their suppressive function, and increased antigen-specific IFN-ϒ secretion from T cells (Fig. 2). VEGF, including tumor-derived VEGF, plays an important role in the induction of MDSCs. It is therefore plausible that nintedanib suppresses MDSC induction and function through inhibition of VEGFR signaling in MDSCs. Although the proportion of intratumoral MDSCs did not differ between the PD-1 blockade-alone group and the PD-1 blockade plus nintedanib group, nintedanib may have attenuated the suppressive function of MDSCs, thereby potentially contributing to increased T cell infiltration within the tumor. Also, nintedanib decreased the density of αSMA+PDPN+ and αSMA+CD90+ CAFs (Fig. 3), and increased the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells into tumors (Fig. 4B, C). IFN-ϒ is a crucial cytokine for anti-tumor immunity. Previous studies have indicated that IFN-ϒ enhances MHC-class I expression on tumor cells, rendering them more immunogenic, and increases PD-L1 expression in the TME [40]. Nintedanib increased the percentage of IFN-ϒ+CD4+ and CD8+ T cells infiltrating into tumors (Fig. 4A). Finally, the addition of nintedanib to αPD-1 mAb therapy significantly inhibited both CT26 and MC38 tumor progression compared to αPD-1 mAb monotherapy (Fig. 5). The nintedanib-mediated enhancement of effector T cell number and function was insufficient to induce tumor regression. In addition, immunosuppressive mechanisms other than MDSCs and CAFs are likely operative within the tumor microenvironment, and inhibition of these additional pathways may be required to achieve a sufficient antitumor effect. Indeed, the antitumor efficacy was enhanced when nintedanib was combined with PD-1 blockade. Our data indicate that nintedanib inhibits MDSCs and CAFs, thus increasing the induction of effector T-cell-infiltrating tumors.

MDSCs are potent suppressor cells that express cytokine-induced immature cell surface markers [41]. A high frequency of MDSCs has been associated with poor outcome of αPD-1 mAb therapy and chemotherapy [42, 43]. In addition, the inhibition of MDSCs enhances the therapeutic effects of immunotherapy [44]. We demonstrated that nintedanib significantly reduced the percentage of total MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs in the spleen (Fig. 2B). In tumor-bearing hosts, PMN-MDSCs comprise the majority of MDSCs [41]. A previous study has shown that Fas-L expressed on PMN-MDSCs decreased TILs and attenuated the efficacy of immunotherapy including αPD-1 mAb therapy [45]. In addition, inhibition of PMN-MDSCs increases the anti-tumor effects of αPD-1 mAb therapy [46]. Consistent with these findings, we showed that nintedanib increased the density of CD4+ and CD8+ TILs, and the combination of nintedanib and αPD-1 mAbs significantly delayed tumor progression (Fig. 4B, C and Fig. 5).

The TME can be divided into three immune statuses, immune-desert, immune-excluded, and inflamed, depending on the presence of immune cells, particularly T-cell infiltration [47]. Excluded immune deserts are considered cold tumors and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have limited efficacy. Immune exclusion is a condition in which tumor stromal cells, including CAFs, prevent effector T cells from infiltrating tumors [48]. Treatments targeting CAFs may improve the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors by infiltrating T cells into the tumor. PDGF and FGF transform normal fibroblasts into CAFs; accordingly, nintedanib may decrease the number of CAFs by blocking the PDGF and FGF signaling pathways [19]. Although various surface markers of CAFs have been identified, we focused on podoplanin, CD90, and αSMA, which are expressed in activated CAFs, to investigate the influence of nintedanib [24]. Nintedanib significantly decreased the number of SMA+PDPN+ and SMA+CD90+ CAFs in the TME (Fig. 3). In addition, nintedanib enhanced the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into internal area of the tumor and IFN-ϒ secretion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in TME (Fig. 4). These results indicate that inhibiting VEGF, PDGF, and FGF signaling pathways may suppress MDSCs and CAFs and augment the effects of αPD-1 mAb therapy by promoting the induction of effector T cells and their infiltration into the tumor. Previous studies have demonstrated the inhibitory effects of nintedanib on CAFs [49–51]. In particular, Kato et al. reported, using the B16F10 tumor model, that nintedanib reduces FAP+ CAFs and enhances the antitumor immune activity of CD8+ T cells [49]. Yamanaka et al. further demonstrated that nintedanib inhibits the secretion of multiple cytokines from CAFs and suppresses tumor progression in a murine cholangiocarcinoma model [50]. In addition to its inhibiting FGFR and PDGFR, nintedanib also targets VEGFR, suggesting broader effects on the tumor microenvironment. Given these findings, we focused on its potential effects on MDSCs and extended this line of investigation. We found that nintedanib suppresses MDSC induction as well as their T cell-suppressive function.

Our study had several limitations. First, we employed murine colorectal cancer cell lines in this study because CAFs were more readily induced in the CT26 and MC38 tumor models. Although we have performed similar experiments using the murine lung cancer cell line LLC, this model exhibited limited sensitivity to PD-1 blockade, making it difficult to demonstrate a clear enhancement of therapeutic efficacy with nintedanib. In addition, tumor cell-line-based transplantation models may not fully recapitulate the immune landscape of human primary tumors. Furthermore, we did not confirm whether nintedanib inhibited the suppression of effector T cells by CAFs in vitro. The percentage of GFP+CD45−αSMA+PDPN+CAF or GFP+CD45−αSMA+CD90+CAF in the tumor tissue was low and the number of CAFs needed for in vitro experiments could not be isolated. In this study, the nintedanib-associated suppression of MDSCs and CAFs and the concomitant increase in T cells in the tumor microenvironment were not shown to be causally linked, and the contribution of off-target effects cannot be ruled out. Moreover, bulk RNA-seq was performed to evaluate gene expression; therefore, we did not evaluate gene expression in MDSCs or CAFs using single-cell RNA sequencing. In addition, in clinical practice, whether nintedanib is effective in tumor-bearing hosts in which MDSCs and CAFs have already been induced, and tumor immunity is severely suppressed should be determined.

In conclusion, we showed that nintedanib inhibits MDSCs and CAFs and augments the effects of αPD-1 mAb therapy. A previous phase I trial in patients with advanced solid tumors demonstrated the safety and moderate efficacy of nintedanib in combination with pembrolizumab [52]. Although the present study was conducted exclusively in murine models, our findings provide a rationale for future investigations using blood and tumor tissue samples from patients with lung cancer complicated by idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis to evaluate the effects of nintedanib on MDSCs and CAFs. In addition, these results support further future clinical evaluation of nintedanib in combination with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, particularly in cancer patients whose primary resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade is associated with MDSCs and CAFs.
