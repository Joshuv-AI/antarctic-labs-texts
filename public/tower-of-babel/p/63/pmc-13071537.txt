A Nurse‐Led Educational Intervention for Patients With Type 1 Diabetes Using Continuous Glucose Monitoring
Authors: Marcos Pazos‐Couselo, Verónica Izquierdo, Mercedes Muñoz Martinez, Francisco Javier Gómez Romero, José‐Ramón Muñoz‐Rodríguez, Antía Fernández‐Pombo, Jesus Moreno‐Fernandez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe primary barrier to maximizing the benefits of intermittently scanned continuous glucose monitoring (isCGM) is low scan frequency. Higher daily scan frequency correlates with better glycemic control.AimTo evaluate the effect of a nurse‐led educational intervention on scan frequency and behavioral change in adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) showing low scanning frequency.MethodsIn this 12‐week quasi‐experimental study, adults with T1D using isCGM and low scan frequency participated in a single, individualized, direct education session led by a diabetes nurse educator. The intervention focused on increasing daily scan frequency and supporting patient engagement in self‐management.ResultsThirty‐four patients using isCGM participated. Mean daily scan frequency increased from 3.1 to 6.1 scans/day following the intervention. This rise was associated with an 8.0% improvement in TIR. A positive correlation was observed between scan frequency and TIR, with each additional daily scan associated with a 0.51% increase in TIR.Linking Evidence to ActionA single, targeted educational session can significantly improve isCGM adherence and glycemic control in adults with T1D and low adherence, supporting its value as a practical strategy in routine clinical care.Trial RegistrationThe protocol was publicly registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05570162)

Introduction

Attaining tight blood glucose control prevents the onset of chronic complications in people with type 1 diabetes (T1D) (Diabetes Control and Complications Trial Research Group 1993; Nathan et al. 2005). Self‐monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) has been the main tool for effective glycemic therapy adjustments (Chung et al. 2020; Elsayed et al. 2023; Miller et al. 2013). However, repeatedly performing daily glucose fingerpricks is painful and offers limited information about glucose levels (Gomez‐Peralta et al. 2020; Hermanns et al. 2019).

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems were developed as a practical alternative to the limitations of SMBG, providing information that enables patients to make more informed treatment decisions (Gomez‐Peralta et al. 2020). Flash technology (Abbott Diabetes Care, Witney, UK), called intermittently scanned continuous glucose monitoring (isCGM), displays interstitial glucose concentrations during a 14‐day period through a quick scan of an external reader (Chico et al. 2020). Findings from randomized controlled trials (RCT) and real‐world studies have shown that isCGM is a useful tool for improving quality of life and glycemic control in people with T1D (Bolinder et al. 2016; Castellana et al. 2020; Charleer et al. 2020; Deshmukh et al. 2020; Gordon et al. 2020; Lameijer et al. 2021; Nathanson et al. 2021; Pease et al. 2020; Roussel et al. 2021).

The main limiting factor in obtaining benefits from isCGM is scanning frequency. Higher daily scan frequency correlates with better glycemic control. A real‐world observational study found that higher scanning rates were linked to improved glycemic markers (Dunn et al. 2018). Another study reported similar trends (Gomez‐Peralta et al. 2020). In a recent prospective study, an inverse correlation between hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels and time in range (TIR) with daily scanning frequency, 1 year after initiating isCGM, was observed (Moreno‐Fernandez et al. 2023).

Educational programs centered on isCGM have been developed to empower persons to improve their diabetes knowledge and self‐management. In a RCT with patients on intensive insulin therapy and isCGM, education on the Flash system led to improvements in HbA1c (−0.17%) and TIR (3.8%). However, clear scan frequency was not specifically addressed, and no increase in scanning was observed during follow‐up (Hermanns et al. 2019). The impact of isCGM education has been reported in patients with type 2 diabetes (Yaron et al. 2019), while the benefits of diabetes programs for patients using real‐time CGM systems have been well established (Hee Yoo et al. 2022; Pemberton et al. 2021; Schlüter et al. 2021; Strategies to Enhance New CGM Use in Early Childhood (SENCE) SENSE Study Group et al. 2021).

This study aimed to evaluate the effect of a nursed‐led, single‐session educational intervention focused on isCGM use, targeting daily scanning frequency and behavioral changes in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics

A 12‐week prospective observational study with a quasi‐experimental pre‐post design was conducted to assess the impact of a structured educational session on Flash scanning frequency and glycemic control in adults with T1D. The study was approved by the Castilla‐La Mancha Public Health System (Servicio de Salud de Castilla‐La Mancha, SESCAM) Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05570162).

The study was conducted in the Diabetes Technology Unit at Ciudad Real General University Hospital, where prior findings on isCGM use in this public health population have been reported (Moreno‐Fernandez et al. 2023).

The primary outcome was to evaluate the effect of an isCGM‐focused educational intervention on daily scan frequency and behavioral changes. An increase of 7–8 scans per day has been associated with a 10% improvement in TIR, while optimal glycemic control (HbA1c < 53 mmol/mol or 7%) has been linked to ≥ 20 scans/day (Dunn et al. 2018; Gomez‐Peralta et al. 2020). Assuming optimal control (100%) at 20 scans/day, an increase from 4 (20%) to 12 (60%) scans/day corresponds to the expected TIR change. With an SD of 15% (per prior data Moreno‐Fernandez et al. 2023), a sample of 34 participants provided > 90% power to detect an 8‐scan/day difference at the 0.05 significance level. A 10% attrition rate was accounted for.

Study Participants

Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with a clinical diagnosis of T1D on multiple daily injections (MDI), using isCGM (FreeStyle Libre 2, Abbott Laboratories, IL, USA) for ≥ 6 months with low adherence (≤ 4 scans/day). Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, planning pregnancy, or being treated with other insulin therapies different from MDI. The level of HbA1c was not an inclusion criterion. All participants were active isCGM users and had previously completed a Flash educational program at the beginning of this therapy according to the regulations of the Castilla‐La Mancha Public Health System.

To identify patients with low adherence, scan frequency was verified using LibreView software (Abbott Laboratories, IL, USA). Eligible participants were recruited via consecutive non‐random sampling through phone calls until the required sample size was reached. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained after providing oral and written study information. All participants received regular follow‐up care at the Diabetes Technology Unit, Ciudad Real General University Hospital.

Study Procedure

The intervention consisted of a single, structured, individualized, one‐hour, face‐to‐face educational session delivered by an experienced nurse, who conducted all sessions to ensure consistency. The session focused on improving engagement with isCGM, with the primary goal of increasing scan frequency to > 11 times/day.

Based on a self‐management approach, the session combined theoretical knowledge with practical skills training, supported by tailored educational materials (Figure S1). Common barriers to isCGM use were addressed, and participants discussed their individual goals with the nurse.

Participants practiced interpreting interstitial glucose values and trend arrows through guided review and with problem‐solving exercises, to enhance informed decision‐making regarding their self‐management. Personalized strategies were discussed to help integrate scanning into various times and daily activities with the aim of increasing isCGM frequency to > 11 scans/day.

A description of the isCGM educational session is provided in Table S1. The follow‐up period lasted 12 weeks after the educational intervention.

Variables and Outcomes

Data were collected via standardized case report forms and patient interviews. Data from the isCGM systems were downloaded from each patient's LibreView account linked to the healthcare center at baseline and at the 12‐week follow‐up.

The primary outcome was the change in mean daily scan frequency before the educational intervention and at the 12‐week follow‐up. The secondary outcomes included changes in isCGM active time, scan timing, reasons for low isCGM use, glycometric index, insulin use, and percentage of patients meeting TIR targets per international guidelines (Battelino et al. 2019). Glycometric index encompassed: time above range (TAR) Level 2: > 250 mg/dL, TAR Level 1181–250 mg/dL, TIR 70–180 mg/dL, time below range (TBR) Level 1 54–69 mg/dL, TBR Level 2 < 54 mg/dL of interstitial glucose, mean interstitial glucose (MIG), glucose management indicator (GMI), and coefficient of variation (CV). All glycemic data were based on two‐week isCGM periods, per international consensus recommendations (Battelino et al. 2019).

Statistical Analysis

A cross‐sectional and longitudinal/prospective analysis was conducted on patients meeting inclusion criteria. Quantitative variables are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) with 95% confidence interval (CI), and qualitative variables as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Paired Student's t‐tests or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were used for within‐group comparisons, and chi‐squared tests for proportions.

A bivariate analysis was conducted to identify candidate variables for a linear regression model, with the change in daily scanning frequency during follow‐up as the dependent variable. Spearman's correlation (quantitative variables), Mann–Whitney U (dichotomous), and Kruskal–Wallis tests (polytomous) were used. Variables with the lowest p‐values were entered into a multivariable linear regression model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

isCGM Use

Before the educational intervention, the mean scan frequency was 3.1 ± 1.4 scans/day, with an isCGM active time of 59.5%. The most frequently reported reasons for low scanning frequency were omission (32.4%), feeling well (23.5%), and lack of time (11.8%). Less frequent reasons included low confidence in isCGM data (8.8%), fear of knowing results (8.8%), and diabetes distress (5.9%).

At 12‐week follow up, significant increases were observed in scan frequency and active time: mean scan frequency rose to 6.1 ± 3.0 scans/day and isCGM active time to 71.8%. The mean increase in scan frequency was 3.0 scans/day [95% CI: 1.8 to 4.1; p = 0.001] and in active time, 12.2% [95% CI: 5.1 to 19.4; p < 0.001]. The scan frequency also increased significantly after breakfast (19.4% vs. 58.1%, p = 0.004), after lunch (12.9% vs. 54.8%, p = 0.001), after dinner (6.5% vs. 48.4%, p < 0.001), and upon waking (6.5% vs. 29.0%, p = 0.043) (Table S2).

Multiple linear regression was conducted to assess the effect of age, diabetes duration, and initial GMI on the daily scan frequency difference. The GMI decrease overall model was not significant (p = 0.445).

MIG was significantly reduced by 17.9 mg/dL (95% CI: −36.8 to 1; p = 0.025). No significant changes were observed in other glycometric outcomes (Table 2).

Insulin Use

The daily bolus frequency increased by 0.5 bolus/day [95 CI 0.1 to 2.1; p = 0.047] from the beginning to the follow‐up. However, we did not detect differences in basal or bolus daily dose during the follow‐up (Table S3).

Discussion

Our study highlights the glycemic benefits of a brief, structured educational intervention designed to address suboptimal daily scanning frequency among patients with T1D using isCGM. The main finding was that an increase of 3.1 scans/day was associated with an 8% improvement in TIR, with each additional daily scan linked to a 0.51% increase in TIR.

Integrating technology into diabetes care is essential; however, technology alone does not guarantee positive results (Isaacs et al. 2020). When integrated with structured education, the benefits of these tools are enhanced, leading to improved clinical outcomes, increased patient‐reported self‐efficacy and satisfaction, and reduced drop‐out rates (Hermanns et al. 2019; Isaacs et al. 2020; Izquierdo et al. 2024; Lind et al. 2024; Yaron et al. 2019). The FLASH program developed by Hermanns et al. was the first isCGM‐based diabetes program, designed to provide patients with the knowledge and skills to utilize data provided by isCGM. The program demonstrated improvements in HbA1c (−0.28% at 6 months) and reduced diabetes‐related distress, with benefits attributed to more frequent use of both trend arrows for treatment adjustments and evaluation of their glucose data. Notably, participants maintained a high scanning frequency throughout the study (13.9 vs. 10.1 scans/day, p = 0.933) (Hermanns et al. 2019).

In contrast, our educational intervention focused on increasing scanning frequency through a single, targeted 1‐h session. Despite being a single 1‐h session, our results highlight the importance of increasing daily scanning frequency to improve glycemic outcomes in patients with diabetes who have low isCGM adherence. In fact, we observed a clinically relevant reduction in GMI (−0.4%) supported by an 8% improvement in TIR. Two recent 3‐h CGM educational programs (Kim et al. 2024; Lind et al. 2024), including one focused on isCGM, have successfully implemented short duration interventions, demonstrating their feasibility for both patients and healthcare professionals.

Several studies have highlighted the benefits of structured education for patients using rtCGM technology. Pemberton et al. reported that in children structured education increased TIR by 9.6% and reduced TBR < 70 mg/dL by 8.3%. Schlüter et al. demonstrated that training improved T1D patients' knowledge and skills; however, HbA1c reduction was of 0.1%, p = 0.04 (Schlüter et al. 2021). A recent study found that rtCGM with individualized education improved TIR by 15.3% compared to rtCGM alone in poorly controlled T1D adults (Hee Yoo et al. 2022).

Recent findings from the Flash‐UK study demonstrated a 0.8% reduction in HbA1c after 24 weeks of isCGM initiation in poorly controlled adults with T1D. This result was achieved through a daily scan frequency of 11 scans/day, which was correlated with an average HbA1c of 7.9% (Leelarathna et al. 2022). Real‐world data from isCGM users helped to demonstrate the relationship between daily scanning frequency and the glycemic benefits obtained through Flash technology. In a European analysis, Dunn et al. reported that estimated HbA1c gradually decreased from 8.0% to 6.7% as scan rates increased from lowest to highest scan groups (4.4 and 48.1 scans/day, respectively). Participants showed a 16.3 daily scan frequency associated with an HbA1c of 7.2% and TIR of 60.8% (Dunn et al. 2018). Another study reflected comparable trends, with a mean scan frequency of 13.2 scans/day and similar diabetes control of HbA1c 7.3% and TIR of 58% (Gomez‐Peralta et al. 2020).

Our previous data from adults with T1D in a public healthcare system aligned with earlier findings, showing an inverse relationship between daily scan frequency and HbA1c, and a direct association with TIR (Moreno‐Fernandez et al. 2023). The current study further supports this association, demonstrating a significant increase in TIR following an increase in daily scans. However, in the absence of a randomized controlled trial specifically designed to isolate the effect of scan frequency on glycemic outcomes, these findings reflect correlation rather than causation.

Strength and Limitations

This study is the first to evaluate the impact of a single, educational intervention specifically designed to enhance daily isCGM scanning frequency and improve glycemic outcomes using a patient‐centered, self‐management approach. It contributes to the limited literature exploring the association between scanning frequency and glycemic outcomes and provides practical strategies and educational materials to support patients with suboptimal adherence.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a quasi‐experimental study with no control group, the findings suggest an association but cannot establish causality. Second, the baseline glycemic data were based on 14 days with only 59.5% isCGM active time, whereas ≥ 70% use is generally needed for accurate 3‐month estimates (Riddlesworth et al. 2018; Xing et al. 2011). Nevertheless, in low‐adherence populations, this snapshot may be acceptable for initial evaluation. Third, the 12‐week follow‐up limits conclusions about long‐term sustainability, as adherence and clinical benefits may diminish over time. Finally, although the intervention was focused on increasing scan frequency, it also included basic guidance of how to interpret glucose values and trends due to basic ethical considerations, making it difficult to isolate the specific effects of scanning behavior alone.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that a single‐session diabetes educational intervention can lead to behavioral changes that positively impact glycemic outcomes. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive approaches that integrate diabetes technology and education while addressing specific barriers to self‐management. Moreover, our results underscore the need for further research on tailored educational programs and acknowledgment and support of nurse‐led educational initiatives.
