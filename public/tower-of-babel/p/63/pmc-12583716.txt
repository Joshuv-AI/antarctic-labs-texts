Depression in mice causes decreased neuronal excitability and enhanced frequency adaptation in medial prefrontal cortex pyramidal neurons
Authors: Anita Maria Rominto, Francesca Montarolo, Luna Berrino, Matilde Loddo, Antonio Bertolotto, Filippo Tempia, Eriola Hoxha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression is a major problem of contemporary society, with limited treatment efficacy due to incomplete understanding of cellular and molecular mechanisms. The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) is involved in stress response and depression, but the neuronal mechanisms are still debated. We induced a depressive-like behavior in mice by chronic social defeat stress. mPFC neuronal activity was recorded in cortical slices of susceptible mice that developed social avoidance, in resilient and in controls not subjected to the stress protocol. In susceptible mice, pyramidal neurons of mPFC layer 2/3 displayed reduced excitability with preserved passive membrane properties. Analysis of evoked firing revealed that the decreased excitability was due to a pronounced frequency adaptation. The mechanisms were investigated by analyzing single action potentials. We found a higher voltage threshold and an enhanced afterhyperpolarization, which are considered as determinants of hypo-excitability. Increased frequency adaptation impairs the ability to sustain firing during afferent excitatory inputs, thus disrupting the synchronization and stability of the mPFC network activity. Since mPFC layer 2/3 is connected to other brain regions involved in emotional regulation and stress responses, our results suggest that its neuronal dysfunction can be an important mechanism of mood alteration and depressed behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22321-7.Keywords: Chronic social defeat stress, Medial prefrontal cortex, Pyramidal neurons, Neuronal excitability, Frequency adaptation, DepressionSubject terms: Neuroscience, Stress and resilience

Introduction

The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) plays a pivotal role in the network of response to stress and in the development of the symptoms of depression including social avoidance, helplessness and anhedonia1–4. In fact, in patients affected by depression and in animal models, the mPFC undergoes a decrease in volume associated with a reduction in the dendritic tree (for a review, see5. Moreover, transcranial stimulation exerts the strongest antidepressant effect when applied to the mPFC6–9. However, the neuronal mechanisms acting in the mPFC to cause depression are still far from clear.

In order to investigate the mechanisms of depression in animal models, several protocols have been proposed to induce a depressive-like behavior in mice. Such methods are based on the fact that stress is a major risk factor in the development of depression10,11. The chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) protocol is considered as a physiological paradigm to induce a depression-like state, with high construct, face, discriminative and predictive validity12–15.

In patients with major depression and in CSDS mice it has been reported that markers of neuronal activity are reduced in individuals that developed a depressed behavior compared to resilient and controls16. Optogenetic stimulation of the mPFC reversed the symptoms, confirming that a reduced mPFC activity is causally linked to depression16. However, the nature of the reduction in mPFC activity is not clear. Abe et al.17 reported a decrease of spontaneous electrical activity recorded by multiple electrodes in the ventral portion of the mPFC in mice susceptible to CSDS, but the neuronal mechanisms were not studied. Neuronal activity is the result of neuronal excitability combined with the effects of excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs. Actually, some studies found alterations in synaptic transmission on mPFC pyramidal neurons, consisting in a depression of excitatory synapses with a reduction of dendritic branches and spines18–20 and of postsynaptic AMPA and NMDA receptors19, which are rescued by treatment with antidepressant drugs21. Other studies found deficits in GABAergic synapses (reviewed in22).

In the mPFC, the cells more directly involved in depression are layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons23. Layers 2/3 are the main input station of the mPFC, receiving afferents from hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, dorsal raphe nuclei, and locus coeruleus24. In addition to local projection, layers 2/3 of the mPFC send cortico-cortical and cortico-fugal projections, and efferences to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) mainly arise from these layers.

It is highly relevant to understand the mechanisms of the reduction of neuronal activity in depression, and an important determinant of neuronal activity is constituted by membrane excitability. Surprisingly, very few studies were focused on the intrinsic excitability of pyramidal neurons of the mPFC in models of depression. Interestingly, Liu et al.25 used a chronic restraint stress and reported no change in intrinsic excitability in layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons of the mPFC. They also recorded layer 5 pyramidal neurons, finding a selective increase of excitability in basolateral amygdala (BLA)-projecting mPFC neurons, but only in the dorsal portion of this area. Therefore, the important issue of changes in intrinsic excitability of mPFC pyramidal neurons in animal models of depression is still unsettled.

In this study, we used the CSDS in mice and focused on changes induced by this chronic stress protocol in neuronal excitability of layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons of the mPFC. The majority of recordings were from the prelimbic (PL) area, but we found a homogeneous effect also in the neighboring infralimbic (IL) cortex (Fig S1, S2, S3). We show that, in mice that developed a depressive-like behavior following CSDS, pyramidal neurons of layers 2/3 of the mPFC generated fewer action potentials in response to the injection of depolarizing current. An analysis of such responses showed that the reduced number of action potentials was due to an increase in frequency adaptation, a mechanism that dampens neuronal responses to excitatory inputs26.

Results

Chronic social defeat stress induces social avoidance in susceptible mice

Chronic stress is a key factor in the onset and progression of depression, and many animal models implement different stressors to induce this disorder27. In this study, CSDS was used to induce a depressive-like behavior in C57BL/6j mice. Experimental animals underwent 10 consecutive days of CSDS (Fig. 1a, b), while control mice were not exposed to social stress. On day 11 all mice were tested on the social interaction test (Fig. 1c). Susceptibility or resilience to chronic social stress was assessed by comparing their social interaction (SI) ratio (Fig. 1d, e) and using 1 as the threshold for dividing them into these categories. The SI ratio of susceptible (n = 4) mice was below 1 which was significantly lower than the SI ratio of control (n = 5) and resilient (n = 13) mice (Fig. 1e) (one way ANOVA, F(2,19) = 15.16, Tukey’s multiple comparison, control vs. susceptible P = 0.0005, resilient vs. susceptible P = 0.0001). No differences were observed for the SI ratio between control and resilient mice which both displayed values above 1 (Fig. 1e) (one way ANOVA, Tukey’s multiple comparison, control vs. resilient P > 0.05).

During the target session susceptible mice spent significantly less time in the interaction zone compared to the no target session (Fig. 1f) (target vs. no target, two-way ANOVA repeated measures, F(2,19) = 24.55, Sidak’s multiple comparison, P = 0.005) while control and resilient mice displayed a normal social behavior (Fig. 1f) (target vs. no target, two-way ANOVA repeated measures, Sidak’s multiple comparison, P < 0.0001) (across groups target comparison: control vs. susceptible and resilient vs. susceptible, two-way ANOVA repeated measures, Sidak’s multiple comparison, P < 0.0001). Susceptible mice, during the target session, spent significantly more time in the corners compared the no target session (Fig. 1g) (target vs. no target, two-way ANOVA repeated measures, F(2,19) = 16.38, Sidak’s multiple comparison, P < 0.001), thus displaying social avoidance, a hallmark of depressive-like behavior. On the other hand, the time spent in the corners for control and resilient mice was comparable across session (Fig. 1g) (target vs. no target, two-way ANOVA repeated measures Sidak’s multiple comparison, P > 0.05). Two-way ANOVA repeated measures revealed a significant difference in time across groups during the target session (Fig. 1g) (control vs. susceptible and resilient vs. susceptible, two-way ANOVA repeated measures Sidak’s multiple comparison, P < 0.0001). For locomotor activity (Fig. 1h), no interaction between stress responses (resilient and susceptible) and session types was found (two-way ANOVA repeated measures, F(2,19) = 1.351, P > 0.05).

Passive membrane properties are unaltered after chronic social stress

To investigate the effects of chronic social stress on layers 2/3 pyramidal neurons in the mPFC of resilient versus susceptible mice, whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed 1 day after the CSDS protocol (Fig. 2a) (control = 11 cells; resilient = 11 cells; susceptible = 10 cells). Passive membrane properties were first assessed: no significant difference was found in input resistance (Fig. 2c, d) (control: 132.7 ± 14.99 MΩ; resilient: 174.6 ± 15.55 MΩ; susceptible: 153.4 ± 18.98 MΩ; one way ANOVA, F(2,29) = 1.728, P > 0.05), resting membrane potential (Fig. 2e) (control: −69.14 ± 1.47 mV; resilient: −70.95 ± 0.89 mV; susceptible: −73.22 ± 0.89 mV; one-way ANOVA, F(2,29) = 3.034, P > 0.05) and sag ratio (Fig. 2f) (control: 1.204 ± 0.03; resilient: 1.184 ± 0.038; susceptible: 1.187 ± 0.02; Kruskal-Wallis test P > 0.05).

Chronic social stress decreases neuronal excitability and enhances frequency adaptation in susceptible mice

To evaluate whether chronic social stress affects the intrinsic excitability of mPFC pyramidal neurons, action potential firing in response to depolarizing current steps (20–400 pA, + 20 pA increments) was analyzed (Fig. 3a, c). All three groups exhibited a progressive increase in firing with increasing current injection. A two-way repeated measure ANOVA revealed significant main effects on current injection and stress responses, as well as a significant interaction between these factors (F(38,551) = 5.278, P < 0.0001). In fact, susceptible mice displayed a significantly lower number of action potentials compared to control mice at both low (at 120 pA susceptible mice fired 9.2 ± 0.66 action potentials vs. 14.27 ± 1.76 of control, P = 0.046; and vs. 13.82 ± 2.8 action potentials of resilient, P > 0.05) and high current injection (Fig. 3c) (at 400 pA susceptible mice fired 23 ± 2.28 action potentials vs. 34.09 ± 1.64 of control, P = 0.003; and vs. 33.55 ± 2.82 action potentials of resilient, P = 0.0251). No significant difference was observed between control and resilient groups. Rheobase analysis did not reveal a significant difference between groups (Fig. 3b, d) (control: 48.05 ± 3.9 pA; resilient: 46.19 ± 5.25 pA; susceptible: 55.11 ± 6.2 pA; one way ANOVA, F(2,27) = 0.8046, P > 0.05).

Further analysis showed that the decreased firing rate in susceptible mice was associated with significantly longer inter-spike intervals (ISIs) (Fig. 4a, b). The 1 st, 2nd, 5th, 7th and 10th ISIs were examined from firing responses at 200 pA of injected current. While no significant difference was found in the early ISIs (from the 1 st to the 5th) between the three groups, pyramidal neurons from susceptible mice exhibited significantly longer ISIs from the 7th and the 10th (Fig. 4a, b) (Mixed-effect model, Tukey’s multiple comparison; 7th ISI of susceptible: 93.43 ± 13.84 ms vs 50.59 ± 5.32 ms of control, P = 0.035; and vs. 55.17 ± 9.5 ms of resilient, P > 0.05; 10th ISI of susceptible: 83.98 ± 4.81 ms vs. 52.38 ± 4.5 ms of control, P = 0.0005; and vs. 51.01 ± 7.83 ms of resilient, P = 0.0073). Control and resilient groups showed no differences in ISIs. Moreover, the coefficient of variation (CV) of ISIs, calculated as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean, was significantly higher in susceptible mice compared to the other groups (Fig. 4c) (one way ANOVA, F(2,27) = 5.956, Tukey’s multiple comparison, vs. control P = 0.0078, vs. resilient P = 0.0331).

Susceptible mice exhibit altered action potential properties

Based on the observed decrease in firing activity of mPFC pyramidal neurons of susceptible mice, further analysis was conducted to determine whether this adaptation could be attributed to alterations of the features of action potentials (Fig. 5a). Therefore, the first action potential elicited at 200 pA was analyzed (Fig. 5b). Susceptible mice exhibited a significantly depolarized threshold for spike initiation compared to controls (Fig. 5c) (control: −45.02 ± 11.14 mV; resilient: −42.4 ± 0.83 mV; susceptible: −41.17 ± 1.185 mV; one way ANOVA, F(2,29) = 4.454, P = 0.0451; Tukey’s multiple comparison, control vs. susceptible P = 0.0413, resilient vs. susceptible P > 0.05). No significant differences were found in the action potential latency (Fig. 5d) (control:16.69 ± 1.13 ms; resilient: 14.74 ± 2.07 ms; susceptible: 20.61 ± 1.8 ms; one way ANOVA, F(2,29) = 2.972, P = 0.067; Tukey’s multiple comparison, control vs. susceptible P > 0.05, resilient vs. susceptible P > 0.05). No significant differences were observed between groups in action potential amplitude (Fig. 5e) (control: 92.57 ± 2 mV; resilient: 96.71 ± 1.522 mV; susceptible: 91.17 ± 1.76 mV; one way ANOVA, F(2,29) = 2.624, P > 0.05). Analysis of the afterhyperpolarization (AHP) revealed that susceptible mice exhibited a significantly higher AHP amplitude compared to controls (Fig. 5f) (control: −7.65 ± 0.06 mV; resilient: −8.641 ± 0.8 mV; susceptible: −10.72 ± 1.07 mV; one way ANOVA, F(2,29) = 3.337, P = 0.0496; Tukey’s multiple comparison, control vs. susceptible P = 0.042, resilient vs. susceptible P > 0.05).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that susceptible mice with robust social avoidance, a sign of depressed-like behavior caused by CSDS, display a marked reduction in intrinsic excitability of layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons of the mPFC. These neurons fire significantly fewer action potentials in response to depolarizing current injections, display longer ISIs during sustained firing, exhibit a higher threshold for action potential generation, and show an increased AHP amplitude compared to control and resilient mice. Interestingly, this hypo-excitability arises from enhanced frequency adaptation rather than alterations of passive membrane properties, implicating a dysregulation of active conductances after chronic stress.

Frequency adaptation is a key property of neurons by which they modulate their firing response during sustained depolarization. This adaptation is primarily mediated by K+ currents, including A-type (Kv4.x), M-type (Kv7/KCNQ) and Ca2+-activated K+ channels (SK and BK types)26,28,29. These currents cumulatively dampen neuronal responsiveness. The findings of increased ISIs and AHP amplitude, together with the enhanced action potential threshold, suggest an upregulation of some of these adaptation-mediating K+ currents in response to chronic stress that contributes to the pathophysiology of depression. The larger AHP amplitude suggests enhanced Ca2+-activated K+ channel activity, which are known to mediate AHP that controls frequency adaptation30.

Similarly, the increased action potential threshold might reflect alterations in subthreshold activated voltage-gated K+ channels.

The increased frequency adaptation observed could impact information processing in the layer 2/3 of the mPFC, as it receives inputs from other brain regions involved in emotional regulation and stress responses. This would impair the ability of pyramidal neurons to sustain firing during afferent excitatory inputs, thus disrupting the synchronization and stability of the mPFC network activity, leading to the behavioral phenotype of social avoidance observed in depressed-like mice.

These findings are in line with previous research on mPFC dysfunction in depression. In patients with major depression disorder, the mPFC shows reduced activity measured by the rate of glucose metabolism31, and similar reduction of neuronal activity is seen in animal models of depression16,17. While those studies demonstrated the presence of reduced activity, results of this research reveal a specific cellular mechanism contributing to this hypo-activity: increased frequency adaptation, which might be due to altered K+ channel function. This provides a better understanding of the neurophysiology of depression and suggests that K+ channels might represent potential therapeutic targets. Indeed, there is growing evidence that K+ channel dysfunction is implicated in psychiatric disorders. Alterations in Kv4.2 and Kir4.1 channels have been associated with increased vulnerability to stress and depressive behaviors32–35. Further studies are needed to determine which specific potassium currents are dysregulated in pyramidal neurons.

In conclusion, this study reveals that susceptibility to stress-induced depression is associated with reduced intrinsic excitability of mPFC layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons due to enhanced frequency adaptation. This cellular mechanism likely contributes to the reduced mPFC activity observed in both animal models and human patients with depression, constituting a neuronal mechanism that can be addressed by novel therapies.

Materials and methods

Animals

The experimental in vivo procedures were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines in compliance with national (D.L. N.26, 04/03/2014) and international law and policies (new directive 2010/63/EU). All experimental procedures were carried out at the Neuroscience Institute Cavalieri Ottolenghi (NICO), approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of Turin and authorized by the Italian Ministry of Health (authorization number 546/2020-PR). The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

C57BL/6j male mice of 8 weeks and 4–6 months old CD1 male mice were obtained from Charles River Laboratories and used in experiments.

Chronic social defeat stress

C57BL/6j male mice were exposed to chronic social defeat stress as previously described13. One week before the social defeat experiment, CD1 aggressive mice (termed as “resident aggressors”) were single housed on one side of a home cage divided by a transparent perforated Plexiglass barrier. C57BL/6j male mice (termed as “intruder”) were exposed to chronic social stress by introducing it in the side of the resident aggressor for a 10-minute session of repeated attacks. At the end of the session the intruder was transferred to the other side of the cage and spent there the following 24 h. This was repeated for 10 consecutive days and on each day the intruder was introduced to a new resident aggressor cage. After the last session of social defeat, the intruder was single housed for 24 h (Fig. 1a, b). Control C57BL/6j mice were pair housed in the same cage with one mouse per side and introduced to a new cage daily, mimicking the change of housing of the defeated C57BL/6j mice, but with no physical contact with their cage mate.

Social interaction test

Social interaction test (SIT) was performed on day 11, at the end of the 24 h period during which experimental mice were singly housed following chronic social stress. The test comprised two sequential sessions in an open field arena (50 × 50 cm) containing a wire mesh enclosure. During the “no target” session the experimental C57BL/6j mouse freely explored the arena with an empty enclosure. Following a brief removal, the “target” session introduced a non-aggressive CD1 mouse into the enclosure, allowing measurement of explorative and social behavior. Each session lasted 150 s and the locomotor activity of C57BL/6j mice was recorded with a camera placed on the roof of the arena. The time spent in the interaction zone (the area close to the wire mesh enclosure), the time spent in the two opposite corners and the total distance travelled by the mouse were analyzed using Ethovision XT15 video track system (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, The Netherlands) (Fig. 1c). The social interaction ratio was calculated as the time spent in the interaction zone in the target session divided by the time spent in the interaction zone in the no target session. Defeated mice with a ratio below or above 1 were classified as susceptible or resilient to social stress, respectively.

Slice preparation

Coronal mPFC slices were obtained from experimental mice as previously described36. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (Isoflurane-Vet, Merial, Italy) and transcardially perfused, then brains were extracted and sliced with a thickness of 300 μm using a vibratome (VT-1000 S, Leica, IL, United States), and initially incubated at 35° C for 10 min. For perfusion, cutting and initial incubation aCSF solution containing (in mM) 93 NMDG, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 25 glucose, 2 thiourea, 5 Na-ascorbate, 3 Na-pyruvate, 0.5 CaCl2 4H2O and 10 MgSO4 7H2O, ph adjusted at 7.3–7.4, was used. Then slices were incubated at room temperature in aCSF solution containing (in mM) 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1,25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 20 glucose, where they remained throughout the experiment. The same solution was then used for recordings. All aCSF solutions were bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2.

Electrophysiology

For whole-cell patch-clamp, individual slices were transferred to a recording chamber and continuously perfused at 2 mL/min with recording aCSF at 29 ± 1 °C. Gabazine (20 µM) and kynurenic acid (1 mM) were added to inhibit GABAA and ionotropic glutamate receptors, respectively. Recordings were obtained from visually identified layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons in mPFC regions (PL, IL) using borosilicate glass pipette with a resistance of 4–5 MΩ when filled with the internal solution containing (in mM) 140 K-Gluconate, 4 MgCl2, 0.5 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 4 Na2ATP, 0.4 Na2GTP (pH adjusted at 7.3). Current-clamp experiment was performed with PatchMaster software and EPC-10 patch-clamp amplifier (HEKA Elektronik, Lambrecht/Pfalz, Germany). Pyramidal neurons were distinguished from interneurons by their shape and spiking pattern. Subsequent confirmation of recorded neuronal identity and location in the mPFC was made using Alexa Fluor™ 488 Hydrazide (Invitrogen, A10436) (100 µM) added to the internal solution. Resting membrane potential was defined as the membrane potential measured with no injected current. A series of hyperpolarizing and depolarizing current steps from − 100 to + 400 pA, with a duration of 1000 ms were applied to pyramidal cells to elicit voltage responses. Input resistance was calculated by applying hyperpolarizing currents steps from − 20 to −100 pA, measuring the peak voltage responses, and determining the slope of the linear fit of the resulting I/V plot. The sag ratio was calculated by applying a hyperpolarizing current step of −100 pA, then the difference between the peak amplitude and the steady-state was measured (Fig. 2, b). The rheobase was determined by applying a ramping current injection from 0 to + 400 pA over 4 s, starting from the cell’s resting membrane potential without holding the cell at a fixed voltage. The minimal current required to evoke the first action potential during this ramp was then calculated using the formula “Current (pA) = (400 pA/4000 ms) x threshold onset (ms)”.

Data were analyzed offline using Axograph (AxoGraph Scientific, Sydney, Australia) and Igor Pro 9 software (WaveMetrics, Lake Oswego, OR, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check whether the data followed a normal distribution. For the data sets that passed the normality test, the one-way ANOVA test was used, followed by a post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. In case of failure of the normality test, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± SEM.
