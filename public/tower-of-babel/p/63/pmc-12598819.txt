A survey on the current status of Korean Medicine practices for asthma among Korean Medicine doctors
Authors: Soo-Dam Kim, Sungha Kim, Hee-Jae Jung, Kwan-Il Kim, Yee Ran Lyu, Beom-Joon Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma is a chronic respiratory disease affecting millions of people worldwide, with a growing prevalence in South Korea. Korean medicine (KM), which includes herbal medicine, acupuncture, moxibustion, cupping, and other holistic therapies, is widely used for asthma treatment in South Korea. However, data on the clinical practices of Korean medicine doctors (KMDs) are limited. This study aimed to assess the current status of KM practices in treating asthma.MethodsA clinical practice survey was conducted from 22 July to 5 August 2024, targeting all licenced KMDs. A total of 27,662 KMDs were invited and 402 completed the survey. The questionnaire collected data on demographics, diagnostic and treatment practices and challenges in the management of asthma. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the findings.ResultsHerbal medicine (97.76%) and acupuncture (68.41%) were the primary treatments for asthma, with high patient satisfaction (93.78% for herbal medicine). The most prescribed herbal formulas were Maekmundong-tang (43.03%) and Cheongsangboha-tang (41.29%). Most KMDs treated mild asthma (67.71%) and reported high levels of integration with Western medicine (WM), with 38.56% of KMDs indicating that 76 − 100% of their patients used Western medications concurrently. Key challenges included financial barriers (64.43%) and a need for better access to diagnostic tools and standardised treatment protocols.ConclusionThis clinical practice survey provides valuable information on the clinical practices of KMDs in asthma care, underscoring the significant role of herbal medicine and acupuncture. Background

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory respiratory disease characterised by recurrent symptoms of shortness of breath, coughing and wheezing, affecting individuals of all ages worldwide [1, 2]. The 2019 Global Burden of Disease collaboration estimates that approximately 262 million people suffer from asthma. This causes a significant burden by resulting in premature death and reduced quality of life, ranking as a leading cause of disability and disease burden globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [3–5]. In South Korea, the prevalence of asthma has steadily increased from 1.62% in 2006 to 4.74% in 2015, highlighting the growing socioeconomic burden [6].

The primary goal of asthma treatment is to achieve control over the condition and maintain normal daily activities. Therefore, various medications are commonly used, including inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and symptom relievers. However, long-term use of these medications, especially at high doses, can lead to side effects such as osteoporosis and oral candidiasis [7, 8]. As a result, interest in complementary and alternative approaches as adjunct or alternative treatment for asthma has been steadily growing.

Korean medicine (KM), which includes herbal medicine, acupuncture, moxibustion, cupping, and other holistic therapies, has been widely used in South Korea for centuries. KM is regarded as a core component of the Korean healthcare system, functioning in parallel with conventional Western medicine (WM) [9]. Increasing evidence supports its efficacy in the management of chronic diseases, including asthma. Studies suggest that herbal medicines used in KM can improve asthma symptoms by reducing inflammation, improving immune regulation and overall respiratory function [10, 11]. Furthermore, recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that herbal medicines, when combined with standard therapies, can significantly improve lung function and reduce the frequency of asthma exacerbations, demonstrating their therapeutic potential [12]. These treatments have been effective in alleviating symptoms without causing significant adverse effects, making them a safer alternative or complement to conventional treatments [11–14].

Since public healthcare data provide limited information on detailed diagnostic and therapeutic practices, especially for treatments not covered by insurance, real-world survey data are essential to comprehensively understand current clinical patterns, identify unmet needs in clinical practice, and inform the development of standardised treatment protocols and integrative care strategies for asthma. This study aimed to address this gap by conducting a survey of Korean medicine doctors (KMDs) to collect comprehensive data on diagnostic methods, treatment modalities, patient demographics and the limitations experienced by KMDs in asthma care. These findings will offer foundational data to develop clinical pathways (CP) and clinical practice guidelines that can optimise the role of KM in asthma management and support its integration into South Korea’s healthcare policies. This data collection is valuable for informing policy decisions to improve public health outcomes, address healthcare costs and reduce the socioeconomic burden associated with asthma.

Methods

Survey population and data collection

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical practices of KMDs in the management of asthma. All licenced KMDs registered with the Association of Korean Medicine (https://akom.org/English/Index) were invited to participate. A total of 27,662 KMDs were contacted via e-mail and text messages between 22 July and 5 August 2024. The Korean Institute of Oriental Medicine, in collaboration with the Association of Korean Medicine, distributed the survey using the SurveyMonkey platform (https://www.surveymonkey.com). The invitations included a link to the online questionnaire, along with a description of the purpose of the study and how the data would be used.

The first page of the online survey included a detailed explanation of the study, informing participants that the collected data would only be used for research purposes. It was also noted that the survey would take approximately 15 min to complete. Before proceeding with the survey, participants were asked to review the objectives and contents of the study and confirm their consent to participate voluntarily and allow the collection of their data. Participants who did not provide their consent were directed to exit the survey. The survey was conducted anonymously and the responses were securely collected without identifiable personal information.

Design and structure of the survey

The questionnaire was developed as a web-based self-report tool to collect both qualitative and quantitative data from KMDs regarding their clinical practices in asthma management. The questionnaire was initially developed based on previous survey research conducted by the research team and was later revised according to feedback from practitioners actively involved in asthma care to ensure the relevance and clarity of each question [15]. To further refine the tool, a pilot test was conducted with 10 experienced KMDs to assess clarity and usability, and minor revisions were made based on their feedback. The final survey comprised of 60 questions, including nine sub-questions, categorised into five key sections: clinical practice patterns, diagnostic methods, treatment approaches, efficacy and safety evaluations and additional considerations.

The clinical practice section included questions related to the number of patients with asthma treated annually, outpatient versus inpatient ratios, frequency of visits, duration of treatment, costs and challenges faced in clinical settings. The diagnostic section focused on factors considered in diagnosis, including comorbidities, diagnostic tools, and collaboration with WM. In the treatment section, participants were asked about specific interventions, such as herbal medicine, acupuncture and other therapies, along with the patient education provided during treatment. Efficacy and safety evaluations addressed the frequency of follow up, key indicators of treatment success, adverse reaction rates and the overall effectiveness and safety of asthma treatment. These outcomes were based on KMDs’ perceptions and self-reported clinical experiences, rather than direct patient-level data. The survey also included a section for additional opinions, such as the need for CPs and potential areas for improvement in asthma care. Demographic information was collected in the final section, which included questions on sex, age, clinical experience, location of practice and specialisation of the respondents.

The questionnaire was designed to take approximately 15 min to complete and incomplete or duplicate responses were excluded to ensure data integrity. Furthermore, the survey was structured to prevent submission if any questions were left unanswered, minimising missing data. The English version of the questionnaire developed for this study is provided in Supplementary.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the survey data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For survey items that allowed multiple responses, percentages were calculated using the number of respondents who answered the item as the denominator. To supplement the descriptive aims of the study, subgroup analyses were conducted to explore differences according to the availability of collaboration with WM. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables, and independent two-sample t-tests or Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used for continuous variables. Statistical significance was determined at a two-sided p-value of < 0.05. All data were analysed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Ethical approval and participant consent

This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine on 1 July 2024 (No. I-2405/005–001-01). Participants were fully informed about the purpose and details of the study before voluntarily agreeing to participate. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, ensuring that their personal information would be protected and that the data collected would be used exclusively for academic research purposes.

Results

Demographic and characteristics of survey respondents

A total of 402 KMDs completed the survey, corresponding to a response rate of approximately 1.5% (402 of 27,662 invited). Among the respondents, 70.90% were male. Most participants were in their 30 s (34.83%), followed by those in their 40 s (32.59%) and 50 s (23.13%). A small percentage were under 30 years old (2.49%) or over 60 years old (6.97%). Regarding clinical experience, 25.62% had been practicing for 5–10 years, while 23.63% had 20–30 years of experience. KMDs with over 30 years of experience accounted for 10.95% of respondents, and those with less than five years of practice made up 5.97%. Geographically, 32.34% of respondents were based in Seoul, 22.14% worked in Gyeonggi Province, and other regions were less represented. In terms of workplace distribution, 77.36% of respondents worked in general KM clinics, while 13.93% were employed in KM hospitals. Only 1.74% worked in respiratory care specialized clinics, and 22.64% reported working in facilities offering integrative care with WM. Regarding specialty certification, 26.37% of respondents were specialists, with the highest proportion specializing in Korean internal medicine (39.62%) and acupuncture (22.64%). The remaining respondents were general practitioners (Table 1).

Current status of KM clinical practice in asthma

KMDs treated an average of 14.20 asthma patients per year (Standard deviation = 22.61). Most care was provided on an outpatient basis, with 89.6% of KMDs treating patients exclusively in outpatient settings, while only 1.2% treated patients solely as inpatients. The most common schedule for outpatient visits was weekly (34.83%), followed by visits as needed (28.11%). For inpatient care, 6.97% of KMDs reported hospital stays lasting 1–2 weeks. The average treatment duration for asthma symptom control ranged from 3–6 months for 39.05% of KMDs, while 37.81% reported a treatment period of 1–3 months. According to responses from KMDs, the average out-of-pocket cost per asthma treatment session, excluding herbal medicine, was most commonly reported as between 10,000 and 20,000 South Korean Won (KRW) (42.54%). The most common age groups of asthma patients were 60–69 years (43.53%) and 50–59 years (39.55%). A significant number of patients delayed seeking care, with 58.21% waiting over a year from symptom onset. Mild asthma, classified as GINA 1–2, was the most commonly treated severity, with 67.71% of KMDs selecting it as the primary condition they manage. Most patients (76–100%) concurrently used Western medications, as reported by 38.56% of KMDs, with ICS being the most commonly used. Dissatisfaction with Western treatments (73.13%) was the primary reason patients sought KM treatment. The main challenge reported by KMDs was the financial burden on patients (64.43%) (Table 2).

Diagnostic practices in asthma

KMDs primarily diagnosed asthma based on clinical symptoms such as wheezing and shortness of breath (95.02%), followed by auscultation findings (32.34%) and the evaluation of comorbidities like allergic diseases (24.63%). The most commonly assessed comorbidities were allergic diseases (88.06%), sinusitis (37.31%), and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) (26.87%). Pulmonary function tests were considered the most essential diagnostic tool (70.15%), followed by imaging tests such as chest X-rays (54.73%) and allergy tests. In terms of traditional KM diagnostic criteria, Zang-Fu pattern differentiation was the most frequently used method (66.67%), followed by Eight-Principles pattern differentiation (48.01%) and Sasang constitutional diagnosis (27.86%). The most frequently diagnosed patterns were lung deficiency (66.24%), phlegm-dampness accumulation (41.48%), and external wind-cold (29.58%) (for definitions of each diagnostic term and pattern, see Table S1). Collaboration with WM was considered important by 91.79% of KMDs, primarily for accessing diagnostic tests like pulmonary function tests (82.66%) and for the treatment of acute exacerbations (57.72%) (Table 3).

Therapeutic interventions in Korean medicine for asthma

The survey revealed that KMDs most frequently used herbal medicine (97.76%) for asthma treatment, followed by acupuncture (68.41%) and lifestyle changes such as smoking cessation (33.08%). In terms of patient satisfaction, herbal medicine received the highest rating (93.78%), followed by acupuncture (38.31%) and pharmacopuncture (17.66%). The most commonly prescribed herbal formulas were Maekmundong-tang (43.03%), Cheongsangboha-tang (41.29%), and Socheongryong-tang (31.34%). The most frequently used herbal ingredients included Ophiopogon tuber (Maekmundong, 73.88%), Apricot seed (Haengin, 64.18%), and Ephedra (Mahuang, 63.18%). Acupuncture was primarily performed using body acupuncture (72.89%), with Feishu (BL13) being the most frequently used point (56.22%). Among pharmacopuncture options, Hominis placenta (38.06%) was the most common. Regarding patient education, 49.50% of KMDs provided guidance on asthma management. Among them, 69.50% focused on symptom control, 59.00% on environmental management, and 50.50% on the proper use of inhalers and medications (Table 4).

Efficacy and safety of asthma treatment

KMDs primarily evaluated the efficacy of asthma treatment on a monthly basis (43.03%), followed by biweekly evaluations (30.35%). The most frequently used assessment indicators included respiratory symptom evaluations (68.91%) and patient satisfaction (61.19%). Additionally, 42.29% of respondents assessed asthma-related quality of life. Regarding overall treatment effectiveness, most KMDs believed their treatments were “generally effective” (42.54%) or “somewhat effective” (41.79%), while only a small proportion considered them “highly effective” (13.43%).

Safety evaluations were conducted primarily on a monthly basis (53.98%) or every two weeks (20.15%). The most common safety indicators included monitoring adverse reactions (65.67%) and assessing vital signs (48.26%). Among KMDs who perceived adverse reactions (10.20%), most reported that such events were observed in less than 5% of their patients (34.15%). The most frequently observed adverse effects were gastrointestinal issues such as dry mouth, nausea, or vomiting (43.90%), followed by nervous system effects like anxiety or insomnia (26.83%) and cardiovascular issues such as increased blood pressure or palpitations (24.39%). The primary treatment methods associated with these adverse effects included herbal medicine (75.61%) and lifestyle changes (26.83%). Regarding the association between combined Korean and WM treatment and adverse reactions, 4.88% of KMDs believed there was a clear causal relationship, while 34.15% considered it probable. These findings highlight the importance of continuous monitoring and evaluation to ensure the safety of asthma treatments integrating KM (Table 5).

Additional survey results

The survey results highlight several important aspects related to the development of a CP for KM in asthma care. Most of the respondents (53.48%) indicated that the development of a CP for asthma treatment in KM is crucial, while 42.29% considered it somewhat necessary. When asked what components should be included in the CP, the most frequently selected items were KM diagnostic criteria (69.15%), KM treatments such as herbal medicine and acupuncture (72.64%) and patient management methods like lifestyle adjustments (68.16%). In terms of using the CP, 54.23% of KMDs reported that they would use it frequently, while 38.56% indicated that they would use it occasionally. The survey also asked which non-insurance-covered herbal formulas would be most beneficial if covered by insurance. Cheongsangboha-tang (34.58%) and Maekmundong-tang (29.35%) were the options chosen most frequently. Regarding diagnostic tools, 52.74% of the respondents indicated that they would frequently use a simplified pulmonary function test if it was available for asthma diagnosis and assessment and 37.06% indicated that they would use it occasionally. These findings emphasise the strong interest in integrating both KM and Western diagnostic methods to improve asthma care (Table 6).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed according to the availability of collaboration with WM at the institution. There was no significant difference in the average duration of treatment for asthma symptom control between groups (p = 0.637). The average out-of-pocket cost per visit was significantly higher in the collaboration group (p = 0.003) (Fig. 1A), while the mean cost of prescribed herbal medicine did not differ significantly (p = 0.272). No significant difference was found in asthma severity (p = 0.239) or proportion of patients receiving Western medications (p = 0.877). Perceived treatment effectiveness differed significantly (p = 0.009) (Fig. 1B). No significant differences were observed in adverse event experience and frequency (Table S2).

Subgroup comparison of (A) average out-of-pocket cost per visit (excluding herbal medicine) and (B) perceived effectiveness of treatment, according to the availability of collaboration with Western medicine (hospital vs. clinic). p < 0.05 for both comparisons

In diagnostic and evaluation practices, the collaboration group more frequently reported difficulty in treatment (p = 0.045) and low cost-effectiveness (p < 0.001). Pulmonary function tests (p = 0.001), chest X-rays (p < 0.001), and blood tests (p = 0.020) were more commonly used in the collaboration group. Pulmonary function test results (p = 0.017), patient satisfaction (p = 0.044), and quality of life assessments (p = 0.034) differed significantly between groups. Safety evaluations using blood tests (p < 0.001) and urine tests (p = 0.005) were also more frequent in the collaboration group (Table S3).

Discussion

This clinical practice survey provides valuable information on the clinical practices of KMDs for the treatment of asthma in South Korea, highlighting key patterns in diagnosis, treatment approaches and the integration of KM with conventional WM. Asthma remains a major global and national healthcare challenge [3–6, 16], and this study offers a comprehensive overview of how KM contributes to the management of this chronic disease.

Although acupuncture, moxibustion, and cupping are generally covered by public insurance in Korea, herbal medicine was reported as the most frequently used treatment modality (97.76%), followed by acupuncture (68.41%). This preference may reflect the known anti-inflammatory and immune-modulating effects of herbal medicine in asthma management [12, 17, 18]. In contrast, acupuncture and moxibustion are typically used for symptomatic relief, such as easing cough and dyspnea, rather than directly addressing the underlying pathophysiology [19]. Consequently, both clinicians and patients may prioritize treatment effectiveness over insurance coverage when selecting therapeutic modalities. The most commonly prescribed herbal formulas, including Maekmundong-tang (43.03%), Cheongsangboha-tang (41.29%), and Socheongryong-tang (31.34%), have been recognized for their potential to alleviate asthma symptoms by targeting the immune and respiratory systems [20–25]. Acupuncture was also widely used with Feishu (BL13) being the most commonly utilized point, consistent with its known benefits in promoting respiratory health [26].

Our findings indicate that KMDs predominantly manage mild to moderate asthma, with 67.71% of respondents reporting that they most frequently treat mild cases. Notably, 38.56% stated that 76–100% of their asthma patients concurrently use Western medications such as ICS, suggesting that KM is often integrated with conventional care [12]. Compared to other conditions commonly treated in KM clinics, asthma care displays distinct characteristics, particularly in that it is predominantly managed alongside conventional WM. [15, 27]. Patients with asthma tend to seek KM treatment more than one year after symptom onset (58%), show higher rates of concurrent Western medication use and biomedical diagnostic testing (70.1% for pulmonary function tests; 54.7% for chest X-rays). This pattern may reflect a subpopulation of patients who experience limited response or intolerance to conventional therapies and thus seek complementary options. These findings suggest that asthma patients turning to KM may represent a more treatment-resistant group with greater reliance on integrative care, thereby underscoring the importance of close collaboration with WM [28, 29]. Patient satisfaction with KM was high (93.78% for herbal medicine), indicating that it may offer effective symptom relief with fewer side effects compared to long-term corticosteroid use [30].

Despite these promising results, KMDs face notable challenges in asthma care, with the financial burden on patients being the most frequently reported issue (64.43%). This highlights the need for increased financial support or expanded insurance coverage for KM treatments to improve accessibility for a broader population [31]. Additionally, 91.79% of KMDs emphasised the need to collaborate with WM, particularly for access to diagnostic tools such as pulmonary function tests (82.66%) and the management of acute exacerbations. This underscores the importance of continuing integration between KM and conventional medicine to provide comprehensive care, especially for patients with severe asthma. However, the study also identified areas for improvement in KM practice. While traditional diagnostic methods such as Zang-Fu pattern differentiation (66.67%) are widely used, the lack of standardised diagnostic and treatment protocols remains a challenge for KMDs [32]. To address this, the development of a CP for asthma care in KM, which 53.48% of the respondents considered “very necessary,” could help standardise care and optimise asthma management in KM settings.

Subgroup analyses based on the availability of collaboration with WM (KM–WM collaborative institutions vs. KM-only institutions) provided further insights into the characteristics of asthma care. Institutions with such collaboration, typically hospitals, reported significantly higher out-of-pocket costs per visit, with a higher proportion of KMDs charging 20,000–30,000 KRW or more (p = 0.003) (Fig. 1A). This cost difference is likely attributable to differences in consultation fees and patient copayments between hospital- and clinic-level institutions. In addition, hospitals more frequently employed advanced diagnostic modalities such as pulmonary function tests (p = 0.001), chest X-rays (p < 0.001), and blood tests (p = 0.020), which may contribute to increased medical expenses. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the average duration of treatment for symptom control (p = 0.637), asthma severity (p = 0.239), or the proportion of patients using Western medications, including ICS (p = 0.877), suggesting that the baseline clinical characteristics were comparable across groups.

However, a notable divergence was found in the perceived effectiveness of treatment (p = 0.009) (Fig. 1B). KMDs at KM–WM collaborative institutions more frequently rated their treatments as “somewhat effective,” whereas those at KM-only institutions more often described them as “mostly effective.” This difference may stem from institutional expectations and differing evaluation frameworks. KM–WM collaborative institutions, typically hospital-based, tended to utilize more objective clinical indicators such as pulmonary function test results (p = 0.017), whereas KM-only institutions placed greater emphasis on subjective measures such as patient satisfaction (p = 0.044) and asthma-related quality of life (p = 0.034). The greater reliance on standardized, quantifiable assessments in collaborative settings may lead to more conservative evaluations of treatment effectiveness, in contrast to the more impression-based assessments seen in primary KM clinics.

Additionally, KMDs at collaborative institutions more frequently cited challenges in asthma care, including treatment difficulty (p = 0.045) and low cost-effectiveness (p < 0.001), which may reflect the complexity of care coordination and higher costs in hospital settings. These institutions also more frequently performed objective safety evaluations such as blood (p < 0.001) and urine tests (p = 0.005); however, the reported rates of adverse events and their perceived causality remained comparable between groups. This suggests that KM treatment may be a safe therapeutic option, even when evaluated using stricter clinical safety protocols. These findings suggest that collaboration with WM influences not only the cost structure but also the diagnostic approach and perceived effectiveness of KM treatments. Although patient characteristics are similar across settings, institutional dynamics, such as standardized assessments and interdisciplinary care, appear to shape practice patterns and perceptions of treatment effectiveness in asthma care.

The findings of this survey provide valuable guidance for future research and practice in KM for asthma care, particularly in the design of clinical studies and the development of CP. For example, in designing randomised controlled trials, researchers could target specific patient groups, such as those with mild to moderate asthma, as indicated by the study (Table 2). The KM interventions highlighted in this research could serve as priority candidates for future studies. Furthermore, the development of CP could benefit from incorporating diagnostic and treatment elements that are consistent with the needs and opinions of KM practitioners and patients, as identified in the survey. The interdisciplinary collaboration between KM and WM is also crucial, especially in hospital settings, to provide safe and effective asthma management when both KM and Western treatments are used concurrently (Table 3). This approach would strengthen the evidence base necessary to integrate KM into national asthma treatment guidelines.

There are several limitations to this study that should be acknowledged. First, the response rate to the survey was relatively low, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. However, the demographic characteristics of the respondents were largely consistent with national statistics [33]. Approximately 70% of respondents were male, similar to the gender distribution in the national KMD workforce. Most were in their 30 s or 40 s, reflecting the predominant age group among KMDs. Respondents were geographically distributed across South Korea, with higher representation from metropolitan areas such as Seoul and Gyeonggi Province, which aligns with the regional concentration of KMDs. Additionally, most respondents were practicing in KM clinics, consistent with the national composition of KM institutions. These similarities suggest that the sample may be reasonably representative of the broader KMD population involved in asthma care. Nonetheless, due to the low response rate, full representativeness cannot be guaranteed and the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Additionally, the survey relied on self-reported data, which introduces the possibility of bias, especially regarding the reported effectiveness of treatments [34, 35]. Future studies should aim for larger sample sizes and incorporate objective measures of treatment outcomes to strengthen validity. Although this study provides valuable information on KM practices in asthma care, it lacks detailed information on the frequency of treatment by type of intervention. Future research should explore these aspects to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the role of KM in asthma management.

Conclusions

This clinical practice survey offers valuable information on the real-world clinical practices of KMDs in asthma care, detailing specific treatment patterns and the role of KM interventions such as herbal medicine and acupuncture. By highlighting treatment approaches and patient groups suited to KM, this study provides the foundation for future clinical trials, targeted CP development and safe integration with WM. These findings clarify the potential role of KM in asthma management and offer essential data to advance its application in the Korean healthcare system.
