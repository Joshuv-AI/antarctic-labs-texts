The relationship between women’s awareness of sustainable menstrual cups, their menstrual attitudes, and genital hygiene behaviors: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Aynur Ataman Kufacı, Funda Erol, Tuba Yılmaz Bulut, Hurmus Kuzgun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between women’s awareness of menstrual cups, their menstrual attitudes, and their genital hygiene behaviors is emerging as an important research topic in the field of women’s health globally. This study examined the relationship between women’s awareness of menstrual cups as a sustainable menstrual hygiene product, their menstrual attitudes, and their genital hygiene behaviors.MethodThis descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted with 542 women recruited through proportional stratified sampling from Turkey’s seven geographical regions. Data were collected using the Personal Information Questionnaire, the Menstruation (Period) Symptom and Attitude Scale, and the Genital Hygiene Behavior Scale. Menstrual cup awareness was assessed through a single binary (yes/no) self-report question.ResultsThe average age of participants was 27.97 (± 8.23). Of the participants, 78.8% stated that they had heard of menstrual cups before, which was considered an indicator of menstrual cup awareness in the study. Women who were unaware of menstrual cups showed higher menstrual perception scores, indicating that they perceived menstrual bleeding more positively as a natural process (mean = 34.40, p = 0.048). Conversely, women aware of menstrual cups demonstrated significantly higher scores on the menstrual discomfort and concealment dimensions, which reflects lower menstrual discomfort and concealment tendency (p < 0.001). They also showed superior genital hygiene behaviors, including better general hygiene habits (p = 0.048), menstrual hygiene practices (p = 0.038), and awareness of abnormal findings (p = 0.049). Multivariate logistic regression revealed that menstrual cup awareness was higher in younger age groups (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.98, p = 0.003), and that higher menstrual discomfort and concealment tendency scores (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16, p = 0.011) and increased awareness of abnormal findings (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.01–1.23, p = 0.030) were associated with increased menstrual cup awareness.ConclusionThis study revealed that women’s menstrual cup awareness was significantly associated with their menstrual attitudes and genital hygiene behaviors. Participants who were aware of menstrual cups tended to be younger. Additionally, unmarried participants and those with higher levels of education exhibited higher menstrual cup awareness. According to logistic regression analysis, menstrual cup awareness was found to decrease with age. It was determined that menstrual cup awareness increased as menstrual discomfort and concealment tendency scores and awareness of abnormal findings scores increased.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

The menstrual period is the physiological process occurring roughly once a month, characterized by the shedding of the uterine lining (endometrium) through vaginal bleeding, beginning at puberty and ending with menopause [1]. This process, which is a natural part of the female life cycle, poses a significant challenge for managing hygiene and comfort [2]. Inadequate menstrual hygiene management can lead to various issues, including infection risk, reproductive health problems, withdrawal from social life, school and work absences, and decreased quality of life [3, 4]. In this context, it is evident that genital hygiene behavior is an important factor that affects not only women’s physical health but also their quality of life [5]. Features of the product used, such as skin compatibility, leak control, comfort, duration of use, and easy availability, play a decisive role in ensuring hygienic conditions [6]. Women in low- and middle-income countries who lack access to disposable sanitary products are more likely to use unhealthy alternatives, which leads to poor genital hygiene and infection [7, 8]. The results highlight the importance of raising awareness about the menstrual cup as a sustainable, environmentally friendly, and affordable option.

The menstrual cup is a bell-shaped instrument made of high-quality medical silicone and is placed in the vagina during the menstrual period to collect menstrual blood [9]. It is a cost-effective, sustainable, and practical product that can be used safely and effectively for 5 to 10 years [9, 10]. Compared to other menstrual products, it has advantages such as comfort, appearance, dryness, long-term use, less smell, freedom of movement, and affordability [10–12]. The ability of the menstrual cup to retain blood for 6–12 h provides freedom and comfort outside the home [13]. The studies have indicated that the menstrual cup is considered reassuring since it does not cause skin disorders such as sores, redness, itching, pain, or peeling [14, 15]. The menstrual cup has been shown to help maintain physical activity during menstruation, thereby improving quality of life [16]. The literature has reported findings that suggest menstrual cup use improves hygiene behaviors [17–20]. Although existing studies have primarily focused on experiences with menstrual cup use and do not explicitly address such outcomes, it may be expected that increasing awareness of menstrual cups could enhance women’s familiarity with their own bodies, encourage more conscious hygiene practices, and consequently strengthen genital hygiene behaviors. On the other hand, menstrual cup awareness is thought to be positively associated with menstrual attitude [21].

Menstrual attitude is the totality of a person’s cognitive, affective, and behavioral tendencies toward the menstruation process. Individual, social, and cultural factors are associated with menstrual attitude [22]. While some women perceive menstruation as a shameful, dirty situation they should hide [23, 24], some consider the same to be a natural, healthy process which is a part of the physiology and a guarantee of fertility [25, 26].

Within the framework of the Health Belief Model, women who gain awareness and realize the benefits of menstrual cups more clearly may perceive the menstrual process as a manageable experience and develop a positive menstrual attitude [27]. In their study [28], Ahuja and Singh found that women’s health belief perceptions are significantly associated with their attitudes toward sustainable menstrual products. Although the literature shows that menstrual cup awareness is positively associated with the attitude toward the product [29–31], its relationship with women’s menstrual attitudes in general remains under-researched. In this context, the study addresses an important gap in the literature and makes a unique contribution to the field.

Research purpose

This study aimed to assess the relationship between women’s menstrual cup awareness, their menstrual attitude, and genital hygiene behaviors.

Methods

Design

This study has a descriptive and cross-sectional design. The STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist criteria were used to ensure the accuracy of the report on the research process [32].

Participants

The research population covered all women over the age of 18 who had experienced menstruation and were living in Turkey. The female population aged 18 and over in Turkey is 32,233,351 [33]. The number of women aged 18 and over by region is as follows: the Mediterranean Region, 3,809,702; the Eastern Anatolia Region, 2,119,285; the Aegean Region, 6,399,735; the Southeast Anatolia Region, 2,592,687; the Central Anatolia Region, 4,891,602; the Black Sea Region, 3,038,375; and the Marmara Region, 9,381,965 [34]. If the population size is known, the sample size is calculated using the formula n = Nt²pq / (d² (N-1)) + t²pq. In the formula, N is the population size, n is the sample size, p is the probability of the event occurring, q or 1-p is the probability of the event not occurring, d is the accepted ± sampling error rate, and t is the theoretical value in the t-table at a specific significance level [35]. Using the above formula, with a 5% margin of error and a 98% confidence interval, the minimum sample size for the population specified was calculated to be 542 individuals.

The study sample was proportionally stratified by Turkey’s seven geographical regions. According to this method, the population was divided into different subgroups, and a certain percentage of individuals from each group (quota) was included in the sample. First, the quota ratios were determined by dividing the population of each region by the total population. Then, the pre-determined sample size of 542 was multiplied by the percentages to determine the minimum number of individuals to be included in the study from each region. Accordingly, the number of individuals to be included in the research from each region was determined as follows: Mediterranean Region: 64, Eastern Anatolia Region: 36, Aegean Region: 108, Southeastern Anatolia Region: 44, Central Anatolia Region: 82, Black Sea Region: 51, and Marmara Region: 157.

Due to the large research population and difficulty of achieving the required sample size, data were collected using the snowball sampling method, one of the purposive sampling methods. The survey was conducted via Google Forms and distributed to participants through the social platform. Women over the age of 18 who agreed to participate in the study, had no cognitive, emotional, or verbal communication issues, were literate, were currently menstruating, and had access to a device (phone, tablet, computer) suitable for filling out the online survey form were included. A total of 6 women declined to participate in the study. The study was conducted with 542 participants comprising a predetermined number of individuals from each region. As the number of participants who chose not to fill out the questionnaire was not known, the dropout rate could not be calculated.

Data collection tools

The research survey consists of three parts: (1) Personal Information Questionnaire (2), Menstruation (Period) Symptom and Attitude Scale, and (3) Genital Hygiene Behavior Scale.

Personal information questionnaire

The questionnaire [36–39], developed by researchers based on the literature, consists of 16 questions concerning factors related to women’s socio-demographic characteristics, menstrual cup use, menstrual attitude, and genital hygiene behavior.

Menstruation (Period) symptom and attitude scale

The five-point Likert-type scale developed by Düzbayır [40] comprises two sections: “Symptom” and “Attitude”. The “Attitude” section was used in this research. The Menstrual Attitude Scale (MAS) has 19 items and comprises four subscales: menstrual perception, discomfort and tendency to conceal menstruation, feelings about menstruation, and menstruation as a health indicator. Items 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, and 16 in the Attitude section are reverse-scored. Each sub-dimension of the scale is scored separately. Higher scores on the ‘Menstrual Perception’ sub-dimension indicate higher menstrual perception. As the score obtained on the “Menstrual Discomfort and Tendency to Conceal Menstruation” sub-dimension increases, menstrual discomfort and concealment tendency decrease. As the score obtained in the “Menstrual Feelings” sub-dimension increases, positive attitudes toward feelings related to menstruation increase. As the score obtained on the sub-dimension ‘Menstruation Perceived as an Indicator of Health’ increases, perception of menstruation as an indicator of health increases. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the attitude section of the scale is 0.79 [40]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was found to be 0.797.

Genital hygiene behavior scale (GHBS)

It is a five-point Likert-type scale developed and validated by Karahan [41]. The scale has a total of 23 items and three subscales: General Hygiene Habits (first 12 items), Menstrual Hygiene (items 13–20), and Awareness of Abnormal Findings (items 21–23). The scale ranges from a minimum of 23 to a maximum of 115 points. Higher scores indicate that genital hygiene behavior is positive. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale is 0.80 [41]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was calculated as 0.802.

Data collection

The data were collected between May and July 2025. Using snowball sampling on social media, women over 18 from Turkey’s seven geographical regions were recruited, and data were collected online via Google Forms. Participants were asked to share the online survey link via social media applications or social networking sites with their acquaintances who met the eligibility criteria. Participants who agreed to participate in the study approved the informed consent form online. They were asked to complete the survey in about 15–20 min according to their own thoughts. Various measures were taken in order to improve the data quality and minimize the potential risks related to the online data collection process. In this context (1), participants were reassured of anonymity and confidentiality (2), a clear and understandable survey design was preferred (3), explanatory guidelines were provided to prevent possible misunderstandings (4), inclusion criteria were clearly stated (5), participants were sent weekly reminders until they completed the survey (6), the responses obtained were evaluated for consistency. The data collection process continued until the targeted sample size for each stratum was achieved, and participant recruitment was terminated for the strata where the targeted sample size was achieved. The number of responses from each stratum was regularly monitored by the researchers. Since all scale items in Google Forms were mandatory, the system did not allow submission until all questions were answered. Therefore, there were no missing data or partially completed forms in the final dataset.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27 [42], and R software [43] were used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range (Q1–Q3) for continuous variables, and as frequency and percentage for categorical variables. Then, the normality distribution of the Menstrual Attitude Scale and Genital Hygiene Behavior Scale by groups was assessed using kurtosis and skewness coefficients. If the kurtosis coefficient had values within the range of ± 2 and the skewness coefficient had values within the range of ± 2, the data would be considered to be normally distributed [44].

For comparisons between two groups of normally distributed continuous variables, the independent samples t-test was used. For comparisons between two groups of non-normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. In addition, associations between categorical variables were examined using the chi-square test of independence or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, selected according to Cochran’s rule regarding expected cell frequencies.

Menstrual cup awareness was assessed through a single binary (yes/no) self-report question that asked participants to state whether they had heard of menstrual cups before. Using variables found to be significant in mean comparisons, the relationship between menstrual cup awareness (1 = Aware, 2 = Unaware) and independent variables was evaluated using a binary logistic regression model. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The multicollinearity coefficients between the independent variables in the model were examined using the generalized variance inflation factor, which takes into account the degrees of freedom in categorical variables, and the GVIF values were reported. When interpreting the GVIF values for independent variables, the commonly used threshold of VIF = 10 in the literature was adopted [45, 46].

The logistic regression graph was obtained using the sjPlot package [47] available in the R software. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was used to determine the reliability of the scales. In all calculations and interpretations, the statistical significance level of “p < 0.05” was taken into account.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration for medical research involving human volunteers. Ethical approval to conduct the study was obtained from the Kocaeli University Faculty of Medicine Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Date: April 24, 2025, No: 2025/194). Participants were informed online about the purpose and procedures of the study, and informed consent was obtained from them. Throughout the research, the principles of confidentiality and anonymity were strictly adhered to. In this regard, all data obtained were stored on a password-protected computer, accessible only to researchers, and were used solely for the analysis process of the current research.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

It was determined that the mean age of participants was 27.97 ± 8.23 years and that the vast majority were single (63.3%). As to the educational levels, 77.3% were observed to have a bachelor’s degree. The percentage of those who stated that their period prevented them from going to work or school was 62.7%. The proportion of participants aware of menstrual cups was 78.8%. The most commonly used product was sanitary towels (87.4%). The majority changed this product every 1–3 h (57.0%), and almost all (96.8%) dispose of the product by throwing it in the bin (Table 1).

Table 5 shows the relationship between menstrual cup awareness and certain variables. The variables of age (p < 0.001), marital status (p = 0.001), educational status (p = 0.001), and monthly expenditure on menstrual products (p = 0.022) were significantly associated with menstrual cup awareness. Thus, the participants who were aware of menstrual cups were younger. On the other hand, participants who were single and received higher education demonstrated higher menstrual cup awareness. In addition, as the monthly expenditure on menstrual products increased, the menstrual cup awareness also increased. Whereas, no significant relationship was found between menstrual cup awareness and the variables of having been informed about menstruation before menarche (p = 0.290), menstruation-related interference with work or school (p = 0.058), and frequency of changing the menstrual product (p = 0.469).

Discussion

The relationship between women’s awareness of the menstrual cycle, their menstrual attitudes, and hygiene behaviors has emerged as a noteworthy area in women’s health literature. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between women’s menstrual cup awareness, their menstrual attitudes, and genital hygiene behaviors.

Although 78.8% of respondents stated they were aware of menstrual cups, only 1.5% of them reported use. This significant gap between awareness and use is thought to be due to cultural differences and practical concerns. Studies have reported that use rates remain low due to deeply rooted cultural norms, perceived hygiene concerns, and practical concerns (insertion) [13, 48, 49]. Furthermore, studies examining ways to reduce this significant gap between awareness and use have emphasized the need to expand educational programs and leverage peer support and social networks to reduce prejudices against menstrual cup use [2, 50, 51]. However, menstrual cup awareness rates have been reported to be higher in Europe and North America, while use remains limited due to factors such as social norms, hygiene concerns, and cost [52]. The relatively low number of participants using menstrual cups (1.5%) in the current study limited the ability to conduct comparative or regression-based analyses between users and non-users. Therefore, the current study primarily focused on findings related to awareness.

Our study identified several factors that may explain why, despite widespread awareness of menstrual cups, their use remains low. These include lack of knowledge about use, fear of insertion and removal, difficulty with cleaning, fear of leakage, cost, and fear of damaging the hymen. Similarly, other studies have also indicated that women have insufficient knowledge about the product [50], experience difficulties with insertion and removal [53–55], and are afraid of hymen rupture [56, 57]. When evaluated within the framework of the Health Belief Model, concerns and uncertainties about menstrual cups emerge as factors that need to be addressed along with the perceived barriers to product use. As the model suggests, providing accurate information, sharing experiences, and highlighting the hygienic, economic, and environmental benefits of menstrual cups may reduce these perceived barriers and, in turn, increase the likelihood of adopting the behavior. This situation highlights that evaluations regarding menstrual cup use should consider not only the physical characteristics of the product, but also cultural differences and menstrual health awareness level.

The current study determined that participants’ genital hygiene behaviors were generally positive. Given that the lowest possible score on the scale was 23 and the highest was 115, the mean score obtained (93.54 ± 9.67) can be considered quite high. In another study, the mean total GHBS score was 71.07 ± 10.48 [56]. A further study conducted among women aged 15–49 reported a mean score of 78.5 ± 11.9 [58]. This difference can be attributed to the participants’ age, educational status, and access to health information.

In the current study, women who were aware of menstrual cups demonstrated better genital hygiene behavior compared to those unaware, indicating a relationship between menstrual cup awareness and genital hygiene behaviors. However, it should also be considered that women with better hygiene behaviors might be more likely to seek health-related information, which might have increased their likelihood of reporting awareness. Within the framework of the Health Belief Model, this finding contributes to the interpretation of the relationship between awareness and preventive health behaviors. From another perspective, providing women with resources and educational opportunities regarding menstrual health is considered a supportive element related to hygiene practices [59, 60].

According to the literature, social norms and stigma can hinder the use of sustainable menstrual products, making hygiene management more challenging [61]. In their study, Februadi and colleagues [62] have demonstrated a significant relationship between increased environmental awareness and the tendency to use green products, including menstrual cups. This result shows that menstrual cups can be considered a public health intervention that supports environmental sustainability. They have also indicated that this information may encourage more sustainable behaviors in menstrual hygiene management [62]. Therefore, women’s awareness of alternative products such as menstrual cups has been linked to conscious, healthy choices related to personal hygiene habits and environmental sustainability.

In our study, women with lower menstrual cup awareness demonstrated higher mean menstrual perception scores. A study by Shastry and Gomathy [53] involving 120 women aged 18–45 reported that only 5.8% of the participants were aware of menstrual cups. Another study [57] determined that women who were aware of the menstrual cup were more likely to use it for menstrual hygiene management. Women who are unfamiliar with the menstrual cup may hold more positive attitudes toward the product because they are unaware of its potential disadvantages. Conversely, those who are highly aware of menstrual cups may struggle with concerns about insertion and removal, as well as hygiene, which can be associated with doubt and fear [2]. Questioning cultural taboos surrounding menstruation is considered crucial for changing women’s attitudes and behaviors. Studies have shown that educating women about sustainable menstrual health can lead to positive changes in their menstrual attitudes. It has been reported that women who received relevant training are more likely to view the menstrual cycle as a natural biological phenomenon and experience less stigma associated with it; likewise, it has been emphasized that training may encourage more appropriate and thoughtful use of menstrual hygiene products [56, 63]. A study by Davile et al. [2] reported significant improvements in women’s hygiene practices, including the correct use of sanitary pads and menstrual cups, following educational interventions. However, findings in the literature remain mixed [2, 53, 56, 57, 63]. This situation may stem from the fact that, as knowledge increases, women become more open to using menstrual cups as a sustainable product, while also adopting a more critical approach to their suitability for everyday life.

In the current study, the bivariate analysis showed that the mean age of women who were aware of menstrual cups was lower. This finding was also confirmed in the binary logistic regression analysis, showing that the age variable significantly predicted menstrual cup awareness. Accordingly, for each additional year of age, the menstrual cup awareness rate decreased by 5%. This finding suggests that menstrual cup awareness is higher among younger age groups. Similarly, studies in the literature have reported that menstrual cup awareness is higher among younger age groups [64, 65]. These differences between age groups may be related to varied ways of accessing information sources, frequency of social media use, educational levels, and cultural attitudes. Gharacheh et al. [50] reported that young women could access health information more easily through social media and that this might be associated with their level of awareness regarding menstrual hygiene products. Gesese et al. [66] identified social media use as one of the main determinants of menstrual cup awareness. Modern healthcare solutions, especially innovative and sustainable products such as menstrual cups, are more readily embraced by the younger population through social media and digital campaigns. Therefore, the relationship between age and menstrual product awareness may vary not only with age but also with social norms and individuals’ access to information channels.

Our study found that for each unit increase in the scores of menstrual discomfort and tendency to conceal menstruation, menstrual cup awareness increased by 9%. Given that higher scores on this subscale indicate a decrease in menstrual discomfort and concealment tendency, women who experienced less menstrual discomfort and had a lower tendency to conceal menstruation had increased awareness about menstrual cups. There are a limited number of studies on this topic. In their study conducted with college students, Strait and Yagnik (2025) [67] found that individuals who used menstrual cups had significantly more positive attitudes toward their menstrual cycle compared to those who did not use the product. Furthermore, Davile et al. [2] found that barriers and facilitators related to the menstrual period affected the menstrual cup use. It is thought that with the reduction of negative perceptions and stigmatizing attitudes toward menstruation, menstruation can be addressed more openly, which in turn can support access to information about sustainable menstrual products.

Our findings indicated that each unit increase in awareness of abnormal findings increased the likelihood of menstrual cup awareness by approximately 12%. This finding suggests that women with higher awareness of abnormal findings are likely to be more open to seeking information about menstrual health and more inclined to learn about alternative menstrual products. The literature has shown that individuals with a high level of literacy regarding genital health and hygiene are more likely to be aware of reusable products, such as menstrual cups [51]. In this context, a recent study indicated that educational programmes designed to promote genital hygiene behaviours might also enhance awareness of abnormal findings [68]. Increased awareness of abnormal findings may enhance women’s tendency to monitor their own bodies, question signs, and participate more actively in health-related decision-making processes that are important for public health. This increases health-seeking behavior related to genital health and may, in the long term, promote the use of low-cost, environmentally sustainable products.

Strengths and limitations

One of the most notable strengths of the current study is its inclusion of a broad group of participants from different regions of Turkey. This diversity allowed for the assessment of menstrual cup awareness and menstrual hygiene attitudes within a culturally varied context. However, since no measurement tool with proven validity and reliability was identified for menstrual cup awareness, it was measured using a single yes/no question, which captures only participants’ knowledge of the product’s existence rather than comprehensive awareness. In addition, the study identified the reasons why women from a specific cultural background do not use menstrual cups, which are products that support environmental sustainability. To facilitate access to the study group, which is hard to reach, the snowball sampling method was used. While this method is effective for collecting data from specific populations, it has certain limitations, such as underrepresentation and potential bias in reflecting the broader population. As participants may share similar educational backgrounds, experiences, or perspectives due to being members of the same social networks, we acknowledge the selection bias inherent in snowball sampling. Therefore, to enhance the representativeness of the sample, participants were recruited from all regions of Turkey rather than from a single region.

However, as the study is cross-sectional, it reflects the situation at a single point in time and does not examine changes or developments over time. This limits the ability to establish precise causal relationships between variables. Although the sample included participants from all regions of Turkey, individuals living in areas without internet access could not be represented. Collecting data through an online questionnaire might have limited the participation of individuals without access to digital tools or the internet, potentially excluding women with lower socioeconomic status and reducing the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the fact that most participants had a bachelor’s degree might have also limited generalizability. Studies to be conducted with individuals with lower levels of education may provide more representative and generalizable results.

Conclusion and recommendations

The current study found that although menstrual cup awareness was high among women, the product use rates were low. This significant difference indicates that menstrual cup awareness does not always translate into actual use. In this context, the level of awareness was found to be associated with certain sociodemographic characteristics.

It was determined that the participants who had heard of menstrual cups were younger, single, and had a higher level of education. However, menstrual cup awareness was determined to decrease with age. Higher scores on the subscale of menstrual discomfort and tendency to conceal indicated lower discomfort and concealment tendency. Accordingly, our study found that women with lower menstrual discomfort and concealment tendency had higher menstrual cup awareness. On the other hand, it was determined that as participants’ awareness of abnormal findings increased, their menstrual cup awareness also increased. The voluntary nature of participation and the high educational level of the sample limited the generalizability of the findings to all women in Turkey. Therefore, the findings apply to groups of women with characteristics similar to those of our participants. In future studies, conducting research with more heterogeneous samples that include different age groups, educational levels, and both urban and rural settlement characteristics may increase the generalizability of the results. In light of these findings, nurses, midwives, and professionals working in women’s health may enhance the acceptability and applicability of menstrual cups by providing accurate information about menstrual cups and alternative menstrual hygiene products as part of counseling services and education programs. However, in order to thoroughly address menstrual cup awareness, it is important to develop measurement tools with proven validity and reliability. In addition, it is recommended that experimental studies based on theoretical frameworks such as the Health Belief Model be planned to address perceived barriers and beliefs related to menstrual cups among groups of women with varying sociocultural and educational levels. Furthermore, future studies should be designed to more comprehensively examine the individual, cultural, and structural factors influencing menstrual cup use.
