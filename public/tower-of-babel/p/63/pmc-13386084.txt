Machine learning evaluation of clinical, social and behavioural factors influencing progression from pre-diabetes to type 2 diabetes: a retrospective cohort study in southeast Michigan
Authors: Ewelina Niedzialkowska, Sujoy Roy, Mary Townsend, Kevin E Heinrich, Berk Celik, Ramin Homayouni, Alexandra Halalau
Published: 2026

Introduction

Pre-diabetes, a precursor to type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) characterised by hyperglycaemia, affects 97.6 million (38%) of adults aged 18 years or older in the adult US population.1 On the other hand, type 2 DM affects approximately 38.1 million (14.7%) adults in the USA.1 In 2021, type 2 DM was ranked as the eighth leading cause of death nationwide.1 Longitudinal studies indicate that approximately 5–10% of individuals with pre-diabetes progress to type 2 DM annually, highlighting the significant public health concern.2 Recent studies also indicate that patients with pre-diabetes have a higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular events.3 Normalisation of haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) from the pre-diabetic range is associated with fewer cardiovascular events, whereas patients who progress to diabetes represent a particularly high-risk group.3

Medical care for patients diagnosed with type 2 DM accounts for one in four healthcare dollars in the USA, 61% of which are attributable to the disease itself.4 Given the substantial healthcare costs associated with type 2 DM management, prioritising the prevention of rapid progression from pre-diabetes to type 2 DM through individualised risk assessment is essential.

Machine learning (ML) algorithms have emerged as powerful tools in pre-diabetes research, enabling identification of key risk factors for progression to type 2 DM. In recent years, several studies identified traditional variables such as demographic characteristics, haematological biomarkers and physical measures but only one study has thus far incorporated social determinants of health such as insurance status and education and none have incorporated behavioural factors into risk prediction models. Others have identified insurance status as a key risk factor, finding that older adults covered by Medicare had a lower likelihood of progressing to type 2 DM, whereas younger individuals without Medicare were at increased risk.4 Therefore, we aimed to use ML models to evaluate the influence of specific clinical and social and behavioural determinants of health (SBDH) factors on progression from pre-diabetes to type 2 DM within 1 year.

Research design and methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients with pre-diabetes receiving care between 1 January 2008 and 31 December 2023 at Corewell Health system, which is a large multicentre health system in southeast Michigan, comprising eight geographically and demographically diverse hospitals and over 100 outpatient clinics. The cohort inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in figure 1. Eligible individuals met the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) HbA1c values between 5.7 and 6.4 for two consecutive years; (3) no prior diagnosis of type 2 DM and no history of metformin use; (4) body mass index (BMI) measurement available at baseline year; (5) HbA1c available in the following year (Y1). The study was reviewed and approved by our institutional review board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived because the analysis used retrospective, de-identified electronic health record data and posed minimal risk to participants.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was incident type 2 DM, defined as progression to HbA1c ≥6.5% during the year following the baseline, consistent with type 2 DM diagnostic criteria from the American Diabetes Association.5

Covariates

A total of 76 covariates were included in the predictive modelling analysis, selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature and data availability. A detailed description of these covariates is provided in online supplemental table S1. Covariates were derived from structured data fields (including problem lists, medical history, billing diagnoses, medications and routinely collected laboratory panels) during clinical encounters. Laboratory reference ranges were based on Corewell Health system standards. A total of 17 different SBDH factors were captured from both structured sources (International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) Z-codes and screening questionnaires) and unstructured clinical notes using a novel natural language processing (NLP) algorithm.6 7 Notably, depression was included as a covariate from two distinct sources. One source included any ICD-10 code listed in the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services chronic conditions warehouse. The ‘depression chronic condition’ covariate was solely based on ICD-10 codes from the electronic medical record (EMR) recorded any time prior to the index year. In contrast, the ‘depression-SBDH’ covariate was restricted to a 1 year look back period from the index year and incorporated multiple sources, including ICD-10 codes, screening questionnaire data and NLP-derived signals from clinical notes.

All categorical variables were encoded as numerical values based on alphabetical order (ordinal encoding). For example, the binary gender variable was transformed such that ‘Female’ was coded as 0 and ‘Male’ as 1. No imputation was performed for missing values. Records with missing data were retained as-is to represent ‘real-world’ scenarios and to avoid introducing bias through imputation methods. Additionally, no feature selection was conducted prior to model training to allow the learning algorithm to evaluate the full set of potentially relevant covariates without introducing selection bias.

Model training and testing

Five tree-based ML algorithms were evaluated: Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), CatBoost, Decision Tree and Random Forest. A detailed description of the various ML approaches is provided in online supplemental materials. These methods were chosen for their inherent ability to handle sparse and missing data as well as non-linear interactions.8 9 Optimal hyperparameters for each algorithm were determined via grid search, using cross-validated performance as the selection criterion.10 Calibration curves were used to compare predicted probabilities against observed outcomes across probability bins, relative to a perfectly calibrated reference line (online supplemental figure S1).11 Additionally, Brier scores were calculated to quantify the accuracy of probabilistic predictions (online supplemental figure S2).12

Model training and evaluation were conducted using stratified 10-fold cross-validation to ensure balanced outcome distribution across folds.13 To address class imbalance and ensure equal representation of the outcome classes, we performed random undersampling of the majority class (those who remained in pre-diabetes range) to match the number of cases in the minority class (those who progressed to type 2 DM during the following year). In addition, alternative class-balancing approaches were explored such as the in-built balanced class weighting options available for each ML model. Model performance was assessed using standard classification metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Lastly, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework was used to evaluate the relative importance and directionality of model features for the best-performing algorithm, providing interpretable insights into key drivers of type 2 diabetes progression.14 Additional methodological details are provided in the online supplemental materials.

Because this study used a real-world EMR dataset, a traditional a priori sample size calculation was not performed. Instead, we assessed data adequacy using multiple approaches. The cohort included a large and diverse patient population with sufficient outcome events relative to the number of candidate predictors. Model performance demonstrated stability across repeated k-fold cross-validation with minimal variability in discrimination metrics.

Patient and public involvement

There was no involvement from patients or public in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans for this research study.

Results

Population characteristics

From the initial cohort of 67 809 individuals who had two consecutive years of HbA1c values between 5.7 and 6.4 (figure 1). 11 809 individuals met the inclusion criteria, from which 815 individuals (6.9%) progressed to type 2 diabetes (HbA1c ≥6.5%) within 1 year (table 1). There were no significant differences in age, race or insurance type between those who progressed to type 2 diabetes and those who remained in the pre-diabetes range. However, a significantly lower proportion of females progressed to type 2 diabetes compared with those who did not (50.1% vs 57.8%, p<0.001).

Table 1 summarises the clinical characteristics and chronic conditions that differed significantly between those who transitioned to type 2 diabetes and those who did not. Altogether, we analysed demographic characteristics, 12 clinical variables, 27 chronic conditions and 17 SBDH extracted from the electronic medical record. Notably, a screening form was documented in the electronic health record for 69.2% of the study cohort. Among SBDH, nicotine dependence (18.4%) and depression (8.6%) were the most prevalent followed by inadequate physical activity (4.0%), alcohol abuse (1.8%) and stress (1.2%) (online supplemental table S2). The prevalence of all other SBDH factors was below 1.0%. None of the SBDH factors were significantly different between individuals who remained prediabetic compared with those who progressed to type 2 DM within a year (online supplemental table S2).

At baseline, individuals who progressed to type 2 diabetes had significantly higher mean HbA1c (within the pre-diabetes range), BMI, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine, triglycerides and white blood cell (WBC) count, as well as significantly lower high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels (table 1). Additionally, those who developed type 2 diabetes had a higher prevalence of chronic conditions, including acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, heart failure (HF) and hypertension. In contrast, hypothyroidism and osteoporosis were significantly less common among individuals who progressed to type 2 diabetes.

Machine learning

A variety of ML approaches (online supplemental methods) were evaluated for their ability to predict incident type 2 diabetes within 1 year (Y1) using demographic, clinical, social and behavioural data collected at baseline. Among the models assessed, CatBoost demonstrated superior calibration performance and Brier scores (lower is better) compared with other methods (online supplemental figures S1 and S2). It also achieved the highest performance (mean AUC 0.78) across 10-fold cross-validation iterations (figure 2). With respect to class imbalance, we observed no significant differences in model performance between the built-in balanced class weighting approach and random undersampling of the majority class, as measured by AUC (figure 2). Consequently, all subsequent analyses were performed using the balanced undersampling approach. We also evaluated the incremental contribution of SBDH variables and found that their inclusion did not significantly improve model discrimination compared with models containing only clinical and demographic variables (figure 2). Finally, subgroup analyses stratified by race, sex and age demonstrated comparable mean AUCs across demographic groups (online supplemental figure S3), suggesting consistent model performance across these populations. However, the Asian subgroup exhibited greater variability in AUC estimates across cross-validation iterations.

We next used SHAP analysis to examine the relative importance of variables across 10-fold cross-validation sets (figure 3A). The most influential predictor of diabetes progression was the prior-year (baseline) HbA1c, with a mean SHAP value of 1.02 (0.06 95% CI). Other key predictors, with SHAP importance values (mean (95% CI)) in decreasing order were low abnormal HDL cholesterol (0.17 (0.02)), BMI (0.17 (0.02)), high abnormal triglycerides (0.12 (0.01)), high abnormal white blood cell count (0.12 (0.01)), age (0.11 (0.01)) and gender (0.09 (0.01)). Additional laboratory measures and a history of certain chronic conditions were also predictive, although with lower average importance scores. Only depression SBDH was consistently among the top 25 features with an average SHAP importance value of 0.02 (figure 3A).

For exploratory analysis, we focused on the results of one set with the AUC of 0.78 (median ranked) (online supplemental figure S4). With respect to categorical variables, low HDL, high WBC, high triglycerides, high ALT, male gender, HF and depression were positively predictive of diabetes progression (online supplemental figure S4A). On the other hand, high low-density lipoprotein (LDL), ischaemic heart disease, low thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and TSH testing were negatively predictive of diabetes progression. For continuous variables, we found that the influence of baseline HbA1c and BMI were linearly correlated with prediction of 1-year progression to type 2 diabetes measured by HbA1c (online supplemental figure S4B and C). However, predictive influence of age was negatively correlated with progression to type 2 DM as measured by HbA1c (online supplemental figure S4D). Further analysis revealed that the predictive SHAP value of age was non-linear across all 10-fold cross-validation sets (figure 3B). Between ages 40 and 50, baseline age was positively predictive of transitioning from pre-diabetes to type 2 DM in year 1. However, between age 50 and 70, baseline age was negatively predictive. Beyond age 70, the SHAP values were highly variable.

Although depression SBDH was individually not highly influential on prediction of diabetes progression, it showed strong interactions with mean (standard deviation) interaction scores of 4.54 (1.38) and 4.03 (1.21) for baseline HbA1c and BMI, respectively (online supplemental figure S5). Figure 3C, D show the predictive value of depression across 10-fold cross-validation sets while overlaying the average HbA1c and BMI, respectively. The average HbA1c is lower and BMI is higher among individuals with depression compared with those without depression.

Discussion

Although multiple SBDH factors were examined in our study, only depression emerged as a significant predictor of transitioning to type 2 DM. Depression was notably prevalent in our study cohort, affecting 8.6% of individuals. Numerous studies have shown a strong association between depression and increased risk of progression from pre-diabetes to type 2 DM.15 16 Biologically, depression is linked to dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, resulting in elevated cortisol levels, leading to hyperglycaemia. Depression is also associated with poor lifestyle behaviours, including reduced physical activity, unhealthy diet and medication non-adherence, which further increase metabolic risk.17 The Lifelines Cohort Study found that individuals with both pre-diabetes and depressive symptoms had the highest risk of developing type 2 DM, compared with those with pre-diabetes alone.15 Wang et al showed that depressive symptoms in pre-diabetes patients were independent risk factors for progression to type 2 DM, with higher depression severity conferring greater risk.16

Notably, we found an interaction between depression and BMI in the ML model. Depression was a positive predictor of diabetes progression and individuals with depression at baseline had higher average BMI (figure 3D). Our results are consistent with the heterogeneous concept of depressive disorders with potential metabolic subtypes.18 19 According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, atypical depression is characterised by mood reactivity and symptoms such as increased appetite, weight gain, hypersomnia, leaden paralysis or rejection sensitivity. In contrast, melancholic depression involves loss of pleasure or mood reactivity and is often characterised by reduced appetite, weight loss, early morning awakening, psychomotor changes, excessive guilt and worse morning symptoms.20 Previous studies have shown that adults with depression-related weight gain had an increased risk of type 2 diabetes compared with those with no depression, whereas adults with depression and symptoms of weight loss were not at increased risk of type 2 diabetes.21

Among the five tree-based ML algorithms tested in our study, CatBoost yielded the best predictive performance, which achieved a mean AUC of 0.78, which is comparable to previously reported ML models. ML models trained on an Israeli cohort achieved AUCs of 0.753 for prediction of type 2 DM transition within 1–5 years horizons.22 Key predictors included glucose, HbA1c, HDL and age. Interestingly, BMI was only predictive at years 2–5. They also reported a simplified model using eight variables performed comparably to the full model with 84 features.22 Another ML model developed using an Israeli cohort identified BMI, HbA1c, glucose, triglycerides, WBC and HDL as the most significant predictors with reported AUC of 0.865.22 Liu et al studied a Chinese cohort aged ≥65 using multiple algorithms, with XGBoost achieving the best performance.23 fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides, waist circumference (WC), BMI and ALT were key predictors with WC being more predictive than BMI.24 Aoki et al applied a random forest survival model to a large US cohort identifying HbA1c, glucose, HDL (initial and slope), age and gender as top predictors.25 HDL contributed ~20% to model performance, suggesting a possible risk association despite its traditional protective role. Although this is a comprehensive model in a US population, SBDH were not included.25 Console et al used data from Canadian and Italian populations to develop a multilabel classifier for glycaemic states, identifying fasting blood sugar and HbA1c as the most important features.26 Zhang et al modelled 5-year progression and identified FPG, HDL, ALT/ aspartate aminotransferase, BMI, age and monocyte count as top predictors in the Chinese population.25

Consistent with prior studies, our analysis showed that HbA1c was the strongest predictor of progression to type 2 diabetes from pre-diabetes.22 23 25 27 Other laboratory values including low HDL, high triglycerides, high total cholesterol, elevated LDL, high ALT, elevated WBC count and low TSH were also strongly predictive of type 2 DM progression (online supplemental figure S4A), as previously reported by others.23 27 In addition, higher BMI and male gender predicted type 2 DM progression, consistent with prior studies.24 28 29 Male gender was associated with increased risk of progression, aligning with findings reported in the current literature.30 31 Regarding chronic conditions, HF, hypertension and depression emerged as positive predictors of type 2 DM progression, whereas ischaemic heart disease was a negative predictor (online supplemental figure S4A). HF has been independently linked to increased type 2 DM incidence,32 while hypertension and hyperlipidaemia have also shown significant correlations.30

Although several studies have identified age as a strong predictor of progression from pre-diabetes to diabetes, our findings provide additional insights into the complex relationship between age and diabetes onset.22 25 26 We observed that age was positively predictive of diabetes progression (defined as HbA1c ≥6.5%) between approximately 40 and 50 years of age, after which its predictive effect became negative (figure 3B). Furthermore, the contribution of age to model predictions became increasingly variable across the 10-fold cross-validation sets beyond age 70, suggesting greater uncertainty in its predictive value among older adults. One potential explanation is that HbA1c levels are known to increase with age even among otherwise healthy individuals, raising concerns about the accuracy of HbA1c as a diagnostic marker of diabetes in older populations.33 Consistent with this interpretation, previous ML studies have reported that age was not a significant predictor of incident diabetes, defined by fasting plasma glucose >7.0 mmol/L, among individuals aged 65 years and older.23 Collectively, these findings suggest that the relationship between age and diabetes risk may be nonlinear and influenced by other age-related factors.

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) has transformed the management of diabetes by providing detailed, real-time insights into glycaemic patterns. However, its application for predicting progression to diabetes is only beginning to emerge. Recent studies have shown that CGM-derived metrics, particularly measures of hyperglycaemia such as time above range, can identify individuals at increased risk of future dysglycaemia across diverse clinical settings. For example, CGM metrics have demonstrated strong predictive performance for gestational DM in pregnant women34 35 and post-transplant DM among kidney transplant recipients.36 In addition, ML approaches incorporating CGM data have enhanced risk stratification for early type 1 diabetes progression and identified metabolic subphenotypes characterised by insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction before the onset of overt diabetes.37 38 Consistent with these observations, an international consensus report has proposed CGM as a minimally invasive screening tool for early-stage (stage 2) type 1 diabetes.39 Collectively, these findings suggest that CGM captures subtle perturbations in glucose regulation that may provide prognostic information beyond traditional markers such as fasting glucose and HbA1c. Indeed, dynamic CGM-derived markers that quantify the temporal variability and complexity of glucose fluctuations have been shown to discriminate diabetes status and progression, and may offer an alternative to fasting plasma glucose or oral glucose tolerance testing for identifying high-risk individuals.40 Future efforts to develop robust and generalisable models for predicting progression from pre-diabetes to type 2 diabetes may benefit substantially from the integration of CGM-derived measures.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it leverages a large, demographically diverse US cohort, enhancing the generalisability of its findings. Second, the predictive model uniquely integrates both traditional clinical risk factors and SBDH, which are often excluded from existing models. The use of the CatBoost algorithm provided strong predictive performance, and revealed unique interactions between depression and baseline HbA1c or BMI in predicting progression to type 2 DM.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. As a retrospective analysis, the study relies on the accuracy and completeness of the EMR. Key lifestyle factors known to influence type 2 diabetes risk such as diet, physical activity and medication adherence were often under-reported and could not be robustly incorporated. Although this study leveraged a large, multicentre health system dataset, the findings may not be fully generalisable to other healthcare settings. Additionally, the model was not externally validated, which may limit its broader applicability. It is important to note however, that the primary objective of this study was not to develop a clinically deployable or generalisable prediction model that would require external validation. Rather, our goal was to leverage ML methods to identify and explore social and behavioural factors that may contribute to progression from pre-diabetes to type 2 diabetes. The inclusion criteria requiring at least two consecutive years of HbA1c measurements likely biased the sample for individuals with consistent healthcare access, potentially limiting generalisability to underserved populations. Although the high SBDH screening completion rate strengthens data reliability, this may further bias findings toward patients who are more engaged with the healthcare system.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply ML approaches to evaluate the contribution of SBDH to progression from pre-diabetes to type 2 diabetes. Although the inclusion of SBDH variables did not substantially improve overall model performance, likely due to their low prevalence, our findings provide several important insights. In particular, ML identified depression as a potentially meaningful contributor to diabetes progression, especially among individuals with higher BMI and lower baseline HbA1c levels. However, because the model was not externally validated, these findings should be regarded as exploratory and warrant confirmation in independent external cohorts before informing clinical practice. Subject to such validation, comprehensive diabetes prevention strategies may benefit from incorporating routine behavioural health screening and targeted interventions for depression.
