Loss of long-range co-expression is a common feature in cancer
Authors: Diana García-Cortés, Enrique Hernández-Lemus, Jesús Espinal-Enríquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer cells display common features and enabling characteristics collectively known as the Hallmarks of Cancer, which occur alongside alterations in the regulatory mechanisms controlling gene transcription. Gene co-expression networks (GCNs) provide a framework to identify correlated gene sets that may share these regulatory mechanisms. Previously, we reported the loss of long-range co-expression in breast, lung, kidney, and hematopoietic cancer GCNs. Here, we expand the analysis to fifteen tissues, comprising 8772 samples from two independent datasets. Unlike healthy phenotypes, cancer GCNs show that the strongest gene-pair interactions are intra-chromosomal, with their strength decaying as base-pair distance increases. Tumor GCN communities are strongly associated with cancer-related processes and are enriched in gene families located on the same chromosome. In contrast, normal GCN communities are linked to metabolic and cell maintenance processes. Riboproteins remain highly co-expressed in both cancer and normal GCNs, highlighting their importance for cell viability. Notably, in other chronic diseases the loss of long-range co-expression is absent, suggesting it is a distinctive feature of cancer.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Oncology

Introduction

The Hallmarks of Cancer1, a conceptual framework that has evolved over the past two decades, describe the distinctive characteristics that cancer cells acquire as they progress toward tumorigenesis2–4. Enabling characteristics, such as genome instability, the tumor microenvironment, and epigenetic alterations facilitate the acquisition of hallmark capabilities5,6. Furthermore, alterations in multiple regulatory mechanisms drive the manifestation of cancer hallmarks. In gene transcription, these mechanisms include cis-regulatory elements, specialized proteins (e.g., transcription factors), and epigenetic markers, all operating within a specific chromatin configuration7–9.

Gene co-expression network (GCN) analysis has been extensively applied to cancer transcriptomic data to identify biologically coherent gene modules, tissue-specific regulatory programs, and clinically relevant biomarkers10–12. They enable comparisons of global and local connectivity patterns across different phenotypes, as well as their functional implications13–15. Early large-scale studies using TCGA data and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) demonstrated that co-expression modules are strongly associated with biological processes and tumor phenotypes, and that hub genes within these modules can exhibit prognostic value across multiple cancer types14,16. Subsequent work has applied similar frameworks to specific malignancies or molecular subtypes, revealing associations with immune infiltration, tumor progression, and patient outcomes17. More recently, single-sample network approaches have been introduced to capture inter-patient heterogeneity and to enable patient-level stratification based on network-derived features18–21.

While these studies have firmly established the utility of GCNs for understanding cancer biology, most have focused on module detection, hub identification, or clinical association within individual cancer types or subtypes. In contrast, relatively little attention has been paid to the genome-wide spatial organization of co-expression, particularly to the distinction between intra- and inter-chromosomal interactions and the dependence of co-expression strength on genomic distance.

By analyzing gene co-expression profiles and building GCNs using RNA-Seq data in cancer tissue and its normal counterpart, we have previously described a loss in the proportion of inter-chromosomal gene co-expression interactions in breast cancer and breast cancer molecular subtypes22–25, lung cancer26, clear cell renal carcinoma27, and hematopoietic cancers28. There, the majority of significant co-expression links connect gene pairs in the same chromosome and these display a highly localized co-expression pattern. Conversely, their normal tissue-derived networks, present a higher number of inter-chromosomal interactions.

Local high co-expression is a characteristic that has been reported for normal tissues, both in bulk RNA-Seq29 and single cell analysis30, reinforcing the conclusion that gene order in eukaryotes is not random31,32. However, this phenomenon has been observed in gene pairs within a local vicinity of less than 1Mb33,34. Our previous results indicate that in cancer, this high co-expression expands to longer distances and its prevalence throughout different tissues suggests it may be a common characteristic of the disease. These results, in both cancer and normal phenotypes, highlight the need to extend the analysis of gene co-expression and the mechanisms that jointly drive the transcription of multiple sets of genes.

In this work, we simultaneously evaluated the loss of long-range co-expression in fifteen tissues (8,772 samples) by integrating data from distinct data sources: TCGA35 and UCSC Xena36 (which includes samples from the GTEx and TCGA collaborations37). This is in order to determine whether (i) the loss of inter-chromosomal co-expression is a common feature in cancer, (ii) there is a dependency between high co-expression and physical distance between genes located in the same chromosome, and (iii) whether these phenomena are exclusively found in cancer. To do this, we analyzed gene co-expression profiles and GCNs from said samples, compared their topological properties through network community extraction, and identified biologically associated communities. The present study aims to complement existing GCN literature by systematically characterizing the large-scale topological reorganization of co-expression networks across multiple cancer types, with a specific focus on long-range transcriptional coordination. Our analysis allowed us to confirm that loss of long-range co-expression is indeed a common and distinctive feature in cancer.

Results

In cancer, highly co-expressed gene pairs are intra-chromosomal

Gene expression matrices were downloaded from Genomic Data Commons (TCGA) and UCSC Xena. Normalization and removal of low-expressed genes were performed as described in the Methods section. Table 1 displays the number of samples and expressed genes in each expression matrix. We calculated Mutual Information (MI) between pairs of genes as a measure of co-expression for each tissue and sample type independently. MI is a measure of statistical dependency from information theory that quantifies the amount of information one random variable contains about another. A high mutual information value indicates a strong statistical dependency or shared information between the two variables, while a low MI suggests independence or little to no relationship between them. The number of gene pairs analyzed varied by tissue, ranging from 49.07 million in skin to 108.86 million in lung, due to the number of genes in each expression matrix.

Gene pairs were classified based on their chromosomal location. If both genes were located on the same chromosome, the pair was considered an intra-chromosomal co-expression interaction. If the genes were located on different chromosomes, the pair was classified as an inter-chromosomal interaction. On average, gene pair datasets contain 5.33% intra-chromosomal interactions. In normal tissues, this percentage remains stable across different thresholds of top MI values, ranked in descending order. However, in cancer tissues, higher fractions of intra-chromosomal interactions appear among the top MI values. Figure 1 presents a heatmap showing the fraction of intra-chromosomal pairs for various thresholds of top MI values in both cancer and normal tissues. For bladder samples, these values are also depicted in a line plot, while Supplementary Fig. 1, provides line plots for the remaining tissues.

Heatmap showing the fractions of intra-chromosomal interactions at different top mutual information (MI) thresholds for cancer and normal tissues across all fifteen datasets.

In normal co-expression profiles, this fraction remains stable across thresholds, whereas in cancer, the top MI interactions are predominantly intra-chromosomal. For bladder, the fractions are also displayed as a line plot, with the remaining tissues shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

To evaluate whether the number of samples influenced the high fraction of intra-chromosomal pairs observed in cancer profiles, we performed a bootstrap-based analysis on tissues with the lowest number of normal samples: bladder, uterus, and thyroid. Due to the computational demands of the algorithm, we performed 20 bootstraps using MI with random sampling of the corresponding number of normal samples per tissue: 19, 35, and 56. Additionally, we calculated Spearman correlation for 100 bootstraps, sampling 25, 50, and 100 samples per tissue. The results of the bootstrap analysis are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. While the fraction of intra-chromosomal interactions increases with sample size, cancer profiles consistently exhibit high fractions of intra-chromosomal pairs among top MI values, even with substantially reduced sample sizes. These findings indicate that the observed phenomenon is not an artifact of unequal cohort sizes.

Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were applied to the intra-chromosomal fractions in cancer and normal tissues, comparing values from the 1000-bin and up to 50 million interactions, with logarithmic increments in the starting bin. These tests reveal significant differences in the distributions, with p-values ranging from 1e-16 to 0.005 (Supplementary Table 1) when considering the top 1 million interactions. This finding suggests that in all analyzed cancer types, the strongest co-expressed gene pairs are located on the same chromosome, whereas normal tissues do not show a preference for high co-expression among intra-chromosomal interactions.

Distance dependent MI decay is a common feature in cancer

After establishing that the strongest co-expression interactions in cancer occur between genes on the same chromosome, we investigated whether correlation values depend on the chromosomal distance between these intra-chromosomal genes. We assessed intra-chromosomal co-expression by sorting gene pair interactions based on their base pair distance. Interactions from all chromosomes were included to provide a global evaluation of intra-chromosomal co-expression patterns. Figure 2 upper panel displays the average MI values for 1000-interaction bins, with corresponding standard deviations plotted against distance, in colon tissue. Supplementary Fig. 3 provides similar plots for all fifteen tissues in the study.

Average Mutual Information (MI) and standard deviation for bins of 1000 intra-chromosomal interactions plotted against average gene-gene base pair distance in colon tissue.

The lower panel shows p-values from Wilcoxon rank sum tests comparing MI distributions between bins. In normal tissue, no significant differences are observed between bins, while in cancer, distant bins differ significantly, but neighboring bins show similar values. Supplementary Fig. 3 includes similar plots for the other tissues.

In cancer, gene pairs separated by shorter distances, defined as less than 10Mb, exhibit higher MI values, and as the distance increases, co-expression decays to a plateau. In contrast, normal intra-chromosomal co-expression profiles display this pattern only for genes in very close proximity. To assess these differences, we performed Wilcoxon rank sum tests to compare the distribution of MI values in each bin against all other bins of intra-chromosomal pairs. P-values, shown for colon cancer in the lower panel of Fig. 2 and for the full cohort in Supplementary Fig. 3, indicate that in cancer, neighboring bins are similar, while distant bins differ significantly. Conversely, normal-derived bin distributions do not show significant differences related to base pair distance. Thus, not only do the strongest co-expressed genes reside on the same chromosome but are also located in close proximity, within a distance of less than 10Mb.

Distinct intra-chromosomal interaction patterns in cancer co-expression networks

We used the top 100,000 MI pairs from normal and cancer tissues to construct gene co-expression networks (GCNs). In these networks, nodes represent genes, and selected gene pairs are connected by edges, also referred to as links. Each link is associated with its corresponding MI value. Table 2 presents their main properties, while Fig. 3 provides a visual representation of networks for selected tissues. Supplementary Fig. 4 includes networks for all tissues. As previously noted, at this cutoff, most interactions in cancer networks involve gene pairs on the same chromosome, whereas normal networks resemble what we observed earlier in breast and lung cancer: a giant component primarily composed of inter-chromosomal links22,24,26.

Given the known impact of microsatellite instability (MSI) on genome integrity and transcriptional regulation in specific cancer types, we evaluated whether the high presence of intra-chromosomal links was associated with high MSI. We used published estimates of MSI prevalence across TCGA cohorts derived from exome-sequencing data, where samples were classified as MSI-stable or MSI-high38. We calculated the Pearson correlation between the fraction of intra-chromosomal links and the fraction of MSI-high tumors per cancer type. The analysis revealed a small positive but not significant correlation (ρ = 0.282, p = 0.374), primarily driven by the high fraction of MSI-high tumors in uterus (0.297) and colon (0.19) cancers. While MSI may contribute to transcriptional alterations in specific cancer contexts, it is unlikely to be a major driver of the high intra-chromosomal fractions observed in cancer networks.

The community structure of co-expression networks is associated with biological processes

The force-directed layout applied to the co-expression networks for visualization (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4) suggests a modular structure influenced by chromosomal gene location, particularly in cancer GCNs with a higher fraction of intra-chromosomal links. To explore this further, we performed a community structure analysis using the Louvain algorithm (ref. 41). This algorithm has been shown to effectively identify biologically relevant network structures42,43. Table 2 lists the number of communities identified in each network, as well as their modularity values, which measure how strongly a network is divided into distinct groups. Networks from tissues with higher fractions of intra-chromosomal interactions tend to exhibit higher modularity, consistent with the visual impression from the force-directed layouts.

We also calculated the PageRank centrality value for each node in the network to assess its importance within the network. Communities were named after the gene with the highest PageRank value. To evaluate whether transcription factor activity is associated with the formation of these communities, we compared the PageRank values of transcription factors and non-transcription factors in cancer and normal networks. We found that PageRank values were significantly higher for transcription factors in brain, ovary, and thyroid cancer networks, while significantly lower in six of the nine networks with high intra-chromosomal link fractions: bladder, breast, colon, kidney, liver, and lung. The distribution of these values for normal and cancer networks is presented in Supplementary Fig. 5. These results suggest that transcription factor activity, a regulatory mechanism typically involving inter-chromosomal interactions, does not drive the community structure observed in these co-expression networks, where the majority of links are intra-chromosomal.

For each community, we calculated its chromosomal assortativity, a nominal assortativity to quantify the community’s bias towards a single chromosome. The distributions of chromosomal assortativities for cancer and normal co-expression networks are presented as violin plots in Fig. 7. These plots show that network communities in cancer tend to have values close to 1, indicating that genes in a community mostly belong to a single chromosome. In contrast, normal GCNs exhibit an opposite trend, with chromosomal assortativity values clustering towards −1 in tissues such as bladder, breast, liver, ovary, prostate, thyroid, and uterus, and values distributed between −1 and 1 in other tissues. This distribution suggests either a link bias towards different chromosomes or mixed trends, respectively.

Chromosomal assortativity in network communities of all fifteen tissues analyzed.

Normal tissues tend towards values near −1, indicating communities are primarily composed of inter-chromosomal links. In contrast, cancer tissues show assortativity values closer to 1, reflecting communities dominated by intra-chromosomal interactions.

Shared GO processes in co-expression networks’ communities

We conducted Gene Ontology (GO) over-representation analyses to identify biological processes associated with each community in GCNs. As described in the Methods section, we only considered GO terms with padj < 1e−10. By linking communities from the fifteen networks to their enriched biological processes, we created a bipartite network for both normal and cancer phenotypes. We then selected processes with a high degree (D > 10) to identify the most common GO processes in each phenotype. The resulting networks are displayed in Figs. 8 and 9.

In normal networks (Fig. 8), common GO processes generate two connected components. The largest includes terms related to translation and protein localization, such as ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis and translational initiation, while the other component contains terms related to response to virus. We found no common GO processes in bladder and uterus enriched GO terms.

In cancer networks (Fig. 9), the bipartite subnetwork of common GO processes forms five connected components. The largest connected component (identified with the number 1) is related to immune response and includes the process most commonly found in cancer networks: adaptive immune response. It is enriched in twenty-one communities from all cancer types, except for skin cancer. Some of these communities include genes in chromosome 6, encoding almost all members from the major histocompatibility complex II, such as HLA-DP, HLA-DM, HLA-DOA, HLA-DQ, and HLA-DR. Other processes include positive regulation of T cell activation, lymphocyte proliferation, and inflammatory response.

We also find one component that mirrors the ribosome-associated processes in normal networks (number 2), with terms like translational initiation and establishment of protein localization to the endoplasmic reticulum, indicating high co-expression among ribosomal genes. A third module (3) includes twelve communities with HOXA and HOXB gene families, linked to processes like embryonic skeletal system morphogenesis. These communities are entirely intra-chromosomal, located on chromosomes 7 (HOXA) and 17 (HOXB).

The PCDHG and PCDHB protocadherin clusters form another component (4), associated with homophilic cell adhesion via plasma membrane adhesion molecules. This cluster includes communities from twelve cancer types, such as bladder, breast, kidney, ovary, and thyroid. Most communities are intra-chromosomal, with genes on chromosome 5 showing underexpression, particularly in bladder, breast, and uterus, as previously reported in Luminal A breast cancer44.

The last component (5) is associated with histone-related activities, featuring GO processes such as nucleosome assembly and chromatin organization involved in negative regulation of transcription, involving genes from the H2A, H2B, H3C, H4C, H1, and H4 families.

In normal network communities, we identified five sets. The largest, related to metabolism and cellular respiration, includes communities from eight tissues with processes like mitochondrial translation and regulation of lipid metabolic. The muscle development set also spans eight tissues, featuring processes such as cardiac cell development and myofibril assembly. Tissue-specific enriched processes

The normal- and cancer-only networks allowed us to identified GO terms uniquely associated with specific tissues, highlighting individual features of their co-expression networks and linking them to known tissue-specific biological processes. For example, the spermatogenesis set in the normal network includes a single community from testis, where the gene with the highest PageRank score is Boll (or BOULE), which is essential for sperm development45. This community is associated with processes like cilium movement, axoneme assembly, spermatogenesis, and single fertilization. Genes in this community are underexpressed in cancer samples (Supplementary Fig. 6). In the skin normal co-expression network, enriched terms in the MLANA community include melanin biosynthetic process, melanin metabolic process, and pigment biosynthetic process. The MLANA (Melan-A) gene, encodes a protein involved in melanosome biogenesis46. Genes in this community that are also present in the skin cancer network exhibit an overexpression trend.

Conversely, in the brain cancer GCN, the CCKBR community is associated with processes such as neurotransmitter secretion, synaptic signaling, and regulation of membrane potential. The 257 genes involved in the CCKBR community enrichments exhibit an underexpression trend, with an average log2 fold change of − 0.703. GO terms and differential expression of associated genes are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 6.

The loss of inter-chromosomal co-expression is not present in other diseases

To determine if the loss of inter-chromosomal co-expression is a phenomenon exclusively present in cancer, we analyzed four RNA-Seq datasets downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. GSE174367, a study of late-stage Alzheimer’s Disease (AD)47, with 44 AD samples and 46 control samples from brain tissue; GSE184050, a Type-2 Diabetes (T2D) study48 with 50 blood T2D samples and 66 blood control samples; GSE165303, a study on the development of human dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM)49, with 50 human heart samples with DCM and 51 non-failing biobanked heart tissue, and GSE192959, where authors derived a transcriptional signature for future hepatocellular carcinoma for patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)50, including 48 HCC-naive samples. We calculated mutual information between gene pairs using the same algorithm as for cancer datasets and extracted the top 100,000 interactions to construct co-expression networks.

In these diseases, the fraction of intra-chromosomal interactions in co-expression networks is similar for both normal and disease phenotypes, as shown in Fig. 12 and Supplementary Fig. 7. For AD, the intra-chromosomal fractions are 0.060 for normal and 0.0558 for disease networks. In T2D networks, 0.0733 and 0.0734; in DCM 0.069 for both networks, and 0.063 for the NAFLD network.

Co-expression networks from Alzheimer’s disease and type-2 diabetes composed by the top 100,000 MI interactions.

In contrast to cancer networks, these networks exhibit lower modularity values, indicating weaker community structure. The AD networks have modularity values of 0.469 for normal samples and 0.479 for cases, while the diabetes and the cardiomyopathy networks show modularity values closer to those observed in normal cancer tissues, with 0.575 for normal samples and 0.605 for cases in T2D, and 0.585, and 0.573 in DCM, respectively. The NAFLD network shows a 0.479 modularity.

Overrepresentation analysis reveals shared processes between normal and disease networks in both diabetes and Alzheimer’s disease. In diabetes, both networks include communities related to sensory perception (e.g., smell and taste), oxygen transport, and immune-related processes such as regulation of type I interferon production and response to viruses. Additionally, the normal diabetes network includes a community associated with ribosome activity and protein assembly, resembling shared processes in cancer networks. In Alzheimer’s disease, the cases network contains only three communities with significant GO process overrepresentation, associated with adaptive immune response, mRNA processing, and cytoplasmic translation. The normal Alzheimer’s network also includes a community related to cytoplasmic translation and another associated with multiple immune-related processes. We found no significantly enriched GO processes in the DCM and the NAFLD netwoks.

These findings suggest that the structure of these networks is less modular and less strongly associated with biological processes compared to cancer networks. Furthermore, none of these networks present chromosome associated gene interaction patterns, and for both phenotypes, normal and disease networks have similar structures, suggesting that the loss of inter-chromosomal co-expression is not observed in these chronic degenerative diseases.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized two cancer-associated phenomena: the loss of inter-chromosomal co-expression and the distance-dependent decay of gene co-expression for intra-chromosomal interactions. Co-expression profiles, calculated using Mutual Information (MI), revealed that the strongest correlation values occur between genes located on the same chromosome. Furthermore, by linking intra-chromosomal interactions to the physical distance between genes (measured in base pairs), we found that, in cancer, high correlation values are concentrated in close proximity, within a distance of 10Mb, with some tissues reaching a decay plateau around 50 Mb. In contrast, normal tissues (i.e., healthy adjacent tissue used as control in TCGA and samples tagged as Normal Tissue in GTEx-Xena) do not exhibit a bias towards intra- or inter-chromosomal interactions or any specific physical distance between gene pairs, except for very close distances, as illustrated in the left side of the distance plots for normal tissue profiles (Fig. 2). These results are robust to sample size variation, as corroborated by a bootstrap analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2), and are statistically significant, as demonstrated by Wilcoxon rank sum tests comparing MI distributions for bins of 1000 intra-chromosomal interactions in each tissue (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Previous studies have demonstrated high local gene co-expression in normal tissues for genes located near each other (within 1 Mb), regardless of strand or transcription orientation30,31,33,34, indicating that gene transcription is influenced by physical location. It is important to note that our MI calculations compare gene expression vectors independently, without being influenced by chromosomal position or phenotype. Inter-chromosomal gene regulation is often mediated by higher-order chromatin organization and epigenetic mechanisms, which are relevant for interpreting co-expression patterns. However, the assessment of various regulatory elements—such as enhancers, transcription factor binding sites, CTCF binding sites, and Hi-C contacts—performed by ref. 29 revealed that no single feature can fully explain the co-expression patterns observed in normal tissues; instead, these elements contribute complementarily.

Our results further suggest that neither chromatin architecture nor epigenetic regulation alone can account for the systematic loss of long-range co-expression observed across cancers. Comparisons between the genomic span of intra-chromosomal co-expression communities and topologically associating domain (TAD) sizes derived from Hi-C data in matched cancer types show partial correspondence but no one-to-one relationship22. In breast cancer Hi-C analyses, we observe a pronounced loss of statistically significant long-range chromatin contacts and a distance-dependent decay in contact frequency in tumors relative to non-cancer samples, mirroring the co-expression decay reported here51. Importantly, non-cancer Hi-C data also exhibit distance-dependent decay, albeit substantially weaker, suggesting that changes in 3D genome organization contribute to, but do not solely drive, co-expression network topology.

Additional epigenetic layers further support this interpretation. Analyses of microRNA-mediated regulation in breast and kidney cancers, as well as DNA methylation profiling in clear cell renal cell carcinoma, reveal that epigenetic alterations strongly affect the expression of specific genes and pathways, but do not explain the global reorganization of long-range co-expression52–54. Similarly, in Luminal A breast cancer, CTCF binding sites do not systematically flank highly co-expressed intra-chromosomal clusters, indicating that classical chromatin insulation mechanisms are not the primary determinants of these compact communities44. Furthermore, we found no association with copy number alterations or similar differential expression trends in both Luminal A and B subtypes55,56, and our analyses revealed no significant association between microsatellite instability (MSI) and the high fraction of intra-chromosomal interactions.

Although all fifteen cancer profiles show a loss of long-range co-expression, Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3 reveal differences in extent among tissues. Networks from bladder, lung, breast, ovary, uterus, liver, colon, kidney, and esophagus, exceed 83% of intra-chromosomal interactions, while brain, pancreas, testis, skin, prostate, and thyroid profiles show less pronounced differences, possibly reflecting tissue-specific cancer manifestations. Thyroid GCN shows the highest number of unique interactions and it lacks some common features from other networks, such as the presence of a community of HOX genes. According to GLOBOCAN, thyroid cancer has one of the lowest mortality rates57 and the most common subtypes, papillary and follicular, have a 5-years survival rate of almost 100%58,59. We have shown that in papillary and follicular thyroid cancer, relevant pathways are significantly less deregulated than in the most aggressive subtypes60. Therefore, the particular features of the thyroid co-expression profile may reflect its less aggressive nature. Further research into subtype-specific networks could be valuable.

When comparing intersections among networks, a clear difference emerges between cancer and normal phenotypes: normal GCNs share fewer interactions than cancer networks. We previously reported this for lung cancer, where squamous cell lung carcinoma and lung adenocarcinoma networks shared more interactions than their normal counterparts26. The co-expression program is a manifestation of the cell state, and the similarity among cancer tissues from various origins suggests functional parallels. We propose that the extensive set of shared intra-chromosomal interactions is linked to cell dedifferentiation processes during tumor development61–63, closely related to phenotypic plasticity, one of the newly described hallmark capabilities6.

Despite the small number of connections shared among normal co-expression networks, a significant cluster of riboprotein-encoding genes forms the largest component in the common normal tissue network (Fig. 6). This module also appears in the common cancer network, indicating that ribosomes maintain a high co-expression pattern in both normal and cancer tissues. As essential cellular components, ribosomal genes are highly conserved across species64, which may explain their prevalence in co-expression networks. Their presence underscores the importance of the translation process and it exhibits the capability of co-expression networks to capture important characteristics associated with a phenotype.

Regarding network structure, cancer GCNs exhibit observable modular behavior (Supplementary Fig. 4), with higher modularity values than normal networks (Table 2), particularly in networks with high intra-chromosomal fractions. This suggests that, in normal phenotypes, genes interact in a less compartmentalized manner, with transcriptional events driven by cellular functions and environmental signals. In contrast, segregated modules in cancer networks may indicate a partial loss of information spread or a gain in specific co-expression regions.

Chromosomal assortativity (Fig. 7) serves as a metric to summarize a key phenomenon: normal networks communities tend towards inter-chromosomal interactions, while cancer communities predominantly consist of intra-chromosomal links. Furthermore, no networks exhibit chromosomal assortativity values near zero, indicating communities are formed from either intra- or inter-chromosomal links. The modular composition, distance-associated co-expression decay, and high chromosomal assortativity in network communities suggest highly orchestrated co-expression events involving nearby genes in cancer GCNs. The loss of long-distance communication at the transcriptomic level and the emergence of co-expression hotspots may contribute to the disruption of essential cellular functions.

This is further illustrated in bipartite networks of Gene Ontology processes shared across more than ten tissues, whether normal or cancer, and in the networks of normal-only and cancer-only processes (Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10, Fig. 11). GO terms in normal networks are associated with cellular functions such as translation, metabolism, and development. In contrast, while inter-chromosomal cancer communities show a notable presence of immune response-associated processes, most GO processes are enriched in same-chromosome communities comprising gene families such as HOXA and HOXB, protocadherins, histone variants, and metallothioneins.

These gene families are known to be altered in different cancer types. HOX genes are implicated in the formation of cancer stem cells65–67 contributing to both hematologic malignancies and solid tumors. They are also associated with the dedifferentiation process and phenotypic plasticity6. Protocadherins may exhibit low expression due to long-range epigenetic silencing via hypermethylation, which promotes migration and proliferation in various cancers68–70. Metallothioneins display diverse expression patterns that affect tumor growth, microenvironment remodeling, and drug resistance71,72. Histones play a crucial role in the regulation of chromatin structure and are involved in cancer development and progression73, with aberrant expression of certain histone variants reported in multiple cancer types74.

In addition to modules shared among multiple networks, some GCNs reveal tissue-specific associations. For example, the brain cancer co-expression network presents a set of neural system-related processes with predominantly underexpressed genes. This suggests an association among differential gene expression, gene co-expression, and functional features of a specific network community, demonstrating the ability of GCNs to capture phenotype-specific manifestations.

The affirmation that loss of long-range co-expression is specific to cancer is supported by results presented in Fig. 12. Four chronic-degenerative diseases (Alzheimer’s disease, Type-2 Diabetes, Cardiomyopathy, and Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease) present strong similarities in their co-expression landscapes, with less modular structures and lower association with biological processes. Evaluating the presence of this phenomenon in other diseases requires additional disease-specific datasets.

Despite their unique features, our results are consistent across fifteen cancer tissues compared to their normal counterparts. There are no significant differences related to data source; both TCGA and UCSC Xena datasets include tissues with varying levels of distant co-expression loss. Moreover, the number of samples does not appear to influence network structure, as previously assessed in breast22 and lung cancer26. However, there are some considerations that need to be addressed in future analyses. For instance, the hierarchical clustering shown in Fig. 5 does not group according to tissue similarity or cancer origin. Further evaluation of the nature of the displayed similarities is needed. In that same figure, the biggest intersection sets which include esophagus, skin, and pancreas interactions, belong to the UCSC Xena dataset, with data from GTEx; we should evaluate whether the pre-processing steps added some bias.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous GCN studies in that both cancer and normal networks exhibit biologically meaningful structure, including communities enriched for coherent functional processes and strong tissue specificity. These features, widely reported in WGCNA-based analyses and other co-expression frameworks, are also evident in our networks, reinforcing the robustness of co-expression as a representation of underlying transcriptional programs. At the same time, our results extend the existing literature by addressing a complementary set of questions that are not explicitly examined in most prior studies. Whereas many GCN analyses focus on identifying modules, hub genes, or clinical associations within specific cancer contexts, our work emphasizes genome-wide topological properties, particularly the balance between intra- and inter-chromosomal co-expression and the distance dependence of gene-gene interactions along chromosomes. These spatial features are largely orthogonal to module-centric analyses and cannot be directly inferred from standard WGCNA outputs. Our contribution is to show that, across a wide range of tumor types, these networks undergo a systematic and reproducible reorganization characterized by a loss of long-range co-expression and a pronounced distance-dependent decay of intra-chromosomal interactions. By focusing on genome-wide spatial topology rather than module detection alone, this work provides a complementary perspective on how transcriptional coordination is altered in cancer.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of bulk RNA-seq data. Bulk RNA-seq represent averaged transcriptional signals across heterogeneous cell populations, which can obscure cell type-specific expression and co-regulation. Additionally, tissue heterogeneity and variability in tumor purity may influence the observed co-expression patterns. However, cellular heterogeneity is not exclusive to tumors: normal tissues also comprise diverse epithelial, stromal, and immune cell populations whose proportions vary across individuals. Despite this, normal tissue-derived networks do not exhibit the pronounced loss of inter-chromosomal co-expression or the strong distance-dependent decay observed in cancer networks. If compositional effects or tumor purity were the primary drivers of these patterns, similar large-scale topological changes would be expected in normal tissues or in other heterogeneous biological contexts.

Moreover, for spurious correlations driven by bulk averaging to systematically generate the highly reproducible, pan-cancer loss of long-range co-expression observed here, distinct cancer tissues would need to share broadly similar cellular compositions, particularly with respect to immune and stromal infiltration. This scenario is biologically implausible, as each tissue—and its malignant counterpart—exhibits a characteristic immune landscape. Together, these observations indicate that while bulk RNA-seq data have inherent limitations, tissue heterogeneity and tumor purity alone cannot account for the systematic, distance-dependent loss of long-range co-expression observed in cancer.

Future research could address current limitations by incorporating single-cell RNA-seq datasets, which would enable the identification of specific cell types driving the observed phenomenon. Integrating multi-omics data would further clarify how altered regulatory mechanisms contribute to the disruption of long-range gene co-expression. In parallel, the pronounced topological differences between normal and tumor networks (particularly the loss of intra-chromosomal interactions) suggest that network-derived features may be leveraged by machine learning approaches for cancer classification, stratification, and diagnostic applications. Targeted experimental studies will also be essential to unravel the molecular processes underlying this alteration.

The loss of long-range co-expression in cancer phenotypes appears to reflect a fundamental reorganization of transcriptional regulation. The variability observed across cancer types likely reflects differences in genomic instability, chromatin remodeling, and regulatory rewiring specific to each tumor type. Cancers characterized by high levels of structural variation and widespread epigenetic reprogramming may exhibit a more pronounced disruption of inter-chromosomal coordination, whereas tumors with lower global chromosomal instability or more differentiated cellular states may retain partial long-range transcriptional organization. Furthermore, tissue-specific regulatory architectures and distinct tumor microenvironmental contexts likely modulate the extent to which genome-wide transcriptional coordination is altered. Based on these observations, we suggest that the loss of long-range co-expression is not driven by a single molecular event but instead represents an emergent, systems-level property of malignant transformation arising from the combined and non-linear effects of chromatin reorganization, epigenetic remodeling, transcription factor rewiring, and altered nuclear architecture. Notably, this coordinated topological shift is not observed in other chronic diseases analyzed under the same framework, reinforcing the interpretation that genome-wide disruption of long-distance transcriptional coordination may constitute a distinctive systems-level feature of cancer.

Methods

Databases

RNA-Seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas35 was downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) Data Portal for bladder, breast, kidney, lung, thyroid and uterus tissues in STAR-Counts format. Cancer datasets included samples tagged as Primary Tumor, while normal datasets included samples tagged as Solid Tissue Normal. Gene expression RSEM expected counts from the TCGA TARGET GTEx project were downloaded from the UCSC Xena browser36. This dataset integrates TCGA and GTEx75 samples using the Toil RNA-seq pipeline37. For brain, colon, esophagus, liver, ovary, pancreas, prostate, skin, and testis, normal counts were obtained from GTEx-Xena, tagged as Normal Tissue, while cancer counts were identified as Primary Tumor. RSEM expected counts were transformed from log2+1 to integer count values. Sample counts per tissue and condition are shown in Table 1. RNA-counts for GSE174367, GSE184050, GSE165303, and GSE192959, were downloaded directly from Gene Expression Omnibus.

Data processing

The RNA-Seq data processing and differential expression analysis pipeline was adapted from ref. 22. A Snakemake76 workflow was implemented to create a reproducible analysis: https://github.com/ddiannae/llrc-pipeline. A second pipeline was created for the analysis of co-expression profiles and co-expression networks: https://github.com/ddiannae/llrc-networks.

Pre-processing and normalization

Pre-processing was performed per tissue and sample type. TCGA samples were annotated using GENCODE v36 (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/gdc-data-processing/gdc-reference-files). Only protein-coding genes, conventional chromosomes, and genes present in GENCODE v44 (July 2023) were retained. After assembling raw count matrices, datasets from both sources followed the same pipeline.

The NOISeq R library77 was used for quality control. Genes with mean expression < 10 or zero counts in more than 50% of samples were removed. The removal of low-expressed genes resulted in gene expression matrices containing different numbers of genes across tissues. Gene counts expression boxplots revealed that some samples from the XENA database had extremely low values; hence, samples with mean expression values two standard deviations above or below the total mean were excluded.

Gene counts were transformed to transcripts per million (TPM). Principal Component Analysis plots revealed overlap between normal and tumor samples, thus ARSyNSeq78 was applied for multidimensional noise reduction using default parameters.

Mutual information (MI) calculation

Mutual Information (MI) is a measure of statistical dependence that quantifies the amount of information one random variable contains about another. Expressed in terms of entropy, it is defined as:

Where H(X) and H(Y) are the marginal entropies, H(X∣Y) and H(Y∣X) are the conditional entropies, and H(X, Y), is the joint entropy of X and Y.

We used the ARACNE algorithm79 to calculate MI values between pairs of genes as a measure of co-expression, based on their associated expression in each tissue and sample type independently. A Singularity container was created to run ARACNE within the Snakemake workflow (https://github.com/ddiannae/ARACNE-multicore).

Differential expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes in cancer samples were identified using the DESeq280 R package. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate (FDR), with padj < 0.05 as the threshold and no threshold was applied to log fold change (logFC) values.

Co-expression profile analysis

Identification of intra-chromosomal fraction

Gene pairs were ranked by MI in descending order, and the fraction of intra-chromosomal interactions was calculated at thresholds ranging from 1000 to 100 million top MI pairs. Two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used to compare intra-chromosomal fractions between cancer and normal phenotypes, starting from the top 1000 interactions and incrementing the starting bin logarithmically up to the top 50 million interactions.

Distance versus MI analysis

Intra-chromosomal gene pairs were grouped into bins of 1000 interactions based on base pair distance. For each bin, mean MI, mean distance, and MI standard deviation were calculated. Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to compare the MI values from each bin against all other bins of intra-chromosomal interactions. We define long-range co-expression as either inter-chromosomal gene-gene associations or intra-chromosomal associations between genes separated by more than 10 Mb. This threshold was selected because it approximately corresponds to the size of the centromeric region of the smallest human chromosome, providing a biologically meaningful boundary between local and long-distance genomic interactions.

Network analysis

Networks and community detection

Co-expression networks were constructed using the top 100,000 MI interactions per tissue and sample type. Communities were detected using the Louvain algorithm41 implemented in igraph81, with MI values as link weights. Community names were assigned by calculating PageRank scores for all genes, also using igraph, and selecting the highest one.

Nominal assortativity

Chromosomal assortativity was calculated by dividing the difference between intra- and inter- chromosomal links by the total number of links in a community.

GO overrepresentation analysis

We used the enrichGO function from the clusterProfiler R package82 to identify over-represented Biological Process terms in Gene Ontology (GO)83. Enrichment analysis was performed for communities with at least five genes, retaining GO terms with a minimum size of ten. The universe set was defined by the genes in the original expression matrix for each tissue. GO terms with padj < 1E−10, adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, were considered significant.
