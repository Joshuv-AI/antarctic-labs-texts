Arteriovenous fistula-associated angiosarcoma
Authors: Shreya Jalali, Benson Law, Bret Wehrli, Oonagh Scallan, Shane Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Angiosarcomas are a rare, aggressive, and insidious type of soft tissue sarcoma and are often misdiagnosed, causing delays in treatment. We report the case of a 53-year-old renal transplant patient with a thrombosed arteriovenous fistula who developed angiosarcoma that was incorrectly presumed to be a hematoma caused by trauma. Despite surgical resection and adjuvant radiation, the patient developed local recurrence proximal to the excision site and pulmonary metastasis. This case report highlights the importance of routine pathological analysis of symptomatic arteriovenous fistulas and prompts consideration for the early resection of arteriovenous fistulas post-transplant that demonstrate concerning changes.

Angiosarcomas, a rare entity, comprise only 1% to 2% of soft tissue sarcomas.1, 2, 3 They typically originate from vascular endothelial cells in areas with existing blood vessels or lymphatic channels, often in cutaneous sites, and can form abnormally organized solid masses or nodules.4 Early diagnosis is crucial, as angiosarcomas that arise from the liver or deeper soft tissues and those larger than 5 cm portend a poorer prognosis.5,6 Their genetic heterogeneity and insidious presentation, often beginning as a lesion resembling a bruise and easily mistaken for a benign cutaneous finding, contribute to the diagnostic challenge.7,8 Once identified, they often extend beyond what is discernible on physical examination and can lead to systemic multifocal disease through hematogenous or lymphatic spread.7,9 Metastases are often to the lungs, as with other sarcomas, and occasionally to the liver, bones, or lymph nodes.10,11

Angiosarcomas are generally aggressive tumors with vigorous mitotic activity. In most studies, the overall survival at 5 years ranges from 30% to 56%.6 A study by Holden et al12 of 72 patients revealed a 5-year survival rate of 12%, with half the patients succumbing within 15 months of treatment. The extent of the primary tumor does not significantly influence survival.12

The development of angiosarcomas within thrombosed arteriovenous (AV) fistulas is a rare but previously reported phenomenon.13,14 There are very few case reports and no accurate literature regarding their incidence in this setting. It has been proposed that AV fistulas may contribute to the development of angiosarcoma through altered lymphatic drainage and abnormal intraluminal blood flow.13,14 These changes can affect endothelial metabolism and proliferation, creating a “lymphedema-like” microenvironment that may promote tumorigenesis.13,14

Case

This case report describes a 53-year-old male with end-stage renal disease secondary to membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, who provided consent for publication of his case details and images. He had a left radiocephalic AV fistula created remotely for dialysis, which he used for 2 years until receiving a renal transplant from a living-related donor in 1993, after which the fistula thrombosed. His medication regimen consisted of cyclosporine, prednisone, and nifedipine. For some years, he had an asymptomatic grape-sized mass along his AV fistula. He reported hitting that arm and then noticed another mass growing adjacent to that area, which was painful. He was referred to vascular surgery for evaluation of a 2 to 3 cm mass adjacent to his old fistula, which was presumed to represent a hematoma secondary to trauma. He reported a shooting pain radiating into his thumb, and the pain had improved and was present now only on palpation of the mass. This finding was confirmed on physical examination, and a bedside ultrasound study demonstrated a thrombosed AV fistula with no discernible flow in the radial artery and good flow through the ulnar artery. After failing to improve with multimodal analgesia, he underwent an operation for resection of the left radiocephalic pseudoaneurysm, which appeared to originate from the cephalic vein, along with ligation of the radial artery.

During the operation, an incision was made along the old AV fistula, and dissection was carried down to the presumed hematoma or pseudoaneurysm capsule. Dissection around the edge of this capsule circumferentially was enough to isolate it. The radial artery was controlled and ligated. The pseudoaneurysm was then dissected and removed.

Results

A specimen thought to be the left radial artery pseudoaneurysm from the old AV fistula was sent to pathology, which revealed that this 3.5 × 3.0 × 1.0 cm tan-brown firm mass with a nodular lesion was a prominent vascular malignancy. A large fibrin clot within a large vessel lumen was shown to be associated with a hypercellular area of spindled and epithelioid cells containing pleiomorphic nuclei, open to clear chromatin and prominent nucleoli (Fig 1). These cells appeared poorly formed, anastomosing with vascular channels and demonstrating red blood cell extravasation (Fig 2). Immunohistochemistry showed diffuse positivity for ETS-related gene (ERG) and clusters of differentiation 31. The diagnosis was angiosarcoma, extending to the specimen margins, with characteristic pleiomorphic endothelial cells forming irregular vascular sinusoids alongside normal vascular channels.7,10 Of note, the histologic grade is not known to be accurate in predicting outcomes for cutaneous angiosarcomas.7 He underwent a subsequent magnetic resonance imaging to better visualize the vascular neoplasm (Fig 3), as well as a computed tomography thorax that revealed six small peripheral lung nodules (Fig 4). These were deemed too small for biopsy. The tumor board recommended neoadjuvant radiation therapy, which he received. This was followed by a secondary operation for a wide excision of the angiosarcoma. The ellipse was approximately 10 × 8 cm in the skin. After dissection of the subcutaneous tissue, the deep fascia was raised, and then we went beneath the tumor dorsally and ventrally. We took sheaths over the tendons but left most of the tendon intact. There was residual angiosarcoma in that specimen, but with margins over 1.5 mm. Nine months later, however, the left arm had a small area of violaceous nodular thickening about 1.5 cm proximal to the excision site, likely a marginal recurrence. By 14 months, slowly enlarging pulmonary nodules, consistent with metastatic disease, were observed. Chemotherapy with paclitaxel and localized radiation led to good primary nodule response and stable lung disease.

Unfortunately, 21 months after his second operation for definitive excision, he deteriorated due to the rapid progression of his metastatic disease in his lungs, and he opted for palliative chemoradiation. Five months later, he passed away from hypoxic respiratory failure.

Discussion

The mainstay of treatment of angiosarcoma is surgical resection with margins, as the tumor can extend well past its visible borders,15,16 and frozen sections are unreliable.8 Even after complete local resection, which can be challenging given the proximity to other key anatomic structures,9 the systemic spread will ultimately dictate the ongoing pathogenesis of the angiosarcoma.6 Adjunct measures can improve outcomes, such as radiation therapy,8,17 chemotherapy,18 immunotherapy,19,20 and bland, chemoembolization or radioembolization.21 Antiangiogenic therapies have also been attempted; vascular endothelial growth factor A monoclonal antibodies such as bevacizumab have shown a significant response in angiosarcoma with at least a partial response or disease stability.22 The nuances of treatment depend on the anatomical location (eg, cutaneous, deep tissue, and organ) and the presence of metastasis. However, the general understanding is that even years after local resection and treatment, most patients do eventually develop metastatic disease.9 With metastatic disease, 5-year survival is approximately 30%, with a median survival of about 7 months.2

With respect to diagnosis and prognosis of angiosarcomas arising from AV fistulas, a review of 22 cases by Oskrochi et al14 found that initial presentations were frequently misdiagnosed as thrombosis or infection, with the diagnosis of angiosarcoma only established after a delay of 2-40 weeks. Most of these were post-transplant patients receiving immunosuppression, and their mean survival remained poor (8.8 ± 3.7 months).

Conclusions

This case of AV fistula-associated angiosarcoma highlights several principles. First, sending resected fistula specimens to pathology routinely and without exception is essential. Second, this case prompts reflection on how to best approach the treatment of angiosarcoma, given its aggressive nature and early metastatic course. Lastly, as Oskrochi et al14 suggested, clear guidelines on ligating AV fistulas post-transplant need to be established. Once the graft kidney is stable, routine ligation of unused AV fistulas could reduce the risk of angiosarcomas; however, this will have to be balanced against accepting the creation of a new fistula in the future if the transplant goes on to fail during their lifetime. In this case, the fistula was nonfunctional and early resection in the face of fistula changes should have been considered. Ultimately, these decisions need to be patient-centered and tailored to the individual, but the possibility of post-transplant fistula sarcoma cannot be overlooked.
