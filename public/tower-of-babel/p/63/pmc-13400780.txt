Interbacterial AI-2 signaling orchestrates chemotaxis, attachment, and antimicrobial peptide resistance to promote Salmonella colonization
Authors: Anmol Singh, Abhilash Vijay Nair, RS Rajmani, Dipshikha Chakravortty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryQuorum sensing enables bacteria to coordinate collective behaviors crucial for survival and infection. Here, we show that Salmonella Typhimurium employs LuxS/autoinducer-2 (AI-2) signaling to regulate multiple stages of pathogenesis. AI-2 functions as a chemoattractant and modulates the expression of chemotaxis and motility-associated genes, promoting flagellar assembly and adhesion to the epithelial host cells. Loss of LuxS/AI-2 signaling diminishes hilD and SPI-1 gene expression, unveiling a regulatory link between quorum sensing and virulence determinants. Further, AI-2 signaling governs resistance to antimicrobial peptides through the pmrD/AB system and supports intracellular proliferation in the host cells. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that LuxS/AI-2 controls distinct gene networks spanning invasion, survival, and immune evasion. In murine infection models, inhibition of LuxS/AI-2 signaling in combination with antibiotics can be an alternative therapeutic approach. Our findings establish LuxS/AI-2 as an integrated regulatory hub in Salmonella pathogenesis and highlight quorum sensing as a targetable mechanism for therapeutic intervention.Keywords: quorum sensing, Salmonella, chemoattractant, antimicrobial peptide

Introduction

Bacteria were initially thought to be solitary microbes that led an independent life cycle of multiplying and increasing their numbers, and some of them could cause life-threatening infections. However, a series of discoveries led to the elucidation of bacterial communication mechanisms, referred to as quorum sensing (QS).1,2,3 This process involves the production and secretion of chemical signals that are sensed by the neighboring cells in the bacterial community and orchestrate a myriad of genes and pathways, resulting in coordinated behaviors such as adaptation, virulence, and other critical community behaviors, underscoring the advantages of communal living among bacterial populations.4,5 Bacterial pathogenesis is very well governed by this communication paradigm. Upon entry into the host, the pathogen encounters diverse environmental cues that will necessitate coordinated regulation of colonization, nutrient acquisition, and virulence factors.

QS is a primary coordinator of bacterial virulence in several pathogens that cause severe bacterial infection. The disruption of the QS regulator abaM in Acinetobacter baumannii led to enhanced surface motility and biofilm formation but paradoxically attenuated virulence, highlighting the fine balance QS maintains between colonization traits and pathogenicity.6 In Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), the Agr QS primes antioxidative response mechanisms by regulating gene expression and respiratory states of bacteria, thereby protecting against lethal hydrogen peroxide concentrations.7 While in Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae), QS regulates virulence and carbohydrate metabolism-associated genes mediating biofilm formation and middle ear infection.8 QS also fosters antimicrobial resistance development in pathogens such as the enteric pathogen S. pneumoniae, where it drives the activation of AcrAB-TolC efflux pumps, enhancing the pathogen’s capability to expel toxic compounds like antibiotics.9

Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (STM) is a prominent etiological agent of food-borne illnesses and a leading contributor to non-typhoidal salmonellosis (NTS).10 It faces very long odds while seeking to colonize and breach the intestinal barrier, including acidic pH, limited nutrient availability, bile salt toxicity, and competition from gut microbiota. It is critical for enteric pathogens like Salmonella to establish and increase in number, reaching the infectious dose to invade the host epithelial cells within the host gut. Upon breaching the epithelial barrier, Salmonella is engulfed by macrophages in the lamina propria, facilitating its systemic dissemination throughout the host.11,12,13,14 With the fluctuations of extreme conditions, Salmonella needs to be adept at sensing, responding, and adapting to unprecedented situations. This principle applies broadly to intracellular pathogens, which utilize complex mechanisms to infect, evade host immunity, and replicate within the host.

Salmonella produces and detects a universal signaling molecule, autoinducer-2 (AI-2), for its communication. The AI-2 is generated by the LuxS enzyme as a byproduct of the activated methyl cycle (AMC), from the S-adenosyl methionine (SAM) and 4,5-dihydroxy-2,3-pentanedione (DPD) as its precursors. AI-2 is sensed by the periplasmic binding protein LsrB and subsequently transported into the bacterial cytosol by the ATP- binding cassette (ABC) transporter complex encoded by the lsr operon (LsrA, LsrB, LsrC, and LsrD). Once internalized, AI-2 is phosphorylated by the LsrK kinase. Phosphorylated AI-2 then binds to the transcriptional repressor LsrR, thereby relieving LsrR-mediated repression of the lsr operon and promoting further AI-2 uptake (Figure 1A).15,16 AI-2 is broadly produced and detected across bacterial genera, allowing both intra- and inter-species communication.17 Studies suggest that LuxS/AI-2 QS systems regulate virulence, biofilm formation, motility, and antimicrobial resistance.18,19,20 Further, there is evidence of involvement of AI-2 in motility and chemotaxis in the Ag43-dependent aggregation of E. coli.21 The study also shows that chemotaxis toward self-produced AI-2 provides E. coli a fitness advantage during gut colonization, and hinges on fructoselysine metabolism.22 AI-2, a molecule produced and detected by numerous bacterial species, has also been found to be mimicked and synthesized by eukaryotic cells.23 Research has demonstrated that several bacterial species exhibit a chemotactic response to AI-2, highlighting its crucial role in forming intricate multispecies communities.24 Notably, AI-2 is thought to influence the bacterial community structure in the mammalian gut following antibiotic-induced dysbiosis and to bolster colonization resistance against specific enteric pathogens.25 Thus, it is apparent that bacterial communication is a key to its pathogenesis. Despite these findings, significant gaps remain in understanding the advantages of communal living and how AI-2 signaling benefits pathogens in physiologically relevant conditions. Considering the enteric pathogen Salmonella, though several studies highlight the role of AI-2 signaling in the regulation of SPI-1 and motility-associated genes, the physiological significance remains only partially understood in Salmonella pathogenesis despite being a thoroughly established system.18,26,27 Moreover, the regulatory network and molecular mechanism governing the coordinated response and behavior mediating the pathogen’s virulence remain an open question.

In our present study, we elucidate that the LuxS/AI-2 signaling cascade governs the processes of the initial attachment and invasion into intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) in vivo in mice and in vitro by governing chemotaxis and flagellar motility. Furthermore, LuxS/AI-2 signaling is active within the pathogen’s intracellular niche and assists in intracellular survival by regulating the pmrABC operon, which provides resistance against antimicrobial peptides (AMP). Importantly, as an alternative approach to curb pathogen infection, inhibition of AI-2 signaling in combination with antibiotics efficiently diminished Salmonella organ burden and delayed mortality in mice models. This study provides mechanistic evidence that the LuxS-AI-2 signaling serves as the central regulatory signaling pathway in bacteria, directing pivotal bacterial processes at various stages of infection within the host environment.

Results

AI-2 signaling couples chemotaxis to flagellar switch control for coordinated motility in bacteria

To initiate this study, we employed gene knockout strains of STM 14028s (STM WT), targeting key components of the AI-2 QS system, including the AI-2 synthase gene (luxS), the sensor/receptor gene (lsrB), the kinase gene (lsrK), and the repressor gene (lsrR). Previous studies have suggested that deletion of the luxS gene in Salmonella impairs motility; however, it remains unclear whether this effect is mediated by AI-2 dependent QS, as LuxS also participates in central metabolic pathways. To assess the role of the complete LuxS/AI-2 signaling, we performed the swim and swarm motility assay upon deletion of luxS, lsrB, lsrK, and lsrR. We observed that motility was significantly impaired in luxS, lsrB, and lsrK knockout strains compared to the STM WT (Figures 1B, 1C, S1A, and S1B). On the contrary, STM ΔlsrR behaves as STM WT. Importantly, in both our previous and this study, we observed no growth defect upon deletion of luxS, lsrB, lsrK, and lsrR in Luria Bertani (LB) media (Figure S1C). We next aimed to untangle the interconnected mechanisms that orchestrate motility behaviors. Many motile bacteria control their swimming direction by regulating the sense of flagellar rotation in response to sensory cues. FliM is a key component of the flagellar motor’s switch complex, along with FliG and FliN, which is thought to interact with one of the chemotaxis proteins, CheY.28,29 Since LuxS/AI-2 signaling is known to regulate bacterial gene expression, we hypothesized that it may also influence the expression of motility-associated genes. We observed that the mRNA expression of fliM, cheY, and motA, which are involved in flagellar motor function,30 was downregulated in STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK strains compared to STM WT under in vitro LB growth conditions (Figures 1D–1F). Following this, we noted that genes involved in transcription regulation of flagella assembly, flhD, fliA, fliC, fljB,31 were also downregulated in the absence of AI-2 signaling (Figures S1D–S1G). Further, our promoter activity assays for motA, cheY, and fliC demonstrated that the expression of these genes is reduced in the absence of LuxS/AI-2 signaling (Figures S1H–S1J), and absolute mRNA expression analysis demonstrated a comparable pattern of gene regulation between STM WT and LuxS/AI-2 signaling knockout strains (Figures S2A and S2B). In contrast, we observed that the control genes gapA and gyrA were not differentially expressed in knockout strains in LB medium or upon infection in Caco-2 epithelial cells (Figures S2C–S2F). Following reduced expression, we performed immunofluorescence to examine flagellin FliC expression on the surface of STM. We observed that the presence of the flagellin FliC was significantly lower on the surface in the knockout strains of luxS, lsrB, and lsrK than in the STM WT (Figures 1G, 1H, and S3A). We further validated our result by using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and observed that in the absence of AI-2 sensing and signaling, the number of flagella is reduced on the surface of STM ΔluxS and STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK (Figure 1I). We confirmed these findings by inhibiting AI-2 signaling using (Z)-4-Bromo-5-(bromomethylene)-2(5H)-furanone (BF-8), a competitive inhibitor of DPD, in STM WT, and by supplementing STM ΔluxS with exogenous DPD or STM WT-derived spent media. We observed that BF-8 treatment reduced FliC flagellin levels and flagellar abundance, whereas DPD or spent media supplementation increased them, as evidenced by immunofluorescence and TEM, respectively. (Figures S3B–S3D). From these data, we propose an interconnected regulatory circuit in which LuxS/AI-2 signaling is associated with altered expression of fliM and motA, suggesting a possible role in modulating flagellar organization and motility in Salmonella.

AI-2 sensing in Salmonella assists in chemotaxis and enhances the attachment to Caco-2 epithelial cells

Further implying that QS molecule receptors assist beyond detection and import of signaling molecules in bacteria. Hence, we speculate that the LsrB receptor in Salmonella may act as a chemoreceptor, aiding in attraction to the AI-2 molecule. We used a μ-Slide Chemotaxis chamber (ibidi) for the chemotaxis assay (Figure S4A). DPD, STM WT spent media, and serine were used in the second reservoir of the chamber. Serine was used as a positive control as a chemoattractant. We found that the number of STM WT transmigrated toward spent media, DPD, and serine was higher than that of STM ΔluxS and STM ΔlsrB, as determined by colony-forming unit (CFU) and a bright-field microscope (Figures 2A–2C). The explanation for the reduced transmigration of STM ΔluxS and STM ΔlsrB toward serine is due to reduced flagella activity. These data suggest that LuxS/AI-2 signaling controls flagellar motility and chemotaxis.

Salmonella is a pathogen that primarily infects the ileum, the distal part of the small intestine.32 Salmonella has evolved mechanisms to gain access, attach, and infect the IECs of the crypt to survive and replicate. We speculate that Salmonella is attracted toward AI-2 molecules of the intestinal crypts, where fluid flow is resisted, and this can explain why Salmonella moves to the distal part of the intestine. Epithelial cells release an AI-2 mimic in response to bacteria, and bacteria might benefit from this AI-2 mimic via their AI-2 QS receptors.23 We were curious whether LuxS/AI-2 modulates the attachment to epithelial cells. Our adhesion assay data suggest that STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK are compromised in their ability to adhere to Caco-2 epithelial cells in comparison to STM WT (Figures 2D, 2E, and S4B). On the other hand, the STM ΔlsrR and luxS, lsrB, lsrK, and lsrR complemented gene in the respective knockout strain behave similarly to the STM WT. Upon inhibition of LuxS/AI-2 signaling with BF-8 in STM WT and exogenous DPD and STM WT spent media treatment to STM ΔluxS, suggests AI-2 sensing and signaling enhance the attachment to epithelial cells (Figure S4C). Notably, our in vitro growth kinetics study in the presence of the BF-8 inhibitor did not exhibit an inhibitory or bactericidal effect on STM (Figure S4D). Together, these findings suggest that LuxS/AI-2 contributes to the regulation of motility and host-pathogen interaction, indicating a potential role in the establishment of infection.

LuxS/AI-2-mediated communication facilitates the breaching of intestinal epithelial barriers and the invasion of gut epithelial cells

Adhesion and invasion represent the crucial step of successfully establishing infection by bacterial pathogens and cellular invasion, which is followed by intracellular multiplication, dissemination to other tissues, or persistence.33,34,35 Previously, it has been reported that luxS mutant shows reduced invasion into epithelial cells in vitro.26 Having observed that LuxS/AI-2 enhances the attachment to Caco-2 cells, we also conjecture that LuxS/AI-2 signaling regulates adhesion events and thereby aids in the invasion of Salmonella into the epithelial cells. We infected the Caco-2 epithelial cell with STM strains and observed that STM WT, STM ΔlsrR, and luxS, lsrB, lsrk, lsrR complemented in STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, ΔlsrK, and STM ΔlsrR, respectively, invade more efficiently compared to STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and ΔlsrK (Figure 3A). Further, we treated the STM ΔluxS with exogenous DPD, the STM WT spent media, and the STM WT with the inhibitor BF-8, followed by infection in Caco-2 epithelial cells. Exogenous DPD and spent media treatment restored invasion compared to untreated STM ΔluxS, whereas inhibition with BF-8 reduced STM WT invasion (Figures S4E–S4G). Next, we sought to investigate the physiological relevance of bacterial signaling by studying the invasion of the knockout strains into the IECs of C57BL/6 mice (Figure 3B). Interestingly, STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and ΔlsrK invaded significantly less into the Peyer’s patches of the C57BL/6 mice compared to STM WT (Figure 3C). STM WT invasion was reduced upon BF-8 treatment. In contrast, STM ΔluxS invasion increased with the treatment of DPD and STM WT spent media (Figure S5A). The immunofluorescence study with the histopathological sections of the mouse ileum further reinforces the reduced invasion and colonization of the mutants in the mouse IECs (Figures 3D, S5B, and S5C). Thus, it is evident that AI-2 signaling facilitates breaching of the gut barrier and invasion into the IECs.

Because QS governs extensive genetic networks in bacteria, we anticipated that in Salmonella, it orchestrates several pathways and that invasion is not solely dependent on QS-mediated adhesion events. It is shown that the master regulator HilD regulates Salmonella invasion and motility via activating gene expression of the type 3 secretion system 1 (SPI-1) and flagellar motility genes.36,37 We hypothesized that LuxS/AI-2 signaling regulates the hilD and thus other SPI-1 master regulators downstream of hilD to modulate the invasion or infection in host cells. We observed that hilD, hilA, and invF, master regulators of SPI-1 were downregulated in STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK in vitro LB media compared to STM WT (Figures 3E–3G and S6A). Further, the hilD promoter activity assay determined that the expression is downregulated in STM ΔluxS and STM ΔlsrK compared to STM WT (Figure S6B). Even under SPI-1-inducing conditions (0.3 M NaCl), all knockout strains exhibited growth comparable to STM WT (Figure S6C). Thus, in Salmonella, LuxS/AI-2 signaling intersects with the hilD regulon to control SPI-1 master regulators, underscoring its central role in gene regulation at early events of infection.

LuxS/AI-2 signaling is active within the intracellular niche and facilitates survival within Caco-2 epithelial cells

As the LuxS/AI-2 signaling is a key language for Salmonella communication, we sought to explore its role in the next stages of infection. To begin, we asked how Salmonella Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium) regulates the expression of the genes within the lsr operon and luxS upon infection into host epithelial cells. We evaluated the mRNA expression of luxS and lsr operon genes (lsrB, lsrK, lsrR) in STM WT upon infection in the Caco2 cell line. We noted that luxS expression was similar at 2 and 6 h post-infection and downregulated at 16 h post-infection compared to 2 h while the lsr operon genes were slightly higher at post 6 h infection compared to 2 h post-infection (Figures 4A and 4B). Consistent expression of genes suggests that AI-2 signaling may be active inside the host cell. Following this, we observed that STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK proliferate less in comparison to STM WT, STM ΔlsrR, and complemented strains of STM within the Caco-2 cells (Figure 4C). The fold replication was reduced in the BF-8-treated culture of STM WT (Figure S7A). Conversely, upon treatment of WT spent media and exogenous DPD molecule, STM ΔluxS fold replication was recovered (Figures S7B and S7C). These observations likely stem from AI-2-mediated signaling, suggesting that bacteria engage in communication within the intracellular niche. To further confirm whether this signaling is active inside the host cells, we generated the reporter strain, which produces green fluorescence on LuxS/AI-2 activation. Firstly, we checked the QS active bacteria during the in vitro growth in LB media. Flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy results suggest that QS active bacterial cells were increased in mid-log phase to early stationary phase (Figures 4E and S7D). Moreover, when we gave the STM WT spent media treatment to STM ΔluxS (containing reporter construct), we observed that STM ΔluxS became QS active (Figure S7E). Upon infection in Caco-2 epithelial cells with reporter STM WT suggests that Lux/AI-2 signaling is active inside the host cells (Figure 4F). Taken together, this suggests that LuxS/AI-2 signaling is active and facilitates survival within Caco-2 epithelial cells.

LuxS/AI-2 cascade enhances Salmonella survival by mediating evasion from host AMP defences

The innate immune system within the gut relies substantially on AMPs from epithelial cells, including cathelicidins and defensins, which help maintain intestinal homeostasis by targeting pathogens and regulating the gut microbiota.38 Our previous study showed that the LsrR repressor protein negatively modulates the expression of the phoP gene, which protects against acidic stress.39 In addition, previous studies have shown that cationic AMPs can activate the PhoP/PhoQ two-component system (TCS), which, in turn, regulates the expression and activation of pmrABD and provides protection against AMPs.40,41,42,43,44,45,46,47 We considered that LuxS/AI-2 signaling might aid in combating AMP sensitivity via regulating phoP/phoQ. In continuation, we observed that STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK were more sensitive compared to STM WT and STM ΔlsrR under in vitro conditions in the presence of sub-MIC (minimum inhibitory concentration) concentration of polymyxin (Figures 5A, S7F, and S7G). Subsequently, we noted that the mRNA expression levels of pmrD, pmrA, and pmrB were reduced in STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK compared to STM WT under polymyxin-treated conditions in vitro in LB media (Figure 5B). However, in STM ΔlsrR, their expression levels were comparable to those in STM WT. Thereafter, pmrD, pmrA, and pmrB gene expression level reduction was observed upon infection in Caco-2 epithelial cells in STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK compared to STM WT (Figures 5C–5E). Although our study did not directly investigate the involvement of AMPs, these findings indicate that LuxS/AI-2 signaling regulates pmrD/pmrAB expression and may therefore contribute to AMP resistance mechanisms.

LuxS/AI-2 signaling regulates critical gene targets to assist in multiple stages of Salmonella infection in the host

As we observed, LuxS/AI-2 signaling is involved in various stages of Salmonella infection by regulating multiple genes. Following this, we performed transcriptomic analysis between STM WT and QS knockout strains STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK to identify and categorize the genetic targets and thereby define the genetic networks governed by LuxS/AI-2 signaling. We found that, among the 4,728 gene variables, 507, 1,291, and 225 genes were significantly differentially expressed in STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK, respectively, compared to STM WT. Out of which, 316 are downregulated and 191 are upregulated in STM ΔluxS, 902 are downregulated and 389 genes are upregulated in STM ΔlsrB, while 106 are downregulated and 119 are upregulated in STM ΔlsrK in comparison to STM WT (Figures 6A–6C and S8A). Gene enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that prominently downregulated genes belong to the flagellar classes (fliC/fljB family gene), chemotaxis (cheY, motA, motB, etc.), secretory protein, ribosomal, and protein synthesis genes(rpsT, rpmH, rpsO, rmf, infA)and virulence-associated genes (sopD2, sseI, pipB, sopB, sifA, etc.) in QS knockout strains (Figures 6D and 6E). Interestingly, the heatmap of the top-downregulated 50 genes also includes genes related to flagellar motility (fliC/fljB), chemotaxis (motA), and virulence (hilD, pipB, sseI, etc.) (Figures S8B–S8D). Although the deletion of LuxS/AI-2 signaling abolished Salmonella virulence, multiple genes related to metabolic pathway like histidine metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, carbon metabolism, citrate cycle (aceE, aceF, poxB, sucA, sucC, cyoF, cyoA, cyoB, prpC, etc.), amino acid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, lipid acid metabolism, nitrogen cycle, and arginine and proline metabolism, etc. were upregulated in absence of AI-2 signal and sensor. The luxS mutant displayed a distinct profile, including selective downregulation of propanediol utilization (pdu) genes. These findings indicate that loss of AI-2 signaling does not broadly suppress metabolic gene expression but is instead associated with altered transcription of metabolic pathways. While the RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data do not establish a direct mechanistic coupling between metabolic adaptation and virulence gene regulation, it suggests that disruption of AI-2 signaling is accompanied by widespread transcriptional reprogramming. Together, these findings highlight clear differences in the transcriptional responses associated with disruption of AI-2 synthesis, sensing, and processing components. Hence, justifies the significant differences in the pathogenic traits and behavior of STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK compared to STM WT. A detailed list of upregulated and downregulated genes in knockout strains is provided in the Table S2. Taken together, the transcriptomic analysis suggests that the LuxS/AI-2 signaling regulates multiple critical gene targets to assist in multiple stages of Salmonella infection in the host.

Inhibition of LuxS/AI-2 signaling in combination with antibiotic treatment reduces Salmonella colonization in vivo and enhances mice survival

Salmonella is known to be a successful pathogen that can overcome various host immune responses such as oxidative stress, nitrosative stress, acidic pH, etc.48 In our recent study, STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK showed less colonization and delayed mouse mortality compared to STM WT infection, and upon treatment of BF-8 (4 mg/kg mice body weight) to STM WT-infected mice, organ burden was reduced, and mouse survivability increased.39 Following this, we observed in co-infection studies in mice that STM WT outcompeted STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, or STM ΔlsrK in the liver, spleen, mesenteric lymph node (MLN), and intestine (Figures 7A and 7B). Given its regulatory role, we hypothesized that blocking LuxS/QS signaling could be exploited as a translational intervention against infection. We therefore assessed whether combining this inhibition with antibiotics could enhance efficacy at lower antibiotic doses (2 mg/kg of each).49,50 We first infected the mice with STM WT via oral gavage and initiated treatment on the 3rd day post-infection (Figure 7C). On the 3rd day post-infection, we observed organ colonization and slight weight reduction, confirming infection (Figures S9A–S9C). After each day of treatment of BF-8 with or without antibiotics for consecutive days, on the 6th day post-infection, we noted that organ colonization was significantly less with the combination of low concentration 2 mg/kg of body weight (of BF-8 and ciprofloxacin or ceftazidime) (Figures 7C–7F, S9D, and S9E). With three doses of a combination of BF-8 with ciprofloxacin or ceftazidime, started on the 3rd day post-infection, mice survived to 14 or 15 days, respectively. On the other hand, low doses of BF-8 and antibiotics also delayed mortality in mice compared to untreated mice (Figures 7G, 7H, and S9F). Furthermore, tissue histopathology of liver and spleen, suggested that a combination treatment of BF-8 inhibitor with antibiotics improved the health of the mice more effectively with a less disease score (Figures 7I and S10). These data suggest that the AI-2-mediated signaling is required for in vivo colonization of Salmonella in mice, and signaling inhibition can be a promising strategy in combination with low doses of antibiotics for the treatment of Salmonella infection in the era of emerging antibiotic-resistant Salmonella strains.

Discussion

Adapting its tactics and employing multiple defense systems, pathogens like Salmonella has become a potent enteric pathogen, occupying several host niches during its pathogenesis. The opening moves during its pathogenesis involve the expansion and competition in the gut, followed by attachment and invasion. Of the several factors, chemotaxis and motility contribute majorly to such a process. Here, we showed that Salmonella LuxS/AI-2 signaling controls motility via regulating the flagellar switch protein, flagellar gene expression, and flagellar organization. Since the accumulation of AI-2 upregulates the lsr operon, we also observe that the genes lsrB, lsrK, and lsrR were upregulated during the mid-log and late-log phases.39 This provides an additional context to our current observations, as flagella formation is primarily during its exponential growth phases in vitro. Deletion of the luxS gene in Salmonella leads to impaired motility; however, whether this phenotype is driven by AI-2-mediated QS or by the metabolic role of LuxS remains unresolved. In this context, our findings provide important mechanistic insight by demonstrating that LuxS/AI-2 signaling directly regulates motility through modulation of flagellar gene expression and organization. Specifically, we show that AI-2 sensing and uptake via the Lsr system, along with LsrK-mediated phosphorylation, are critical for orchestrating downstream regulatory pathways controlling motility. These observations support a QS dependent role of LuxS in motility regulation rather than a purely metabolic effect.

The gastrointestinal tract is a dynamic environment where microbes compete for space and nutrients through various mechanisms. Many gut-associated bacteria produce AI-2 via the LuxS enzyme, including a significant proportion of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria, and a few members of the phyla Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria.51,52 Besides, species of the families Barnesiellaceae and Muribaculaceae also contribute to AI-2 production in the gut.53 Being an enteric pathogen and a sensor of the universal communication signal, Salmonella may profit from the AI-2 produced by the gut commensal in the intestinal lumen. This further demonstrates that intra-bacterial communication and interactions might be exploited by pathogens to win the arms race.

Natural fluid flow in the intestine can remove autoinducers, disrupting QS. S. aureus utilizes autoinducers in the crypts (non-existent flow) and enhances pathogenicity by producing enterotoxin B.54 Similarly, Salmonella targets the ileum crypts to invade epithelial cells, ensuring survival and replication. Motility toward the ileum of the intestine is essential, and sensing AI-2 plays a pivotal role in Salmonella pathogenesis. Our results suggest that Salmonella can move toward the AI-2 molecule, and LsrB might work as a chemoreceptor. HilD is the master regulator of motility and the SPI-1 gene. The present study shows that LuxS/AI-2 signaling regulates the hilD gene expression, thereby aiding in motility, attachment, and invasion of epithelial cells in vitro and in vivo conditions. This likely account for the impaired adhesion and invasion of these mutants, as the SPI-1-encoded T3SS system and effector molecule mediate host cell invasion. Our data extend prior reports by linking AI-2 signaling to the regulation of key virulence regulators, including hilD, and SPI-1 genes such as hilA and invF, thereby coupling motility with invasion processes. This is consistent with earlier studies suggesting that AI-2 can functions as a chemo attractant and promotes bacterial aggregation, but our work advances this understanding by identifying LsrB as a potential chemoreceptor and demonstrating how AI-2 signaling integrates chemotaxis, motility, and virulence gene regulation.

Our findings further indicated that accumulation and phosphorylation of AI-2 are necessary for controlling the expression of the pmrD/AB. The pmrD/AB could be regulated by PhoP, resulting in reduced survival of STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, and STM ΔlsrK in vitro in the presence of polymyxin and in the epithelial cells.41,46 Our previous study highlighted the regulatory mechanism of LuxS/AI-2 in controlling the phoP/Q transcription.39 Hence, in such a context, our present study further highlights the broader, interconnected regulatory roles of LuxS/AI-2 in several defense strategies of this pathogen. Importantly, LuxS/AI-2 signaling is maintained within the intracellular niche, underscoring the critical role of bacterial communication in supporting pathogen survival and mediating host immune evasion. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that LuxS/AI-2 signaling controls various critical genes. As previously reported, the regulation of the phoP/Q operon by the LsrR repressor through the promoter binding activity of the repressor, it can be assumed that some of these genetic controls may also involve the LsrR-binding activity.39

Dissection of the LuxS/AI2 signaling pathway and its regulation of cellular processes suggested that there are differential transcriptional responses across AI-2 pathway mutants, highlighting distinct functional roles of its components. Notably, the lsrB mutant exhibited the highest number of downregulated genes, with pronounced repression of flagellar genes, chemotaxis-associated genes, translation-associated and cell surface/membrane genes, indicative of a shift toward a low-growth, energy-conserving physiological state. As LsrB mediates initial AI-2 sensing and uptake, its absence likely disrupts perception of population density cues and prevents activation of downstream regulatory circuits, resulting in widespread transcriptional alterations and a stress-adapted phenotype. The TEM analysis of flagella (Figure 1I) also showed no flagellar structure on STM ΔlsrB, further justifying the regulation. In contrast, the lsrK mutant displayed comparatively limited changes, including changes to the lsr operon, flagellar, and virulence-associated genes, suggesting that AI-2 phosphorylation plays a more confined role in fine-tuning intracellular signaling. Interestingly, both luxS and lsrB mutants showed upregulation of metabolic pathways, suggesting a probable compensatory metabolic rewiring in response to disrupted signaling and/or central metabolic imbalance. Furthermore, specific pathways such as propanediol utilization were selectively downregulated in the luxS mutant, supporting the notion that LuxS influences gene expression not only through QS but also via its role in central metabolism. Unlike lsrB and lsrK, which are involved in AI-2 sensing and processing, LuxS plays a dual role in both QS and central metabolism via the AMC. The propanediol utilization (pdu) pathway supports Salmonella intestinal colonization by enabling the use of microbiota-derived 1,2-propanediol as a carbon and energy source. Coupled with inflammation-induced electron acceptors such as tetrathionate, this pathway enhances anaerobic respiration and promotes competitive expansion over commensal microbiota in the gut.55 This might suggest a justification of strains specific differences in colonization and competition in the gut. Together, these findings support a hierarchical organization within the AI-2 signaling system, wherein signal perception via LsrB exerts broad control over growth-associated transcriptional programs, downstream processing via LsrK provides regulatory fine-tuning, and LuxS integrates metabolic state with signaling outputs to modulate niche-specific pathways in Salmonella.

Our present work also demonstrated that the organ burden of Salmonella was reduced upon treatment of mice with inhibitor BF-8 in combination with low doses of antibiotics ciprofloxacin and ceftazidime. Considering the increasing emergence of antimicrobial-resistant Salmonella strains worldwide, there is a need for alternative approaches to curb infection. In this context, targeting LuxS/AI-2 signaling presents a promising anti-virulence approach, given its role in coordinating multiple regulatory pathways linked to motility, invasion, and intracellular survival. However, it is important to acknowledge that LuxS/AI-2 represents a conserved and widely distributed communication system among both pathogenic and commensal bacteria, and its inhibition may therefore have broader impacts on the gut microbiota. Although, our objective was to evaluate whether BF-8 could potentiate or restore antibiotic efficacy under conditions, where conventional antibiotic treatments become less effective due to increasing antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, while the combinatorial approach appears promising, its broader impact on microbial ecology and resistance development requires further investigation before therapeutic application. We emphasize that our study does not propose LuxS/AI-2 inhibition as a standalone antimicrobial strategy. Notably, our findings demonstrated that the AI-2 inhibitor BF-8 does not exert a direct bactericidal effect on Salmonella, supporting its role as a signaling modulator rather than a conventional antibiotic. Instead, our current results suggest that interference with AI-2 signaling can enhance the efficacy of conventional antibiotics when used at lower doses. Thus, targeting LuxS/AI-2 signaling may be better positioned as an adjunctive therapeutic strategy that complements existing antibiotics, rather than replacing approaches that directly target pathogen-specific virulence mechanisms.

Overall, our study provides evidence for a causal link between AI-2 chemotaxis, gut colonization, and niche segregation of Salmonella strains in the murine gut. It further establishes a link between AI-2 chemotaxis, invasion, and colonization in vivo. We suggest that similar mechanisms of AI-2-mediated host colonization might exist in other chemotactic enteric bacteria, and inhibition of LuxS/AI-2 signaling might open avenues for antibiotic resistance of pathogenic bacteria.

Limitations of the study

Although our findings support a role for LuxS/AI-2 signaling in regulating motility, adhesion, invasion, intracellular replication, and antimicrobial resistance-associated phenotypes in S. Typhimurium, the metabolic and QS functions of LuxS could not be completely separated in the present study. Therefore, some of the observed phenotypes may partially arise from metabolic perturbations associated with LuxS deficiency. In addition, while our data suggest a potential connection between AI-2 signaling and the regulation of HilD and pmr-associated pathways, direct mechanistic evidence establishing this regulatory link remains to be elucidated. Furthermore, the therapeutic implications of BF-8 observed in this study should be considered preliminary, as additional pharmacological characterization, and assessment in clinically relevant infection models are necessary before translational conclusions can be drawn. Importantly, the findings of the present study are limited to the S. Typhimurium 14028s background used in this work, and further studies in additional strains and bacterial species will be necessary to determine the broader applicability of these observations.

Method details

Bacterial gene knockout and strain generation

Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium strain 14028 and isogenic mutants were used for all experiments. luxS, lsrB, lsrK, and lsrR gene knockout and luxS complement strains were made in our previous study.39 All knockouts were generated as described previously using the one-gene-step chromosomal gene inactivation method demonstrated by Datsenko and Wanner.56 Briefly, the STM WT strain was transformed with the pKD46 plasmid expressing the λ-red recombinase system under an arabinose-inducible plasmid. The pKD3 and pKD4 plasmids were used as templates to amplify the chloramphenicol and kanamycin resistance cassette using designed knockout primers. The amplified reaction products were purified using chloroform-isopropanol precipitation. The purified PCR product was transformed into pKD46-containing STM WT expressing λ-red recombinase system. The transformed cells were plated on LB agar with chloramphenicol or kanamycin. The knockout strains were confirmed by PCR using primers corresponding to regions ∼100 bp upstream and downstream of the relevant genes.

Briefly, for the complement strain, the double-digested PCR amplified purified luxS gene (containing BamH1 and HindIII restriction site sequences) was used to clone in double-digested pQE-60. The respective ligated vector was transformed into the STM ΔluxS strains to generate the complement strains. For the reporter plasmid plsr-GFP generation, 256 bp of upstream of lsrA were cloned into the promoter less vector pUA66. Finally, the reporter plasmid was transformed into the STM WT and STM ΔluxS.

Bacterial strains were revived on LB agar, with or without antibiotics, and LB broth cultures of wild-type, knockout, and complemented strains were incubated at 37°C (170 rpm) using an orbital shaker. Whenever necessary, antibiotics were used, including Kanamycin (50 μg/mL), Chloramphenicol (25 μg/mL), and Ampicillin (50 μg/mL).

Cell culture and maintenance

Caco-2 epithelial cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Lonza), supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% sodium pyruvate, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The cells were seeded into the respective cell culture plates for each experiment to conduct intracellular survival assays and intracellular gene expression experiments.

Gentamicin protection assay

Caco-2 cells were seeded in tissue culture plates for infection. Cells were infected with STM WT, STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, STM ΔlsrK, STM ΔlsrR, and STMΔluxS:luxS (log phase culture growing overnight in LB broth). The multiplicity of Infection (MOI) 20 was used for the intracellular survival assay and Salmonella gene expression study (qRT-PCR). Following the infection of STM strains into the cell line, the plate was spun for 5 min at 600 rpm using a Rota-Superspin R–NV swing bucket centrifuge. After that, the plate was incubated for 25 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Then, the infected cells were washed with 1X PBS twice and treated with a 100 μg/mL concentration of gentamycin. After 1 h, the media were changed with a reduced concentration of gentamycin (25 μg/mL) and incubated until time point. The time points 2 h, 6 h, and 16 h were taken for the qRT-PCR sample and 2 h and 16 h for the intracellular survival assay.

DPD supplementation (of 25 μM, 50 μM, 100 μM, and 200 μM DPD) or spent media of STM WT collected at 3 h in LB growing treated STM ΔluxS, and LuxS/AI-2 signaling inhibition,57,58 the inhibitor (Z)-4- bromo-5-(bromomethyllene)-3-methylfuran-2(5H), known as BF-8 treatment, was given to the STM WT culture, which was used for the gentamycin protection assay to study the adhesion, invasion, and intracellular proliferation of STM.

Invasion and intracellular survival assay

The cells were lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS at specific time points post-infection. For the invasion, CFU was determined at 2 h post-infection samples and CFU in pre-inoculum. Percent invasion was calculated by using the formula- [ CFU at 2 h]/[ CFU of pre-inoculum] ×100. For the fold proliferation, CFU was determined at 2 h and 16 h post-infection samples. Fold proliferation was determined using the formula [ CFU at 16 h]/[ CFU at 2 h].

Adhesion assay

Adhesion assay was done as gentamycin protection assay with modification. Infections were given to Caco-2 cells at MOI 20 and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Thereafter, washing with 1X PBS twice to eliminate any loosely adherent bacteria. After lysing the mammalian cells with 0.1% Triton X-100, samples were taken to release the bacteria into the sample. After the collected samples were plated on LB agar plates at the appropriate dilutions and incubated at 37 °C, the CFUs were enumerated. The following formula was used to calculate the percentage adhesion = [(CFU at 10 min post-infection)/(CFU of the pre-inoculum)]× 100.

In vitro motility assay

- 2 μL of bacterial culture was spotted onto the agar plates supplemented with 1% casein enzyme hydrolysate, 0.5% yeast extract, 0.5% NaCl, and 0.5% glucose with 0.3% agar (swim agar plates) and 0.5% agar plates (swarm agar plates). The swim agar plates were incubated at 37°C for 6 h, and swarm agar plates were incubated for 12 h. The diameters of the motility halos were measured at least three angles of three replicate plates, and then images were taken using a BioRad ChemiDoc.

Chemotaxis assay

- A chemotaxis assay was done by using μ-Slide Chemotaxis, ibidi. μm-Slide consists of two distinct opposing liquid chambers or reservoirs separated by a small liquid transition zone of 1 mm. Overnight grown STM WT, STM ΔluxS, and STM ΔlsrB were followed by two washes with motility buffer (MB; 10 mM KPO4, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 67 mM NaCl, pH 7), and then resuspension to a final OD600 to 0.3 in MB supplemented with 3.5% glucose (MBg).21 To reduce metabolic activity, cells were chilled for 20 min. A motility buffer was applied to the transition zone. The first reservoir was completely filled with the bacterial cell suspension, the second reservoir with STM WT spent media (Collected at 3 h of LB growth), synthetic DPD molecule, and the known chemoattractant L-serine. In the experiment, the number of bacteria that transmigrated from the first (right) reservoir into the second (left) reservoir was quantified by CFU analysis and bright-field microscopy (Leica microscope SP8 Falcon, 63X).

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

For the gene expression study in Luria Bertani (LB) media, overnight-grown culture in LB media was subjected to a subculture (at 1:100) in LB. At 3 h, 6 h, 9 h, and 12 h, bacterial cell pellets were collected in TRIzol (from TaKaRa, RNA isoPlus- 9109) and stored at −80°C. Total RNA was isolated by chloroform extraction followed by Isopropanol precipitation. To evaluate quality, RNA was quantified using NanoDrop and examined on a 2% agarose gel. To produce cDNA, 2 μg of RNA sample was treated with DNase I at 37°C for 1 h (TaKaRa), followed by heating for 10 min at 65°C. Then, DNA-free RNA samples were used to make cDNA by using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit provided by TaKaRa (Cat# RR037A).

The gene expression of Salmonella upon Infection in Caco-2 infection was given at MOI 20. At 2 h, 6 h, and 16 h post-infection, samples were collected in TRIzol (from TaKaRa, RNA isoPlus- 9109) and kept at −80°C. Total RNA isolation and cDNA were synthesized using a similar protocol described in the kit provided, TRIzol (from TaKaRa, RNA isoPlus-9109) and TaKaRa (Cat# RR037A) reagents according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The list of expressions Primer is provided in the Table S1. Quantitative PCR was carried out using an SYBR Green Q-PCR kit (Takara), and 16S rRNA was used as an internal control. Ct values were first normalised to 16S rRNA to calculate ΔCt and relative gene expression was determined using the ΔΔCt method, with the 3 h time point (LB culture) and 2 h post-infection (Caco-2 infection) used as the respective reference conditions for normalization. For comparative analyses, expression levels were calculated relative to STM WT, which served as the reference strain. Absolute expression was calculated by 2−ΔCt.

Promoter activity assay

The promoter regions of the target genes (motA, fliC, cheY and hilD) were PCR-amplified from STM WT and cloned upstream of the promoter-less GFP gene in the low-copy reporter vector pUA66. The resulting recombinant plasmids were transformed into the STM WT and knockout strains. Overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 into fresh LB medium and grown at 37°C with shaking. At the time points (3 h, 6 h, 9 h, and 12 h), bacterial growth was measured as optical density at 600 nm (OD600), and GFP fluorescence was recorded using a fluorescence plate reader (excitation at 488 nm, emission at 510 nm). Promoter activity was expressed as normalized fluorescence intensity (GFP/OD600).

RNA sequencing and analysis

Bacteria were grown till mid-log phase (4 h in LB media, OD600 ≈ 0.4) and RNA extraction was performed using Qiagen RNeasy Mini kit (Cat. No. 74104) with a few modifications. The RNA samples were quantified on Nanodrop and Qubit flurometer using appropriate standards. Appropriate dilutions were loaded on a High sensitivity RNA screen tape and run on Agilent Tape station 4200 to determine the RNA integrity number (RIN). For Library preparation, 1000 ng of RNA sample was used for ribodepletion by using NEB Next rRNA depletion kit Bacteria (Cat. no. NEB E7850X), which includes Probe hybridization of RNA, RNase H digestion, DNase 1 digestion according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library prep kit for Illumina (Cat. no. E7760L), was used to construct double-stranded cDNA libraries from the rRNA depleted RNA, using RNA fragmentation, first strand cDNA synthesis with random primers, second strand cDNA synthesis, end repair of double stranded cDNA, adapter ligation, removal of excess adapter using sample purification beads, PCR enrichment of adapter ligated DNA and clean-up of PCR products as instructed by the manufacturer. The cleaned libraries were quantified on Qubitflurometer and appropriate dilutions loaded on a D1000 screen tape to determine the size range of the fragments and the average library size.

The original raw data from the Illumina platform is transformed into sequenced reads by base calling. Raw data are recorded in a FASTQ file containing sequence information (reads) and corresponding sequencing quality information. The quality of the reads was assessed using Fast QC v 0.11.9 before proceeding with the downstream analysis.59 The reads were passed through SortMeRNAv4.3.760 and were used against all eight rRNA databases provided to filter and remove rRNA reads. The trimmed reads were then passed through fastp61 to remove adaptors, low-quality bases (Q < 36 bp). For mapping and quantification, Salmonella Typhimurium 14028s reference genome and annotation were downloaded from (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/000/022/165/).

GCF_000022165.1_ASM2216v1/). HISAT262 was used to create the reference index, and the trimmed & filtered reads were mapped to the indexed genome using HISAT2. Quantification was performed with feature Count.63 A total of 4728 features were assigned in the raw count file. The raw count file is made into raw counts for individual sets, and the rest of the differential analysis is done according to the sets. The library sizes are plotted for individual sets. The transcripts with low read counts (< count of at least 10 for a minimum of 2 samples) were removed. The filtered read counts were normalized by transforming to the rlog transformation function (rlog) of DESeq264 and are used for further analysis. Differential gene expression was analyzed using the DESeq function from the DESeq2 package with a false discovery rate (FDR) cut-off of ≤0.05 and a minimum expression log2-fold change (FC) of ≥2/1. The counts are illustrated with a volcano plot. The top genes with the largest log2fold change are plotted as a heatmap.

Transmission electron microscopy

Flagella were visualized using the protocol described previously.35,65 Briefly, overnight bacterial cultures were subcultured in LB media and incubated until it reached an OD of 0.1. During the subculture, STM WT was treated with AI-2 signaling inhibitor BF-8, and STM ΔluxS with STM WT spent media and synthetic DPD. The bacterial cultures were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The bacterial cells were washed with 1X PBS twice, and finally, the cells were resuspended in 100 μL of 1X PBS. 10 μL of the cell suspension was added on a copper grid, air dried, stained with 1% uranyl acetate for 30 s, and visualized under a transmission electron microscope (Thermo-Fischer Scientific).

Immunofluorescence

After appropriate post-infection incubation time in the adhesion assay, the media was removed, and the cells were washed with 1X PBS and fixed with 3.5% Paraformaldehyde for 10 min. The cells were then washed with 1X PBS, incubated with the required rabit-raised anti-Salmonella primary antibody in a buffer containing 2% BSA, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4°Cmin. After washing with 1X PBS, the secondary antibody tagged to a fluorochrome was added and incubated. For Caco-2 epithelial cells, FM4-64 dye was used. The coverslips were washed with PBS, mounted on a clean glass slide using mounting media containing an anti-fade reagent, and observed under the confocal microscope (Leica microscope SP8 falcon, at 63X oil immersion).

For the FliC study, strains were subcultured and grown in LB media supplemented with BF-8, DPD, and STM WT spent media appropriately until the log phase of growth (OD 0.1). After washing with 1X PBS, the samples were smeared onto a clean glass slide, air-dried, and stained as described using a specific antibody in the buffer.

Mice intestine histochemistry samples were incubated with the required rabbit-raised anti-Salmonella primary antibody in a buffer containing 0.01% saponin and 2% BSA, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4°Cmin. After washing with 1X PBS, the secondary antibody tagged to a fluorochrome was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The dye DAPI was used to stain the intestinal nucleus. 40X oil immersion was used to study the mice intestine histopathology (Zeiss 710 microscope, at 63X oil immersion).

Polymyxin treatment and survival assay

Polymyxin MIC was determined by using microbroth dilution methods. Polymyxin treatment (sub-MIC concentration 0.5 μg/mL) was given to the overnight STM strains (107 cfu) in LB media. At 3 h post-treatment, CFU was determined by spread plate methods. For the time-dependent survival assay, we collected the treated sample at 3 h, 6 h, 9 h, and 12 h and calculated the CFU. For the mRNA expression study, samples were collected during mid-log phase (6 h) in Trizol and kept at −80°C for further RNA isolation.

Flow cytometry

Reporter strain STM WT-plsr-GFP and STM ΔluxS-plsr-GFP were grown in LB media, and samples were taken out at different time points and fixed with 3.5% paraformaldehyde. The positive GFP percentage population was determined in the sample by using BD FACS Verse.

In vivo animal experiment

Male C57BL/6, aged 5 to 6 weeks, were obtained from the Central Animal Facility, IISc.

Invasion assay

- 5–6 weeks old C57BL/6 mice were infected by orally gavaging 107 CFU of STM WT, STM ΔluxS, STM ΔlsrB, STM ΔlsrK, STM ΔlsrR, and STM ΔluxS:luxS. Following 6 h of post-infection, the intestinal tissue was collected, thoroughly washed and homogenized and CFU was enumerated on differential and selective SS agar by serial dilution followed by plating. BF-8 (10 μg/mL) treated STM WT culture and DPD supplementation (100 μM DPD) or STM WT spent media treated STM ΔluxS was also used for the invasion study in mice.

In vivo, competition assay

- Competition assay was done between STM WT and STMΔluxS/STMΔlsrB/STMΔlsrK. Infection was given by oral gavage with 107 CFU/mL of culture in a 1:1 ratio. Post five days of infection, the intestine (Peyer’s patches), MLN (mesenteric lymph node), spleen, liver, and blood were aseptically extracted to examine the colonization in the organs. The CFU was enumerated on differential and selective Salmonella-Shigella (SS) agar and respective antibiotics containing LB agar plates. The competitive index was calculated for a WT strain and knockout strains by dividing the ratio between CFU (Knockout strain) and CFU (STM WT) by the ratio of both strains in the inoculum.

In vivo infection and BF-8/antibiotics treatment

- 5–6 weeks old C57BL/6 mice were infected by orally gavaging 107 CFU of STM WT. Post 3rd day of infection, one cohort of mice was sacrificed for the organ burden of Salmonella. The rest of the cohort of mice were treated with either 2 mg/kg of body weight of BF-8, ciprofloxacin, ceftazidime, or a combination of BF-8 with both antibiotics intraperitoneally for three consecutive days. After the 3rd day of treatment, mice were sacrificed the next day, and the organ burden was determined by CFU enumeration.

Mice survival assay

The mice were given 107CFU of STM WT (overnight grown cultures) orally to compare the survival and weight alterations of mice post-infection and BF-8/antibiotics treatment. Post 3rd day of infection, mice were treated with either 2 mg/kg of mouse weight of BF-8, ciprofloxacin, ceftazidime, or a combination of BF-8 with both antibiotics, intraperitoneally for three consecutive days. The mice were observed every day to determine their survival and weight, and the results were expressed as a percentage of survival (Kaplan-meier curve) and weight reduction.

Quantification and statistical analysis

As mentioned in the figure legends, each experiment has been independently repeated two to five times. GraphPad Prism 8.4.3 was utilized for all statistical analyses. As the figure legends state, the statistical analyses included an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test, One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc test, and two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. A non-parametric one-way ANOVA (Kruskal-Wallis) test with Dunn’s post-hoc test was performed for the animal experiment. P-values less than 0.05 were regarded as significant. The analysis is presented as mean ± SD with information on group sizes, and p values ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗p < 0.05 mentioned in the respective figure legends.
