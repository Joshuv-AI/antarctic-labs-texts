Uranyl Recognition through Cucurbit[7]uril-Bipyridinium Carboxylate Pseudorotaxanes as Supramolecular Chelators
Authors: Jing-yang Wang, Qun-yan Wu, Yuan-yuan Liang, Kong-qiu Hu, Li-yong Yuan, Cai-shan Jiao, Meng Zhang, Lei Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exploration of new ligands for actinide recognition is desirable for developing more efficient separation processes, and pseudorotaxane-type supramolecular complexes have the attributes of simple synthesis and structural adaptability. In this work, we propose two novel CB[7]-based bipyridinium carboxylate pseudorotaxane ligands and evaluate their potential as efficient chelators for uranyl recognition. A combination of characterization methods including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), fluorescence analysis, mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), infrared spectroscopy (IR), and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD), together with theoretical calculation, demonstrates the feasibility of such a kind of pseudorotaxane chelator for uranyl recognition via host–guest synergistic chelation. This work establishes a new type of cucurbituril-carboxylate supramolecular chelator and will inspire the development of more other supramolecular ligands for actinide recognition.

Introduction

Since the advent of uranium-based fission reactors in the nuclear industry, the research on actinide chemistry has attracted increasing attention. − For example, in order to realize the separation and recovery of spent fuel, the extraction methods such as the PUREX (Plutonium Uranium Recovery by Extraction) process , using the tributyl phosphate (TBP) extractant have been developed. Meanwhile, there is also a growing interest in pursuing efficient recovery of minor actinides from PUREX raffinate. , It is believed that the exploration of new ligands (extractants) will help to achieve high-efficiency identification of actinide ions and develop more efficient actinide separation processes. −

To improve the binding ability and recognition efficiency of ligands to actinides, one important approach is to develop chelating ligands by increasing coordination sites − (Figure a), such as 2,9-diamide-1,10-phenanthroline (DAPhen) with N, N, O, and O-tetradentate binding sites, which has multiple binding interactions with target ions. The binding strength can be further improved by introducing a preorganized macrocyclic backbone, − which has also been used in ligand design for actinides, as exemplified by a macrocyclic chelator incorporating intra- and extra-ring terephthalamide coordination units (Figure b). Most recently, it is reported that supramolecular complexes, , such as cucurbituril-based pseudorotaxanes (Figure c), can serve as supramolecular ligands for capturing different uranyl species through varying coordination modes. Unlike cumbersome synthesis steps required for further functionalization of a macrocyclic ligand, the functionalization of a pseudorotaxane complex, on the basis of macrocyclic units, is through introducing a guest moiety via supramolecular inclusion, which is taken to be rather simple and efficient. More importantly, the mechanically interlocked structure between host and guest components in pseudorotaxane enables excellent conformational adaptivity, which helps to meet the coordination requirements of target ions.

Typical organic chelators for actinide coordination: (a) preorganized 2,9-diamide-1,10-phenanthroline with different substituents; (b) a macrocyclic ligand incorporating intra- and extra-ring terephthalamide coordination units; (c) multidentate pseudorotaxane ligand DPO@CB[7] (DPO, 4,4′-bipyridine-N,N′-dioxide); and (d) CB[7]-based [2]­pseudorotaxanes used in this work involving the bipyridinium carboxylate guest component with a “N”-shape p-xylene spacer and terminal carboxyl functional groups.

For a pseudorotaxane chelator, cucurbituril, such as cucurbit[6]­uril (abbreviated as CB[6]) or cucurbit[7]­uril (abbreviated as CB[7]), can serve as an ideal macrocyclic host due to many carbonyl groups on its both ends that are capable of metal coordination. − It should be noted that the binding of guests with cucurbituril macrocycles occurs primarily through encapsulation of the aromatic rings by the cucurbiturils’ hydrophobic cavity, while the terminal carbonyls hardly participate in this encapsulation, rendering them to be available for uranyl coordination if the total spatial orientation is preferred. Besides, of equal importance is the guest component that could provide functionalities of both supramolecular inclusion and metal coordination. Usually, alkyl diammonium or bipyridinium units contribute to the functionality of supramolecular inclusion by cucurbituril hosts, while additional coordination groups like O-donors are needed for metal coordination. Actually, the metal coordination process of organic ligands with conformationally flexible or rigid skeletons always involves an entropy penalty, and such an unfavorable entropy penalty in entropy should be efficiently compensated by enthalpic changes by increasing the binding strength of coordination groups with metal centers. Since carboxylate groups serve negative-charged O-donors bearing good capability for uranyl coordination, it is the functional group that has been mostly modified on the guest molecules of pseudorotaxane chelators, such as C6BPCA3@CB[6] and C7BPCA3@CB[6] in situ formed under hydrothermal conditions. However, a direct observation of coordination interaction of carboxylate-involved pseudorotaxane chelators with uranyl in ambient conditions is still lacked. Herein, we propose a new kind of CB[7]-based pseudorotaxane ligands with bipyridinium carboxylic acids as guest components, where a p-xylene spacer with a “N”-shape conformation, instead of the hexylidene or heptylidene spacer in CB[6]-based pseudorotaxanes, matches to CB[7] containing a larger cavity size and is functionalized with terminal carboxyl groups for subsequent metal coordination (Figure d). In this work, the interactions of such pseudorotaxane ligands, (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])­(NO3)2 and (HXyBPCA3@CB[7])­(NO3)2, with uranyl were studied comprehensively by combining different techniques including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), fluorescence analysis, mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), infrared spectroscopy (IR), and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD). Notably, crystal structures of the corresponding uranyl complexes were determined, which reveal that both pseudorotaxane ligands can serve as uranyl chelators by host–guest synergistic chelating. Furthermore, bonding behaviors between uranyl and both pseudorotaxane ligands were analyzed in detail by theoretical calculations.

Experimental Section

Materials and Methods

1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE III (500 MHz, Bruker, Switzerland) with deuterium oxide (D2O) as a solvent. ESI-MS spectra were acquired with a Bruker AmaZon SL ion trap mass spectrometer (Bruker, USA). Solid-state fluorescence spectra were measured on a Hitachi F-4600 fluorescence spectrophotometer with excited wavelength at 420 nm (for uranyl) or 334 nm (for the pseudorotaxane ligand) with a scan speed of 60 nm/min. The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded from KBr pellets in the range of 4000–400 cm–1 on a Bruker Tensor 27 spectrometer.

Syntheses of Uranyl Compounds U_ XyBPCA4@CB[7] and U_ XyBPCA3@CB[7]

Caution! Due to the radioactive and chemically toxic nature of uranyl chloride and nitrate, suitable precautions for safety and protection must be taken, and all operations should follow the criteria while handling these substances. (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])­(NO3)2 (0.040 g, 0.024 mmol) was dissolved in water (1 mL) and mixed with UO2Cl2 (0.010 mg, 0.029 mmol) in a stainless-steel autoclave with the initial pH of 2.40. The mixture was sealed and kept at 150 °C for 24 h. After the mixture was gradually cooled to room temperature, light yellow prismatic crystals of U_ XyBPCA4@CB[7] that were suitable for single-crystal X-ray characterization were obtained. Yield: 13.0 mg, 56.0% based on the pseudorotaxane ligand. Similarly, yellow block crystals of U_ XyBPCA3@CB[7] could be synthesized from (HXyBPCA3@CB[7])­(NO3)2 and UO2Cl2 in the presence of 30 μL of NaOH aqueous solution (1 M).

Single-Crystal X-Ray Diffraction Measurements

Single-crystal diffraction patterns of all compounds reported in this work were recorded by using a Bruker D8 VENTURE X-ray diffractometer with a Mo Kα X-ray source (λ = 0.71073 Å). All crystal structures were solved by means of direct methods (SHELXL-97) and refined with full-matrix least-squares on SHELXL-2014 based on the Olex2 software package. All non-hydrogen atoms were refined with anisotropic displacement parameters, and SIMU and ISOR were used to constrain the displacement parameters of the phenyl groups and solvent water molecules and even out the electron density associated with disordered portions of the moieties. The carbon-bound hydrogen atoms were placed at calculated positions, and all hydrogen atoms were treated as riding atoms with an isotropic displacement parameter equal to 1.2 times that of the parent atoms. Crystallographic data in this work have been deposited with Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre, and the CCDC numbers are 2466386–2466390, including 2466386 for (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])­(NO3)2, 2466387 for (HXyBPCN3@CB[7])­Br2, 2466388 for (HXyBPCA3@CB[7])­(NO3)2, 2466389 for U_ XyBPCA4@CB[7], and 2466390 for U_ XyBPCA3@CB[7].

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Pseudorotaxane Ligands

The CB[7]-based pseudorotaxane ligand in the form of nitrate, (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])­(NO3)2, was first synthesized from isonicotinic acid followed by supramolecular encapsulation of CB[7] (Figures S1 and S2). Meanwhile, to study the effect of molecular configuration on uranyl coordination, the other pseudorotaxane isomer, (HXyBPCA3@CB[7])­(NO3)2, with a different guest configuration was also used, which starts from a 3-cyano precursor (Figure S3) to reduce possible steric hindrance of the carboxyl group upon supramolecular inclusion by CB[7] and requires further transformation of cyano groups in the (XyBPCN3@CB[7])­Br2 intermediate (Figures S4 and S5) to final carboxylic acid forms. Molecular structures of both pseudorotaxane ligands were confirmed by single-crystal diffraction characterization (Figure ).

Interactions of Pseudorotaxane Ligands with Uranyl

With both pseudorotaxanes in hand, we first studied their interaction with the uranyl ion (Figure ). 1H NMR analysis shows that the addition of an uranyl ion induces a upfield shift for the chemical environments of Ha and Hb in the carboxylpyridinium ring of (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])2+ pseudorotaxane, and this trend becomes more pronounced after carboxyl groups undergo deprotonation in the presence of NaOH (Figure a,b), suggesting possible coordination of the carboxyl group with uranyl. In addition, the interaction between uranyl and the pseudorotaxane ligand is evidenced by fluorescence analysis, which shows that the luminescence of the (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])­(NO3)2 unit decreased with the increasing amount of uranyl (Figure c). However, it is a pity that the inherent fluorescence of uranyl is quenched by the pseudorotaxane, which makes it impossible to determine the binding constant through the fluorescence titration experiment.

Probing the interaction of (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])2+ with the uranyl ion: (a,b) 1H NMR of (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])2+ in the presence of uranyl and/or NaOH in the range of 3.5–9.5 (a) and 8.4–9.2 (b); (c) changes in the fluorescence signal of (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])­(NO3)2 as the amount of uranyl added increases; (d) ESI-MS spectra of a mixture of (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])­(NO3)2 and uranyl dissolved in water (L: (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])2+); and (e) a proposed coordination model of the deprotonated pseudorotaxane chelator XyBPCA4@CB[7] to the uranyl ion.

Further characterization by mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) reveals two characteristic peaks at 593.75 and 599.38 (Figure d), which correspond to a uranyl complex of the pseudorotaxane ligand ([HXyBPCA4@CB[7]-H]+[UO2])3+ and its monohydrate, respectively. Subsequently, the characteristic signal at 593.75 was further isolated and analyzed through a cascade (MS/MS) mode, which gave a series of emerging mass peaks corresponding to fragment ions (HXyBPCA4@CB[7]: 225.88, 579.38, 716.13, and 725.13) (Figure S6). Detailed assignment of these secondary mass peaks (Figure S7) suggests that, even if one carboxyl group in the bipyridinium dicarboxylate guest molecule (i.e., [HXyBPCA4]2+) is broken (m/z 225.88), uranyl can still effectively bind to the pseudorotaxane unit (m/z 579.38); furthermore, when the decomposition of the guest’s backbone (m/z ∼225.88) leads to an disintegration of the pseudorotaxane structure, uranyl is trapped by macrocyclic CB[7] (the peaks at 716.13 and 725.13). The above characterization confirmed the good binding ability of the CB[7]-based pseudorotaxane ligands used here to uranyl ions. Infrared (IR) spectroscopy analysis also gives characteristic vibrational signals of [O = UO] corresponding to uranyl near 920 cm–1 (Figure S8). Considering possible binding sites in deprotonated pseudorotaxane XyBPCA4@CB[7], a coordination model of XyBPCA4@CB[7] to uranyl is proposed (Figure e). Similarly, the interaction of [HXyBPCA3@CB[7]2+ with uranyl is also confirmed by combining 1H NMR (Figure S9), ESI-MS (Figures S10 and S11), and IR (Figure S12).

Crystal Structures of Uranyl Compounds of Pseudorotaxane Chelators

Motivated by possible interactions between the pseudorotaxane chelators and uranyl ion, we further managed to acquire the single-crystal structures of such pseudorotaxane-based coordination systems. Fortunately, two uranyl complexes with CB[7]-based pseudorotaxane ligands, i.e., U_ XyBPCA4@CB[7] and U_ XyBPCA3@CB[7], were obtained (Figures S13 and a,c).

Crystal structures of U_ XyBPCA4@CB[7] and U_ XyBPCA3@CB[7]: (a) a complete picture of the chemical structure of U_XyBPCA4@CB[7] consisting of a pair of XyBPCA4@CB[7] pseudorotaxane units and the monomeric uranyl center; (b) local coordination structure showing the chelating of uranyl by XyBPCA4@CB[7] (only one in spite of a certain kind of the central symmetry disorder); (c) a complete picture of the chemical structure of U_XyBPCA3@CB[7] consisting of a pair of XyBPCA4@CB[7] pseudorotaxane units and the hydroxyl-bridged dimeric uranyl center; (d) local coordination structure showing the chelating of uranyl by XyBPCA3@CB[7] (two uranyl centers are equivalent in chemical structure); and (e) changes in 1H NMR spectra before and after uranyl coordination for U_XyBPCA4@CB[7].

As expected, it is found that the pseudorotaxane unit serves as a bidentate chelator of uranyl in both uranyl compounds, whether a mononuclear uranyl center or a binuclear uranyl center is chelated (Figure b,d). Specifically, in U_XyBPCA4@CB[7], each uranyl center with a certain degree of disorder (probably attributed to partial occupation of the uranyl center) is bound by a pair of center-symmetrical XyBPCA4@CB[7] pseudorotaxane ligands and two additional chloride ions, giving a deformed hexagonal bipyramidal geometry (Figure S14a). Bond distances between uranyl and bidentate XyBPCA4@CB[7] pseudorotaxane (Table S3) are 2.244(12) Å for U1–O3 (an oxygen atom from the carboxyl of the XyBPCA4 guest molecule) and 2.788(11) Å for U1–O7 (an oxygen atom from the carbonyl of CB[7]), respectively, where the latter one that employs the carbonyl group of CB[7] to coordinate with uranyl is much longer than the former one based on a carboxylate group. Besides, another chloride ion is involved in the coordination sphere of the uranyl center with the bond distance (U1–Cl1) of 2.526(17) Å.

Similarly, the coordination behavior of the pseudorotaxane ligand in U_XyBPCA3@CB[7] (Figure S14b) resembles that observed in U_XyBPCA4@CB[7], though a hydroxyl-bridged dimeric uranyl unit is present due to uranyl hydrolysis and oligomerization at elevated temperatures. Considering the differences of these two pseudorotaxane isomers in the molecular structure (4-carboxyl vs 3-carboxyl), the differences between these two uranyl structures seem to be reasonable. The bond distances between the uranyl and XyBPCA3@CB[7] pseudorotaxane chelator are 2.328(4) Å (U1–O4) and 2.505(4) Å (U1–O8), respectively, and the other ones among the equatorial coordination sphere of uranyl are 2.352(4) Å (U1–O3), 2.360(4) Å (U1–O3′), and 2.690(5) Å (U1–Cl1). Interestingly, the U–O bond between uranium and the carbonyl group of CB[7] in U_XyBPCA3@CB[7] is shorter than that observed in U_XyBPCA4@CB[7], which should be closely related to the geometry of the pseudorotaxane chelator. Specifically, the 3-substituted carboxyl group of the bipyridinium guest molecule is spatially closer to the terminal carbonyl groups of CB[7] with a shortest distance of 3.169(4) Å) (Figure S15), which facilitates its participation in the coordination of uranyl together with CB[7]. On the other hand, both distances of uranyl-carbonyl bonds in U_XyBPCA4@CB[7] and U_XyBPCA3@CB[7] are slightly larger than those for uranyl complexes with cucurbit[6]­uril (∼2.38 Å) or cucurbit[7]­uril (∼2.43 Å) but is consistent to that for C7BPCA3@CB[6] pseudorotaxane (∼2.52 Å), which suggests the restraining effect of the adjacent carboxyl group coordinated to uranyl.

The involvement of uranyl in U_ XyBPCA4@CB[7] and U_ XyBPCA3@CB[7] was further confirmed by a series of characterization results. The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of these two uranyl compounds show characteristic vibration peaks of the [UO] bond at 914 and 920 cm–1, respectively (Figure S16). ESI-MS analyses of crystals of U_ XyBPCA4@CB[7] and U_ XyBPCA3@CB[7] dissolved in water afford m/z peaks integrating both components of the pseudorotaxane and uranyl units (Figure S17), though signals reflecting the whole components of such uranyl compounds could not be acquired due to ionization-induced partial decomposition of the whole compound during the ESI-MS analysis process. Since the pattern of uranyl coordination is preserved after crystal samples of U_ XyBPCA4@CB[7] and U_ XyBPCA3@CB[7] dissolved in an aqueous solution, the changes in 1H NMR spectra before and after uranyl coordination for these two uranyl compounds are further analyzed (Figures e and S18). It can be seen that, after the chelation of the uranyl ion, the chemical environment of the pyridinium group at one end of the guest molecule changes, and such a coordination-induced effect is more pronounced for XyBPCA3@CB[7] than its p-isomer.

It is noticed that although the accurate binding coefficient, unfortunately, cannot be obtained currently due to component multiplicity of the supramolecular system, the combination of above-mentioned characterization methods including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), fluorescence analysis, mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), infrared spectroscopy (IR), and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) can also provide valid evidence on the binding between uranyl and pseudorotaxane ligands. Moreover, uranyl concentrations (0.5–10 mmol/L) used here for experimental research resemble those dissolved in HNO3 solutions during spent fuel reprocessing (U: hundreds of grams per liter and Pu: grams per liter), and hence, it is believed that the exploration of the pseudorotaxane ligand’s potential in spent fuel reprocessing could offer a valuable reference for the separation of actinides during spent fuel reprocessing.

Theoretical Calculations

To understand the bonding behavior between the pseudorotaxane ligands and uranyl, theoretical calculations were performed to analyze typical coordination bonds around uranium in U_ XyBPCA4@CB[7] and U_ XyBPCA3@CB[7] using model structures extracted from their crystal structures (Figure S19). The results of Mayer bond orders (MBOs) and Wiberg bond indices (WBIs) are consistent with the change trend of bond distances, and MBOs and WBIs of bonds between uranium and the carboxyl groups (U1–O3 in U_XyBPCA4@CB[7]: 0.598 (MBO) and 0.626 (WBI); U1–O4 in U_XyBPCA3@CB[7]: 0.446­(MBO) and 0.485­(WBI)) are larger than those of bonds between uranium and carbonyl groups from CB[7] (U1–O7 in U_XyBPCA4@CB[7]: 0.217 (MBO) and 0.184 (WBI); U1–O8 in U_XyBPCA3@CB[7]: 0.241 (MBO) and 0.293 (WBI)). Conclusion

In summary, two CB[7]-based bipyridinium carboxylate pseudorotaxane ligands, (HXyBPCA4@CB[7])­(NO3)2 and (HXyBPCA3@CB[7])­(NO3)2, have been prepared and evaluated as new uranyl chelators. A combination of characterization techniques including NMR, ESI-MS, fluorescence analysis, IR, and single-crystal diffraction, together with theoretical calculation, demonstrates the potential of such pseudorotaxane ligands for uranyl recognition via host–guest synergistic chelation. This work expands the types of cucurbituril-based supramolecular chelators and can certainly encourage the development of more functional ligands for coordination recognition of actinides. Further work in the future will be conducted on enhancing the binding capacity for actinides, assessing recognition selectivity for actinides versus competing ions, and verifying separation performance of such pseudorotaxane-based ligands.
