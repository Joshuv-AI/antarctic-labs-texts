Implementation and results of paired vagus nerve stimulation (Vivistim) program for neurorestoration following ischemic stroke: A single-center experience
Authors: Anthony K. Nwankwo, Shashank N. Patil, Micaela C. Nelson, Taylor G. Burch, Ketan R. Bulsara, Christopher R. Conner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Vivistim, otherwise known as vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) paired with rehabilitation (paired VNS) for chronic upper extremity (UE) deficits post stroke, has been increasingly utilized. We present our 2-year institutional implementation of paired VNS.Methods: Patients who underwent paired VNS at our institution within the first 2 years of implementation were included. Following implantation, patients underwent ≥6 weeks of paired VNS. Fugl–Meyer assessment for UE (FMA-UE) and Wolf-Motor Function Tests (WMFT), both of which assess UE motor function, were obtained preoperatively and post paired VNS.Results: Twenty of 21 patients were implanted without complication. The mean age was 63 years, and 81% were male. Mean improvement in FMA-UE was 10 (n = 16) points and 0.7 (n = 2) points on WMFT, demonstrating a clinically meaningful response defined by a 6- or 0.4-point improvement on FMA-UE and WMFT, respectively. All patients reported satisfaction with paired VNS. One patient suffered a surgical site infection resulting in explant. There were no other adverse events. Our institution received full reimbursement from all insurers, both commercial and public.Conclusion: Despite distinct methodologies, retrospective analysis of our institution’s paired VNS experience demonstrates a promising 2–3× improvement in functional outcomes similar to VNS-REHAB. Critical factors potentially contributing to our success include initiation of a monthly multidisciplinary conference, outreach to local occupational therapy, physiatry practices, and stroke awareness groups. Our institution’s results provide external validation and demonstrate the generalizability of paired VNS for the robust VNS-REHAB trial. Paired VNS implementation proved clinically impressive and economically profitable for our institution, suggesting adoption is sensible.

INTRODUCTION

Approximately 80% of individuals with acute stroke experience upper limb motor impairment, and 50–60% will have ongoing deficits 6 months later.[2,4] These impairments can significantly affect daily activities, social participation, and overall quality of life.[11] Notably, 20% of stroke survivors report extremely poor quality of life at both 1- and 5-years post-stroke, compared to only 3% of matched controls.[12,13]

To address these challenges, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) paired with targeted rehabilitative therapy (paired VNS) has emerged as a promising intervention for improving upper limb function post ischemic stroke. The 2021 VNSREHAB trial, a pivotal, triple-blinded, sham-controlled randomized clinical trial, demonstrated that when compared to control, paired VNS, otherwise known as Vivistim, resulted in a two-fold improvement for both the primary Fugl–Meyer assessment for upper extremity (FMA-UE) and the secondary Wolf motor function test (WMFT) outcome measures immediately after the 1st day of in-clinic therapy. On day 90, following completion of in-clinic therapy and home activation, a clinically meaningful response on WMFT, defined by a 0.4-point increase, and FMA-UE was defined as a 6-point increase, of which was associated with a 50% increase in UE function, was achieved in nearly 50% of the VNS group. These clinical improvements persisted after 1 year with a reported responder rate of 66.2.%.[2,5] Despite encouraging clinical trial results, the Vivistim system remains a relatively new practice, with limited patient exposure and real-world implementation. Thus, further study exploring the transition from clinical trials with strict criteria to institutional implementation for traditional clinical settings that emphasize treating diverse patient populations is warranted. The following is a retrospective observational cohort study of our institution’s experience adopting paired VNS in patients recovering from ischemic stroke.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This is a single institution’s retrospective experience of paired VNS implementation for patients with moderate to severe UE impairment at least 6 months post ischemic stroke. All patients who underwent paired VNS at our program fell within the on-label guidelines for U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved (FDA-approved) Vivistim treatment; each patient was provided informed consent before undergoing elective treatment. Institutional Review board (IRB) approval was obtained for this retrospective institutional implementation review project.

Eligibility criteria, screening, and baseline assessments

All patients who underwent Paired VNS therapy within the 2 years of institutional implementation were included in this study. Relevant ICD codes were used to appropriately identify patients for retrospective review. Patients either self-referred, many of whom found information through the surgeon’s website, or received a consultation referral from a physician with whom they are currently established. During the initial consultation visit, patients were screened via thorough medical history and full neurological exam. Critical qualifications included no history of left vagotomy, at least 6 months post-ischemic stroke with documented persistent UE deficits, and absence of paresis or severe spasticity (defined by Ashworth ≥3) that significantly inhibits the ability to perform rehabilitation assessments (FMA-UE and/or WMFT) or inhibits UE movement. Additional criteria for paired VNS clearance are listed in Table 1. Patients who remained potential candidates were referred to occupational therapy for baseline FMA-UE assessments. Persistent moderate-to-severe UE deficit after ischemic stroke was defined as an FMA-UE score <50, per FDA guidelines. Of note, the VNS-REHAB clinical trial methodology only included patients with a pre-therapy baseline FMA-UE score between 20 and 50.[2,6] The trial used FMA- UE as a baseline, follow-up, and primary functional outcome measure, whereas WMFT was a secondary functional outcome measures.[2,6,7] Patients with clinically apparent deficits that scored above 50 on FMA-UE were deemed ineligible for treatment; our institution elected to use the WMFT as a baseline assessment, instead of demonstrating functional impairment not captured in FMA-UE that may improve with Vivistim. Once patients were deemed appropriate candidates, the institution and surgeon worked together to obtain insurance coverage, while patients underwent a preoperative work-up. All patients were informed about paired VNS, and consented before operation.

Device and implantation

The Vivistim device has two implantable components, including an implantable pulse generator and an electrode lead. The non-implantable system includes a stroke application and programming software and a wireless transmitter that allows trained medical professionals to program stimulation parameters and deliver VNS during rehabilitation therapy. Following each participant’s consultation, surgical consent, and pre-implantation physical exam, the participant underwent implantation with the Vivistim device. The implantation procedure and postoperative discharge fell in line with aforementioned studies.[2,6,7]

In-clinic rehabilitation therapy

Once patients recovered from the VNS device implantation procedure, and were cleared by the surgeon at the 2-week postoperative visit, the in-clinic paired VNS sessions were started. These sessions typically occurred 3 times/week over the course of 6 weeks. A programmer set the device stimulation parameter before the patients’ first rehabilitation session. Typical device settings were 0.8 mA current with 30 Hz frequency and 0.5 s stimulation pulse width.[6,7] After the standard 6-week in-clinic rehabilitation therapy, most of the patients continued paired VNS via personal choice or per occupational therapist recommendation.

Out of clinic therapy

Once cleared for in-clinic therapy, and the VNS device was programmed, patients were permitted to perform self-guided out-of-clinic therapy by self-activating the VNS with a magnet swipe across the IPG. Our institution encouraged patients to self-activate the device, up to a maximum of 8 times each day, just before engaging in hobbies, exercises, or activities at home or in the community. This practice was unlike the VNS-REHAB methodology, in which participants were restricted from self-activating the device until completion of the in-clinic rehabilitation protocol, and required to perform a prescribed exercise regimen during their single daily activation allotment.[2,7]

Outcome measures

During 3-month postoperative follow-up, the surgeon followed changes in preoperative to post paired VNS FMA-UE/WMFT scores as an objective measure to determine the effectiveness of Vivistim for patients beyond the confines of strict clinical trial guidelines. Patients who chose to continue occupational therapy beyond the standard paired VNS continued to be tracked at 6, 9, and 12 months) follow-up appointments. Additional outcome measures tracked during follow-up appointments included changes in objective motor strength and spasticity scores, activities of daily living/instrumental activities of daily living independence level, subjective patient satisfaction report, and presence of adverse outcomes.

Retrospective data retrieval and statistical analysis

IRB approval was obtained for retrospective review. ICD codes were used to identify patients; all implanted patients over the first 2 years of institutional adoption were included in this observational retrospective review. Data was retrieved from the electronic medical record chart review for 21 patients. An overall descriptive analysis was conducted for the full sample (n = 21, 100%) of which a subset denoted by Group A (n = 16, 76%) had associated post-paired VNS FMA-UE scores for comparison. The second subset, denoted by Group B (n = 5, 24%) consisted of the patients who did not have a post-paired VNS FMA-UE score. The reasons for no score are as follows: Patient 1 had an unrelated medical event a few days before obtaining a final post-paired VNS FMA-UE, Patient 2 had a surgical site infection which required explant and inability to complete therapy, Patient 3 postoperative FMA-UE data unavailable, Patient 4 and 5 had near perfect preoperative FMA-UE scores over 60, thereby requiring qualification for paired VNS by WMFT, instead. A final subset denoted by Group C (n = 2, 10%) includes the two individuals requiring WMFT as a primary measure to qualify for VNS. Simply put, Groups C and A similarly completed baseline and post-paired VNS functional assessments, but Group C falls within Group B; they do not have post-paired VNS FMA-UE scores.

A descriptive analysis was conducted for both the total cohort and Group A; the decision was made to conduct a second separate descriptive analysis for Group A for the following reasons: (1) Group A comprises 76% of the total sample size, (2) a small data set is more susceptible to large changes when focusing on a subset, (3) it is most similar to the VNSREHAB trial with FMA-UE as a primary outcome measure, (4) minimization of skew present in a small study from the two patients in Group C who do not have post-paired VNS scores to balance their inflated baseline FMA-UE, and finally (5) incomparable improvement metrics between group C and A. Finally, the individuals in Group C were summarized and compared. Continuous variables were summarized using the mean and standard deviation (SD), as well as the median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Data analysis was conducted with Microsoft Excel.

RESULTS

Patient population and characteristics

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Between May 2024 and December 2025, a total of 21 patients underwent VNS device implantation; Most patients completed paired VNS therapy. One patient experienced a surgical site infection, requiring explant, a second patient suffered an unrelated medical event — a few days before final FMA-UE assessment, and a third patient reported completion of paired VNS, of which we were unable to obtain scores. The median age for participants was 63 (n = 21, SD = 11.90), and 17 (81%) were male. 18 (85%) of patients were right-handed. Slightly less than half of the patients, 9 (42.86%), suffered strokes secondary to Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion, and 10 (47.62%) experienced non-motor deficits, most of which were related to aphasia, and a couple related to numbness and/or paresthesia. The mean time between the date of ischemic stroke and VNS implantation was 4.24 (SD = 3.96) years.

DISCUSSION

Ischemic strokes account for 87% of all recorded strokes, with 50–60% of patients experiencing significant long-term functional motor impairments that affect their quality of life and activities of daily living.[8,14] Over the last decade, there has been a paucity of clinical advancements made to improve the post-stroke recovery process, specifically in the chronic phase. We report the experience of 21 patients with a history of ischemic stroke and chronic, moderate to severe UE motor impairment who underwent implantation of the Vivistim device (MicroTransponder, Inc, Austin, TX) followed by rehabilitation for a total of 6 weeks. The VNS-REHAB outcomes were used as a guiding metric, and the methodology was modeled with some variation. Our cohort’s average age was similar to VNSREHAB’s. The patients in Group A also had a similar mean baseline FMA-UE to that found in VNS-REHAB. Some patients extended treatment beyond the standard 6 weeks of paired VNS, per Occupational therapy (OT) recommendation or personal decision. During the standard 6 weeks of in-pair VNS clinic therapy, the patients were also encouraged to engage in self-activation as much as up to the approved 8 times daily.

Our results demonstrate a significant increase in functional motor recovery post-paired VNS using the validated FMA-UE or WMFT scale.[3] The efficacy of using paired VNS for patients with chronic UE motor impairment following an ischemic stroke has been well established in the literature.[2,6,9] The largest of these clinical trials is VNS-REHAB, a randomized, triple blind, sham-controlled trial conducted on patients with moderate to severe UE impairment at least 9 months after an ischemic stroke.[2] In the trial, the active VNS group showed an average improvement of 5.8 points, compared with 2.8 points in the sham group on the FMA-UE at day 90 post-paired VNS and home activations, a difference that reached statistical significance. Moreover, there was a nearly 50% response rate in patients receiving paired VNS, double that of patients receiving sham stimulation. For our cohort, most of the group A patients achieved a clinically significant improvement in their FMA-UE scores, with a mean increase of 10 points (n = 16), with a 69% response rate, both outputs almost twice that reported in VNS-REHAB.

Notably, 2 of the 16 patients in Group A had a baseline FMA– UE score <20. These two patients demonstrated a 19 and 24 point improvement in FMA-UE after paired VNS therapy, which significantly increased the overall mean FMA-UE improvement for the cohort. Further, the two patients in Group C with baseline FMA-UE scores >50 instead qualified for paired VNS via WMFT; both demonstrated improvement though only one had a clinically significant improvement above the 0.4 MCID. This represents an important distinction compared to the VNS-REHAB subgroup analysis of patient outcomes stratified by baseline FMA-UE scores as patients <20 and >50 were excluded in their study. Taken together, our findings suggest that a hard cut-off for baseline FMAUE score to determine eligibility may be excluding patients who may stand to benefit from paired VNS therapy. Further research examining additional preoperative factors that can predict paired VNS therapy success is therefore necessary.

Despite these promising findings, there is a paucity of literature aimed at optimizing this treatment modality and detailing methods for transitioning paired VNS clinical trials into clinical practice at an institutional level. In this report, we describe our workflow in implementing paired VNS at this institution. Our average time from initial evaluation to surgery was only 4.5 months. Despite the nascency of the paired VNS program at our institution, we have seen significant and consistent demand increase within the 1st year. Our success beyond previously reported trials may be attributed to an institutional paradigm that amalgamates both social and academic resources, emphasizing coordination amongst clinicians, therapists, and social workers to streamline care of our patients from initial evaluation to post-VNS implantation rehabilitation. By partnering with local stroke awareness groups, physiatry, and occupational therapy practices, we have expanded our search process to identify patients who may benefit from paired VNS and increase awareness of this treatment option. In addition, by utilizing monthly multidisciplinary conferences, we have promoted collaboration amongst clinicians across a variety of subspecialities and developed a strong referral base to connect potentially eligible patients with neurosurgeons to evaluate for VNS candidacy.

One possible concern of implementing paired VNS into clinical practice may be the uncertainty of reimbursement rates. Patients can often experience limitations in treatment options available to them due to a lack of insurance coverage.[1] This, in turn, may impede a physician’s ability to offer the best care available due to the inability of these patients to afford treatments. Using the Vivistim system, our results demonstrate a full reimbursement rate for paired VNS and all related procedures. This is irrespective of the insurance status of the patient or their medical insurance payer. Our institution received reimbursement from Medicare, Medicare supplemental plans, commercial plans, and Medicaid. Insurance authorization was managed through a business associate agreement between our institution and the device manufacturer. This allowed for efficient prior authorization and appeal processing.

Limitations

Our study is not without limitations. Most notable is our small sample size, incomplete data for 3 patients, and the required use of group subsets to more clearly characterize the results. A small cohort increases the susceptibility of our data to outliers, therefore inhibiting generalizability to a larger population. Further, the ratio of female patients who underwent VNS therapy was significantly lower than that of males, decreasing generalizability to the population. In addition, we only included patients from one institution, and all patients received their implantation by one neurosurgeon. This poses a concern for the overall generalizability of our findings and does not account for variation in healthcare systems practiced or surgical technique. Further, this retrospective observational study with a different methodology and goals cannot be directly compared to a clinical trial. Rather, the clinical trial data were used as a general metric for result comparison. A clinically meaningful response in VNS-REHAB was determined by a certain increase post-paired VNS compared to baseline at day 90. Our study includes post-paired VNS scores up to 12 months postoperative. Therefore, our analysis of a significant increase in functional outcome scores and a higher response rate than the VNS-REHAB trial is not without significant confounding variables. VNS REHAB only enrolled patients with an FMAUE score between 20 and 50. Our institution, in contrast, treated two patients with baseline FMA-UE scores below 20 and used WMFT to confirm eligibility and assess treatment effect in two patients scoring above a 50 on baseline FMA-UE. Though we saw improvement in this subset of patients, the small sample limits generalizability.

CONCLUSION

This study retrospectively analyzed our institution’s initial two-year experience implementing paired VNS therapy for patients with chronic ischemic stroke and persistent upper-extremity deficits. Improvement in WMFT or FMA-UE scores was observed in all patients, with a mean FMA-UE improvement of 10 points following paired VNS therapy. We believe our program’s favorable outcomes may reflect several institutional factors, including partnerships with local therapists, stroke awareness groups, and physiatrists to improve awareness and referral pathways; multidisciplinary conferences to identify patients most likely to benefit; successful insurance authorization; early allowance of independent VNS activation outside the clinic; and close longitudinal follow-up to support patient adherence and motivation. Collectively, these findings suggest that institutional implementation is feasible and financially sensible.

Further studies are needed to determine whether the upper-extremity improvements observed in our cohort are sustained beyond two years, whether similar benefits are seen in female patients, and whether objective preoperative factors beyond baseline WMFT and FMA-UE scores can predict response to paired VNS therapy.
