Hospitalization costs and out-of-pocket expenses for stroke patients in mainland China
Authors: Wen-Jun Tu, Mingxin Wang, Yangyi Fan, Xianwei Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to assess hospitalization expenditures and out-of-pocket burden (OOPB, defined as out-of-pocket expenses [OOPE] divided by disposable annual income multiplied by household size and expressed as a percentage) in patients with stroke in mainland China from 2019 to 2020.MethodsThis research encompassed patients from hospitals jointly affiliated with the Chinese Hospital Quality Monitoring System and the Big Data Observatory Platform for Stroke of China. We focused on individuals hospitalized between 2019 and 2020, who were primarily diagnosed with a stroke, as classified under the ICD-10 codes: ischemic stroke (IS), intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). We estimated the out-of-pocket rate (OOPR, defined as OOPE divided by hospitalization expenditures expressed as a percentage), high OOPB, and catastrophic healthcare payments (defined as OOPB exceeding 30%) at the national and provincial levels. The correlation between OOPR and in-hospital mortality in different provinces was also examined.ResultsThe database included 7,226,376 stroke patients from 1,599 hospitals, of whom 58.6% were male. The OOPR and OOPB in stroke patients were 32.6% (95% CI, 31.9%-33.3%) and 6.3% (95% CI, 6.1%-6.4%), respectively. For IS and ICH, more than two-thirds (22/32) of China's provinces had an OOPR of over 28.0%, while three-quarters of provinces (24/32) for SAH had an OOPR of over 28.0%. For IS and ICH, none of the provinces had catastrophic healthcare payments, whereas for SAH, over half of the provinces (18/32) did. We analyzed the relationship between OOPR and stroke in-hospital mortality at the provincial level, but the data did not confirm a correlation between the two factors (IS: r=-0.172, P=0.34; ICH: r=0.078, P=0.67; SAH: r=0.041, P=0.82).ConclusionThe acute hospitalization costs and OOPB on stroke patients is significant, with nearly a third of their medical costs paid out-of-pocket. This underscores the pressing necessity to advance the development of a more inclusive healthcare system, particularly in economically challenged regions.Keywords: Stroke, Hospitalization expenditures, Out-of-pocket, China

Introduction

With China’s aging population, the increasing prevalence of stroke-related risk factors, inadequate management, and other contributing factors have led to an escalating economic and social burden associated with stroke [1, 2]. Over the past decade, governments and academic institutions have made significant strides in stroke prevention and control [3, 4]. However, stroke remains a serious issue in China. Data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 indicated that in 2019, there were 3.94 million new stroke cases and 28.76 million prevalent stroke cases in China, underscoring the substantial burden of stroke in the country [5]. Stroke has emerged as a principal focus in the realm of Medicare payments due to its high fatality rate and the severe disabilities it often causes [3–5].

In China, the economic levels of different regions vary considerably - the eastern coastal regions are economically developed, while the western regions are economically underdeveloped. The insurance market size is closely associated with regional economic development, and East China accounts for the largest share of China’s premium income for health insurance [6]. In China, total health expenditure includes three financing components—government health expenditure (HE), social HE, and out-of-pocket expenses (OOPE) [7]. Changes in health financing policies have led to a rapid rise in medical costs and OOPE for people in China since 1980 [8]. Furthermore, the Chinese government implemented a series of healthcare reforms to reduce the OOPE, and the out-of-pocket expenses rate (OOPR, was defined as OOPE divided by hospitalization expenditures multiplied by 100%) in China declined from 60.0% in 2001 to 28.8% in 2016 [9]. However, the financial burden for medical care is still excessive, and significant differences in medical level and commitment among different regions have been proposed. Zhang et al., [10] showed that the OOPE as a percentage of total inpatient cost was about 24.1% in Guangzhou from 2006 to 2013, while another study in Shenyang indicated it being 42.5% from 2015 to 2017 [11]. High OOPE are barriers to healthcare access and treatment compliance [12]. In recent years, an increase in the proportion of people in the general population facing a high OOPE and catastrophic healthcare payments (CHP) has been reported [12–17], but there have been no estimates of the OOPE burden and its effects on households at the national and provincial levels in China.

Interestingly, Heeley. et al. [18] showed that among 4739 China stroke patients, the OOPR was 87.6%, and 71.0% of patients had experienced CHP in 2006. A nationwide and cross-sectional study including 49,588 hospitalized patients who had been diagnosed with ischemic stroke in the China Health Insurance Research Association (CHIRA) claims database showed that the OOPR for the Basic Medical Insurance Scheme for Employees (BMISE) and the Basic Medical Insurance Scheme for Urban Residents (BMISUR) were 25.4% and 43.5%, respectively [19]. Nevertheless, this study was limited to ischemic stroke patients and involved a relatively small cohort (fewer than 50,000 participants), which means it may not accurately represent the full economic impact of stroke on patients in China [19]. Consequently, our objective was to evaluate the hospitalization costs and the out-of-pocket financial burden faced by stroke patients in mainland China over the period from 2019 to 2020, utilizing data from a national database.

Methods

Data sources

The Hospital Quality Monitoring System (HQMS) is an official data collection system covering all tertiary hospitals. “Medical Record Home page” was collected by HQMS, and a summary of the patient’s hospitalization information, including 346 variables such as outcomes at discharge, financial information (Hospitalization expenditures (HE) and out-of-pocket expenses (OOPE)), etc. [20, 21]. Bigdata Observatory Platform for Stroke of China (BOSC) was established in 2020 by the Ministry of Health China Stroke Prevention Project Committee (CSPPC) to provide support for the stroke risk factor screening intervention management, stroke center declaration, certification and direct data reporting, stroke first aid map management, and monitoring of treatment status of stroke hospitalized patients, etc [22–24]. Compared with HQMS, which only collects information on the Medical Record Home page, the BOSC database also collects detailed information on risk factors, laboratory tests, acute treatment, and complications of stroke patients [25].

In December 2021, we conducted a process of matching hospital names between the HQMS and BOSC. We found that a total of 1599 hospitals co-existed on both platforms. Consequently, from 2019 to 2020, we extracted data on all patients whose initial medical record included a diagnosis of stroke—specifically ischemic stroke (IS, ICD-10: I63), intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH, ICD-10: I61), or subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH, ICD-10: I60)—from those 1,599 hospitals.

This study collected information about demographics (sex, age, and race) and financial information (HE and OOPE). Household disposable annual income (HDAI) measures the income of households after taking into account net interest and dividends received and the payment of taxes and social contributions. In this study, we calculated HDAI by multiplying the median per capita disposable income of residents in China in 2020 by the number of people per household (seventh national population census) [26, 27]. In addition, out-of-pocket rate (OOPR) and out-of-pocket burden (OOPB) were calculated. OOPR was defined as OOPE divided by HE multiplied by 100%, and OOPB was defined as OOPE divided by HDAI multiplied by 100%. We also aimed to explore the impact of the OOPR on patient mortality outcomes. Therefore, we investigated the correlation between OOPR and in-hospital mortality across various provinces to understand how financial burdens might influence patient survival. This study calculated OOPB as OOPE during hospitalization divided by an imputed annual household disposable income (provincial median × household size). This methodological choice creates a clear scale mismatch and likely leads to a systematic underestimation of the true financial burden, especially considering the substantial post-discharge costs (rehabilitation, long-term care, and productivity losses) characteristic of stroke. Therefore, the computed CHP should be interpreted strictly as a proxy indicator of in-hospital financial pressure, and not as true household catastrophic expenditure in the WHO sense.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were shown as mean (standard deviation, SD) and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). All comparative rates with 95% CIs were presented. Total family OOPE spending on health care that exceeds 10% of household disposable income was defined as high OOPB [12]. The OOPB that exceeds 30% was defined as Catastrophic healthcare payments (CHP) [18]. As this study uses a proxy measurement based on in-hospital out-of-pocket expenses and imputed provincial median income, the term “catastrophic healthcare payments” should be interpreted cautiously as referring to a proxy indicator of in-hospital financial pressure, rather than true household catastrophic health expenditure in the WHO sense. The Healthy China 2030 plan aims to decrease the proportion of OOPE in total healthcare expenditure to 28% by 2020 and further to 25% by 2030 [8]. Accordingly, in this study, we set the threshold for the OOPR at 28%, with the understanding that exceeding this benchmark signifies a failure to meet these overarching national goals. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify factors (such average length of state, nationality, gender, rural and urban, age etc.) associated with the likelihood of high OOPR and CHE. Results were presented as the odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The logistic regression models were performed using individual-level patient data for prediction. SAS (SAS Institute), version 9.3, was used to perform all statistical analyses.

Human ethics and consent to participate declarations

The analyses, including logistic regression, were performed on de-identified discharge record-level data submitted by participating hospitals, rather than purely hospital-aggregated summary data. All analytical data were fully aggregated and anonymized prior to access by the investigators, and no individual patient-level raw data were accessed or involved at any stage of the study. This study was conducted in strict adherence to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. It was granted ethical exemption (Reference No.: NRCR-21-109) by the Ethics Review Board of the Rehabilitation Hospital, National Research Center for Rehabilitation Technical Aids, given the use of publicly accessible datasets devoid of personal identifiers.

The relationship between OOPR and stroke in-hospital mortality was analyzed at the province level. The data did not confirm a correlation between the two factors (IS: r=-0.172, P = 0.34; ICH: r = 0.078; P = 0.67; SAH: r = 0.041, P = 0.82). In a logistic regression analysis that controlled for other variables, residing in rural areas was found to be significantly associated with a higher OOPR, with an odds ratio (OR) of 3.11 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.45–3.85), and a greater likelihood of CHE, with an OR of 4.03 (95% CI: 3.11–5.09).

Discussion

Using the hospitalization-based proxy indicator of out-of-pocket burden (OOPB), heavy financial burden was observed in approximately one out of sixteen provinces for ischemic stroke (IS) patients, while no province exceeded the catastrophic threshold in this acute-phase assessment. By comparison, five-sixths of provinces showed an increased financial burden for intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) patients under the same proxy measure, yet none met the criteria for catastrophic expenditure based solely on in-hospital costs. Meanwhile, nearly all provinces reported a high financial burden, and half of the provinces faced a catastrophic financial burden due to out-of-pocket healthcare expenses for subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) patients. Currently, medical insurance coverage for IS in China is relatively comprehensive, whereas coverage for hemorrhagic strokes, particularly in underdeveloped areas, is notably insufficient. Conceptual framework of financial burden in stroke patients in China

The target of Healthy China 2030 is to reduce the percentage of OOPR to 28% by 2020 [8]. Our research data showed that 68.8% of the provinces for IS and ICH and 75% for SAH did not meet the target. A study including stroke patients from 39 hospitals across six cities in Liaoning Province between 2015 and 2017 found that the OOPR was 42.5% [11]. The OOPR in China declined from 60.0% in 2001 to 28.8% in 2016 [8]. We found that the OOPR in people with stroke was 32.6%, suggesting that stroke patients have significantly higher OOPR than other diseases in China [8].

Our work suggests that stroke is associated with about ¥88.4 billion per year in hospitalization-level expenditures in China, or over 8% of the ¥1 trillion in China’s national healthcare expenditure, and patients need to pay ¥28.8 billion on their own, accounting for 0.07% of patient disposable income. The World Health Organization speculates that an increase of 1% in OOPR will increase the catastrophic health expenditure of households by 2.2% [8]. When OOPR is less than 30%, people can be protected from health financing risk, and the disparity in health service use can be reduced between people with different income levels [7]. A previous study showed that in 2008, OOPB for all diseases was 12.1%, and 13.0% of households experienced CHP in China [28]. There is still a long way to go for stroke patients for China’s medical insurance coverage, and even the OOPR in some provinces exceeds 50%.

We were surprised to observe that Tibet, an economically less developed region in western China, exhibited remarkably low out-of-pocket ratio (OOPR) and out-of-pocket burden (OOPB) for stroke patients, approaching near-zero levels in some measures. This finding is particularly intriguing and warrants further investigation. While it may reflect enhanced financial risk protection in the region, alternative explanations—such as variations in data reporting systems, special subsidies, differences in case-mix, or local healthcare and reimbursement practices—could also contribute to these differences. Fujian presents a contrasting pattern. Interestingly, the out-of-pocket ratios (OOPRs) in Fujian rank among the highest across all three stroke subtypes (IS, ICH, and SAH). This is noteworthy given that Fujian is a more economically developed province compared to those with the lowest OOPRs (such as Qinghai, Gansu, and Xinjiang). These observations may suggest potential regional variations in insurance reimbursement levels, benefit structures, healthcare pricing, or other contextual factors influencing financial protection for stroke patients, including in more developed areas. The relatively higher observed patient burden in Fujian warrants further exploration to better understand the underlying contributors and identify potential opportunities for strengthening financial risk protection. It should be pointed out that the latest round of medical reform in China originated in Sanming, Fujian [29], and further evidence is needed to confirm whether the reform has aggravated the OOPR of stroke patients. Furthermore, the geographical distribution across Chinese provinces of the OOP is clearly conditioned by the regional distribution of HDAI. In addition to HDAI, there are many other factors that may lead to differences in OOPE. Although we did not obtain relevant information, comparing the two extremes (Fujian and Tibet) could be more accurately reported to understand what are the determinants of the difference beyond the availability due to better HDAI. This will be an interesting point for future research.

Our study makes several contributions to the national discussion on healthcare coverage in China. For instance, it provides valuable insights for provinces to tailor their medical insurance policies for stroke patients based on local conditions. Governments in less developed areas should consider adopting flexible insurance models and attracting social capital. The central government is advised to increase medical investments, acknowledge regional disparities, and enhance inter-regional coordination and peer support. China began developing national health accounts in the early 1980s with the World Bank’s assistance, and initiated health system reforms in the early 2000s [30]. The latest round of reforms, launched in 2009, has markedly improved universal health insurance coverage [31]. Additionally, the Sanming Three-in-One Model is highlighted as a potentially effective approach for China’s systemic healthcare reform [29]. China’s healthcare insurance primarily consists of the Urban Resident Medical Insurance and the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme (NCMS) for rural residents. At the current stage, the Urban Resident Medical Insurance is operating well (with the reimbursement rate for hospitalized patients generally exceeding 80%), but the situation of the NCMS still requires further improvement. Although NCMS has a good coverage rate, issues such as annually increasing premiums for patients and low reimbursement rates (often not exceeding half) persist. The NCMS is also an issue that the government needs to focus on next, as it is crucial to the realization of the Healthy China 2030 objectives. In this study, we did not differentiate between the reimbursement situations of different types of medical insurance, which would weaken the value of this study.

This research encompassed the vast majority, about 80%, of hospitalized stroke patients in China, including both hemorrhagic and ischemic strokes, and spanned all provincial administrative regions. The data was meticulously sourced from inpatient medical records, ensuring a higher degree of accuracy. The findings of this study hold substantial significance in guiding government decisions to refine medical insurance strategies for stroke patients.

This study has several limitations. First, the data were limited to the acute in-hospital phase following stroke, as captured primarily through the Hospital Quality Monitoring System (HQMS). Consequently, out-of-pocket expenses (OOPE) and overall burden beyond hospitalization—such as post-discharge medical costs, productivity losses due to disability, caregiving expenses, and long-term societal impacts—were not assessed. This restricts the ability to capture the total economic ripple effect of stroke on Chinese society, future studies incorporating longitudinal post-discharge data would enable a more comprehensive assessment of the overall economic burden. In addition, the study lacks granular data on specific cost components, such as medications, surgical procedures, high-cost medical consumables, or specialized interventions (e.g., intravenous thrombolysis or endovascular thrombectomy). Without this breakdown, it is challenging to identify the primary drivers of financial burden for patients, future analyses with detailed itemized cost data would better pinpoint key contributors to hospitalization expenses. Second, analyses were based on aggregated hospital-level metrics aggregated to the provincial level (by hospital location), without individual patient-level or post-discharge information, and without weighting by hospital case volume. This approach may introduce ecological bias or be influenced by hospital-specific outliers, particularly for provinces with high OOPB or cost-to-HDAI proportions (CHP). Differences in provincial insurance coverage or reimbursement policies could contribute, but these could not be directly examined due to the absence of detailed insurance benefit structures. Third, the study was restricted to data from 2019 to 2020, reflecting a relatively short time window. Although HQMS data quality and coverage have improved substantially since 2019, a longer temporal span (e.g., 5–10 years) would enable better assessment of trends in OOPR and OOPB over time. In future work, we plan to extend the analysis to more recent years (e.g., comparing 2019 with 2024 or later) to track changes in financial protection, reimbursement policies, and pricing dynamics across provinces. Fourth, this study used a uniform national median per capita disposable income to calculate OOPB, which fails to adequately capture income disparities across provinces, urban–rural areas, and socioeconomic classes. As a result, it may systematically underestimate the true out-of-pocket burden for low-income groups and less developed regions. This limitation could lead to an overly optimistic overall assessment of the financial protection provided by medical insurance and may underestimate the need for targeted interventions for vulnerable populations. Incorporating province-specific or income-stratified disposable income data in future studies would significantly improve the accuracy and equity of burden measurements. Finally, this analysis did not confirm a significant correlation between the out-of-pocket rate (OOPR) and in-hospital mortality at the provincial level. This analysis is based on provincial-level aggregated data and is therefore susceptible to the risk of ecological fallacy. Moreover, in-hospital mortality, as a short-term and crude outcome measure, may not adequately capture the impact of financial factors on stroke patients’ timeliness of care, quality of care, or post-discharge prognosis. Consequently, these results should be interpreted strictly as descriptive observations at the provincial level and should not be over-interpreted in terms of causality or policy implications. Future studies should investigate the potential influence of OOPR on post-discharge survival and long-term prognosis. In addition, while multivariable analysis identified rural residence as a significant risk factor for higher OOPR and catastrophic healthcare expenditure (CHE), the lack of detailed stratification by insurance type (e.g., BMISE vs. BMISUR) and hospital tier limits deeper exploration of moderating effects. This renders the findings somewhat general in terms of policy implications. Future research with more granular data on insurance subtypes and facility levels would enable more targeted recommendations.

Conclusions

The costs of hospitalization and out-of-pocket expenses for stroke patients in China are substantial when compared to the average income levels, with catastrophic healthcare payments being a frequent occurrence, especially among patients with hemorrhagic stroke. This situation underscores the urgency of advancing the development of a comprehensive healthcare system in China, with a special focus on economically underdeveloped regions.
