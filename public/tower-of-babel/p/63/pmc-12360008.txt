Validating high-fidelity simulation for assessing procedural skills in nursing education: a kane framework approach in Ghana
Authors: Simon Ntumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSimulation-based assessments (SBAs) are increasingly embraced as reliable tools for evaluating procedural competence in nursing education. In Ghana, however, their formal adoption and validation remain limited. This study investigated the construct validity of high-fidelity simulation-based assessments (HFSAs) for nursing students using Kane’s Validity Framework, which structures validation across four inferences: scoring, generalization, extrapolation, and implication. Using a quantitative, cross-sectional design, data were collected from 150 final-year students across three accredited nursing institutions in Ghana. For the scoring inference, inter-rater reliability was strong (ICC = 0.77–0.84), and internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.83), confirming that assessors applied the rubric consistently and that items reliably measured procedural competence. Generalization was supported by a moderate correlation between simulation and OSCE scores (r = 0.45, p < 0.01), indicating that both assessments capture related clinical skills. For extrapolation, multiple regression revealed that simulation scores significantly predicted clinical practicum performance (R² = 0.42, p < 0.01), suggesting that HFSA results meaningfully reflect real-world competence. Factor analysis further validated the unidimensionality of the simulation tool, and Multi-Trait Multi-Method (MTMM) analysis demonstrated strong convergent validity and minimal method bias. Regarding implication, Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ = 0.67, p < 0.01) showed substantial consistency between pass/fail decisions based on simulation and OSCE scores. These findings provide robust evidence that HFSAs are not only psychometrically sound but also practically defensible for evaluating clinical readiness in Ghanaian nursing education. The results support the integration of simulation into competency-based curricula, while highlighting the need for sustained investment in simulation infrastructure, assessor calibration, and equitable access across institutions.Keywords: Simulation-based assessments, Nursing education, Procedural skills, Construct validity, Clinical competence, Ghana

Introduction

In recent decades, the field of health professions education has witnessed a paradigm shift from traditional, time-based training models to competency-based education (CBE) [1, 2, 4, 48]. This learner-centered approach prioritizes the acquisition of clearly defined skills, knowledge, and attitudes necessary for effective and safe clinical practice, with a strong emphasis on measurable learning outcomes and authentic performance-based assessments [5, 8, 45, 56, 62, 68, 79]. Unlike conventional assessments that often focus on theoretical knowledge or checklist-style evaluations, CBE requires robust methods to assess learners’ real-world ability to perform complex clinical tasks. This shift has profound implications for the design and delivery of assessments in nursing education, particularly in high-stakes domains such as procedural competence. Among the most influential innovations aligned with CBE is high-fidelity simulation (HFS), which recreates clinical environments using sophisticated manikins, interactive case scenarios, and real-time feedback mechanisms [38, 40, 47, 59]. HFS offers a controlled, risk-free platform for students to repeatedly practice and refine critical procedural skills, such as intravenous catheter insertion, wound dressing, medication administration, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation [12, 41, 43, 68]. By enabling learners to engage in deliberate practice, receive immediate feedback, and reflect on their performance, simulation bridges the gap between classroom theory and clinical application.

Globally, HFS has gained widespread acceptance as both a teaching and assessment tool in nursing and allied health education. Numerous meta-analyses and systematic reviews have demonstrated its effectiveness in improving clinical judgment, decision-making, psychomotor skills, and learner confidence [1, 9, 16, 17, 19, 72]. As a result, simulation-based assessments (SBAs) have emerged as promising alternatives to more traditional modalities like Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs), which may lack contextual realism and afford limited opportunities for skill repetition. SBAs are particularly advantageous for evaluating procedural competence, given their capacity to simulate high-risk or infrequent clinical situations in a standardized and ethically appropriate manner. Despite these benefits, the adoption of SBAs for summative evaluation has raised persistent concerns about validity and reliability. Specifically, construct validity the extent to which the assessment accurately measures the intended clinical competencies remains a critical area of scrutiny [11, 18, 39, 56]. Without rigorous and transparent validation processes, there is a risk that simulation scores may reflect superficial performance or test-taking behavior rather than genuine clinical ability. This is especially pertinent when simulation is used in high-stakes decision-making, such as graduation, licensure, or clinical placement. To address such concerns, the educational measurement community has increasingly advocated for the use of comprehensive validation frameworks, chief among them the Kane Validity Framework [7, 33, 44–46, 53, 55, 84]. This argument-based approach conceptualizes validation as a chain of inferences from scoring, to generalization, to extrapolation, and finally to implications each of which must be supported with empirical evidence and logical reasoning. The Kane framework not only allows for a nuanced understanding of validity but also aligns with the evolving emphasis on assessment as a process of evidence-based decision-making in education.

While numerous validation studies grounded in Kane’s framework have been conducted in high-income countries, there remains a significant gap in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where educational and clinical contexts differ markedly. In Sub-Saharan Africa, for instance, nursing education systems face multiple structural and pedagogical challenges, including overcrowded clinical sites, insufficient faculty supervision, inadequate resources, and high student-to-patient ratios [27, 36, 50, 68, 80, 83]. These limitations not only hinder effective clinical training but also necessitate the use of alternative learning and assessment environments such as simulation. Recognizing this, several African nations such as South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria have begun to integrate simulation into nursing curricula, often through international partnerships, donor-funded projects, or institutional pilot programs [4, 10, 18, 60, 61, 65]. However, despite this growing momentum, many simulation initiatives in Africa remain fragmented, under-resourced, and disconnected from national regulatory frameworks or long-term policy strategies. More importantly, empirical studies evaluating the validity of SBAs within African contexts are scarce, especially for procedural competencies that directly impact patient outcomes and healthcare delivery. Most existing research focuses on simulation as a teaching tool, with limited attention to its effectiveness and fairness as an assessment method. This lack of context-specific validation limits the ability of educators, policymakers, and regulators to make informed decisions about the use of simulation for evaluating clinical competence [75].

In Ghana, the integration of simulation into nursing education is a relatively recent but increasingly important development, driven largely by escalating challenges in the clinical training environment. The expansion of nursing and midwifery programs spurred by national efforts to meet healthcare workforce demands has resulted in a significant rise in student enrolment across public training institutions. However, this growth has not been matched by a proportional increase in clinical placement opportunities. Many hospitals, clinics, and community health centers are already overwhelmed with service delivery responsibilities and lack the capacity to accommodate the growing number of students for hands-on training. Additionally, persistent staff shortages and an overburdened health system have led to insufficient clinical supervision, undermining the quality and consistency of experiential learning during practicum periods. In response to these systemic constraints, the Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) of Ghana the regulatory authority for nursing education has formally recognized the transformative potential of simulation as a strategy for enhancing clinical competence, promoting patient safety, and bridging the longstanding theory–practice gap in pre-service education [24, 26, 66, 80]. Simulation allows students to engage in repeated practice of critical procedures in a low-risk, standardized environment, mitigating the risks associated with limited real-world exposure and inconsistent supervision. Moreover, simulation supports the national policy goal of producing “fit-for-purpose” graduates who can perform independently in resource-constrained health settings upon entry into practice.

In light of this policy shift, several public nursing and midwifery training institutions particularly those affiliated with teaching hospitals and health sciences universities have taken proactive steps to establish simulation laboratories. While the sophistication of these labs varies, most are equipped with low- to mid-fidelity manikins, procedural task trainers, and basic audiovisual equipment to facilitate instruction, debriefing, and performance evaluation. These facilities are commonly used to support competency development in key skill domains such as basic life support, intravenous line insertion, wound dressing, injection administration, catheterization, and antenatal care procedures. Instructors use structured scenarios and checklists aligned with the national curriculum to ensure that students acquire the psychomotor skills and clinical reasoning required for safe practice. However, the implementation of simulation remains uneven across the country. While urban-based institutions benefit from stronger infrastructure, donor support, and partnerships with tertiary hospitals, rural and smaller colleges often lack the resources, faculty training, and institutional capacity to fully operationalize simulation-based education. Moreover, most simulation activities remain instructional rather than assessment-driven, with limited efforts to formally validate simulation-based assessments for summative or licensure purposes. As a result, the potential of simulation to serve as a robust, equitable, and standardized assessment tool remains largely untapped in the Ghanaian context. This uneven development underscores the urgent need for empirical validation of simulation-based assessments within Ghana’s nursing education system. By generating context-specific evidence on their reliability and construct validity, stakeholders can better determine the feasibility of integrating simulation not only as a pedagogical tool but also as a credible method for evaluating clinical competence at scale.

While this progress represents a significant step toward modernizing nursing education in Ghana, the implementation of simulation remains fragmented and lacks a coordinated national strategy or standardized curriculum framework. There are currently no uniform guidelines on the duration, frequency, or assessment criteria for simulation-based learning, and substantial variability exists across institutions in terms of infrastructure, faculty training, and pedagogical integration. Of particular concern is the absence of a systematic protocol for validating simulation-based assessments (SBAs). Without robust, contextually grounded validation efforts, it is difficult to ascertain whether the outcomes of simulation sessions particularly those used for summative or high-stakes evaluations accurately reflect students’ real-world clinical competencies. This lack of standardization and evidence-based validation raises critical concerns about the fairness, credibility, and interpretability of simulation assessment scores. It also has implications for high-stakes decisions such as licensure, certification, and graduation, which may depend heavily on students’ performance in simulated clinical scenarios [66, 67, 71, 74, 83]. In the absence of empirical validation frameworks such as those grounded in the Kane Validity Framework educational stakeholders, including regulatory bodies and employers, may be unable to make defensible judgments about a graduate’s readiness for autonomous clinical practice. The increasing integration of high-fidelity simulations (HFS) into nursing education globally marks a significant pedagogical advancement aimed at improving clinical competence, decision-making, and patient safety. In both high- and low-resource settings, simulation offers an alternative or supplement to traditional clinical placements, which are often constrained by logistical, ethical, or safety considerations [13, 23, 70]. In Ghana, the adoption of simulation is gaining momentum in response to expanding student enrolments and declining access to adequately supervised clinical practice environments [54]. However, while the instructional benefits of simulation have been widely promoted, considerably less attention has been paid to the validity of simulation-based assessments (SBAs), particularly those used to make high-stakes decisions regarding student progression, graduation, and licensure. One of the most critical but underexplored aspects of SBA is construct validity the extent to which the assessment accurately measures the intended clinical or procedural skill [50, 61, 69, 78, 82]. Without compelling evidence of construct validity, assessment outcomes may be misleading, potentially leading to either the premature advancement of underprepared students or the unfair penalization of competent candidates. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Ghana, where simulation is often implemented with limited resources, undertrained faculty, and variable institutional infrastructure, the lack of rigorous validation protocols exacerbates the risk of compromised assessment quality and equity [3, 10, 12, 52]. Furthermore, a critical review of the existing literature in the Ghanaian and broader Sub-Saharan African context reveals that most studies on simulation focus predominantly on learner satisfaction, knowledge acquisition, or perceived realism [11, 21, 54, 73]. While such findings provide valuable insights into the acceptability and feasibility of simulation in resource-constrained settings, they fall short of addressing the psychometric robustness of the tools used to assess procedural competencies. Few, if any, studies have undertaken systematic, theory-guided investigations into whether simulation scores can be generalized, extrapolated to real clinical settings, or used to support consequential educational decisions.

The Kane Validity Framework [44, 45] provides a comprehensive and widely accepted model for validating complex assessments, particularly in performance-based disciplines such as medicine and nursing. It structures the validation process around four key inferences scoring, generalization, extrapolation, and implications each of which must be supported by empirical evidence. To date, however, no published study in Ghana has applied this framework to examine the validity of high-fidelity simulation assessments used for evaluating procedural skills in nursing students. This gap in the literature and practice is both theoretically and practically significant. It limits the ability of regulators, educators, and employers to make defensible decisions based on simulation performance, particularly in high-stakes contexts. It also hampers efforts to align simulation-based assessment with international quality assurance standards. Therefore, this study seeks to address this critical void by establishing the construct validity of high-fidelity simulation-based assessments for procedural skills in Ghanaian nursing education using the Kane Validity Framework. By generating localized, contextually relevant validity evidence, the study aimed to enhance the quality and credibility of simulation-based assessment in Ghana and contribute to the global discourse on simulation validity in LMICs.

Methodology

Research design

The study adopted a cross-sectional correlational research design to rigorously investigate the construct validity of high-fidelity simulation-based assessments (SBAs) used to evaluate nursing students’ procedural skills in Ghana. The choice of a quantitative design was informed by the study’s objective to generate statistically analyzable evidence on the relationships between simulation performance and established indicators of clinical competence, such as Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) scores and clinical practicum evaluations. The cross-sectional nature of the design enabled the researcher to collect data at a single point in time across multiple institutions, providing a snapshot of the validity evidence within the academic year under review. This design was particularly well-suited for examining correlations between different assessment modalities that purportedly measure the same construct namely, procedural competence. The use of a correlational approach allowed for the testing of predictive and concurrent relationships between scores obtained from simulation-based assessments and those from other validated performance measures. This was in line with previous simulation validity studies that employed similar quantitative methods to assess construct validity in both high-income and low- and middle-income country (LMIC) contexts [14, 23, 31]. Furthermore, the study was conceptually anchored in the Kane Validity Framework [45], which guided the gathering and interpretation of quantitative evidence to support four inferential stages: scoring, generalization, extrapolation, and implications. This framework provided a structured lens for evaluating how simulation scores are generated, how generalizable they are across contexts, how well they predict real-world performance, and whether they support meaningful decisions (e.g., passing or licensing). This theoretically grounded design ensured that the study not only established statistical correlations but also interrogated the legitimacy of inferences made from simulation scores.

Study setting

The research was conducted across three public nursing training institutions located in southern and middle Ghana, selected using purposive sampling. These institutions were chosen based on their adoption of high-fidelity simulation (HFS) technology as a regular component of clinical skills instruction and assessment. All three institutions were accredited by the Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) of Ghana and affiliated with public universities offering diploma and degree programs in general nursing. These institutions had, over the past five years, invested in simulation laboratories equipped with mannequins such as the Laerdal SimMan 3G and Gaumard’s HAL S3201, along with video recording capabilities for debriefing sessions. They had also begun integrating SBAs into their summative clinical assessments, although with varying levels of formality and standardization. Simulation exercises at these sites typically included procedural skills such as intravenous therapy, catheterization, wound dressing, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation, among others. By selecting institutions from both the middle and southern zones of Ghana, the study captured a range of simulation practices that reflect the emerging diversity in simulation implementation within the country. All participating institutions operated under the standardized guidelines of the NMC Ghana, which emphasizes the demonstration of clinical competence as a prerequisite for both graduation and registration/licensure [20, 22, 32, 69]. This regulatory environment further justified the relevance of examining whether SBAs yield valid and defensible evidence of students’ clinical readiness.

Population and sample

The target population for this study comprised final-year nursing students enrolled in either diploma or bachelor’s degree programs in the selected institutions. These students had completed simulation-based assessments in procedural skills as part of their clinical education and had also undergone OSCEs and clinical practicum evaluations. Final-year students were selected because they were closest to professional licensure and had been exposed to the full spectrum of assessment modalities, making them ideal candidates for investigating the construct validity of simulation scores. A sample of 150 students was drawn using stratified random sampling to ensure equitable representation from each of the three institutions. The strata were defined by institutional affiliation, and students were randomly selected within each stratum. Stratification was necessary to avoid sampling bias and to ensure that institutional variations in simulation exposure and assessment practices were proportionally reflected in the sample. The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software for correlation and multiple regression analyses. Based on a conventional alpha level of 0.05, a desired statistical power of 0.80, and a medium effect size (r = 0.30), the minimum required sample size was estimated at 138 [80]. The final sample of 150 participants accounted for potential attrition and incomplete data.

Participants were excluded if they had missed any of the three assessments (SBA, OSCE, or practicum), or if their simulation scores were unavailable due to technical malfunctions or missing rater data. This sample provided a robust basis for conducting inferential statistical analyses required to evaluate the strength and plausibility of the inferences laid out in the Kane Framework. It also ensured that the study had sufficient statistical power to detect meaningful relationships among the variables of interest.

Instrumentation

This study utilized three primary instruments to collect quantitative data on students’ clinical competence as measured through simulation-based assessments, OSCEs, and clinical practicum evaluations. Each instrument was selected for its alignment with the construct of procedural competence and its relevance to the inferential stages of the Kane Validity Framework.

Simulation-based assessment rubrics

High-fidelity simulation performance in this study was evaluated using a structured procedural skills rubric that was adapted from the widely validated Creighton Competency Evaluation Instrument (CCEI). The original CCEI has been extensively used in global nursing education contexts and is well-regarded for its reliability, internal consistency, and ability to measure clinical competence objectively during simulation scenarios. For this study, the instrument was carefully modified to suit the Ghanaian nursing education context while retaining its core psychometric properties. Adaptations included alignment with local procedural protocols, incorporation of context-specific clinical tasks, and language adjustments to ensure clarity and cultural relevance for both raters and students. The rubric comprised two components: global performance indicators and task-specific items tailored to common nursing procedures in Ghana. These included intravenous (IV) cannulation, wound dressing, urinary catheter insertion, vital signs monitoring, basic life support, and oxygen therapy [63, 64]. Each item on the rubric was scored using a five-point Likert scale, with anchors ranging from 1 (novice or unsafe performance) to 5 (expert-level proficiency). The scale assessed critical domains such as technical accuracy, procedural efficiency, adherence to clinical guidelines, patient safety, infection prevention, and communication with simulated patients. The inclusion of both technical and non-technical skills reflected the holistic nature of clinical competence expected of final-year nursing students. To ensure objectivity in scoring, the simulation-based assessments were conducted under standardized conditions, and each student’s performance was rated independently by two trained faculty assessors. These assessors underwent a rater calibration workshop prior to data collection, which involved norming sessions using sample video scenarios, discussions on rubric interpretation, and consensus-building exercises to minimize inter-rater discrepancies. The inter-rater reliability statistics calculated from the study confirmed a high level of agreement, validating the success of these training efforts. Prior to full implementation, the adapted rubric was subjected to a thorough content validation process involving a panel of experts, including experienced nurse educators, simulation specialists, and regulatory body representatives. The panel reviewed each rubric item for relevance, clarity, and alignment with national clinical competencies established by the Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) of Ghana. The rubric achieved a Content Validity Index (CVI) above 0.80 for all items, confirming strong content alignment. Following the expert review, a pilot study was conducted with 15 final-year students from a non-participating institution. Feedback from this pilot informed refinements to item wording and scoring descriptors to enhance usability during high-fidelity simulation sessions. The structured rubric provided comprehensive coverage of both the cognitive and psychomotor dimensions of procedural competence. It was also theoretically grounded in the scoring and generalization inferences of Kane’s Validity Framework. The rubric’s capacity to generate consistent, objective scores contributed directly to the reliability of the assessment process, while its ability to measure a coherent set of competencies across different procedures supported claims of construct validity. Additionally, the standardization of criteria and scoring scales made it suitable for both formative feedback and high-stakes summative evaluations. Overall, the simulation-based assessment rubric proved to be a psychometrically sound and contextually appropriate instrument for evaluating procedural skills among nursing students in Ghana’s evolving competency-based education system.

Rater training and calibration

Prior to data collection, calibration workshops were organized across the participating institutions to ensure consistency and objectivity in scoring simulation-based assessments. These sessions were designed to train assessors on the interpretation of rubric criteria, standardization of score ranges, and strategies for minimizing rater bias during performance evaluations. To promote shared understanding and alignment, facilitators used sample video recordings of prior simulation sessions as reference cases. Assessors reviewed these examples collaboratively, discussed discrepancies, and reached consensus on appropriate score assignments for various levels of performance. During the main assessment phase, rater agreement was continuously monitored through the computation of intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs), providing empirical evidence of scoring reliability across raters and institutions.

Objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) scores

Students’ Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) scores were retrieved from institutional academic records corresponding to the same semester during which the simulation-based assessments were administered. The OSCE is a well-established, structured method of evaluating clinical competence through direct observation of students’ performance on a series of standardized tasks. Each student was required to rotate through multiple clinical stations, with each station designed to assess a discrete clinical skill such as wound care, medication administration, communication with patients, or basic life support. The stations were conducted under strictly controlled conditions and timed intervals, typically ranging from 5 to 10 min per station. Trained faculty members served as examiners and assessed students using detailed checklists that aligned with the national clinical competency standards prescribed by the Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) of Ghana. These checklists evaluated both procedural accuracy and non-technical skills such as communication, critical thinking, infection prevention, and patient safety. All OSCE stations were piloted and validated internally prior to implementation to ensure content relevance, task realism, and scoring objectivity. The OSCE scores provided a critical external measure of clinical competence, widely recognized for their reliability and validity in assessing performance under pressure. In the context of this study, the OSCE served as both a concurrent validity benchmark and a comparator for simulation performance. Because OSCEs are standardized across most nursing training institutions in Ghana, they provided a stable reference point to assess whether simulation-based assessments measured overlapping skill domains. Correlations between OSCE and simulation scores thus contributed evidence toward the generalization and extrapolation inferences of Kane’s Validity Framework. A moderate positive correlation (r = 0.45, p < 0.01) between the two sets of scores suggests that while distinct in format, both assessments captured similar constructs of procedural and clinical reasoning competence. Moreover, the use of OSCE data strengthened the study’s methodological triangulation by offering a contrasting yet complementary method of clinical assessment. This alignment allowed for deeper exploration of whether simulation assessments could predict or correspond with performance in established high-stakes examinations. Additionally, the agreement between pass/fail decisions in OSCE and simulation results (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.67) provided further evidence of decision consistency, reinforcing the implication inference for using simulation scores in progression and licensure decisions.

Clinical practicum evaluation scores

The third instrument comprised the end-of-placement evaluation reports completed by clinical supervisors during students’ final clinical postings. These structured evaluations were based on standardized forms developed by the NMC and assessed key performance areas such as procedural accuracy, patient safety, infection control, critical thinking, and adherence to professional standards. Ratings were typically provided using a numerical scale and included both observational data and summative judgments from preceptors. These evaluation scores were included to support extrapolation inferences, as they reflected real-world clinical competence across various practice settings. Their inclusion allowed the study to assess whether high scores in simulation correlated with competent performance in authentic clinical environments. All scores from the three instruments were numerically coded to enable quantitative analysis. Data from different institutions were standardized in format to ensure comparability. Calibration workshops were conducted prior to data collection to harmonize scoring approaches and rubric interpretations across assessors and institutions.

Data collection procedure

The data collection process followed a systematic, ethically guided protocol across the three participating nursing training institutions. Prior to data collection, formal approval was obtained from the institutional heads of each school, who facilitated access to student assessment records and coordinated with internal research liaisons. Eligible students were approached, and written informed consent was obtained after explaining the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and measures taken to ensure confidentiality. Data were collected retrospectively from academic records and anonymized by assigning unique identifiers to each participant. The simulation-based assessment scores had been rated by two independent faculty assessors at the time of assessment. Copies of the OSCE scores and clinical practicum evaluations were retrieved from the schools’ examination and clinical coordination units. To assess the scoring reliability of simulation-based assessments, the researcher reviewed the scores assigned by both raters for each student and computed inter-rater reliability using the intra-class correlation coefficient. The data collection period lasted approximately six weeks and was closely monitored to ensure consistency in data extraction protocols and to resolve any discrepancies in scoring formats or documentation across the institutions. All collected data were securely stored in password-protected digital files and locked cabinets where physical documents were involved.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were entered into IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26 for analysis. A range of descriptive and inferential statistical techniques were employed, organized around the four key inferences of the Kane Validity Framework. To assess the consistency and reliability of simulation scores, inter-rater reliability was computed using the Intra-Class Correlation Coefficient (ICC). High ICC values (≥ 0.75) were interpreted as indicative of good agreement between independent raters. Additionally, the internal consistency of the adapted CCEI rubric was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with values above 0.80 considered acceptable for high-stakes assessments. To evaluate the extent to which simulation scores generalized to other performance-based assessments, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated between students’ simulation scores and OSCE scores. Moderate to strong positive correlations (r ≥ 0.30) were expected if both assessments measured overlapping domains of procedural competence. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the predictive validity of simulation scores in explaining variance in clinical practicum evaluation scores. Independent variables included simulation scores and OSCE scores, while the dependent variable was the practicum evaluation rating. Where preliminary analysis suggested significant institutional variation in scores, hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) was considered to account for the nested structure of the data (students nested within institutions). To assess the utility of simulation scores for decision-making, descriptive statistics were used to explore the distribution of scores across students. In addition, decision consistency analysis was conducted to determine the alignment between simulation pass/fail decisions and those from OSCEs and clinical practicum outcomes. Cut-score analysis was performed to evaluate whether the simulation thresholds used for passing aligned with real-world competence as evidenced in other assessments. All inferential analyses were conducted at a significance level of p < 0.05, and effect sizes (e.g., Cohen’s d, R²) were reported to indicate the practical significance of the findings.

Validity and reliability of instruments

Prior to full implementation, all three instruments underwent rigorous content validation and pilot testing. The simulation rubric and clinical evaluation tools were subjected to expert review by panels comprising senior nursing faculty, simulation specialists, and curriculum experts from the participating institutions. The review focused on alignment with Ghanaian clinical training standards, clarity of items, and coverage of essential competencies. Following expert review, a pilot study was conducted with 15 final-year nursing students from institutions not included in the main study. Feedback from this pilot informed minor revisions to improve item clarity and scoring guidance. The Content Validity Index (CVI) was computed based on expert ratings of relevance and clarity, with all subscales achieving CVI values above 0.80, indicating strong content validity.

During full-scale data analysis, construct validity was further assessed using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to examine the underlying structure of the simulation and practicum assessment tools. Factor loadings and internal consistency metrics supported the unidimensionality of key domains, lending support to the validity of using these instruments for evaluating procedural competence.

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to rigorous ethical standards throughout the research process. Ethical clearance was obtained from the University of Education, Winneba Institutional Review Board (IRB), and additional permission was sought from the administrative heads of the participating institutions. All ethical protocols were strictly followed to ensure the protection of participants’ rights and data. Participation was entirely voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all student participants after a full explanation of the study’s objectives, data usage, and confidentiality measures. Students were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without penalty. To maintain confidentiality, all data were anonymized using coded identifiers. Personally, identifiable information was removed from all records prior to analysis. Hard copy documents were stored in locked filing cabinets, and digital data were encrypted and stored on password-protected computers accessible only to the research team. All procedures were guided by principles of beneficence, autonomy, and justice, in accordance with standard ethical frameworks for human subjects research (Israel & Hay, 2006). These considerations ensured that the research met both institutional and international standards for ethical conduct.

Lower communalities, such as for documentation (0.38), suggest slightly weaker but still meaningful contributions, possibly due to variability in how documentation is emphasized or taught across institutions.

The unidimensional factor structure supports the use of a total score for summative or high-stakes assessment decisions, while the high internal consistency and item discrimination metrics enhance confidence in its interpretive use within the Kane Validity Framework, particularly under the scoring and generalization inferences.

Discussion of results

This study set out to evaluate the reliability and construct validity of high-fidelity simulation-based assessments (HFSAs) in Ghanaian nursing education using Kane’s Validity Framework as the guiding theoretical lens. The findings contribute meaningful empirical evidence in support of simulation-based performance assessments as reliable and valid tools for measuring procedural competence in pre-service nursing education. In the context of Ghana where traditional assessments often suffer from variability, subjectivity, and access constraints these findings are particularly salient [57–59, 75, 78]. They demonstrate the potential for simulation to improve assessment quality, educational equity, and decision-making in nursing licensure and professional readiness. The scoring inference which examines whether scores are consistent, replicable, and free from measurement error was strongly supported. Inter-rater reliability coefficients ranging from ICC = 0.77 to 0.84 indicate a high degree of agreement among independent raters evaluating student performance on the simulation rubric. This is a crucial finding given that subjective judgments can compromise the objectivity of clinical assessments. The strength of this agreement suggests that rater calibration workshops were effective in standardizing the application of rubric criteria across institutions and scenarios [17, 28, 30, 61]. Additionally, the internal consistency of the simulation rubric, demonstrated by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83, confirms that items on the instrument measure a unified construct procedural competence. High item-total correlations and a split-half reliability coefficient of 0.79 further affirm the internal coherence of the instrument. Together, these findings validate the instrument as not only statistically robust but also practically implementable in high-stakes decision-making contexts. This emphasis on reliable scoring is particularly relevant in Ghana, where OSCEs and clinical practicum evaluations may lack formal mechanisms for rater standardization [14, 35, 37, 68]. The simulation rubric used in this study offers a model for how structured assessment tools can raise standards of fairness and transparency. In an educational landscape increasingly driven by accountability, such instruments offer a defensible alternative to unstructured evaluations [49, 51, 52, 70].

The generalization inference examines whether test scores remain stable across varied conditions, raters, or populations. The moderate yet statistically significant correlation between simulation scores and OSCE results (r = 0.45) indicates that both assessments tap into overlapping constructs of procedural competence. This convergence supports the argument that simulation-based assessments are not anomalous or artificially inflated experiences, but rather complementary tools that extend the assessment portfolio. Additionally, the simulation scores explained 42% of the variance in clinical practicum performance, offering further generalizability evidence. This finding suggests that students who performed well in simulation were also likely to exhibit competence in real-world clinical environments, even under different conditions and evaluators [9, 32, 35, 42, 72, 82]. Importantly, this strengthens confidence in simulation as a predictor of readiness for patient care. The low percentage of score variance attributed to institutional affiliation (ICC = 0.18) offers further support for generalizability. Despite differences in institutional resources, faculty composition, or student demographics, simulation-based assessments produced relatively stable results. This is highly significant in Ghana, where disparities in educational infrastructure exist between urban and rural training colleges. The findings imply that with minimal baseline resources (e.g., trained raters and mid- to high-fidelity manikins), simulation can offer a consistent and scalable model of performance assessment across diverse contexts [11, 12, 25, 34, 36, 76, 77].

The extrapolation and implication inferences are central to the construct validity argument. They address whether simulation scores can meaningfully predict performance in future, real-world settings and whether the decisions based on those scores (e.g., pass/fail, readiness for licensure) are justified. Here, the evidence was particularly compelling. Regression analysis revealed that simulation scores significantly predicted clinical performance during practicum (R² = 0.39), suggesting that students who demonstrated procedural competence in simulated scenarios were more likely to perform effectively in authentic patient care situations [3, 6, 15, 29, 81]. This reinforces Kane’s extrapolation inference, showing that simulated performance is not confined to the artificiality of the lab, but maps meaningfully onto actual clinical competence. The decision consistency between simulation scores and OSCE pass/fail outcomes (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.67) lends strong support to the implication inference. It indicates that simulation-based decisions are aligned with other high-stakes evaluative judgments, such as those used in traditional OSCEs. These results underscore the potential of simulation not only to assess what students know, but also to inform credible decisions about what they are ready to do in practice [1, 9, 68, 79]. In the Ghanaian context, where clinical placements are often overcrowded, faculty supervision is limited, and opportunities for hands-on practice may be unequally distributed, simulation-based assessments offer a standardized, equitable, and transparent mechanism for verifying competence. They provide educators and regulators with an alternative pathway for verifying that graduating students are safe, prepared, and ready for licensure. As Ghana continues to implement Competency-Based Education (CBE) models, this study highlights how simulation can operationalize CBE principles in both teaching and assessment, especially when traditional clinical exposures are insufficient or inconsistent.

Theoretical framing and practical reflections

The application of Kane’s Validity Framework served not only as a conceptual guide but also as a methodological scaffold for organizing and interpreting the study’s psychometric evidence. Kane’s model moves beyond simplistic notions of validity as a single statistical indicator, instead offering a layered approach that interrogates the chain of inferences made from assessment scores. These inferences scoring, generalization, extrapolation, and implication are particularly useful in evaluating performance-based assessments like high-fidelity simulations, where judgments extend beyond numerical scores to real-world consequences such as clinical licensure and patient safety. In contrast to fragmented validation strategies that treat reliability and criterion validity as isolated metrics, Kane’s framework encourages researchers and decision-makers to consider the entire interpretive argument. It poses a critical question at each stage: Are we scoring student performance consistently? Are those scores generalizable across tasks and settings? Do they predict meaningful outcomes beyond the test? And are the decisions made based on these scores defensible and fair? This approach has significant practical implications for health professions education in Ghana and other LMICs, where simulation-based technologies are increasingly adopted but often without rigorous contextual validation. In many cases, educational interventions including simulation equipment, curricula, and assessment rubrics are imported wholesale from high-income countries (HICs) with vastly different clinical environments, cultural norms, and resource constraints. Without localized validation, there is a risk of applying performance criteria that do not align with the actual practice contexts or competencies required in local healthcare systems.

By applying Kane’s framework in a Ghanaian setting, this study demonstrates how a globally respected model of validity can be localized to assess whether simulation-based assessments are fit for purpose in under-resourced environments. Each inferential link in the validity chain was empirically tested using data from local nursing schools, trained assessors, and existing regulatory tools (e.g., OSCEs and practicum evaluations). In doing so, the study offers a methodological blueprint for future simulation research in LMICs one that balances international best practices with contextual sensitivity. Moreover, the theoretical framing provided by Kane encourages a shift in educational leadership and policy from compliance-based implementation to evidence-informed practice. Rather than adopting simulation simply because it is a global trend, institutions can use frameworks like Kane’s to build locally grounded validity arguments that justify the use of simulation in assessment, curriculum reform, and accreditation processes. This level of reflection is crucial for health professions education systems striving for sustainability, equity, and relevance in the face of evolving healthcare demands. Ultimately, the structured application of Kane’s Validity Framework does more than affirm the statistical soundness of the simulation-based assessments it legitimizes them as ethically responsible and pedagogically meaningful tools in preparing the next generation of nurses in Ghana and similar contexts. It bridges the often-fragmented worlds of psychometrics, educational practice, and policy-making by providing a common language for interpreting and improving assessment quality.

Conclusion

This study offers promising evidence supporting the potential of High-Fidelity Simulation-Based Assessments (HFSAs) as reliable and valid tools for evaluating procedural competence in Ghanaian nursing education. Across multiple psychometric analyses, simulation scores demonstrated acceptable levels of inter-rater reliability, generalizability, and predictive validity. These findings suggest that HFSAs may serve as credible complements or in some contexts, viable alternatives to traditional assessment modalities such as OSCEs and clinical practicum evaluations, particularly where clinical learning environments are overstretched or inconsistently supervised. While these results align with global trends in competency-based nursing education, caution is warranted in interpreting simulation as a universal solution. The study’s implementation benefited from purposively selected institutions with some degree of simulation infrastructure and trained faculty. Wider adoption may encounter practical constraints, including limited access to high-fidelity manikins, uneven rater training capacity, and insufficient institutional funding to maintain and scale simulation centers. Moreover, the introduction of simulation-based assessments must be culturally responsive and aligned with national regulatory frameworks to ensure long-term sustainability and acceptance. Nonetheless, the use of Kane’s Validity Framework allowed for a structured and transparent validation process, encouraging institutions to approach simulation not only as a pedagogical innovation, but as an assessment strategy grounded in evidence. For curriculum developers, regulatory bodies like the Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC), and academic leaders, the study offers a foundation for rethinking existing models of clinical evaluation. With thoughtful investment in faculty development, context-sensitive standard-setting, and gradual infrastructure enhancement, simulation-based assessments could strengthen transparency, fairness, and competence assurance in Ghana’s nursing education system. Looking forward, additional research is needed to evaluate the long-term outcomes of simulation-based training, particularly its impact on clinical decision-making, patient safety, and interprofessional teamwork. Qualitative investigations into student and educator perceptions of simulation-based assessments—especially around issues of fairness, anxiety, and engagement would also offer valuable insights. While HFSAs cannot address all the systemic challenges in health education, they represent a strategic opportunity to modernize assessment practices and better prepare graduates for the demands of contemporary healthcare delivery. In sum, this study contributes to the emerging body of African scholarship on simulation-based education by illustrating how structured validation approaches can bridge innovation with contextual relevance. HFSAs, when carefully implemented and continuously evaluated, hold promise as part of a broader shift toward more accountable, equitable, and outcomes-oriented nursing education in Ghana and comparable settings.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, it is recommended that nursing education stakeholders in Ghana particularly the Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC), nursing faculties, and health training institutions integrate High-Fidelity Simulation-Based Assessments (HFSAs) into both formative and summative evaluation frameworks. Given the demonstrated reliability and predictive validity of HFSAs, their adoption can help ensure fairer, more standardized measurement of clinical competence, especially in settings where clinical exposure may be inconsistent or limited. To support this integration, institutional investment in simulation infrastructure is essential. This includes the procurement of advanced manikins, simulation software, and equipment that accurately mimic real-life clinical scenarios. However, the success of simulation-based education does not rest solely on the availability of tools; it also requires well-trained personnel. As such, faculty development programs should be instituted to build educators’ capacity in simulation design, facilitation, and scoring. Partnerships with international institutions or simulation networks can be leveraged to accelerate this upskilling process. At the policy level, it is recommended that the Ministry of Health and the Ghana Tertiary Education Commission (GTEC) develop national simulation guidelines and standards. These guidelines should cover aspects such as minimum simulation hours, ethical considerations, assessment rubrics, and standard-setting procedures to ensure consistency and equity across institutions. A phased implementation strategy, beginning with pilot institutions, could allow for iterative improvements before broader scale-up. Furthermore, continuous research and monitoring should accompany the rollout of simulation-based assessments. Educational researchers should be encouraged to investigate the long-term impacts of simulation training on clinical judgment, patient safety, and professional readiness. In addition, studies that examine the cost-effectiveness of simulations in relation to traditional assessment methods will be valuable for guiding policy decisions and institutional budgeting. Finally, the adoption of HFSAs should be framed within a broader move towards competency-based nursing education in Ghana. Simulation should not be viewed as a standalone assessment tool but rather as a key component of a comprehensive curriculum that integrates theory, skills, and professional values. In doing so, Ghana can position itself as a regional leader in innovative, high-quality nursing education that meets both local health needs and global standards.

Limitations of the study

Despite the robustness of the findings, this study is not without limitations. First, the research was conducted within a limited number of nursing training institutions in Ghana, which may affect the generalizability of the results to the broader population of nursing schools across the country or in other sub-Saharan African contexts. Institutional differences in simulation resources, faculty expertise, and student demographics may introduce contextual biases that could influence the reliability and predictive validity of simulation-based assessments. Moreover, the use of convenience sampling for participant selection may have introduced sampling bias, potentially affecting the representativeness of the findings. Second, while the study employed a range of psychometric and statistical methods to assess reliability and validity, it relied primarily on quantitative data. This approach, while useful for identifying patterns and relationships, does not capture the nuanced experiences and perceptions of students and instructors involved in simulation-based assessments. As such, the study may have overlooked important qualitative factors such as anxiety levels, motivation, or perceived fairness, which could influence performance and acceptance of simulations. Future research incorporating mixed-methods designs would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness and acceptability of HFSAs in the Ghanaian nursing education context.
