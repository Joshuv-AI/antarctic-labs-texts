The Associations Between Acid‐Suppressive Drug Intake and Symptom Profiles in Functional Dyspepsia Patients Referred for Open‐Access Endoscopy
Authors: Scheepers Janne, Carbone Florencia, Matheussen Hanne, Van den Houte Karen, Tack Jan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFunctional dyspepsia (FD) is characterised by postprandial fullness, early satiation, epigastric pain, or burning without structural abnormalities on upper endoscopy. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are the preferred first‐line therapy. Given that the diagnosis of FD is largely based on symptom assessment, it remains unclear whether acid‐suppression meaningfully influences symptom presentation.ObjectiveThis exploratory study aimed to determine the prevalence of acid‐suppressive therapy (PPIs or H2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs)) among FD patients and to compare symptom patterns between patients with and without these treatments.MethodsIn this single‐centre, cross‐sectional observational study, ambulatory patients referred for diagnostic upper endoscopy completed the Waiting Room Questionnaire (WRQ). Endoscopic findings were recorded, and FD was defined using Rome IV. Acid‐suppression was defined as self‐reported use of PPIs or H2RAs. Symptom frequency was compared between patients on and off therapy.Results1158 patients completed the WRQ; 27% fulfiled the criteria for FD. Among FD patients, 60% reported acid‐suppressive therapy, similar to patients with endoscopic findings. FD patients receiving acid‐suppression reported higher frequencies of early satiation (OR = 1.68, 95% CI 1.08–2.60), nausea (OR = 1.93, 95% CI 1.23–3.01), and epigastric pain (OR = 1.74, 95% CI 1.12–2.69). In subgroup analyses, therapy was associated with more frequent epigastric burning in postprandial distress syndrome (OR = 3.81, 95% CI 1.50–9.67) and higher frequencies of postprandial fullness (OR = 2.90, 95% CI 1.24–6.80) and early satiation (OR = 2.34, 95% CI 1.13–4.85) in the overlap subgroup.ConclusionsFD is prevalent in open‐access endoscopy, and acid‐suppressive therapy is frequently used in this population. FD patients receiving acid‐suppression reported slightly higher frequencies of several symptoms, highlighting the need to further explore these associations.

Introduction

Functional dyspepsia (FD) is a prevalent disorder of gut‐brain interaction, defined by the Rome IV criteria as symptoms affecting daily life which are thought to originate from the gastroduodenal region, such as postprandial fullness, early satiation, epigastric pain and burning, without underlying organic disease likely to explain the symptoms. FD affects an estimated 7.2% of the global population and represents a significant burden on healthcare systems and patients' quality of life [1, 2].

FD is categorised into two subtypes based on symptom presentation: postprandial distress syndrome (PDS), where the main symptoms are bothersome meal‐related fullness or early satiation at least three times per week, and epigastric pain syndrome (EPS), characterised by pain or burning in the epigastric region at least once per week. In the Rome IV global epidemiology study, the majority of patients (67%) fulfil PDS criteria, while 18% of patients exhibit overlapping features of PDS and EPS, and 15% exhibits only EPS symptoms [1]. The diagnosis of FD requires exclusion of underlying organic causes, typically through upper endoscopy, and relies on symptom assessment to determine whether the patient meets the diagnostic criteria.

According to the UEG and ESNM consensus on FD, management should begin with the eradication of Helicobacter pylori when present. Otherwise, first‐line therapy involves acid‐suppression with a proton pump inhibitor (PPI), regardless of the subgroup. If symptoms persist, prokinetic agents may be considered. For patients who do not adequately respond to these initial treatments, central neuromodulators such as low‐dose tricyclic antidepressants are introduced [3].

A study from our group showed that symptoms improved with initiation of PPI therapy but also with the interruption of therapy in FD patients with longer‐standing PPI use [4]. While PPIs alleviate symptoms in some patients, particularly those with epigastric pain, their overall efficacy is limited, with an estimated number needed to treat for an additional beneficial outcome of 15 [5]. This modest benefit must be weighed against the consequences of prolonged use, mainly drug interactions, risk of infectious gastroenteritis, and rebound acid hypersecretion upon discontinuation, potentially reinforcing continued therapy [6]. As a result, PPIs and other acid‐suppressive agents, such as H2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs), are overutilized in patients with FD.

In addition to these clinical challenges, PPIs are a frequently prescribed class of medication and are considered to generate high healthcare costs, both in Belgium and globally [7, 8, 9, 10]. Given that the diagnosis of FD is largely based on symptom assessment, it remains unclear whether acid‐suppressive therapy meaningfully influences symptom presentation. A better understanding of how these drugs influence symptom patterns is needed.

This study aims to determine the prevalence of acid‐suppressive therapy (PPI or H2RA) among patients diagnosed with FD in an open‐access endoscopy setting. Second, as a hypothesis‐generating endpoint, gastrointestinal symptom patterns were compared between patients with FD and its subgroups on and off acid‐suppressive therapy to evaluate whether these treatments are associated with distinct symptom profiles.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional observational study involved ambulatory adult patients referred for upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy at a Belgian open‐access endoscopy setting (UZ Leuven). Consecutive patients presenting at the endoscopy unit for a diagnostic upper endoscopy were invited to complete the self‐administered waiting room questionnaire (WRQ) [11]. This questionnaire includes the Rome IV gastro‐duodenal questionnaire with pictograms, a 4‐item heartburn questionnaire [12], and a question identifying their most bothersome symptoms. Demographic factors were collected, including age, gender, height, weight, smoking behaviour (yes/no), daily alcohol consumption (yes/no), weight loss, and intake of acid‐suppressive therapy. The questionnaire was provided in French and Dutch to native speakers. The endoscopic outcome was documented. The main endoscopic findings were categorised as GERD (according to LA classification [13]), Barrett's oesophagus, peptic ulcer, and malignancy. Other endoscopic findings (such as hiatal hernia) were noted but not analysed as a separate category.

For the present analysis, we defined FD based on the Rome IV criteria, including only patients with no relevant endoscopic findings able to explain the symptoms. Negative or minimally abnormal outcomes such as hiatal hernia and Grade A GERD were therefore considered acceptable for inclusion in this exploratory analysis. Patients with previous abdominal surgery or intake of Nonsteroidal Anti‐Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) were excluded from diagnosis of FD.

Classification of Acid‐Suppressive Therapy

Patients were classified as being on or off acid‐suppressive therapy, defined as PPIs and H2Ras, based on self‐reported medication use, this was determined using the yes/no question: ‘Do you currently use medication for stomach acid, such as omeprazole, Losec, Logagastric, Nexiam, Dakar, lansoprazole, Pantozol, Zurcale, Zantac, ranitidine, Tagamet, cimetidine, Pantomed, or pantoprazole,…?’ In addition, responses to the question ‘Which other medication do you take?’ were reviewed to identify any reported use of PPIs or H2RAs. Frequency, dosage, duration, and acid‐suppressant type were not assessed.

Symptom Assessment

FD symptoms were assessed using the WRQ, applying the predefined Rome IV frequency thresholds to determine symptom presence [14]. Based on this, the prevalence of individual symptoms, including bloating, nausea, excessive belching, postprandial fullness, early satiation, epigastric pain and epigastric burning, were compared between patients on and off acid‐suppressive therapy [11].

The presence of heartburn was assessed using a part of the WRQ that has demonstrated concordance with increased oesophageal acid exposure during pH monitoring. A positive questionnaire was defined by four positive answers in this section [12].

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were summarised. Group comparisons were performed in two ways: (i) Symptoms were dichotomised according to the established Rome IV thresholds, and associations with PPI use were assessed using multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, and daily alcohol intake. (ii) Using the frequency scores as ordinal variables, assessed using a multivariable linear regression model, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, and daily alcohol intake. Adjusted odds ratios (OR) are presented with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Given the exploratory nature of this trial, no corrections for multiple testing were applied for the main results. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion. A p‐value equal to or lower than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Demographical data are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version30.0.0.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) or GraphPad Prism (version10.5.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee in UZ Leuven (S54013). Patients have been included in two waves: one in 2007 and one between 2012 and 2015. All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participants

In total, 1158 ambulatory patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy completed the WRQ. The overall cohort was 59% female, with a median age of 49 (16) years and a median BMI of 25 (5) kg/m2 (Table 1). Endoscopic evidence for GERD was noted in 386 patients (33%): 283 patients with grade A, 87 with grade B, 14 with grade C, and 2 with grade D. Barrett's oesophagus was documented in 69 patients (6%), peptic ulcer disease in 55 patients (5%), and malignancies in 17 patients (1%). No endoscopic findings, including no hiatal hernia or low‐grade oesophagitis, were found in 289 patients (25%).

In total, 308 patients (27% of the entire cohort) were diagnosed with FD. This cohort included 89 patients diagnosed with overlapping GERD grade A.

The proportion of female patients was significantly higher in the FD cohort compared with the remainder of the study population (71% vs. 55%, p < 0.0001). Patients with FD were significantly younger compared with non‐FD patients (44.5 (28) versus 50 (25) years, p = 0.0002), and had a significantly lower BMI (23.3 (6.5) kg/m2 versus. 24.8 (6.5) kg/m2, p = 0.0004). No significant differences were observed between the groups with respect to smoking status or alcohol consumption.

Among the 308 FD patients, 47 (15%) had a positive response on the heartburn section of the questionnaire. Of these, 29 patients (9% of the total FD cohort) lacked endoscopic evidence of gastroesophageal reflux. The proportion of patients with a positive reflux questionnaire was similar among those receiving and not receiving acid‐suppressive therapy (18% vs. 12%, respectively, p > 0.05), with comparable rates of functional heartburn in both groups (9% vs. 8%, respectively, p > 0.05).

Use of Acid‐Suppressive Therapy

Among FD patients, 60% reported current use of acid‐suppressive therapy. This rate of usage was significantly higher in patients with FD compared with the remainder of the study population (63% vs. 56%, p = 0.04). Data on acid‐suppressive therapy were missing for 12 FD patients. No differences in BMI or gender distribution were observed between patients receiving and not receiving acid‐suppression. A numerically, but not significantly higher age was observed among patients receiving acid‐suppressive therapy compared to those not receiving acid‐suppressive therapy (median age 47 (28) versus. 40 (26) years, p = 0.069). There was no significant difference in acid‐suppressant intake between the diagnostic groups: 56% usage in GERD, 74% in Barrett oesophagus, 55% in peptic ulcer, and 65% in malignant disease.

Within the FD cohort, use of PPIs or H2RAs showed modest variation across symptom subtypes: 61% of patients with pure EPS, 56% of those with pure PDS, and 70% of patients with EPS–PDS‐overlap were receiving acid‐suppressive therapy (p > 0.05).

Symptom Profiles in FD Patients With and Without Acid‐Suppression

Among patients with FD, the most frequently reported symptoms above the Rome threshold were postprandial fullness (74%), bloating (68%), and epigastric pain (67%).

Significant differences were noted in frequencies of early satiation, nausea and epigastric pain. Early satiation was more common among patients taking acid‐suppression, when comparing frequencies above versus below the Rome threshold (50% vs. 36% respectively, OR = 1.70, 95% CI 1.01–2.88) (Figure 1, Table S1). This association was corroborated when symptom frequency was analysed as an ordinal variable (OR = 1.70, 95% CI 1.08–2.60) (Figure 2). Similarly, nausea occurred more frequently in patients receiving acid‐suppressive therapy, based on the binary comparisons (48% vs. 36% respectively, OR = 1.93, 95% CI 1.11–3.40); this difference remained significant in the ordinal analysis (OR = 1.93, 95% CI 1.23–3.01). Both in the binary comparison (73% on acid‐suppressants vs. 63% off acid‐suppressants, OR = 1.96, 95% CI 1.10–3.63) and the ordinal analysis (OR = 1.74, 95% CI 1.12–2.69), epigastric pain was significantly more frequent among patients taking acid‐suppressants. No differences were found in frequency scores for postprandial fullness, bloating, excessive belching, and epigastric burning.

FD Rome IV Subgroup Analysis

A subgroup analysis was conducted by stratifying the FD cohort into the Rome IV subtypes. In the EPS subgroup (n = 77), no statistically significant differences were observed between patients receiving and not receiving acid‐suppression.

In the PDS subgroup (n = 106), epigastric burning occurred more frequently among patients receiving acid‐suppressive therapy only when symptom frequency was analysed as an ordinal variable (OR = 3.81, 95% CI 1.5–9.67). Note that the symptom duration did not meet the criteria for EPS–PDS overlap. Nausea was more frequent in the binary analysis (OR = 2.97, 95% CI 1.11–8.65). This association was not confirmed in ordinal analyses.

In the EPS–PDS overlap subgroup (n = 125), patients receiving acid‐suppressive therapy reported higher frequencies of postprandial fullness and early satiation. For postprandial fullness, binary frequency scores were significantly higher (98% on acid‐suppressants vs. 89% off acid‐suppressants, OR = 9.06, 95% CI 1.142–190.9), a finding that was confirmed in the ordinal analysis (OR = 2.9, 95% CI 1.24–6.80). For early satiation, a numerically increased frequency was observed in the binary analysis (67% on acid‐suppressants vs. 50% off acid‐suppressants, OR 2.33, 95% CI 0.89–7.35), and ordinal frequency analysis demonstrated a statistically significant increase among patients receiving acid‐suppressive therapy (OR = 2.34, 95% CI 1.13–4.85).

Discussion

In this exploratory cross‐sectional study, 1158 ambulatory patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy completed the WRQ. In one quarter of patients, no endoscopic abnormalities were identified. Nearly one‐third of patients fulfiled the Rome IV criteria for FD, underscoring the high prevalence of FD in patients referred for open‐access endoscopy. These results are consistent with the expected prevalence rates [15]. More than half of FD patients reported the use of acid‐suppressive therapy, a proportion comparable to that observed in patients with organic endoscopic findings. Postprandial fullness, bloating, and epigastric pain were the most commonly reported symptoms among patients with FD. Within the overall FD cohort, patients receiving acid‐suppressive therapy reported higher frequencies of several symptoms: early satiation, nausea, and epigastric pain. When stratified by FD subtype, no significant differences in symptom frequency were observed in patients with EPS. In contrast, patients with PDS receiving acid‐suppressive therapy reported higher frequencies of epigastric burning. In the EPS–PDS‐overlap group, acid‐suppression was associated with increased frequencies of postprandial fullness and early satiation.

The high prevalence of acid‐suppressive therapy observed in the FD cohort, 60%, most likely reflects current treatment recommendations in which PPIs are considered a first‐line treatment for FD. A notable observation is that acid‐suppressive therapy was numerically more prevalent among patients with FD than among those diagnosed with GERD or peptic ulcers. This finding further underscores the high rate of PPI use within the FD cohort. The widespread use of acid‐suppression in this population reinforces the importance of adhering to guideline recommendations, which advocate a limited therapeutic trial of 4–8 weeks followed by reassessment of treatment [3].

In a recent epidemiological trial using the Rome IV foundation global epidemiology database, an acid‐suppressive intake of 43.0% was noted in patients with FD [16]. However, our study population comprised patients referred for upper endoscopy at a referral centre, who may represent a subgroup with a higher symptom burden or more refractory symptoms. This referral pattern may partially explain the high rate of acid‐suppressive therapy use. The questionnaire did not capture information regarding dosage or regimen; the analysis included both patients on on‐demand and continuous therapy.

The subtle differences in symptom frequency observed in this study have several plausible explanations. An important consideration is that the diagnosis of FD was established irrespective of current acid‐suppressive therapy use. Consequently, a subgroup of patients who initially presented with dyspeptic symptoms and were treated empirically with acid‐suppressive therapy may have experienced sufficient symptom improvement to no longer meet the Rome IV criteria for FD at the time of assessment. Patients who respond favourably to acid‐suppression are therefore underrepresented in the present analysis, introducing a potential selection bias that should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. On the other hand, it is customary for patients with FD to present to specialist care only when acid‐suppressive therapy has been explored.

In addition, patients with a higher baseline symptom burden or more refractory symptoms are more likely to be prescribed acid‐suppression and may also display only a partial response. As a result, the higher frequency of certain symptoms observed among patients receiving acid‐suppression may, at least in part, reflect greater underlying symptom severity rather than an effect of the acid‐suppression itself.

The associations observed between acid‐suppressive therapy and increased frequencies of early satiation and nausea are noteworthy, as these symptoms are not primarily acid‐mediated and are more closely linked to possible mechanisms of FD, such as impaired gastric accommodation and delayed gastric emptying.

As this is a cross‐sectional trial, no causal link can be made regarding the obtained results. However, preliminary findings from a prospective trial suggest a potential mechanistic link. In that trial, discontinuation of chronic PPI therapy in patients without a stringent indication for continued therapy was associated with a significant reduction in the severity of bloating, postprandial fullness, and early satiation [17]. These findings raise the possibility that acid‐suppressive therapy may contribute to symptom burden in a subset of patients.

In this study, patients were eligible for an FD diagnosis when GERD grade A was present. The Lyon Consensus 2.0 classifies such lesions as inconclusive for GERD [18]; in patients with grade A oesophagitis, symptoms cannot be confidently attributed to reflux disease. This was also found in the Kalixanda study, revealing high prevalences of grade A oesophagitis in asymptomatic subjects [19]. In our study, acid‐suppressing therapy was restricted to PPIs and H2RAs without the inclusion of potassium‐competitive acid blockers (PCABs), which were not marketed in Europe at the time of the trial. As PCABs provide more potent acid inhibition, it is conceivable that some patients may have had incomplete acid suppression or PPI‐resistant GERD [20, 21]. Although this probably represents a small group [22], it may have influenced symptom patterns and should be considered when interpreting our findings.

This study has several important strengths. A cohort of 1158 ambulatory patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was included, providing substantial power and reflecting routine clinical practice in a referral care setting. FD was defined according to Rome IV criteria, and symptom assessment was performed using the validated WRQ. Analysis of symptom frequency as ordinal variables preserved maximal informational content, while dichotomisation according to Rome IV criteria allowed more clinically meaningful interpretations.

Several limitations should be noted. The cross‐sectional design precludes any causal interpretation between acid‐suppressive therapy and symptom patterns. Second, acid‐suppressive therapy use was self‐reported, introducing the possibility of misclassification. Medication use may have been underreported due to incomplete knowledge of treatment, leading to a potential underestimation of acid‐suppressive intake. Moreover, no detailed information was available regarding dosage, duration, acid‐suppressant type, or indication for therapy, limiting more granular analysis. The intake of antacids was not assessed. This may have influenced symptom reporting, particularly in patients with epigastric pain, and some participants may have misclassified antacids as acid‐suppressants. All symptoms and medication were self‐reported, which may introduce recall bias.

Although this study applied the Rome IV criteria, we anticipate similar findings under the Rome V [23]. This would likely redistribute overlap patients into the PDS subgroup, potentially strengthening postprandial associations while maintaining the link between acid‐suppression and epigastric burning. The higher prevalence of nausea among patients receiving acid‐suppressants is noteworthy, as predominant nausea is considered an exclusion criterion for FD in Rome V, suggesting that these patients might be reclassified.

In conclusion, FD is highly prevalent in an open‐access endoscopy setting, and acid‐suppressive therapy is frequently used in this population. In the present analysis, patients with FD receiving acid‐suppressive medication reported slightly higher frequencies of early satiation, epigastric pain and nausea. This cross‐sectional analysis has its limitations, and the observed associations do not imply causation. Nevertheless, these findings underscore the importance of critically evaluating the ongoing need for PPI therapy in patients with FD. Re‐evaluation after an initial treatment course remains essential.
