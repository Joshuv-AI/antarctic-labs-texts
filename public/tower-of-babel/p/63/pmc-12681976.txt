Development and validation of an individualized online calculator for early mortality risk after hepatectomy among patients with HCC
Authors: Si-Yuan Wang, Yong-Kang Diao, Yu-Ze Yang, Shuo Jin, Yong-Yi Zeng, Wei-Min Gu, Ya-Hao Zhou, Hong Wang, Ting-Hao Chen, Xian-Ming Wang, Ying-Jian Liang, Jie Li, Lan-Qing Yao, Li-Hui Gu, Chao Li, Han Wu, Jia-Hao Xu, Ming-Da Wang, Yu-Chen Li, Fu-Jie Chen, Xue-Dong Wang, Feng Shen, Timothy M Pawlik, Tian Yang, Jia-Hong Dong

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer death globally. Despite improved surgical techniques, early post-hepatectomy mortality remains a critical concern. Current staging systems and liver function classifications fail to estimate early mortality risk to guide surgical decision-making. We aimed to develop and validate an individualized online calculator to predict early post-hepatectomy mortality for HCC.MethodsPatients undergoing curative-intent hepatectomy for HCC from 2011 to 2021 at 11 Chinese centers were included. The training cohort comprised nine centers, while the external validation cohort included two centers. Multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of postoperative 90-day mortality, which were incorporated into an online calculator. Discrimination was assessed using the concordance index (C-index) and calibration by graphical plots.ResultsAmong 4966 patients, 90-day mortality was 4.1%. Predictors of 90-day mortality included patient performance status, prothrombin time, albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade, aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRI), tumor burden score and gross vascular invasion. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination in training and validation (C-index 0.816 and 0.801) cohorts. The proposed model outperformed staging systems (American Joint Committee on Cancer AJCC and Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer BCLC) and liver function classifications (Child-Pugh, APRI, ALBI, and Fibrosis-4 Index FIB4) (p < 0.001). Calibration was accurate in both cohorts. The calculator achieved sensitivity of 79.0% and specificity of 71.8% to identify high-risk patients. Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the model had superior net benefit compared with staging systems and liver function classifications.ConclusionsAn individualized online calculator was developed and validated to predict early post-hepatectomy mortality for HCC. By identifying high-risk patients, this tool may guide surgical decision-making.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Hepatectomy, Survival, Morbidity, Futile surgery

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a rising global incidence caused by the aging population and increasing prevalence of chronic liver disease.1, 2, 3 Hepatectomy remains the mainstay of potentially curative treatment for patients with resectable HCC and preserved liver function.3, 4, 5 However, the decision to proceed with surgery must carefully weigh the potential survival benefit against the risk of postoperative mortality, particularly early deaths within 90 days after surgery that entirely negate any oncological benefit.6, 7, 8, 9

Avoiding “futile” surgeries that do not meaningfully prolong life and may even hasten death is a fundamental tenet of surgical ethics and patient-centered care.10, 11, 12 Current guidelines recommend against hepatectomy for HCC when the risk of postoperative 30-day mortality exceeds 3%.13,14 However, accurately predicting this risk for an individual patient remains challenging. Although traditional HCC staging systems like the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) classification provide valuable prognostic information, they were not designed to predict surgical risk.15, 16, 17 Liver function assessments such as the Child-Pugh score,18 the aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRI),19 the albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade,20 and fibrosis index based on four factors (FIB4) stratify patients on the basis of functional liver reserve, but fail to capture other critical determinants of postoperative outcomes like tumor burden and patient performance status.

Existing surgical risk calculators for HCC have several limitations precluding widespread adoption. Most of these methods were derived from small single-center cohorts, lack external validation, and rely on complex intraoperative factors not known preoperatively when the decision for or against surgery must be made.21, 22, 23 Thus, there is an unmet need for an accurate, validated, and easy-to-use preoperative risk stratification tool to inform surgical decision-making and help avoid futile HCC resections.

To address this need, we aimed to develop and externally validate an individualized online calculator for predicting the likelihood of 90-day mortality after hepatectomy for HCC. By incorporating key preoperative variables spanning liver function, tumor characteristics, and patient performance status, we sought to create a holistic yet simple tool to guide surgical eligibility, support shared decision-making, and ultimately minimize futile surgeries.

Methods

Study population and design

Adults (age ≥18 years) who underwent curative-intent hepatectomy for newly diagnosed HCC from January 2011 to December 2021 at 11 hepatobiliary centers in China were identified. HCC diagnosis was confirmed histopathologically. Patients undergoing emergency hepatectomy for tumor rupture, as well as individuals with recurrent HCC, and those with missing survival data or less than 90 days of follow-up were excluded. The training cohort comprised patients from nine participating hospitals (First Hospital of Jilin University, Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital, the Fourth Hospital of Harbin, Pu'er People's Hospital, Liuyang People's Hospital, Ziyang First People's Hospital, First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University & Shandong Provincial Qianfoshan Hospital, First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, and Fuyang People's Hospital). The validation cohort comprised patients from two hospitals (Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital and Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital of Shanghai). This study was approved by Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No. 23642-0-02), and informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design. The study followed the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis guidelines.24

Candidate predictors and outcomes

Potential predictive variables were selected a priori on the basis of clinical experience and existing literature.18, 19, 20,25,26 Preoperative demographic, clinical, laboratory, and imaging data were extracted from prospectively maintained databases at each center. These variables included age, sex, obesity, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, diabetes mellitus, etiologies of liver disease, presence of cirrhosis, presence of portal hypertension, hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count, prothrombin time (PT), albumin (ALB), bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate transaminase (AST), tumor size, tumor number, and presence of gross vascular invasion by preoperative imaging. Portal hypertension was diagnosed by the presence of either esophageal varices or splenomegaly with platelet count below 100 × 109/L. From these variables, several composite indices were calculated, including the APRI,19 the ALBI grade,20 the FIB4,27 and the tumor burden score (TBS).28 APRI was calculated using the equation: APRI = AST (/ULN) ÷ Platelet (109/L) × 100; ALBI grade was calculated using the equation: ALBI = [log10 bilirubin (μmol/L) × 0.66] + [albumin (g/L) × (−0.085)]; FIB4 was calculated using the equation: FIB4 = [age (years) × AST (U/L)]/[platelet (109) × ALT 1/2(U/L)]; and TBS was defined as the square root of the sum of the squared largest tumor diameter (in cm) and squared number of tumors.

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 90 days after hepatectomy for HCC, ascertained through medical record review and telephone follow-up. Causes of death were categorized as progressive liver function deterioration, sepsis, early fast HCC recurrence/progression, upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage, intra-abdominal hemorrhage, or other etiologies.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using chi-squared or Fisher's exact tests. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or medians (interquartile ranges [IQR]) based on the results of normality testing (Shapiro–Wilk test), and compared using t-test or Mann-Whitney U tests. Missing data were imputed using multiple imputation. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were fitted in the training cohort to identify predictors of 90-day mortality. Backward stepwise selection was performed, retaining variables with p < 0.05. To minimize collinearity, we prioritized composite scores over their individual components and checked variance inflation factors (with values > 5 indicating collinearity). Discrimination was evaluated by calculating concordance index (C-index) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Calibration was visually assessed using calibration plots and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The Youden Index was used to determine the optimal cut-off value with the highest sensitivity and specificity to identify the high risk group as indicated by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.29 To assess the fit of the model, the predictive performance in both the training and validation cohorts was evaluated using ROC curves. Subsequently, the predictive performance between this prediction model and two commonly used staging systems for HCC (the BCLC Staging and the 8th AJCC TNM classification), as well as liver function classifications (the Child-Pugh grade, the APRI, the ALBI grade, and the FIB4), were compared using the ROC curves and decision curve analysis (DCA). A larger area under the ROC curve indicated a greater discriminatory ability and a more accurate performance. Because DCA can display the true- and false-positive fractions as functions of the risk threshold, it compensates for the deficiency of ROC curves.30,31 The predictive performance of the prediction model was illustrated by plotting calibration plots employing the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. The model was internally validated using 1000-iteration bootstrapping to correct for optimism. An Internet browser-based calculator based on this nomogram model was programmed in JavaScript. The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (version 3.5.1. http://www.r-project.org/). A p-value <0.05 was considered to indicate significant difference in a two-tailed test.

Results

Patient characteristics

The final analytic cohort comprised 4966 HCC patients treated with hepatectomy with curative intent across 11 hospitals; 3433 patients were in the nine-center training cohort and 1533 were in the two-center external validation cohort (Fig. S1). Baseline characteristics were balanced between cohorts (Table 1). Overall, the patients’ median age was 54 years (IQR 47–63), 85.4% of patients were male, 89.8% had HBV, 70.1% had cirrhosis, and 7.8% had Child-Pugh B liver function. Median tumor size was 4.6 cm, 19.8% of patients had multiple tumors (tumor number ≥2), 10.2% had gross vascular invasion, 15.3% underwent laparoscopic hepatectomy (no robotic hepatectomy in the whole cohort), and 22.0% underwent major hepatectomy.

Early postoperative mortality

Within 90 days after hepatectomy, 205 (4.1%) patients had died, with similar rates in the training (4.2%) and validation (4.0%) cohorts. Leading causes of postoperative death were liver function progressive deterioration (45.3%), sepsis (19.5%), early fast HCC recurrence/progression (18.0%), upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage (5.9%), intra-abdominal hemorrhage (2.9%), and others (8.3%) (Table S1).

Univariable and multivariable analysis

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses of preoperative variables identified several variables that were associated with early postoperative mortality after hepatectomy of HCC in the training cohort (Table 2). After stepwise multivariable regression, six predictors (incorporating nine distinct variables) remained in the final parsimonious model: ECOG performance status 1–2 (odds ratio [OR] 1.925, 95% CI 1.343–2.759, p < 0.001), PT > 14.0 s (OR 1.178, 95% CI 1.057–1.314, p = 0.003), ALBI grade (OR 2.029 per higher grade, 95% CI 1.328–3.100, p = 0.001), APRI (OR 1.126 per higher score, 95% CI 1.050–1.206, p = 0.001), TBS (OR 1.117 per unit, 95% CI 1.079–1.156, p < 0.001), and gross vascular invasion (OR 3.485, 95% CI 2.353–5.161, p < 0.001).

Model performance

Incorporating the six identified predictors (nine distinct variables), a nomogram was constructed by assigning weighted risk scores related to each variable (Fig. 1). To estimate the probability of early mortality, all of the scores were summed and the total score was located on the total points scale, which, in turn, was associated with the probability of early mortality. The performance of the nomogram model was assessed using the ROC curve in the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 2). The model demonstrated excellent discrimination with a C-index of 0.816 (95% CI 0.783–0.848) in the training cohort and 0.801 (95% CI 0.752–0.849) in the external validation cohort. The bias-corrected C-index derived from bootstrapping was 0.808, confirming minimal overfitting. Calibration plots demonstrated close agreement between predicted and observed early mortality risk across both cohorts (Hosmer–Lemeshow p > 0.05) (Fig. S2).

Using the Youden index, an optimal risk score cutoff of 54 points was identified for clinical decision making. Applying this threshold, the model achieved sensitivity of 79.0% and specificity of 71.8% for predicting early mortality in the training cohort. Similar performance was noted in the validation cohort, with a sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 69% (Table S2).

Comparison with staging systems and liver function classifications

The individualized calculator markedly outperformed both AJCC TNM classification (C-index 0.717, 95% CI 0.680–0.753) and BCLC staging (C-index 0.706, 95% CI 0.671–0.742) in discriminating early postoperative mortality (p < 0.001 for both comparisons; Fig. 3A). Moreover, the calculator demonstrated superior performance compared with liver function classifications, including APRI (C-index 0.642, 95% CI 0.599–0.686), ALBI grade (C-index 0.617, 95% CI 0.577–0.657), FIB4 (C-index 0.587, 95% CI 0.546–0.628), and Child-Pugh grade (C-index 0.543, 95% CI 0.518–0.569) (all p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Decision curve analysis further demonstrated superior net clinical benefit of the calculator across a wide range of risk thresholds compared with that of other staging systems and liver function classifications (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

Significant improvements in surgical technique and perioperative care have reduced in-hospital mortality following hepatectomy for HCC, and multiple large case series from high-volume centers have reported perioperative mortality rates below 5%.32,33 Consistent with these benchmarks, our large multicenter study of 4966 patients demonstrated a 90-day mortality rate of 4.1%. On the basis of these findings, we developed and externally validated a novel risk prediction model for early mortality after HCC resection. The model, which incorporated six identified predictors (nine routinely available preoperative variables, i.e., total bilirubin, albumin, AST, platelet count, largest tumor size, tumor number, ECOG performance status, PT, and gross vascular invasion status), demonstrated excellent discrimination and calibration in both derivation and validation cohorts. The model outperformed traditional HCC staging systems (BCLC, AJCC TNM) and established liver function classifications (Child-Pugh, ALBI, APRI, and FIB4) to predict 90-day postoperative mortality. The model was made available as a user-friendly online calculator to facilitate individualized risk assessment and shared decision-making between clinicians and patients considering HCC resection. By entering these simple preoperative variables, clinicians and patients are able to engage in shared decision-making informed by individualized risk prediction.

The proposed model addresses several key limitations of existing risk assessment tools for HCC surgery. First, by focusing exclusively on preoperative variables, the model enables risk stratification during the preoperative treatment planning phase, when decisions about surgical candidacy are made. Second, by incorporating a comprehensive set of predictors spanning liver function, tumor characteristics, and patient performance status, the model provides a more holistic and nuanced assessment of risk compared with tools that focus on a single domain. Third, by using readily available, objective clinical variables, our model is more practical and generalizable than those requiring specialized testing or subjective assessments. Finally, by providing individualized risk estimates rather than broad risk categories, the model supports patient-centered shared decision-making and personalized treatment planning.

The excellent performance of the proposed model likely derived from several key strengths in its design and execution. First, we assembled a large, geographically diverse cohort of nearly 5000 HCC patients undergoing hepatectomy at 11 high-volume centers across China. This approach provided substantial statistical power and generalizability to our findings. Second, we selected candidate predictor variables on the basis of clinical experience and prior literature, focusing on objective preoperative factors readily available during surgical planning. This approach ensured that the model can be easily applied in real-world practice to guide decision-making. Third, we employed rigorous statistical methods for model building and validation, including bootstrap resampling to correct for optimism and decision curve analysis to assess clinical utility. Finally, we provided the model as a free online calculator for easy use by clinicians and patients worldwide.

The six identified predictors (nine distinct preoperative variables) in the final model reflect the complex interplay of liver dysfunction, tumor burden, and patient performance status that drive early postoperative mortality. Poor performance status, captured by the ECOG scale, likely reflects sarcopenia, malnutrition, and limited physiologic reserve to withstand the stress of surgery.34,35 PT (i.e., international normalized ratio) is a well-established marker of hepatic synthetic function, with prolongation indicating more advanced liver disease.18,36 The ALBI and APRI scores offer more granular assessments of hepatic dysfunction than traditional Child-Pugh classification, incorporating both biochemical parameters (albumin, bilirubin, AST) and hematologic indices (platelet count).19,20 Gross vascular invasion and high TBS are intuitive predictors of early postoperative mortality in that both factors necessitate more extensive liver resection and increase the risk of postoperative liver failure, as well as early fast recurrence.37,38 Notably, TBS provided superior prognostic value compared with tumor size and number alone, underscoring the importance of considering both anatomic and biologic factors in HCC risk assessment.28 Previous studies have demonstrated that TBS outperforms individual tumor characteristics in predicting both short- and long-term outcomes after liver resection for primary liver cancers.39 In a large multicenter study of HCC patients with portal vein tumor thrombus, TBS emerged as a stronger predictor of early recurrence than tumor size or number.40 These findings highlight the utility of integrating multiple dimensions of tumor burden into a composite score for more accurate risk stratification.

Our model has several potential applications in clinical practice. First, it can help identify patients at prohibitively high risk for early postoperative mortality who may be better served by non-surgical therapies like ablation or embolization. Second, even for patients who ultimately proceed with hepatectomy, the model can guide preoperative optimization strategies and inform postoperative monitoring and support. Third, the model can facilitate more informed shared decision-making between surgeons and patients, providing a personalized estimate of risk to anchor discussions about goals of care and treatment preferences. Finally, the model could be used to standardize reporting of postoperative short-term outcomes across centers, enabling fairer comparisons of surgical quality and performance.

Our study involved several limitations. First, although the current cohort was the largest used for an HCC resection risk model to date, additional prospective validation in Western and non-Asian populations would be valuable for confirming the generalizability of our model. Second, the model did not account for some emerging prognostic factors like sarcopenia and systemic inflammatory markers, which may provide incremental predictive value.41, 42, 43 Third, the model predicted all-cause 90-day mortality, which may not perfectly capture deaths directly attributable to surgery as opposed to underlying liver disease or cancer progression. Nonetheless, we believe 90-day mortality is a highly relevant endpoint for surgical decision-making, because deaths within this timeframe are unlikely to be offset by any oncologic benefit.

Conclusions

In the current study, we developed and validated a novel risk prediction model for 90-day mortality after hepatectomy for HCC, incorporating six key preoperative predictors (nine routinely available variables). The model, presented as a user-friendly online calculator, demonstrated excellent discrimination and calibration, outperforming traditional staging systems and liver function classifications. We believe that this tool will help guide patient selection, inform shared decision-making, and ultimately reduce rates of futile surgery for this challenging malignancy. Prospective validation and impact studies are needed to confirm the real-world benefits of this promising new model.
