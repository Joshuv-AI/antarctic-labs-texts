Blockchain-IoT and optioneering driven framework for smart water management in emerging urban areas
Authors: Nibi Kulangara Velayudhan, Dina Zaman, Aryadevi Remanidevi Devidas, Manoj Kumar Tiwari, Johanna Sophie von Lieres, Maneesha Vinodini Ramesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present a blockchain-enabled IoT framework for transforming intermittent water distribution networks (WDNs) into continuous supply in Edakochi, Kerala, India, which faces critical hydraulic and climatic challenges. The framework integrates geospatial modeling, hydraulic retrofitting, and digital governance. An EPANET model, supported by Kerala Water Authority (KWA) data, guided optioneering of pumped and gravity source augmentations. The optimal configuration (O1+O2+O3) ensured full serviceability and raised supply to 218 LPCD. To enable demand-sensitive distribution, an IoT-blockchain network (BCN) supports consumer-level monitoring and smart-contract reward-penalty mechanisms. Two months of consumption from 350 households showed more than 50% exceeded prescribed limits, demonstrating BCN capacity for dynamic governance and conservation incentives. Among four consensus algorithms, Proof of Authority performed best (low mining-time and energy), suiting real-time, resource-constrained deployments. The approach is scalable to similar urban contexts, contingent on expanded real-time sensing, and offers policy-aligned, technologically feasible path to sustainable water management in rapidly urbanizing regions.

Introduction

Nearly 80% of the global population is concentrated in lower and middle-income countries (LMICs), experiencing rapid urbanization and densification coupled with acute climate-related stresses.1 Presently, seven out of ten largest megacities in the world are situated in resource-strained LMICs, with their size and population increasing by the day.2 Although these urban conglomerations promise better economic opportunities and livelihood, the rapid rural-to-urban exodus has resulted in disparate liveable conditions in most cities of the developing world. Rapid rural-to-urban migration in emerging cities drives sharp increases in population density, placing disproportionate stress on existing water and sanitation infrastructure.3,4 This often results in reduced per capita availability, intermittent supply, and overloading of sewerage systems, particularly in low-income neighborhoods with limited service coverage. Furthermore, the overlaying impact of climate change has further aggravated the challenges of these urban populations’ access to basic amenities of water and sanitation.5 To acknowledge these global challenges, the United Nations has framed “clean water and sanitation for all communities by 2030” as one of the 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 6), and access to affordable and acceptable water is directly or indirectly associated with achieving the core objectives of 12 out of the 17 SDGs.6

Intermittent water supply (IWS), characterized by unpredictable and limited service periods remains prevalent in LMIC urban areas impacting approximately 300 million people worldwide.7,8 India epitomizes these challenges with over 34% of its population concentrated in urban areas covering only 3% of its land yet contributing more than 60% of national GDP.9,10 Despite national initiatives such as the Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission (JNNURM) and the Smart Cities Mission significant progress remains hampered by techno-economic, financial, and political barriers.11,12,13

Urban water distribution networks (WDNs) in these regions are often outdated, degraded, and provide limited service coverage.14 Intermittency results in unreliable and inequitable distribution, elevated contamination risks due to pipeline depressurization events, and substantial water losses.15 Water utilities frequently encounter absolute scarcity from seasonal droughts, economic scarcity from power outages, structural inadequacies, and technical scarcity from operational mismanagement. Consequently, even networks originally designed for continuous supply are compelled to operate intermittently16,17 making IWS unsustainable for achieving long-term water security and climate resilience.8,18

Traditional hydraulic retrofitting methods, such as pipeline replacements, pump optimization, capacity augmentation, or localized storage and reservoir expansions, have consistently proven insufficient in fully addressing these complex challenges to create adaptive and resilient systems.19 However, these interventions are typically static, designed for historical demand patterns, and implemented without integrating real-time monitoring, demand forecasting, or adaptive governance.20,21 As a result, such upgrades cannot effectively respond to fluctuating demand, climate variability, or governance challenges, leading to recurring supply inequities and system inefficiencies despite significant capital investments.22,23 Moreover, conventional hydraulic approaches typically neglect operational inefficiencies, real-time data integration, and robust consumer accountability mechanisms. Additionally, incomplete or inconsistent database updates further complicate effective hydraulic analysis and network optimization.24 Thus, infrastructure upgrades often narrowly focus on immediate demands without effectively transforming the networks into adaptive and resilient systems.

Recent technological advancements highlight the potential of digital integration and Information and Communications Technology (ICT) to overcome these limitations. The Internet of Things (IoT) facilitates real-time monitoring of essential network parameters (e.g., flow, pressure, quality) enhancing operational responsiveness and predictive maintenance capabilities.25,26 Blockchain technology addresses these concerns by providing a secure, decentralized, and transparent platform for recording water usage data and automating governance actions via smart contracts. Even, its practical application within urban water management remains limited revealing a notable gap between theoretical potential and validated field implementations.27

To address these gaps, this study proposes a comprehensive blockchain-enabled IoT framework that integrates hydraulic retrofitting, and blockchain-driven management to transform intermittently supplied WDNs into reliable and equitable water delivery systems. The study specifically addresses the practical challenges associated with the hydraulically deficient WDN serving the Edakochi region of Kochi City, Kerala, India, which is managed by the Kerala Water Authority but it lacks the detailed hydraulic modeling and systematic upgrades.

The motivation behind this work is 2-fold: (1) enhancing urban water supply reliability, and equity, through advanced hydraulic retrofitting, and (2) ensuring data integrity, transparency, and consumer accountability using secured technological advancements. When compared with the existing fragmented approaches, the proposed integrated framework combines hydraulics with ICT.

This holistic solution not only restores continuous, pressurized water delivery but also addresses the socio-technical dimensions critical for sustainability. The proposed framework demonstrates scalability, operational efficiency, and superior performance compared to conventional solutions when validated by real-word data.

Related work

Water distribution networks in many developing and semi-urban regions continue to operate under IWS conditions, resulting in inefficiencies such as pressure deficits, leakages, and inequitable access. Several studies have examined retrofitting to address these issues.

The study in Nagpur, India, evaluated pipe resizing and pump capacity upgrades using EPANET simulation to transition toward 24/7 supply. Moreover, it highlighted improvements in pressure and flow however,it lacks the integration with real-time monitoring.28 A combined approach of GIS with EPANET to identify low-pressure zones and proposed zoning with pressure-reducing valves in one of the old cities’ networks in Aurangabad is presented in.29 This study emphasized pressure and lacks in addressing consumption behavior. A hydraulic model is proposed for sector-wise water redistribution to combat inequitable supply in Udaipur WDN. This approach is assumed to be in static demand conditions for the simulations.30 A sectoral supply management strategy using stored tank analysis and supply scheduling is presented in,31 however, this method lacking real-time operational control.

As part of the ICT-enabled upgradation of the WDN, a smart water grid in Madeira Island is developed by integrating IoT with SCADA, and the proposed method achieves over 80% leakage reduction. However, it lacked consumer engagement and decentralized control.31 HydroIQ in Nairobi retrofitted IoT-based smart meters with blockchain-backed billing in an IWS setting. This improves billing transparency and user trust, however, it did not alter hydraulic configurations or ensure equitable pressure delivery.32 In rural Spain, a digital twin of a gravity-fed network is proposed, integrating AI anomaly detection and a private Ethereum blockchain for secure and real-time decision-making. Though effective in low-density networks, its scalability and integration with optioneering were limited.33 A blockchain-IoT framework for a desalinated water network, enabling real-time billing and leak detection is proposed for Saudi Arabia, however, the validation in pressure-variable networks are performed.34 A household-level smart contract framework for water usage prediction and behavior-based incentivization using machine learning and blockchain is developed in.35 While it is a promising solution, it operated on simulated datasets with no hydraulic validation. Despite the success of these isolated approaches, significant gaps remain in the literature. Hydraulic retrofitting studies rarely account for dynamic demand behavior or governance automation. Similarly, ICT deployments often lack secure and trustworthy data layers while overlooking physical constraints. Moreover, blockchain systems remain disconnected from core hydraulic dynamics. As a result, there is a lack of comprehensive solutions that integrate physical infrastructure upgrades with real-time monitoring and decentralized, trust-based management. Existing studies typically focus either on supply-side improvements or on consumer-side accountability, but seldom address both simultaneously.

Consensus algorithm evaluation and recommendation

To evaluate consensus algorithms for a blockchain-enabled IoT WDN, we processed 2,500 real-time transactions from approximately 400 households over a two month period. The mining time of each transaction was recorded under four consensus protocols PoW, PoA, PoT, and PoV and analyzed for computational efficiency, latency, scalability, and implementation suitability.

Proof of work

Demonstrated high variance in mining times, with several peaks exceeding 0.2 s as depicted in Figure 3. This is a direct result of the dependency of the algorithm on solving complex cryptographic puzzles to validate blocks, while ensuring network integrity and strong security, which demands significant energy and computing resources. For IoT-enabled WDNs, especially in resource-constrained urban settings, this results in excessive latency, making PoW unsuitable for real-time operational needs.Figure 3Consensus Algorithm—PoW

Consensus Algorithm—PoW

Proof of authority

Outperformed all other algorithms with mining times consistently between 0.001 and 0.008 s per transaction, as depicted in Figure 4. This stability and responsiveness stem from PoA’s structure, which entrusts block validation to a predefined set of authority nodes. Its deterministic nature, reduced computational overhead, and faster block finality make it ideal for water utilities and municipal environments, where trusted institutions are already exist. Furthermore, the low variance supports applications like automated billing, leakage detection, and equitable valve actuation rely on low-latency data processing.Figure 4Consensus Algorithm—PoA

Consensus Algorithm—PoA

Proof of time

As shown in Figure 5, PoT resulted in relatively stable and prolonged mining times, generally ranging from 0.25 to 0.45 s. Although it consumes less energy than PoW, its reliance on randomized wait times undermines its suitability for WDN scenarios requiring rapid data response and precision in supply control.Figure 5Consensus Algorithm—PoT

Figure 6 illustrates a promising latency profile of PoV, with mining times often below 0.005 s. However, PoV necessitates frequent synchronization among dynamically selected validators, introducing coordination complexity and trust dependencies. Recommendation

From a systems engineering and utility governance standpoint, PoA emerges as the most pragmatic choice. Its low-latency, energy efficiency, and operational simplicity offer an optimal balance for blockchain-integrated water management. These characteristics support seamless integration with real-time sensor networks, enable efficient data validation, and ensure reliable service for diverse stakeholders. Although PoV is technically promising, it requires maturity in consensus stability and validator governance before it can be widely adopted. PoW and PoT remain less viable for responsive, sustainable smart water infrastructure.

Overall recommendations

Based on this study, PoA should be considered the most effective consensus algorithm for real-time blockchain-integrated water management systems. Its minimal latency, high throughput, and low energy consumption make it ideal for smart city infrastructure where responsiveness and scalability are crucial. Integrating blockchain with IoT-based WDNs enhances transparency in metering, consumer behavior analysis, and dynamic policy enforcement. By coupling hydraulic retrofitting strategies with BCN-enabled control and data logging, utilities can bridge the gap between infrastructure enhancement and governance reform. The real-time visibility and accountability offered by PoA-based blockchain systems can further be extended to support future regulatory innovations and community-based management models.

This work presents a blockchain-enabled IoT framework for smart water management by transitioning legacy intermittent WDNs into continuous supply systems with enhanced serviceability. The case study conducted in Edakochi, Kerala, India, an area facing severe hydraulic inefficiencies exacerbated by climate-induced stresses, provided a realistic backdrop for model development and validation. A calibrated EPANET-based hydraulic model was developed using secondary data from the KWA. Simulation results underscored the need to augment both water sources and energy systems to achieve consistent residual pressures, leakage reduction, and energy-efficient continuous supply. In collaboration with KWA, feasible augmentation strategies were identified and evaluated using optioneering approaches, which tested the impact of additional pumped and gravity-fed sources. Among these, the combined option (O1+O2+O3), integrating a dual-pumped source and elevated storage reservoir, delivered the highest serviceability, ensuring 100% node coverage with at least satisfactory pressure, and significantly increasing per capita daily water availability to 218 LPCD.

To transition from equitable to demand-sensitive distribution, a blockchain network (BCN) was integrated with the hydraulic solution. The BCN recorded consumer-level demand and implemented a reward-penalty model via smart contracts, supporting dynamic water trading and transparent usage enforcement. Two months of consumption data from 350 consumers revealed that over 50% exceeded prescribed usage limits, thereby falling into the penalty zone. This insight facilitates data-driven policymaking-offering pathways to evolve from fixed quotas to tiered pricing models, incentivized conservation programs, and targeted demand regulation. To ensure the blockchain layer performance in a resource-constrained IoT environment four consensus algorithms PoW, PoA, PoT, and PoV were benchmarked. PoA was found to be the most efficient, with mining times between 0.001 and 0.008 s and minimal energy overhead. Unlike PoW, which is computationally expensive and slower, PoA aligns with institutional trust structures in utilities and supports real-time data processing essential for smart WDN operations.

While the framework is generalizable, its success depends heavily on the availability of real-time IoT sensor data and accurate localized WDN mapping. A key limitation is the absence of field-deployed sensor infrastructure which constrained the extent of model calibration and real-time validation. Nonetheless, the study establishes a replicable and scalable blueprint for smart water management systems, particularly suited to emerging urban areas aiming to overcome infrastructural inertia with technological and policy innovation.

Suggestions for future research

Further research focuses on deploying the proposed BCN framework in real-world water utility operations to measure practical effectiveness, user acceptance, and behavioral impact. Future work can also explore hybrid consensus approaches that combine the strengths of PoA and PoV, optimizing for both governance flexibility and performance. Incorporating federated edge computing could help in overcoming the connectivity and processing constraints in under-resourced areas. In-depth security audits, including resilience to cyberattacks, node collusion, or trust breaches, are essential for future-ready deployment. Additionally, a life cycle assessment evaluating the energy, emissions, and sustainability implications of consensus algorithms should be carried out to inform green digital infrastructure development.

Furthermore, the framework will be extended to a hybrid design, integrating rainwater harvesting, watershed protection, managed aquifer recharge, and participatory water budgeting into the BCN-IoT layer with smart contract rules for lifeline volumes and verified recharge credits. Comparison of digital-only, nature based, and hybrid pathways will be performed via total cost, scalability, equity, and environmental co-benefits for both community-managed systems and existing centralized systems. Moreover, explore the integration of nature-based solutions within a digital-hydraulic framework of the Edakochi to co-optimize hydraulic performance and ecological resilience.

Limitations of the study

This study used a simulated blockchain testbed rather than a full-scale field implementation; consequently, practical issues, such as intermittent connectivity, hardware failures, and edge-to-cloud latency in remote areas were not evaluated. This study lacks full-scale field validation and does not explicitly model user-centric heterogeneity, behavioral variability, digital literacy, and socio-economic diversity, so external validity and equitable adoption remains insufficiently tested. The consumer behavior assumptions underlying the reward-penalty mechanism were derived solely from quantitative usage data, without incorporating behavioral psychology or socio-economic context. Importantly, the current reward-penalty model does not parameterize allowances by household size, effective occupancy, or essential/lifeline needs, reliance on an average LPCD threshold may therefore misclassify legitimate demand. The blockchain evaluation assumed honest validator participation under PoA and did not model Byzantine or adversarial behavior. Finally, scalability tests were limited to around 400 household nodes, leaving open questions about stability at larger urban scales or in areas with constrained digital infrastructure.

Method details

Study area

Kochi is the third largest and second most populous cities in Kerala covering 95.88 sq. km with an estimated population of 625,345 as per the estimation on 2023.38The city experiences moderate drought during dry summer spells and is among the 100 cities chosen for the Smart Cities Initiative to upgrade civic infrastructure and enhance liveability.39 Water supply system in Kochi was first established in 1903 under the King of Cochin and later managed by the Cochin Municipal Council. Over the century, water services have expanded and are now managed by the Kerala Water Authority (KWA). Raw water is drawn from the Periyar and Muvattupuzha rivers and treated in two plants with a combined capacity of 325 million liter per day (MLD). Although most households rely on piped water, many residents supplement the their supply with alternative sources during the summer.

This study examines the Edakochi region, which covers municipal divisions, wards 15 and 16 (see below figure), as a representative case of WDN of Kochi. The area, containing over 4,000 households per Census of India (2011), comprises about 3% of the population of the city and faces multiple geographical challenges along with climate-induced stresses that threaten drinking water security and impact WDN performance.38 Being a low-lying seafront region, its groundwater is highly saline, forcing residents to depend on inadequate piped water. The framework illustrated in below figure addresses critical challenges in WDN such as resource scarcity, intermittent supply, inefficient infrastructure, and the lack of transparency and accountability in water management, especially in urban and semi-urban areas. This framework provides a holistic approach for smart water management that considers perspectives of the community on WDN, existing WDN attributes, planned enhancements on WDN by utility, real-time monitoring of WDN attributes to capture their dynamics, and finally, a blockchain-based water trading. The framework has three major modules such as hydraulic up-gradation module, the IoT module, and the BCN module. Hydraullic upgradation for the WDN module focused on mitigating intermittent supply through retrofitting strategies such as source augmentation, energy-efficient pumping, and gravity-based distribution enhancements. IoT-enabled WDN facilitates in real-time data acquisition and feedback control by deploying sensors to monitor flow, pressure, and consumption across the WDN, supporting timely operational decisions. Data sourcing

This study conducted a comprehensive assessment of the water supply system in the study area by integrating both primary and secondary data sources. Primary information was collected through a field survey using participatory rural appraisal techniques to actively engage the local community and evaluate challenges related to drinking water access and piped water supply. Qualitative data were triangulated using resource mapping, transect walks, and priority ranking methods.41,42,43 Four focus group discussions involving 29 female participants from microfinance self-help groups and two semi-structured interviews with Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) provided additional insights into community experiences and perceptions regarding water availability and quality.

The transect walk identified key drinking water sources and low-pressure zones. Many private borewells were found to be ineffective due to high groundwater salinity, while public taps often non-functional served around ten households each. Interviewees highlighted poor operation and maintenance of the piped water network, which led to inconsistent supply and increased reliance on tanker lorries and alternative sources, especially during the monsoon season. Water quality concerns included salinity, discoloration, and excessive chlorine levels in some areas. Participants consistently prioritized the need for addressing salinity and ensuring a reliable and sufficient drinking water supply.

In parallel, secondary information is obtained from the Kerala Water Authority (KWA) to supplement the field data. The WDN in the area constructed approximately 30 years ago and subject to periodic retrofitting and expansion, supplies treated water from the Periyar River through the Aluva treatment plant. The treated water is stored at Palluruthy and pumped over a distance of 3 km to household storage tanks. However, inadequate pressure frequently hinders direct transfer to overhead tanks. Despite the presence of water meters at all domestic connections, nearly 40% are nonfunctional, contributing to significant revenue losses for KWA. Water consumption is recorded bi-monthly and the pricing follows an increasing block tariff structure.44

The integrated assessment approach, combining community engagement with institutional data, provides a robust and contextually grounded understanding of the operational, infrastructural, and socio-environmental dimensions of the water supply system.

WDN model construction

In the absence of a digitized map, a hydraulic model for the WDN built from scratch using EPANET 2.2 integrated with QGIS via the QGISRed plugin.45 The study area boundary extracted from Google Earth Pro as KML files, converted to raster, and used to plot contours in GIS. Elevation estimated using the Natural Neighboring Points algorithm of SAGA to finalize the DEM.46 Structural details, including reservoir, pump, and junction positions, along with pipe length, diameter, and material, are provided in PDF format by service providers, while nodal elevations are estimated using QGIS. Pipe roughness coefficients determined from material and network age.47 The Palluruthy pumphouse, located 2.8 km from the supply area, operates two 75 HP pumps and one 90 HP pump on alternate days.

Transitioning the WDN to deliver optimal hydraulic performance required model verification and stage-wise optioneering exercises. The base model (BM) was qualitatively verified using field survey data for low and high pressure zones. In India, optimal hydraulic outputs for urban WDNs follow Central Public Health and Environmental Engineering Organisation (CPHEEO) guidelines, which account for city type, sewerage provisions, and average residential building heights.47,48,49 Minimum residual pressure (P) is specified between 7 m and 17 m, depending on building storeys, while the average supply volume (Q) ranges from 70 to 150 Liters Per Capita Per Day (LPCD) based on population density and sewage systems. CPHEEO and Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) recommend that residual pressure should not exceed 21 m and supply volume remain under 280 LPCD (IS:1172–1993) to prevent structural failure. Although CPHEEO does not regulate supply duration (T), continuous supply (T = 24 h) remains the universal benchmark.18

Based on the comprehensive field survey conducted in the Edakochi region, the major water-related issue concerning the residents was reported as the lack of a reliable perennial drinking water supply, which highlighted the low serviceability of the public water services in this region. The study area comprised two municipal divisions (Wards 15 and 16), although there is no hydraulic demarcation in the WDN serving the region. A heterogeneous level of serviceability and supply modes was observed at the consumer level (house connections) in the region. A significant number of households are deprived of optimum drinking water provision on a daily basis. None of the households are provided with ample water quantity at adequate residual pressure such that it directly reaches the overhead tanks for 24 h a day, to our best knowledge. Some households have a ‘sump-pump-overhead tank’ provision to circumvent intermittency and low-pressure issues, while others manage with ground-level tanks and containers to store water. The localities with extremely low residual pressures within the study area were identified as Kannagatt and Palamattam, which majorly depended on public taps and tanker lorries to fulfill their daily water requirement. Consumer nodes of the WDN lying in these localities were used to qualitatively calibrate the hydraulic base model and verify the simulated pressure trends with that encountered in the real-field. Although the operators at Palluruthy pump station claimed to operate it for 14 to 16 h a day, an average household in the Edakochi region received water at low pressure only for 3 h in the mornings (5 a.m.–8 a.m.) and evenings (3 p.m.–6 p.m.) daily. Through verbal communication with the KWA personnel, it was informed that an approximate volume of 5 MLD of water was delivered to the study area, which indicated a water loss of 30%. Thus, triangulating the qualitative information from the field survey facilitated the development of a fairly credible hydraulic model of the WDN through qualitative verification and used for simulation-based upgrading.

A thorough knowledge of the existing hydraulic performance of the WDN is a prerequisite to simulating any upgrading or retrofitting scenarios with its model. In a standard hydraulic modeling and analysis procedure, the WDN model is subjected to a calibration and validation exercise prior to scenario simulation. A true calibration process entails comparing the simulated model parameters to the corresponding hydraulic parameters obtained through real-field observations. However, a similar exercise is nearly improbable in most Indian WDNs not equipped with any hydraulic sensors for data accumulation.50,51 As such, a qualitative model verification process was undertaken wherein zones with very low serviceability (i.e., low-pressure and low-flow regimes) were identified through thematic analysis of survey information and analyzed with respect to the pressure profiles of the modeled consumer nodes falling within these zones.

In this study, the WDN model was constructed in GIS and EPANET 2.2 using primary and secondary information. A demand-driven approach (DDA) was considered to simulate the model following a constant demand pattern for the supply duration (T) of 6 h. The model was subjected to a sequential qualitative verification process based on engineering judgment. The Hazen C values (roughness coefficients) of the network pipes were decreased according to their age and pipe material.48,49 The pump efficiency was also reduced by 40% to account for its age. An estimated 30% water loss was introduced as background leakage by adjusting the emitter coefficients and emitter exponents of the model. The verified WDN model so obtained and conforming to the real-field operational trends is termed the Base Model (BM) and utilized for simulating WDN upgrading strategies. WDN optioneering and performance upgrading

To upgrade the network to optimal serviceability, both water and energy resources needed enhancement to address deficiencies in supply volume, residual pressure, and duration. A stagewise simulation-based optioneering exercise conducted using the BM to eliminate all pressure-deficient nodes while ensuring none of the node exceeded the 21 m upper pressure limit.

IoT system for WDN supply management

IoT-based solutions enable the systematic collection of data from critical components of water distribution networks, including pipes, pumps, and storage systems. Geographic information systems (GIS) are often used to manage and visualize the spatial aspects of this infrastructure. Sensors deployed across the network measure key parameters such as flow, pressure, and water level, transmitting these data through communication technologies like Wi-Fi, 5G, or LoRa to centralized or cloud-based platforms. These platforms facilitate real-time monitoring, analysis, and decision-making to optimize water distribution, manage demand, detect leaks, and respond swiftly to abnormalities. Closed-loop control strategies leverage sensor feedback to automate operations, thereby enhancing overall system efficiency. Furthermore, the integration of IoT with AI-driven techniques enables predictive maintenance, demand forecasting, and intelligent resource allocation. These capabilities significantly improve both cost-effectiveness and operational reliability. With ongoing advancements, IoT holds substantial potential to transform traditional water distribution systems into smarter, and more resilient infrastructures. The IoT system monitors the pressure, flow, water level of the reservoirs and get the demand data to the BCN for further processing.52

Blockchain for WDN management

A blockchain architecture for WDN supply management supports blockchain intelligence including machine learning (ML) for anomaly detection, Trusted Authority (TA) nodes for verification, smart contracts for automating responses (e.g., leak alerts or valve control), and a distributed ledger for maintaining immutable records of events and transactions.

To ensure data integrity and consensus, the system incorporates both Proof-of-Work (PoW) and Proof-of-Action (PoA) mechanisms. PoW can be applied where decentralized edge nodes validate critical events such as leaks or abnormal pressure by solving computational challenges, thus preventing data tampering in trustless environments. In contrast, PoA is employed in permissioned networks, where predesignated authority nodes (e.g., municipal water boards or utility providers) validate transactions and manage the ledger, ensuring high efficiency, low energy consumption, and fast decision-making suitable for real-time WDN control.36

The role of each entity in the WDN sharing scenario is as follows: when a household or consumer receives data on current and predicted usage for the next n days, they may recognize an excess supply and choose to trade this with the utility. Such trading can occur at two levels: inter-community (within the broader network) and intra-community. The edge analytics unit collects real-time WDN data and forwards it to the cloud server, where machine learning models predict consumption and supply. These predictions are integrated with the blockchain system, which employs a trusted authority module to generate hash-key pairs that enable secure information exchange between consumers, the utility, and inter-community participants via the cloud servers. Water trading details and transaction records are stored on the blockchain. Selected cloud servers and edge devices, chosen by ML algorithms, act as miners to perform blockchain mining tasks and generate new blocks. The cloud server also maintains trading information from the edge units, enforces smart contracts, and executes computation-intensive tasks offloaded by consumers. In addition, smart contracts provide off-chain storage, alleviating pressure on the blockchain. The primary objective of the regulatory body is to maximize social welfare by supervising and managing water-saving, trading behaviors and incentivizing consumers via the decentralized blockchain network with various utilities. The regulatory bodies want to provide benefits such as subsidies, for those consumers are all within limits, and want to restrict overusage and encourage their responsible development. However, the utilities want to maximize their profit and serviceability; hence, introducing the optioneering schemes and IoT monitoring systems for WDN can helps in achieve both. Utilities analyze consumer supply and consumption through the IoT-enabled WDN network, and regulatory bodies provide updates and reports on WDN through the blockchain-enabled IoT network. This will lead to the supervision and development of governance, policies, and regulations, such as incentives.54,55

Conversely, consumers possess the autonomy to decide whether to engage in water-saving practices to minimize their own water bills. However, when the consumers get a supply of more than the predefined limits, they have to participate in the scheme or fall into a restrictive category (which may increase the tariff for them). In the WDN water-saving scheme, the regulatory bodies and utilities aim to achieve an equitable supply which mainly relies on the efforts of water utilities and consumers in water-saving practices. Utilities and consumers strive to maximize their profits. Precisely, the Utilities’ net profits are calculated as the difference between their total profits and cost where the total profits include the restrictions and subsidies from the regulatory bodies according to their water-saving behaviors and the costs are their efforts including the introduction of water-saving technology and so on. Similarly, the total profits of the consumers include the incentive profits from the utilities the costs of the punishment by the utilities, and the efforts due to implementing water-saving.55
