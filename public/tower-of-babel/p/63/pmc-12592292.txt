Association of sarcopenic obesity with cognitive dysfunction and dementia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chunbian Tang, Ying Song, Fengran Tao, Jiayi Hao, Li Yan, Xiuxia Shi, Runnan Pei, Baoqi Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCurrently, the association of sarcopenic obesity (SO) with cognitive dysfunction and dementia remains unclear. This study aims to investigate their potential association.Materials and methodsPubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov were systematically searched until 30 December 2024. Observational studies investigating the association of SO with cognitive dysfunction and dementia were included. A random-effects model was applied for meta-analysis.ResultsSeventeen studies with 274,996 participants were included, of which 11 were included in quantitative analyses. The pooled odds ratio for cognitive dysfunction in patients with SO versus non-SO was 1.71 (95% CI: 1.39–2.11, p < 0.001, I2 = 90.7%). Subgroup analyses showed consistent associations across study design, geographic region, cognitive dysfunction subtypes, and obesity criteria. Significant results were also observed in males (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.07–3.03, p = 0.028), general populations (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.50–2.54, p < 0.001), sarcopenia defined by muscle function and/or mass (OR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.35–2.07, p < 0.001) or muscle function alone (OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.14–4.69, p = 0.020), and cognitive assessment via clinical records (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.21–2.33, p = 0.002) or scales (OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.37–2.21, p < 0.001).ConclusionsSO appears associated with cognitive dysfunction. However, due to high heterogeneity, very low certainty of evidence, and potential biases, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Future high-quality prospective studies are needed to validate this conclusion.PROSPERO registration number: CRD420251011745.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03222-1.Keywords: Sarcopenic obesity, Cognitive dysfunction, Dementia, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Cognitive dysfunction refers to a decline in cognitive abilities, including memory, attention, and language skills [1]. When severe enough to interfere with daily activities, it may be classified as dementia [2]. Globally, approximately 50 million people live with dementia, a number projected to rise to 152 million by 2050 [3]. Dementia impacts individuals, families, and economies, with an estimated annual global cost of US$1 trillion [3]. Despite this high disease burden, no definitive cure currently exists for dementia [4]. Therefore, research identifying risk factors associated with dementia and cognitive impairment is critical for early detection and intervention.

Sarcopenia is defined as a progressive and generalized loss of muscle mass and strength, which is associated with adverse outcomes such as falls, fractures, frailty, mortality, and cognitive impairment [5]. Another significant age-related change in body composition is the accumulation of adipose tissue, leading to an increased body fat percentage and obesity [6]. However, the relationship between obesity and dementia remains controversial. While some studies suggest that late-life obesity may be an independent risk factor for dementia, others report a reduced risk in obese individuals—a phenomenon known as the “obesity paradox” [7].

Sarcopenic obesity (SO) is defined as the co-occurrence of sarcopenia and obesity in the same individual [8]. The global prevalence of SO is approximately 11%, though this varies by region and population [9]. The incidence of SO is rising rapidly, primarily due to global population aging and the ongoing obesity epidemic [8]. Although sarcopenia and obesity are distinct conditions, they share common pathophysiological mechanisms, including lifestyle factors, aging, increased production of inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species, and endocrine dysfunction [8]. Their combination may have a synergistic effect, exacerbating the risk of cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and impaired physical function [6]. Given the potential interaction between obesity and sarcopenia and the debated “obesity paradox”, the relationship between SO and cognitive dysfunction requires further investigation. Currently, evidence linking SO to cognitive dysfunction remains controversial, possibly due to variations in diagnostic criteria, study designs, and population characteristics. To date, no systematic review or meta-analysis has comprehensively synthesized the available evidence on SO in relation to cognitive dysfunction and dementia. Therefore, this study aims to investigate their association.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) statement [10] and was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the protocol number CRD420251011745. We searched for literature published in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and gray literature from ClinicalTrials.gov until 30 December 2024. Keywords included “sarcopenic obesity” “cognitive dysfunction” “dementia” “cognitive impairment”. The complete search strategy is available in the Supplementary material. Our search was restricted to articles published in English. Additionally, we identified references by searching the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews.

The eligibility criteria for this meta-analysis were established following the PECOS (Patient/Population; Exposure; Comparison; Outcome; Study design) framework [11]. We excluded individuals with life-threatening cancers, major psychiatric disorders such as major depressive disorder, neurological disorders such as stroke and Parkinson’s disease, or medical disorders that might affect neuropsychological function. The exposure was SO, defined according to guidelines or consensus, with non-SO individuals as comparators. Outcomes included cognitive impairment manifestations like mild cognitive impairment (MCI), dementia, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and cognitive decline. Eligible study designs comprised cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional studies.

Data extraction

Two investigators independently extracted data using standardized, pre-designed extraction forms. Any discrepancies were discussed between the researchers until a consensus was reached. The following data were extracted: author, publication period/year, study design, data source, country, exposure, comparator, sample size/events, evaluation criteria for SO and cognitive function, follow-up period, type of cognitive outcome, statistical methods and adjusted covariates.

Quality assessment

The quality of the cohort studies and cross-sectional studies was assessed by two authors using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) [12] and Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) [13] tool, respectively. The NOS criteria include the representativeness and selection of participants, detection of exposure, assessment of outcomes, and evaluation of follow-up. Studies with an NOS score >7 were deemed high-quality. The AHRQ tool includes 11 items, each rated as “yes”, “no” or “not reported”. A “yes” answer was scored as 1, while “no” or “not reported” was scored as 0. Scores of 4–6 were rated as medium quality and 8–11 points as high quality. Any disagreements were resolved through joint re-evaluation until a consensus was reached.

Grading the evidence

We assessed the quality of evidence for the outcomes using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system. This evaluation considered five factors: Risk of Bias, Inconsistency, Indirectness, Imprecision, and Publication Bias. Based on these criteria, the evidence was classified into one of four levels: high, moderate, low, or very low. Evidence from observational studies was initially considered low-quality.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using STATA (version 17.0; Stata Corp, College Station, TX). The primary outcome was the pooled odds ratio (OR) of any type of cognitive dysfunction for SO participants versus non-SO participants. Anticipating between-study heterogeneity, the DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model was used to calculate ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [14]. In studies that did not report the OR for cognitive dysfunction, other risk measurements (relative risk [RR] or hazard ratio [HR]) were used as approximations for OR to analyze the association between cognitive dysfunction and SO.

To account for confounding variables, the adjusted OR was used for the analysis. Statistical heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using the χ² test and I² statistic. I² values of 25%, 50%, and 75% are considered indicators of low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [15]. Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot for the primary outcome and Egger’s test, with P < 0.05 indicating the presence of publication bias [16]. Sensitivity analyses were performed by removing each study individually, conducting meta-analyses using crude results, and excluding studies involving populations with specific diseases.

To test the potential sources of heterogeneity, we performed several subgroup analyses based on study design (prospective cohort or cross-sectional study), geographic regions (Asia, North America, Europe or South America), patient sex (female or male), cognitive dysfunction subtypes (dementia, MCI, or cognitive impairment), population characteristics (disease-specific populations or general populations), diagnostic criteria of sarcopenia (muscle function alone, muscle mass alone or muscle function and/or mass), diagnostic criteria of obesity (body mass index [BMI], waist circumference [WC], or body composition) and measurement of cognitive function (clinical records, scale tests or self-reported and/or scale tests). Subgroup differences were significant if P interaction < 0.05.

If a study provides multiple results using different SO diagnostic criteria or cognitive function assessments, only the most common was included. Among studies investigating the association between SO and cognitive dysfunction in the same cohort, the one with the largest sample size was selected.

Results

Search results and study characteristics

The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. Our systematic search yielded 949 records after duplicate removal, of which 56 underwent full-text screening for eligibility. Ultimately, 17 studies involving 274,996 participants were included in the final review, with 11 studies eligible for quantitative analyses.

Among the 17 included studies, 4 were prospective cohorts [7, 17–19] and 13 were cross-sectional studies [20–35]. All participants were aged ≥ 21 years, with female representation ranging from 41.6% to 89.9%. These studies were conducted in Asia (n = 11), Europe (n = 1), North America (n = 4), and South America (n = 1), with publication dates ranging from 2018 to 2024. The meta-analysis incorporated 253,506 participants with sample sizes ranging from 120 to 208,867 (further characteristics are summarized in Table 1). Description of the diagnostic criteria for SO and the measurement of cognitive function

In our analysis, the diagnostic criteria for SO required the coexistence of sarcopenia and obesity. For sarcopenia diagnosis, three main criteria were used: muscle function only (5 studies), muscle mass only (7 studies), or muscle function and/or mass (6 studies). Obesity was assessed using various criteria across studies, with BMI being the most common, followed by WC. Of the 17 studies, Fu et al. [24] and Booranasuksakul et al. (2023) [35] employed four different diagnostic criteria for SO assessment, and Batsis et al. [17] utilized three criteria. The assessment of obesity in SO typically incorporated BMI, WC, and fat mass percentage (FM%) [26]. Obesity was assessed using various criteria across studies, with BMI being the most common, our meta-analysis incorporated Definition 1 results from Fu et al., Batsis et al., and Booranasuksakul et al. (2023) [36, 37].

Cognitive function was assessed using diverse methods across the included studies, including self-reported measures, standardized cognitive tests, and clinical records. Among the standardized tests, the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) were most frequently employed, followed by the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) and Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS). Booranasuksakul et al. (2023) [35] employed four distinct cognitive assessments (Animal Fluency [AF], DSST, Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease Word Learning [CERAD-WL], CERAD Delayed Recall [CERAD-DR]). In the meta-analysis, we included the DSST results as it was commonly used in the included studies, helping to reduce potential heterogeneity.

Risk of bias

Risk of bias assessment revealed that all studies to be rated as having a low risk of bias. Details are presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Main analysis

Fourteen comparisons from 11 studies were pooled for the primary outcome. The combined results demonstrated a significant association between SO and higher odds of any type of cognitive dysfunction (OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.39–2.11, p < 0.001), with substantial heterogeneity observed (I2 = 90.7%) (see Fig. 2). Asymmetry in funnel plots (see Supplementary Fig. 1) and Egger’s test (p = 0.01) indicated evidence of publication bias. Sensitivity analyses via leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that no single study had a significant impact on combined outcome (lowest OR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.27–1.84; highest OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.47–2.25). Furthermore, sensitivity analyses by using 8 crude results (OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.52–2.66, p < 0.001) (see Supplementary Fig. 2) and excluding 3 studies involving disease-specific populations (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.50–2.54, p < 0.001) (see Supplementary Fig. 3) both demonstrated a significant association between SO and any cognitive dysfunction, suggesting the stability of the meta-analysis results.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses categorized by study design, geographic region, sex, cognitive dysfunction subtypes, population characteristics, diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia and obesity, and measurement of cognitive function are presented in Table 2 (forest plots are shown in Supplementary Figs. 4 to 11). Notably, the association between SO and higher odds of any cognitive dysfunction type remained consistent across subgroups stratified by study design, geographic region, cognitive dysfunction subtypes, and diagnostic criteria for obesity. Compared with MCI (OR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.27–2.34, p < 0.001) and cognitive impairment (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.14–1.64, p = 0.001), SO demonstrated the strongest association with dementia (OR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.43–3.13, p < 0.001), followed by MCI. A significant link between SO and any type of cognitive dysfunction was observed in males (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.07–3.02, p = 0.028), but not in females (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 0.91-2.00, p = 0.133). However, the difference between genders was not statistically significant (P interaction = 0.391). Among the included studies, three enrolled participants with specific diseases, whereas the others did not. SO was significantly associated with cognitive dysfunction in general populations (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.50–2.54, p < 0.001), but not in those with specific diseases (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.97–1.49, p = 0.093) (P interaction = 0.005). In subgroup analyses based on diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia, SO showed significant effects when defined by muscle function alone (OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.14–4.69, p = 0.020) and muscle function and/or mass (OR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.35–2.07, p < 0.001), but not by muscle mass alone (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.97–1.50, p = 0.099) (P interaction = 0.046). In the clinical records (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.21–2.33, p = 0.002) and scale tests groups (OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.37–2.21, p < 0.001), SO was found to correlate with any type of cognitive dysfunction, whereas no such correlation was observed in the self-reported and/or scale tests group (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 0.67–3.13, p = 0.344) (P interaction = 0.906).

Systematic review

There were a few additional studies excluded from our meta-analysis because of incompatible data such as regression coefficients and the lack of raw data. There were 5 studies that supported the conclusions of our meta-analysis. Tolea et al. [21] reported that SO was associated with the poorest global cognitive performance across all four SO definitions compared with sarcopenia, obesity, or neither condition. Low et al. [33] showed a significant link between SO and reduced RBANS total scores. Similarly, Ju et al. [18] observed greater cognitive decline in sarcopenia-obesity groups versus controls. Additionally, Liu et al. [19] showed lower global cognition scores in individuals with both sarcopenia and abdominal obesity. Cao et al. [34] identified a negative relationship between waist-to-calf circumference ratio (WCR) and cognitive function.

However, one study provided evidence against the conclusions of our meta-analysis. A cross-sectional study by Oba et al. [31] found no significant differences in cognitive function between patients with SO and patients without SO. More details on these studies can be found in Table 3.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 studies comprising 274,996 participants, SO was associated with higher odds of any type of cognitive dysfunction compared to non-SO. Subgroup analyses revealed that this association was consistent across all subgroups categorized by study design, geographic region, cognitive dysfunction subtypes, and obesity diagnostic criteria. The association was also observed in subgroups of males, general populations, sarcopenia diagnosed by muscle function and/or mass or muscle function alone, and cognitive function assessed via clinical records or scale tests. However, no significant association was found among females, sarcopenia diagnosed by muscle mass alone, cognitive function assessed via self-report and/or scale tests, or individuals with specific diseases.

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive quantitative synthesis to investigate the association between SO and cognitive dysfunction. Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated that sarcopenia is linked to an elevated risk of cognitive impairment [38]. Notably, an “obesity paradox” may exist in the relationship between obesity and cognitive dysfunction. This study further adds value by suggesting that SO appears associated with cognitive dysfunction.

Several interrelated mechanisms may explain the potential link between obesity, sarcopenia, and cognitive dysfunction, including decreased participation in physical activity, low-grade chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance [21]. First, insulin resistance may serve as a potential mediating factor in the associations among sarcopenia, obesity, and cognitive function. Body fat and muscle catabolism activate inflammatory processes and the presence of insulin resistance. In the presence of insulin resistance, excess levels of circulating insulin may contribute to increased β-amyloid (Aβ) [22, 24]. Second, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and C-reactive protein (CRP) have demonstrated positive correlations with fat mass and negative correlations with muscle mass. Elevated levels of IL-6, CRP, and TNF-α have been associated with accelerated cognitive decline in older individuals. Notably, TNF-α and CRP have been shown to penetrate the blood–brain barrier and directly damage neurons, promoting the formation of apolipoprotein E and exacerbating neurological dysfunction [39, 40]. Third, previous studies have found that growth hormone secretion is decreased in SO individuals [41]. Hormones are associated with cognitive function and sex hormones have brain-protective effects [42].

A central challenge in SO clinical research is the lack of a standardized definition, driving inconsistencies in findings on the SO-cognitive dysfunction association. Fu et al. [24] investigated the SO-MCI link in middle-aged and older adults using four criteria (visceral fat area [VFA], WC, fat mass percentage [BF%], and BMI) in the same cohort, finding VFA-, WC-, or BF%-defined SO associated with increased MCI odds, but BMI-defined SO showed no significant association (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 0.67–3.12). However, the SO group under the BMI criteria included only 42 patients, which might introduce bias. Similarly, Booranasuksakul (2023) [35] evaluated four SO definitions (appendicular skeletal muscle mass index [ASMI] + FM%, ASMI + fat mass index [FMI], FM/ fat-free mass [FFM], and trunk fat mass [TrFM]/ appendicular skeletal muscle mass [ASM]) and found three correlated with cognitive impairment while ASMI + FMI did not (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 0.90–2.20). Thus, future studies should prioritize diagnostic criteria consensus to enhance comparability.

According to World Health Organization (WHO) reference standards and National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) guidelines, the cut-off values for BMI, WC, and body composition are considered equivalent [43–46]. We performed subgroup analyses based on sarcopenia diagnostic criteria, and the results showed that SO was associated with cognitive dysfunction in the muscle function alone and muscle function and/or mass groups, but not in the muscle mass alone group (P interaction = 0.046). This association persisted after excluding Wu’s study female data [32], but the test for interaction was not significant (P interaction = 0.484). This aligns with several prior studies. Tou et al. [27] found no SO-cognitive impairment link in adjusted models, suggesting this might stem from the appendicular lean mass index (ALMI) having no independent impact on cognitive function. They further noted that obesity combined with low muscle strength or poor physical performance was correlated with higher odds of global and domain-specific cognitive impairment. Similarly, Oba et al. [31] observed no cognitive difference between patients with and without SO, possibly due to the use of ASMI for sarcopenia diagnosis. However, their study population comprised elderly individuals with cardiometabolic diseases, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, a longitudinal study demonstrated that poor muscle function was a better predictor of incident MCI and cognitive decline than reduced lean muscle mass [47]. Therefore, future research should focus on evaluating the relationship between muscle mass loss and cognitive dysfunction.

Subgroup analysis showed that SO was associated with cognitive dysfunction exclusively in general populations (P interaction = 0.005), and this association may be masked by inflammation, metabolism, or medications in those with specific diseases [48]. This indicates our conclusions may only apply to the general populations, highlighting the need for further research into the SO-cognitive dysfunction link in disease-specific populations. In sex-stratified analysis, a correlation between SO and cognitive dysfunction was only observed in males; however, the difference between genders was not statistically significant (P interaction = 0.391). This observation is inconsistent with the findings of several prior studies [7, 20]. Such discrepancies may be attributed to population variations or unmeasured sex-specific confounding factors, highlighting the need for additional high-quality research in this area. In cognitive measurement subgroups, SO was associated with cognitive dysfunction in clinical records or standardized neuropsychological tests but not in self-report and/or scale tests. Self-reported measures may be less sensitive than objective assessments. However, only one study has used the self-report and/or scale tests, resulting in insufficient evidence.

Our subgroup analysis by obesity assessment indicators showed that SO defined by WC, body composition, or BMI were all associated with cognitive dysfunction. Notably, the OR for the association between BMI-assessed SO and cognitive dysfunction was higher than that for WC- or body composition-assessed SO, which contradicts previous studies that supported a more significant association between WC-detected obesity and cognitive outcomes in the elderly [49]. Existing studies have confirmed that BMI has low sensitivity for detecting obesity in the elderly and cannot distinguish body composition (i.e., cannot identify the proportion of muscle and fat), whereas WC can identify a larger proportion of obese individuals more accurately [50]. Additionally, other studies have suggested that body fat percentage may be a better indicator for assessing obesity in this population [51]. Prioritizing the clarification of which indicator can more accurately capture the “geriatric SO phenotype linked to cognitive dysfunction " is critical for future research.

In our study, 4 studies were prospective cohort studies with follow-up periods ranging from 7 to 14 years. All demonstrated that SO was associated with increased long-term risk of cognitive dysfunction, providing preliminary evidence for inferring their causal relationship. It should be noted that the SO status of participants may undergo dynamic changes over the follow-up period, which could have a potential impact on the results. Additionally, despite statistical adjustment for potential confounders in each study, residual confounders (e.g., physical activity, social engagement, depressive status, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, dietary intake) may remain, leading the observed association strength to deviate from the true causal effect. Thus, more rigorous future studies are needed to clarify the SO-cognitive dysfunction association.

The high heterogeneity observed in our meta-analyses highlights the complexity of interpreting pooled effects for the SO-cognitive dysfunction association. To address this, we used random-effects meta-analysis as well as sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses. While subgroup analyses revealed some changes in heterogeneity, suggesting that the examined factors might potentially contribute to between-study inconsistency, these differences were insufficient to confirm any of them as the primary source of heterogeneity in this study. The high level of heterogeneity and the negative findings from subgroup analyses may stem from the limited number of available studies in this field and the methodological inconsistencies across studies. In addition, numerous potential factors (e.g., outcome definition, lifestyle, concomitant medication use, methodological quality, age, and publication bias) warrant further investigation. These factors may have collectively contributed to the significant heterogeneity observed in this study. However, due to the limited number of available studies and inconsistent reporting, further exploration of these aspects was not feasible. Future studies should prioritize standardizing SO diagnosis and incorporating stratification or subgroup analyses based on these factors to generate higher-quality evidence for clarifying the SO-cognitive dysfunction association.

This study has several limitations. First, there was substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. Although our subgroup analyses identified some potential sources of heterogeneity, they did not significantly reduce the overall level of heterogeneity, and many contributing factors remain unclear. Therefore, the results of this study should be treated with caution. Second, caution is warranted when interpreting certain subgroup analyses (e.g. evaluation criteria based on self-reported and/or scale tests), as the limited number of studies may reduce statistical power and increase variability. Third, most studies were cross-sectional, precluding determination of temporal sequence and causality. SO may increase the risk of cognitive dysfunction, and vice versa (i.e., cognitive impairment may lead to lifestyle changes that promote SO). Further validation should be performed using a longitudinal design. Finally, potential publication bias was noted, despite our systematic search strategy and inclusion of recent literature. Despite these limitations, this meta-analysis offers the most comprehensive evidence to date on the SO-cognitive dysfunction association, encompassing diverse populations and methodologies.

Conclusions

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 observational studies comprising 274,996 participants, an association appeared to exist between SO and cognitive dysfunction. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the very low certainty of evidence, high heterogeneity, and methodological limitations of the included studies. High-quality prospective studies will be needed in the future to validate this conclusion.
