Investigating Neotongue Atrophy and Postoperative Outcomes in Patients Undergoing Glossectomy With Free Flap Tongue Reconstruction
Authors: Audrey Abend, Sophia Salingaros, Adrienne Biskaduros, Matthew Spano, Matthew Wright, Sara Strauss, Jason A. Spector, David I. Kutler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFollowing glossectomy, tongue reconstruction via autologous free flap can reapproximate native form and function, significantly improving postoperative quality of life. Various studies have examined functional outcomes between the commonly employed radial forearm free flap (RFFF) and the anterolateral thigh free flap (ALTFF); however, changes in neotongue volumetric bulk are less clearly understood.Study DesignWe aimed to assess postoperative outcomes and volume changes in patients undergoing glossectomy.SettingThis is a retrospective chart review of patients who underwent glossectomy with reconstruction at a single academic center.MethodsPatients who underwent hemiglossectomy with RFFF or ALTFF reconstruction were retrospectively identified. Patient demographics, past medical history, and treatment course were collected. Perioperative imaging was utilized to measure native tongue, tumor, and postoperative neotongue volumes.ResultsOf 35 patients identified, ALTFF patients presented with greater tumor stage and depth of invasion than RFFF patients. Volumetric analysis of 17 patients revealed a mean tongue size decrease of 31.68 cm3 (SD, 42.02). Correlational analyses revealed a significant negative relationship of tongue volume over time (Spearman coefficient = −0.52, P = .04). Length of time was significant independent of flap type or mass volume (F = 4.85, P = .046). No significant relationship was found between flap type, tumor size, and change in total tongue size. ALTFF patients trended towards a greater rate of percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube dependence than RFFF patients at 1 month, but by 3 months all patients tolerated oral intake; however, ALTFF patients demonstrated a smaller absolute reduction in body mass index (BMI) compared to RFFF patients.ConclusionPostoperative neotongue volume decreased in both the ALTFF and RFFF cohorts over time but was not impacted by flap type or tumor burden. Further research is warranted to understand the impact of flap design on neotongue atrophy.

Carcinoma of the tongue is the most common malignancy of the oral cavity, 1 with an estimated incidence of almost 20,000 new cases in 2024. 2 Definitive treatment often requires various degrees of surgical resection in addition to chemoradiation. Tissue defects following glossectomy can significantly impair the tongue's native capacity for speech articulation, swallowing, mastication, and oral hygiene maintenance, posing a significant medical and quality of life burden on the patient. 3 While smaller defects may heal via primary closure or skin grafting, tongue reconstruction via microsurgical anastomosis of autologous flap tissue is an ever‐evolving tool in a plastic surgeon's armamentarium to reapproximate the tongue's form and function in more complex resections. Free flap reconstruction for patients who undergo hemiglossectomy can improve swallow ability compared to primary closure and can be instrumental in improving functional outcomes postoperatively. 4

The most commonly employed techniques for tongue reconstruction remain the radial forearm free flap (RFFF), preferred for smaller volume restoration, and the anterolateral thigh free flap (ALTFF), historically preferred for larger defects. 5 Though popular across many reconstructive indications, the RFFF is associated with functional and aesthetic donor site morbidity. 6 Meanwhile, the ALTFF boasts the advantage of a bulkier design capable of filling larger head and neck defects, and is generally associated with lower donor site morbidity. However, the use of ALTFF may present greater technical challenges due to the increased anatomical variation. 5

Studies examining postoperative outcomes collectively lack a definite conclusion on the superiority of one flap type versus another. 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Previous systematic reviews further suggest that neotongue bulk, protuberance, and area of resection preferentially impact postoperative outcomes rather than flap design, in addition to high patient motivation, strong family support, and regular care team follow‐up. 3 , 14 , 15 Importantly, neotongue bulk is known to vary over time: surgeons usually extract a slightly larger flap volume than the resection amount to counter postoperative free flap shrinkage due to adjuvant radiotherapy, muscle atrophy, denervation, and transitional ischemic effects. 16 , 17 , 18 However, few studies have quantified volumetric changes that occur after the tongue is reconstructed, nor have there been studies examining whether one flap type confers greater risk of atrophy than the other. In this study, we aimed to assess the preoperative and postoperative tongue bulk and treatment course in patients undergoing glossectomy with immediate RFFF or ALTFF reconstruction.

Methods

Patient Cohort and Demographics

This study was reviewed and approved by the Weill Cornell Institutional Review Board. Electronic medical records (EMRs) were retrospectively reviewed for adult patients who received immediate flap‐based reconstruction post‐glossectomy between May 2009 and June 2024. Patients with total glossectomy or prior/staged flap reconstruction were excluded. Patients' demographic information, medical history, oncologic history, surgical information, treatment course, and postoperative outcomes were collected from the EMR. Of note, tumor and nodal staging reflected the AJCC edition in use at the time of diagnosis (7th edition for 2010‐2017 and 8th edition for 2018‐2024), and staging was not retrospectively standardized across editions. This approach reflects real‐world clinical practice and the limitations of retrospective data availability. Patients that underwent resection of multiple areas of the oral cavity were also included, provided glossectomy was a component of the procedure.

Tongue Bulk

Preoperative native tongue, tumor, and postoperative neotongue (remaining native tongue and flap) volumes were radiologically measured from available computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and/or positron emission tomography (PET)‐CT imaging. Patients who did not have preoperative or postoperative imaging available were excluded from this analysis. Volumes collected were based on cubic multiplication of length, width, and depth measurements, taken as approximations due to the irregular shape of the tongue. Typically, the sagittal plane was utilized for longitudinal measurement and the axial place for transverse measurement, with a third measurement determined at the discretion of the radiologist. Latest imaging prior to surgery was used for statistical analysis, while postoperative scans used were, at the earliest, 3 months postoperatively. Appropriate scans at up to 2 years postoperatively were collected.

Perioperative and Long‐Term Functional Outcomes

Glossectomy resection extent was categorized as partial (less than one third of the tongue), hemi (one third to one half), or subtotal (half to three fourths), according to the operative note and the amount of tissue resected. Flaps were classified by their anatomic definitions and separated into RFFF and ALTFF. Tumor length, width, and depth dimensions were recorded from the postoperative pathology report. Duration of percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube placement was calculated from initial placement to removal. PEG tube dependence was determined if the patient had a PEG tube in place and was receiving all nutrition by tube feed (nothing by mouth). PEG tube dependence was documented at 1 and 3 months postoperatively. Baseline preoperative body mass index (BMI) and most recently documented BMI within the EMR were additionally collected to evaluate longitudinal BMI change as a potential surrogate marker of postoperative nutritional status and oral intake.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were reported as mean with standard deviation for normally distributed variables or median with interquartile range (IQR) for non‐normally distributed variables. Normality was tested with the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Fisher's exact tests were used to compare categorical variables. t tests were used to compare normally distributed continuous variables, and Spearman correlation and Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were used to compare non‐normally distributed continuous variables. Analysis of variance (ANOVA), analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), and Kruskal‐Wallis tests were used to compare normally and non‐normally distributed continuous variables in greater than two groups, respectively. A P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant. Prism 10 (GraphPad), R (R Core Team), and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation) were used for all analyses.

Results

Patient Demographics

During the study period, 35 patients underwent glossectomy with flap‐based reconstruction (22 RFFF, 13 ALTFF, Table 1). The median age was 62 years (IQR: 53, 71). The median BMI was 25.8 kg/m2 (20.3, 28.7), which differed significantly across flap types (RFFF, 27.8; ALTFF, 23.0; P = .034). The majority of patients in each flap group self‐identified as “white” race and “not Hispanic or Latino” ethnicity. Comorbidities were similar between flap types, the most common being a diagnosis of hypertension (n = 13, 37%) followed by hyperlipidemia (n = 11, 31%). Most patients reported never smoking tobacco products (n = 25, 71%) or current alcohol use (n = 26, 74%).

Oncologic Characteristics

Almost all patients were diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma of the tongue (n = 33, 94%, Table 2). Tumor stage differed between flap type: while most commonly stage 3 in both RFFF (n = 13, 59%) and ALTFF (n = 5, 38%) cohorts, four ALTFF patients had stage 4 disease (31%) versus no RFFF patients (P = .038). Most patients had no nodal involvement (N0) (n = 20, 57%). Median tumor volume was non‐significantly greater in ALTFF patients (10.1 cm3) than RFFF (6.8 cm3, P = .861), but median tumor depth was significantly greater in ALTFF patients (1.75 cm) than RFFF (1.20 cm, P = .019).

Treatment Course

Of the total cohort, 14 patients (40%) had undergone prior oral cavity surgery (Table 3). Only two patients underwent preoperative chemotherapy (6%). RFFF patients predominantly underwent partial (n = 9, 41%) and hemi (n = 9, 41%) glossectomy, similarly with ALTFF patients (partial, n = 5, 38%; hemi, n = 5, 38%, P > .999). Most patients simultaneously underwent neck dissection (n = 31, 89%), tracheostomy (n = 34, 97%), and PEG tube placement (n = 34, 97%), and almost a third of patients underwent mandibulotomy (n = 11, 31%), with no significant differences between cohorts regarding these additional procedures. Most patients underwent postoperative radiation (RFFF, n = 16, 73%; ALTFF, n = 11, 85%, P = .680) and some patients underwent postoperative chemotherapy (RFFF, n = 5, 23%; ALTFF, n = 4, 31%, P = .698). Infection was the most common postoperative complication, which occurred in 5 patients (14%) total.

Neotongue Bulk

Eighteen subjects did not have adequate preoperative or postoperative imaging available. As such, 17 subjects (male, n = 11, 65%; female, n = 6, 35%) ages 19 through 72 (mean, 56.6) were identified for tongue bulk assessment (Table 4). Nine (53%) patients underwent RFFF reconstruction and eight (47%) underwent ALTFF reconstruction. All patients received postoperative radiation. The size of the native tongue with tumor mass incorporated was 145.93 cm3 (SD, 47.3). Mean tumor size alone was 17.27 cm3 (SD, 16.9). Post‐reconstruction, mean neotongue size (with flap) measured 115.18 cm3 (SD, 45.54) at the last available imaging (mean, 341 days postoperatively). Total tongue size decreased on average 31.68 cm3 (SD, 42.02), and averaged a 19.2% (SD, 21.9) decline from the native tongue volume. RFFF flaps decreased on average 25.9 cm3 (SD, 17.04) representing a 18.9% (SD, 13.2) shrinkage, and ALT decreased on average 38.3 cm3 (SD, 60.1) representing a 19.6% (SD, 29.9) shrinkage. Radiographic measurements did not vary significantly between flap types.

Postoperative Outcomes

Median follow‐up time was shorter for RFFF patients, at 19.5 (10.4, 39.9) months, versus ALTFF patients at 27.1 (3.30, 53.4) months, though not significant (P = .423, Table 6). Median duration of PEG tube placement was 122.0 (98.5, 244.8) days for RFFF and 128.4 (65.0, 202.5) days for ALTFF (P = .423). At 1 month, ALTFF patients trended towards a higher rate of PEG tube dependence (ie, intaking all nutrition via tube feed), with four patients (31%) remaining nil per os (NPO) versus one RFFF patient (5%, P = .052). By 3 months, all patients were tolerating oral intake even if the PEG tube was still in place. Absolute change in BMI from preoperative baseline to the patient's most recent follow‐up measurement differed significantly between the RFFF and ALT cohorts. Patients undergoing RFFF reconstruction experienced a greater mean decrease in BMI (mean ΔBMI −4.43 kg/m2) compared to those undergoing ALT reconstruction (mean ΔBMI −0.66 kg/m2; Welch's t test, P = .001).

Discussion

It is well‐known that post‐reconstructive free flaps face significant shrinkage in the postoperative period, particularly when exposed to radiation therapy. 17 , 19 , 20 Radiation‐induced shrinkage is hypothesized to occur due to decreased peripheral circulation, fat necrosis, and contraction. 19 , 21 Though studies on flap shrinkage post‐hemiglossectomy to date have shown somewhat mixed results on the effect of radiation, only irradiated patients were included in this analysis to remove the possible confounding factor. 17 Few studies have reported volumetric changes in tongue bulk as opposed to functional outcomes, due to the challenge of radiographically measuring tongue bulk. 16 When examining irradiated RFFF and ALTFF, overall tongue bulk was found to significantly decrease with time compared to the preoperative tongue volume. Both ALT and RF flap‐reconstructed tongues decreased in volume by approximately 20%, suggesting flap size may need to be designed to be 1.2 times larger than the original defect. The finding of significant neotongue shrinkage over time is consistent with existing literature. In their retrospective cohort of 22 patients with any head and neck lesion requiring a free flap transfer, Kamizono et al reported an average duration of 8.7 months until ALT flap volume reached its smallest size, at which point the flap had decreased on average to 47.7% of its intraoperative volume. 19 Similarly, in their retrospective study of 10 patients comparing ALTFF and RFFF reconstruction of hemiglossectomies, Younis et al found that the mean percentage shrinkage between 1 and 6 months after surgery was 39.6% ± 3.9% for ALTFF and 51.1% ± 6.2% for RFFF reconstruction. 21 Interestingly, within our cohort, the choice of flap type did not impact the resulting neotongue size. It is possible a significant correlation was not captured in the present cohort due to the heterogeneity of glossectomy type included (rather than limited to hemiglossectomy) and limited sample size; however, longitudinal decrease in neotongue bulk was not correlated with tumor mass, which was included as a proxy for extent of resection. These findings may suggest the adaptability of free flap techniques to tumor characteristics.

In this study, the higher tumor stage and greater depth of invasion found in ALTFF patients did not significantly impact neotongue volume changes or postoperative outcomes measured, suggesting there are multiple other patient‐specific factors that can impact post‐reconstructive recovery. Functional ability, including swallow and speech, is expected to gradually improve postoperatively as the wound heals, surgery‐induced inflammation subsides, and the patient becomes accustomed to the neotongue anatomy. Prior studies suggest maximal postoperative swallow ability occurs after around one year, 5 with many patients achieving preoperative parameters. 3 PEG tube placement at the time of reconstruction occurred in all patients in this study except one, who received a nasogastric tube instead. By 3 months, all patients had regained oral intake to some capacity, in concordance with reported progressive return of swallow function. 15 Length of PEG tube placement did not differ significantly between flap types. Importantly, PEG tube removal relies not only on speech‐language pathologist (SLP) swallow evaluation, but also external factors such as patient and provider availability for the removal. While our analysis demonstrated a significantly greater reduction in BMI among patients undergoing RFFF reconstruction, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Patients in the ALT cohort presented with larger and more advanced tumors, which may have contributed to greater baseline dysphagia and preoperative nutritional compromise prior to resection, thereby potentially attenuating the degree of postoperative BMI decline observed in this group.

Considering the recovery of speech, resections at the tip of the tongue and larger (T3‐T4) tumor burden, and postoperative radiation therapy have a negative impact on speech post‐reconstruction, with many studies also suggesting a positive correlation of intelligibility with neotongue volume and protuberance. 3 , 9 , 14 , 22 Appropriate flap volume must accurately interact with nearby structures (eg, teeth, palate), and therefore the excision of adjacent tissue additionally affects speech capability directly. 23 , 24 Intelligible speech production post‐reconstruction is generally high across flap types. 5 , 7 , 9 , 25 Although PEG dependence improved over time in this cohort, validated functional speech and swallowing assessments were not uniformly available. Therefore, the relationship between radiographic neotongue volume change and functional recovery remains unclear.

This study is not without limitations. As with any retrospective review, the analyses are reliant upon the completion and validity of the EMR. Additionally, patients were heterogeneous with respect to oncologic treatment and perioperative management, which may have influenced postoperative neotongue volume changes and functional recovery. Future prospective studies incorporating detailed chemoradiation regimens may help better characterize the relationship between treatment intensity and longitudinal neotongue volume change.

The statistical power was limited with a small sample size. An important and significant limitation of analysis on tongue volume change was the use of pathologist‐ and radiologist‐reported cubic measurements as a proxy for tongue and tumor measurements, which likely overapproximates true volumes. Though cubic approximation does not fully capture the complex three‐dimensional geometry of postoperative tongue and free‐flap tissue, this approach allowed for reproducible assessment of relative volumetric change across heterogeneous CT and MRI studies in a retrospective cohort. Future studies using standardized imaging protocols and segmentation‐based volumetric analysis may provide greater precision and improved assessment of true correlation.

Furthermore, because immediate postoperative imaging was not uniformly available, a true postoperative volumetric baseline could not be established, thereby limiting precise atrophy over time. Accordingly, the observed reductions in neotongue bulk should be interpreted as longitudinal radiographic volume trends rather than definitive quantification of flap atrophy.

Finally, assessment of swallowing and speech outcomes in this cohort was limited by the absence of standardized, validated functional outcome measures. As a surrogate marker, BMI change was evaluated as a potential indicator of swallowing impairment; however, BMI reduction is multifactorial and may be influenced by numerous oncologic, treatment‐related, and patient‐specific factors independent of swallow function.

Conclusion

Post‐glossectomy with free flap reconstruction, neotongue volume decreases over time irrespective of flap type, initial tumor size, or change in total tongue size. Further investigation with standardized postoperative imaging timepoints and segmentation‐based volumetric analysis is needed to more precisely characterize true neotongue atrophy. Identifying modifiable surgical and treatment‐related factors associated with preserved neotongue bulk may help optimize long‐term functional outcomes.
