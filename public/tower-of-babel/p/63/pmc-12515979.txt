Psychosis induced by invocation presenting as possession state: A case of Kitousei‐Seishinbyo still emerging in modern Japan
Authors: Fumiya Miyano, Nobuyuki Mitsui, Yutaka Fujii, Takahiro A Kato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKitousei‐Seishinbyo, or psychosis induced by invocation, was first described by Morita in 1915 as a culture‐bound syndrome characterized by possession‐like states and personality transformation triggered by prayer or spiritual practices. New psychosocial triggers (e.g., self‐improvement seminars, emerging religious groups) have been identified. However, reports of possession‐related episodes have become extremely rare. Diagnostic confusion with dissociative identity disorder or other specified dissociative disorders persists.Case PresentationA woman in her 50s, with no prior psychiatric history, developed acute possession‐like symptoms after visiting a fortune‐teller. She was told her misfortune was caused by the spirit of her aborted child. Soon after, she entered trance states, spoke in altered voices (male and childlike), and exhibited personality changes, agitation, and dangerous behaviors, leading to involuntary admission. On evaluation, laboratory tests, electroencephalogram (EEG), and imaging were unremarkable. During hospitalization, she exhibited mutism, rigidity, temporary auditory hallucinations, and somatic complaints such as heaviness and dizziness, which fluctuated depending on spiritual contact. Retrospective review revealed significant psychosocial stressors, including marital discord, caregiving burden, and strained family relationships. Management involved family psychoeducation, occupational therapy, and low‐dose psychotropics (tandospirone and zolpidem). Her symptoms gradually stabilized, and she voluntarily discontinued spiritual practices. She was discharged after 62 days with improved family dynamics and sustained recovery.ConclusionThis case illustrates a present‐day example of Kitousei‐Seishinbyo triggered by encountering a fortune‐teller. Improvement was achieved through combined psychosocial interventions and low‐dose pharmacotherapy.Keywords: culture‐bound syndromes, dissociative disorders, Kitousei‐Seishinbyo, psychotic disorders, trance and possession disorders

BACKGROUND

Kitousei‐Seishinbyo (psychosis induced by invocation) was introduced by Morita in 1915. It is characterized by two notable features: (A) the onset of illness is triggered by psychogenic factors such as prayer or related phenomena; (B) personality transformation is regarded as a central aspect. 1 Typical cases of Kitousei‐Seishinbyo with a clear onset and brief course centered on possession symptoms resemble “Dissociative Trance, Other Specified Dissociative Disorder” in DSM‐5‐TR and “6B63 Possession trance disorder” in ICD‐11. Kitousei‐Seishinbyo, dissociative trance, and dissociative identity disorder differ in terms of symptoms, the presence or absence of amnesia, number of identities, and sociocultural contexts (Table 1).

Over time, the condition evolved into the broader field of “possession research,” encompassing phenomena such as personality transformation and altered states of consciousness. Recently, new psychosocial triggers have emerged, including self‐improvement seminars and newly established religious organizations. 2 Despite this evolution, the frequency of reported possession‐related episodes has declined due to the rise of scientific rationalism, paralleled by a decrease in academic interest and publications. Treatment remains challenging, particularly where blurred self‐other boundaries or psychological vulnerability are present, necessitating further clinical scrutiny. 3

CASE PRESENTATION

Patient background

A woman in her 50s with a history of cervical cancer treated by hysterectomy 17 years ago, with no family psychiatric history. She was the third of four siblings, grew up in a rural town of Japan. She had a normal development with no apparent childhood problems, but her father was strict and unrelaxed in the home. After high school, she worked on her family's farm and married in her late twenties. She became a homemaker with two daughters (one pregnancy ended for financial reasons). Her husband dominated family decisions and was often absent, while her mother‐in‐law criticized her, causing conflict. She suppressed her emotions, confiding only in her eldest daughter. She had enjoyed card‐based fortune‐telling since high school and recently worked part‐time as a card‐based fortune‐teller, frequently visiting spiritual sites with friends. Before hospitalization, her eldest daughter moved out, and she lived with her husband, mother‐in‐law, and younger daughter.

Current medical history

On Day X − 4, the patient visited a fortune‐teller with a friend. The fortune‐teller told her misfortune was caused by the spirit of her aborted child. She was given incense sticks for a charm and returned home. That evening, she felt unwell and went to bed early. On Days X − 3 and X − 2, she appeared anxious and displayed impaired attention, failing to prepare meals for the correct number of people.

Early on Day X − 1, she burned incense while talking with the fortune‐teller, who instructed her to visit Shrine C. She began meditating, claimed to have “three eyes,” then suddenly spoke in a loud male voice and groaned. Her family took her to a temple for cleansing; after the ritual, she returned to normal but believed spirits remained. That night, her voice became childlike and affectionate.

On Day X, she decided to quit fortune‐telling and went to a shrine for purification. During the ritual, she became agitated, spoke in a male voice, and said the family must die. After police intervention, she showed further agitation and was referred to our department. She was hospitalized involuntarily with her husband's consent. On the same day, seclusion was initiated.

On Day X + 1, lab work, head computed tomography (CT), and electroencephalogram (EEG) were performed, all unremarkable. The patient recalled trance states, identity alteration, and temporary auditory hallucinations, which had largely resolved. However, somatic complaints, including a sensation of heaviness and anxiety, persisted. She remained calm during the day but exhibited mutism, accompanied by full‐body rigidity, and reported experiencing “head buzzing” at night.

On Day X + 6, open observation began. Tandospirone citrate 10 mg was initiated to ease anxiety, and zolpidem 5 mg was added later due to nocturnal awakenings. By Day X + 11, seclusion ended, and on Day X + 15, occupational therapy was initiated. Dizziness and buzzing sensations occurred intermittently but resolved within hours. Her symptoms emerged acutely and fluctuated with spiritual contact. Given the rapid resolution in a protective environment, a diagnosis of Kitousei‐Seishinbyo was made.

Retrospective review revealed numerous psychosocial stressors: marital discord, caregiver burden, conflict surrounding her daughter's marriage, and the anticipated cohabitation with her mother, who had mild cognitive impairment. In addition to clinical interventions, we introduced family psychoeducation, structured occupational therapy, and home‐visit support. Creating space for emotional expression and reducing interpersonal strain within the household contributed to sustained recovery. She voluntarily chose to distance herself from spiritual practices, which we supported as part of relapse prevention. She was discharged on Day X + 62 (Table 2).

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

Differential diagnoses such as schizophrenia and dissociative identity disorder were excluded given the transient course, lack of persistent psychotic features, and rapid response to psychosocial care. It is crucial to distinguish culturally accepted ritualized trance 4 , 5 from pathological dissociative trance, which is involuntary and disruptive, underscoring the importance of cultural context.

Kitousei‐Seishinbyo often emerges during life transitions when latent psychosocial stressors surface, as in our case with marital conflict and caregiving burdens. These were managed through distancing and gradual activation via occupational therapy. Psychotherapeutic approaches are challenging: while Kawatani and Nagaoka 6 reported benefits of therapy targeting dissociation, Kitanishi and Toyohara 3 noted that Morita and Naikan therapies could trigger possession, underscoring the risks in vulnerable patients. Long‐suppressed conflicts may manifest symbolically—as deities or animal spirits such as foxes—allowing individuals to assert superiority or transcend existing roles through personality transformation. Takahashi and Shibata 7 recognized examples where previously one‐sided hierarchical, marital, or parent–child relationships were reversed, and new relationships were maintained in their own way, and named this the “status reversal phenomenon.” In the present case, the relationship with the husband had been a one‐sided hierarchical relationship, but as a result of advice from the attending physician not to increase the psychological load after discharge, the relationship changed to a new one, with the husband now doing some of the household chores.

Pharmacologically, haloperidol and related antipsychotics have frequently been employed. 2 , 8 Miura 9 reported that quetiapine was effective in treating hallucinations and possession‐related delusions resistant to haloperidol. Internationally, Hale and Pinninti 10 described a case of ghost possession in a 22‐year‐old Indian man with dissociative disorder or paranoid schizophrenia, resistant to exorcism but responsive to antipsychotics. No standardized treatment exists for Kitousei‐Seishinbyo; therefore, pharmacotherapy should be tailored individually.

In our case, somatic symptoms such as dizziness, anorexia, and “buzzing” sensations were observed. Kato 11 conceptualized such states as “bodily affinity” with possession, underscoring their therapeutic relevance. These symptoms improved with tandospirone. Tandospirone is expected to suppress sympathetic overactivity and stabilize the parasympathetic nervous system via serotonin 1A receptors in the amygdala, hypothalamus, and other areas responsible for emotional and autonomic functions, leading to a reduction in autonomic symptoms (e.g., palpitations, breathlessness, and stomach discomfort). There have been no previous reports of tandospirone being successful in Kitousei‐Seishinbyo, and further cases are expected to accumulate.

In the Japanese context, possession phenomena have long been reported. Uchimura 12 described the “Imu” among the Ainu people of Hokkaido as a classical example, while Daiguuji 13 comprehensively reviewed cases such as kitsune‐tsuki (fox possession) and kamigakari (divine possession). These accounts illustrate that possession states in Japan have historically been embedded in cultural and social frameworks. Our case, triggered by a fortune‐teller's ritual, may be regarded as a contemporary manifestation of this cultural continuum.
