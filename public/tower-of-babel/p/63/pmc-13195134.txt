Reinforcement learning based resource allocation scheme for vehicular communication in 5G networks for smart cities
Authors: S. Brindha, P. P. Shehila Nasreen, Paresh Sagar, Subhra Sankha Sarma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With an emphasis on improving energy efficiency (EE) and lowering power consumption of rapidly growing connected vehicles and infrastructures, Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) communication is emerging as a fundamental element in the development of smart cities. This paper introduces an innovative reinforcement learning (RL)-based method for dynamic resource allocation within 5G-enabled V2X networks, focusing on EE and minimizing power consumption. The suggested framework adeptly modifies transmission power, and spectrum allocation in real-time, responding to fluctuating traffic patterns and network demands. By facilitating ongoing learning and decision-making, the RL system guarantees optimal resource utilization while preserving high-quality service and low-latency communication. Q-learning is employed to dynamically regulate power levels in urban vehicular scenarios, taking Doppler shift, user mobility, and changing traffic conditions into account. Experimental evaluations demonstrate a substantial decrease in power consumption and an improvement in network efficiency providing a sustainable solution for smart mobility initiatives, promoting the advancement of greener, more reliable, and energy-efficient urban transportation systems.

Introduction

The fifth generation (5G) of wireless communication has fundamentally changed the landscape of wireless connectivity, providing remarkable speed, reliability, and network capacity. Among its various applications, Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) communication is particularly noteworthy for its potential to transform intelligent transportation systems by facilitating seamless data exchange among vehicles, infrastructure, pedestrians, and networks, thus enhancing road safety and traffic efficiency1,2. However, V2X networks encounter considerable challenges in resource allocation due to the highly dynamic nature of vehicular environments. These challenges include high user mobility, fluctuating channel conditions, and strict latency requirements, all of which necessitate effective strategies to optimize power consumption while preserving communication performance. While increasing transmission power can improve signal quality and throughput, it frequently results in excessive energy usage, which impacts long-term sustainability1,3.

Reinforcement Learning (RL), a subset of machine learning, has surfaced as a promising approach to address such resource allocation issues in wireless systems. RL allows agents to learn optimal strategies through interaction with their environments and to adapt to changing conditions without needing prior knowledge of the system’s state – a vital capability in dynamic vehicular scenarios4. To enhance learning efficiency and scalability in these complex environments,a lightweight form of Q-learning because it is a computationally light approach that is suitable for vehicle environments. Unlike deep RL, which requires neural networks and a lot of processing, tabular Q-learning converges quickly and is suitable for embedded devices inside a vehicle. RL adeptly models complex relationships between critical parameters such as signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR), power levels, and vehicular positions, rendering it suitable for large-scale, real-time V2X communication systems5. This research presents a RL-based methodology aimed at optimizing resource distribution in 5G-enabled V2X communication systems, with the goal of enhancing energy efficiency while ensuring low-latency and high-reliability performance. The model takes into account practical limitations such as interference, Doppler shifts, and user mobility, thus aligning the learning process with real-world scenarios6.

In the context of smart cities, V2X technology acts as a crucial foundation, facilitating intelligent transportation capabilities such as adaptive traffic management, pedestrian safety notifications, and accident prevention systems. These functionalities are heavily dependent on ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC) and dynamic resource allocation7. By dynamically modifying transmission power through learning-based techniques, the proposed framework guarantees effective communication without incurring excessive energy expenditures. The decentralized aspect of reinforcement learning also enables vehicles to function autonomously, adjusting in real-time to changing traffic and network conditions8.

In conclusion, this study introduces a scalable and adaptive RL-based resource allocation strategy specifically designed for 5G V2X environments. By achieving a balance between power management and energy efficiency, it fosters the advancement of sustainable and intelligent vehicular communication infrastructures that are vital for the implementation of smart city initiatives.

Related works

Researchers globally have investigated numerous strategies to tackle the issues of resource allocation in vehicular 5G networks. The studies outlined below represent key contributions to the domain, against which our proposed work is positioned. Xiao et al. introduced a centralized energy-efficient power allocation framework for D2D-based V2X communication networks, utilizing fractional programming and the Dinkelbach algorithm. Although effective under QoS constraints, its dependence on static channel conditions restricts real-time adaptability. In contrast, our research utilizes a decentralized Q-learning-based model that is more appropriate for high-mobility vehicular environments1. Lim et al. formulated an adaptive transmit power control strategy for D2D communications in SWIPT-based IoT cellular networks, emphasizing throughput and energy harvesting. However, their static, low-mobility design is in stark contrast to our real-time, mobility-aware RL model specifically designed for 5G V2X2. Akhter et al. presented a semi-centralized spectrum sharing model for V2X communication in smart cities, employing heuristic techniques. While it improves QoS through roadside units and base stations, it lacks the necessary adaptability. Our approach, grounded in RL, offers distributed and dynamic power control that is well-suited for rapidly changing vehicular networks3.

Again, Liang et al. implemented RL for D2D spectrum allocation, framing the issue as an MDP and utilizing a Deep Q-Network. While this effectively addresses stable D2D scenarios, our focus is on energy-efficient power allocation in V2X settings, where the topology is subject to rapid changes4. Pan and Yang proposed a RL-based framework aimed at enhancing energy efficiency in D2D systems. Their centralized training method performs effectively in static networks, whereas our Q-learning approach is lightweight and facilitates decentralized, mobility-aware decision-making5. Huynh et al. presented a Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL) approach for dynamic spectrum access in ambient backscatter (ABC)-assisted D2D systems. Although QRL demonstrates rapid convergence, it primarily targets ultra-low-power IoT applications. In contrast, our classical RL method is designed for power control in high-mobility V2X scenarios6. Yu and Tang7 created a hybrid RL framework for cooperative D2D communications, effectively balancing centralized and distributed methodologies. Our model, however, distinguishes itself by utilizing a fully decentralized architecture that is well-suited for autonomous vehicles that demand real-time adaptability7. Xu et al. executed a Federated RL model aimed at resource allocation within V2X networks, facilitating distributed learning while ensuring privacy preservation. Nonetheless, this approach necessitates federated coordination. Our strategy removes the aggregation layer, allowing for independent decision-making and achieving faster convergence8. Kaytaz et al. proposed a hierarchical RL (HRL) framework for dynamic RAN slicing in 5G V2X, addressing resource allocation at both the slice and user levels. In contrast, our Q-learning model emphasizes low-complexity user-level decisions for power allocation, incurring minimal overhead9. Indulekha and Venkatesh introduced a reinforcement learning approach for ISAC-aided V2X, optimizing both sensing and communication processes. While their method is effective for dual-purpose tasks, our approach prioritizes energy-efficient communication in resource-constrained settings without the need for additional sensing feedback10.

Kim et al. introduced an improved resource allocation strategy for 5G V2X communication, emphasizing latency reduction and spectrum efficiency. However, this strategy is dependent on model-based optimization. In contrast, our reinforcement learning (RL) model adjusts to real-time fluctuations in Signal to Interference plus Noise Ratio (SINR), distance, and interference11. Wang et al. investigated the joint formation of platoons and resource allocation within cellular V2X networks. While their method is effective for collaborative driving, it presupposes coordination among vehicles. Our research facilitates independent vehicular agents by employing decentralized learning for power control12. Saxena et al. suggested a lightweight Quality of Service (QoS)-aware allocation technique for NR-V2X, with the objective of enhancing reliability in congested networks. Our research complements this by incorporating energy efficiency as a vital optimization metric through adaptive reinforcement learning13. Hu and Chu presented a social-aware resource allocation strategy for V2X within cellular networks. Although their method leverages user behaviors for optimization, our RL model is solely driven by system-level parameters (such as SINR and interference), rendering it less complex and more broadly applicable14. Farhadi et al. tackled multi-tenancy slicing for 5G resource allocation in power distribution systems. While their work is pertinent in smart grid scenarios, their focus diverges from our objective of achieving low-latency, energy-efficient V2X communication under mobility constraints15. Dong et al. conducted a study on the selection of managers and the allocation of resources for V2X platoon systems utilizing finite block length coding. Their research addresses the issue of latency in group coordination. In contrast, our approach focuses on uncoordinated vehicular networks, highlighting the importance of energy-aware and independent decision-making16.

Xue et al. created a power allocation scheme enabled by deep reinforcement learning (RL) for multi-connectivity C-V2X downlink communication. Although it is a powerful solution, it relies on deep models that incur significant computational overhead. Our model, however, utilizes Q-learning to ensure lightweight adaptability in real-time vehicular systems17. Samy et al. investigated power optimization within THz band vehicular networks, aiming at high-frequency communication. Their method is specific to certain frequencies, while the proposed approach is broader, focusing on learning-based power control that is i of frequerrespectivency bands18. Huang et al. examined beamforming and power allocation in multi-antenna C-V2X systems. Their physical-layer model demands substantial hardware resources, whereas our reinforcement learning-based method prioritizes software-driven, decentralized power control with minimal reliance on infrastructure19. Finally, Vu et al. introduced a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for channel and power allocation in C-V2X platoon systems. Although their learning design is similar, the proposed single-agent approach is more straightforward and scalable for diverse vehicle scenarios, eliminating the need for coordinated learning or joint policies20.

Although previous works has examined a variety of methods for resource allocation in wireless and vehicular networks, many of these methods depend on centralized architectures, static optimization, or complex deep learning models. Some strategies concentrate on D2D communication or low-mobility IoT environments, which diminish their effectiveness in the rapidly changing, interference-laden contexts typical of 5G-enabled V2X systems. Other approaches introduce ideas such as spectrum reuse, federated learning, hierarchical deep reinforcement learning, or quantum learning, which yield promising outcomes but frequently necessitate comprehensive network knowledge, synchronized model updates, or substantial computational resources–elements that hinder scalability and real-time implementation. In contrast, the proposed work presents a lightweight, fully decentralized reinforcement learning framework utilizing Q-learning that dynamically adjusts to real-world variables such as SINR, Doppler shift, interference, and traffic conditions. Unlike deep reinforcement learning models that require extensive training and hardware support, this solution provides quick convergence, minimal overhead, and is appropriate for embedded vehicular devices. Furthermore, by concentrating specifically on energy-efficient power allocation rather than multi-layer or joint sensing-communication optimization, the proposed approach guarantees real-time responsiveness and simplicity. Its decentralized characteristic enhances scalability in dense networks, and its adaptability renders it highly applicable for next-generation intelligent transportation systems functioning under mobility constraints. These attributes render the project both technically robust and practically implementable in realistic 5G V2X scenarios.

System model

Figure 1 illustrates the system model of a 5G-enabled V2X communication system implemented at a bustling urban intersection, demonstrating the intricate interactions among various network components within an intelligent transportation framework. This scenario encompasses vehicles, pedestrians, roadside units (RSUs), 5G base stations (gNBs), mobile edge computing (MEC) nodes, and adjacent smart infrastructure such as buildings. Vehicles engage in Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) communication through direct sidelinks (PC5) to share critical safety information, including speed and braking events. Pedestrians equipped with connected devices take part in V2P communication via short-range or cellular multicast links to improve safety in crosswalks or areas with limited visibility. RSUs, strategically located at intersections, facilitate Vehicle-to-Infrastructure (V2I) communication, enabling updates on traffic signals and hazard alerts. The gNBs support cellular V2X (C-V2X) communication by overseeing connectivity, mobility, and long-distance messaging. MEC nodes situated near RSUs or gNBs deliver ultra-low-latency processing for real-time applications such as collision avoidance or coordination of autonomous driving. Dotted lines in the illustration signify various communication links–V2V, V2P, V2I, and Vehicle-to-Network (V2N)–highlighting the integration of satellite or cloud connectivity for expanded network access.Figure 1System model of V2X communication portraying all signals among users.

The urban landscape, defined by its roads, trees, and structures, presents tangible challenges like shadowing, interference, and Doppler effects that affect the reliability of communication. This system model emphasizes the dynamic and diverse characteristics of V2X networks, underscoring the necessity for intelligent, decentralized, and adaptive power allocation strategies. The RL-based method suggested in the paper tackles these issues by facilitating real-time decision-making that takes into account fluctuating SINR, mobility, and traffic conditions, thereby promoting energy-efficient and dependable communication in the smart cities of the future.

Robustness to network failures and dynamic mobility

In practical 5G-enabled V2X deployments, temporary infrastructure failures such as UAV malfunction, RSU outage, or coverage degradation may occur due to blockage, mobility, or hardware limitations. The proposed decentralized Q-learning framework inherently provides robustness under such conditions.

Each vehicular agent independently updates its Q-values based on locally observed states such as SINR, interference, and distance. When coverage degrades, the resulting reduction in SINR leads to lower reward values, prompting the agent to explore alternative transmit power strategies. This enables adaptive recovery without centralized coordination.

Furthermore, unpredictable mobility and rapid traffic fluctuations are incorporated into the state representation, allowing continuous policy adaptation in dense urban scenarios. This ensures graceful performance degradation rather than abrupt communication failure.

In these situations, it is essential to effectively manage interference among multiple users alongside differing QoS requirements, such as those for safety-critical messages compared to infotainment. Conventional optimization techniques often fall short in providing real-time adaptability in these high-dimensional, time-varying systems. RL presents a viable solution by allowing vehicles and infrastructure nodes to learn optimal power allocation strategies through environmental interaction. By utilizing real-time channel state information (CSI), link priorities, and network feedback, RL agents can adjust transmission power dynamically to minimize interference, lower energy consumption, and uphold QoS. Reinforcement learning

RL is a machine learning approach in which an agent acquires decision-making skills through interactions with a dynamic environment to fulfill a specific goal. In the realm of V2X communication, the RL agent monitors the state of the wireless environment, including factors such as the distance between nodes, traffic density, and signal strength, and chooses a suitable action, which is usually a discrete level of transmission power. Following the chosen action, the agent is awarded a reward that measures the effectiveness of that action regarding EE and communication quality, often represented by metrics like SINR. The primary objective of the agent is to develop an optimal policy that maximizes the long-term cumulative reward while balancing power consumption and QoS. In contrast to traditional optimization techniques, RL does not necessitate prior knowledge of channel conditions or system models and can adjust in real-time to variations in vehicular topology, interference, and mobility. This adaptability renders RL particularly effective for decentralized and scalable power allocation in 5G-enabled V2X networks, where rapid and intelligent decision-making is crucial for sustaining reliable and energy-efficient communication within practical limitations.

Impact of imperfect channel state information

In practice, with high-mobility V2X communications, estimating the channel state may not always be perfect, owing to factors like Doppler effect, fading, and delay in feedback mechanisms. Such imperfect information may drive decision-making processes in an unpredictable manner, resulting in suboptimal power choices.

However, since the objective of Q-learning is to maximize long-term cumulative reward, the random variations of the channel are averaged over several learning episodes, thereby providing robustness to the learning algorithm despite imperfect channel state information.

Conclusion and future trends

This work proposes a decentralized reinforcement learning solution for power allocation in 5G enabled V2X communication in smart cities. Q-learning is utilized for dynamic power control with the consideration of SINR, vehicle mobility, traffic density, and interference.

This decentralized solution provides the advantage of scalability, network failure robustness, and smooth integration with 5G NR-V2X infrastructure. Simulations demonstrate that this solution can adapt to different urban communication conditions while being practical for real-time implementation.

Future Work We believe that exploring the world of multi-agent reinforcement learning can be a great way to encourage cooperative vehicular optimization, thus optimizing spectrum usage in areas with high traffic density. Moreover, integrating federated learning with this solution can be a great way to test this solution on real 5G testbeds.
