The Role of Enterococcus faecium in the Synergistic Clearance of High‐Risk HPV: A Clinical Study on Cervicovaginal Microbiota Regulation
Authors: Jun Yang, Mengya Gao, Danfeng Wang, Ziwei Li, Sijing Li, Min Zhou, Li‐Na Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPersistent infection with high‐risk human papillomavirus (HR‐HPV) is defined as the principal cause of cervical cancer. Given the important role of cervicovaginal microbiota in HPV acquisition and disease progression, this study aims to evaluate the clinical efficacy of the probiotic Enterococcus faecium ( E. faecium ) in synergistically promoting clearance of persistent HR‐HPV infection.MethodsWe conducted a prospective clinical study involving 365 female patients with persistent HR‐HPV infection. Following necessary anti‐infective treatment, participants were allocated by their preference to one of three groups: an intervention group receiving E. faecium supplementation (n = 124), a regular follow‐up group (n = 120), or a blank control group (n = 121). Their HR‐HPV status and microecological parameters were assessed at 0, 6, and 12 months. Additionally, 24 participants were selected for 16S rRNA sequencing to analyze changes in the cervicovaginal microbiota composition during the follow‐up period.ResultsSupplementation with E. faecium resulted in a significantly higher HPV clearance rate compared to the regular follow‐up and control groups (55.65% vs. 29.17% vs. 13.22%, p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis confirmed that smoking (OR = 0.110, 95% CI 0.014–0.857, p = 0.035) and duration of persistent HPV infection (OR = 0.872, 95% CI 0.794–0.958, p = 0.004) were independent factors affecting HPV clearance. In the experimental group, the ecological shift was evidenced by a marked increase in the abundance of Lactobacillus (from 54.01% to 75.65%) and a significant reduction in several pathobionts, including Atopobium, Prevotella, and Staphylococcus (p < 0.001). Importantly, the abundance of Lactobacillus was substantially higher in HPV‐negative individuals than in those with persistent infection (93.78% vs. 29.96%, p < 0.001).ConclusionOur findings indicate that in women with persistent HR‐HPV infection, E. faecium supplementation was associated with higher rates of HPV negativity and a shift toward a Lactobacilli‐dominated vaginal microbiota, although causal relationships and underlying mechanisms require further investigation.Keywords: 16S rRNA sequencing, cervicovaginal microbiota, Enterococcus faecium , HR‐HPV

Introduction

Cervical cancer poses a significant threat to women's health worldwide [1, 2, 3, 4]. According to the 2022 global cancer statistics report, it ranks as the leading incident cancer among women in 25 countries [5]. Persistent infection with high‐risk human papillomavirus (HR‐HPV) is defined as the principal cause of cervical cancer [6]. However, the effective treatment of persistent HR‐HPV infection remains a significant clinical limitation.

Studies have demonstrated that there is a significant association between abnormal vaginal microecology and HPV infection [7]. Lactobacillus, one of the most common probiotics, is a keystone species in the female vaginal environment [8, 9, 10]. Notably, Enterococcus faecium ‐a facultative anaerobic bacterium, is also a significant constituent of the cervicovaginal microbiota and is distributed across multiple human sites, such as the intestinal tract, vagina, amniotic fluid, oral cavity, and stomach [11]. This bacterium metabolizes L‐lactic acid [12], and existing research indicates it may exhibit synergistic antiviral properties, with some clinical efficacy observed [13, 14]. However, the role of Enterococcus faecium in modulating the cervicovaginal microbiota remains poorly understood. This study aims to employ a prospective design in a population with persistent HPV infection, integrating virological outcomes with microbiome analysis to preliminarily validate its clinical application, thereby offering new strategies for cervical cancer prevention and control.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Objectives

From April 2023 to April 2025, women with persistent HR‐HPV infection who attended the Cervical Disease Diagnosis and Treatment Center of Women and Children's Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing Health Center for Women and Children) were selected as the study subjects. Participants were divided into an experimental group (receiving Enterococcus faecium intervention) and control groups (non‐intervention group). Cervicovaginal microecology, Thinprep Cytologic Test (TCT), HPV‐DNA, colposcopy findings, and adverse reactions were assessed before and during the follow‐up period. Cervical biopsy was performed when necessary to evaluate clinical outcomes. 16S rRNA microbial diversity sequencing was used to analyze the composition and structure of the vaginal microbiota.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Aged over 20 years with a history of sexual activity; (2) Persistent HR‐HPV infection for one year or longer; (3) TCT showing no abnormal cells; (4) Colposcopic assessment revealing no significant lesions in the vulva/vaginal wall/cervix, with precancerous lesions and carcinoma ruled out; (5) Provided fully informed consent for the study; no pregnancy plans during the study period for participants of childbearing age; consistent and correct use of condoms during sexual activity. Exclusion criteria: (1) History of cervical glandular epithelial abnormalities; (2) Combined severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, uncontrolled thyroid disease, diabetes, or other major organ diseases; (3) Concurrent malignancy in other organs; (4) Presence of immune system diseases or use of immunosuppressive drugs; (5) Inability to complete scheduled follow‐up visits; (6) Previous allergy to related substances (including Enterococcus faecium , condoms, etc.).

Experimental Grouping

The enrolled patients were divided into 3 groups according to the patients' wishes: Experimental Group: Participants received anti‐infective treatment (if diagnosed with vaginitis) and probiotic treatment: Enterococcus faecium vaginal capsules (10 days as one treatment course) (Active ingredient: Enterococcus faecium ; Manufacturer: Xi'an Zhenghao Biopharmaceutical Co. Ltd.; Specification: 0.25 g/capsule; China National Drug Approval No. H10980293) until vaginal microbiota reaches normal standards. Enterococcus faecium vaginal capsules continuous administration is limited to a maximum of 3 treatment courses/3 months. Regular follow‐up Group: This group followed the same schedule and methods for follow‐up as the Experimental Group. Interventions were limited to the treatment of vaginitis, without the administration of Enterococcus faecium . Blank Control Group: Participants in this group were re‐examined at 0 and 6 months after the study initiation. Those who had not reached the follow‐up endpoint continued follow‐up until 12 months. Corresponding treatments were provided promptly if clinical symptoms related to vaginitis emerged during follow‐up, but Enterococcus faecium was not used. The standardized anti‐infective therapies were as follows: Patients with bacterial vaginosis received 0.75% metronidazole gel 5 g administered intravaginally once daily for 5 days; those with drug allergies received 2% clindamycin gel 5 g administered intravaginally once daily for 7 days. For patients with aerobic bacterial vaginitis, 2% clindamycin cream 5 g was applied vaginally once daily for 7 days. For those with hypersensitivity, cefuroxime axetil 250 mg was administered twice daily for 7 days. Patients with vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC) received a single dose of intravaginal clotrimazole 0.5 g; those allergic to clotrimazole received a single oral dose of fluconazole 0.15 g. For severe VVC, the treatment regimen was extended to intravaginal clotrimazole 0.5 g on days 1 and 4, or oral fluconazole on days 1 and 4 for those allergic to clotrimazole. For drug‐resistant cases, medication was selected based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing reports. Patients with trichomoniasis received a single oral dose of metronidazole 2 g; those with drug allergies received a single oral dose of tinidazole 2 g. Patients with atrophic vaginitis were treated with topical estrogen cream; those with concomitant bacterial infection received additional anti‐infective treatment (as described above). CV patients underwent sitz baths with sodium bicarbonate solution (dissolving 1 tablespoon of NaHCO3 in 4 L of warm water for sitz baths, once every 2 days, for 10 days) [15]. CV patients were excluded from the experimental group. Combined therapy is adopted for mixed vaginitis.

The study endpoint was defined as HPV clearance concurrent with normal cytological and colposcopic assessments. Participants whose HPV test remained positive by 6 months were followed until the 12‐month mark, at which time vaginal microecology, cervical cytology, HPV testing, and colposcopic assessment were repeated, with biopsy performed if necessary. Clearance: Defined as the disappearance of all HPV types (primary endpoint) [16, 17]. Improvement: Defined as the disappearance of some HPV types (secondary endpoint).

Vaginal swabs were collected from each participant before the intervention and at the study endpoint (after intervention), designated as follows: A1 (Experimental group—pre‐treatment), A2 (Experimental group—post‐treatment), B1 (regular follow‐up group—pre‐treatment), B2 (regular follow‐up group—post‐treatment), C1 (Blank Control group—pre‐treatment), C2 (Blank Control group—post‐treatment). The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committees of Chongqing Health Center for Women and Children (Women and Children's Hospital of Chongqing Medical University) (Approval No. 2023–013). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. All experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant nomenclature guidelines and regulations.

Sample Collection and Processing

Vaginal samples were collected from participants in each group using sterile swabs according to the experimental protocol. Immediately after collection, samples were stored at −80°C for subsequent DNA extraction. Using a stratified random approach, eight participants were selected from each clinical group, evenly distributed by age category (20s, 30s, 40s, and ≥ 50 years; two per category). Each participant contributed paired pre‐ and post‐intervention samples. All sample collections excluded individuals who had engaged in sexual activity, vaginal douching, or used intravaginal medications within 48 h prior to sampling. DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and 16S rRNA sequencing were performed by Shanghai OE Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution are described as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical data are presented as numbers or percentages, with group comparisons performed using Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test. Ordinal data were analyzed using the rank‐sum test. Binary logistic regression was employed for multivariate analysis. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Alpha and beta diversity analyses were conducted using QIIME 2 software. Alpha diversity of samples was assessed using indices including the Chao1 index and Shannon index. Beta diversity was evaluated by unweighted Unifrac principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on the unweighted Unifrac distance matrix calculated in R. Differential analysis was performed using ANOVA/Kruskal‐Wallis/t‐test/Wilcoxon statistical algorithms implemented in R packages. LEfSe was used for differential analysis of species abundance profiles.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 505 patients were initially recruited from the Cervical Disease Diagnosis and Treatment Center of the Women and Children's Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing Health Center for Women and Children) between April 2023 and April 2025. Among them, 114 patients were excluded for various patient‐related reasons, and 16 were lost to follow‐up. In the Enterococcus faecium treatment group, two patients experienced mild vulvar pruritus during medication, which resolved after discontinuation. No recurrence of vulvar pruritus was observed following re‐administration. Ultimately, a total of 365 patients were enrolled in the study. The process of patient inclusion and exclusion flowchart is summarized in Figure 1.

The mean age of participants in the three groups ranged from 42 to 43 years, with no statistically significant difference in age distribution (p > 0.05). Furthermore, there were no significant differences among the groups regarding age at first sexual intercourse, number of sexual partners, pregnancy history, parity, or education level (p > 0.05). The duration of persistent HPV infection, the proportion of HPV16/18 type infection, and the proportion of single versus multiple HPV type infections were similarly balanced across the groups. Clinical Outcomes

Vaginal Microecological Changes Pre‐ and Post‐Intervention Across Groups

The overall vaginal microecological profiles were comparable among the three groups at baseline, with no statistically significant differences in any measured indicators. The majority of patients exhibited vaginal microecological imbalance (Table S1).

Following the intervention, greater changes in microecological indicators were observed in the E. faecium group compared to the other two groups. The proportion of participants with vaginal cleanliness grades I–II increased from 41.94% to 73.39%, and the rate of normal cleanliness in the E. faecium group was significantly higher than that in the regular follow‐up and blank control groups (73.39% vs. 45.83% vs. 33.88%). After E. faecium intervention, 80.65% of patients maintained a white blood cell count within 0–15/HPF, whereas the proportion of patients with elevated inflammation (> 15/HPF) remained relatively high in both the regular follow‐up and blank control groups (36.67% and 39.67%, respectively). The median Nugent score in the E. faecium group decreased from 4.92 at baseline to 2.0 post‐treatment, while the median AV score dropped from 4.03 pre‐treatment to 0.03 post‐treatment, demonstrating significantly greater magnitude of change compared to other groups (p < 0.05). The most pronounced intergroup difference after intervention was observed in the composition of dominant bacterial flora. The proportion of participants with Gram‐positive large bacilli as the dominant flora in the E. faecium group increased from 39.52% to 73.39%. In contrast, only modest increases were seen in the regular follow‐up group (from 45.83% to 55.83%) and negligible change in the blank control group (from 34.71% to 33.88%). This intergroup difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001), suggesting that E. faecium intervention may promote lactobacilli colonization.

Additionally, pH values decreased significantly in the E. faecium group, and the negative rate for hydrogen peroxide production increased from 6.45% to 69.35%, with both outcomes being significantly superior to those in the control groups (p < 0.001). No marked changes in these indicators were observed in the regular follow‐up or blank control groups, indicating that E. faecium may help restore the metabolic function of lactobacilli in the vaginal environment (Table S2).

Alpha and Beta Diversity Analysis

Alpha diversity analysis showed no statistically significant differences in the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices among the A1, B1, and C1 groups at baseline. During the follow‐up period, significant differences in the Chao1 and Shannon indices were observed between groups A2 and C2 (Figure 2). Beta diversity analysis revealed no notable differences in overall microbial community structure among the three groups before intervention. However, significant divergence in community structure was observed post‐intervention (Figure 3). Specifically, the community structure in group A2 differed significantly from those in groups B2 and C2 (Bray‐Curtis distance, ADONIS, p < 0.05), while no significant difference was detected between groups B2 and C2 (p > 0.05) (Figure 4). Intragroup analysis indicated a significant shift in microbial composition within the E. faecium group before and after intervention (A1 vs. A2, p < 0.05). In contrast, neither the regular follow‐up group nor the blank control group exhibited significant structural changes in microbiota between pre‐ and post‐intervention timepoints (Figure 5).

16S rRNA Microbiome Sequencing of Vaginal Secretions

A total of 24 participants were enrolled for 16S sequencing, comparing eight from the E. faecium group (five HPV‐cleared individuals), eight from the regular follow‐up group (two HPV‐cleared individuals), and eight from the blank control group (one HPV‐cleared individual). From each participant, one vaginal secretion sample was collected both before and during the follow‐up period, yielding 48 samples in total for analysis. According to the study design, samples were classified into the following groups: Group A (experimental group: A1 pre‐intervention, A2 at the study endpoint), Group B (regular follow‐up group: B1 pre‐intervention, B2 at endpoint), and Group C (blank control group: C1 at enrollment, C2 at endpoint).

Relative Abundance Analysis of Vaginal Microbiota Across Groups

Intra‐group and inter‐group analyses were performed to evaluate the composition of vaginal microbiota before and during the follow‐up period across the three groups. Across all subject groups, the top 15 most relatively abundant genera remained largely consistent over time, with 13 genera consistently present among the top 15 at all time points. Lactobacillus was the most abundant genus in all participant groups (Figure 6). However, prior to the intervention, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus was below 70% in all groups. Other genera, including Gardnerella, Atopobium, and Prevotella, showed relatively high abundances across groups before the intervention.

Group comparisons of the relative abundance of vaginal microbiota at the genus level before treatment. (A) the situation before the intervention and (B) the situation during the follow‐up period.

During the follow‐up period, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus increased significantly in the E. faecium group (from 54.01% to 75.65%), while the abundances of Atopobium, Prevotella, and Staphylococcus decreased markedly. LEfSe multi‐group comparative analysis confirmed that the changes in these four genera were statistically significant across the three groups. A modest increase in Lactobacillus abundance was also observed in the regular follow‐up group (from 36.81% to 46.32%). In contrast, the blank control group exhibited a pronounced decline in Lactobacillus abundance (from 65.74% to 33.03%). Concurrently, most pathogenic bacteria showed an increasing trend in the B2 and C2 groups.

Sneathia, a genus associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes [18], was present at low overall levels in both the experimental and regular follow‐up groups and decreased in both. However, two participants in the blank control group showed a marked increase in its abundance. LEfSe multi‐group comparative analysis indicated that this inter‐group difference was statistically significant, suggesting that this genus may be relatively sensitive to anti‐infective therapy (Figure 7).

Vaginal Microbiota Analysis

All enrolled patients were followed up to monitor their HPV infection status. Regardless of the intervention received, those HPV‐negative individuals were assigned to the clearance group (N, Negative), while those who remained positive for at least one HPV type were assigned to the persistent infection group (P, Positive). No significant differences in alpha diversity were observed between the two groups before the intervention. During the follow‐up period, however, statistically significant differences emerged in the Chao1 index, Shannon index, and Simpson indices between the clearance and persistent infection groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 8).

Analysis of relative abundance at the genus level showed that in the clearance group, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus increased from 49.74% to 93.78% (Figure 9). This was accompanied by a marked decrease in the abundances of Atopobium, Prevotella, Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium, Streptococcus, and Anaerococcus. LEfSe analysis indicated that the differences in these genera between the clearance and persistent infection groups were statistically significant. Following the intervention, the microbial composition differed substantially between the HPV clearance group and the persistent infection group. The relative abundance of Lactobacillus was significantly higher in the clearance group (93.78% vs. 29.96%), whereas genera such as Prevotella, Atopobium, Streptococcus, and Staphylococcus showed significantly lower abundances in the clearance group (p < 0.05).

Clearance of HPV

Significant differences in HPV infection clearance and improvement rates were observed among the three groups during the follow‐up period (p < 0.05). By the 12‐month follow‐up, the experimental group exhibited a significantly higher HPV clearance rate compared to the regular follow‐up and blank control groups (55.65% vs. 29.17% vs. 13.22%, p < 0.001). No cases of progression to HSIL were documented in any group during the 12‐month follow‐up period. While subgroup analysis comparing single‐type and multiple‐type HPV infections revealed no significant differences in clearance rates within Enterococcus faecium or the blank control groups. However, in the regular follow‐up group, patients with multiple HPV infections demonstrated a significantly higher clearance rate compared to those with a single HPV infection (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Binary Logistic Regression Analysis

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with HPV clearance [18, 19, 20]. Results showed that smoking (OR = 0.110, 95% CI 0.014–0.857, p = 0.035) and duration of persistent HPV infection (OR = 0.872, 95% CI 0.794–0.958, p = 0.004) were independent factors affecting HPV clearance. Smokers had a significantly lower clearance rate than non‐smokers; a longer duration of persistent HPV infection was associated with a lower clearance rate. Age, age at first intercourse, number of sexual partners, gravidity, parity, educational level, single vs. multiple HPV infection status, and presence of HPV 16/18 infection showed no significant association with HPV clearance (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

Recent studies have underscored the close relationship between the vaginal microecology and persistent HPV infection [21]. Although the precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood, an imbalance in the vaginal microbiota is regarded as a potential contributor to the development of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and cervical cancer [14, 22]. Lactobacilli play a key role in maintaining vaginal microecological homeostasis by producing lactic acid, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and bacteriocins, which suppress the growth and proliferation of pathogenic bacteria. Furthermore, they help preserve the integrity of the vaginal mucosal barrier, defend against viral and harmful bacterial invasion, and enhance local anti‐infective and anti‐tumor immune responses in the vaginal environment [23]. A Lactobacillus‐dominated vaginal microenvironment is thus more favorable for resisting HPV invasion or promoting its clearance [24, 25]. A decline in Lactobacillus abundance is associated with reduced resistance to HPV, thereby increasing susceptibility to infection. Our study demonstrated that the relative abundance of Lactobacillus in women with persistent HR‐HPV infection was below 70% at enrollment, significantly lower than that in healthy individuals. At the conclusion of the experiment, patients who tested negative for HPV showed a markedly higher relative abundance of Lactobacillus (93.78%) compared to the persistent infection group (29.96%). Concurrently, the relative abundances of genera such as Prevotella, Atopobium, Streptococcus, and Staphylococcus were significantly reduced, and vaginal microecological parameters tended to normalize [25, 26]. On the other hand, bacteria including Prevotella, Atopobium, and Staphylococcus may act synergistically to facilitate HPV colonization, while Streptococcus, Corynebacterium, and Anaerococcus may also play supportive roles in this process [27, 28].

We observed that in the regular follow‐up group, the HPV clearance rate was significantly higher in patients with multiple infections than in those with a single infection (55.56% vs. 13.33%, p < 0.001). However, in the binary logistic regression analysis, HPV single or multiple genotype infection was not an independent factor affecting HPV negativity. The association between multiple HPV infections and the risk of cervical lesion progression remains controversial. Some studies suggest that multiple HPV infections do not promote cervical lesion progression [29, 30], possibly because when one HPV type proliferates, others remain latent. A study on HPV infection among women in South Africa found no statistically significant difference in the detection rate of multiple HPV infections between cervical cancer patients and those with high‐grade cervical lesions [31]. A clinical study by Ni X et al. involving 2999 patients demonstrated that, compared with single‐type infection, multiple HPV infections significantly reduced the risk of cervical HSIL and were associated with a higher clearance rate [32]. In contrast, other evidence suggests that multiple infections may promote the occurrence and progression of cervical lesions [33]. Future studies should incorporate viral load and genotype‐specific interaction factors, which may help resolve this contradiction. Smoking (including second‐hand smoke exposure) has been associated with high‐risk HPV infection and HPV‐related pathogenesis, including cervical cancer [34]. Harmful substances and their metabolites in tobacco can suppress local cervical immunity, promote HPV‐driven cell transformation, and facilitate tumorigenesis. In this study, smoking was also an independent factor affecting HPV negativity (OR = 0.110, 95% CI 0.014–0.857, p = 0.035). Furthermore, we found that a longer duration of persistent HPV infection was associated with a lower rate of HPV negativity (OR = 0.872, 95% CI 0.794–0.958, p = 0.004), suggesting that the duration of persistent infection is an independent risk factor for HPV negativity. This finding is consistent with previous studies [35, 36]. Possible mechanisms include the following: With prolonged infection, HPV DNA is more likely to integrate into the host genome [37], leading to sustained expression of viral proteins E6/E7, which in turn suppresses the host immune response [38]; long‐term viral infection may induce tolerance in the local immune microenvironment, reducing the body's ability to clear the virus. Moreover, a longer duration of persistent infection is associated with an increased risk of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) or cervical precancerous lesions. The reduced stability of epithelial cells in the lesioned area further hinders natural viral clearance. Therefore, in clinical practice, patients with HPV persistent infection lasting more than 12 months should receive enhanced follow‐up and intervention. Numerous studies consistently agree that HPV16 is the most persistent HPV genotype. Multiple large cohort studies have shown that the clearance rate of HPV16 is lower than that of other HPV genotypes [39, 40]. The persistence of HPV18 lies between that of HPV16 and other high‐risk types, with considerable variation across regions and populations [41]. However, in large‐sample screening studies conducted in some regions of China, HPV16/18 have shown ‘faster clearance’. In a study by Yang et al. involving a large sample population in Guangzhou and Southern China, HPV16 cleared faster than other genotypes [42]. Another study in southern China reported that the clearance time for HPV16/18 was shorter than that for other high‐risk types [43]. This may be due to more aggressive treatment and follow‐up for individuals with HPV16/18 infection, a phenomenon resulting from differences in management strategies, rather than indicating a lower intrinsic oncogenic potential. In our study, the presence of HPV16/18 infection showed no significant association with HPV clearance (p = 0.689). Future in‐depth investigations may be needed in cohort studies with larger sample sizes.

Our experiments show that the most significant post‐intervention differences among the three groups were shifts in the dominant flora and corresponding changes in microecological indices. In the experimental group, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus increased significantly following Enterococcus faecium intervention (from 54.01% to 75.65%), whereas the relative abundances of BV‐associated pathogens, including Atopobium, Prevotella, and Staphylococcus, decreased markedly. In contrast, minimal changes were observed in the control groups before and after intervention (p < 0.001). These findings suggest that the application of E. faecium preparation may promote the colonization and functional recovery of lactobacilli. Specially, E. faecium treatment effectively modulated the structure of the vaginal microbial community, while anti‐infective therapy alone showed no superior regulatory effect compared to the natural course observed in the blank control group. This indicates that anti‐infective therapy alone may be inadequate for restoring a healthy vaginal microecology. The timely supplementation of E. faecium could thus address the limitations of conventional antibiotic therapy.

Ultimately, the resultant vaginal microenvironment in the experimental group was more conducive to Lactobacillus growth, allowing it to become the dominant flora and sustain a stable state. This likely contributed to the observed shortening of disease duration, reduced inflammatory recurrence, and long‐term maintenance of microecological homeostasis.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as this was a non‐randomized and non‐blinded study without a placebo control, causal relationships cannot be established. Second, no additional 12‐month follow‐up was conducted after the first documented HPV negativity to confirm the durability of viral clearance; moreover, CIN progression and long‐term clinical outcomes were not included as study endpoints. While LEfSe was chosen for its ability to capture major biological signals and intuitive visualization, it does not fully account for the compositional nature of microbiome data. At the mechanistic level, strain‐level colonization of E. faecium was not verified, and no longitudinal tracking was performed. Direct evidence regarding potential metabolic interactions between E. faecium and Lactobacillus is lacking. Functional parameters, including lactate levels, immune mediator expression, and epithelial barrier responses, were discussed theoretically but were not directly measured. In addition, viral load, E6/E7 transcriptional activity, viral integration status, and local mucosal immune profiling were not systematically evaluated. Furthermore, the study was a clinical exploratory study; actual probiotic colonization adherence was not confirmed. The relatively small sample size for 16S rRNA sequencing may have limited the statistical power of microbiome analyses. Given that all study groups received anti‐infective treatment, the independent effect of E. faecium cannot be fully isolated. Differences in follow‐up intensity among participants may have introduced potential follow‐up bias and detection bias, which therefore cannot be excluded. Future investigations employing larger sample sizes, randomized placebo‐controlled designs, and integrated virological and mucosal immunological assessments are warranted to more rigorously validate and elucidate the clinical and microbiome‐associated findings observed in this study.
