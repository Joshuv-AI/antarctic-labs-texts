The role of early intervention with upper limb rehabilitation robots in upper limb functional reconstruction and improving sarcopenia-related indicators in stroke patients
Authors: Aoying Niu, Yangyu Tao, Xiaoguang Wang, Jing Dong, Zijiao Xue, Fengyu Cai, Xinhong Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke causes muscle atrophy and functional decline, affecting quality of life. Exercise training reduces post-stroke muscle loss. This study explores if rehabilitation robots enhance upper-limb function and muscle mass in stroke patients. We selected 80 patients who visited the North China University of Science and Technology Affiliated Hospital from June 2024 to April 2025 for a randomized controlled trial. After excluding missing values, the patients were divided into two groups: the control group (n = 38) and the intervention group (n = 39). The control group received 4 weeks of conventional rehabilitation therapy, while the intervention group underwent upper-limb robot-assisted training in addition to the conventional therapy. The primary outcomes comprised the Fugl–Meyer Assessment of the Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) score, handgrip strength (HG), and the appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI). After 4 weeks of treatment, significant differences were observed in the FMA-UE scores, HG, and ASMI between the two groups (P < 0.05). The intervention group showed superior outcomes compared to the control group, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). This study provides evidence that robot-assisted training improves upper-limb motor function and enhances sarcopenia-related indicators—specifically muscle mass and muscle strength—in acute stroke patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43814-z.

Introduction

Stroke, also known as cerebrovascular accident (CVA), is a neurological disorder caused by ischemic or hemorrhagic brain injury following cerebrovascular pathology. Its main pathophysiological subtypes are cerebral infarction and cerebral hemorrhage.

Stroke is a leading cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide. The Global Burden of Disease Study reported that stroke-related mortality rose from 5.29 million to 6.17 million between 2007 and 20171. Upper-limb dysfunction affects approximately 73–88% of first-time stroke survivors and 55%–75% of those with chronic stroke2,3. This disparity reflects the greater neural complexity of upper-limb control, which recovers more slowly than lower-limb function; only a minority of patients achieve functional recovery within six months4. Post-stroke upper-limb impairment manifests as weakness, sensory loss, restricted range of motion, and diminished fine motor control, substantially compromising quality of life and social engagement. These deficits result in physical disability, psychological distress, and social isolation, placing substantial burden on patients and caregivers5,6.

Sarcopenia, characterized by progressive and generalized loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function, is increasingly recognized as a distinct geriatric syndrome7. Stroke patients are at elevated risk of malnutrition and muscle weakness due to dysphagia, limb paresis, and gastrointestinal dysfunction8. The prevalence of sarcopenia is substantially higher in stroke patients than in healthy controls9. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that sarcopenia affects 8.5–68.4% of stroke patients (pooled prevalence: 33.5%), with higher rates observed in females10. Sarcopenia leads to a significant decline in muscle mass and strength, which further impairs the upper-limb motor function of stroke patients, resulting in decreased grip strength, hand–eye coordination deficits, and limitations in activities of daily living. Additionally, it elevates fall risk, metabolic morbidity, and caregiver burden11,12. Research demonstrates that motor unit numbers in the hemiplegic limb decline rapidly within 4 h of cerebral infarction onset. Notably, the unaffected side also exhibits progressive muscle atrophy and weakness between 3 weeks and 6 months post-onset13,14. Current conventional rehabilitation for post-stroke motor impairment comprises neuromuscular electrical stimulation, neurodevelopmental treatment15, exercise therapy, and occupational therapy16. However, evidence-based exercise guidelines for stroke patients with comorbid sarcopenia remain lacking. This study examines the effects of upper-limb rehabilitation robotics on motor function and muscle mass in early-stage stroke patients, aiming to inform clinical practice.

Methodology

Study design

This study is a prospective, randomized controlled trial. After confirming that patients voluntarily participated and met all inclusion criteria, they were enrolled in the study. Prior to the start of the experiment, block randomization was performed using R software (version 4.3.0) to ensure baseline balance between groups. The block size was randomly determined to enhance allocation concealment. The randomization sequence was enclosed in a series of opaque, sealed envelopes, each containing an assignment card indicating the group (control group [CG] or intervention group [IG]). After signing the informed consent form, patients were sequentially assigned by the study coordinator to either the control or intervention group according to the card. The intervention period was 4 weeks for both groups. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of patients and therapists was not possible. However, all outcome assessors were unaware of the group assignments to minimize assessment bias.

This study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all study procedures complied with the ethical guidelines of the World Medical Association. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of North China University of Science and Technology (NO. 20231011054) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration Number: ChiCTR2300077859) on 22/11/2023. Before the study began, all potential participants were given comprehensive details about the study’s goals, methods, possible risks, anticipated benefits, and other pertinent information. Participants voluntarily signed written informed consent after fully understanding their rights and responsibilities.

Study object

This research enrolled stroke patients who were hospitalized in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at the Affiliated Hospital of North China University of Science and Technology between June 2024 and April 2025.

Patients must meet the inclusion criteria outlined in the "Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Stroke" from the Fourth National Cerebrovascular Disease Academic Conference. Intervention protocols

In this study, both the control and intervention groups received standard medical treatment to manage blood pressure, blood glucose levels, and other relevant conditions. Both groups also participated in conventional rehabilitation training. The intervention group received additional upper-limb robot-assisted training on top of the standard rehabilitation. Based on each patient’s specific condition, we designed personalized training programs using the rehabilitation robot, which included tailored exercise intensity, frequency, and patterns, to maximize the rehabilitation outcomes. The rehabilitation program17 was implemented by a multidisciplinary team comprising physicians, nurses, occupational therapists, physical therapists, speech therapists and registered dietitians, all of whom worked together to deliver comprehensive rehabilitation services to the patients. These approaches18 are recommended for managing stroke-related sarcopeni.

This study was conducted by a multidisciplinary team, including registered dietitians who performed nutritional risk screening and dietary counseling for all participants. Nutritional risk was assessed within 24 h of enrollment using the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002). Participants with NRS-2002 scores ≥ 3 or evident malnutrition received oral nutritional supplements prescribed by dietitians until nutritional risk resolved or study completion. Height and weight were measured at baseline and 4 weeks post-intervention to calculate body mass index; between-group balance was assessed at baseline to control for nutritional confounding.

Control group

Training Content: Physical agent therapy (medium-frequency pulse electrotherapy) for 30 min per session; acupuncture on the affected limb for 30 min per session; exercise therapy for 40 min per session, including guidance on proper limb positioning, activities of daily living training, joint range-of-motion exercises, manual hand function training, and balance training; occupational therapy for 40 min per session, including training for hand function recovery, such as grasping, extending, and opposition exercises. Training frequency: 140 min per session, 1 set per day, 5 days per week, for a continuous period of 4 weeks.

Intervention group

Training Content: Rehabilitation robot-assisted training was added to the regimen of the control group. Before the first training session, therapists assessed the patients’ physical condition, muscle strength, muscle tone, and range of joint motion, and set personalized training intensity and goals. Therapists chose three interactive modules from the robot system and selected the difficulty level according to the participants’ upper-limb function, with each module lasting 5 min. To standardize daily treatment duration across groups and isolate the specific effects of robotic training, the intervention group received 125 min of conventional rehabilitation. Specifically, occupational therapy was reduced from 40 to 25 min relative to the 140-min conventional protocol administered to the control group, while all other treatment components remained unchanged.

This study utilized an end-effector upper-limb robot (EM-BURT03-01A Upper-Limb Rehabilitation Training System). The Burt robot is the first domestically developed end-effector-driven three-dimensional upper-limb rehabilitation robot. It features a three-degree-of-freedom robotic arm that conforms to human biomechanics, enabling full-cycle, three-dimensional combined movements of the shoulder and elbow joints for patients. It ensures the safety of patient training and provides an exceptional interactive experience. The system can provide weight-reduction assistance of 0.0–4.0 kg for the patient’s forearm. The training modes include passive, assisted, active, and resistance modes.

Participants sit upright in front of a computer screen with their affected upper limb secured to the robotic training handle. Those with weaker strength use elastic bandages for fixation. The training mode (passive, assisted, active, or resistance) is selected based on the patient’s upper-limb muscle strength: passive mode for grade 0 strength, assisted mode for grades 1–2, with gradually reduced assistance as the patient improves, active mode for grade 3, and adjustable resistance for grades 4–5, depending on the patient’s condition. A therapist stands by to protect and monitor the participant, minimizing compensatory movements. Training should be immediately halted if the patient experiences discomfort or a sudden increase in muscle tone. Prior to each morning session, a blinded assessor independent of treatment delivery conducted standardized evaluations using the robot’s integrated system. Outcome measures included maximal workspace volume of the affected limb in three-dimensional space, peak isometric strength (N·m), and active engagement ratio. Therapists determined parameter adjustments based on evaluation reports and patient-reported fatigue (Borg Scale), guided by predefined criteria for quantitative progression. All modifications followed a bidirectional, stratified adjustment algorithm (Table 1). Assessment

The primary outcome measures include

FMA-UE19: The scale consists of 33 items and is used to assess the motor function of both the proximal and distal parts of the patient’s upper limb. Each item is scored on a 3-point scale from 0 to 2: 0 indicates an inability to perform the movement, while 2 indicates the ability to perform the movement fully and easily. The total score of the scale is 66 points, with higher scores indicating better recovery of upper limb motor function.

Assessment of Grip Strength in the Affected Hand (Hand Grip, HG): Grip strength is an important indicator for assessing upper-limb muscle strength and is closely related to the patient’s ability to perform daily activities20. In this study, HG was measured using the Xiangshan CAMRY EH101 electronic dynamometer (Xiangshan Holdings Co., Ltd., Zhongshan, China). This device features a measurement range from 0 to 180 kg, a minimum scale division of 0.1 kg, and an accuracy of ± 3%. According to the 2019 criteria of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS)21, grip strength less than 28 kg in males or less than 18 kg in females is considered abnormal. This standard is widely used in clinical practice and research and effectively reflects the decline in upper-limb muscle strength. For the test, the patient sits with both arms hanging naturally, elbows extended, and grips the dynamometer with one hand without swinging or touching any part of the body. The patient holds the grip for 1 s, rests for 1–2 min, and repeats the test three times, with the maximum value recorded.

Measurement of Muscle Mass(Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass Index ,ASMI): In this study, the InBody H20B bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) was used to measure appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) and to calculate the Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass Index (ASMI). The calculation for ASMI is as follows: ASMI = ASM (kg) / height2 (m2). ASMI is commonly used to assess sarcopenia and muscle function status. According to the 2019 consensus of the AWGS21, an ASMI < 7.0 kg/m2 in males and < 5.7 kg/m2 in females indicates muscle mass loss.

Secondary outcome measures include

Muscle Thickness (MT)22: In this study, the Supersonic Aixplorer ultrasound diagnostic system (Supersonic Imagine, Aix-en-Provence, France) was used for measurement, equipped with an SL15-4 high-frequency linear array probe (frequency range: 4–15 MHz) and utilizing the 2D grayscale ultrasound combined with Shear Wave Elastography (SWE) mode. During the examination, the patient was placed in the supine position, and the measurement site was selected at the upper one-third midpoint of the biceps brachii. Care was taken to avoid applying excessive pressure on the muscle tissue during the procedure. Each biceps brachii was measured three times, with images saved for each measurement. The maximum value from the three measurements was taken as the final result. All measurements were conducted collaboratively by two experienced ultrasound technicians.

Activities of Daily Living (ADL):The Modified Barthel Index (MBI)23 was used for assessment. This scale comprises 10 items, including dressing, eating, bathing, bowel and bladder control, and walking. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest), with higher scores indicating greater independence. The total score for the MBI is 100, and a higher score indicates stronger self-care ability in patients.

Statistical analysis

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the anticipated changes in the Fugl-Meyer Assessment of the Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) score. Based on pilot experiments and previous research, it was expected that the FMA-UE score in the robot-assisted training (RAT) intervention group would be 5 points higher than that in the control group, with a standard deviation of 10 points. To detect this difference, the significance level was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed test), with a statistical power (1-β) of 80%. Using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7), it was determined that 36 participants were needed per group. Considering an approximate dropout rate of 10%, 40 participants were ultimately included in each group, bringing the total sample size to 80.

Statistical approach

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), and differences between the two groups were assessed using independent samples t-tests. For within-group comparisons before and after treatment, paired samples t-tests were used. For continuous data that were not normally distributed, results were presented as the median (M) with the first and third quartiles (Q1, Q3), and group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were displayed as counts and percentages (%) and analyzed using the chi-square test. The significance level for all statistical tests was set at P < 0.05. To ensure the objectivity and impartiality of the study, all statistical analyses were performed by researchers who were not involved in the allocation of participants.

Results

Participant recruitment, allocation, and compliance with intervention

This study enrolled 108 eligible patients (66 males and 42 females) hospitalized in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine from June 2024 to April 2025. Recruitment was terminated once the predetermined sample size (n = 80, 55 males and 25 females) was reached. A total of 28 patients did not participate in the study for the following reasons: 19 refused to sign the informed consent form, 3 were transferred to the ICU due to worsening conditions, and 6 were transferred to other medical institutions for treatment needs. Ultimately, 80 patients completed baseline assessments and were randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group, with 40 patients in each. No serious adverse events directly related to the intervention were reported during the study. However, one participant in the intervention group experienced a worsening of their condition, leading to their transfer to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU). This participant was unable to continue with the rehabilitation program. The event was assessed by the study team and determined to be unrelated to the intervention. In the control group, two patients terminated training due to early discharge (likely reasons included personal preference and improved health). These participants were excluded from the final analysis. Discussion

In this study, we conducted a randomized controlled trial to examine whether robot-assisted therapy has favorable outcomes for upper-limb functional recovery and sarcopenia-related indicators in acute-phase stroke patients. According to the diagnostic framework of the AWGS 201921, sarcopenia is defined by the coexistence of low muscle mass, low muscle strength, and/or low physical performance. However, the present study did not include physical performance measures (e.g., gait speed, five-times-sit-to-stand test) due to the impaired ambulatory function in acute-phase stroke patients. Therefore, a complete diagnosis of sarcopenia according to the full AWGS 2019 criteria was not feasible in this study. Instead, we focused on two core components of sarcopenia—muscle mass and muscle strength—which are also established risk factors for the development of sarcopenia. Muscle mass was assessed using ASMI, and muscle strength was assessed via handgrip strength, both measured at baseline and at 4 weeks. In the intervention group, ASMI increased from 7.534 ± 1.427 kg/m2 at baseline to 10.645 ± 2.335 kg/m2 at 4 weeks (t = − 13.146, P < 0.001). In the control group, ASMI increased from 7.841 ± 1.833 kg/m2 to 8.616 ± 1.714 kg/m2 (t = − 5.189, P < 0.001). In the intervention group, HG improved from 4.956 ± 3.334 kg at baseline to 9.879 ± 4.610 kg at 4 weeks (t = − 10.987, P < 0.001). In the control group, handgrip strength increased from 5.132 ± 3.314 kg to 7.313 ± 4.167 kg (t = − 6.825, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that robot-assisted training may improve key sarcopenia risk factors—specifically muscle mass and muscle strength—in acute stroke patients, and may thereby help reduce the risk of developing sarcopenia in this population. Further studies incorporating full physical performance assessments are warranted to confirm whether these benefits translate into a reduced incidence of confirmed sarcopenia.

In terms of motor function, there was no significant difference in the baseline FMA-UE scores between the two groups of participants (t = − 0.266, P > 0.05). After four weeks of intervention, the FMA-UE scores in both groups significantly improved from baseline (tIG = − 12.927, PIG < 0.001; tCG = − 7.796, PCG < 0.001). The intervention group showed a significantly greater improvement compared to the control group (F = 0.914, P < 0.05), suggesting that robot-assisted training may be more effective in promoting upper-limb functional recovery. This outcome may be attributed to the high-intensity, repetitive task training provided by the robotic system. By precisely controlling the movement trajectory and resistance, it helps patients to re-establish the correct movement patterns24. Dehem et al.[25,26] have shown that early upper-limb robot-assisted training after stroke can improve motor function and activities of daily living, which is similar to the findings of this study. Upper-limb motor dysfunction after stroke is closely related to decreased cortical excitability and abnormal neural innervation on the affected side27. Studies have shown that repeated voluntary movement training of the paralyzed limb can expand the cortical control area and enhance neural conduction efficiency through motor relearning28. This may involve synaptic reorganization in the motor cortex and reconstruction of neural networks. Systematic reviews of robot-assisted arm training after stroke29,30 have also demonstrated that this approach can improve upper limb function, muscle strength, and activities of daily living.

Rehabilitation therapy helps to delay the loss of motor neurons, prevents disuse atrophy of paralyzed muscles, and is beneficial for restoring skeletal muscle mass and strength. Exercise prescriptions are based on the principle of progressive overload and are adjusted as needed31. In this study, robot-assisted training enabled patients to gradually progress from passive training to assisted, active, and resistance training, reflecting the principle of progressive overload. HG and the ASMI are important indicators for assessing muscle quality and physical function and are closely related to the concept of sarcopenia. Grip strength of the affected side is a direct reflection of upper limb muscle strength and accurately indicates the contraction capacity of the muscles in the affected hand and forearm. Enhanced grip strength facilitates fine motor activities such as dressing, eating, and grasping objects, thereby significantly improving the patient’s self-care ability. Moreover, maintaining muscle mass helps to balance the tension of flexor and extensor muscles, which can alleviate spasticity following a stroke. Robot-assisted training, through precise and repetitive movement guidance, reinforces correct movement patterns and induces neuroplasticity32, thereby improving muscle strength and motor coordination. In this study, significant changes in grip strength on the affected side (F = 0.148, P < 0.05) and ASMI (F = 3.619, P < 0.001) also confirmed the positive effects of robot-assisted training on neural remodeling.Previous studies have shown that long-term robot-assisted training can enhance muscle activation33,34 and increase the cross-sectional area of muscle fibers35, which may be related to the repetitive stretching motions provided by rehabilitation robots. Additionally, robot-assisted resistance training can significantly activate the mTOR signaling pathway, promote protein synthesis, and thereby achieve muscle hypertrophy36,37, slowing down muscle atrophy. This finding is consistent with the results of MT(F = 4.182, P < 0.05) changes observed in this study.

Stroke-related sarcopenia (SRS) is clinically defined as the rapid decline in muscle mass, changes in muscle structure, asymmetric functional alterations in both limbs, and age-independent muscle atrophy that occur after a stroke9. It has been reported that 53% of stroke patients experience sarcopenia38,39, a prevalence higher than that in the general elderly population, and it has a negative impact on rehabilitation outcomes. Resistance training and endurance exercises have been proven to be effective in improving sarcopenia40,41. However, stroke patients, with their reduced muscle strength, physical function, and balance capabilities, often find it difficult to engage in traditional resistance training (such as dumbbells, push-ups, and machine-based exercises). Robot-assisted training dynamically adjusts resistance to provide safe and precise resistance stimulation and supports high-repetition movements. This enhances mitochondrial activity and energy metabolism in muscles, thereby delaying muscle atrophy42,43. Studies have shown that resistance training effectively improves upper- and lower-limb function in older adults and specific patient groups (such as stroke and spinal cord injury patients)44,45, reduces the incidence of sarcopenia, and maximizes functional recovery outcomes.

The goal of stroke rehabilitation is not only to improve motor function but also to help stroke patients regain independence and the ability to perform activities of daily living (ADL). In this study, we selected daily function as one of the outcome measures, and the results showed that robot-assisted training is effective in improving ADL in these patients, which may be related to the improvement in motor function46. Moreover, the robot-assisted upper-limb training system includes a rich library of games and virtual training scenarios, providing patients with progressive and highly repetitive passive and active movement training. Compared to traditional training, this approach not only increases the fun of training but also enhances patient motivation, thereby encouraging patients to improve their self-care abilities47,48.This is consistent with the results of this study, in which the intervention group had a significantly higher MBI score than the control group at the end of treatment (F = 4.041, P < 0.05). Rehabilitation robots enhance the appeal and interactivity of the rehabilitation process, thereby increasing patient engagement and adherence, and more effectively improving patients’ motor abilities and quality of life.

This study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted in the rehabilitation department of a comprehensive tertiary hospital in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, this study did not include physical function measures such as gait speed or five-time chair stand tests; therefore, a complete sarcopenia diagnosis according to AWGS 2019 criteria was not feasible. Future studies should incorporate comprehensive physical function assessments when participants’ functional status permits, to fully evaluate the effects of upper-limb rehabilitation robotics on sarcopenia outcomes. Additionally, the relatively short intervention duration (4 weeks) and follow-up period limit the ability to verify the generalizability of the results. Further studies with longer intervention duration and follow-up periods are warranted to confirm whether these benefits translate into a reduced incidence of confirmed sarcopenia.
