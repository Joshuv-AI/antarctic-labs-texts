Long-term outcomes of hydroxychloroquine dose reduction in Puerto Ricans with systemic lupus erythematosus: 6-year follow-up
Authors: Kamille Ruiz, Lilliana Serrano-Arroyo, Valeria Valentín-Rosa, Grecia Torres-Borges, Luis M Vilá
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objectiveTo reduce the risk of hydroxychloroquine (HCQ)-induced retinopathy, the American Academy of Ophthalmology recommends a maximum HCQ dose of ≤5.0 mg/kg/day. Previously, we reported that reducing HCQ to this threshold did not affect clinical outcomes in Puerto Rican patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) after 2 years. To evaluate longer term effects, we extended follow-up to 6 years post-HCQ dose adjustment.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study on a cohort of Puerto Rican SLE patients whose HCQ doses were adjusted to ≤5.0 mg/kg/day. Disease activity (per-Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index), lupus exacerbations, hospitalisations, disease damage (per SLICC/ACR Damage Index) and pharmacologic treatments were assessed. Outcomes during the 4-year extension were compared with preadjustment and initial 2-year postadjustment periods. A control group of SLE patients who did not require HCQ dose adjustment was also included.ResultsAmong the 304 patients included in the study, 60 required HCQ dose adjustment, whereas 244 patients did not require modification of their HCQ regimen and served as controls. During the 4-year extension period, a small but statistically significant increase in disease damage was observed in patients who underwent HCQ dose adjustment compared with earlier follow-up periods. Tacrolimus exposure significantly increased, whereas other immunosuppressive therapies remained stable. No increase in disease activity, lupus exacerbations or hospitalisations was observed. Clinical features were comparable between patients who underwent HCQ dose adjustment and those who did not, although patients in the adjusted-dose group had lower weight-based daily HCQ dosing and were less likely to develop HCQ-induced retinopathy.ConclusionsIn this cohort, reducing HCQ to ≤5.0 mg/kg/day was associated with a slight increase in long-term disease damage. No adverse outcomes on disease activity, exacerbations or hospitalisations were observed, supporting the long-term safety of HCQ dose adjustment in Puerto Rican SLE patients.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus; Outcome Assessment, Health Care; Antirheumatic Agents

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In a 6-year follow-up of Puerto Rican patients with SLE, reducing HCQ doses to ≤5.0 mg/kg/day was associated with a modest but significant increase in long-term disease damage, while disease activity, lupus exacerbations, hospitalisations and corticosteroid exposure either decreased or remained stable. When patients who underwent HCQ dose adjustment were compared with those who did not require adjustment, both groups received less than 5 mg/kg/day of HCQ; however, patients with dose adjustment had a lower mean weight-based daily HCQ dose and a lower frequency of HCQ-induced retinopathy. This association is likely attributable to differences in HCQ exposure rather than a causal protective effect of dose adjustment.

Introduction

Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) plays a critical role in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) treatment due to its ability to reduce flares, prevent organ damage and improve survival.1 However, long-term use can lead to HCQ-induced retinopathy, with risk increasing over time. To minimise this risk, the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) updated its guidelines in 2016 to recommend a dose of ≤5.0 mg/kg/day based on actual body weight.2 Previously, we evaluated the clinical impact of implementing the AAO guidelines in Puerto Rican patients with SLE and found no major changes in disease activity or damage over a 2-year period.3 However, the therapeutic effect of HCQ may persist for a few years after dose modification, potentially limiting the interpretation of short-term studies. For this reason, we extended our observation to 6 years to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the long-term clinical impact of HCQ dose reduction. We also included a control group of SLE patients who did not require HCQ dose adjustment.

Despite the well-established clinical benefits of HCQ, debate persists regarding its optimal dosing, particularly how to balance retinal safety with disease control, and whether current weight-based recommendations may result in underdosing for some patients.4 Our study addresses this gap by examining extended outcomes after HCQ dose adjustment, offering valuable insights into the safety and effectiveness of guideline-based dosing in a population with long-standing SLE.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective study in a cohort of SLE patients receiving care at the University of Puerto Rico Medical Sciences Campus and a private rheumatology practice in San Juan, Puerto Rico. The study included patients whose HCQ dose was adjusted to ≤5.0 mg/kg/day based on actual body weight, in accordance with the 2016 AAO guidelines, and who had at least 6 years of follow-up after the adjustment. Eligible participants who met the 1997 revised American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria for SLE5 were ≥21 years old, of Puerto Rican descent (self and four grandparents) and had stable HCQ use for at least 2 years prior to dose modification. The baseline visit was defined as the first encounter when the HCQ dose adjustment was implemented. Follow-up assessments occurred every 6 months for 2 years before and 6 years after the adjustment. As a control group, we included SLE patients who did not require HCQ dose adjustment and therefore continued on their usual dose. To be eligible, these patients must have received HCQ treatment for at least 2 years. Exclusion criteria included patients who had never received HCQ and those with less than 2 years of HCQ exposure. The study protocol was approved by the University of Puerto Rico Medical Sciences Campus Institutional Review Board.

Variables

Demographic factors (age, sex, disease duration), cumulative SLE manifestations, disease activity, damage accrual, selected comorbidities and pharmacologic treatments were recorded. Disease activity was assessed using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI),6 and cumulative damage using the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/ACR Damage Index (SDI).7 Outcome measures included SLEDAI scores, disease flares, hospitalisations (overall and SLE-related), SDI scores, corticosteroid exposure and dosing, and use of additional immunosuppressive therapies. A lupus flare was defined as an increase in disease activity, evidenced by the new onset or worsening of clinical manifestations attributable to SLE, which required therapeutic escalation, including initiation or dose escalation of corticosteroids, or the addition or intensification of immunosuppressive therapy. The medical records of SLE patients who were hospitalised during the study period were reviewed to determine whether each hospitalisation was attributable to a disease exacerbation. Mortality was also recorded.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of the research.

Statistical analysis

SLEDAI scores, flares, hospitalisations, SDI scores, corticosteroid exposure, prednisone dose and other immunosuppressive drug use were compared before and after HCQ adjustment. Data were grouped into three periods: 2 years preadjustment, 2 years postadjustment and a 4-year extension (total 6 years postadjustment). Paired analyses were performed using the McNemar’s test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for continuous variables.

Demographic, cumulative SLE manifestations, disease activity, disease damage, comorbidities and pharmacologic treatment at last clinical visit were compared between patients who required HCQ dose adjustment and those who remained on their usual dose. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Variables that were significant in the bivariate analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model adjusting for sex, age and disease duration.

A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analysed using SPSS Statistics, V.31 (IBM, Armonk, New York).

Results

From the overall cohort of 412 patients with SLE, 108 patients did not meet eligibility criteria and were therefore excluded from the analysis. Among the 304 included in the study, 60 required HCQ dose adjustment and 244 SLE patients did not (since they were already on less than 5 mg/kg/day of HCQ) and served as controls. There were no patients who met the criteria for dose adjustment and yet remained on their prior dose. All patients from the original study were retained in follow-up, and none developed HCQ-related adverse events, including drug-induced retinopathy. This subgroup was followed for 6 years after dose adjustment. At the last study visit, the mean (SD) age was 49.8 (15.1) years, and the mean (SD) disease duration was 19.5 (9.3) years. All patients were women. The average HCQ dose across the study period was approximately 242 mg/day.

During the 4-year extension period, a slight but statistically significant increase in disease damage was observed compared with both the preadjustment and initial 2-year postadjustment periods. Nevertheless, mean (SD) SDI scores remained relatively low: 0.84 (1.28), 0.98 (1.42) and 1.23 (1.53), respectively (table 1). A significantly lower proportion of SLE patients experienced disease flares during the 2-year period after HCQ dose adjustment (5.0% vs 21.6%, p=0.006) and the 4-year extension period (6.7% vs 21.6%, p=0.035) compared with the preadjustment period. A higher proportion of patients received tacrolimus during the extension period compared with the preadjustment period (13.3% vs 1.7%, p=0.016). In contrast, a significant reduction in mean daily prednisone dose was observed when comparing the preadjustment period with both the 2-year postadjustment period (7.9 mg/day vs 4.8 mg/day, p=0.047) and the 4-year extension period (7.9 mg/day vs 4.0 mg/day, p=0.002). No significant changes were noted in the use of other immunosuppressive therapies. Similarly, no increases in disease activity or hospitalisations were observed during the extension period. No deaths occurred throughout the study.

Next, demographic characteristics, cumulative SLE manifestations, disease activity, damage accrual, comorbidities and pharmacologic treatments at the last clinical visit were compared between patients who required dose adjustment and those who did not. In total, 244 SLE patients did not require HCQ dose adjustment and therefore continued on their usual dose. In the bivariate analysis, patients who required HCQ dose adjustment were more likely to be female, older, have a longer disease duration and show a significantly higher frequency of arthritis (80.0% vs 64.3%, p=0.02), leucopenia (61.7% vs 41.0%, p=0.004), lymphopenia (86.7% vs 68.9%, p=0.006) and a longer mean (SD) duration of HCQ treatment (16.3 (15.6) vs 12.7 (14.8) years; p=0.002) (table 2). They also had a lower frequency of overweight/obesity (20.0% vs 68.0, p<0.001) and HCQ-induced retinopathy (0.0% vs 16.8%, p<0.001) as well as a lower mean (SD) daily HCQ dose (242.4 (53.2) vs 336.9 (88.2) mg/day; p<0.001) and mean weight-based dosing (4.1 (0.9) vs 4.6 (1.3) mg/kg/day; p=0.007). No significant differences were observed in disease activity, damage accrual or lupus pharmacologic treatments between the groups. In the multivariate analysis adjusted for sex, age and disease duration, overweight/obesity, HCQ daily dose and weight-based HCQ dosing remained significantly associated with HCQ dose adjustment, whereas arthritis, leucopenia, lymphopenia and HCQ treatment duration did not (table 3). HCQ-induced retinopathy could not be included in the multivariable model because no patients in the HCQ dose-adjustment group presented with retinopathy.

Discussion

In this 4-year extension study of 60 Puerto Rican patients with SLE, we assessed the long-term impact of adjusting HCQ doses to ≤5.0 mg/kg/day, followed for a total of 6 years after adjustment. Although absolute SDI scores remained low, a modest but statistically significant increase in damage accrual was observed. Tacrolimus, commonly prescribed for lupus nephritis, was used more frequently during follow-up. However, when compared with patients who remained on the same HCQ dose, no significant differences in tacrolimus exposure were observed between the groups. By contrast, prednisone doses remained consistently below 5 mg/day in most patients during the final 4 years, supporting sustained disease control with reduced corticosteroid exposure. Disease activity was stable, with less disease flares and no significant changes in hospitalisations, suggesting that overall, HCQ dose adjustment did not compromise long-term disease control. Moreover, no significant changes were observed in disease activity, disease damage or pharmacologic treatment between patients who had HCQ dose adjustment and those who did not, except that patients who remained on the same dose had a higher frequency of HCQ-induced retinopathy.

The modest but statistically significant increase in damage observed in our patients is consistent with the natural accumulation of irreversible damage in long-standing SLE, rather than to HCQ dose reduction. The absence of parallel increases in disease activity, lupus exacerbations or immunosuppressive drug exposure supports this interpretation. Moreover, no significant differences were observed in damage accrual at the last study visit between patients who required HCQ dose adjustment and those who did not and remained on the same dose. Similar patterns of progressive damage, despite low disease activity, have been described in other cohorts.8 9 In the multiethnic British study by Segura et al, 77% of patients developed damage, with SDI scores rising steadily over decades.8 Likewise, in the post hoc analysis by Bruce et al, belimumab slowed but did not eliminate SDI progression when compared with standard therapy.9

Our findings are consistent with the Lupus-Cruces cohort, where patients maintained clinical remission for 5 years on low-dose HCQ (~3 mg/kg/day) without an increase in lupus flares.10 In contrast, other studies have reported adverse clinical outcomes following HCQ dose reduction. For example, a recent case-crossover study associated lower HCQ doses (≤5 mg/kg/day) with an increased risk of hospitalisation for active SLE.11 However, that design focused on short-term fluctuations in HCQ exposure by comparing each patient’s dose during hospitalisation with prior stable periods, thereby capturing transient risk. By contrast, our study followed patients for 6 years, assessing long-term outcomes in established SLE with generally low baseline activity—an approach more appropriate for evaluating the cumulative effects of guideline-based dose adjustments, particularly in light of HCQ’s long half-life.

Differences in design and outcomes may explain why our results also differ from recent studies that evaluated HCQ taper or discontinuation. The Canadian multicentre and international SLICC inception cohorts both reported increased short-term flare risk, especially when patients were still on prednisone at taper.12 13 The Italian remission-only study, which incorporated HCQ blood level monitoring, found no statistically significant dose–flare association but noted a protective effect of stable therapeutic levels and no correlation between prescribed dose and blood level.14 The Performance of OCT C-scan in the Screening for Retinopathy Related to Synthetic Antimalarials (PERFOCTAPS) study linked dose reduction after ophthalmologic findings to more than double the flare risk, most within the first 6 months.15 All studies focused on flare-based outcomes and short-term risk, whereas our Puerto Rican cohort—managed uniformly per AAO 2016 guidelines, without drug level monitoring, and spanning a range of disease activity and duration—showed no increase in flares but a slight rise in SDI over time, despite stable or improved SLEDAI and reduced prednisone use. These differences—study design (short-term case-crossover or flare-focused cohorts vs long-term observational follow-up), outcome measures (flare incidence vs SDI progression), baseline activity (remission-only or early disease vs mixed activity levels) and population ethnicity (predominantly White or multiethnic vs exclusively Puerto Rican)—may account for the discrepancy.

We found that patients who did not require a reduction in HCQ had a higher frequency of HCQ-induced retinopathy as well as overweight/obesity. Although both groups received less than 5 mg/kg/day of HCQ, patients who underwent dose adjustment had lower HCQ weight-based daily dose than those who did not. Because current safety guidelines recommend using actual body weight—rather than ideal body weight, as in previous guidelines16—to determine the 5 mg/kg/day cut-off, patients with overweight or obesity likely did not meet criteria for dose adjustment. It would be of interest to evaluate whether applying the cut-off based on ideal body weight would reduce the risk of HCQ-induced retinopathy in this subgroup.

Our study has some limitations. First, its retrospective design and the inclusion of an exclusively Puerto Rican female cohort with long-standing disease may limit the generalisability of the findings. Although our findings suggest that HCQ dose adjustment is safe with respect to long-term clinical outcomes in our cohort, these results may not be generalisable to other ethnic populations. Prior evidence indicates that the risk of SLE flare associated with reduced HCQ dosing may vary across ethnic groups, and this potential heterogeneity should be carefully considered when extrapolating our results.17 Second, the relatively small sample size reduces the statistical power to detect rare events. Third, HCQ blood levels were not measured, preventing confirmation of true drug exposure. Finally, we were unable to compare the longitudinal evolution of clinical outcomes, such as disease activity, flares and damage, between patients who did and did not require HCQ dose adjustment, as complete longitudinal data were not consistently available for the latter group. This group was not the focus of the original investigation, which was restricted to patients requiring HCQ dose adjustment. Therefore, future prospective, multiethnic studies with standardised long-term monitoring are needed to determine whether sustained HCQ dose reduction contributes to damage accrual and to identify strategies to reduce this risk.

In summary, in this SLE cohort with stable disease and low disease activity 2 years after HCQ dose adjustment according to AAO guidelines, no increase in disease activity, flares, hospitalisations or corticosteroid dose was observed during the 4-year extension period. However, a modest rise in SDI was observed over time, underscoring the importance of continued monitoring in this population. Notably, patients who underwent HCQ dose adjustment had a lower prevalence of HCQ-induced retinopathy than those who did not require dose adjustment, an association likely related to differences in HCQ exposure rather than a causal protective effect of dose adjustment.
