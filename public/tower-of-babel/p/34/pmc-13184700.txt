Basic science is a fundamental core to our understanding and practice of medicine. I am not sure I really understood that when I first started studying medicine. In high school, my sciences included chemistry and biology, both of which I loved, probably biased by excellent teachers, Ms Goh and Mr Hopkins. Unfortunately, I skipped physics in pursuit of maths and music. I suppose I had no way of knowing at that stage that my career speciality would be heavily dependent on an intricate understanding of basic physics principles. Due to this lack of physics I ended up doing one year of pre-med which encompassed the big three (chemistry, biology, physics). After that we started doing the modules you expect to do in medical school (anatomy, pharmacology etc.), but this continued to be peppered with basic sciences, such as biochemistry and physiology. At the time, understanding complicated formulas about the motion of air or the chemical structure of metabolites really didn't seem relevant to me being a healthcare worker (I do appreciate that medical education has really changed since then, and educators have been forced to teach these basic sciences with an emphasis on context in medical practice).

As I started practising medicine I then really understood the importance of these core basic science concepts. And now as a clinician scientist, my world is acutely focused on understanding how different molecules, different chemical structures and different microorganisms impact our bodies, our anatomy and our physiology.

Breathe is the educational journal of the European Respiratory Society (ERS), and the journal has really focused on tangible, practical clinical education dusted with interesting clinical cases. One of my visions as editor in chief was to expand our online real estate into education in medical science. Physiology education has been a big (and popular) aspect of the journal, and we've now had a few more articles on respiratory science topics. In this issue we decided to focus on the molecules of respiratory medicine (a rather broad and intentionally vague topic).

Through scientific exploration we as a community have revolutionised care for so many pulmonary diseases. Probably the condition that has changed the most through scientific discovery is cystic fibrosis. Ireland has a high prevalence of cystic fibrosis, and during my specialist training, rotating through our cystic fibrosis centres was tough; emotional, complicated and honestly morbid. Now, we rarely have cystic fibrosis patients in hospital and a significant proportion of time in this sub-speciality has refocused on ambulatory care. Of course, we have other examples of big scientific discoveries leading to big treatment and outcome changes in respiratory medicine such as in asthma and COPD. Some of these important molecules are discussed in an excellent review by Kearney et al. [1] and are also discussed in this issue's journal club [2].

More recently, the importance of molecules and understanding basic science is probably best highlighted with lung cancer where we have been able to change the survival of this once fatal disease with new targeted therapies. We thus have a great upcoming review on the molecules in lung cancer from M. Talty and co-workers, as well as reviews on immune checkpoints in lung cancer [3] and the complications of immunotherapy in lung cancer [4]. Along the same theme, but in a different disease, we also have a upcoming wonderful piece on immune checkpoints in interstitial lung disease.

We also have an great editorial from Ananth et al. [5] as part of the ERS “My Career Path” series. As always, I would like to thank all our authors, associate editors, ERS Assembly Secretaries and the editorial team for helping me put this issue together.
