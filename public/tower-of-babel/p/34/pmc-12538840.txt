Physical activity and cardiovascular-metabolic disease risk across cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Wei Zhao, Qing Yan, Chuan Mou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome has become a major global health burden, while physical activity, as an important lifestyle intervention, remains unclear regarding its protective effects on disease risk across different CKM stages.MethodsWe utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), including 7,159 participants. According to the quartile distribution of total weekly metabolic equivalents, all participants were divided into four groups: Q1 group (≤ 1732.5 MET-min/week, reference group), Q2 group (1732.6–4158.0 MET-min/week), Q3 group (4158.1–9744.0 MET-min/week), and Q4 group (> 9744.0 MET-min/week). For CKM analysis, participants were categorized into two groups: low-risk CKM (stages 0–2) and high-risk CKM (stages 3–4), with high-risk CKM progression defined as the risk of being in stages 3–4 versus stages 0–2. We employed three progressively adjusted logistic regression models to assess the associations between physical activity metabolic equivalents and outcomes of heart disease, stroke, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), cardiometabolic disease (CMD), and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM). Dose-response relationships were evaluated using restricted cubic spline functions, and subgroup analyses and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve assessments were conducted to evaluate predictive performance.ResultsIn the fully adjusted model, compared to Q1, Q4 was significantly associated with reduced risks of heart disease (OR = 0.760, 95%CI: 0.631–0.912), stroke (OR = 0.571, 95%CI: 0.376–0.850), diabetes (OR = 0.701, 95%CI: 0.551–0.888), CVD (OR = 0.741, 95%CI: 0.621–0.883), CMD (OR = 0.728, 95%CI: 0.621–0.853), and CMM (OR = 0.529, 95%CI: 0.360–0.762). Similarly, for high-risk CKM progression, the Q4 group demonstrated significant protective effects with a 27.5% risk reduction (OR = 0.725, 95%CI: 0.617–0.850, P < 0.001), while Q2 and Q3 groups showed no significant associations. Dose-response analysis revealed significant linear relationships for all diseases (P overall < 0.05). Subgroup analyses identified age, gender, and residence as significant effect modifiers, with greater health benefits observed among elderly individuals, males, and urban residents. ROC analysis showed good predictive performance with area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.7 for all diseases, with CMM showing the highest predictive effect (AUC: 0.827).ConclusionsAmong CKM stage 0–4 populations, high-level physical activity provides significant protective effects against cardiovascular and metabolic diseases. This highlights the important role of physical activity intervention in the prevention of these diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05216-3.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of disease burden globally, with approximately 330 million CVD patients in China [1]. With the accelerating global aging process and changing lifestyles, the comorbidity of cardiovascular diseases with metabolic disorders and chronic kidney disease has become increasingly prominent [2, 3]. In 2023, the American Heart Association first introduced the concept of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, defining it as a multi-organ dysfunction health disorder caused by the interconnection of obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease [4]. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey demonstrated that 26.3% of participants had at least one CKM-related condition, while the proportion of individuals with multiple CKM diseases increased significantly between 1999 and 2020, with the prevalence of all three CKM diseases coexisting doubling during this period [5]. The pathogenesis of CKM syndrome primarily stems from excessive and/or dysfunctional adipose tissue, which secretes pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative products, leading to arterial, cardiac, and renal tissue damage while reducing insulin sensitivity [6, 7]. An epidemiological study revealed that patients with multiple CKM diseases had significantly increased mortality risk, with cardiovascular death risk in patients with diabetes combined with chronic kidney disease being 16%, 3%, and 6% higher than those with diabetes or chronic kidney disease alone, respectively [8].

Physical activity, as an important lifestyle intervention, plays a crucial role in preventing cardiovascular and metabolic diseases. Exercise prevents hypertension through modulating sympathetic nervous and renin-angiotensin systems, optimizing sodium handling, and improving endothelial function [9]. Simultaneously, it enhances insulin sensitivity and promotes skeletal muscle glucose uptake, thereby reducing diabetes incidence risk [10, 11]. Although the benefits of physical activity in cardiovascular disease prevention have been widely recognized, within the newly proposed CKM syndrome framework, the specific effects of physical activity on cardiovascular metabolic disease risk across different CKM stages remain understudied. Particularly in developing countries like China, due to differences in genetic background, lifestyle, environmental factors, and socioeconomic conditions, the protective effects of physical activity may exhibit unique characteristics.

Therefore, based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative large-scale cohort dataset, this study aims to systematically evaluate the associations between physical activity metabolic equivalents and the risk of heart disease, stroke, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cardiometabolic disease, and cardiometabolic multimorbidity among patients in CKM stages 0–4, explore dose-response relationships, identify effect modifiers in different population subgroups, and provide scientific evidence for developing individualized physical activity intervention strategies and prevention management of CKM syndrome.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was based on data from CHARLS, a nationally representative large-scale cohort study. CHARLS employed a multistage stratified probability proportional sampling method and completed the national baseline survey in 2011–2012, covering 150 counties (districts) and 450 villages (neighborhood committees) across 28 provinces nationwide, successfully interviewing 17,708 individuals from 10,257 households [12]. The study adopted a face-to-face follow-up mode every 2–3 years, with continuous tracking until 2020. The implementation of CHARLS strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (approval number: IRB00001052-11015), with all participants providing written informed consent.

Our study utilized data from the third survey conducted in 2015 for analysis. From the 21,096 participants surveyed in 2015, we excluded participants who had been diagnosed with heart disease, stroke, or diabetes at baseline (2011–2012) to ensure that the analytical population consisted of incident cases of relevant diseases. After screening and excluding participants with missing key variables, a final total of 7159 participants were included in the analysis (Figure S1).

Assessment of physical activity and metabolic equivalents

In CHARLS data, participants’ physical activities were categorized into three intensity levels, including vigorous activities (high-intensity physical activities such as weightlifting and digging), moderate-intensity activities (moderate-intensity activities such as cycling and mopping), and light activities and walking [12]. Referring to the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) guidelines and previous studies [13–15], we assigned corresponding metabolic equivalent values to the three different intensity levels of physical activities, with vigorous activities, moderate-intensity activities, and light activities set at 8.0 METs, 4.0 METs, and 3.0 METs, respectively. During the survey, participants were required to report the duration and frequency of each activity, with duration categorized as 0 min, 10–29 min, 30–119 min, 120–239 min, and ≥ 240 min. Since the survey provided time intervals rather than specific values, we used the median of each interval as the calculation basis following previous studies [15, 16]. Based on participant-reported data, we calculated total weekly metabolic equivalents (Total MET-minutes/week) using the formula: MET value of each activity × activity duration × weekly frequency. According to the quartile distribution of total weekly metabolic equivalents, all participants were divided into four groups: Q1 group (≤ 1732.5 MET-min/week, reference group), Q2 group (1732.6–4158.0 MET-min/week), Q3 group (4158.1–9744.0 MET-min/week), and Q4 group (>9744.0 MET-min/week).

Definition of disease outcomes

All disease diagnoses in this study were based on participants’ self-reported physician diagnoses. Heart disease was defined as participant-reported physician-diagnosed heart disease, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, congestive heart failure, or other heart diseases. Stroke was defined as participant-reported physician-diagnosed cerebrovascular accident. Diabetes was defined as participant-reported physician-diagnosed diabetes or glucose abnormalities, including impaired glucose tolerance and impaired fasting glucose.

Based on the above single disease definitions, we further constructed composite disease outcomes. CVD was defined as a composite outcome of heart disease or stroke [17], cardiometabolic disease (CMD) was defined as the presence of any one of heart disease, stroke, or diabetes [18], and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) was defined as the simultaneous presence of at least two diseases among heart disease, stroke, and diabetes [18, 19].

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome staging

Our study adopted the 2023 American Heart Association CKM syndrome staging criteria and classified them in combination with Chinese population characteristics [4]. CKM Stage 0 comprised individuals without CKM risk factors, including normal body mass index, waist circumference, blood glucose, blood pressure, and blood lipids, with no evidence of chronic kidney disease or cardiovascular disease. CKM Stage 1 included individuals with overweight or obesity, defined as body mass index (BMI) ≥ 23 kg/m² according to Asian population standards, abnormal waist circumference (≥ 80 cm for women, ≥ 90 cm for men), or prediabetic state (fasting glucose 100–124 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7%−6.4%). CKM Stage 2 comprised individuals with metabolic risk factors or chronic kidney disease, including hypertriglyceridemia (≥ 135 mg/dL), hypertension, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, or chronic kidney disease. CKM Stage 3 included individuals with excess/dysfunctional obesity, other metabolic risk factors, or chronic kidney disease who developed subclinical cardiovascular disease, defined as 10-year cardiovascular disease risk ≥ 20% or high-risk chronic kidney disease status. We used the PREVENT risk prediction model developed by the American Heart Association to assess the probability of cardiovascular disease events occurring within the next 10 years [20]. CKM Stage 4 comprised individuals with clinical cardiovascular disease, defined as a history of chronic heart failure, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, or stroke.

Covariates

Covariate selection was based on previous studies and clinical relevance [16, 21], including sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and health status indicators. Sociodemographic characteristics included age (continuous variable), sex (male/female), education level, marital status, residence (urban/rural), and retirement status (yes/no). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never smoked/former smoker/current smoker) and drinking status (never drink/occasional drinking/regular drinking). Health status indicators included BMI (kg/m², as a continuous variable). BMI was classified according to Chinese adult standards, including normal weight (18.5–23.9 kg/m²) and overweight (≥ 24.0 kg/m²). All covariate information was collected from standardized questionnaires in the 2015 survey through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained investigators.

Statistical analysis

In descriptive analysis, continuous variables were presented as means (standard deviations) and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). For between-group comparisons, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables.

We constructed three progressively adjusted logistic regression models to assess the association between physical activity metabolic equivalents and heart disease, stroke, diabetes, CVD, CMD, CMM, and high-risk CKM progression [22]. For CKM analysis, participants were categorized into two groups: low-risk CKM (stages 0–2) and high-risk CKM (stages 3–4), with high-risk CKM progression defined as the risk of being in stages 3–4 versus stages 0–2 [22]. Model 1 was the unadjusted model, Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, and Model 3 was the fully adjusted model, further adjusting for education level, marital status, residence, retirement status, smoking, and drinking status based on Model 2. Results were presented as odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was employed to explore nonlinear associations between metabolic equivalents and disease risk, with 3 knot positions set at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles, using the 10th percentile as the reference point, and likelihood ratio tests were used to assess the statistical significance of nonlinear associations.

Subgroup analysis explored the heterogeneity of protective effects of physical activity across different populations, with stratification variables including age (< 65 years vs. ≥ 65 years), sex (male vs. female), BMI grouping (normal vs. overweight), residence (urban vs. rural), smoking status (yes vs. no), and drinking status (yes vs. no). Interaction terms were added to logistic regression models to test effect modification, with likelihood ratio tests used to assess the statistical significance of interactions. We also constructed prediction models based on physical activity metabolic equivalent quartile groupings, using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to calculate area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence intervals to evaluate predictive performance.

Sensitivity analyses

To test model robustness, we conducted three sensitivity analyses. First, we further incorporated CESD10 score, sleep duration, hypertension, and C-reactive protein as covariates in Model 3 to verify result robustness. Second, we analyzed total metabolic equivalents as a continuous variable (per 1000 MET-min/week unit). Additionally, multilevel models were employed to consider clustering effects at the city level to assess potential impacts of geographic factors.

For missing values in metabolic equivalent data, multiple imputation was used for completion, with the number of imputations set at 5, and imputation variables including relevant covariates such as age, sex, education level, and residence [23]. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0), with two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 7,159 participants were recruited for analysis and stratified by quartiles of physical activity metabolic equivalents (Table 1). Among them, 1,305 (18.2%) had heart disease, 726 (10.1%) had stroke, 1,435 (4.0%) had diabetes, 1,444 (20.2%) had CVD, 1,897 (26.5%) had CMD, and 325 (4.5%) had CMM. With increasing physical activity metabolic equivalents, the incidence of all diseases showed a significant declining trend (P < 0.001). Regarding demographic characteristics, participants had a mean age of 60.44 ± 8.18 years, which decreased with increasing physical activity levels (P < 0.001). The mean BMI was 24.39 ± 3.37 kg/m², showing a declining trend with increasing physical activity levels (P < 0.001). Overall, females comprised 55.0% and males 45.0% of the cohort; urban residents accounted for 36.8% and rural residents 63.2%. In terms of health behaviors, 26.3% of participants had smoking habits and 35.4% had drinking habits. In the distribution of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome staging, Stage 2 was most common (47.3%), followed by Stage 4 (20.2%), with the proportion of high-risk stages showing a declining trend as physical activity levels increased (P < 0.001).

Table 2 shows the association between physical activity levels and disease risks among participants across CKM syndrome stages 0–4. In Model 3, after full adjustment for age, sex, BMI, marital status, retirement status, residence, education level, smoking, and drinking status, higher levels of physical activity were significantly associated with reduced risks of multiple diseases compared to the lowest physical activity level (Q1). For heart disease, only the Q4 group showed significant protective effects, with a 24.0% risk reduction (OR = 0.760, 95% CI: 0.631–0.912, P = 0.003). For stroke, both Q2 and Q4 groups demonstrated significant protective effects, with risk reductions of 33.5% (OR = 0.665, 95% CI: 0.449–0.968, P = 0.037) and 42.9% (OR = 0.571, 95% CI: 0.376–0.850, P = 0.007), respectively. For diabetes, CVD, and CMD, significant protective effects were observed only in the highest activity level group (Q4), with risk reductions of 29.9% (OR = 0.701, 95% CI: 0.551–0.888, p = 0.004), 25.9% (OR = 0.741, 95% CI: 0.621–0.883, P = 0.001), and 27.2% (OR = 0.728, 95% CI: 0.621–0.853, P < 0.001), respectively. Similarly, for CMM, the Q4 group showed the most significant risk reduction of 47.1% (OR = 0.529, 95% CI: 0.360–0.762, P = 0.001). Similarly, for high-risk CKM progression, the Q4 group demonstrated significant protective effects with a 27.5% risk reduction (OR = 0.725, 95% CI: 0.617–0.850, P < 0.001), while Q2 and Q3 groups showed no significant associations (Q2: OR = 1.005, 95% CI: 0.859–1.175, P = 0.952; Q3: OR = 0.958, 95% CI: 0.824–1.113, P = 0.575).

Figure 1 illustrates the dose-response relationships between total physical activity metabolic equivalents and various diseases among CKM stage 0–4 participants. All diseases demonstrated significant overall associations (P overall < 0.05), with risks continuously declining as physical activity levels increased. For heart disease (P overall = 0.003, P nonlinear = 0.105), stroke (P overall = 0.016, P nonlinear = 0.926), diabetes (P overall = 0.002, P nonlinear = 0.095), CVD (P overall = 0.001, P nonlinear = 0.149), and CMM (P overall = 0.003, P nonlinear = 0.536), the relationships were primarily linear with non-significant nonlinear associations. However, CMD exhibited a nonlinear relationship (P nonlinear = 0.041). Similarly, both CKM progression analyses showed significant linear dose-response relationships: CKM stages 0–1 vs. 2–4 (P overall = 0.001, P nonlinear = 0.463) and CKM stages 0–2 vs. 3–4 (P overall = 0.001, P nonlinear = 0.543), with predominantly linear associations and non-significant nonlinear effects.

Subgroup analysis results for CKM stage 0–4 participants (Figs. 2 and 3) revealed significant heterogeneity in the protective effects of physical activity across different populations. Age was the most important moderating factor, showing significant interactions with heart disease (p = 0.024) and CMD (P = 0.038). Among individuals ≥ 65 years, the protective effect against heart disease was stronger in the Q4 group, with a 35.6% risk reduction (OR = 0.644, 95% CI: 0.463–0.887), while those < 65 years showed a 23.3% reduction (OR = 0.767, 95% CI: 0.611–0.961). However, for CMD, younger populations demonstrated superior protective effects (OR = 0.676, 95% CI: 0.556–0.820) compared to older populations (OR = 0.749, 95% CI: 0.565–0.990). Sex showed significant interaction with stroke (P = 0.034), with males in the Q4 group experiencing a 49.9% risk reduction (OR = 0.501, 95% CI: 0.246–0.955), demonstrating superior protective effects compared to females (OR = 0.610, 95% CI: 0.358–1.015). Residence showed significant interactions with diabetes (P = 0.012), CMD (P = 0.025), and CMM (P = 0.026), with urban residents deriving greater benefits from high-level physical activity, showing risk reductions of 56.0% (OR = 0.440, 95% CI: 0.272–0.683), 44.1% (OR = 0.559, 95% CI: 0.409–0.755), and 75.0% (OR = 0.250, 95% CI: 0.096–0.542) for diabetes, CMD, and CMM, respectively, while rural residents showed relatively weaker protective effects. Additionally, BMI showed significant interaction with diabetes (P = 0.033), smoking status with diabetes (P = 0.042), and drinking status with diabetes (P = 0.012) and CMM (P = 0.031), with normal-weight individuals, smokers, and different drinking status groups all showing significant protective effects in the Q4 group. Overall, among CKM stage 0–4 populations, elderly individuals at high risk for heart disease, males at high risk for stroke, urban residents, and normal-weight individuals may derive greater health benefits from increased physical activity.

ROC curve analysis results (Figs. 4A-D) demonstrated good predictive performance of different physical activity levels for various diseases among CKM stage 0–4 participants. The predictive performance across physical activity quartile groups (Q1-Q4) remained relatively stable, with all diseases showing AUC values exceeding 0.7, indicating moderate to good predictive capability. CMM exhibited the highest predictive performance across all physical activity level groups, with AUC values ranging from 0.826 to 0.828. Similarly, high-risk CKM progression showed consistently good predictive performance with AUC values of 0.815–0.816 across all quartiles, demonstrating stable discriminative ability. The AUC values for heart disease, stroke, diabetes, CVD, and CMD remained stable within ranges of 0.767, 0.792–0.794, 0.739, 0.766–0.767, and 0.757–0.758, respectively. Notably, as physical activity levels increased from Q1 to Q4, the predictive performance for each disease remained essentially consistent, with minimal variation in AUC values, indicating stable discriminative ability of physical activity levels for disease risk prediction across different activity intensities. These results suggest that physical activity metabolic equivalents serve as good and stable predictive factors for cardiovascular and metabolic diseases and CKM progression in CKM stage 0–4 populations, with particularly significant predictive effects for CMM and high-risk CKM progression.

Sensitivity analyses validated the robustness of the study findings. First, after further incorporating CESD10, sleep duration, hypertension, and C-reactive protein into Model 3, the protective effects of physical activity remained significant (Table S1). Second, when total metabolic equivalents were analyzed as a continuous variable, each 1000 MET-min/week increase significantly reduced disease risk (Table S2). Additionally, results from multilevel models accounting for city-level differences were consistent with the primary analysis (Table S3).

Discussion

This large-scale longitudinal study including 7,159 participants across CKM stages 0–4 provides robust evidence for the protective effects of physical activity against heart disease, stroke, diabetes, CVD, CMD, CMM and high-risk CKM progression. The findings demonstrate a clear association between higher levels of physical activity and reduced risk of all observed diseases. Furthermore, physical activity exhibited stronger protective effects against cardiometabolic multimorbidity.

Our observed reduction in CVD risk through physical activity is consistent with several landmark studies. A population-based cohort study encompassing 131,558 CVD patients demonstrated that each 500 MET-min/week increase in physical activity reduced mortality by 14% in the secondary prevention group and 7% in the primary prevention group [24]. Another UK Biobank study of 359,773 participants showed that high-intensity physical activity reduced cardiometabolic disease incidence by 13% and mortality risk by 27% [25]. Our finding of a 29.9% reduction in diabetes risk is well-supported by existing evidence. Research demonstrates that exercise reduces diabetes incidence through improved insulin sensitivity and enhanced skeletal muscle glucose uptake [26]. A systematic review encompassing 28 prospective cohort studies confirmed that both low- and high-intensity physical activity can reduce type 2 diabetes risk by 26% to 36% [27]. Additionally, our observed 42.9% reduction in stroke risk aligns closely with epidemiological evidence. The Harvard Alumni Health Study demonstrated that physical activity of varying intensities can reduce stroke risk by 18–46%, while our dose-response analysis further revealed the linear characteristics of this association [28].

The protective effects of physical activity on the cardiovascular system involve multiple biological pathways. Exercise prevents hypertension by modulating sympathetic nervous system activity, renin-angiotensin system activity, sodium handling, and improving endothelial function [29, 30]. At the endothelial level, physical activity enhances vascular nitric oxide production, reduces reactive oxygen species generation, and revitalizes the endothelium through activation of endogenous progenitor cells [26, 31, 32]. Exercise also improves lipid metabolism, with aerobic physical activity reducing triglyceride and low-density lipoprotein concentrations while increasing high-density lipoprotein concentrations [33]. For the metabolic system, exercise improves insulin sensitivity and skeletal muscle glucose uptake, with GLUT4 translocation persisting for 48 h after a single exercise session, while long-term training increases total GLUT4 protein content in skeletal muscle [34, 35]. Physical activity also promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic gene expression through activation of the AMPK pathway and PGC-1α signaling [36, 37]. Regarding renal protection, exercise protects kidney function by reducing oxidative stress, controlling inflammation, managing blood pressure, and improving metabolism [38].

Interestingly, we found that the protective effects of physical activity against cardiometabolic multimorbidity were significantly stronger than those against individual diseases. CKM syndrome primarily stems from excessive and/or dysfunctional adipose tissue that secretes pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative products, leading to tissue damage in arteries, heart, and kidneys [4, 39]. The potent protective effects of physical activity against comorbid conditions can be explained through several physiological mechanisms. First, physical activity exerts powerful anti-inflammatory effects, with regular exercise significantly reducing C-reactive protein levels and decreasing inflammasome activation, simultaneously suppressing both cardiovascular and metabolic inflammation [32, 40, 41]. Second, exercise-induced adaptive responses, including enhanced mitochondrial function, improved cellular antioxidant capacity, and regulated inflammatory factor balance, may constitute the molecular basis for its potent protection against complex disease states [42, 43].

Our subgroup analyses identified age, sex, and residence as important effect modifiers. Individuals ≥ 65 years gained more significant protective benefits against heart disease, primarily because elderly populations have high baseline risk, limited physiological reserves, and often multiple risk factors, making physical activity produce greater relative effects [44]. Sex showed significant interaction with stroke, with males demonstrating markedly superior outcomes compared to females. This may be related to hormonal differences, as estrogen plays important roles in endothelial function and vascular protection, and males may engage in more exercise [45]. Urban residents gained greater benefits from high levels of physical activity, primarily due to diverse exercise types, adequate medical resources and health education, and better exercise facilities in cities, while rural populations generally have insufficient physical activity and face constraints including limited resources and geographical dispersion [46, 47]. Furthermore, our dose-response analysis revealed important clinical guidance value. All diseases showed significant overall associations, with most displaying linear relationships, while CMD demonstrated non-linear relationships, suggesting threshold effects. This aligns with epidemiological evidence that global physical inactivity contributes to premature deaths, emphasizing the importance that even modest increases in physical activity can provide significant health benefits. ROC analysis showed that physical activity has good predictive value for various diseases, with CMM showing the best predictive performance, further confirming the central role of physical activity in CKM syndrome prevention.

Our study is based on the nationally representative CHARLS cohort, encompassing 7,159 participants from 150 counties across 28 provinces, with large sample size and extensive geographical distribution. This is the first large-scale study of physical activity protective effects based on CKM syndrome staging criteria, providing important epidemiological evidence for this emerging clinical framework. Additionally, we adopted the latest 2023 American Heart Association CKM staging criteria and made localized adjustments considering Chinese population characteristics, covering the complete disease spectrum from no risk factors to clinical disease, providing more comprehensive risk assessment. However, our study has several limitations. Physical activity levels relied on self-reporting, which may introduce recall bias and could lead to overestimation of physical activity levels or underestimation of disease risks. Despite including multiple covariates for adjustment, some uncontrollable factors such as dietary patterns and genetic susceptibility may still influence results. Furthermore, our study population primarily consisted of middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals, and the generalizability of results may be limited by racial, cultural, and socioeconomic factors.

Conclusion

Our study confirms that physical activity provides significant protective effects against heart disease, stroke, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cardiometabolic disease, and their multimorbidity in patients across CKM stages 0–4. Additionally, high-level physical activity demonstrated significant protective effects against high-risk CKM progression, with a 27.5% risk reduction, indicating its effectiveness in preventing advancement from low-risk to high-risk CKM stages. The protective effects of physical activity against multimorbidity are particularly prominent, highlighting its important value in preventing complex disease patterns. The dose-response relationship indicates that even modest increases in physical activity can provide health benefits, emphasizing the important role of physical activity as a primary prevention strategy for CKM syndrome.
