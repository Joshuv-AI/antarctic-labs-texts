Cardioprotection by Allium ampeloprasum subsp. Iranicum: attenuation of cyclophosphamide-induced cardiotoxicity via modulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory mediator
Authors: Fatemeh Abedi, Kobra Shirani, Mahya Motie Nasrabadi, Ameneh Omidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCyclophosphamide (CTX) is a widely used chemotherapeutic agent whose routine administration can induce cardiotoxicity, limiting its therapeutic efficacy. Allium ampeloprasum subsp. Iranicum extract (AAE) has been proposed as a natural cardioprotective agent owing to its rich phenolic and flavonoid content. This study investigates the protective potential and underlying mechanisms of AAE against CTX-induced cardiotoxicity.MethodsNMRI mice were assigned to control, CTX, AAE, and AAE + CTX groups. We assessed: (i) body and heart weight metrics; (ii) histopathology of cardiac tissue; (iii) serum cardiac injury markers (AST, LDH, CK-MB); (iv) myocardial lipid peroxidation (TBARS); (v) antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, GSH-Px); (vi) proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β); and (vii) histological heart status as a cross-tissue readout. Data were analyzed for significance against the CTX-alone and control groups.ResultsCTX induced marked cardiotoxicity, evidenced by weight loss, cardiac histopathology disruptions, elevated TBARS, depleted SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities, and increased serum AST, LDH, CK-MB, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. Pretreatment with AAE markedly mitigated CTX effects: TBARS were reduced; SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities were restored or enhanced; serum cardiac injury markers declined; and proinflammatory cytokines decreased. Body weight loss associated with CTX was attenuated, with AAE-associated weight gain surpassing controls. Histology showed preservation of myocardial architecture and reduced vacuolization in cardiac tissue.ConclusionCollectively, these findings indicate that AAE exerts multi-targeted protection through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective actions, thereby attenuating CTX-induced cardiotoxicity. These findings position the Iranian accession of A. ampeloprasum as a promising nutraceutical adjunct to CTX therapy, with potential to enhance tolerance and preserve cardiac function. Further work should characterize bioactive constituents, assess pharmacokinetics and interactions with CTX, and explore translational applicability in clinical setting.

Introduction

Cyclophosphamide (CTX) is an effective alkylating agent used in chemotherapy and as an immunosuppressant in various disorders (Tian et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). While it can be life-saving, it carries a risk of cardiotoxicity that can limit its use (Gephart et al., 2025). Cardiac injury is often dose-dependent and may manifest as acute, subacute, or chronic. Acute presentation typically occurs within days of administration and can include myocarditis, congestive heart failure, arrhythmias, pericarditis, and edema (Wadia, 2015; Atalay et al., 2014; Ayza et al., 2020a). Management is largely supportive and stage-dependent, and for patients who require ongoing cancer therapy, alternative regimens with reduced cardiotoxic risk should be considered. Prevention strategies focus on risk stratification before initiating cyclophosphamide, using the lowest effective dose, development of new formulations for reducing the toxicity, gradual infusion when possible, and vigilant monitoring during treatment (Haj-Yehia et al., 2025; Razavi-Azarkhiavi et al., 2016). The CTX proposed cardiotoxicity mechanisms involve endothelial injury, capillary leak, systemic inflammatory response, and direct toxic metabolites that damage cardiomyocytes and the microvasculature. CTX causes excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), lipid peroxidation, and endothelial injury, leading to cardiomyocyte damage and impaired function (Alizadehasl et al., 2024). Plant-derived antioxidants offer a promising strategy to counteract cyclophosphamide-induced cardiotoxicity by targeting oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammation. Plant antioxidants, including polyphenols (curcumin, resveratrol, quercetin), flavonoids (naringenin, hesperetin), micronutrients with antioxidant roles, terpenoids (thymoquinone, limonene), and carotenoids (lycopene, beta-carotene), can scavenge ROS, preserve mitochondrial integrity, modulate inflammatory signaling, and improve endothelial function. Evidence spans in vitro and animal studies showing reduced oxidative stress markers and improved cardiac outcomes; human data are limited and require larger trials (Ayza et al., 2020b; Shaernejad et al., 2025; Syahputra et al., 2022).

Allium ampeloprasum subsp. Iranicum is a regional variant of the Allium genus in the Amaryllidaceae family. It is a culinary Allium valued for its pungent flavor and aroma and is grown in temperate regions of the Near and Middle East. The plant is a perennial or biennial herb with underground bulbs, tall hollow stems, linear basal leaves, and an umbel of small flowers (Abedi et al., 2024; Fatoorechi et al., 2016). It represents a feasible candidate for cardioprotection due to its rich phytochemical profile, which includes organosulfur compounds, polyphenols, and flavonoids. Sulfur-containing constituents may mitigate oxidative stress in cardiac tissue, while polyphenols and flavonoids enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses and support mitochondrial integrity. Anti-inflammatory effects and improved endothelial function may further protect the myocardium and microvasculature. These constituents underpin antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-fibrotic properties that align with mechanistic pathways implicated in cardiotoxicity, such as oxidative stress, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)/tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) signaling, and apoptosis, suggesting potential to mitigate ROS overproduction, lipid peroxidation, and inflammatory cascades while preserving tissue integrity (Song et al., 2025; Rajpoot and Sharma, 2025).

Ethnopharmacological use and dietary familiarity further support its feasibility, though rigorous toxicity and interaction assessments are essential in the chemotherapy context (Shahsavari et al., 2023; Mosavat et al., 2015). Given local availability and traditional use, standardized extracts of A. ampeloprasum could be developed for cardioprotection against cyclophosphamide-induced toxicity, contingent on precise dosing, quality control, and evaluation of herb–drug interactions. Preclinical studies are necessary to elucidate mechanisms, optimize dosing, and establish safety before clinical translation. Key actions include scavenging ROS, boosting endogenous antioxidants, reducing proinflammatory mediators, and mitigating heart injury. The study framework should profile phytochemicals, test efficacy in relevant in vitro/in vivo cardiotoxicity models, monitor oxidative/inflammatory biomarkers, and assess CTX compatibility. If protective effects are shown, data could support data-driven, cost-effective adjunctive strategies for safer dosing and improved oncology outcomes. While previous studies have investigated the cardioprotective effects of other Allium species such as Allium sativum (garlic) and Allium cepa (onion) against cardiotoxicity, no study to date has examined the protective potential of A. ampeloprasum against cyclophosphamide-induced cardiotoxicity (Kharadi et al., 2016; Bedir et al., 2025). Furthermore, the existing literature primarily focuses on single antioxidant or anti-inflammatory parameters without providing an integrated assessment of both oxidative and inflammatory pathways. The novelty of the present study lies in: (1) the first evaluation of A. ampeloprasum as a cardioprotective agent against CTX-induced cardiotoxicity, (2) a comprehensive assessment of biochemical, histopathological, antioxidant, and inflammatory markers within a single study, and (3) providing a foundation for future mechanistic studies and identification of bioactive compounds.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the protective potential of A. ampeloprasum against CTX-induced cardiotoxicity through rigorous preclinical assessment of biochemical, histopathological, antioxidant, and inflammatory parameters.

Materials and methods

Plant sample preparation

A. ampeloprasum was collected in the mountainous regions of Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari, located in Iran, in the spring of 2023. Specialists from the Islamic and Complementary Medicine Department at Iran University of Medical Science, situated in Tehran, confirmed and authenticated this plant specimen. The collected plant materials were air-dried in the shade at room temperature and ground into a fine powder. One hundred grams (100 g) of the dried powder was macerated in 300 mL of ethanol–water mixture (80:20, v/v) at 25 °C for 72 h with occasional shaking. The mixture was then filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph, Germany). The concentrated extract was freeze-dried (FreeZone, Labconco, USA) and further dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C for 5 days (Sukhdev et al., 2008). The final dried A. ampeloprasum extract (AAE) was stored at 4 °C until further use.

Determination of total phenolic content

Total phenolic content was measured using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method as described by Singleton and Rossi (1965). Briefly, 0.5 mL of AAE solution (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 2.5 mL of 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 2 mL of 7.5% sodium carbonate (Na2CO3). The mixture was incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 30 min. The absorbance was read at 765 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). A standard curve was prepared using gallic acid (0–200 μg/mL; R2 = 0.998). Results were expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of dried extract (mg GAE/g extract) (Singleton and Rossi, 1965).

Determination of total flavonoid content

Total flavonoid content was determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric assay according to Zhishen et al. (1999). One milliliter of AAE solution (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 0.3 mL of 5% sodium nitrite (NaNO2). After 5 min, 0.3 mL of 10% aluminum chloride (AlCl3) was added, followed by 2 mL of 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) after another 5 min. The final volume was adjusted to 10 mL with distilled water. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm. Quercetin (0–100 μg/mL; R2 = 0.996) was used as the standard. Results were expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalents per gram of dried extract (mg QE/g extract) (Zhishen et al., 1999).

Animals

Male NMRI mice, aged between six and eight weeks and with a weight range of 28–33 g, were procured from the Faculty of Medical Sciences at Tarbiat Modares University, located in Tehran, Iran. The mice were kept in groups of five within polystyrene cages and allowed a week to adjust to the lab setting before any experiments. During this adjustment phase, the environment was controlled at a temperature of 20–22 °C, with humidity levels around 35%, and a cycle of 12 h of light followed by 12 h of darkness. The mice also had unlimited access to regular rodent food and water. All procedures complied with ethical standards and were approved by the ethical committee of Tarbiat Modares University, with the assigned approval number IR. MODARES.AEC.1401.014.

Treatment protocols

Male NMRI mice were assigned randomly into four distinct experimental groups (n = 5):

Group 1 (AAE only): Received 150 mg/kg AAE orally (gavage) once daily for 14 consecutive days.

Group 2 (AAE + CTX): AAE (150 mg/kg) orally once daily for 14 days and CTX (20 mg/kg) intraperitoneally (IP) once daily during the last 5 days (days 10–14). On days 10–14, AAE was administered 2 h before CTX to prevent direct chemical interaction.

Group 3 (CTX only): Received CTX 20 mg/kg IP once daily for 5 days (days 10–14).

Group 4 (Control): Received normal saline orally once daily for 14 days. Experimental procedures adhere to those used in earlier published investigations (Abedi et al., 2024).

Body and relative organ weight

To determine weight changes, the initial body weight of each mouse was recorded at baseline (day 1) before the start of any treatment. On day 14, mice were anesthetized using ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) 2 h after the last dose. After anesthesia, mice were killed by spinal cord transection. Heart weight was measured manually, and relative organ weight was calculated as a fraction of organ weight compared to total body weight (Shiehzadeh et al., 2022).

Serum collection and tissue preparation

Before obtaining tissue samples, blood was drawn from each mouse individually through cardiac puncture, with the collected blood being placed into sterile, empty centrifuge tubes. The blood samples were then left to clot for 10 min in a 37 °C environment, subsequently followed by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 10 min to isolate the serum component. Following this, the separated serum was sent off to a lab for a comprehensive biochemical examination covering numerous different factors. Following that, the hearts were carefully taken out and then washed using chilled isotonic saline solution to eliminate any blood and unwanted particles. The tissues intended for histopathology studies, lipid peroxidation tests, and antioxidant enzyme analyses were processed properly.

Histopathological studies

For histopathological analysis, cardiac tissues were preserved in a solution of 10% neutral-buffered formalin for a period of 24 to 48 h. Once the fixation was complete, the specimens underwent processing, were embedded in paraffin, and then cut into slices that were 5 μm thick. The slices were dyed with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and observed using a light microscope to evaluate structural alterations that signify tissue damage or disease (Garba and Bouderbala, 2022). Histological evaluations were performed by two independent pathologists who were blinded to the experimental groups. Biochemical parameters

Heart function was assessed by measuring aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and creatine kinase-myocardial fraction (CK-MB) (Saleh et al., 2020).

Protein extraction and normalization

For antioxidant enzyme and cytokine assays, heart tissues were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v). The homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Total protein concentration in each supernatant was determined using the Bradford method (Bradford, 1976) with bovine serum albumin as the standard (Kielkopf et al., 2020).

Lipid peroxidation assay

The amounts of malondialdehyde (MDA), which indicates the breakdown of lipid, were measured with the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) test kit (NalondiTM Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit, Navand Salamat, Iran) following the directions given by the producer (Shirani et al., 2023a).

Antioxidant enzymes assay

Antioxidant enzyme activities in heart tissue were measured using commercial assay kits, including SOD (Nasdox™–Superoxide Dismutase Assay Kit, Navand Salamat, Iran), catalase (CAT) (Nactaz™-Catalase Enzyme Activity assay kit, Navand Salamat, Iran), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) (Glutathione Peroxidase Assay Kit, Navand Salamat, Iran). All assays were performed according to the manufacturer's protocols (Garba and Bouderbala, 2022).

Inflammatory cytokine parameters

Heart inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6) (CN: KPG-MIL6, Karmania Pars Gene, Iran), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) (CN: KPG-MIL1b, Karmania Pars Gene, Iran), and TNF-α (CN: KPG-MTNF-a, Karmania Pars Gene, Iran), were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits in accordance with the manufacturers' protocols (Razavi-Azarkhiavi et al., 2016).

Statistical analyses

All results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Data among groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's honest significant differences post-hoc test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism statistical software version 20.

Results

Total flavonoid and phenolic compounds content

The quantitative analysis showed that one g of the dry extract of AAE contained 197.85 mg of total phenolics and 334.66 mg of total flavonoids.

Body and relative heart weight

The starting body weights were roughly comparable across all experimental groups. Following the treatments, the AAE group exhibited a statistically significant increase in body weight compared to the control group (p < 0.001). As shown in Table 1, the CTX-only group had a final body weight (29.6 ± 2.2 g) that was significantly lower than that of the negative control group (33.4 ± 3.3 g) (p < 0.05), indicating that CTX treatment induced a marked reduction in body weight. In contrast, the AAE + CTX group showed a final body weight (34.0 ± 2.9 g) that was significantly higher than that of the CTX-only group (p < 0.01), demonstrating that AAE pretreatment effectively prevented CTX-induced body weight loss. Furthermore, analysis of heart weight and relative heart weight showed no significant difference between all the different test groups (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that pretreatment with AAE confers substantial protection against CTX-induced cardiotoxicity, as evidenced by preserved body weight gain, restored cardiac histology, attenuation of serum cardiomyocyte injury markers, reduced myocardial lipid peroxidation, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, and suppression of proinflammatory cytokines. Our findings are consistent with other studies investigating the protective effects of the Allium genus against cardio-toxicity. For example, Allium cepa leaves extract has been found to inhibit doxorubicin (DOX)-induced CK-MB and LDH release in serum while increasing GSH in animal models of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, suggesting a potential role in protecting the heart from peroxidative damage. High-dose Allium sativum essential oil demonstrated significant cardioprotective effects against lead-induced toxicity by improving lipid profiles, enhancing NO levels, and modulating inflammatory markers (Rajpoot and Sharma, 2025; Nausheen et al., 2014; Seshadri, 2021).

CTX-induced cardiotoxicity was characterized by marked elevations in TBARS in heart tissue, along with depletion of endogenous antioxidant defenses (SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px). The rise in TBARS indicates enhanced lipid peroxidation and oxidative membrane damage, a well-documented consequence of CTX metabolites and ROS generated during CTX biotransformation. The concomitant decrease in SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities suggests overwhelming oxidative stress and compromised redox buffering capacity in cardiomyocytes (Yousefsani et al., 2024). Pretreatment with AAE substantially mitigated TBARS accumulation and reversed the suppression of antioxidant enzymes. Specifically, AAE elevated SOD activity compared with CTX alone, restored CAT activity toward control levels, and markedly improved GSH-Px function. The concurrent reduction in TBARS and restoration of SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities suggest that AAE both limits oxidative damage and supports the heart's own antioxidant systems. This pattern aligns with a broad-spectrum antioxidant effect, likely mediated by phenolic and flavonoid constituents of AAE, which can scavenge ROS, upregulate endogenous antioxidant systems, and stabilize mitochondrial function (Shirani et al., 2023b; Hassanpour and Doroudi, 2023).

In parallel, CTX caused a significant rise in serum AST, LDH, and CK-MB, classic biomarkers of myocardial injury. The observed attenuation of these enzymes by AAE pretreatment indicates reduced cardiomyocyte membrane leakage and improved cellular integrity (Huang et al., 2024). This protective effect on plasma cardiac markers corroborates the histopathological findings of preserved myocardial architecture in AAE-pretreated groups. Beyond the cardiac tissue, CTX markedly elevated proinflammatory mediators within the heart, including TNF-α and IL-6, with downstream amplification of inflammatory signaling cascades that exacerbate oxidative damage and cell death. AAE pretreatment significantly dampened TNF-α and IL-6 levels, suggesting that anti-inflammatory mechanisms contribute to its cardioprotective effects. Flavonoids and phenolics can inhibit NF-κB signaling, suppress inducible nitric oxide synthase and cyclooxygenase-2 expression, and reduce the recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells. The reduction of TNF-α and IL-6 observed here is consistent with such mechanisms and likely reduces cardiomyocyte apoptosis and necrosis triggered by inflammatory mediators (Al-Khayri et al., 2022; Ekowati et al., 2023). IL-1β dynamics also point to a modulatory effect of AAE on the inflammatory milieu; while CTX elevated IL-1β, pretreatment with AAE significantly decreased IL-1β levels compared with the CTX-only group, indicating broader anti-inflammatory activity that may limit inflammasome activation and downstream caspase–1–mediated of IL-1β (Tong et al., 2022).

Histological evaluation reinforced the biochemical findings. Negative control and AAE-alone groups exhibited intact heart tissue, whereas CTX induced notable cardiac disruptions, including vacuolization and structural disorganization. Pretreatment with AAE prior to CTX preserved normal myocardial architecture and mitigated vacuolization in heart tissue, indicating cross-tissue protective effects and supporting the notion that AAE stabilizes cellular membranes and preserves mitochondrial function. The preservation of cardiomyocyte integrity aligns with reduced leakage enzymes and diminished oxidative damage, providing a coherent link between molecular, biochemical, and histological outcomes.

CTX treatment led to a reduction in body weight, whereas AAE promoted greater body weight gain than controls, highlighting potential systemic benefits of AAE beyond cardioprotection. Notably, the AAE-alone group exhibited a significant increase in body weight compared to the control group (p < 0.001). This observation suggests that AAE may have systemic nutritional or metabolic effects independent of its cardioprotective action. Possible explanations include improved appetite, enhanced nutrient absorption, or reduced basal metabolic stress. However, since body weight is a non-specific parameter, these possibilities require separate investigation in future studies. While body weight is a non-specific readout, its improvement suggests better overall metabolic status and possibly reduced catabolic stress, which may contribute to enhance cardiac resilience (Ferrannini et al., 2023; Salehi et al., 2025).

Mechanistic interpretation and potential pathways can be coherently integrated as follows: The phenolic and flavonoid content of AAE provides direct radical scavenging capacity, enabling electron donation to stabilize reactive species, which reduces lipid peroxidation, preserves membrane integrity, and sustains mitochondrial function, thereby limiting downstream cardiomyocyte injury. By restoring SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities, AAE enhances the heart's intrinsic capacity to neutralize ROS, limiting oxidative damage and improving cellular redox homeostasis. Its anti-inflammatory effects are indicated by inhibition of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), suggesting interference with NF-κB–dependent transcription and inflammasome pathways, which curtail inflammatory cell recruitment, cytokine amplification, and subsequent oxidative damage together with membrane stabilization (Ahmed et al., 2014).

Limitations and future directions

The current study has several limitations. First, the study focuses on a single dose and time point, which limits understanding of the full protective window of AAE. Second, the sample size was relatively small (n = 5 per group), and although statistical analysis confirmed sufficient power, larger cohorts would further strengthen the findings. Third, the AAE extract was not chemically standardized, and the major bioactive compounds were not identified using HPLC or LC-MS. To address these limitations, future studies should include dose–response studies and kinetic analyses of biomarkers to define the optimal dosing regimen, validation in larger cohorts (n = 8–10), and identification and quantification of specific phenolic and flavonoid constituents using HPLC or LC-MS to enable standardization and reproducibility. Additionally, to strengthen causal inferences regarding the pathways through which AAE exerts its effects, future work should include molecular mechanistic studies at the level of signaling pathways, such as NF-κB–dependent transcription, Nrf2 pathway activation, and apoptosis markers including caspase-3 and Bcl-2 family regulation, together with in vitro antioxidant assays to clarify which components drive the observed protection.

Conclusion

Collectively, these findings suggest that AAE is associated with multi-targeted protective effects including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective actions, thereby attenuating CTX-induced cardiotoxicity in this preclinical model. These findings position the A. ampeloprasum as a promising nutraceutical adjunct for further investigation. However, as no tumor model or anticancer efficacy experiment was performed, we cannot comment on whether AAE interferes with CTX's antitumor activity. Further work should characterize bioactive constituents, assess pharmacokinetics and potential interactions with CTX, directly evaluate signaling pathways such as NF-κB and Nrf2, and explore translational applicability in tumor-bearing models and clinical settings.
