Functional analyses identify the L1 ORF2p Cryptic domain as a hub for intramolecular interfaces required for retrotransposition
Authors: Afzaal M Shareef, Guizi Chen, Claiborne M Christian, Benjamin Freeman, Dawn LaCoste, Shuaihua Gao, Edgar Africano Camargo, Jude N Ubi, Victoria P Belancio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndonuclease (EN) and reverse transcriptase (RT) functions of Long Interspersed Element-1 (L1) ORF2p are required for DNA-nicking and complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis during retrotransposition. These domains are homologous to cellular/viral proteins and are well-studied. The ORF2p Cryptic region has no known homologues, is evolutionarily conserved among L1-like elements, and is required for retrotransposition through an unknown mechanism. While ORF2p structures establish Cryptic interactions with the EN, RT, and C-terminal domains (CTD), functional importance of these interactions remains unclear. We examine functional contributions of these intramolecular interactions in retrotransposition through mutagenesis, co-immunoprecipitation, cDNA synthesis, RNA binding, retrotransposition, cytotoxicity, and molecular modeling analyses. We demonstrate that Cryptic functions as an independent domain capable of reestablishing functional intramolecular interactions in trans to rescue cDNA synthesis. The Cryptic:RT interface is required for cDNA initiation/elongation and may also affect EN function in retrotransposition, while eliminating ORF2p toxicity. In contrast, the Cryptic:CTD interface is not required for first strand cDNA synthesis but affects retrotransposition and increases ORF2p toxicity. Overall, intramolecular interactions involving Cryptic are required for multiple steps of retrotransposition and propose Cryptic as a nonenzymatic structural domain and a promising target for therapeutic interventions in cancers with upregulated L1 expression.

Introduction

The endogenous Long Interspersed Element-1 (L1) is the only retroelement in the human genome capable of autonomous retrotransposition, a multistep process of self-amplification within the host genome [1–6]. This process begins with the transcription of full-length L1 loci, followed by the translation of the resulting messenger RNA (mRNA) into two essential proteins: ORF1p and ORF2p [3, 4, 7–11]. ORF1p is an RNA-binding protein possessing nucleic acid chaperone properties [10, 12–16]. ORF2p is a multifunctional protein containing endonuclease (EN) [3, 17] and reverse transcriptase (RT) [18–20] activities, necessary for generating new L1, SINE (Alu), and SVA inserts [4, 21–24]. Studies in cultured cells and pan-cancer genomic analyses connected L1 ORF2p enzymatic functions with large genomic deletions, structural rearrangements, and translocations [25–32]. When overexpressed, L1 induces DNA damage response, leading to apoptosis or senescence [25, 27, 33–35]. Additionally, L1 ORF2p RT has been shown to trigger inflammatory and DNA damage responses in cultured cells [25, 36]. The consequences of L1 expression have far-reaching effects on key biological processes such as evolution and human diseases [2, 26, 37–48]. L1 contribution to sporadic genetic diseases and cancer is well-documented [26, 45, 48–54] establishing it as a significant endogenous mutagen. Mounting experimental evidence links adverse effects of L1 expression to normal and pathological aging, age-related neurodegeneration, and autoimmune diseases [34, 36, 55–58], all of which underscore its potential broad impact on human health.

The key sequential steps of mammalian L1 integration were originally deduced from biochemical studies of the Bombyx mori R2 element [59–69]. In this model, EN nicks double stranded DNA, which creates a single stranded DNA that serves as a primer for RT-mediated complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, initiating a process known as target primed reverse transcription (TPRT) [62]. The TPRT is followed by the integration of a new L1 copy in the host genome, which is both aided and suppressed by host DNA repair in mammals [27, 70–76]. First strand cDNA synthesis step of the TPRT process reported for R2 is captured in the L1 element amplification protocol (LEAP) assay performed using cellular fraction enriched for L1 RNP formed by transfected mammalian L1 [9, 10, 20, 77]. Despite the conservation of the RTs of R2 ORF protein and mammalian L1 ORF2p, their protein structure and domain arrangements differ significantly [65, 78–80]. Therefore, species-specific knowledge about L1 ORF2p function in mammalian L1 retrotransposition cannot be extracted from the R2 ORFp studies. Recently, L1 ORF2p nicking and first and second strand cDNA synthesis steps of the TPRT process have been confirmed using purified full-length human ORF2p [78–81]. The resolution of the full-length human L1 ORF2p structure identified multiple intramolecular interactions between the well-studied EN and RT enzymatic domains and other nonenzymatic ORF2p sequences [78–80]. Some of these interactions are observed to be dynamic [78–80] but their impact on the ORF2p function in retrotransposition remains unknown.

Outside the functional EN (aa 1–239) [3] and RT (aa 505–1067) domains [78, 82], L1 ORF2p contains three additional annotated sequences (Fig. 1A) [3, 4, 18, 19]. These include the Cryptic region (aa 240–380) [83, 84], positioned adjacent to the C-terminus of the EN domain, the Z domain (aa 381–440) [19, 85], located adjacent to the C-terminus of Cryptic, and the C-terminal domain (CTD; aa 1068–1275) [4, 78, 86, 87] (Fig. 1A). Bipartile Alu retrotransposition studies identified specific amino acids within the Cryptic region that are required for Alu retrotransposition and determined that co-expression of two ORF2p protein fragments reestablish ORF2p specific intramolecular interactions to perform ORF2p-driven retrotransposition [83]. A follow up tri-alanine mutational analysis of ORF2p reaffirmed that these three regions harbor amino acids crucial for L1 retrotransposition [88]. The significance of these regions for retrotransposition and the potential need for their cooperation in this process is further supported by findings that certain human:mouse ORF2p chimeras lose their ability to retrotranspose [89].

The structure of the full-length ORF2p shows that Cryptic (a part of the region recently referred to as EN linker or Tower, Fig. 1A) consists of three alpha helices folded to interact with the RT, EN, and CTD domains [78] (Fig. 1B). Two alternative ORF2p structures, described as open and closed conformations, are observed using electron microscopy [80]. In the open conformation, Cryptic interacts with the EN domain, while in the closed conformation, Cryptic interacts with the CTD domain [80]. Published studies of EN-Cryptic protein fragments determined that Cryptic suppresses EN-induced DNA damage response [25] and cytotoxicity [33, 84], supporting that Cryptic may function to modulate the timing of the EN activity during retrotransposition. Collectively, these studies suggest a central role of Cryptic in intramolecular ORF2p interactions that are critical to its function in retrotransposition. While these studies are informative, most of the approaches express ORF2p as fragments or perform in vitro analyses [78, 80, 83, 84]. Therefore, further investigation using full-length ORF2p is needed to determine the functional impact of Cryptic intramolecular interactions on ORF2p-driven retrotransposition within the mammalian environment.

Building on these findings, we investigate the functional significance of the ORF2p intramolecular interactions in mammalian cells that model complex host environment and involve an intact RNA template absent in in vitro approaches [78, 80, 83, 84]. In this study, we focus on defining functional contributions of interfaces formed by intramolecular Cryptic interactions with the EN, RT, and CTD domains to the full length ORF2p-driven retrotransposition. By integrating outcomes of retrotransposition, RNA binding, co-immunoprecipitation, cDNA synthesis, and cytotoxicity assays performed in mammalian cells, and by modeling of ORF2p molecular dynamics, we determine that Cryptic, which sequence retains significant conservation beyond mammalian L1s [83], is involved in multiple steps of retrotransposition carried out by the full-length mammalian ORF2p. By performing trans-complementation assays, we demonstrate that Cryptic functions as an independent domain of ORF2p that can reestablish functional intramolecular interactions in trans to support ORF2p-driven initiation/elongation of cDNA synthesis. We further demonstrate that mutations altering Cryptic mediated intramolecular interactions not only affect different steps of retrotransposition, but also modulate ORF2p toxicity, resulting in ORF2p variants with similar levels of retrotransposition but significantly different toxicity. Overall, our results establish that intramolecular ORF2p interactions involving Cryptic region are as important for the ORF2p function as its intact enzymatic activities.

Materials and methods

Plasmids

The codon-optimized ORF2 constructs expressing the full-length wildtype ORF2p (untagged or N-terminally Gal4 tagged) and ORF2p variants [F273A F274A (FF:AA) and W288A D289A (WD:AA)] and ORF2p variants with CTD truncations (1262Δ,1249Δ, 1129Δ, and 950Δ, where Δ denotes truncation of the C-terminal end of ORF2p) were previously reported [86]. The pcDNA 3.1/Hygro (+) plasmids expressing full-length ORF2p variants [S364A (S:A), F367A E368A (FE:AA), I370A N371A K372A (INK:AAA), and EN (- -) WD:AA (mutation of the EN active site in the WD:AA ORF2p expressing plasmid)] were generated using QuikChange XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis (Agilient, Cat #200 517). pcDNA 3.1/Hygro (+) plamids expressing the ORF2p FF:AA, WD:AA, S:A, FE:AA, INK:AAA variants with either the EN active site mutated (D205A, H230A) or the RT active site mutated (D702A) were generated by Genscript through site-directed mutagenesis. pcDNA 3.1/Hygro (+) plasmids expressing C-terminally 3xFLAG tagged ORF2p variants (FF:AA, and WD:AA) were also generated using QuikChange XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis (Agilient, Cat #200 517).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Two million HeLa cells per T-75 flask per sample were seeded 16–18 h prior to transfection. Cells were transfected with 3 µg of pcDNA plasmid expressing 3x-FLAG tagged Cryptic (aa 240–380) and 3 µg of pcDNA plasmid expressing T7 tagged EN (aa 1–239), Z–RT (aa 381–773), CTD (aa 900–1275), or CTD 1210Δ (900–1210) using 12 μL of PLUS reagent and 24 μL of Lipofectamine (Invitrogen). pcDNA plasmid (Cat #V87020) was used as a control and filler to keep the same amount of DNA in each transfection reaction. Cells were harvested 24 h post transfection in 500 μL lysis buffer [50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.5% Triton X, pH 7.2] containing 5 μL each of phosphatase inhibitors 2 and 3 (Sigma) and HALT protease inhibitor (Fisher, Cat #78 410). Cell lysates were sonicated three times for 6 s at a 10 root mean square (RMS) frequency using the Microson XL 2000 (Misonix) sonicator and the insoluble fraction was separated by centrifugation for 15 min at 15 000 rpm. Total protein concentrations in the soluble cell lysate were measured using Bradford assay (Bio-Rad). Then, 500 µg of total protein for each sample was incubated with 40 μL of 50% M2-FLAG agarose beads slurry (Sigma) at 4⁰C overnight. The beads were washed three times using wash buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X, pH 7.4). The protein was eluted in sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) buffer with β-mercaptoethanol and samples were run on acrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, Cat #4561093EDU) and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane using the iBlot 2 (Invitrogen). The membrane was probed with T7 (Cat #D9E1X) and FLAG (Cat #2368S) primary antibodies for detection of T7 and FLAG tagged proteins and secondary rabbit horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated antibody (Santa Cruz, Cat #sc-2357). Western blots were subject to chemiluminescence (Bio-Rad) for visualization. Band (corresponding to individual proteins) and background intensities were measured using Image Lab (Bio-Rad). In each sample supporting FLAG-tagged Cryptic expression, quantification was performed using intensity of both bands. Background was subtracted from the intensity of values of every band. The resulting values were used to determine the ratio of T7 tagged proteins to FLAG tagged Cryptic. For each condition, the intensity value was divided by that of the corresponding co-immunoprecipitated Cryptic fragment (WT, FF:AA, WD:AA, S:A, FE:AA, or INK:AAA). This yielded a ratio reflecting the amount of ORF2p fragment bound per unit of Cryptic fragment. To allow comparison across conditions, all ratios were subsequently normalized to the ratio obtained with the wildtype Cryptic fragment (WT = 1). Statistical significance was determined using t-test for unpaired samples (n = 3, n = 5 for WD:AA and FF:AA Cryptic with CTD) function in Prism to compare each experimental sample to the wildtype control. Statistical analyses report P <.05. Only significant differences are shown in figures. Standard deviation was determined using Prism.

Alu retrotransposition and complementation assays

Alu retrotransposition assay was adapted from protocol as previously described [21]. A total 500 000 HeLa cells per T-75 flask per sample were seeded 16–18 h prior to transfection. Then, 400 ng of indicated ORF2 expression plasmid was co-transfected with 400 ng of either empty control plasmid or Cryptic or EN Cryptic expression plasmid and 400 ng of the Alu retrotransposition reporter construct, containing a Neomycin cassette, using 6 μL Plus reagent (Invitrogen) in 200 μL Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 8 μL Lipofectamine reagent (Invitrogen) in 92 μL DMEM. Cell culture media was supplemented with 0.45 mg/ml G418 ∼24 h post transfection. Colonies were stained with crystal violet solution (0.2% crystal violet, 5% acetic acid, 2.5% isopropanol) after 2 weeks of G418 selection and counted. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test for unpaired samples (n = 3) and each sample was compared to indicated control. Statistical analyses report P <.05. Only significant differences are shown in figures. Standard deviation was determined using Prism.

RNA immunoprecipitation assay

Two million HeLa cells per T-75 flask per sample were seeded 16–18 h prior to transfection. The cells were transfected with 6 µg of pcDNA plasmid expressing ORF2 protein using 12 μL of PLUS reagent and 24 μL of Lipofectamine (Invitrogen). Cells were harvested 24 h post transfection in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton-X, pH 7.2) containing protease inhibitors and RNasin Plus (Promega). Cell lysates were sonicated three times for 6 s using the Microson XL 2000 (Misonix) sonicator and the insoluble fraction was separated by centrifugation for 15 min at 15 000 rpm. The total protein in the soluble cell lysate was measured using Bradford assay (Biorad). Then, 500 µg–1 mg of total protein for each sample was incubated with 40 μL of 50% M2-FLAG agarose beads slurry (Sigma) overnight. The beads were washed three times using wash buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton-X, pH 7.4). The bead–antibody–protein complex was then subject to DNase treatment, and the mRNA was eluted from the protein by incubating the complex at 75°C for 10 min. cDNA was generated using ∼50 ng of eluted mRNA, the Avian Myeloblastosis Virus (AMV) RT-PCR kit (Promega, Cat #A3500) and an Oligo dT 30 primer (5′-TTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTT-3′). The cDNA was used in PCR with Oligo dT 30 primer and ORF2 forward primer (5′-ATCAAGAAGATGTGGCAC-3′). The PCR product was run on a 2% agarose gel for visualization. DNA bands were extracted from the gel and purified using a gel extraction kit (Qiagen). Purified DNA was sequenced for validation (ElimBio).

L1 element amplification protocol (LEAP)

LEAP was adapted from protocol as previously described [20, 77]. Two million HeLa cells per T-75 flask with two flasks per sample were seeded 16–18 h prior to transfection. The cells were transfected with 4.5 μg ORF2p indicated expression plasmids or Z–RT-CTD expression plasmid and co-transfected with either 0.5 μg of empty plasmid or indicated Cryptic expression plasmid (trans-complementation experiments) using 12 μL of PLUS reagent and 24 μL of Lipofectamine (Invitrogen). Cells were washed three times with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and harvested in 5 ml prechilled PBS. Cell suspensions were centrifuged at 6000 RPM for 5 min at 4⁰C and supernatant removed. Resulting cell pellets from two flasks for each sample were combined and lysed in 500 μL of LEAP lysis buffer (1.5 mM KCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM Tris–Cl, 1% deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, EDTA, and RNAsin), incubated on ice for 5 min, and centrifuged for 15 min to remove debris. Supernatant was collected and layered on top of an 8.5% sucrose cushion that was layered on top of a 17% sucrose cushion. Samples were centrifuged at 36 500 RPM for 2 h at 4⁰C. Supernatant was removed and the resulting pellet was re-suspended in 100 μL of deionized water supplemented with 10 μL HALT protease inhibitor (Fisher) and 2 μL RNAsin (Promega). Protein concentration was determined using Bradford assay and samples were brought to equal protein concentrations using water and then were brought to a final concentration of 25% glycerol. ORF2p LEAP reaction was performed using equal amounts of protein (100–500 ng) in the final reaction with 0.4 μM LEAP primer shown below.

Molecular dynamics modeling of ORF2p variants

The initial atomic structure of the wildtype protein was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank [78], which contains the experimentally resolved coordinates of the complex. Mutant variants were generated using AlphaFold 3 [90], which predicts full-length protein structures with high accuracy. The resulting mutant structures were visually inspected and compared against the wildtype to verify folding integrity and active-site geometry. Structural alignments were carried out in PyMOL [96], which allowed superposition of wildtype and mutant backbones prior to alignment with the RNA structure, ensuring consistency in subsequent comparative analyses. All-atom MD simulations were performed with GROMACS 2021.4 [97–100]. Separate systems were built for apo proteins and for protein–RNA complexes. For each system, three independent replicates were generated, each constructed and equilibrated separately to ensure statistical robustness. The simulations used the AMBER99SB-ILDN [101] force field, which provides refined backbone and side-chain torsional parameters, together with the SPC/E explicit water model [102] to represent solvent. Each system was solvated in a cubic box with periodic boundary conditions, and counterions (Na⁺, Cl−) were added to neutralize the net charge. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm to remove steric clashes and relax unfavorable contacts. Equilibration proceeded in two stages: heating from 0 to 300 K under an NVT ensemble with the V-rescale thermostat [103], a stochastic velocity rescaling method that improves canonical sampling, followed by pressure equilibration at 1 bar under an NPT ensemble using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat [104]. Production simulations of 10 ns were then run for each replicate using the leapfrog integrator with a 2 fs timestep. Linear Constraint Solver (LINCS) [105] constraints were applied to fix bond lengths, and Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) [106] electrostatics were employed with a 1.0 nm cutoff for short-range interactions. Backbone atoms were used for least-squares fitting to compute root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) values. Distances between residues of interest were quantified by measuring Cα–Cα separations. Binding free energy was estimated using gmx_MMPBSA (version 1.6.4) [107, 108] across all three replicates. The method decomposed free energy into van der Waals (ΔEvdW), electrostatic (ΔEele), and internal (ΔEint) terms using AMBER99SB [109] parameters, with solvation contributions calculated via the OBC2 generalized Born model [110] (igb = 5) for the polar component and SASA/LCPO with surface tension γ = 0.0072 kcal·mol⁻¹·Å⁻² (probe radius 1.4 Å) [111, 112] for the nonpolar component. These calculations provided a detailed energetic comparison between wildtype and mutant proteins in both apo and RNA-bound states. Statistical significance was determined using Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for unpaired samples (n = 3) function in Prism to compare overall RMSD and RMSF distributions of each variant to the wildtype control. Statistical analyses report P <.05. Only significant differences are shown in figures.

Results

Cryptic is an independent structural domain involved in functional ORF2p intramolecular interactions

To assess whether interfaces formed by Cryptic (aa 240–380) with the rest of the ORF2p protein are required for ORF2p-mediated cDNA synthesis, we conducted a LEAP assay [20, 77] that detects RT-mediated cDNA synthesis (Fig. 1C). L1 ribonucleoprotein complexes (RNPs) were enriched from HeLa cells that were transiently co-transfected with a plasmid expressing a Z–RT-CTD fragment (aa 349–1275) of the ORF2p and a plasmid expressing either wildtype or mutant Cryptic fragments (Fig. 1C). The Cryptic variants FF:AA (aa 273, 274) or WD:AA (aa 288, 289) were used [83]. The PCR step of the LEAP analysis is performed using R0 and F1 PCR primers, which are expected to generate a 500 bp product (Fig. 1C). This experimental approach shows that the full-length ORF2p generates the expected 500 bp product (Fig. 1C, lane 4). In contrast, the Z–RT-CTD protein is unable to generate the 500 bp fragment when expressed alone (Fig. 1C, lane 5). The Z–RT-CTD defect in cDNA synthesis is rescued by the co-expression of the wildtype Cryptic fragment (Fig. 1C, lane 6). FF:AA or WD:AA Cryptic variant fragments do not restore the Z–RT-CTD defect in cDNA synthesis under the same experimental conditions (Fig. 1C, lanes 7 and 8). The protein expression levels were assessed using western blot (Fig. 1C, western blot panel). These findings demonstrate that not only is Cryptic required for the Z–RT-CTD to perform cDNA synthesis, but it functions as an independent structural unit capable of performing its function in trans. This also shows that when expressed as a protein fragment Cryptic region establishes interactions with other ORF2p domains required for the ORF2p-mediated cDNA synthesis in mammalian cells. This result identifies Cryptic (aa 240–380) as an ORF2p domain required for cDNA synthesis independent of other defined ORF2p domains.

The structure of the full-length ORF2p established Cryptic as three alpha helices folded in a “propeller”-like shape interacting with the enzymatic EN and RT domains and the Z and CTD (Fig. 1B) [78, 80]. The functional consequences of these intramolecular interactions in the mammalian environment are currently not known. To test whether these intramolecular interactions can be captured experimentally in mammalian cells, we performed a co-IP using lysates from HeLa cells transiently co-transfected with plasmids expressing 3x-FLAG tagged Cryptic and either an EN (aa 1–239, with active site mutations at D205A, H230A), RT (aa 511–773), Z–RT (aa 381–773), or CTD (aa 774–1275) domains tagged with a T7-tag. FLAG tagged protein complexes from these lysates were enriched using FLAG antibody-conjugated beads as previously described [92]. Western blot analyses of the co-IP samples using FLAG- and T7-specific antibodies show that Cryptic interacts with the EN, RT, Z–RT, and CTD domains of ORF2p (Supplementary Figs 1 and 2). Supporting these findings, AlphaFold predictions show that these ORF2p fragments fold similarly to the full-length structure (Supplementary Fig. 3). RNase treatment of co-IP lysates shows that interactions between Cryptic and the EN, Z–RT, and CTD domains appear to be independent of RNA (Supplementary Fig. 4). Taken together, these results confirm that Cryptic forms intramolecular interfaces with the EN, Z–RT, and CTD domains consistent with those observed in the reported full-length ORF2p structure [78].

Mutations of Cryptic sequence affect its interactions with the Z–RT and CTD domains

Given that the Cryptic domain reestablishes intramolecular interactions required for the RT function of the Z–RT-CTD fragment in trans (Fig. 1C), we reasoned that specific amino acid residues within Cryptic are necessary to form intramolecular interactions with the Z–RT and CTD domains. Based on the full-length ORF2p structure [78], F273 is important for interaction between two alpha helices of Cryptic and F274 interacts with the F491 residue in the Z domain (Fig. 2A). The W288 and D289 residues interact with the D539 and S508 residues in the RT domain (Fig. 2A). To determine whether the amino acids F274, F273, W288, and D289 are required for Cryptic interaction with Z–RT, we performed a co-IP using lysates from HeLa cells transiently co-transfected with a plasmid expressing a T7-tagged Z–RT fragment and a plasmid expressing either 3x-FLAG tagged wildtype, FF:AA, WD:AA, or FF:AA WD:AA Cryptic variants (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 5). FLAG tagged protein complexes from these lysates were enriched using beads that were conjugated to FLAG antibodies. For each sample, ORF2p fragment (EN, ZRT, or CTD) was quantified relative to the corresponding co-immunoprecipitated Cryptic fragment (WT, FF:AA, or WD:AA). The resulting ratio between these signals was then normalized to the wildtype ratio for comparison across samples. Western blot analyses of the co-IP samples using FLAG and T7 specific antibodies show that there is a significant decrease in the interaction between the Z–RT and Cryptic protein fragments when FF, WD, or all four residues are mutated to alanine (P <.01 for all significant comparisons, Student’s t-test, Fig. 2B). The effect of these Cryptic mutations on its interaction with the EN and CTD is less disruptive. Only the FF:AA mutation significantly reduces Cryptic interaction with the EN domain (P <.01, Student’s t-test, Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 6). The interaction of the other two Cryptic variants with the EN domain trended lower than the wildtype but did not reach statistical significance. The FF:AA and WD:AA Cryptic variants interact with the CTD as efficiently as the wildtype Cryptic (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 7). Only the Cryptic variant containing both the FF and WD mutations interacts significantly less efficiently with the CTD (P <.001, Student’s t-test, Fig. 2D). These results establish that amino acids F273, F274, W288, and D289 contribute to Cryptic interaction with Z–RT in mammalian cells, which is consistent with the intramolecular interactions observed in the ORF2p structure [78, 80]. These results also show that perturbations of the Cryptic interaction with Z–RT may affect interfaces Cryptic forms with EN and CTD domains, although to a lesser extent and with yet unknown functional outcomes.

Based on the full-length ORF2p structure [78], Cryptic amino acids S364, F367, E368, I370, N371, and K372 interact with amino acids H1225, M1229, A1233, S1097, and S1098 within the CTD [78] (Fig. 3A) to form the Cryptic:CTD interface. To determine whether mutations of these amino acids affect Cryptic interaction with the CTD, we performed co-IP using lysates from HeLa cells transiently co-transfected with a plasmid expressing a T7-tagged CTD fragment and a plasmid expressing either 3x-FLAG tagged wildtype, S:A, FE:AA, or INK:AAA Cryptic variants. Immunoprecipitation of the FLAG tagged constructs shows that there is a significant increase in Cryptic interaction with CTD when the S364 residue is mutated to an alanine (Supplementary Fig. 8C). However, the FE:AA and INK:AAA Cryptic mutations do not significantly disrupt its interaction with the CTD domain, as no statistically significant differences were observed compared with the control (P >.05, Student’s t-test, Supplementary Fig. 8), although a down trend is observed with the FE:AA variant. There was also no effect of these Cryptic mutations on its interaction with the EN and Z–RT (Supplementary Figs 9 and 10). These results are consistent with redundancy in the interaction of Cryptic residues with the CTD as other amino acids contribute to the Cryptic CTD interaction [78]. Building on this result, we implemented a truncation-based all-or-nothing approach. We generated a CTD 1210Δ construct containing a truncated T7-tagged CTD sequence, which is missing sequences corresponding to aa 1211–1275, to test its interaction with the 3x-FLAG tagged wildtype Cryptic using co-IP (Fig. 3B). Western blot analysis shows a significant decrease in the interaction between wildtype Cryptic and the truncated CTD compared to the wildtype Cryptic interaction with the wildtype CTD (Fig. 3B lanes 11 and 12, and Fig. 3C, P <.01, Student’s t-test). These results show that there is redundancy in the Cryptic interaction with CTD, which prevented significant perturbation of the Cryptic:CTD interface by individual mutations. Our results also show that truncation of the CTD eliminates this redundancy and disrupts the Cryptic:CTD interface which is consistent with the Cryptic:CTD interaction observed in the full-length ORF2p structure [78].

Mutations in the Cryptic Z–RT and CTD interfaces differentially affect ORF2p-driven retrotransposition and its trans-complementation by Cryptic

Our co-immunoprecipitation results establish that specific amino acids within Cryptic impact Cryptic:Z–RT and Cryptic:CTD interfaces (Figs 2 and 3). Given these results, we assessed whether mutation of amino acids involved in the intramolecular interactions in the full-length ORF2p affect retrotransposition. Plasmids expressing either wildtype ORF2p or ORF2p variants (FF:AA, WD:AA, S:A, FE:AA, and INK:AAA) are co-transfected in HeLa cells with a plasmid expressing an Alu-Neo-cassette as previously described (Supplementary Fig. 11) [21]. Cells harboring de novo Alu integrations are selected using Neomycin (G418) containing media. Among the Cryptic:Z–RT interface variants, the FF:AA variant exhibited a three-fold reduction in retrotransposition compared to the wildtype ORF2p (P <.01, Student’s t-test, Fig. 4A, lanes 2 and 3). In contrast, the WD:AA variant showed a 20-fold decrease in retrotransposition relative to the wildtype ORF2p (P <.001, Student’s t-test, Fig. 4A, lanes 2 and 5). Retrotransposition of the WD:AA variant was reduced seven-fold compared to the FF:AA variant (P <.001, Student’s t-test, Fig. 4A, lanes 3 and 5). These results show that the Cryptic:Z–RT interface is important for retrotransposition and that specific mutations of the Cryptic:Z–RT interface differentially affect retrotransposition.

While mutations in the Cryptic:CTD interface had no significant effect on their interaction, we tested whether the ORF2p variants with the S:A, FE:AA, and INK:AAA mutations influence retrotransposition. The S:A ORF2p variant does not affect retrotransposition when compared to the wildtype ORF2p (Fig. 4B, lanes 2 and 3). In contrast, the FE:AA variant shows a two-fold reduction in retrotransposition relative to wildtype ORF2p (P <.05, Student’s t-test, Fig. 4B, lanes 2 and 5). The INK:AAA variant supports six-fold less retrotransposition than wildtype ORF2p (P <.01, Student’s t-test, Fig. 4B, lanes 2 and 7) and exhibits a four-fold reduction in retrotransposition compared to the FE:AA variant (P <.01, Student’s t-test, Fig. 4B, lanes 5 and 7). Together with our co-immunoprecipitation results, these findings show that despite having no quantifiable effect on the interaction between Cryptic and the CTD domain, the FE:AA and INK:AAA variants have a significant impact on ORF2p-driven retrotransposition. Additionally, these results show that specific mutations within the Cryptic:CTD interface differentially affect retrotransposition.

Our LEAP results show that Cryptic interacts with Z–RT-CTD in trans and is required for the Z–RT-CTD mediated cDNA synthesis. To determine whether Cryptic can restore or interfere with the Cryptic:Z–RT and Cryptic:CTD interfaces in trans, we co-expressed wildtype Cryptic with the full-length ORF2p FF:AA, WD:AA, S:A, FE:AA, INK:AAA variants and tested them in the retrotransposition assay. Co-expression of wildtype Cryptic significantly, but only partially, increased Alu retrotransposition driven by the WD:AA variant (two-fold increase; Fig. 4A, lanes 5 and 6) and did not increase or interfere with Alu mobilization supported by other ORF2p variants (Fig. 4A, lanes 3 and 4 and Fig. 4B, lanes 3–8). We confirmed that co-expression of the wildtype Cryptic also partially rescues retrotransposition of the Neo-cassette-tagged L1 containing WD:AA mutations in the ORF2p sequence (two-fold increase, P <.05, Student’s t-test, Supplementary Fig. 12). Our data also show that co-expression of the Cryptic FF:AA variant does not restore retrotransposition by the WD:AA ORF2p (Supplementary Fig. 13). These results demonstrate that when expressed in trans, Cryptic reestablishes intramolecular interactions with the RT within the defective ORF2p WD:AA variant, allowing partial restoration of retrotransposition. This functional Cryptic ORF2p interaction is only observed with the WD:AA ORF2p variant, as expression of Cryptic in trans neither inhibited nor improved retrotransposition of the FF:AA, S:A, FE:AA, or INK:AAA ORF2p variants. These findings show that the intramolecular interactions formed by these variants are favored over trans-Cryptic effects.

ORF2p containing mutations in the Cryptic:Z–RT interface retains mRNA binding in mammalian cells

ORF2p-mediated cDNA synthesis is required for retrotransposition and is a multistep process that involves mRNA binding, initiation, and elongation. Our results thus far establish sequence requirements for the Cryptic interactions with the EN, Z–RT, and CTD and the initial understanding of the interdependence of these interactions (Figs 2 and 3). Additionally, our results show that the FF:AA and WD:AA ORF2p variants have retrotransposition defects compared to wildtype ORF2p (Fig. 4A). Based on these results, we assessed whether the defect in retrotransposition of FF:AA or WD:AA variants is due to their ability to bind ORF2 mRNA. To assess this possibility, we performed an RNA binding assay. Plasmids expressing 3x-FLAG tagged wildtype, FF:AA, and WD:AA ORF2p were transiently transfected in HeLa cells, expressed proteins were immunoprecipitated using beads conjugated to FLAG antibodies. Bound ORF2p complexes were then eluted from the beads, and the resulting fraction was assessed by western blot to confirm the presence of the wildtype and mutant ORF2p in the eluted fractions (Fig. 5B). This approach determined that all proteins are present in the eluted fraction (Fig. 5B). Using equal amounts of the input based on the protein concentrations, we performed RT-PCR to detect the presence of the ORF2 mRNA in the eluted fractions (Fig. 5C). Codon optimized ORF2 sequence was used for this experiment to distinguish transiently expressed experimental mRNA from endogenous L1 mRNA. Gal4 tagged ORF2p expression plasmid was used as control for FLAG tag selection specificity (Fig. 5C, lane 4). The expected size RT-PCR products are observed only in the RT-PCR reactions containing 3x-FLAG tagged ORF2p and show that the wildtype and ORF2p variant proteins bind mRNA in this assay (Fig. 5C, lanes 6, 8, and 10). Sequencing confirmed that this product corresponds to the codon optimized ORF2 sequence expected to be amplified in this assay (Supplementary Fig. 14). This result demonstrates that similar to the wildtype ORF2p the FF:AA and WD:AA ORF2p variants retain the ability to bind their respective mRNA in mammalian cells. While this result does not distinguish between functional and nonfunctional ORF2p mRNA interactions, it justifies testing whether the WD and FF residues are critical to the cDNA synthesis steps downstream of mRNA binding.

WD:AA and FF:AA mutations in the Cryptic:Z–RT interface affect cDNA initiation and/or elongation steps

Given that the FF:AA and WD:AA ORF2p variants are defective in retrotransposition (Fig. 4) but not mRNA binding (Fig. 5), we assessed the effect of WD:AA or FF:AA mutations on the ORF2p mediated cDNA synthesis by performing a LEAP assay [20, 77]. ORF2p RNPs were isolated from HeLa cells, transfected with the full-length wildtype, FF:AA, or WD:AA ORF2p expression plasmids as previously described [83, 89]. The RNPs were used in an in vitro cDNA synthesis reaction as described (Fig. 1C). The resulting cDNA was used for PCR with R0 and F1 PCR primers that amplify a 500 bp region of the ORF2 sequence [Fig. 6, F1 (500) panel]. Sequencing of the LEAP PCR products and the mRNA control confirmed that recovered products are generated using ORF2 mRNA as a template (Supplementary Fig. 14). This approach shows that the FF:AA ORF2p makes 500 bp cDNA products as efficiently as the wildtype ORF2p (Fig. 6, lanes 4 and 5). In contrast, the WD:AA ORF2p is unable to generate cDNA of this size (Fig. 6, lane 6). This result establishes that the WD:AA ORF2p is defective in either initiation and/or elongation steps of cDNA synthesis, while the FF:AA ORF2p is as efficient as the wildtype ORF2p in making 500 bp cDNA.

The defect of the FF:AA ORF2p in retrotransposition (Fig. 4) and the inability of the FF:AA Cryptic to rescue cDNA synthesis of the Z–RT-CTD fragment (Fig. 1C), prompted us to assess the ability of the FF:AA ORF2p to generate longer cDNA products. We used PCR primers F2, F3, and F4 that are complementary to ORF2p sequences 5′ of the F1 primer. These primer pairs are expected to generate 600, 700, and 800 bp PCR products, respectively (Fig. 6B). This approach shows that the FF:AA ORF2p variant appears to be less efficient than the wildtype ORF2p at producing 600, 700, and 800 bp cDNA products (Fig. 6C, lanes 4 and 5). Quantification of 700 bp and 500 bp LEAP products generated by the wildtype ORF2p and the FF:AA variant (n = 3) shows that the FF:AA variant is significantly less efficient at producing longer cDNA compared to the wildtype ORF2p (P <.001, Student’s t-test, Fig. 6D). Taken together with our co-IP analysis and retrotransposition data, these results are consistent with Cryptic WD and FF residues interaction with the Z–RT domains of ORF2p being important for the ORF2p function in initiation and/or elongation of cDNA synthesis. However, the individual effects of these mutations on these steps vary drastically, consistent with their performance in retrotransposition (Fig. 4A).

To assess whether Cryptic interaction with the CTD contributes to cDNA synthesis in mammalian cells using an ORF2 mRNA template, we tested the ORF2p variants containing S:A, FE:AA, INK:AAA mutations in the LEAP assay. Using primers F1 and F3, which are expected to generate 500 and 700 bp PCR products, respectively, we show that the S:A, FE:AA, INK:AAA ORF2p variants do not impact cDNA synthesis (Fig. 7D, lanes 4–7). In fact, relative to the ORF2p expression levels (Fig. 7C) and input RNA (Fig. 7D + AMV) S:A and INK:AAA ORF2p variants generate cDNA levels comparable to those of the wild type ORF2p (Fig. 7, lanes 4, 5, and 7). Using LEAP assay, we then tested the ability of the ORF2p variants containing CTD truncations to carry out cDNA synthesis using F5 and F6 PCR primers designed to amplify the resulting cDNA. These primers are expected to generate 1200 and 1500 bp PCR products for full-length ORF2p, respectively. The F6 primer was used to detect cDNA generated from full-length ORF2p and all truncated ORF2p variants (Fig. 8A). When compared to the full length ORF2p, the ORF2p variants 1262Δ, 1249Δ, and 1129Δ containing CTD truncations do not show any defect in cDNA synthesis (Fig. 8D, lanes 4–7), whereas ORF2p 950Δ containing the largest C-terminal truncation, which includes part of the RT sequence, is incapable of cDNA synthesis (Fig. 8D, lane 8). The robust signal from the 1129Δ variant likely reflects higher amplification efficiency of its smaller amplicon compared with those of the other variants under the same RT-PCR conditions. These findings show that point mutations within Cryptic and truncations of the CTD, disrupting the Cryptic:CTD interface, have no effect on first strand cDNA synthesis in mammalian cells. Our data demonstrates that the CTD domain is dispensable for the first strand cDNA synthesis of long cDNA products using its ORF2 mRNA as a template. It is only when the C-terminal end of the RT is truncated that cDNA synthesis is abolished. The effect of the FE:AA and INK:AAA variants on retrotransposition (Fig. 4B) could reflect their expression levels (Fig. 7C) or support that disruption of the Cryptic:CTD interface by these mutations affects steps upstream and/or downstream of cDNA synthesis. The latter is supported by our observation that the S:A ORF2p variant is expressed at levels similar to the INK:AAA ORF2p variant but has no effect on retrotransposition (Fig. 4B).

Cryptic trans-complements full-length ORF2p WD:AA defect in cDNA synthesis

We show that wildtype Cryptic co-expression restores Z–RT-CTD fragment-mediated cDNA synthesis (Fig. 1C) and improves retrotransposition of the WD:AA ORF2p variant (Fig. 4A). Here we assess whether co-expressing wildtype Cryptic with full-length retrotransposition- defective FF:AA, WD:AA, FE:AA, or INK:AAA ORF2p variants restores or can interfere with their cDNA synthesis. RNPs isolated from HeLa cells expressing the ORF2p variants and wildtype Cryptic were tested in an in vitro cDNA synthesis assay as described above. Results show that co-expression of the wildtype Cryptic did not significantly affect the FF:AA ORF2p variant cDNA synthesis (Fig. 6C, lanes 5 and 7). Wildtype Cryptic co-expression with the WD:AA ORF2p variant notably improves the WD:AA ORF2p defect in initiation and elongation producing detectable LEAP products up to 700 bp long (Fig. 6C, lanes 6 and 8). Co-expression of the wildtype Cryptic with the FE:AA and INK:AAA variants does not appear to affect their ability to perform cDNA synthesis (Fig. 7C, lanes 7 and 8). These findings show that wildtype Cryptic expressed in trans partially rescues the cDNA synthesis defect of the WD:AA ORF2p variant.

Endonuclease function can be trans-complemented through Cryptic trans interactions with the ORF2p WD:AA variant

Our data show that co-expression of wildtype Cryptic with full length ORF2p mutants rescues the retrotransposition and cDNA synthesis defects of the WD:AA ORF2p variant in trans (Figs 4A and 6C). To assess whether Cryptic:EN interface affects Cryptic’s trans-complementation potential, we performed Alu retrotransposition assays using co-expression of the full-length WD:AA ORF2p with EN Cryptic fragments containing either a wildtype sequence or inactivating mutations in EN (D205A, H230A) or WD:AA in Cryptic (EN Cryptic, EN- Cryptic, or EN Cryptic WD:AA, respectively). Our findings show that co-expression of WD:AA ORF2p with either the wild type EN Cryptic fragment or the EN- Cryptic fragment increases its retrotransposition by ∼3- and 4-fold, respectively (Fig. 9A, lanes 3 and 4), whereas the EN Cryptic WD:AA fragment fails to do so (Fig. 9A, lane 5). Compared to Cryptic alone (Supplementary Fig. 15, lanes 2 and 6), both functional and nonfunctional EN Cryptic fragments more effectively rescue retrotransposition off the WD:AA ORF2p variant (Supplementary Fig. 15, lanes 2, 3, 4, and 6). These data show that the intramolecular interactions between the ORF2p EN and Cryptic are relevant to trans-complementation of retrotransposition by Cryptic. However, it is unclear which EN function is used when wildtype EN Cryptic is expressed in this experimental system.

To test this, we co-expressed full-length EN deficient ORF2p variants (EN- ORF2p or EN- WD:AA ORF2p) with the EN Cryptic fragments shown in Fig. 9. Consistent with the RT defect shown in Fig. 6, the EN- WD:AA ORF2p did not support any EN-independent retrotransposition (Fig. 9B, lane 6) observed with the EN- ORF2p variant (Fig. 9B, lane 2). Consistent with the documented EN-independent retrotransposition [74] and variation in the ability of HeLa cells to support retrotransposition [113, 114], the EN- ORF2p variant generates ∼10%–13% of Alu NeoR colonies compared to the Alu NeoR colonies generated by the wild type ORF2p (Figs 9B and 4). The EN- ORF2p was not trans-complemented by any EN Cryptic fragments (Fig. 9B, lanes 3–5), consistent with its intramolecular interactions formed by wildtype Cryptic being favored over trans interactions. In contrast, the EN- WD:AA ORF2p was robustly trans-complemented by the EN Cryptic fragment (∼4 versus ∼580 colonies, P <.01, Student’s t-test, Fig. 9B, lanes 6 and 7). The EN- Cryptic and EN Cryptic WD:AA fragments did not trans-complement the EN- WD:AA ORF2p variant (Fig. 9B, lanes 8 and 9). AlphaFold model predicts that trans-complementation occurs by the EN Cryptic fragment displacing the EN and WD:AA Cryptic within the full-length ORF2p (Supplementary Fig. 16). These results show that only the wildtype EN Cryptic fragment rescues retrotransposition of the EN- WD:AA ORF2p presumably through reestablishing the Cryptic:ZRT interface in trans.

Cryptic interactions with the Z–RT and CTD domains have distinct effects on ORF2p cytotoxicity

We found that although the FF:AA, WD:AA, FE:AA, and INK:AAA mutations significantly decrease retrotransposition, only the FF:AA and WD:AA variants have a cDNA synthesis defect that explains reduction in retrotransposition. Cryptic has been reported to influence ORF2p EN-mediated cytotoxicity in the context of EN Cryptic fragments [84]. We therefore assessed whether the FF:AA, WD:AA, S:A, FE:AA, and INK:AAA mutations may alter ORF2p cytotoxicity and contribute to the observed differences in retrotransposition (Fig. 4). We used a Hygromycin-selection based chronic cytotoxicity assay as previously described [27, 35, 84]. These reported assays established that ORF2p cytotoxicity is associated with its EN and RT enzymatic functions [25, 27, 35]. In this assay, Hygromycin resistant plasmids expressing either wildtype ORF2p or the ORF2p variants (FF:AA, WD:AA, S:A, FE:AA, and INK:AAA) are transfected in HeLa cells and Hygromycin positive cells are selected for by culturing in the Hygromycin-containing media. The ORF2p expression plasmid containing inactivating mutations in both EN and RT domain (EN-RT-) was used as a negative control. This approach determined that compared to the wildtype ORF2p the FF:AA and WD:AA ORF2p variants have reduced cytotoxicity, which is indistinguishable from the EN-RT- ORF2p (P <.05 for FF:AA, P <.01 for WD:AA Student’s t-test, Fig. 10, lanes 1–4). In contrast, the S:A and INK:AAA ORF2p variants exhibit a 50% increase in cytotoxicity compared to the wildtype ORF2p (P <.01 for S:A, P <.05 for INK:AAA, Student’s t-test, Fig. 10, lanes 2, 5, and 7) while the FE:AA variant trends with increased cytotoxicity (Fig. 10, lanes 2 and 6). These results show that the ORF2p variants with altered Cryptic:Z–RT interface have decreased ORF2p cytotoxicity while the ORF2p variants with altered Cryptic:CTD interface have increased ORF2p cytotoxicity. The latter is consistent with the reduced protein expression levels of these variants (Fig. 7C).

Cryptic ORF2p variants impact ORF2p-induced DNA damage response

To assess whether the contrasting effects of the Cryptic:ZRT and Cryptic:CTD interfaces on ORF2p cytotoxicity (Fig. 10) are due to differences in cellular DNA damage response to their expression, we expressed the ORF2p Cryptic variants and measured the levels of γH2AX. γH2AX levels detected for each variant were normalized to the expression levels of the corresponding ORF2p variant, with the wildtype ratio set as 1 (n = 3, see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). Our results show that expression of the ORF2p Cryptic:ZRT variants had no impact on γH2AX levels while the Cryptic:CTD variants trended to have increased levels of γH2AX compared to the wildtype ORF2p; however, these differences were not statistically significant (P >.05, Student’s t-test, Supplementary Fig. 17).

To test individual contributions of the ORF2p EN and RT functions, we assessed the DNA damage response triggered by the Cryptic interface mutants in the context of inactivated EN or RT catalytic domains (EN-RT+ or EN+RT-). We expressed ORF2p variants carrying Cryptic interface mutations (FF:AA, WD:AA, S:A, FE:AA, INK:AAA) with either a mutated EN (EN-RT+) or RT (EN+RT-) in HeLa cells and measured γH2AX levels. Wildtype ORF2p and catalytically inactivating mutations in EN (EN-RT+), RT (EN+RT-), or both (EN-RT-) were included as controls to distinguish the contributions of EN and RT activities. Expression of the FF:AA and WD:AA variants harboring an inactive EN (EN-RT+) triggered two-fold lower levels of γH2AX when compared to the EN-RT+ control (P <.05 for FF:AA and P <.01 for WD:AA, Student’s t-test, Fig. 11, lanes 1, 5, and 6). This result is consistent with the RT defects in cDNA synthesis observed in the FF:AA and WD:AA variants (Fig. 6) and their reduced cytotoxicity (Fig. 10). In contrast, expression of the S:A variant increased γH2AX signal by ∼1.5-fold compared to the EN-RT+ control (P <.05, Student’s t-test, Fig. 11, lanes 1 and 7). γH2AX levels triggered by the expression of the FE:AA and INK:AAA variants did not reach statistical significance but exhibited a trend of increased levels compared to the EN-RT+ control (Fig. 11, lanes 1, 8, and 9).These results are consistent with increased cytotoxicity of the S:A, FE:AA, and INK:AAA variants (Fig. 10). The levels of γH2AX triggered by the EN-RT+ S:A, FE:AA, and INK:AAA variants were the same as those triggered by the wildtype ORF2p (Fig. 11, lanes 4, 6, 7, and 8), suggesting potential EN contribution to the cytotoxicity observed for the S:A, FE:AA, and INK:AAA variants (Fig. 10). The ORF2p Cryptic variants harboring an inactive RT (EN+RT-), had no measurable impact on γH2AX response compared to the EN+RT- control (Fig. 11, lanes 2 and 10–14), which is consistent with the previously published data establishing that in the context of the wildtype ORF2p, the RT function significantly contributes to the γH2AX signal [25, 27, 35].

Cryptic interface mutations alter ORF2p conformational dynamics identified by molecular dynamics simulations

Our results show that mutations at the Cryptic:ZRT and Cryptic:CTD interfaces modulate ORF2p retrotransposition efficiency (Fig. 4), RT function (Figs 6 and 11), and cytotoxicity (Fig. 10), likely by perturbing interdomain interactions mediated by Cryptic (Figs 1B, 2, and 3). To investigate the structural and dynamic consequences of these interface mutations, we used AlphaFold to model each variant and superimpose it on the wildtype ORF2p structure. The predicted models revealed localized conformational deviations, particularly within the EN and CTD, relative to the wildtype protein (Supplementary Fig. 18). To further characterize the impact of these substitutions on protein stability and domain mobility, we conducted 10-ns MD simulations (three independent replicates per system) using GROMACS [97–100, 108] for the wildtype ORF2p and the FF:AA, WD:AA, S:A, FE:AA, and INK:AAA variants, both in the ligand-bound and apo states.

RMSD analysis revealed that all interface variants exhibited significantly greater conformational fluctuations than the wildtype ORF2p in the apo state (Supplementary Fig. 19; P <.0001, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). ORF2p binding to the mRNA substrate reduced overall structural mobility across all systems, indicating mRNA-induced stabilization; however, the variants continued to display elevated fluctuations relative to the wildtype (P <.0001, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). Estimated binding free energies were comparable between the mutants and the wild type (Fig. 12A), consistent with our experimentally observed RNA-binding results (Fig. 5).

RMSF analysis was used to examine residue-level flexibility and assess how perturbations at the Cryptic:ZRT and Cryptic:CTD interfaces influence local and global dynamics of ORF2p. The FF:AA and WD:AA variants exhibited pronounced fluctuations within the EN domain (residues 1–225) and increased flexibility (∼1 Å) in the PCNA-interacting region (residues 950–1060; Fig. 12B, P <.0001, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) [79]. Variants at the Cryptic:CTD interface displayed elevated fluctuations across the EN and Cryptic domains (residues 1–375) and moderate increases in flexibility throughout the entire protein (Supplementary Fig. 20A, P <.0001, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). Consistent with our co-immunoprecipitation experiments (Figs 2 and 3), RMSF analyses suggest that perturbation of a single Cryptic interface propagates dynamically across ORF2p, leading to global redistribution of flexibility with distinct domain-specific signatures.

Guided by the RMSF-derived flexibility changes in the EN region, we monitored inter-residue distances between catalytic and interfacial residues during the MD simulations to assess potential structural perturbations. Specifically, distances between EN active site residues D205 and H230, and between two sets of EN:Cryptic interface residues T244/E325 and R26/E351, were tracked over 10-ns trajectories. The FF:AA variant exhibited an average increase of ∼2 Å between the EN active site residues compared with the wildtype (Fig. 12C, P <.0001, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test), whereas other variants showed distances comparable to the wildtype model (Fig. 12C). In contrast, the distance between EN:Cryptic interface residues, T244 and E325 increased by ∼2 Å in the WD:AA and FE:AA variants, ∼1 Å in the FF:AA and S:A variants, and exhibited a small but statistically significant decrease in the INK:AAA variant relative to the wildtype (Fig. 12D, P <.0001, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). Distances between the R26 and E351 residues in the EN:Cryptic interface increased by ∼2 Å for the FF:AA and FE:AA variants, and by ∼1 Å for the WD:AA, S:A, and INK:AAA variants relative to the wildtype (Supplementary Fig. 20B, P <.0001, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). These findings are consistent with our experimental observations, supporting the interpretation that mutations within either the Cryptic:ZRT or Cryptic:CTD interfaces perturb intramolecular interactions between Cryptic and other ORF2p domains, thereby influencing the conformation of the EN active site. Moreover, the modeling predicts that altered Cryptic-mediated domain dynamics could modulate ORF2p interactions with host factors such as PCNA.

Discussion

Retrotransposons (e.g. L1, R2, Ty1, Ty3) and retroviruses (e.g. HIV, HERV) contain functionally related RTs [65, 82], which are required for their propagation in host cells. The RTs of the non-LTR retrotransposons (L1, R2) operate in the context of multifunctional proteins (ORF2p and ORFp), which also contain other enzymatic activities such as EN [3]. Many proteins containing multiple functional domains (i.e. polymerases) rely on intramolecular interactions to coordinate timely use of these domains [115–119]. In vitro studies established biochemical properties of the R2 and L1 RT and EN domains in the multistep target primed reverse transcription process [20, 59, 60, 62, 63, 78–80]. Resolution of the full-length mammalian ORF2p structure [78–80], a significant milestone in ORF2p biology, identified intramolecular interactions formed by the Cryptic region (aa 240–380) with the EN, RT, and CTD (Fig. 1B). These findings agree with our published results, that proposed these intramolecular interactions based on assays involving ORF2p fragments [25, 83, 84]. Initially disregarded as a linker sequence, the Cryptic region of ORF2p is highly conserved among L1s across species and lacks homology to any other cellular proteins [83, 88]. The requirement of Cryptic sequence for retrotransposition is documented [83, 88], however, mechanistic studies defining its function are lacking. In this study, we provide experimental evidence that Cryptic functions as an independent multipurpose domain, which is required for optimal ORF2p-driven retrotransposition through the formation of intramolecular interactions with other ORF2p domains (Figs 1C and 4–9).

Our studies establish basic understanding of the relationship between the multiple intramolecular interactions involving Cryptic and the functional outcomes of their perturbation. The first critical finding is that although mutations in both Cryptic:ZRT and Cryptic:CTD interfaces reduce retrotransposition (Fig. 4), the mechanisms underlying this decreased ORF2p efficiency differ. Mutations of the Cryptic:ZRT interface negatively impact ORF2p first strand cDNA synthesis (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 1), decrease ORF2p cytotoxicity (Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 1), and reduce RT-induced DNA damage response (Fig. 11), demonstrating that this interface is essential for the ORF2p RT function in the cDNA synthesis step. In fact, the Z–RT-CTD ORF2p fragment lacking Cryptic is incapable of cDNA synthesis (Fig. 1C), while Cryptic fragment co-expression rescues this defect in trans (Fig. 1C). Consistent with these findings, molecular modeling of the Cryptic:ZRT interface variants predicts increased flexibility in the PCNA unusual binding motif (aa 950–1050) of ORF2p (Fig. 12B) [79], suggesting that changes in ORF2p:PCNA interactions may underly the elongation defects of these ORF2p variants (Fig. 12B). Molecular modeling also predicts alteration of the EN active site in the FF:AA variant (Fig. 12C) and Cryptic:EN interface of the FF:AA and WD:AA variants (Fig. 12D), suggesting that reduced retrotransposition of these variants may not be limited to a single defect. These findings establish that the Cryptic:ZRT interface is required for ORF2p RT function by facilitating RT processivity during cDNA synthesis, potentially through ensuring optimal ORF2p:PCNA interactions.

In contrast, disruption of the Cryptic:CTD interface, through mutations or deletions, does not affect first strand cDNA synthesis (Figs 7 and 8, and Supplementary Table 1). However, mutations of the Cryptic:CTD increase ORF2p cytotoxicity (Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 1) and DNA damage response induced by both ORF2p (Supplementary Fig. 17) and its RT (Fig. 11). These findings show that in the mammalian environment, the Cryptic:CTD interface is involved in steps downstream of first strand cDNA synthesis. Our findings imply that ORF2p Cryptic:CTD variants may have defects in template switching, second strand cDNA synthesis and/or coordinated actions of EN and RT functions proposed to be involved in template switching [78, 79]. This idea is consistent with in vitro studies showing that internal deletion of the Cryptic region that interacts with the CTD (Δ302–389) reduced template switching activity [80] and that truncation of the ORF2p CTD diminished EN nicking activity [78]. We speculate that these defects may underlie increased cytotoxicity (Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 1) and DNA damage response (Fig. 11) observed for Cryptic:CTD ORF2p variants by generating persistent bulky integration intermediates that may be challenging for cellular DNA repair machinery to resolve. Consistent with these observations, MD simulations of ORF2p Cryptic:CTD variants predict increased EN flexibility (Supplementary Fig. 20), suggesting this interface contributes to structural stability required for coordinated EN and RT functions.

While ORF2p variants with mutations in the Cryptic:ZRT and Cryptic:CTD interfaces have different defects, our findings support an interplay between the three interfaces formed by the Cryptic domain with the EN, ZRT, and CTD domains. Co-immunoprecipitation assays demonstrate that mutations perturbing the Cryptic:ZRT interface (FF:AA and WD:AA; Fig. 2B) also decrease Cryptic interaction with the EN and CTD domains (Fig. 2C and D). In line with this, molecular modeling predicts that perturbation of either interface increases EN flexibility (Fig. 12B and Supplementary Fig. 20) and alters the EN:Cryptic interface (Fig. 12D). Moreover, we find that co-expression of the EN deficient Cryptic WD:AA ORF2p variant with a wildtype EN Cryptic fragment restores EN function in retrotransposition (Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 1), presumably through Cryptic:ZRT interactions. This is based on the fact that this trans-complementation is only observed when the ORF2p also has WD:AA mutations and the EN Cryptic fragment has both wildtype EN and Cryptic sequences (Fig. 9). This finding further supports the idea that Cryptic coordinates the functions of the EN, RT, and CTD domains during retrotransposition. Previous findings established that: (i) the EN domain is flexible [79], (ii) Cryptic modulates EN cytotoxicity [84] and EN-induced DNA damage response [25], and (iii) Cryptic interactions with EN and CTD undergo dynamic changes in the two observed ORF2p conformations [80]. Together with our findings, these observations support a model in which dynamic interactions between the Cryptic and other ORF2p domains are needed for coordinated ORF2p enzymatic functions evolved to balance ORF2p-driven retrotransposition with its cellular toxicity. Further work defining the temporal and structural coordination between these domains will be critical to understand the Cryptic function in regulating the sequential steps of retrotransposition.

In summary, our findings provide mechanistic understanding of the functional importance of the intramolecular interactions within L1 ORF2p in mammalian retrotransposition. Specifically, we show that Cryptic, a conserved sequence unique to L1 ORF2p that lacks independent enzymatic activity, functions as a distinct domain. We demonstrate that perturbation of the Cryptic-centered intramolecular interactions can lead to a complete loss of cDNA initiation/elongation by the ORF2p RT or to changes in ORF2p-driven retrotransposition and cytotoxicity. Based on the functional cross talk between the three interfaces involving interactions with Cryptic, we propose that the Cryptic domain is a structural hub that through its intramolecular interactions coordinates ORF2p enzymatic steps during retrotransposition. Our finding that disruption of the Cryptic:Z–RT interface is as efficient in abolishing its cytotoxicity as mutations of the catalytic residues in the ORF2p EN and RT domains offers unique opportunities for development of new classes of ORF2p-specific inhibitors. The uniqueness of this interface overcomes current limitations associated with targeting the EN and/or RT domains that share homology with mammalian cellular proteins, which limit their therapeutic potential. Targeting the Cryptic domain proper folding or its intramolecular interactions have promising therapeutic potential in inhibiting ORF2p retrotransposition and cytotoxicity, both of which are key contributors to DNA damage and genomic instability in diseases like cancer.
