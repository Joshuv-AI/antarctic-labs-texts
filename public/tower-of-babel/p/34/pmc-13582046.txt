Age-Specific Considerations in Bronchiectasis Management: Focus on Older Adults with Emphasis on Anti-inflammatory Treatment
Authors: Maria Gabriella Matera, Vito De Novellis, Raffaella Pagliaro, Vincenzo Parente, Fabio Perrotta, Mario Cazzola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bronchiectasis is increasingly recognized as a chronic inflammatory airway disease involving persistent immune dysregulation, recurrent infection, and progressive lung tissue damage. Older adults bear a particularly high burden of bronchiectasis, as age-related changes such as immunosenescence, multimorbidity, and polypharmacy complicate disease management in this population. Anti-inflammatory therapies are emerging as a central component of treatment strategies, reflecting the growing understanding of inflammation as a key driver of disease progression. However, applying these therapies to older populations requires considering altered pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, increased susceptibility to adverse effects, and comorbid conditions. This review examines current and emerging anti-inflammatory treatments for bronchiectasis, focusing on their relevance, safety, and limitations in older patients. Attention is given to the roles of neutrophilic and type 2 inflammatory pathways, as well as the challenges of implementing precision medicine approaches for older people.

Introduction

Bronchiectasis is a chronic respiratory disease characterized by irreversible dilation of the bronchi, impaired mucociliary clearance, persistent infection, and chronic airway inflammation [1, 2]. It is currently recognized as a disease primarily affecting older adults, with its prevalence, clinical severity, and healthcare burden rising substantially with age [2]. Epidemiological data consistently demonstrate an increase in prevalence and severity with age, positioning bronchiectasis as a condition that primarily affects older adults in many healthcare systems [2–7]. Aging is associated with immunosenescence, chronic low-grade systemic inflammation (“inflammaging”), reduced mucociliary clearance, and increased susceptibility to respiratory infections [8, 9]. In addition, older patients frequently present with multimorbidity and polypharmacy, which complicates pharmacological management significantly [10].

Although bronchiectasis was historically considered a “post-infectious” structural lung disorder, it is now widely recognized as a disease in which inflammation plays a central pathogenic role [11]. Neutrophilic inflammation is the dominant immunological pattern in most patients and contributes directly to tissue destruction, mucus hypersecretion, and disease progression [12]. This conceptual shift has important therapeutic implications. Accordingly, contemporary bronchiectasis management increasingly seeks not only to control infection and modulate inflammatory pathways but also to alter long-term disease progression [11]. This is particularly relevant given the high burden of exacerbations, hospitalizations, and progressive functional decline associated with chronic inflammation [1, 2].

In this context, anti-inflammatory therapy presents both an opportunity and a challenge. While modulating inflammation may improve outcomes, older patients are particularly vulnerable to adverse effects such as immunosuppression, drug interactions, and treatment-related frailty [12]. Thus, when considering anti-inflammatory strategies in this population, it is essential to carefully balance efficacy and safety, and to engage in shared decision making that considers quality of life, functional ability, cognition, comorbid conditions, and risks of polypharmacy. This review, therefore, examines current and emerging anti-inflammatory strategies for bronchiectasis in older adults, emphasizing the age-related physiological changes, comorbidities, and treatment considerations that may influence therapeutic decision making.

Literature Search Strategy

This article is conceived as a narrative review. A comprehensive literature search was conducted using major biomedical databases, including PubMed, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, Mendeley, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The search covered publications up to March 2026. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords related to the disease and interventions of interest. These included, but were not limited to: “bronchiectasis,” “older adults,” “aging,” “inflammation,” “anti-inflammatory therapy,” “corticosteroids,” “macrolides,” “DPP-1 inhibitors,” “brensocatib,” “verducatib,” “florensocatib,” “biologics,” “phosphodiesterase inhibitors,” and “precision medicine.”

Priority was given to randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, observational studies, and guideline documents relevant to bronchiectasis and older adult populations. Because of the limited availability of bronchiectasis-specific evidence regarding several anti-inflammatory interventions, studies from populations with chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD), asthma, and mixed airway diseases were also included if they were considered mechanistically or clinically relevant. Reference lists of selected articles were manually screened to identify additional pertinent publications.

Because this is a narrative review, study selection was not restricted by formal systematic review criteria, and no quantitative synthesis was performed. Instead, evidence was integrated through a critical and interpretative approach that emphasized pharmacological mechanisms, clinical relevance, and unmet needs. The final selection of studies reflects the authors’ expert judgment regarding the studies’ scientific quality, novelty, and relevance to current and future pharmacotherapeutic strategies for older adults with bronchiectasis.

However, it should be acknowledged that, owing to the limited availability of evidence specific to bronchiectasis for several anti-inflammatory interventions, some of the discussed mechanistic and therapeutic data are derived from populations with COPD, asthma, or mixed airway diseases. These studies were included if they were mechanistically or clinically relevant to bronchiectasis. Therefore, a proportion of the evidence originates from non-bronchiectasis cohorts, and extrapolating these findings to bronchiectasis, particularly in older adults, should be done with caution.

Inflammation in Bronchiectasis: Therapeutic Rationale

The inflammatory environment in bronchiectasis is complex and heterogeneous (Fig. 1). It is predominantly characterized by neutrophil-driven airway inflammation. Neutrophils are recruited into airway secretions by interleukin (IL)-8 and leukotriene B4. There, they release neutrophil serine proteases, including neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, and proteinase 3 [2]. Chronically elevated neutrophil serine protease activity beyond protective antiprotease levels leads to structural lung destruction, mucosal defects, increased susceptibility to infection, and worsening clinical outcomes [13]. Concurrently, neutrophil extracellular traps, reactive oxygen species, and multiple downstream inflammatory mediators further exacerbate mucus hypersecretion, epithelial dysfunction, and impaired mucociliary clearance. This prolongs the self-sustaining cycle of infection and airway injury [14]. In addition to neutrophilic inflammation, some patients exhibit type 2 (T2) inflammatory signatures, which are often associated with asthma overlap or allergic features. These patients may exhibit eosinophilia and elevated biomarkers such as fractional exhaled nitric oxide [16]. A pan-European multicohort study found that 22.6% of patients had blood eosinophil counts ≥ 300 cells/μL, and up to 30% showed T helper 2 cell-high inflammation [17]. However, most bronchiectasis patients do not fit this profile, and the clinical significance of eosinophilic inflammation in bronchiectasis is still being investigated [18], which limits the applicability of T2-targeted therapies.

Further, aging modifies the inflammatory milieu. Immunosenescence results in impaired innate and adaptive immune responses, while inflammaging is characterized by chronic low-grade systemic inflammation [19]. These changes contribute to disease persistence and influence responses to pharmacological interventions. In older patients, exaggerated or dysregulated inflammatory responses coexist with reduced immune competence, creating a paradoxical environment in which both infection susceptibility and chronic inflammation increase [20]. Consequently, older adults may experience a more persistent and therapeutically challenging neutrophilic inflammatory state while simultaneously exhibiting reduced tolerance to aggressive immunomodulatory interventions.

Current Anti-Inflammatory Treatments

Inhaled and Systemic Corticosteroids

Corticosteroids have long been a cornerstone of anti-inflammatory therapy in respiratory medicine. However, their role in treating bronchiectasis is limited and not routinely recommended by international guidelines [21] (Table 1). The 2025 European Respiratory Society (ERS) guideline recommends against the routine use of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) for bronchiectasis because of a lack of evidence of benefit and an associated risk of harm [22]. In neutrophilic bronchiectasis, corticosteroids demonstrate limited efficacy. Six randomized trials showed no significant differences in exacerbation frequency, sputum volume, forced expiratory volume in 1 second, or health-related quality of life with ICS compared to placebo. However, adverse events were significantly increased (odds ratio 3.19, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.34–7.61) [22].

From a mechanistic standpoint, corticosteroids may be counterproductive in cases of neutrophilic inflammation, as they interfere with various components of the host defense system and airway immune homeostasis. This creates a therapeutic paradox because corticosteroids effectively suppress eosinophilic inflammation, yet they may also sustain or exacerbate neutrophil-dominant airway injury. Corticosteroids promote eosinophil apoptosis but inhibit neutrophil apoptosis. They do so by inducing the anti-apoptotic protein Mcl-1 [23] and upregulating the leukotriene B4 receptor BLT1 [24], thereby prolonging neutrophil survival and potentially exacerbating protease-mediated tissue damage [25].

In addition to directly affecting the neutrophil lifespan, corticosteroids suppress multiple essential innate immune mechanisms for bacterial clearance [26–29]. They inhibit phagocytosis, degranulation, the oxidative burst, and nitric oxide production in neutrophils and macrophages. Corticosteroids also impair the chemotaxis and migration of macrophages to sites of infection. They suppress cathelicidin production, a key antimicrobial peptide and effector of pulmonary antibacterial defense. This is achieved by augmenting the protease cathepsin D, which leads to microbiota disruption and increased bacterial proliferation in the airways [30]. In a COPD model, ICS-mediated cathelicidin suppression promoted the growth of streptococcal genera in the lung microbiome, an effect that was reversed by the administration of exogenous cathelicidin [30].

In addition, corticosteroids blunt type I interferon (IFN) responses through multiple mechanisms. They inhibit IFN-β expression by suppressing Toll-like receptor-mediated signaling and inducing SOCS1. In macrophages, they impair the transcriptional activity of ISGF3 (STAT1-STAT2-IRF9) by competing for the shared cofactor GRIP1 [31, 32]. In bronchial epithelial cells, glucocorticoids inhibit STAT1/STAT2 phosphorylation following IFN-β stimulation. This suppresses IFN-stimulated gene expression and enhances viral replication [33]. The suppression of antiviral immunity not only delays viral clearance but also increases susceptibility to secondary bacterial infections and enhances mucus production [34]. High-dose ICS use has been independently associated with reduced IFN-α production in response to rhinovirus stimulation [35].

The net impairment of pulmonary immune function, which may be particularly detrimental in bronchiectasis, where chronic bacterial colonization and neutrophil-driven inflammation are central features of the disease pathophysiology, is the result of a combination of effects. These effects include prolonged neutrophil survival, impaired phagocytic and antimicrobial peptide responses, microbiome disruption, and suppressed IFN signaling. These immunological liabilities are likely to have an even greater clinical impact in older adults, whose baseline antibacterial and antiviral defenses are already weakened by immunosenescence.

Despite this evidence, ICSs are still overprescribed. Data from the European Bronchiectasis Registry (EMBARC) show that 52.3% of patients were prescribed ICSs, including 32.7% of those without asthma, COPD, or allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis [36]. Notably, a subgroup of patients with elevated blood eosinophil counts may benefit from ICS treatment. The EMBARC study found a significant reduction in exacerbation frequency among ICS users with elevated eosinophil counts (relative risk [RR] 0.70, 95% CI 0.59–0.84) [36]. Additionally, a post hoc analysis of a randomized trial showed a clinically meaningful symptom improvement with fluticasone in patients with eosinophil counts ≥ 150 cells/μL [2]. However, these data derive from post hoc and observational analyses, and prospective trials are needed before formal recommendations can be made [22].

The safety profile of corticosteroids is of particular concern in older populations. Systemic corticosteroids increase the risk of osteoporosis and fractures (with vertebral fracture risk doubling at doses as low as 2.5–7.5 mg of prednisolone daily), muscle wasting and sarcopenia (affecting up to 60% of long-term users) [37]. They also increase the risk of hyperglycemia and neuropsychiatric effects, including delirium [37]. Even ICSs carry clinically relevant risks. Meta-analyses demonstrate that ICS use is associated with an increased risk of non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) pulmonary disease (OR 3.93, 95% CI 2.12–7.27), with a dose-response relationship and a higher risk associated with fluticasone than budesonide [38]. Inhaled corticosteroid use is also associated with an increased risk of pneumonia. This observation has been consistently reported in COPD populations and is considered biologically relevant to bronchiectasis, given the overlap in chronic airway infection susceptibility [39].

Accordingly, corticosteroid therapy for bronchiectasis should be reserved for clearly defined inflammatory phenotypes rather than being used as routine background treatment. The ERS guideline recommends re-evaluating ICS use in patients without a clear indication and discontinuing it in some cases [22].

Macrolide Therapy

Long-term macrolide therapy is the most well-established anti-inflammatory treatment for bronchiectasis [40] (Table 1). Beyond their antimicrobial activity, agents such as azithromycin and erythromycin exert significant anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, which are largely attributed to their macrocyclic lactone ring structure [41] and are especially important in neutrophil-dominant airway diseases.

They inhibit nuclear factor kappa B activation and broadly suppress p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling, including the ERK1/2, p38, and JNK cascades [42]. They also attenuate Toll-like receptor–mediated signaling, thereby reducing the initiation and amplification of innate immune responses. These mechanisms result in the downregulation of various inflammatory mediators, including proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-α, chemokines, and growth factors that are involved in leukocyte recruitment and activation [42]. This multimodal immunomodulatory profile is particularly relevant in bronchiectasis, where chronic neutrophilic inflammation drives ongoing airway damage [42]. Macrolides markedly affect neutrophil function by reducing their recruitment to inflamed tissues, inhibiting chemotaxis and activation, and promoting apoptosis. This facilitates the resolution of inflammation [43]. While they may enhance certain aspects of neutrophil degranulation, the overall burden of neutrophil-derived proteolytic enzymes is reduced because of decreased cellular influx [43]. Macrolides also influence other components of the immune system. They promote macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory tissue-repair phenotype and enhance phagocytic activity [43]. Furthermore, they regulate adaptive immunity by blocking calcineurin-dependent and mTOR-dependent pathways, which results in decreased T-cell activation and subsequent inflammatory responses [44]. Beyond immunological effects, macrolides improve airway function by reducing mucus hypersecretion, enhancing mucociliary clearance, and strengthening the epithelial barrier [45]. These effects support their role in treating long-term airway diseases.

Clinical trials have demonstrated that long-term macrolide therapy significantly reduces the frequency of exacerbations and improves quality of life in patients with frequent exacerbations [40]. A 2025 ERS guideline meta-analysis of nine randomized controlled trials found a 52% reduction in exacerbation frequency (hazard ratio 0.48, 95% CI 0.37–0.62) and a clinically meaningful improvement in quality of life (mean St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire improvement of 7.26 points, 95% CI -10.94 to -3.59) [22]. A 2026 network meta-analysis confirmed that macrolides reduce exacerbation frequency overall (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.35–0.56), with azithromycin showing the greatest reduction (RR 0.37, 95% CI 0.29–0.48) [46].

International guidelines recommend long-term (> 3 months) oral macrolide therapy for patients with bronchiectasis who experience ≥ 2–3 exacerbations per year [2, 22]. However, the threshold should be individualized based on patient-specific risk factors, symptom burden, and treatment burden. The preferred macrolide is azithromycin, typically administered at a dose of 250 mg daily or 500 mg three times weekly [2, 22]. The 2025 ERS guidelines state that patients with a high daily symptom burden or other risk factors, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection, primary ciliary dyskinesia, COPD, or rheumatoid arthritis, may benefit from macrolide therapy even with fewer exacerbations [22].

In older populations, macrolide therapy requires careful consideration because of cardiovascular risks. QT interval prolongation and arrhythmias are major concerns, especially in older adults with pre-existing heart disease or who are taking multiple QT-prolonging medications [47]. A meta-analysis of 33 studies involving over 20 million participants found that macrolide use was associated with an increased risk of sudden cardiac death or ventricular tachyarrhythmias (RR 2.42, 95% CI 1.61–3.63) and cardiovascular death (RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.06–1.62) [47]. The US Food and Drug Administration label for azithromycin specifically warns that older patients may be more susceptible to the drug’s effects on the QT interval [48]. Among macrolides, azithromycin and erythromycin carry a cardiovascular risk, while roxithromycin was not associated with adverse cardiac outcomes [47].

Additional adverse effects include ototoxicity, which manifests as tinnitus, hearing loss, and vestibular dysfunction, gastrointestinal intolerance characterized by nausea and diarrhea, and hepatotoxicity [22]. Long-term macrolide use is associated with the development of antimicrobial resistance, though meta-analyses of 6- to 12-month trials did not identify this as a significant short-term issue [22, 49]. A critical concern is the development of azithromycin-resistant NTM [22], making NTM screening essential before initiating therapy. These adverse effects are particularly relevant in older adults because a baseline cardiovascular risk, polypharmacy, and age-related pharmacodynamic sensitivity increase the likelihood of clinically significant drug-related complications.

Indirect Anti-Inflammatory Strategies

Non-pharmacological interventions play an important indirect role in reducing airway inflammation in bronchiectasis. These interventions interrupt the “vicious vortex” of infection, inflammation, impaired mucociliary clearance, and structural lung damage [22]. They are particularly relevant for older patients, who have a dramatically higher prevalence of bronchiectasis and limited pharmacological options because of polypharmacy, comorbidities, and adverse drug effects [2]. Shared decision making about treatment is essential for older adults with bronchiectasis and should account for the impact on quality of life, functional ability, cognition, comorbid conditions, and risks of polypharmacy [36].

The 2023 ERS statement on airway clearance techniques, based on a review of 30 randomized trials, concluded that these interventions improve sputum clearance, reduce cough burden and exacerbation risk, and enhance health-related quality of life [50]. Airway clearance techniques are generally considered safe, relatively easy to learn, and suitable for independent performance by patients [1]. Despite this demonstrated efficacy, their use in routine clinical practice remains suboptimal. In the EMBARC registry, only 52% of patients with productive cough reported regular airway clearance technique use [51], while a similarly low proportion (59%) was observed in the US Bronchiectasis Research Registry [52]. This suggests that the benefits shown in clinical trials are not consistently translated into everyday management. This gap is particularly evident in older patients, in whom frailty, reduced mobility, impaired manual dexterity, cognitive decline, and poor long-term adherence may substantially limit both the correct execution and the sustained use of airway clearance techniques. In addition, traditional approaches such as chest wall percussion and postural drainage have progressively declined because they are often uncomfortable and require significant caregiver or healthcare personnel involvement [2]. For this reason, device-assisted strategies such as positive expiratory pressure devices may represent a more practical option for older adults, as they allow greater treatment autonomy without the need for hands-on therapist assistance [1]. Nevertheless, even these approaches require sufficient hand strength, coordination, and cognitive capacity, making individualized instruction and caregiver support essential to optimize their effectiveness in older adults.

Pulmonary rehabilitation is strongly recommended by the 2025 ERS guideline for patients with impaired exercise tolerance [22]. A meta-analysis of seven studies demonstrated significant short-term improvements in exercise capacity and quality of life, with one trial showing a 74% reduction in the odds of experiencing ≥ 1 exacerbation [22]. However, most benefits are not sustained during follow-up distant from the intervention [53]. This is a critical concern for older patients, who face additional barriers to independently maintaining exercise programs. In frail older adults, the effectiveness of pulmonary rehabilitation is often limited not by physiological responsiveness but by reduced adherence, logistical constraints, and competing medical and social priorities.

The ERS Statement on Frailty in Adults with Chronic Lung Disease highlights a significant paradox: frail patients benefit most from pulmonary rehabilitation, yet they also have the highest rates of non-completion [54]. Unpredictable disruptions, such as exacerbations and comorbid flare-ups, and conflicting priorities, such as other appointments and caregiving responsibilities, commonly interfere with attendance for frail patients [55]. Home-based and telerehabilitation models may help frail older patients with bronchiectasis overcome these barriers [54].

These challenges underscore the importance of simple and sustainable preventive strategies in this population. Routine vaccination against influenza, pneumococcal disease, and coronavirus disease 2019, and, where available, respiratory syncytial virus, may reduce the risk of infection-triggered exacerbations and the associated inflammatory burden. This approach aligns with the emphasis in the 2025 ERS guideline on “doing the simple things well” [22], reinforcing the importance of optimizing basic preventive care alongside more complex therapeutic and rehabilitative strategies.

Emerging and Investigational Anti-Inflammatory Therapies

A paradigm shift is underway in the management of bronchiectasis, with multiple novel anti-inflammatory agents in clinical development that target inflammation as a primary therapeutic strategy rather than focusing on infection [56, 57]. This shift is especially important in bronchiectasis, where persistent neutrophilic inflammation causes disease progression even without acute infection.

Protease Inhibition

Protease-mediated tissue injury is increasingly recognized as a pivotal pathogenic mechanism in bronchiectasis. It contributes substantially to perpetuating the inflammatory-infective cycle and causing progressive structural damage to the airways [13]. Among the various proteolytic enzymes involved, neutrophil elastase plays a particularly central role. When released in excessive amounts by activated neutrophils, neutrophil elastase degrades essential extracellular matrix components, such as elastin and collagen. This disrupts epithelial barrier integrity, stimulates mucus hypersecretion, impairs mucociliary clearance, and inactivates endogenous antimicrobial peptides [58]. These effects collectively promote persistent bacterial colonization, recurrent exacerbations, and accelerated decline in pulmonary function [13].

This pathogenic framework has provided the rationale for developing therapies that target dipeptidyl peptidase-1 (DPP-1), also known as cathepsin C, an enzyme that is required for the intracellular activation of neutrophil serine proteases, including neutrophil elastase, proteinase 3, and cathepsin G, during neutrophil maturation [59] (Table 2). Dipeptidyl peptidase-1 inhibitors, particularly brensocatib, act upstream in the inflammatory cascade by reducing the proteolytic burden delivered to the airways while avoiding broad immunosuppressive effects [60]. Clinical trials have demonstrated promising results, including significant reductions in exacerbation frequency and prolongation of time to first exacerbation. There has also been an increase in the proportion of patients who are exacerbation free, thereby supporting the concept of disease modification through targeted inhibition of neutrophil protease activity [60]. Brensocatib was approved by the Food and Drug Administration in August 2025, the European Medicines Agency in November 2025, and the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency in February 2026. Approval was primarily based on the phase III ASPEN trial, an international, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial [61].

Beyond brensocatib, the DPP-1 inhibitor class is expanding with two additional compounds currently in clinical development: verducatib (BI 1291583) and florensocatib (HSK31858). Both are designed to suppress neutrophil serine protease activation by inhibiting cathepsin C. The emergence of several agents within this class suggests that the clinical benefits observed with brensocatib may reflect a broader class effect rather than a compound-specific phenomenon.

Verducatib is a reversible DPP-1 inhibitor with preferential bone marrow exposure, enabling targeted modulation of neutrophil maturation [62]. In the phase II AIRLEAF study, it demonstrated a significant dose-dependent prolongation of time to first exacerbation [63]. The 2.5-mg and 5-mg doses were associated with a numerical reduction in exacerbation risk, and the overall safety profile was comparable to placebo. The CLAIRAFLY trial provided evidence that verducatib has biological activity and is safe in the short term for patients with cystic fibrosis-related bronchiectasis [64]. This finding reinforces the rationale for continued clinical evaluation in larger, longer, efficacy-driven studies. The ongoing phase III AIRTIVITY trial (NCT06872892) is evaluating verducatib versus placebo in a broad bronchiectasis population regardless of etiology [65]. This multicenter, randomized, double-blind study includes a treatment duration of at least 12 months (up to 18 months), and the primary endpoint is the annualized rate of pulmonary exacerbations through week 76.

Florensocatib (HSK31858) is another novel, orally active, selective, and reversible DPP-1 inhibitor [66]. In the phase II SAVE-BE trial, it demonstrated clinically meaningful efficacy in patients with bronchiectasis and a history of frequent exacerbations [67]. Both evaluated doses (20 mg and 40 mg once daily) significantly reduced the annual exacerbation rate compared with placebo, with incidence rate ratios of 0.52 and 0.41, respectively, corresponding to a 48–59% reduction in risk. In addition, florensocatib was associated with a significant decrease in 24-hour sputum volume and a substantial reduction in neutrophil serine protease activity, including that of neutrophil elastase, proteinase 3, and cathepsin G. The treatment was generally well tolerated, with no apparent increase in serious infections or class-related adverse events such as hyperkeratosis or gingival disorders. The phase III confirmatory HOPE-BE study (NCT06660992) is currently ongoing and is expected to further define the efficacy and safety of florensocatib in reducing exacerbation frequency among Chinese adults with bronchiectasis [68].

This therapeutic strategy is particularly relevant for older patients, who often exhibit a more severe disease phenotype [12]. In this population, reducing exacerbation frequency could have a significant clinical impact by preserving functional status, reducing the risk of hospitalization, and limiting the progressive loss of independence. By attenuating chronic neutrophil-driven tissue destruction, DPP-1 inhibitors may also slow the progressive anatomical deterioration that underlies long-term respiratory disability. This is a particularly relevant goal in older individuals with limited physiological reserve.

However, the long-term safety profile of DPP1 inhibition remains an important area of ongoing investigation, particularly in older adults [69]. Concerns persist regarding the sustained attenuation of neutrophil protease activity and its potential impact on host defense mechanisms [70]. Older patients may be inherently more susceptible to persistent respiratory infections, delayed pathogen clearance, and atypical infectious presentations because of impaired innate immunity [71]. Additionally, neutrophil-derived proteases play a role in physiological tissue remodeling and wound repair. Therefore, chronic inhibition could theoretically lead to delayed wound healing, mucosal fragility, periodontal complications, or cutaneous alterations. These issues are particularly significant in frail older populations that are already predisposed to impaired tissue regeneration [72, 73].

Currently, there is only limited supporting evidence for phosphodiesterase (PDE) inhibitors in the treatment of bronchiectasis, and they should still be considered investigational in this setting [74]. Although PDE4 inhibitors such as roflumilast have been shown to reduce the frequency of exacerbations in patients with chronic bronchitis-predominant COPD [75], and dual PDE3/4 inhibitors, such as ensifentrine, have demonstrated both bronchodilatory and anti-inflammatory benefits in COPD [76], no completed clinical studies have yet confirmed a clear therapeutic role for these agents specifically in bronchiectasis. The rationale for considering PDE inhibition in bronchiectasis stems from the significant inflammatory overlap between bronchiectasis and COPD. Phosphodiesterase 4 inhibitors increase intracellular cyclic AMP levels, which attenuates neutrophil-driven inflammation by reducing cytokine release and inflammatory cell activation [75]. This mechanism is particularly relevant because both diseases are characterized by chronic productive cough, mucus hypersecretion, and predominantly neutrophilic airway inflammation. Furthermore, the coexistence of these conditions is clinically significant. Approximately half of patients with bronchiectasis meet spirometric criteria for COPD, and radiological bronchiectasis is evident in up to 60% of patients with severe COPD [77].

Because of its dual mechanism of action, ensifentrine may offer an additional theoretical advantage. Phosphodiesterase 3 inhibition promotes airway smooth muscle relaxation and bronchodilation, while PDE4 inhibition has anti-inflammatory effects [76]. This combined pharmacologic profile has demonstrated clinical benefits for patients with COPD and led to Food and Drug Administration approval in 2024 for use as a maintenance therapy [78].

Despite this pathophysiological rationale, direct clinical evidence in bronchiectasis remains lacking. Although a study investigating roflumilast in non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis (NCT01580748) has been cited in the literature, its results have not been formally published [77]. Consequently, the designation of PDE4 inhibitors as disease-modifying therapies in bronchiectasis is speculative and should not be considered [79]. Similarly, while some expert commentaries have suggested that ensifentrine could be relevant in non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis owing to its anti-inflammatory and bronchodilator properties [78], this remains an unsupported hypothesis without disease-specific trial data.

In older populations, the use of PDE4 inhibitors is limited by an unfavorable tolerability profile. Gastrointestinal adverse effects, including diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, dyspepsia, and reduced appetite, are common and often result in early discontinuation [80]. In frail older patients, these effects may be particularly clinically relevant, as even modest reductions in nutritional intake can accelerate sarcopenia and functional decline [81].

Roflumilast has been associated with clinically significant weight loss averaging approximately 2 kg, which may occur early in the treatment process [80, 82]. In older patients with a low body mass index or pre-existing frailty, this weight loss may further compromise physiological reserve [83].

Psychiatric adverse effects, including insomnia, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, are also more frequent compared with placebo [80]. This is particularly relevant in older populations, where the baseline prevalence of mood disorders and cognitive impairment is higher [84].

High discontinuation rates, which often occur within the first month of treatment because of intolerance, further limit the long-term feasibility of therapy [82]. Age-related pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic variability may also increase susceptibility to adverse effects, particularly in the presence of hepatic or renal impairment and polypharmacy [85].

Overall, gastrointestinal intolerance, weight loss, neuropsychiatric effects, and poor persistence make oral PDE4 inhibition difficult to justify in older populations with bronchiectasis. In contrast, ensifentrine, which is administered via inhalation, appears to offer a substantially more favorable tolerability profile because of reduced systemic exposure [86]. However, despite its improved safety profile compared with oral PDE4 inhibitors, its clinical efficacy in treating bronchiectasis remains unproven, and it should currently be considered purely investigational.

Targeting Neutrophil Recruitment and Intracellular Signaling

C-X-C motif chemokine receptor (CXCR2) antagonists are designed to inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis and reduce the recruitment of inflammatory cells to the airways [11] (Table 3). These antagonists directly target the predominant inflammatory pathway in bronchiectasis. In a phase IIa trial of 52 patients with bronchiectasis, the CXCR2 antagonist AZD5069 reduced sputum neutrophil counts by 69% over 28 days of treatment [87]. Notably, infection-related discontinuations occurred more frequently in the active treatment group, including cases of pneumonia and bronchiectasis exacerbations [87]. Subsequent trials of CXCR2 antagonists in patients with bronchiectasis and COPD showed increased exacerbations and pneumonia incidence, leading to the discontinuation of this therapeutic approach [11]. These findings underscore a critical limitation of upstream neutrophil blockade: the potential to impair host defense in individuals with chronic bacterial colonization.

Additional therapeutic strategies focus on intracellular signaling pathways, such as p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-δ inhibitors. These pathways play a role in neutrophil activation, degranulation, reactive oxygen species generation, phagocytosis, and corticosteroid-resistant inflammation [11, 88]. Overactivation of PI3K in older individuals contributes to dysregulated neutrophil chemotaxis and tissue injury. Preclinical studies suggest that inhibition may partially restore migratory accuracy [11].

The neutrophil extracellular trap-CYBB-p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase axis has been identified as a key mechanism of oxidative epithelial injury in bronchiectasis, especially in patients infected with P. aeruginosa [89]. Nemiralisib, an inhaled PI3Kδ inhibitor, improved recovery after an acute exacerbation of COPD in a randomized trial, but the treatment was associated with cough and raised concerns about broader immune modulation [90].

Although PI3K-targeted therapies are mechanistically attractive, they face significant translational barriers because of this limitation. This is especially relevant in older adults, in whom immunosenescence and reduced antimicrobial reserve may amplify susceptibility to infectious complications and concerns about systemic immunosuppression and infection risk [88, 91]. Neutrophils from older individuals exhibit increased PI3K signaling, contributing to migratory inaccuracy and exaggerated bystander tissue injury rather than effective pathogen clearance [92]. While pharmacological inhibition may restore certain functional aspects of neutrophil behavior, broad pathway suppression risks compromising essential antimicrobial defenses [93]. Age-related accumulation of senescent cells in the lung further compounds this complexity. These cells perpetuate the chronic release of IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-1β, and CXCL8 through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, thereby sustaining neutrophilic inflammation and epithelial dysfunction [94]. In this context, the therapeutic modulation of intracellular signaling must carefully balance the anti-inflammatory benefits against the risk of further weakening the host’s defenses in the biologically fragile, aging lung [95].

Biologic Therapies

Biologic therapies that target specific inflammatory pathways are a form of precision medicine for patients with bronchiectasis, especially those with eosinophilic or T2 inflammatory signatures [96]. However, the role of biologic therapies in bronchiectasis remains investigational. None of these biologics has been validated in large randomized bronchiectasis-specific trials. The available evidence mostly comes from asthma and COPD populations, which limits the applicability of biologics in the current treatment of bronchiectasis. Biologics under investigation include those that target IL-5 (mepolizumab and benralizumab), IL-4 and IL-13 (dupilumab), IL-33 and its receptor ST2, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (tezepelumab) (Table 3).

Although there have been no randomized placebo-controlled trials of biologics specifically in eosinophilic bronchiectasis, a case series of 21 patients with persistent blood eosinophilia (≥300 cells/μL), refractory disease, and frequent exacerbations demonstrated that anti-IL-5/IL-5Rα monoclonal antibodies significantly reduced eosinophil counts and sputum volume, and improved lung function [97]. Real-world data from 97 patients with severe eosinophilic asthma and comorbid bronchiectasis showed that anti-IL-5/IL-5Rα therapy reduced exacerbation frequency significantly (from 74.5% with ≥ 2 exacerbations to 22.1% during the follow-up year, p < 0.001) and decreased the need for oral corticosteroids [98].

Results from the first randomized trial of benralizumab in bronchiectasis (NCT05006573), which was completed in April 2024, are currently awaited [11]. Phase II trials of anti-IL-33/ST2 therapies for COPD did not meet the primary endpoint of reducing exacerbations. Astegolimab (anti-ST2) failed to significantly reduce exacerbations (rate ratio 0.78, p = 0.19), though it significantly lowered blood and sputum eosinophil counts [99]. Itepekimab (anti-IL-33) also missed its primary endpoint (rate ratio 0.81, p = 0.13), though a significant benefit was observed in former smokers [100]. These results suggest that suppressing eosinophilic inflammation alone does not consistently lead to clinical benefits in populations with unselected airway diseases, which reinforces the importance of selecting patients based on biomarkers [11]. In addition to eosinophils, combined inflammatory signatures and disease endotypes that integrate eosinophilic, neutrophilic, and microbiological profiles may be important for future precision medicine approaches in bronchiectasis. Anti-TSLP therapy (tezepelumab) has shown promise in treating severe asthma, regardless of the eosinophil count [101], but its role in treating bronchiectasis remains to be established.

In older adults, biologic therapies present additional challenges beyond disease heterogeneity. Age-related immunosenescence may alter therapeutic responses and safety profiles owing to the complex interaction between biologic immune modulation and baseline immune dysfunction [102]. The risk of infection is a particular concern for older patients, given the reduced host defense reserve and the frequent presence of chronic bacterial airway colonization [12]. The high cost [103] and the need for long-term subcutaneous administration [104] limit the practicality of these therapies for frail older adults. Emerging targeted therapies may address some of these limitations. In particular, oral DPP-1 inhibitors offer the potential advantage of easier administration compared with injectable biologics. However, uncertainties remain regarding their cost, accessibility, and affordability across different healthcare systems [105]. These economic and logistical considerations pose significant challenges to the widespread implementation of novel targeted therapies in bronchiectasis for older adults. Consequently, long-term macrolide therapy, which is widely available and comparatively inexpensive, is likely to remain the most pragmatic option for many patients, particularly in resource-limited settings and among frail older adults.

Critically, this population is underrepresented in clinical trials [106], and therefore, robust age-specific safety and efficacy data are lacking. This evidence gap is particularly relevant for biologics, as immune modulation may pose distinct risks and benefits in aging immune systems compared with younger populations. Age-related pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic changes, including reduced renal and hepatic clearance and altered body composition, together with multimorbidity and frailty, further complicate treatment responses in this population [85], making direct extrapolation of efficacy and safety data from younger trial populations potentially unreliable. Importantly, this limitation is not restricted to biologic therapies but applies broadly to all emerging pharmacological treatments in bronchiectasis.

Future Directions and Precision Medicine

The future management of bronchiectasis is increasingly guided by the principles of precision medicine, particularly through the implementation of a treatable trait framework. This framework represents a shift from syndrome-based management to biologically and clinically stratified treatment strategies. This approach prioritizes identifying and targeting clinically relevant, measurable, and modifiable disease characteristics. Thus, it represents a shift toward more individualized, phenotype-driven care in bronchiectasis [107]. Within this paradigm, the integration of inflammatory endotypes, microbiological patterns, and clinical phenotypes provides the basis for more rational and targeted therapeutic strategies [108].

Recent studies have identified four major inflammatory endotypes in bronchiectasis: (1) milder neutrophilic inflammation with lower exacerbation rates; (2) mixed neutrophilic and T2 inflammation associated with increased exacerbation risk; (3) severe neutrophilic inflammation linked to P. aeruginosa infection; and (4) mixed epithelial and T2 inflammation [108]. These biologically distinct subgroups are associated with different clinical trajectories, suggesting their potential usefulness in biomarker-guided stratification for future interventional trials [56].

Concurrently, neutrophil-derived biomarkers, particularly neutrophil elastase and neutrophil extracellular traps, have emerged as reliable indicators of bronchiectasis disease activity and severity [109], although up to 30% of patients with bronchiectasis exhibit T2-high inflammation with blood eosinophilia, suggesting the existence of a distinct therapeutic subgroup [16]. Additional biomarkers, such as serum fibrinogen, have been associated with disease severity and exacerbation risk [110].

Reflecting a broader transition toward biological stratification, the 2025 ERS guidelines emphasize individualized risk assessment using tools such as the Bronchiectasis Severity Index. This shift moves away from rigid exacerbation thresholds and toward more patient-centered decision making [22]. This transition underscores a broader change in bronchiectasis care, transitioning from a frequency-based approach to a risk-based and biology-based approach.

Despite these advances, substantial disease heterogeneity and the absence of standardized definitions continue to hinder the interpretation and generalization of clinical trial results. This underscores the need for larger studies with harmonized methodologies that incorporate validated phenotypic and endotypic classifications to ensure biologically comparable patient populations [108, 111].

From a therapeutic perspective, optimal disease management requires integrating anti-inflammatory strategies with infection control, airway clearance, and comorbidity optimization [1, 22, 108] (Fig. 2). Current guidelines consistently emphasize the central role of physiotherapist-led airway clearance techniques for patients with chronic productive cough [2, 22]. These techniques are used alongside the individualized use of mucoactive therapies and structured pulmonary rehabilitation programs. The 2025 ERS guidelines emphasize the importance of optimizing foundational aspects of care, which can be summarized as “doing the simple things well” [22]. This includes identifying underlying etiologies, effectively clearing the airways, and appropriately managing medications while awaiting more targeted therapies. Such optimization is particularly critical for older adults, whose treatment complexity and comorbidity burden often obscure the relative contribution of individual disease mechanisms. Specific consideration of age-related physiological changes, multimorbidity, and increased vulnerability to adverse drug effects is necessary in both trial design and clinical implementation. Future clinical trials and post-marketing studies of emerging therapies, including brensocatib, verducatib, and florensocatib, should explicitly include dedicated older and frail subgroups. In addition to traditional efficacy endpoints such as exacerbation frequency, these studies should incorporate geriatric-relevant outcomes, including frailty status, functional capacity, falls, cognitive function, polypharmacy burden, hospitalization rates, and patient-reported quality-of-life measures. Treatment choices should ultimately be guided not only by disease control but also by their impact on these broader domains relevant to aging populations [12].

Conclusions

Bronchiectasis is a chronic inflammatory disease for which anti-inflammatory therapies are playing an increasingly central role in treatment. A network meta-analysis demonstrates that macrolides and DPP-1 inhibitors both reduce the frequency of exacerbations compared with placebo [46]. Macrolides reduce exacerbations by 56% (rate ratio: 0.44; 95% CI 0.35–0.56), and DPP-1 inhibitors reduce exacerbations by 27% (rate ratio: 0.73; 95% CI 0.60–0.88). These results are supported by moderate-certainty evidence.

Despite these benefits, translating them into routine clinical practice for older adults remains challenging. For this population, therapeutic decisions must carefully balance efficacy with an increased susceptibility to adverse effects, frailty, and complications related to polypharmacy [12]. This is particularly relevant for macrolides, as clinically significant adverse effects, such as QT prolongation, ototoxicity, and gastrointestinal intolerance, may further restrict long-term use in older adults with multimorbidity and concomitant therapies [22, 47].

Although emerging anti-inflammatory strategies show promise for targeted intervention, their applicability to older adults with bronchiectasis is uncertain. Exploratory analyses suggest that agents such as brensocatib may be more effective in patients with milder disease, fewer prior exacerbations, and better baseline lung function. These features are less common in older and more complex patient populations [69]. Robust age-specific safety and effectiveness data for brensocatib and other novel anti-inflammatory therapies are still lacking [12].

The optimal management of older patients continues to rely on a multimodal strategy integrating anti-inflammatory treatment, infection control, structured airway clearance, and comprehensive comorbidity management [1, 2, 108]. Consistent with this approach, the 2025 ERS guidelines emphasize individualized risk stratification and patient-centered decision making [22]. These guidelines move beyond rigid exacerbation thresholds toward a more dynamic assessment of disease activity.

As the management of bronchiectasis evolves toward precision medicine based on inflammatory endotypes and treatable traits [112], future research must prioritize the inclusion of older adults in clinical trials and the generation of age-specific safety evidence. Additionally, it is crucial to validate biomarkers and therapeutic strategies in aging populations characterized by multimorbidity and physiological vulnerability. Only through this precision medicine approach, which includes older adults, will it be possible to translate advances in anti-inflammatory therapy into meaningful clinical benefits for those most affected by bronchiectasis.
