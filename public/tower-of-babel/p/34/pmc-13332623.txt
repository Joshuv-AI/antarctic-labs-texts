Heyde Syndrome in Moderate Bioprosthetic Aortic Valve Stenosis
Authors: Husssain Ahmed Raza, Mohammad Basel Allaw, Jordan Harris, Amit Pursnani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeyde syndrome (HS) is a rare disease characterized by aortic stenosis (AS), gastrointestinal bleeding, and acquired von Willebrand syndrome.Case SummaryA 91-year-old woman with anemia and (recurrent) AS with subsequent surgical bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement presented with exertional chest tightness. Work-up revealed marked anemia with hemoglobin of 6.8 g/dL, a degenerative bioprosthetic aortic valve with moderate AS, multiple bleeding cecal angiodysplastic lesions, and platelet dysfunction, supporting a diagnosis of HS. Aortic valve replacement was deferred. Conservative treatment included endoscopic ablation of angiodysplasias, blood transfusions, and iron supplementation.DiscussionExisting guidelines recommend aortic valve replacement for symptomatic severe AS, but no specific recommendations for moderate bioprosthetic valve AS with HS exist. This creates a clinical gap with individualized treatment.Take-Home MessageHS is under-recognized; treatment of HS in the context of moderate bioprosthetic valve AS falls outside evidence-based pathways and may be managed by conservative strategies.Key words: aortic valve, stenosis, valve replacement, valve repair

History of Presentation

A 91-year-old woman presented to the emergency room with dyspnea and chest “pressure” for several weeks, with symptoms progressing over the previous week. She reported dark stools, which she attributed to iron supplements. Vital signs were stable. On cardiac examination, she had a regular rate and rhythm and a soft, grade 2/6, midsystolic ejection murmur with a crescendo-decrescendo pattern.

Past Medical History

The patient had a prior history of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction of 67%, aortic stenosis (AS) with subsequent surgical bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement (19-mm St Jude Trifecta in 2012), postoperative ventricular tachycardia with subsequent implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement that was later upgraded to biventricular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator because of complete heart block and worsening dyspnea on exertion, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and chronic normocytic anemia. There was no prior history of blood transfusions.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included the following: angina, acute coronary syndrome, aortic stenosis, bioprosthetic valve dysfunction, hemolytic anemia, and lower gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB).

Investigations

Complete blood count revealed a hemoglobin of 6.6 g/dL (baseline around 10.0 g/dL). Electrocardiography revealed an A-V dual paced rhythm. Serial troponins were stable in the 43 to 51 ng/L range. Pro–B-type natriuretic peptide was 1,944 pg/mL, roughly 3 times higher than prior levels. Complete metabolic panel was within normal limits. Chest x-ray revealed cardiomegaly with mild prominence of the pulmonary vascularity.

Given the report of chest pressure, the patient underwent coronary computed tomography angiography, which revealed no significant plaque in the left anterior descending artery and nonobstructive coronary atherosclerosis in the left circumflex and right coronary arteries, along with mild bioprosthetic leaflet calcification (Video 1). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 58% and a degenerative bioprosthetic aortic valve (19-mm St Jude Trifecta) with moderate aortic valve stenosis (peak velocity: 3.4 m/s, mean gradient: 22 mm Hg, calculated aortic valve gradient: 1.2 cm2) (Video 2 and Video 3). Although patient-prosthesis mismatch and structural valve deterioration are not mutually exclusive, as many patients with high gradients may have both conditions, given our patient had a mean transvalvular gradient of 11 mm Hg on recent echocardiograms in 2022 and 2024, a markedly increased mean gradient of 22 mm Hg on presentation supported structural valve deterioration as the more likely cause for her moderate AS.1

She then underwent work-up of her anemia, which revealed low iron and ferritin levels consistent with iron deficiency anemia. Hemolysis laboratory findings, including reticulocyte count and haptoglobin, were normal. Gastroenterology was consulted given concern for occult GIB. The patient underwent an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, which found erosive gastropathy with no stigmata of recent bleeding. Subsequently, she underwent a colonoscopy, which found multiple, medium-sized, localized angiodysplastic lesions in the cecum with stigmata of recent bleeding (Figure 1).

Multiple Medium-Sized Localized Angiodysplastic Lesions With Stigmata of Recent Bleeding Found in the Cecum

Given the triad of anemia, bioprosthetic aortic valve stenosis, and cecal angiodysplastic lesions, specific testing for Heyde syndrome (HS) was conducted. The patient demonstrated elevated factor VIII activity (293%; normal: 60%-150%) and von Willebrand factor (vWF) antigen levels (301%; normal 50%-160%), both of which are acute-phase reactants that may increase in response to inflammation or stress. Although vWF activity was just above the normal range (203%; normal range: 50%-200%), closure times were measured using ADP/collagen and epinephrine/collagen platelet function assays to assess for a defect in platelet aggregation. Closure times were elevated for both the ADP/collagen assay (206 seconds; normal: 58-116 seconds) and the epinephrine/collagen assay (230 seconds; normal: 75-187 seconds). These findings supported a diagnosis of HS and aligned with a previous case report suggesting that closure times may be more representative than traditional vWF assays in detecting subtle abnormalities in vWF function.2

Management

Aortic valve replacement (AVR) or valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) was deferred given the bioprosthetic stenosis was only moderate in severity and procedural risks outweighed immediate benefit. Endoscopic ablation of angiodysplasias was performed to control active bleeding. The patient underwent transfusion with 1 unit of packed red blood cells and did not require further blood transfusions during her admission. Antihypertensive medications were held initially because of bleeding concerns but were later resumed. Her aspirin (taking before admission) was held after discussion between the cardiology and gastroenterology teams, given that risks outweigh benefits and no clear anticoagulation or antiplatelet guidelines exist in HS. Frequent monitoring of hemoglobin and cardiac status was advised for early detection of recurrent bleeding or AS progression. She was advised possible intervention if bleeding were to recur.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged 3 days after admission. Upon discharge, her hemoglobin was 7.9 g/dL, and her symptoms of shortness of breath and chest pressure had resolved. She has since followed-up in the cardiology clinic and is doing well. Hemoglobin levels are stable and no further transfusions have been needed. She is scheduled for a repeat echocardiogram 1 year after hospital discharge.

Discussion

HS is a rare multisystem disorder defined by a triad of AS, lower GIB, and acquired von Willebrand syndrome (AVWS).3 Studies indicate that 1% to 3% of patients with moderate to severe AS experience clinically significant GIB.3 The widely accepted pathophysiology involves shearing of large vWF multimers as they pass through a stenotic aortic valve, ultimately resulting in AVWS.3 While awareness of this rare condition is increasing, it is often under-recognized given the diagnosis is often challenging and delayed, as supported by a recent systematic review of 77 case reports that found the mean duration from symptom onset to diagnosis or treatment to be 23.8 ± 39 months.4 Furthermore, this clinical syndrome is infrequently reported in cases of moderate AS and/or in patients who have previously undergone bioprosthetic AVR. Most available literature focuses on severe AS, where the pathophysiological mechanism and the benefits of intervention are most apparent. Although moderate AS can generate sufficient shear stress to cause vWF degradation and subsequent GIB, specific management guidelines remain poorly defined.

A meta-analysis demonstrated that post-AVR recovery of von Willebrand multimers occurred in approximately 87% of patients with HS, and GIB ceased in approximately 73%, supporting valve replacement as the most effective treatment modality.5 Notably, a subanalysis indicated a faster resolution of AVWS and a higher rate of cessation of GIB after surgical aortic valve replacement compared with TAVR.5 However, as these data predominantly originate from populations with severe AS, extrapolation to moderate AS should be approached with caution.

Recent reviews highlight the absence of randomized trial data regarding valve intervention in moderate AS, and current guidelines restrict AVR for moderate AS primarily to patients undergoing concomitant cardiac surgery. The American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) and European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (ESC/EACTS) guidelines assign this a Class IIb (ACC/AHA) and Class IIa (ESC/EACTS) recommendation, reflecting the lack of randomized evidence supporting isolated AVR for moderate AS.6 The TAVR UNLOAD trial results indicate that definitive, practice-changing evidence for routine intervention in moderate AS is not yet available. However, quality-of-life improvements observed in the TAVR UNLOAD trial and other ongoing studies, such as the PROGRESS trial, may inform future guideline updates.7,8 Management remains complex: Although valve replacement is definitive, conservative approaches such as endoscopic therapy, blood transfusion, and close monitoring are essential in moderate cases. Early identification may prevent recurrent bleeding and reduce morbidity by enabling timely AVR, as supported by 2 prior studies finding 73% to 88% of patients had no recurrent GIB after AVR.5,9 A recent systematic review also demonstrates that even moderate AS may precipitate clinically significant HS, underscoring the importance of vigilance in elderly patients with unexplained GIB.4

An additional challenge involves managing antithrombotic therapy in patients with HS, many of whom require aspirin or anticoagulants for comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation or coronary artery disease. In this context, reducing the antithrombotic burden may theoretically lower the risk of GIB. However, formal guidance is limited, and clinical decisions must carefully balance thrombotic risk against bleeding propensity, necessitating multidisciplinary clinical judgment.

Conclusions

Recognition of HS in moderate AS or prior bioprosthetic AVR is rare, posing diagnostic and management challenges. HS should be considered in moderate AS, as it can still generate sufficient shear stress to cause vWF degradation and subsequent GIB. Although valve replacement is definitive in severe AS with high rates of GIB resolution and vWF normalization, conservative strategies including endoscopic therapy and blood transfusions with close monitoring may be more appropriate than AVR in moderate AS. Early identification may prevent recurrent bleeding and reduce morbidity by timely intervention.
