The Diagnostic Dilemma of Incidentally Detected Pulmonary Nodules: A Fatal Case of Pulmonary Epithelioid Hemangioendothelioma
Authors: Qian Yao, Honghui Jiang, Daiyan Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (PEH) is a rare, low‐ to intermediate‐grade malignant vascular tumour. We present a 63‐year‐old woman with incidentally identified bilateral pulmonary nodules, which demonstrated indolent features on PET‐CT (SUVmax 1.8). She returned 4 years later with progressive dyspnoea, a pleural effusion, and radiologic evidence of disease progression. Histopathological examination of a biopsy specimen confirmed PEH, with tumour cells showing positivity for CD31 and a Ki‐67 proliferation index of 20%. Despite treatment with a gemcitabine and carboplatin regimen, she developed pleural metastases and succumbed to her disease within a year of diagnosis. This case underscores the potential for aggressive progression in PEH, the prognostic value of the Ki‐67 index and the critical need for early histopathological confirmation and more effective therapies.Keywords: epithelioid hemangioendothelioma, pleural effusion, pulmonary nodules

Introduction

Epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (EHE) is a malignant vascular neoplasm occupying an intermediate position between hemangioma and angiosarcoma, with a reported incidence of approximately 1 in 100,000 [1]. This tumour can arise at various anatomical sites, most frequently the soft tissue, bone, liver and lung. Accounting for approximately 30% of all EHE cases, pulmonary epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (PEH) is composed of epithelioid or histiocytoid neoplastic cells characteristically arranged in cord‐like and nest‐like structures within a stroma rich in myxochondroid matrix [1]. Owing to its rarity, the published literature on PEH is predominantly composed of case reports and small case series. Consequently, no standardised diagnostic or therapeutic guidelines have been established. This lack of consolidated evidence underscores the importance of detailed case reports in improving the understanding of its clinical behaviour, diagnostic features and management outcomes. In this report, we present an illustrative case of PEH, aiming to contribute to the ongoing clinical characterisation of this rare malignancy.

Case Report

A 63‐year‐old woman with no significant medical history was found to have multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules on a computed tomography (CT) scan performed following trauma in July 2020 (Figure 1A). Subsequent integrated positron emission tomography‐computed tomography (PET‐CT) revealed minimal fluorodeoxyglucose uptake, with a maximum standardised uptake value (SUVmax) of 1.8, findings consistent with a benign process. Since the patient was asymptomatic, a biopsy was not pursued, and she was lost to follow‐up.

Serial chest CT scans obtained at our institution on 2020‐07‐07 (A), 2024‐03‐21 (B), 2024‐11‐29 (C), and 2025‐06‐23 (D). CT revealed multiple solid nodular opacities bilaterally, demonstrating progressive enlargement and numerical increase compared with prior imaging. These lesions exhibited well‐demarcated margins but irregular morphologies, with select nodules demonstrating partial calcification. The dominant lesion, located within the superior lingular segment of the left upper lobe, measured 26 × 31 mm in axial dimensions (B). The post‐therapeutic assessment revealed interval stabilisation of the pulmonary nodules, accompanied by concomitant diffuse pleural thickening (C, D).

In March 2024, she re‐presented with progressive chest pain and dyspnea. Follow‐up chest CT revealed an interval increase in both the size and number of pulmonary nodules, along with a new, moderate left‐sided pleural effusion (Figure 1B). Serum CA‐125 was markedly elevated at 12,570.8 U/mL. A percutaneous core needle biopsy of a representative pulmonary nodule was performed on April 17, 2024. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of pulmonary epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (PEH). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated strong and diffuse positivity for CD31, with a Ki‐67 proliferation index of 20% (Figure 2).

Histopathological examination of lung biopsy specimen (haematoxylin and eosin, original magnification ×100). Histopathological examination revealed epithelioid tumour cells arranged in distinctive cord‐like structures, demonstrating an infiltrative growth pattern within the alveolar septa. The neoplastic cells possessed abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm containing characteristic intracytoplasmic vacuoles (arrows), representing primitive vasoformative lumina—a hallmark feature of epithelioid hemangioendothelioma.

The patient commenced combination chemotherapy consisting of gemcitabine (1.8 g on days 1 and 8) and carboplatin (600 mg on day 1) on a 21‐day cycle. However, only four cycles were administered over 9 months due to suboptimal treatment adherence; the cycles were administered on April 30, May 31, November 27, 2024 and January 18, 2025. Her subsequent clinical course was complicated by recurrent, symptomatic malignant pleural effusions requiring repeated thoracenteses and radiologically confirmed pleural metastases. Her performance status declined progressively, and she succumbed to her disease in July 2025.

Discussion

PEH is an exceedingly rare, low‐ to intermediate‐grade vascular malignancy. It has a female predominance, with an incidence that is 2–4 times higher than in males; up to 42% of patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis [1, 2]. Radiologically, PEH typically manifests as multiple, bilateral perivascular nodules. Some nodules may exhibit calcification, a feature associated with long‐term tumour evolution [3]. This imaging pattern often mimics metastatic disease, highlighting the critical importance of pathological confirmation. While 18F‐FDG‐PET plays a crucial role in evaluating pulmonary nodules, EHE lesions demonstrate variable metabolic activity [4]. Reported SUVmax values range from 1.2 to 17.1 (mean 5.3 ± 3.3) across anatomical locations [5]. However, false‐negative findings with minimal FDG uptake [6], as observed in our case (SUVmax 1.8), have been documented. Such false negatives may result from small lesion size, low tumour metabolic activity, or inadequate cellular density, whereas intense FDG uptake often reflects biological aggressiveness and poor prognosis [7]. The radiological features of PEH may resemble granulomatous diseases, pneumoconiosis, pulmonary hamartoma, or other benign conditions [5]. Therefore, negative 18F‐FDG‐PET findings cannot definitively exclude PEH. In cases of persistent multiple pulmonary nodules, prompt pathological biopsy remains essential for definitive diagnosis.

Histopathologically, PEH is characterised by epithelioid tumour cells exhibiting abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, intracytoplasmic lumina (vacuoles), and the presence of Weibel–Palade bodies. Immunohistochemically, tumour cells are typically positive for endothelial markers, including CD31 and CD34. Molecular studies that demonstrate WWTR1–CAMTA1 or YAP1–TFE3 gene fusions can provide confirmatory diagnostic evidence [8]. In the present case, the combination of typical histomorphology (epithelioid cells with intracytoplasmic vacuolization) and a supportive immunohistochemical profile (positive for CD31) was sufficient for a definitive diagnosis.

Owing to the rarity of PEH, no standardised treatment guidelines exist, necessitating individualised management strategies. Surgical resection represents the primary curative approach for localised disease, though selected cases with bilateral involvement have also undergone successful resection [9, 10]. For asymptomatic patients, active surveillance with 3–6‐month follow‐up is recommended, with some cases demonstrating spontaneous regression over extended periods [8, 11]. In advanced or metastatic disease, conventional chemotherapy remains the mainstay, with regimens combining taxanes or gemcitabine with platinum agents frequently employed [2, 12]. However, as demonstrated in our case, the efficacy of these regimens is often limited. Recently, anti‐angiogenic therapies have emerged as a promising alternative, with reported success using bevacizumab [13], pazopanib [14], sorafenib [15], and other VEGF‐targeting agents [16]. These approaches are supported by the documented overexpression of VEGF in PEH, which correlates with tumour aggressiveness and metastatic potential [16]. For selected patients failing conventional therapies, transplantation may represent a viable option. While liver transplantation has established efficacy for hepatic EHE [9, 17], lung transplantation warrants consideration for PEH patients progressing to respiratory failure, following careful evaluation for vascular invasion.

The prognosis for PEH is highly variable. Adverse prognostic factors include a Ki‐67 proliferation index of ≥ 10%, pleural effusion, symptomatic presentation and extrapulmonary metastases [12]. The clinical course of our patient contrasts markedly with a recently reported case of primary pleural EHE by Chou et al. [18] wherein a 45‐year‐old woman achieved sustained disease stabilisation with weekly paclitaxel. This prognostic disparity likely stems from several distinctions. Demographically, our patient was older and presented with primary pulmonary disease progressing to pleural dissemination, unlike the primarily pleural involvement in Chou's case. More significantly, our case exhibited multiple established high‐risk features: elevated Ki‐67 index (20%), malignant pleural effusion, and imaging‐confirmed pleural metastasis. In contrast, Chou's case lacked documented Ki‐67 elevation at treatment initiation. Despite gemcitabine‐carboplatin chemotherapy, the constellation of adverse prognostic markers in our case likely contributed to the aggressive course and fatal outcome. Conversely, the absence of such high‐risk features may explain the favourable paclitaxel response in Chou's case. This comparison underscores the substantial prognostic heterogeneity in EHE and emphasises the need for risk‐adapted treatment strategies.

This report describes the clinical course of a patient with PEH, highlighting a pattern of initial indolent behaviour followed by aggressive transformation. The patient's initial presentation included incidentally identified pulmonary nodules, leading to the adoption of a conservative approach due to their benign radiographic characteristics. Several years later, the patient was readmitted with chest pain, massive pleural effusion and radiologic progression of the pulmonary nodules, and ultimately succumbed to the disease within 1 year of definitive diagnosis. In the present case, the elevated Ki‐67 proliferation index (20%) and progressive pleural involvement represented key observations that correspond to established markers of poor prognosis. Collectively, this case illustrates several critical insights. First, it underscores the importance of including PEH in the differential diagnosis of multiple pulmonary nodules and obtaining early histopathological confirmation. Second, it corroborates the prognostic significance of the Ki‐67 proliferation index. Finally, it demonstrates the pressing need for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies for advanced‐stage PEH. Future investigations into the molecular drivers of this disease are essential to guide the development of targeted therapies and improve patient outcomes.
