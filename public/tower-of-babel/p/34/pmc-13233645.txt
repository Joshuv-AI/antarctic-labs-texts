Trends in Multi-dose Drug Dispensing Amongst Older Adults in Sweden: A Nationwide Repeated Cross-Sectional Register Study, 2014–2023
Authors: Hilary Shepherd, Kristina Johnell, Jonas W Wastesson, Máté Szilcz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMulti-dose dispensing (MDD) is a medication management system in Sweden that pre-packages regularly used prescription medicines into unit doses. Despite widespread use, there are no recent national estimates of MDD prevalence in Sweden, and little is known about how use evolved through the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic or which patient groups account for most MDD utilisation.ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to describe the prevalence of multi-dose dispensing in older adults in Sweden from 2014 to 2023, spanning the COVID-19 pandemic, and describe trends by patient subgroups.MethodsWe conducted a repeated cross-sectional study of older adults aged ≥ 70 years old in Sweden, 2014–2023. We used nationwide data from the Swedish Prescribed Drug Register linked at the individual level with several other administrative and healthcare registers. The primary outcome was annual period prevalence of MDD, stratified by age, sex, education level, living status, civil status, health status and geography.ResultsPrevalence of MDD amongst adults aged 70 years or older increased by 1.5 percentage points between 2014 and 2023, reaching 12.1% in 2023, and there was no additional increase during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022). In nursing home residents, MDD use approached near-universal levels (from 84.2 to 96.1%). Uptake also increased across other subgroups, most notably amongst people with a dementia diagnosis (+ 45.3 percentage points), those receiving home help services (+ 21.4 percentage points) and those with five or more chronic conditions (+ 15.3 percentage points). Over time, the characteristics of MDD users shifted towards community-dwelling adults receiving home help services, younger older adults (70–79 years old) and individuals with higher education.ConclusionsOur nationwide study indicates that MDD has become an established standard of medication management in Swedish nursing homes and is progressively extending into community settings amongst older adults with complex health needs. Prescribing of MDD remained stable during the COVID-19 pandemic. Future studies should quantify risks and benefits of MDD use especially in vulnerable, older, and frail populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40266-026-01304-x.

Key Points

In Sweden, use of multi-dose drug dispensing (MDD) amongst adults aged 70 years or older increased from 10.6% in 2014 to 12.1% in 2023, with no clear additional increase during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.MDD use increased across almost all living settings and subgroups. By 2023, in nursing homes, MDD use was near universal (used by 96% of residents). When stratified by health characteristics, the largest increases were amongst people with a dementia diagnosis, those receiving home help services, and those with five or more chronic conditions.Over time, MDD users have increasingly been community-dwelling adults receiving home help services, younger older adults (70–79 years), and people with higher education, suggesting a growing role for MDD in supporting older people with complex health needs to live at home.

Introduction

Multi-dose dispensing (MDD) is a medication management system in which regular prescription medicines are pre-packaged in correct doses (excluding medicines that cannot be dosed, for example, ointments, or that must be taken in-person, for example, fluids). MDD systems have been implemented in primary healthcare services across several European countries, including Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland and the Netherlands [1, 2]. Although the specific dispensing procedures and reimbursement models differ between countries, the core principles are the same: medicines are machine-packed into individual bags labelled with the patient’s name, packet contents, dose, the time medicines should be taken, and are delivered at regular intervals. MDD is intended for people who have difficulties managing their own medications, either because collecting medicines via the standard dispensing system is difficult or because they have complex regimens involving multiple medicines. It is frequently used by older adults with multimorbidity living at home and by residents of nursing homes [3]. In Sweden, MDD is typically delivered to the patient every second week in a dosing roll (most common brand name in Sweden: ‘ApoDos’) [4]. As the population of older adults with complex care needs grows and medication regimens become more complex, understanding national trends and user characteristics of MDD is important for planning pharmaceutical services, identifying patients who may benefit from medication reviews, and informing policy on medication management in countries where MDD is used.

Prior research suggests that MDD may improve stock management [5] and reduce time, costs, and errors associated with medicine preparation [3, 6, 7]. One study in the Netherlands reported that patients using MDD in a community setting showed significantly greater medication adherence than non-MDD patients, but sample size was low (n = 215) and based on self-reported adherence [8]. At the same time, MDD may also be associated with an increased number of drugs [4], polypharmacy [9] and potentially inappropriate prescribing [3]. These patterns likely reflect confounding by indication, since individuals selected for MDD are often frailer and have greater care needs than non-users [10]. Despite its widespread use across northern Europe, national level evidence on the prevalence, trends, and characteristics of MDD users remains limited.

In Sweden, 11% of individuals over 65 used MDD in 2008 [10], but these estimates are now almost two decades old, and no study has since examined trends across clinically relevant subgroups. Further, national data describing which patient groups most commonly use MDD, and how their characteristics have changed over time, are limited. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (2020–2022) adds urgency to this question. The pandemic disrupted healthcare delivery, limited in-person pharmacy visits and placed extraordinary pressure on nursing homes and home care services [11, 12]. MDD, as a system that reduces the need for repeated pharmacy contact and supports medication management at a distance, could plausibly have seen increased uptake during this period. Whether this occurred has not been examined. Understanding how MDD is used amongst older adults, including during global pandemics, is needed to anticipate future demand on dispensing services, to assess whether MDD reaches the patient groups who are likely to benefit most, and to evaluate whether healthcare disruptions affect medication management for vulnerable populations. Characterising the patients using MDD may help identify patient groups most likely to benefit from targeted interventions, such as medication reviews.

Therefore, this study aimed to (1) describe the prevalence of multi-dose dispensing in older adults in Sweden from 2014 to 2023, spanning the COVID-19 pandemic, and (2) describe trends by patient subgroup, including age, sex, education level, living status, civil status, health status and geography. We anticipated that MDD prevalence would increase over time in line with population ageing and organisational changes during the pandemic.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We used a repeated cross-sectional study design and calculated the prevalence of MDD each year from 2014 to 2023. We included adults who were alive and aged ≥ 70 each year. We excluded patients with an emigration or immigration date after 1 January or who were < 70 years old by 31 December of the given year.

Data Sources

Data were obtained by linking several Swedish national registers via the personal identity number assigned to all Swedish residents [13]. An overview of the registers is provided in Supplementary Table 1. The Swedish Total Population Register (TPR), maintained by Statistics Sweden, was used to determine the key demographics of sex, age, county of residence in Sweden, country of birth and civil status [14]. It was linked, via personal identity number, to the National Cause of Death Register for date of death used to determine individuals who were alive in each year [15], and to the Swedish Register of Education for highest educational attainment [16]. The Swedish Prescribed Drug Register records all medication dispensed in Sweden [17], including drug substance and brand name, formulation, package and dispensed amount. The register has near-complete national coverage as all pharmacies in Sweden are required to report dispensed prescriptions. These data were used to determine records of multi-dose dispensing each year. The Swedish National Patient Register contains more than 99% of all somatic and psychiatric hospital discharges and specialised outpatient care. It includes details of diagnoses recorded using International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes [18], and has been previously validated for use in population-based research [19]. We used information from this database to determine the presence of dementia and multimorbidity. The National Register of Care and Social Services for the Elderly and Persons with Impairments, maintained by the National Board of Health and Welfare, reports living arrangements and types of care provided on a monthly basis and was used to determine living status [20].

Outcomes and Covariates

The primary outcome was annual period prevalence of MDD in each year from 2014 to 2023. A record of MDD at any point during the year (i.e. 1 January–31 December inclusive) was identified by prescription type in the Swedish Prescribed Drug Register for all years except 2017. In 2017, a report from the National Board of Health and Welfare [21] indicated that approximately 2.4 million records were given incorrect prescription type values between March and May. Therefore, in 2017, a record of MDD was identified by a prescription length of 14 days when calculating the end date of the prescription minus the date the patient purchased the item. This was chosen because MDD is most commonly prescribed every 2 weeks [4]. To determine the treatment duration, we used the prescribed daily dose on the basis of the free text input of the prescriber. This method for determining end date of prescription is described in detail elsewhere [22, 23].

We categorised sex as male or female; civil status as married/cohabiting, single/divorced, or widowed (Supplementary Table 2); and age (calculated on 31 December) as 70–79, 80–89, or ≥ 90 each year. For all analysis years, each patient was assigned their highest attained education level. As all included individuals were ≥ 70 years old, further changes in education were considered unlikely. We stratified education levels into primary/elementary, secondary, and tertiary education levels to align with the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) categories [24] (Supplementary Table 3). We defined six healthcare regions (Supplementary Table 4), and birth country was grouped into broader categories of European, the Americas, Asia and Oceania (combined into a single category due to the small number of individuals born in Oceania, which precluded meaningful separate analysis) and Africa (Supplementary Table 5). Living status was categorised annually using the National Register of Care and Social Services for the Elderly and Persons with Impairments. Individuals with ≥ 2 calendar months of residence in a nursing home in a given year were classified as nursing home residents. This threshold was chosen to exclude short-term stays, such as respite or rehabilitation care, and to capture individuals with stable nursing home residence. Those with > 25 hours of home help services per year (beyond emergency alarm services or support with activities of daily living) during any month of the year were classified as receiving home help services. This cut-off was chosen to identify individuals receiving substantial, regular home help that would plausibly include assistance with medication management, as opposed to minimal services such as safety alarms alone. Both of these living status definitions have been used in previous Swedish register-based research [25]. All other individuals were classified as living in the community. We defined the multimorbidity status for each calendar year by identifying chronic conditions recorded during that year or the preceding five years (a rolling 5-year window, for example, 2010–2014 for the 2014 prevalence estimate, 2011–2015 for the 2015 prevalence estimates, etc.). Conditions were grouped into 60 chronic disease categories on the basis of existing methods [26] (Supplementary Table 6). We then counted the number of chronic conditions within the 5-year window and categorised individuals as having 0–1, 2–4, or ≥ 5 conditions. The presence of an ICD code for dementia in any year was assumed as a diagnosis. The number of concurrent drugs was defined as the number of distinct therapeutic subgroups dispensed between 1 January and 31 March of each year, and categorised as 0-4 (no polypharmacy), 5-9 (polypharmacy) or ≥ 10 (excessive polypharmacy). This was done on the basis of established cut-offs [27, 28] using the 4th level ATC code, which treats drugs within the same therapeutic class as interchangeable, thereby avoiding inflation of the count due to within-class switching. Where civil status or region was missing, the last observed value was carried forward where available, and a missing category was created where it was not available.

These covariates were selected to describe the sociodemographic and clinical profile of MDD users and to capture factors known or hypothesised to influence the need for medication management support. Age, sex, civil status and living status reflect care needs and the availability of informal support. Education level is an indicator of health literacy and socioeconomic status, both of which may affect prescribing patterns and healthcare utilisation. Country of birth was included because language barriers and differences in healthcare-seeking behaviour may influence how older adults access pharmaceutical services, including MDD. Dementia and multimorbidity were included as key clinical indicators of medication complexity. The number of concurrent drugs was included as MDD is intended for individuals with complex medication regimens [9], and the number of concurrent drugs may both drive the need for MDD and reflect prescribing patterns amongst users [29].

Statistical Analysis

Prevalence of MDD use was calculated, overall and by living status, each year from 2014 to 2023 as a proportion of the total population aged 70 years or over. The numerator was the number of unique patients with at least one record of MDD at any point in the given year who were alive and ≥ 70 at the end of each year. The denominator was all unique patients who were alive and ≥ 70 years old at the end of each year. Absolute differences in the proportion of MDD users were calculated as percentage point differences. Relative increases in prevalence between 2014 and 2023 were calculated as percentage increases using the formula [(prevalence2023 − prevalence2014)/prevalence2014] × 100. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations (SD), ranges, and proportions, were used to describe the population of MDD users. As the analyses covered the entire Swedish population aged ≥ 70, no formal significance testing was performed. R version 4.3.1 was used for all analyses.

Ethical Approval

Data were anonymised and stored on a secure server at Karolinska Institutet, accessible only to authorised members of the research group. The Regional Ethical Review Board in Stockholm approved the study (dnr: 2016/1001-31/4, 2020-03525, 2021-02004).

Results

Characteristics of MDD Users

Across all years, most MDD users were female, widowed, and had five or more chronic conditions (Table 1). Over time, a growing share of the population lived at home, were aged 70–79 years, had secondary or tertiary education, were born outside Europe, and had excessive polypharmacy (≥ 10 concurrent drugs). The share of all people using MDD living in nursing homes (47.0–35.9%) and with dementia (40.0–19.3%) declined, reflecting a shrinking underlying population in both groups overall (nursing home residents: 75,526–70,998, people with a recorded dementia diagnosis: 173,126–47,854 - see Supplementary Table 7).

Overall Prevalence of MDD Use

The absolute number of people aged 70 years and older with a record of MDD increased from 135,189 in 2014 to 189,967 in 2023 (40.5% relative increase in number of users). Overall, MDD prevalence increased by 1.5 percentage points from 10.6 to 12.1% (14.2% relative increase in prevalence) over the study period (see Table 2), and there was no additional increase observed during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022).

Prevalence of MDD Use by Subgroups

The greatest absolute increases in prevalence between 2014 and 2023 occurred in people with dementia (31.3–76.6%), people receiving home help services (35.4–56.8%), individuals with ≥ 5 chronic conditions (11.8–27.1%), nursing home residents (84.2–96.1%) and the ≥ 90 age category (39.4–47.7%) (Fig. 1). A notable increase was also observed amongst those with excessive polypharmacy (≥ 10 concurrent drugs, 32.8–37.8%). Prevalence in all other subgroups increased by five percentage points or less during the study period, except for those with 0–4 drugs, 0–1 chronic conditions and those born in the Americas, where a decline of 8.6, 2.3 and 0.7 percentage points were observed, respectively. There was consistenly higher prevalence observed amongst female individuals (between 3.8 and 5.0% higher than male individuals and decreasing over time), individuals with the lowest level of education (between 5.0 and 6.2% higher than those with secondary education and 8.0 and 9.8% higher than those with tertiary education and both increasing over time), widowed individuals (between 7.7 and 11.0% higher than single/divorced people and between 15.5 and 18.6% higher than married/cohabiting people, both increasing over time), and amongst those born in Europe versus Africa (between 3.6 and 4.7% greater in Europe and decreasing over time), but little difference to those born in Asia and Oceania or the Americas.

Prevalence of multi-dose dispensing in subgroups of older adults aged ≥ 70 years, 2014–2023

In relative terms, the largest percentage increases were observed amongst people with dementia (+ 145.3%), those with ≥ 5 chronic conditions (+ 129.8%) and those receiving home help services (+ 60.6%) (Fig. 2). Community-dwelling older adults also showed a substantial relative increase (+ 57.8%). In contrast, nursing home residents showed a more modest relative increase (+ 14.2%), reflecting the already high baseline prevalence in this group. MDD prevalence declined in relative terms amongst those with 0–1 chronic conditions (− 30.1%) and those born in the Americas (− 6.1%). Prevalence of MDD Use Stratified by Living Status

Amongst nursing home residents, the greatest increases were observed in the south-eastern (74.3–92.9%) and northern (78.3–95.0%) healthcare regions of Sweden, the subgroup born in Africa (82.0–97.1%), those married/cohabiting (81.6–96.0%) and those with no dementia (81.3–95.1%) (Fig. 3). Prevalence was consistently higher in female individuals and those living with dementia or ≥ 5 chronic conditions, but differences by education, geography, civil status and age were minimal. Prevalence amongst all subgroups of nursing home residents converged at high levels by 2023 (≥ 93%). In the population receiving home help services, the greatest increases were observed in those with a dementia diagnosis (46.4–78.3%), those born in Africa (17.0–46.9%), and individuals with ≥ 5 chronic conditions (36.4–63.5%) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Amongst those living in the community, the greatest increases were observed in people with dementia (5.8–33.4%), those with ≥ 5 chronic conditions (2.8–9.1%), those aged ≥ 90 years (6.5–12.7%) and individuals who were widowed (3.1–5.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

In Sweden, use of multi-dose dispensing (MDD) amongst adults aged 70 years or older increased from 10.6% in 2014 to 12.1% in 2023. We did not observe an additional increase during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022). Uptake increased in almost all subgroups, but particularly in people diagnosed with dementia (+45.3 percentage points), those receiving home help services (+21.4 percentage points) and those with five or more chronic conditions (+15.3 percentage points). Over time, the profile of MDD users shifted towards community-dwelling adults receiving home help services, younger older adults (70–79 years old) and individuals with higher education. Overall, the findings indicated that MDD has become the predominant mode of medication management in Swedish nursing homes, as 96.1% of residents used MDD by 2023. Results show increased prevalence in community settings over time, indicating that either MDD or its target population of older adults with complex health needs are increasingly present in the community. The COVID-19 pandemic had little discernible impact on MDD prescribing trends.

In addition to providing the first national estimates of MDD prevalence across clinically relevant subgroups and during the COVID-19 pandemic, our prevalence estimates serve as an update to those of Sjoberg et al., who reported that 11% of adults aged >65 years used MDD in Sweden in 2008 [10]. While there is no published cross-country comparison on prevalence, some articles report the number of MDD users at a national level which are substantially lower than our estimates for older adults in Sweden, for example, 2.2% in the Netherlands in 2011 [7], 0.4% in Finland in 2007 [7] and 0.8% in Denmark in 2010 [30]. However, these figures refer to the general population rather than older adults specifically, and differences in population subgroups, definitions, and time windows (both ages and calendar years) make direct comparisons with the aforementioned studies difficult.

The 1.5-percentage-point increase in crude MDD prevalence over 10 years represents a 14.2% relative increase, corresponding to almost 55,000 additional users. Notably, this increase occurred despite a decline in the proportion of older adults residing in nursing homes (from 5.9 to 4.5%) and receiving home help services (from 10.9 to 8.5%, see Supplementary Table 7), the two settings where MDD use is most concentrated. This suggests that the overall increase was driven by growing MDD adoption amongst community-dwelling older adults, who made up an increasing share of the population (from 83.2 to 87.0%). The relatively modest increase in crude prevalence contrasts with much steeper increases in certain high-risk subgroups, particularly people with dementia and those with ≥ 5 chronic conditions. Over time, these populations made up a progressively smaller share of the cohort, while MDD prevalence remained low or even declined in the expanding groups, such as those with ≤ 1 chronic condition. As a result, substantial increases in MDD use within some of these strata translate into only a modest rise in overall prevalence. Taken together with the general shift from nursing homes to home help and community living, these patterns are consistent with MDD use becoming increasingly concentrated amongst older adults with complex health needs living outside institutions. This could indicate that MDD is being used as part of broader efforts to support ageing in place, including home help services that frequently assist with medication management [5]. It should be noted that the shift in the profile of MDD users may partly reflect broader changes in where older adults receive care rather than changes in who is prescribed MDD, as the same patients who would previously have been in nursing homes are now increasingly managed in the community.

The larger increases in MDD prevalence amongst women and widowed individuals likely reflect that women live longer than men and are therefore more likely to survive into older age with multiple chronic conditions requiring medication support [31]. Widowed older adults, who are predominantly women, may also have a greater need for MDD as they lack a cohabiting partner who could assist with medication management. The apparent decline in the share of MDD users with dementia in nursing homes should be interpreted considering two concurrent trends. First, MDD prevalence amongst nursing home residents with dementia continued to increase over the study period, approaching near-universal use. Second, the overall profile of MDD users shifted from nursing homes towards community settings, meaning that while MDD use grew amongst people with dementia in nursing homes, it grew even faster in the community, diluting the relative share of nursing home residents amongst all MDD users.

The growth in MDD prevalence should also be considered in the context of increasing drug utilisation amongst older adults. In our data, the medication burden amongst MDD users shifted upwards over time, with the share using ten or more concurrent drugs increasing from 33.7 to 42.4%, while the median number of drugs rose from eight to nine (Supplementary Table 7). Amongst non-MDD users, the median number of drugs remained stable at four throughout the study period, suggesting that the increasing drug burden was concentrated amongst MDD users. This pattern aligns with broader national trends: a recent study using Swedish register data from 2006 to 2020 found that polypharmacy prevalence amongst older adults increased from 28.6 to 32.8%, driven primarily by faster medication accumulation amongst those aged 75–89 years [32]. Excessive polypharmacy (≥ 10 drugs) also increased nationally over the same period [33]. As therapeutic options expand and treatment guidelines evolve, the growing complexity of medication regimens is likely to increase both the need for and the actual use of MDD. However, whether the observed increase in drug counts amongst MDD users reflects more intensive treatment of underlying conditions, reduced flexibility in deprescribing within the MDD system, or a combination of both, cannot be determined from our data.

Several factors may have affected the overall growth in MDD use over the study period. MDD is designed for patients with stable medication regimens, yet many older adults with multimorbidity require frequent dose adjustments, medication switches, or short-term treatments that are poorly suited to the fixed 2-week dispensing schedule [34]. In such cases, clinicians may prefer standard prescribing to retain flexibility, as MDD has been associated with fewer drug treatment changes compared with ordinary prescriptions [10]. Additionally, initiating MDD requires coordination between prescribers, pharmacies and care services, and its practical implementation is constrained by organisational and interprofessional challenges, which may act as a barrier to uptake, particularly for community-dwelling older adults without regular home help contact. Furthermore, growing evidence of poorer quality of drug treatment amongst MDD users, including higher rates of potentially inappropriate medications and drug–drug interactions [9, 35], may have contributed to more selective use of MDD by prescribers.

The lack of a spike during the COVID-19 pandemic suggests that MDD was not used as an emergency response to limit pharmacy visits during the pandemic and reflects established patterns of use. More broadly, demand for MDD is likely to increase in the future as a growing number of older adults with complex health problems continue to live at home, while the availability of nursing home care remains relatively stable despite population ageing [4, 10, 11, 36]. However, this trajectory is not certain. The same complexity that drives the need for medication support also makes MDD more challenging to implement, as patients with multimorbidity often require frequent medication changes that conflict with the fixed dispensing schedule. Whether MDD use will continue to grow therefore depends on how well the system adapts to serve patients with dynamic treatment needs, for example, better integration of medication reviews into the MDD workflow.

Taken together, these patterns suggest that medication review efforts within the MDD system should be particularly directed towards community-dwelling older adults with high medication burden, where MDD use is growing fastest and the risk of suboptimal prescribing may be greatest. Additionally, future research should quantify both the benefits and risks of MDD use. Potential benefits include reduction in medication errors and drug waste, and increase in medication adherence, while potential risks include reduced flexibility in dose adjustments, polypharmacy, delayed deprescribing and clinical inertia in drug reviews. Future studies should also describe commonly prescribed drugs amongst MDD users and compare rates of MDD use across countries.

Strengths and Limitations

This large nationwide study leverages the longitudinal nature of well-established national registers to provide 10 years of prevalence data on MDD use, overall and across subgroups, amongst all people aged > 70 years in Sweden.

Our study also has limitations. Our study may include misclassification bias due to the method used to define MDD use, dementia and multimorbidity. We may have missed MDD dispensations which are not repeated every 2 weeks and also misclassified dispensations as MDD in 2017 due to known recording issues in the Swedish Prescribed Drug Register [21], which likely explains the peak in prevalence that year. In addition, our operational definitions of MDD use, residence in nursing homes and receipt of home help services are imperfect proxies based on the available registers. We did not determine the exact temporal ordering of these events (e.g. whether MDD started before or after entry into nursing home care or home help services), which may introduce further misclassification. This is unlikely to substantially affect our conclusions, as our aim was to describe patterns rather than to draw causal inferences. We did not have information for diagnoses set in primary care (due to lack of nationwide primary care data in Sweden). Moreover, dementia is likely underdiagnosed in the older population, particularly amongst nursing home residents whose cognitive decline may never be formally evaluated [37]. However, we used the most recognised MDD prescribing schedule of 2 weeks [4], used a dementia diagnosis of ‘ever’ to ensure we captured all historical cases and pre-defined methods to define multimorbidity to make results as comparable as possible with existing literature. Moreover, because we analysed annual prevalence, we may have missed short-term fluctuations in MDD use around the onset of COVID-19. Additionally, the exclusion of individuals who died on or before 31 December of a given year from prevalence calculations may lead to underestimation of the true prevalence of MDD use. Finally, our results may only be generalisable in settings similar to Sweden.

Conclusions

In this study, we found that overall prevalence of MDD increased by 1.5 percentage points between 2014 and 2023 in Sweden and did not change during the COVID-19 pandemic. Prevalence increased in almost all subgroups and MDD use was nearly universal in nursing homes by 2023. In future studies, researchers should quantify risks and benefits of MDD use especially in vulnerable, older and frail populations.
