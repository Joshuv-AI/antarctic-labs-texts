Latent profile analysis of core competency in intravenous therapy among Chinese clinical nurses: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Li Song, Li Yuan, Qiqi Ye, Zhijia Zhang, Jiating Song, Daiyuan Huang, Xi Wen, Qihua Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses’ core competency in intravenous (IV) therapy directly impacts patient safety and treatment outcomes. This study aimed to identify latent classes of nurses’ core competency in IV therapy using latent profile analysis and explore key factors influencing these classes to inform targeted interventions.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2023 to January 2024, involving 821 clinical nurses from 20 provincial-level regions in China. Data was collected using the validated Core Competency in Intravenous Therapy Scale. Latent profile analysis was used to identify competency classes, and multivariate logistic regression was performed to explore risk factors.ResultsThree distinct latent classes were identified: ‘Low IV-Core Competency’ (41.3%), ‘Moderate IV-Core Competency’ (45.9%), and ‘High IV-Core Competency’ (12.8%). Multivariate logistic regression showed that non-specialist nurses in IV therapy and non-participation in intravenous therapy training programs were strongly associated with lower competency.ConclusionsThere is a critical need to improve IV therapy core competency among clinical nurses. Investments in IV specialist nurse development and standardized training are essential to enhance therapy quality and patient safety.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Nurses, Intravenous therapy, Core competency, Latent profile analysis, Risk factors

Background

With the widespread use of peripherally inserted central catheter, midline catheters, and other advanced peripheral venous catheters, intravenous (IV) therapy has been applied across a range of clinical scenarios. The applications span from acute resuscitation to chronic disease management, including chemotherapy and home parenteral nutrition [1–3]. These devices extend dwell time and enable continuous infusion in both hospital and community settings, which shortens patients’ hospital stays and improves their quality of life [4]. Consequently, IV therapy is now one of most frequently employed treatment method in clinical practice. However, the safe use of venous access devices (VADs) is complex. It involves many aspects such as venous assessment, device selection, insertion, and ongoing management, all of which require nursing staff to possess a high level of core competency in IV therapy [3, 5]. This competency served as the foundation for safe IV therapy, because it directly affects nursing care quality, patient safety and treatment outcomes [6, 7]. Nurses who acquire and maintain these competencies are crucial for promoting the appropriate use of VADs in clinical settings [8]. Therefore, healthcare institutions must prioritize developing IV therapy core competency among their nursing staff.

However, there are significant gaps persisting in this area. For instance, a prospective audit in an intensive care unit revealed alarmingly low adherence to of central venous catheter care bundles: only 8% for documentation of dwell-time indication, 3% for aseptic handling technique, 15% for infusion-system integrity, and 17% for dressing management [9]. Similarly, a tertiary-care study found that 52% of adult inpatients received peripheral intravenous catheters inserted over joint surfaces (antecubital fossa, dorsum of hand or wrist), only 19% had eight-hourly visual infusion phlebitis scores recorded, 14.4% exhibited local signs of phlebitis, and 44% were unaware of the catheter’s purpose [4]. These marked departures from evidence-based standards underscore the urgent need for targeted educational programs to enhance nurses’ core competency in IV therapy, reduce catheter-related bloodstream infections and bridge the evidence to practice gap.

At present, most interventions on the core competencies in intravenous therapy mainly focuses on IV knowledge and skills [10–13]. However, core competency in the field of IV therapy not only theoretical knowledge and practical skills, but also involve critical thinking, the ability to educate colleagues, and the capacity to implement practice changes that align with Infusion Nurses Society standards [8]. Moreover, continuous advancements in evidence and technology require these competencies should be regularly updated [14].

Patricia Benner’s Novice-to-Expert Model suggests that the professional growth of nurses is a gradual process from novice to expert. Novice nurses rely on clear rules and guidance due to insufficient knowledge and experience. In contrast, expert nurses accumulate a solid theoretical foundation and rich clinical experience through years of practice, enabling them to solve problems efficiently and possessing the ability to disseminate knowledge and lead disciplinary development. This theory conceptually assumes the existence of heterogeneity subgroups in nursing competency development, but effectively identify these groups in empirical research has long been a challenge. Most of our research on IV therapy competency has used only global scores for stratification, which may obscure important subgroup differences. Because distinct competency subgroups may be influenced by different determinants. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) offers a more nuanced approach. As an individual-centered, model-based clustering technique, LPA can identify homogeneous subpopulations on the basis of multiple observed indicators, overcoming the limitations of traditional variable-centered methods that overlook individual variation [15].

Therefore, this study employ LPA to examine Benner’s theory by identifying latent classes of core competency in IV therapy among nurses and investigating the influencing factors across distinct subgroups. The findings are expected to refine nursing competency theory and provide an evidence base for designing precision interventions, ultimately improving nursing practice quality and patient safety.

Methods

Study design and participants

A multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted in China from October 2023 to January 2024. A stratified convenience sampling approach was used to enhance geographic and institutional representation while accommodating the practical constraints of a large-scale national survey. The sampling strategy included two levels of stratification. First, the country was divided into seven major socio-geographic regions in China (Eastern, Southern, Central, Northern, Southwestern, Northeastern, and Northwestern) to ensure broad coverage. Second, within each region, we aim to include both tertiary and secondary hospitals to reflect diverse clinical settings and resource levels. Hospitals and nurses within these strata were invited to participate on a voluntary basis. This method was selected as the most feasible approach for obtaining a nationwide sample in the absence of a comprehensive sample frame for random selection. The final sample consisted of 821 nurses from 20 provincial-level administrative regions across China (Beijing, Gansu, Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou, Hainan, Henan, Heilongjiang, Hubei, Jilin, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Shaanxi, Shanghai, Sichuan, Tianjin, Xinjiang, Yunnan, Zhejiang, and Chongqing). Eligibility criteria included being: (1) a registered nurse, (2) employed by one of the participating hospital and (3) engaged in clinical practice for at least 6 months. Nurses who were in internship or advanced training programs, or those were leave or otherwise unavailable during the data collection period, were excluded.

Measures

Demographic information

The demographic data collection form was developed based on a review of factors known to influence nursing competency in intravenous therapy [16, 17]. It covered four primary domains: (1) institutional characteristics: hospital level; (2) personal and professional attributes: age, education level (high school or technical secondary, bachelor, master or higher), professional title (nurse, senior nurse, supervisor nurse, associate chief nurse or chief nurse), working experience(<5 years, 6–10 years and >10 years); (3) specialist nurse in IV therapy (yes/no); (4) continuing professional development: participation in accredited IV-related training programs within the preceding 3 years (verified through institutional training records). To ensure content and face validity, the initial questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of 3 experts in IV therapy and nursing research. Furthermore, the questionnaire was pre-tested with a group of 5 clinical nurses to assess clarity, comprehensibility, and completion time. The pre-test confirmed that the items were unambiguous, and no modifications were required. Data from the pre-test was not included in the final analysis.

Intravenous therapy competency assessment instrument

The Core Competency in Intravenous Therapy Scale, originally developed and validated by Wu et al. [18], was used in this study. This instrument comprises 28 items across five theoretically grounded dimensions: Professional Attitude (4 items), Relevant Theoretical Knowledge (7 items), Practical IV Skills (6 items), Critical Thinking & Clinical Judgement (6 items), and Professional Development Capacity (5 items). In its development study, the scale demonstrated excellent psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s α = 0.953 for the full scale and established construct validity. In the present study, the instrument again showed excellent reliability. The Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.966, and the values for the five subscales were as follows: Professional Attitude (0.836), Relevant Theoretical Knowledge (0.908), Practical IV Skills (0.914), Critical Thinking & Clinical Judgement (0.937), and Professional Development Capacity (0.907). Responses were collected on a 5-point Likert scale (‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’), scores ranging from 28 to 140, with higher scores indicating superior competency in intravenous therapy.

Ethics considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (approval no. BE2020-045-01) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants accessed the survey only after providing electronic informed consent on the landing page that explained the study aims, estimated burden, and rights to withdraw. To ensure confidentiality, no personally identifiable information (e.g., name, employee ID, contact details) was collected. Data were collected and stored on a secure servers maintained by the Wenjuanxing platform, which complies with Chinese data security laws. Access to the dataset was restricted to the principal investigators via a password-protected account. The anonymized data was used exclusively for aggregate statistical analysis.

Data collection and quality control

This nationwide study was conducted under the academic supervision of the Gynecological Nursing Committee of the Chinese Nursing Association and the Obstetrical Nursing Committee of Guangdong Association of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, utilizing institutional partnerships with 46 member hospitals of the Guangdong Nurses Association. Data collection employed a secure web-based platform (Wenjuanxing, China’s leading online survey system) with SSL encryption and IP address validation. Hospital nursing departments approved study participation after institutional review; QR codes with unique identifiers were distributed through nursing directors to unit managers, ensuring organizational-level control; Embedded validation checks included: Minimum response time threshold (> 120s); Pattern detection algorithms for repetitive responses; Geolocation verification matching hospital IP ranges. From the initial 1,116 distributed questionnaires, 295 responses were excluded due to: Incomplete datasets (n = 127); Aberrant response patterns (n = 93); Geolocation inconsistencies (n = 75). The final analytical sample comprised 821 validated responses (73.57% validity rate), representing 20 provincial-level regions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL, USA) software. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages; normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and skewed data are presented as median (P25, P75). Chi-square tests and Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric tests were used to examine differences in general data of clinical nurses across categories, and unordered multinomial logistic regression analysis was used for multivariate analysis. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. A Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the univariate analyses (Chi-square tests). All variables in univariate analysis were included in the subsequent multivariate logistic regression model to avoid excluding potentially important confounding factors, regardless of their statistical significance after correction. LPA was preferred over factor analysis or k-means clustering because it accommodates continuous indicators while estimating categorical latent membership, allows class-specific residual variances, and provides posterior probabilities for subsequent regression modelling [19, 20]. Latent profile analysis was conducted in Mplus 8.3 to identify distinct profiles of nurses’ intravenous therapy core competence. Model selection was guided by established criteria outline by Tein et al. [21] and Sinha P et al. [22]. Primary criteria included the Akaike information criterion(AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and sample-sized adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), with lower values indicating better model fit and the Bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT), where a significant p-value (p < 0.05) indicates that a k-profile model significantly outperforms a (k-1) profile model. Secondary criteria comprised entropy, with values exceeding 0.80 suggesting clear profile separation and the Lo-Mendell-Rubin (LMR) test, interpreted cautiously due to its reduced sensitivity under conditions of moderate class separation. Tertiary considerations included class size and interpretability, with classes < 10% of the sample scrutinized for stability and clinical relevance.

Characteristics and naming of the latent classes of core intravenous therapy competencies

Based on Model 3, the distribution score of the three categories across the items of the core competence of intravenous therapy are shown in Fig. 1. According to the score of each item, the three categories were named as follows: Class 1 had the lowest scores on all items of the core competence of intravenous therapy, including Professional Attitude, Theoretical Knowledge, IV Therapy Skills, Critical Thinking, and Professional Development. Thus, it was named ‘Low-IV Core Competency’, accounting for 41.3% (339/821). Class 2 showed medium level of scores on all items. It was named the ‘Moderate-IV Core Competency’, representing 45.9% (377/821) of the sample. Class 3 presented the highest scores on all items. This category was named the ‘High-IV Core Competency’ and accounted for 12.8% (105/821) of the sample.

Univariate analysis of factors influencing core competency in intravenous therapy

The results of univariate analysis of influencing factors for the latent profiles of clinical nurses’ core intravenous therapy competencies are shown in Table 3. There were statistically significant differences in professional titles, work experience, specialist nurses in IV therapy, and completion of accredited IV-related training programs between the three categories of nurses (p < 0.05). Post-hoc tests with Bonferroni correction revealed these associations: the high-competence group was characterized by a significantly greater representation of Supervisor Nurses or above (23.8%) compared to the low-competence group (12.7%). In contrast, a significantly larger proportion of nurses with < 5 years of experience was found in the low-competence group (63.1%) relative to the moderate- and high-competence groups. Furthermore, both IV therapy specialist status (98.1%) and completion of IV training (30.5%) were predominant in the high-competence group compared to the other two groups. However, there were no statistically significant differences in hospital level, age, or educational level in the distribution of latent classes of clinical nurses’ core intravenous therapy competencies (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Dimension-specific findings of IV therapy competency

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore clusters of nurses’ core competency in IV therapy using LPA. The three latent profiles identified in this study align closely with Patricia Benner’s Novice-to-Expert model [23], providing empirical support for the staged development of nursing competency in IV therapy. Benner’s model describes the development process of clinical nurses’ professional knowledge and skills through five stages: novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, and expert, which is rooted in the philosophical thought that nursing practice is an experience learning process integrating knowledge, skills, and attitude [24]. In our study, the ‘Low IV Core Competency’ group likely represent the Novice or Advanced Beginner stages. Nurses in this group rely heavily on standardized protocols, lack the ability to adapt to unexpected clinical scenarios (e.g., managing IV drug extravasation or communicating with patients), and demonstrate weak theoretical knowledge and practical skills, which was consistent with Benner’s description of novices relying heavily on standardized rules and procedures due to limited experiential knowledge. Those in the ‘Moderate-IV Core Competency’ group may correspond to the Competent stage. These nurses have sufficient proficiency in technical dimensions (theoretical knowledge and practical skills) to independently complete routine IV tasks, such as peripheral venous catheter insertion and basic catheter maintenance, but struggle with complex cases (e.g., managing peripherally inserted central catheters for chemotherapy patients) and humanistic care. It is aligned with Benner’s characterization of competent nurses as “task-focused” rather than possessing the holistic perspective needed to address patient-centered needs. The ‘High-IV Core Competency’ group reflects the “Proficient” to “Expert” stages. Their high scores across all dimensions, especially in Professional Development Capacity (including mentoring colleagues and updating evidence-based practices) and Professional Attitude, align with Benner’s portrayal of experts as “contextually aware” and capable of integrating technical skills with patient-centered care. However, the small size of this group (only 12.8% of the sample) highlights the significant gap between current nursing practice and the expert standard in IV therapy, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to promote nurses IV core competency.

Factors associated with IV therapy competency profiles

Lack of IV therapy specialist certification and non-participation in IV training programs were strongly and significantly associated with lower competency levels (low or moderate vs. high). In contrast, demographic variables (age, education) and professional factors (work experience, hospital level) showed no significant associations with IV core competency. These findings are consistent with previous research emphasizing the role of specialized training in enhancing clinical skills, which found that targeted training programs improve proficiency in IV catheter insertion and complication management [25, 26]. Our results further reinforce that formal training programs beyond general professional experience are critical for developing high-level IV competency. Notably, the lack of association between work experience and competency contrasts with a few earlier studies [16, 27], which may reflect differences in how ‘experience’ was operationalized (e.g., years in practice vs. hours of IV-specific practice). The strong association between specialist certification/training and higher competency likely reflects the structured, skill-specific content of these programs, which focus on evidence-based practices, advanced techniques, and error prevention areas critical for IV therapy safety and effectiveness. The non-significant associations with age, education, or work experience suggest that competency in IV therapy is not merely a function of general professional maturity but requires targeted skill development. This may explain why even experienced providers without specialized training showed no advantage in competency levels [28]. Practically, these findings underscore the need for healthcare institutions to prioritize IV therapy training programs and incentivize specialist certification. Standardizing access to such training could help reduce variability in competency and improve patient outcomes, as low IV competency is linked to higher rates of infiltration, infection, and patient discomfort [29]. Theoretically, our results support the construct of ‘competency as skill-specific rather than a byproduct of general professional development, challenging assumptions that experience alone drives clinical proficiency.

Implications for nursing practice and policy

Based on the study’s findings regarding the distribution of IV therapy competency profiles and their key influencing factors, there are clear implications for nursing practice and policy aimed at improving IV therapy competency among clinical nurses and enhancing patient safety.

In clinical practice, a priority is to implement competency-stratified training programs. For the Low-IV Core competency group, a simulation-based learning approach is recommended, combining instruction in fundamental skills with scenario-based exercises to develop critical thinking and communication [13]. Additionally, new nurses in this group should complete a structured 3-month preceptorship under an IV therapy specialist to transfer learning to clinical practice. For the Moderate-IV Core competency group, an IV team mentorship model is advised, focusing on complex cases and humanistic care. Participation in multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where nurses present challenging cases and recieve specialists feedback, can further refine their technical skills [30]. Nurses in the High-IV Core competency group should be formally designated as “clinical mentors”, empowering them to lead unit-based quality improvement projects and train junior staff, thereby embedding a self-sustaining culture of peer-led learning and clinical excellence [31–33]. Furthermore, IV therapy competency assessments should systematically integrate the Professional Attitude dimension. Strength in Professional Development Capacity (reflecting clinical teaching, and research innovation abilities) may stem from the fact that IV therapy core competency represents a critical determinant of IV therapy quality and the specialized skills needed for effective intravenous treatments [34]. As IV technology advances, clinical nurses need not only practical skills but also stronger learning, teaching, and research innovation abilities for professional development. Modern nursing education frameworks increasingly prioritize lifelong learning, requiring nurses to engage in ongoing professional development to maintain licensure and adapt to evolving clinical guidelines [35]. Many healthcare institutions now mandate or incentivize participation in workshops, certifications, and teaching activities related to IV therapy (e.g., training new staff), which directly strengthens skills in clinical teaching and reflective practice. In contrast, the weakness in Professional Attitude (encompassing patient communication, empathy, or safety mindfulness) are concerning, as attitude directly impacts patient satisfaction and adherence to care protocols [9, 36]. Possible drivers include time constraints from high workloads, which prioritize technical efficiency over interpersonal care. This finding was consistent with studies noting that nurses frequently cite limited time amid heavy workloads as a key barrier to in-depth patient communication, often leading to rushed interactions [37]. Additionally, IV therapy curricula also focus overly on technical skill evaluations [38, 39]. Thus, we should incorporate patient-centered metrics, such as patient satisfaction with communication and direct observation of nurse-patient interactions [40].

At the policy level, IV specialist certification and standard training were required. Systematic reviews and multi-center studies consistently report that dedicated vascular access teams led by specialist nurses are associated with significant reductions in central line-associated bloodstream infections(CLABSI) and peripheral intravenous catheter failure rates [41, 42]. Therefore, hospitals should establish, and fund structured IV specialist programs. National nursing practice guidelines should require nurses in high-risk IV therapy settings (e.g. ICU) to obtain IV therapy specialist certification. On the other hand, investing in system-wide standardization is not only a clinical imperative but also an economic one, as it directly targets the reduction of complications like infiltration and bloodstream infections, which are linked to low competency and incur significant costs [43]. A concomitant national standardized curriculum for IV training, incorporating simulation for communication and humanistic care, should be established, an initiative supported by evidence demonstrating that standardized training elevates core competency [44]. Second, optimizing resource allocation to bridge equity gaps. A portion of nursing professional development funding should be directed to training for the low- and moderate-competence groups, prioritizing under-resourced regions and secondary hospitals. The creation of “Provincial IV Therapy Competency Centers” could mitigate disparities by offering centralized training, tele-mentoring from tertiary hospital experts, and monitoring regional outcome indicators. Third, linking competency to institutional accountability. IV therapy competency (e.g., specialist certification rates) was related to patient outcomes [45]. Thus, for Institutions with a high prevalence of low-competence nurses or suboptimal outcomes (e.g. high CLABSI) should implement improvement plans, with financial incentives, in order to promote investing in IV therapy quality and patient safety.

Strength and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the use of LPA allowed for the identification of naturally occurring competency subgroups, offering a more nuanced understanding than traditional aggregate scoring methods. Second, the large sample size (n = 821) enhances the generalizability of the latent profiles and regression findings. Third, the multivariate logistic regression adjusted for multiple potential confounders, strengthening causal inference regarding the role of training and certification. Meanwhile, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about causality; for example, while training participation correlates with higher competency, we cannot definitively conclude that training causes improved performance (reverse causality, e.g., more competent nurses may seek out training, cannot be ruled out). Second, competency was assessed via self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to response bias. Future studies could incorporate objective skill assessments to validate self-reported scores. Third, unmeasured variables such as institutional support for training, mentorship availability, or workload stress may also influence competency and were not included in the regression model. Finally, the geographic distribution of our sample was imbalanced, with 87.6% of participants from Southern China, overwhelmingly from Guangdong province, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other regions.

Conclusions

This study identified distinct competency profiles among nurses, revealing that high proficiency is not the norm. This finding necessitates targeted interventions to address prevalent competency gaps. The pivotal role of specialized certification and continuous training, as opposed to demographic factors, underscores a clear path forward. Healthcare institutions must prioritize the development of IV specialist nurses and implement mandatory, standardized training programs. Moving forward, competency assessments should shift focus from tenure to skill-based evaluations, ensuring that all nurses, regardless of setting or experience, can achieve the high competency essential for patient safety and therapy quality.
