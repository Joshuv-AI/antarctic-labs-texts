Cerebral vasospasm following clipping of unruptured middle cerebral artery aneurysms: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yusuke Otsu, Kohei Miyagi, Chihiro Takashima, Jin Kikuchi, Sosho Kajiwara, Tetsuya Negoto, Kimihiko Orito, Kiyohiko Sakata, Masaru Hirohata, Motohiro Morioka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We report a 64-year-old woman who developed symptomatic vasospasm on postoperative day 7 after clipping of an unruptured right middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysm. Imaging revealed right MCA vasospasm, which resolved with oral antiplatelets and intravenous vasodilators. She was discharged without neurological deficits. Although a few reports describe vasospasm after clipping unruptured aneurysms, this is the first to detail the clinical course, radiological findings, and treatment response. Potential risk factors include female sex, age 40–60, smoking, trigeminal neuralgia, and complex aneurysms. This case underscores the need to consider vasospasm even without subarachnoid hemorrhage for timely intervention.

Introduction

Cerebral vasospasm is a well-known complication of subarachnoid hemorrhage that significantly affects patient prognosis and has no established treatment. Although vasospasms rarely occur after surgery for unruptured intracranial aneurysms, they may be caused by mechanical injury to the vessel wall during surgery or intraoperative bleeding [2]. Herein, we present a case of symptomatic cerebral vasospasm following clipping surgery for an unruptured middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysm.

Case description

A 64-year-old woman with a history of trigeminal neuralgia was referred to our institution after an asymptomatic unruptured aneurysm of the right MCA was incidentally identified on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 1a). The patient was a smoker with no significant history of medication or allergies. Further evaluation using digital subtraction angiography (DSA) identified two aneurysms: a 4-mm wide-neck aneurysm at the M1-M2 bifurcation and a 3-mm wide-neck aneurysm at the M1-anterior temporal artery (ATA) bifurcation (Fig. 1b). Considering the risk of rupture, the patient underwent clipping surgery under general anesthesia. Briefly, a right frontotemporal craniotomy was performed with the patient in the supine position. Both aneurysms in the right MCA were visualized after careful dissection of the Sylvian fissures (Fig. 2a). A straight mini-clip was applied to the M1-ATA aneurysm (Fig. 2b). Additionally, a temporary clip was placed on the M1 segment, and reconstructive clipping of the M1-M2 bifurcation aneurysm was performed. Due to residual neck filling, the initial clip was removed and replaced with two additional weakly curved clips (Fig. 2c). Postoperative indocyanine green video angiography confirmed complete obliteration of both aneurysms (Fig. 2d). The aneurysms were wrapped using small cotton fibers and fibrin glue. Intraoperatively, a transient vasospasm was observed in the inferior M2 trunk, which resolved following topical application of papaverine hydrochloride (Fig. 2e). Postoperative computed tomography (CT) showed no bleeding or cerebral infarction, and the patient did not present with any new neurological deficits; however, a persistent postoperative headache was noted. On postoperative day 7, the patient developed transient left-sided facial paralysis and dysarthria. MRI revealed an acute cerebral infarction at the apex of the right temporal lobe (Fig. 3a), and magnetic resonance angiography showed poor visualization of the distal right MCA vessels. Emergency DSA confirmed vasospasm in the distal M2 segments, which had not been present preoperatively, and conservative treatment was initiated (Fig. 3b-c). Conservative therapy consisting of intravenous fasudil hydrochloride (60 mg/day) and oral aspirin (100 mg/day) was initiated. However, transient dysarthria recurred on postoperative day 9, prompting the addition of cilostazol (100 mg/day). The patient’s condition gradually improved, and fasudil hydrochloride was discontinued on postoperative day 17. CT angiography (CTA) on postoperative day 18 demonstrated marked resolution of the vasospasm (Fig. 3 d-f). The patient was discharged on postoperative day 21 with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0. Discussion

Cerebral vasospasm after clipping surgery for unruptured aneurysms is a rare but serious complication, with approximately half of the reported cases resulting in neurological deficits [6]. The causes of postoperative vasospasms can be broadly classified into direct and indirect factors. The direct factors include mechanical irritation of the vessel wall during surgery, brain compression, and intraoperative bleeding. Mechanical irritation may arise from endothelial dysfunction near an aneurysm, where reduced vasorelaxing lipoprotein production can trigger vasospasm [7, 10]. In this patient, complex surgery for two aneurysms required multiple clip placements, repositioning, temporary clipping, and wrapping, possibly causing significant mechanical stimulation of the vessel walls, as evidenced by intraoperative vasospasm in the M2 inferior trunk. The exact onset of cerebral vasospasm remains unclear since it was identified only 7 days postoperatively. Although the intraoperative spasm resolved with papaverine, postoperative recurrence cannot be ruled out. Moreover, no CTA was performed on postoperative day 1, limiting our ability to confirm or exclude early vasospasm. Fibrin glue may also have contributed, as reports suggest it can induce local inflammatory or vasospastic effects in adjacent vessels [5]. While no immediate intraoperative adverse effects were observed, this possibility warrants consideration. The patient’s indirect risk factors, including female sex, age 40–60 years, smoking, and trigeminal neuralgia, further suggest a multifactorial etiology [6]. Although several previous reports have described vasospasm following the clipping of unruptured aneurysms, to our knowledge, this is the first report to provide a detailed account of the clinical course, radiological findings, and therapeutic response. The present case offers valuable insights into the understanding and management of post-clipping vasospasm in the absence of subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Subsequently, we reviewed 10 cases of cerebral vasospasm following clipping surgery for unruptured MCA aneurysms, including the present case (Table 1) [1–3, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12]. Our analysis revealed that vasospasm occurred between postoperative days 2 and 29. Most patients were women aged 40–60 years without large aneurysms; only one male patient was an older adult aged ≥ 70 years. Intra-arterial vasodilators were administered in 8 of the 12 cases (66.7%), yet neurological deficits persisted in eight cases (66.7%). Notably, favorable outcomes were achieved with oral antiplatelet therapy and intravenous vasodilators in the present case. However, due to the limited number of cases, it was challenging to establish an optimal treatment strategy based on this review. Therefore, preventive strategies should focus on minimizing mechanical stimulation during surgery because procedures, such as aneurysm wrapping or repeated clip applications, could potentially contribute to spasms in some cases, although this possibility remains uncertain. Additionally, imaging modalities and detailed neurological assessments are necessary for early detection and intervention. This study is limited by its design as a single case report. The underlying mechanisms of vasospasm in the absence of hemorrhage remain speculative, and further accumulation of similar cases is necessary to validate the observations and therapeutic strategies described herein.

Conclusion

We present a case involving a middle-aged female smoker with a history of trigeminal neuralgia who developed delayed cerebral vasospasm following clipping of unruptured MCA aneurysms requiring multiple clips. To our knowledge, this is the first detailed case report describing the clinical course, radiological findings, and therapeutic response to cerebral vasospasm after clipping an unruptured aneurysm. Accompanied by a brief literature review, the present case may help clinicians in identifying potential risk factors and recognizing postoperative vasospasm as a rare but significant complication, even in unruptured cases. Although definitive conclusions cannot be drawn from a single case, careful postoperative monitoring using imaging and neurological evaluation may be beneficial, especially in patients with known direct or indirect risk factors.
