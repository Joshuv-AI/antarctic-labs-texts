One-stage exchange arthroplasty for periprosthetic joint infection following total knee arthroplasty with sinus tract: a mid-to short-term follow-up study
Authors: Yongjie Qiao, Qifeng Song, Xiaoyang Song, Peng Liu, Zhenhao Liu, Kai Zhang, Xuewen Kang, Shenghu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn increasing amount of clinical evidence indicates that one-stage exchange arthroplasty provides outcomes comparable to those of two-stage exchange arthroplasty in the management of chronic periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) following total knee arthroplasty (TKA). However, for cases complicated by a sinus tract, most specialists still advocate a two-stage exchange arthroplasty strategy. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of one-stage exchange arthroplasty in treating chronic PJI following TKA with sinus tract formation and to determine the feasibility of this approach in achieving infection control and restoring joint function.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 23 patients with PJI following TKA accompanied by a sinus tract, who were admitted between January 1, 2019 and December 31, 2023. All patients completed a minimum follow-up of 12 months. During this period, microbiological findings, individualized antimicrobial regimens, and incidences of infection recurrence were meticulously documented. To objectively evaluate infection control, serial measurements of serum inflammatory markers—including the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and procalcitonin (PCT)—were obtained preoperatively and on postoperative days 3, 7, 10, and 14, allowing for assessment of their dynamic changes. Furthermore, to assess functional recovery, the visual analog scale (VAS) score, knee range of motion (ROM), and Knee Society Score (KSS) were compared preoperatively and at postoperative day 14, 3 months, and the final follow-up.ResultsThe mean follow-up duration was 14.0 ± 0.9 months. At the final follow-up, the infection-free survival rate was 95.65% (22/23) as defined by the International Consensus Meeting (ICM) criteria, with only one case of recurrence at two months postoperatively. All evaluated clinical outcomes—including the VAS score, ROM, and KSS—demonstrated statistically significant improvements compared to preoperative baseline values (P < 0.001). By the final follow-up, patients had achieved a nearly pain-free state. The mean knee ROM significantly increased from 64.8° ± 5.0° preoperatively to 120.0° ± 4.5°, while the mean KSS improved from 33.1 ± 5.3 points to 87.7 ± 5.2 points.ConclusionFor chronic PJI following TKA with a sinus tract, one-stage exchange arthroplasty, when performed in strict adherence to a standardized protocol, demonstrates favorable potential in infection control and significant functional restoration, with satisfactory medium-term outcomes. Therefore, under strictly standardized surgical protocols, one-stage exchange arthroplasty may be considered a viable treatment option for carefully selected cases of periprosthetic joint infection complicated by a sinus tract following thorough and prudent evaluation.Keywords: One-stage exchange arthroplasty, Periprosthetic joint infection(PJI), Sinus tract, Total knee arthroplasty (TKA), Reimplantation

Introduction

With the increasing utilization of total joint arthroplasty, periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) has emerged as a formidable challenge in orthopedics. Studies have shown that the incidence of PJI ranges from 0.5% to 2.2% after primary TKA and increases to approximately 9% following revision TKA [1, 2]. Sinus tract formation is a typical clinical manifestation of chronic PJI, and its presence significantly increases the risk of treatment failure, with reported rates ranging from 15.2% to 28.7%. In addition, complications related to sinus tracts are associated with substantially increased healthcare costs, reaching up to 1.8 times those of conventional staged procedures [3, 4]. PJI accompanied by a sinus tract is therefore particularly challenging to manage in clinical practice, and existing treatment strategies still lack a unified and standardized approach to sinus tract management. This unmet clinical need constitutes an important rationale and clinical entry point for the present study.

The optimal management strategy for chronic PJI remains controversial. Particularly in cases complicated by a sinus tract, the condition has traditionally been considered an absolute contraindication for one-stage exchange arthroplasty. Current international consensus guidelines, such as the 2018 International Consensus Meeting (ICM) guidelines, still recommend two-stage exchange arthroplasty as the gold standard for these patients [5, 6]. Although the classic two-stage approach can effectively control infection using an antibiotic-loaded cement spacer during the interim period, the prolonged course of antibiotics and the months-long interval often lead to joint stiffness, muscle atrophy, and progressive bone loss in up to 30% of patients. Furthermore, the incidence of spacer-related complications (e.g., dislocation or fracture) is notably high, ranging from 18% to 25% [6, 7]. More critically, the multiple surgical procedures increase healthcare costs (approximately 40% higher than one-stage exchange arthroplasty), while the pre-existing soft tissue defect associated with the sinus tract significantly elevates the risk of failure during the second-stage reconstruction [2]. Given these limitations, the constraints of the conventional two-stage exchange arthroplasty in managing PJI with a sinus tract are becoming increasingly apparent. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop precise treatment strategies that integrate the modulation of the biofilm microenvironment with the restoration of mechanical stability for the treatment of chronic PJI.

In recent years, one-stage exchange arthroplasty—a procedure involving the removal of the infected prosthesis, thorough debridement, and implantation of a new prosthesis in a single operation—has garnered increasing attention due to its distinct advantages. Its core value lies in significantly shortening the treatment timeline, reducing overall healthcare costs, and maximizing the preservation of bone stock and soft tissues through a single surgical event. This approach lays a critical foundation for rapid postoperative functional recovery and substantially enhances the patient’s quality of life [8]. However, the successful implementation of this technique faces significant challenges. The primary difficulty lies in achieving precise preoperative and intraoperative diagnosis and pathogen identification. This accuracy is essential to ensure the completeness of debridement and to guide the selection of both sensitive antibiotic-loaded bone cement and appropriate systemic antimicrobial therapy [9]. Secondly, the complete eradication of biofilm and all colonizing microorganisms demands exceptionally high surgical expertise. Despite this, existing research has predominantly focused on the surgical techniques themselves, while a systematic description of the standardized protocol for managing the sinus tract and its correlation with clinical outcomes remains lacking.

This retrospective study aims to validate the feasibility of one-stage exchange arthroplasty for chronic PJI with a sinus tract, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the development of a precise treatment framework.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: 1. Patients were diagnosed with chronic PJI based on the 2018 International Consensus Meeting (ICM) criteria, with a confirmed sinus tract communicating with the joint prosthesis. 2. Patients were in satisfactory general condition and deemed eligible to undergo the major surgical procedure. 3. Patients were available for continuous follow-up. The enrollment cohort consisted of patients with chronic PJI (symptom duration > 4 weeks) who were admitted between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, and had a sinus tract persisting for ≥ 6 weeks.

Exclusion Criteria: 1. Failure to meet the 2018 International Consensus Meeting (ICM) diagnostic criteria for chronic periprosthetic joint infection (PJI). 2. Poor general medical condition rendering the patient a high-risk candidate for major surgery. 3. Presence of concurrent active infection at other sites or severe immunodeficiency. 4. Patients lost to follow-up.

Outcome measures and clinical definitions

In the present study, the infection control rate was defined as the primary endpoint, while functional outcome measures, including VAS, ROM, and KSS, were designated as secondary endpoints. Infection recurrence or treatment failure was strictly defined in accordance with the criteria of the International Consensus Meeting on Musculoskeletal Infection, integrating clinical manifestations, laboratory parameters, microbiological findings, and reoperation records to ensure standardized outcome assessment. Given the retrospective observational design of this study, no a priori sample size calculation or power analysis was performed, and this limitation has been explicitly acknowledged in the manuscript. All patients received the same implant model with an identical level of constraint, as varying degrees of bone defects and medial and lateral collateral ligament laxity were identified during revision surgery. Antibiotic regimens and treatment durations were individualized based on pathogen characteristics and antimicrobial susceptibility testing, thereby reflecting real-world clinical practice. In addition, metagenomic next-generation sequencing was incorporated as an adjunctive diagnostic tool to support clinical decision-making.

Case series

We retrospectively reviewed 23 cases of chronic PJI with a sinus tract (9 males, 14 females) treated at our institution from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023. The mean follow-up duration was 14.0 ± 0.9 months (range: 12–16). The patients had a mean age of 64.6 ± 6.7 years and a mean BMI of 24.4 ± 4.6 kg/m². The infection involved the left side in 13 patients and the right side in 10. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the 940th Hospital of the Joint Logistics Support Force of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army (Approval No. [2025KYLL209]). Surgical technique

Preoperative management

Preoperatively, a sinus tract with purulent discharge was visible on the knee surface in all patients (Fig. 1). Standard anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs were obtained for each patient (Fig. 2). All patients were required to discontinue systemic antibiotics for at least 2 weeks preoperatively. During this interval, joint aspiration and sinus tract secretion collection were performed for microbial culture. If initial cultures were negative, specimens were incubated for an extended period of 5–21 days to improve pathogen detection. All procedures were performed by the same senior orthopedic surgeon. The surgery was divided into three standardized phases: debridement, intraoperative interval, and reimplantation.

Debridement, irrigation, and disinfection

After general anesthesia and tourniquet application, the previous incision was used to en bloc resect the sinus tract and surrounding suspicious infected soft tissues. The specimen was submitted for histopathological examination. After joint capsulotomy, synovial fluid and three periprosthetic tissue samples were collected for culture, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and mNGS. Complete synovectomy and radical debridement were performed until clean bony surfaces were achieved. The joint was irrigated sequentially with povidone‑iodine, hydrogen peroxide, and normal saline. The prosthesis and all cement debris were removed, followed by thorough debridement of the medullary canals. High‑volume pulsed lavage (> 8000 mL) was performed using povidone‑iodine, hydrogen peroxide, and normal saline in sequence. The joint cavity was then soaked with povidone‑iodine for 15 min [10, 11].

Intraoperative interval

To avoid cross‑contamination, the surgical field was re‑draped, the surgical team re‑scrubbed and renewed gowns and gloves, and a new set of sterile instruments was used.

Prosthesis reimplantation

After additional high‑volume saline pulsed lavage, 0.5 g of vancomycin powder was placed into both the femoral and tibial medullary canals [10, 11]. Constrained femoral and tibial components (Zimmer Biomet, USA) were implanted. Metal augments were used for bone defect management. After final pulsed irrigation, a continuous irrigation and drainage system was placed. The joint capsule, subcutaneous tissue, and skin were closed in layers with the knee flexed at 60°.

Postoperative management

On postoperative day 3, prosthetic placement was evaluated via radiography (Fig. 2). A two-phase antibiotic regimen (in-hospital and post-discharge), guided by preoperative and intraoperative culture results, was implemented in Table 2. Intravenous antibiotics combined with intra-articular antibiotic administration are generally prescribed for 2–3 weeks, followed by a transition to oral antibiotic therapy for approximately 1 month based on clinical symptoms and infection-related inflammatory markers. Patients were closely monitored for infection markers and hepatic/renal function to assess therapeutic efficacy and safety.

Follow-up protocol

Follow-up assessments were performed by independent evaluators and none of the patients underwent reoperations unrelated to infection. All patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months through outpatient reviews and telephone interviews. Data collected included the identified pathogen, antibiotic regimen, and incidence of recurrence. To assess infection control and knee joint recovery, the following parameters were compared: serological infection markers (ESR, CRP, IL-6, PCT) preoperatively and on postoperative days 3, 7, 10, and 14; and the VAS score, ROM, and KSS preoperatively, at 14 days postoperatively, at 3 months postoperatively, and at the final follow-up.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous data conforming to a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared within groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed continuous data are expressed as the median with interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using non-parametric tests. Categorical data are summarized as counts and/or percentages. All tests were two-sided, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We have clarified that repeated-measures analyses appropriately accounted for within-subject correlations. Given the small cohort size, we have acknowledged the risk of type II error and potential overinterpretation of statistical significance in the revised manuscript.

Results

Reinfection

A high rate of infection control was achieved in this cohort. All 23 patients with chronic PJI and sinus tracts treated with one-stage exchange arthroplasty were followed for a mean of 14.0 ± 0.9 months. One patient (4.35%) experienced recurrence(fungal infection), while the remaining 22 (95.65%, 95% CI: 80.2%–100.0%) showed no evidence of infection at final follow-up. All patients received targeted antibiotic therapy during hospitalization (Table 2 for microbiological findings and the antibiotic regimen). Postoperative infection markers demonstrated significant improvement and normalized in most cases (Fig. 3).

Functional outcomes

Significant improvements in joint function were observed. Regarding pain relief, the VAS score markedly decreased from a preoperative value of 8.0 (7.0, 8.0) to 3.0 (2.0, 3.0) at 14 days postoperatively, and further to 2.0 (1.0, 2.0) at 3 months postoperatively, essentially reaching a pain-free state by the final follow-up (P<0.001). The ROM also showed significant improvement, increasing from (64.8 ± 5.0)° preoperatively to (84.8 ± 5.0)° at 14 days postoperatively, and reaching (104.7 ± 4.9)° and (120.0 ± 4.5)° at 3 months postoperatively and the final follow-up, respectively (P < 0.001). The KSS improved from (33.1 ± 5.3) points preoperatively to (65.4 ± 4.6) points at 14 days, (78.0 ± 6.5) points at 3 months, and (87.7 ± 5.2) points at the final follow-up (P<0.001), indicating that knee function was largely sufficient for daily activities (Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, 23 patients with chronic PJI Following TKA complicated with sinus tract formation underwent one-stage exchange arthroplasty. Of the 23 patients, 22 showed no reinfection at the final follow-up, while 1 patient developed recurrent infection two months after surgery and subsequently underwent debridement followed by a two-stage exchange arthroplasty. The infection-free survival rate after one-stage exchange arthroplasty was 95.65%. Additionally, 82.6% of patients achieved good knee functional outcomes.

In the management of chronic PJI Following TKA complicated with sinus tract, international guidelines—such as those from the AAOS and the International Consensus Meeting (ICM)—recommend two-stage exchange arthroplasty as the standard treatment strategy [12–14]. This recommendation is primarily based on evidence showing that, compared with chronic PJI without a sinus tract, cases with sinus tract formation are associated with more mature biofilms and compromised local soft-tissue conditions, both of which significantly increase the risk of reinfection following one-stage exchange arthroplasty [15, 16]. However, the present study demonstrates the potential applicability of one-stage exchange arthroplasty in these complex cases through the optimization of surgical debridement techniques. Achieving success with a one-stage procedure relies critically on meticulous surgical debridement. Our strategy consisted of the following components: (1) Thorough mechanical debridement: Extensive removal of infected, inflamed, and necrotic tissue around the infection site and the sinus tract. The completeness of this step depends heavily on the surgeon’s experience and intraoperative judgment, and is the fundamental prerequisite for surgical success. Inadequate debridement markedly increases the risk of failure after one-stage exchange arthroplasty [17, 18]. (2) Sequential chemical debridement: Large-volume irrigation with normal saline followed by hydrogen peroxide and diluted povidone–iodine solution was used intraoperatively to further eliminate residual microorganisms [19, 20]. (3) Targeted management of biofilms: Biofilm formation is a major barrier leading to antibiotic treatment failure and infection recurrence [21]. To effectively remove biofilms adherent to prosthetic components, bone surfaces, and surrounding soft tissues, Wu et al. [22] reported the use of high-volume pulsatile lavage to assist debridement, reducing local bacterial burden. Nevertheless, other studies have shown that pulsatile lavage alone has limited ability to eradicate mature biofilms, necessitating the adjunctive use of high-concentration local antibiotics to compensate for its shortcomings [23–25]. Although one-stage exchange arthroplasty for chronic PJI with a sinus tract faces significant challenges—including difficulty in biofilm eradication and compromised soft tissue—the combination of surgeon experience–driven thorough mechanical debridement, sequential chemical irrigation, and effective biofilm-targeted strategies (such as pulsatile lavage combined with high-dose local antibiotics) is essential for ensuring procedural success. Among these, complete biofilm removal remains a critical determinant of infection control. Collectively, these measures may substantially reduce the risk of reinfection and provide practical support for the use of one-stage exchange arthroplasty in this high-risk patient population.

Chronic PJI Following TKA complicated with sinus tract presents substantially increased therapeutic challenges. The presence of a sinus tract indicates that the infection has penetrated deep tissues and communicates with the external environment, which not only elevates the risk of polymicrobial infection but also reflects the establishment of a mature biofilm and impairment of the local immune microenvironment [26, 27]. Successful management of such infections requires comprehensive surgical intervention—including debridement and prosthesis management (i.e., one- or two-stage exchange arthroplasty)—combined with standardized and individualized antimicrobial therapy [28].

Antibiotic selection must be strictly guided by microbiological evidence. Although cultures obtained from sinus tract discharge have certain diagnostic value—with reported concordance rates of approximately 70–85% compared with intraoperative tissue cultures—they remain susceptible to contamination or false-negative results [29]. Therefore, standardized microbiological sampling is essential. In this study, patients were required to discontinue antibiotics for two weeks prior to admission. Upon admission, joint aspiration was actively pursued; when aspiration was difficult, sterile saline lavage was performed before sample collection, and specimens were inoculated directly into blood culture bottles to improve pathogen detection.

During surgery, multiple samples of synovial fluid and periprosthetic tissue were collected from infected sites for pathological examination, smear microscopy, conventional cultures, blood culture, and next-generation sequencing (NGS). Through these optimized sampling and diagnostic techniques, our center achieved a culture positivity rate exceeding 80%. As reported by Cao et al. [30], sonication of removed implants and periprosthetic tissues further increased culture positivity from 50–70% to approximately 90%. Systemic and local antimicrobial regimens were selected based on antibiotic susceptibility results. When cultures were negative, empirical therapy covering common Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms was administered [31]. In our protocol, patients received intravenous sensitive or broad-spectrum antibiotics for one week, combined with intra-articular injection of sensitive antibiotics for 14 days. Liver and renal function, electrolytes, inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, IL-6, PCT), and serum drug concentrations were monitored every three days, and antimicrobial therapy was adjusted accordingly. After discharge, patients continued oral empirical antibiotics for one month. Discontinuation of antibiotics required normalization of clinical symptoms and serum inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, IL-6, PCT), followed by a minimum of one year of close follow-up. In addition, Yücel et al. [32] through a comparative analysis of 49 cases of revision hip arthroplasty, systematically elucidated the expression patterns and mechanistic roles of inflammatory biomarkers—such as ESR and CRP—as well as the key comorbidity of diabetes mellitus (DM) in distinguishing infectious from aseptic prosthetic loosening. Their findings confirmed that inflammatory markers and diabetes are critical indicators for differentiating revision arthroplasty types and assessing infection risk. These conclusions also provide important clinical insights for the diagnosis and perioperative management of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) following total knee arthroplasty.

This study was a retrospective, single-center, non-comparative investigation, and therefore lacks the methodological rigor of a prospective design as well as validation from multicenter data, which inevitably introduces certain limitations. In comparison with previous reports, the infection control rate observed in the present study was slightly higher than that reported in some comparable studies [33], while being similar to the outcomes reported in others [34]. The discrepancies among these findings may be primarily attributable to differences in patient inclusion criteria, surgical techniques, postoperative antimicrobial management strategies, and follow-up durations across studies. It should be emphasized that the relatively high infection control rate achieved in this study may be closely related to two key factors. First, all procedures were performed by a single orthopedic surgeon with extensive experience in revision surgery for periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), ensuring a high level of procedural standardization and consistency. Second, strict patient selection criteria were applied, with exclusion of high-risk cases, including those with severe immunocompromise, infections caused by multidrug-resistant organisms, and extensive bone defects unsuitable for one-stage reconstruction. While these measures may have contributed to an improved treatment success rate, they also limit the external validity of the findings. Consequently, the conclusions of this study may be applicable only to patients with similar baseline characteristics and should not be directly extrapolated to all PJI cases complicated by a sinus tract. Two-stage arthroplasty remains the classical and well-established surgical strategy for the management of PJI with a sinus tract, with its stability in infection control supported by multiple studies. However, the present study focused exclusively on the clinical outcomes of one-stage exchange arthroplasty, and the comparative effectiveness between one-stage and two-stage arthroplasty procedures remains to be clarified. Future research should include multicenter, prospective, controlled trials with larger sample sizes and more heterogeneous patient populations to further validate the efficacy and safety of one-stage exchange arthroplasty for PJI with a sinus tract, as well as to delineate its differences in outcomes compared with two-stage exchange arthroplasty. Such efforts would provide more robust evidence to guide clinical decision-making.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 23), which may have reduced the statistical power and limited the ability to detect rare complications or differences among subgroups. Second, the follow-up period was predominantly short to mid-term, precluding a robust assessment of long-term infection control durability and prosthesis survival. Third, the absence of a control group (such as patients undergoing two-stage exchange arthroplasty or debridement alone) restricts the conclusions to preliminary and exploratory evidence. Fourth, as a single-center retrospective study, it is inherently subject to potential selection bias and surgeon-related procedural bias. Finally, because all cases were derived from a single institution and surgeries were performed by an experienced arthroplasty team, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to broader clinical settings or to surgeons with varying levels of expertise.

In summary, the management of chronic PJI with sinus tract formation is a highly complex, multidisciplinary process. Successful antibiotic therapy depends on close integration with surgical techniques, susceptibility-guided individualized regimens, and long-term clinical monitoring. Therefore, under strictly standardized surgical protocols, one-stage exchange arthroplasty may be considered a viable treatment option for carefully selected cases of periprosthetic joint infection complicated by a sinus tract following thorough and prudent evaluation.
