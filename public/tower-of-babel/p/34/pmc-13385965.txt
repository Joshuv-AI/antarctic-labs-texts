Unveiling the sensing potential of innate lymphoid cells
Authors: Raquel Castillo-González, Lucía Sancho-Temiño, Alba Seguí-Pérez, Giuseppe Sciumè, Ivana Stojanović, Aránzazu Cruz-Adalia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInnate lymphoid cells (ILCs) are key regulators of early immune responses and play a central role in mucosal immunity, where they contribute to host defense and tissue homoeostasis. This review synthesizes evidence that ILCs, including natural killer cells (NKs), ILC1s, ILC2s, ILC3s, and lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells, directly sense pathogens via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). Beyond their established role as cytokine responders, emerging data reveal that ILCs engage PRRs to initiate complementary, context-dependent signaling pathways. This direct recognition mechanism redefines the functional landscape of ILCs in early immune surveillance, moving beyond reliance on indirect stromal signals. Collectively, these insights reposition ILCs as active sentinels in host defense and highlight the ILC-PRR axis as a novel therapeutic avenue for modulating immune responses in infectious, inflammatory, and cancer-related diseases.Subject terms: Innate lymphoid cells, Pattern recognition receptors

Introduction

The immune system is composed of a complex network of cells and proteins that protect the host from invading pathogens and maintain tissue homoeostasis. Innate and adaptive immune cells, which have specialised receptors that activate signalling pathways to perform effector functions such as cytokine and chemokine secretion to fight specific threats, are central to these defence mechanisms and participate in the induction of protective immunity1,2. Innate immunity represents the first barrier, providing prompt defence against pathogens, while the adaptive immune system promotes specific and long-lasting responses to detected antigens1,2.

Innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) represent a subset of lymphocytes mainly found in mucosal tissues, where they contribute significantly to immune surveillance and are involved in the regulation of adaptive immunity. However, despite being lymphoid cells that arise from common lymphoid progenitors, ILCs lack antigen-specific receptors. Importantly, they induce early pathogen elimination and contribute to tissue healing and remodelling3–5. Their presence at mucosal barriers is crucial for maintaining a balanced immune response to the microbiome under homoeostatic conditions. During infections, ILCs are key mediators of the early immune response to various pathogens at mucosal barriers. ILC activation promotes inflammatory programs, production of antimicrobial substances, and tissue repair mechanisms, and is essential to effectively combating microbial threats at the mucosal surfaces6,7.

Based on the transcription factors that define their lineage and the cytokine secretion profiles, ILCs are traditionally divided into three subsets: i) group 1 innate lymphoid cells (ILC1s) including natural killer cells (NKs) and interferon (IFN)-γ secreting ILC1s; ii) group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), and iii) group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s)8–10. More recently, a revised nomenclature was approved by the International Union of Immunological Societies, in which ILCs can be classified into five subgroups according to their development and function: NKs, ILC1s, ILC2s, ILC3s, and Lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells5,11. Furthermore, it is well described that the type of pathogen determines the ILC response during infections. Thus, intracellular bacteria or viruses primarily promote ILC1, NK and some ILC3 responses, while extracellular bacterial and fungal infections induce ILC3 and LTi subsets7. NK cells are specialised in releasing lytic lysosomes containing perforin and granzymes, essential for defending against intracellular pathogens, but unlike other ILCs, they are more abundant in the bloodstream4. Notably, ILC1s comprise heterogeneous populations, including both cytotoxic and non-cytotoxic subsets, which contribute to antibacterial immunity through complementary effector functions12,13. On the other hand, ILC2s participate in the immune response against parasitic infections and contribute to tissue repair following tissue damage caused by viral infections9.

How do ILCs respond to external stimuli and pathogens? ILCs are traditionally recognised for their rapid response to cytokine signals derived from other immune or stromal cells in the tissue microenvironment. Indeed, several families of cytokine receptors are expressed on ILCs, allowing them to sense and integrate signals such as IL-12, IL-18, IL-33, IL-23, and TSLP, which drive their activation and effector functions. However, accumulating evidence suggests that ILCs may also directly sense microbial signals through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), much like myeloid cells. Though this remains a relatively underexplored area, certain subsets of ILCs have been shown to express functional PRRs, including toll-like receptors (TLRs) and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors (NLRs). This points to a potential capacity for direct microbial recognition, independent of intermediary cytokine signalling. PRRs are germline-encoded receptors that detect conserved molecular patterns associated with pathogens (PAMPs) or endogenous danger signals (DAMPs)14,15. Upon ligand binding, these receptors trigger downstream signalling pathways that result in the production of cytokines, chemokines, and other immune mediators. The ability of ILCs to engage PRR pathways could represent an additional layer of immune surveillance, complementing their cytokine-driven responses, although more studies are required to fully understand the scope and consequences of this mechanism in different tissues and pathological contexts.

TLRs are the most well-defined PRRs and the first PRRs to be recognised, marking a significant landmark in the immunology field. Their identification served as a crucial link between the immune system’s initial detection of pathogens and the subsequent activation of the necessary immune response to eliminate the infection. For many years, TLRs have been associated with innate immune cells such as macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and mast cells, due to their role in the immediate recognition of pathogens14. However, more recent evidence suggests that TLRs are also expressed by adaptive immune cells and play relevant roles in the establishment of adaptive immune responses2,16. Together, these two branches of the immune system work synergistically to enable the host to initiate and sustain an efficient defence against invading pathogens2. To date, 10 TLRs (TLR1-TLR10) have been identified in humans and 12 TLRs (TLR1-TLR13) in mice, as TLR10 in mice is only present as a defective pseudogene14,17–19. TLRs are type I transmembrane proteins characterised by their structure, consisting of ectodomains with leucine-rich repeats responsible for recognising PAMPs, transmembrane domains, and conserved intracellular Toll-interleukin 1 (IL-1) receptor (TIR) domains essential for downstream signal transduction. Apart from TLR3, all TLRs activate pathways dependent on the myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88). The MyD88-dependent pathway is essential to produce several proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α. In contrast, MyD88 signalling is not required for the upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules such as CD40, CD80, and CD86, leading to the discovery of a second MyD88-independent pathway, which depends on the TIR domain-containing adaptor inducing interferon beta (TRIF) signal adaptor protein. MyD88-independent pathway, which is unique to TLR3 and TLR4 signalling pathways, leads to IFN-β production. All these TLR receptors are expressed differentially across immune cells and exhibit different functions regarding PAMP recognition and immune responses17,20–23. Furthermore, TLRs can be classified into two categories based on their subcellular location: those found on the cell surface, such as TLR1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 10, and 11 and those located within endosomes, including TLR3, 7, 8, 9, 12, and 1320,21 (Fig. 1).

In addition to TLRs, representative innate immune receptors include NLRs, retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG I)-like receptors (RLRs), absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2)-like receptors, cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)/stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and C-type lectin receptors (CLRs)18,24. In this review, we aim to provide an overview of PRR signalling in regulating the function of ILCs. Moreover, we discuss recent insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying TLR-mediated responses in different subsets, highlight relevant findings from in vivo and in vitro experiments, and discuss potential therapeutic strategies targeting TLR pathways in the context of ILC-mediated immune responses.

In summary, a detailed understanding of the interaction between PRRs and innate cells is essential for the development of new immunomodulatory therapies for infectious and inflammatory diseases and the development of new vaccines, opening new directions in the field of PRR and ILC research.

Main text

PRRs in NK cells

NK cells represent 5–20% of circulating lymphocytes in humans and constitute the subset of ILCs with inherent capabilities to detect and lyse both virus-infected cells and tumour cells by producing cytokines, chemokines, and effector molecules such as granzyme and perforin26,27.

Because of their ability to produce IFN-γ and TNF-α when stimulated, NKs share similarities with ILC1s. However, unlike ILC1s, NKs possess cytolytic functions similar to those of CD8+ T lymphocytes27,28. Regarding differential expression of transcription factors, NK cells express EOMES as a key regulator, whereas ILC1s do not. However, some markers can vary depending on the tissue29.

In humans, these cells are characterised by the presence of the Fc receptor CD16, and the majority express CD5627. In particular, the CD56bright NK subset is poorly represented in the peripheral blood while it constitutes the majority of NK cells in secondary lymphoid tissues. This subset has low cytolytic activity but is characterised by high cytokine production. Conversely, the CD56dim subset not only exerts potent cytotoxic activity but also secretes high levels of cytokines in response to antigens30–32. Additionally, CD56dim CD16bright NK cells represent at least 90% of all peripheral blood NK cells, being the major circulating subset33,34.

On the other hand, murine NK cells, specifically from the C57BL/6 strain, express the surface markers NK1.1, NKp46, and CD49b, but not CD3, which is a T cell marker. CD3 is used to exclude contaminating T cell subsets, such as natural killer T cells and NK-like T cells. In other mouse strains, such as BALB/c, NK cells are identified using only CD49b and NKp46 because these strains have allelic variants of NK1.1 that cannot be detected with the widely used PK136 antibody27.

NK cells play a key role in the host response against several pathogens and express TLRs that can modulate NK activation. While the expression and cellular functions of TLRs in macrophages have been widely examined, more recent studies have also demonstrated that TLRs enhance NK cytotoxicity and cytokine production, providing the primary defence mechanism against bacterial, fungal, and viral infections20.

Concerning human NK cells, it has been described that purified cells express transcriptional levels of TLR1-TLR10 4,35. Tlr1 mRNA is expressed with the highest levels, followed by tlr2, tlr3, tlr5 and tlr6 mRNA at moderate levels. However, tlr9 and tlr10 mRNA expression levels were low or undetectable20,35–38. It was also described that TLR ligands can directly stimulate NK cells to secrete cytokines, such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, which play a crucial role in innate immune defence4,35 (Figs. 1 and 3; Table 1).

Regarding murine NK cells, variations in mRNA expression have also been described. One study indicated the expression of tlr1, tlr2, tlr3, tlr4, tlr6, tlr7, tlr8 and tlr9 in hepatic and splenic murine NK cells39 (Fig. 3 and Table 1).

PAMP-triggered TLR activation promotes the binding of adaptor proteins, leading to the activation of downstream molecules that induce cytokine production and enhance NK cytotoxicity. Upon binding of ligands or TLR agonists, these receptors dimerise and undergo conformational changes, triggering the recruitment of downstream adaptor proteins. These adaptors include MyD88, TIR domain-containing adaptor protein (TIRAP)/MyD88-adaptor-like, TIR domain-containing adaptor inducing IFN-β (TRIF)/TIR domain-containing adaptor molecule-1, and TRIF-related adaptor molecule. MyD88 is responsible for intracellular signalling downstream of all TLRs, but there is a MyD88-independent pathway that, following TLR3 or TLR4 signalling, triggers type I interferon production20,22,40. TRIF is important for this MyD88-independent pathway, as well as for the TLR4-mediated production of inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, TRIF-related adaptor molecule (TRAM) participates specifically in the TLR4-mediated, MyD88-independent pathway. However, TIRAP is involved in TLR2- and TLR4-mediated, MyD88-dependent pathways22.

TLRs activate both nuclear factor κB (NF-κB)-dependent and NF-κB-independent pathways, leading to the generation of cytokines and chemokines20,40 (Fig. 1). The interaction between MyD88 and IL-1R-associated kinases (IRAKs) promotes the activation of a complex involving TNF receptor-associated factor 6 and TAB2, subsequently activating TGFβ-activated kinase 1. It plays a crucial role in establishing the different pathways that activate both the NF-κB signalling pathway and mitogen-activated protein kinase pathways20,35,41,42.

PRR signalling outcomes in human NK cell responses

Several studies have shown that stimulation of TLRs by their ligands or agonists can promote differential NK activation20,43. A study showed that TLR1/2 agonist SMU-Z1 enhances NK cell activity against HIV-1 by reactivating latent virus and boosting NK cell cytotoxicity, including increased degranulation and IFN-γ production to target human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-infected cells44. Notably, this effect of TLR2 stimulation on NK cells could be both direct and indirect. Human NK cells can express TLR2, and its expression is upregulated upon treatment with SMU-Z1. However, monocytes express TLR2 at much higher levels, and because these experiments were performed in vitro using PBMCs without NK cell isolation, part of the observed NK activation may be mediated indirectly through monocyte-derived cytokines44. Furthermore, to study the TLR3 signalling pathway, several groups employ a synthetic copolymer polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (poly I:C), which mimics double-stranded (ds) RNA viral products. TLR3 is the specific receptor for dsRNA, a typical intermediate in the reproductive cycle of many viruses45. It has been described that poly (I:C) stimulates TLR3 by promoting p38 MAPK activation, which is important for NK cell functionality. The stimulation with poly (I:C) induces cytotoxic activity, as well as the production of CXCL10 and IFN-γ in human NK cells35,46,47. Moreover, TLR3 or TLR7 agonists, when combined with IL-12 and IFN-α, enhance IFN-γ secretion by human NK cells48, increase the expression of activation markers such as CD69 and CD25, and upregulate cytotoxicity47. In addition, ligands for TLR2, 4, and 5 induce IFN-γ production by purified human NK cells and importantly enhance its cytotoxicity35. Several studies have demonstrated that NK cells are activated by TLR9 agonists (CpG), inducing CD69 expression, followed by the production of IFN-γ and TNF and enhanced cytolytic activity49–51. However, there was controversy over whether this activation is direct or indirect. Thus, another study indicated that purified NK cells do not respond directly to the synthetic oligodeoxynucleotide CpG ODN but are activated by cytokines derived from CpG ODN-stimulated plasmacytoid dendritic cells38. Importantly, it was later demonstrated that NK cells could be triggered by poly (I:C) and ODN AB, but only in the simultaneous presence of proinflammatory cytokines IL-12 or IL-847 (Fig. 3).

Multiple reports observed that different bacterial PAMPs stimulate NKs through different TLRs. The outer membrane protein A from Klebsiella pneumoniae (KpOmpA) and Escherichia coli flagellin, which interact with TLR2 and TLR5, respectively, activated human NKs and induced IFN-γ and α-defensin release. In this line, NK cells from TLR2 knockout mice do not respond to KpOmpA, demonstrating the key role of TLR in these functions37. TLR2 can also recognise Helicobacter pylori-specific membrane-bound lipoprotein HpaA and induces IFN-γ secretion by human NKs52. In vitro stimulation of NKs with Mycobacterium bovis bacille Calmette-Guérin promotes not only the expression of different activation markers such as CD69 and CD25, but also the secretion of IFN-γ and TNF53. Upon Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, TLR2 and TLR4 activate human NK cells but do not induce IFN-γ. Although IL-12 is required, this study reveals that NK cell-APC contact is mandatory to induce this cytokine secretion54. In vitro experiments reveal that Escherichia coli Fimbriae FimH activates both murine and human NK cells, leading to the secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α as well as enhanced cytotoxicity. Further experiments conducted with TLR4 knockout and MyD88 knockout mice and human NK-92 cells, which lack TLR4, demonstrated that activation of NK cells by FimH relies on signalling through TLR4 and MyD88. These findings suggest that NK cells directly detect and respond to FimH through the TLR4-MyD88 signalling axis, contributing to innate protection against cancer or microbial infections55 (Fig. 3).

Human NK cells not only express TLRs, but also NOD1, NOD2 and NLRP3. Both NLR and TLR agonists upregulated the cytotoxic function of NK cells. Poly (I:C) (TLR agonist) and Escherichia coli RNA (NLR agonist) significantly increased TNF-α and IFN-γ expression in human CD3-CD56+ NK cells. This study demonstrates that TLRs and NLRs play a role in enhancing the antitumor cytotoxicity and cytokine-producing activities of human NK cells56.

Functional consequences of PRR activation in murine NK cells

On the other hand, in vivo murine studies have demonstrated that wild-type mice that were treated with CpG and a lethal dose of Francisella tularensis Live Vaccine Strain survived, in contrast to mice lacking TLR9. Although CpG stimulation induces NK cell activation, the experimental design does not allow discrimination between direct and indirect effects, and the observed response may be mediated, at least in part, by accessory cell-derived cytokines57. In addition, IFN-γ production and cytotoxicity in mouse NK cells were also induced via TLR2 stimulation58. Other authors observed that when NK cells are activated via their TLRs, they produce IFN-γ, CCL3, CCL4 and CCL5, which play a crucial role in clearing microbial infections. These responses depended on the specific TLRs and the presence of MyD8839 (Fig. 3).

Although NKs can detect bacterial patterns and be directly activated by TLRs, it is widely recognised that accessory cells play a crucial role in cellular response to microbial pathogens, both indirectly through soluble factors and directly thanks to cell-to-cell contact4,43,59. Several studies have shown that NK activation during bacterial infections frequently involves a cellular interaction with accessory cells59–62. Other findings have shown that NK cell activation by TLR agonists can occur only in the presence of soluble cytokines secreted by accessory cells. Key cytokines such as IL-2, IFN-α/β, IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, and IL-21 have been reported as main triggers for NK cell activation54,63,64. Additionally, other cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-4, IL-1β, IL-7, IL-23, and IL-33, have been identified to increase the survival, proliferation, IFN-γ production, or cytotoxicity of NK cells43.

On the other hand, although NK cells express several CLRs, such as members of the CD94/NKG2 and NKRP1 families, these receptors are structurally related to, but distinct from, classical CLRs. To date, direct sensing of PAMP via classical CLRs has not been clearly demonstrated in NK cells. Instead, these cells may respond indirectly to CLR-mediated signals through interactions with myeloid cells or via endogenous ligands.

In addition to TLRs and CLRs, RLRs such as RIG-I and melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5, cytosolic sensors that trigger the production of interferons, play important roles in regulating both human and murine NK cell functions. Specifically, they contribute to the modulation of antiviral gene expression and IFN-γ production and enhance TRAIL expression as well as cytotoxic activity65–69 (Figs. 2 and 3).

DNA-sensing: cGAS–STING pathway in NK cells

Beyond TLRs and NLRs, NK cells can sense cytosolic DNA through the cGAS–STING pathway (Fig. 2). Cytoplasmic double-stranded DNA originating from viral infections, tumour cells, irradiation, or endogenous sources is detected by cGAS, which catalyses the production of the second messenger 2′3′-cGAMP. cGAMP binds to and activates STING, triggering downstream TBK1-IRF3 signalling and type I IFN production. While this DNA-sensing pathway can initiate antiviral responses, prolonged activation may paradoxically suppress NK cell cytotoxicity, induce apoptosis, and reduce persistence. Consistently, studies in human NK-92 and primary NK cells demonstrated that expression of the viral nuclease Poxin can degrade cGAMP, thereby inhibiting cGAS–STING signalling. Poxin-transgenic NK cells (Pox-NK-92) exhibited reduced type I IFN signalling, decreased apoptosis, enhanced survival under irradiation, and increased cytotoxicity against tumour targets. Notably, Poxin also improved the function of CAR-NK cells in vitro and in xenograft mouse models, enhancing NK cell tumour-killing efficacy70.

Interestingly, in murine tumour models, DNA damage in lymphoma cells triggers the accumulation of cytosolic DNA, which activates the STING-TBK1-IRF3 axis leading to upregulation of RAE1, an activating ligand for the NK receptor NKG2D. This DDR-dependent STING activation enhances NK cell recognition and cytotoxicity, promoting tumour immunosurveillance71. Furthermore, Epimedium extract has been shown to directly activate the NK-intrinsic cGAS–STING-IRF3 axis, upregulating NKG2D and effector molecules to suppress hepatocellular carcinoma growth in vivo72.

At the microenvironmental level, the STING pathway also plays a critical role in overcoming spatial barriers in ‘cold’ tumours. In small cell lung cancer, where NK cells are physically excluded, the activation of vascular STING signalling drives an indirect anti-tumour effect by restoring NK cell extravasation and recruitment. This vascular-mediated infiltration allows NK cells to reach and efficiently destroy MHC-I-repressed tumour cells73.

Thus, the functional outcome of cGAS–STING activation in NK cells is context-dependent: while chronic or excessive DNA sensing within NK cells may impair their function, STING activation in target tumour cells can enhance NK-mediated anti-tumour immunity. These insights underscore the dual role of cGAS–STING in shaping NK cell responses. Collectively, the available data suggest that controlled modulation of DNA-sensing pathways, either by suppressing NK-intrinsic signalling or by exploiting tumour-intrinsic activation, may represent a promising strategy to optimise NK cell–based immunotherapies.

Helper-like ILCs

As stated above, the family of ILCs not only plays a relevant role in defence because they react rapidly during infections or inflammatory responses but also exerts a relevant role in tissue homoeostasis74. Currently, there is limited to no evidence supporting the expression or functional role of NODs, RLRs, and cGAS/STING pathways in helper-like ILC subsets (ILC1, ILC2, ILC3, and LTi cells). The expression of classical CLRs in these ILC subsets is poorly characterised. While some recent studies suggest that certain lectin-like molecules may be expressed by ILC3s, robust evidence for functional classical CLR-mediated pathogen recognition in ILCs is lacking. Therefore, in this section focused on non-cytotoxic ILCs, we will primarily discuss TLRs, which have a better-established role in their biology.

ILC1s

ILC1s are a subset of innate immune cells that play a critical role in the regulation of inflammation and maintenance of tissue homoeostasis. These cells are known for their rapid response to infections, during which they produce a variety of proinflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ and TNF-α. Importantly, ILC1s encompass functionally distinct subsets with either cytotoxic or non-cytotoxic properties, allowing them to exert diverse effector functions during immune responses12,13. ILC1s are distinct from conventional NK cells, despite sharing some similar characteristics, and they are mainly found as tissue-resident cells in various organs75.

TLRs in human ILC1

ILC1s express certain TLRs, which are crucial for recognising PAMPs and initiating immune responses. Circulating neonatal ILC1-like cells in human umbilical cord blood have been shown to express TLR1 but not TLR2, with minimal expression of other TLRs76. Moreover, a study investigating circulating human ILC1s in septic patients found a reduced number of ILC1s in peripheral blood compared to healthy volunteers. This reduction was associated with increased apoptosis, as indicated by active caspase-3 expression. However, the expression levels of TLR2 and TLR4 on ILC1s did not change significantly after activation with zymosan or lipopolysaccharide (LPS), respectively, in either septic patients or healthy volunteers77. These findings suggest that the TLR expression profile of ILC1s can vary significantly depending on the developmental stage (Fig. 4).

TLRs in murine ILC1

Furthermore, studies using murine models have shown that ILC1s from mouse spleens express TLR4, but not TLR2, TLR3, or TLR578. TLR4 has been identified as a key receptor expressed by ILC1s, particularly in the context of atherosclerosis. In this pathology, ILC1s have been found in increased numbers compared to other ILCs, and they contribute to disease progression by enhancing the expression of proinflammatory cytokines in a TLR4/BACH2-dependent manner78. ILC1s isolated from murine spleens produced higher levels of IFN-γ, IL-1β, and IL-6 upon stimulation with oxidised low-density lipoprotein or the TLR4 agonist LPS, but not when stimulated with agonists for TLR2 or TLR3. This suggests that TLR4 signalling is crucial for the activation of ILC1s in this disease model. Furthermore, mice that received Lin−CD127+ST2−c-kit− ILC1 cells from TLR4-deficient mice exhibited fewer atherosclerotic lesions compared to those that received cells from TLR4-sufficient mice, indicating that TLR4 expression on ILC1s is necessary for the development of atherosclerosis78 (Fig. 4).

In addition to TLR4, recent studies have highlighted a role for TLR9 in murine liver ILC1 biology. In a short-term high-fat diet model, ILC1s showed increased TLR9 expression at both mRNA and protein levels, accompanied by enhanced lipid catabolism and upregulation of T-bet79. Pharmacological blockade of TLR9 reduced liver steatosis and significantly decreased ILC1 frequency and differentiation, pointing to a direct role for TLR9 in connecting nutrient-derived signals to ILC1 expansion and metabolic reprogramming79. These findings suggest that TLR9 signalling links innate sensing pathways to metabolic regulation in liver ILC1s under dietary stress. Conversely, during severe inflammation like macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), systemic triggering of TLR9 and TLR3/4 pathways induces a rapid loss of liver ILC1s through mitochondrial apoptosis. Importantly, this study does not formally demonstrate cell-intrinsic TLR signalling in ILC1s, suggesting that their activation and subsequent apoptosis likely occur secondary to a highly inflammatory cytokine milieu rather than direct TLR engagement80.

In summary, murine ILC1s express specific TLRs, particularly TLR4 and TLR9, which play significant roles: TLR4 mediates proinflammatory responses in diseases like atherosclerosis, whereas TLR9 is involved in metabolic regulation in the context of a short-term high-fat diet. In contrast, during MAS, triggering of TLR9 and TLR3/4 pathways leads to rapid apoptosis of liver ILC1s. However, in this context, direct TLR signalling within ILC1s has not been formally demonstrated, suggesting that these effects are likely mediated indirectly through the inflammatory milieu. Furthermore, human data demonstrated that other TLRs such as TLR1 in neonates and TLR2 in septic patients are also expressed. The current understanding of TLR expression in ILC1s is limited, and further research is needed to fully elucidate the functional roles of these receptors in various physiological and pathological contexts (Table 1).

ILC2s

ILC2s are key players in type 2 immunity at barrier sites because they help in the immune system response to parasitic infections and contribute to tissue repair81,82. However, ILC2s are also associated with the development of allergic diseases such as asthma and atopic dermatitis because they also respond to allergen exposures. ILC2s are a cell type that does not possess antigen-specific receptors but releases large amounts of type 2 cytokines such as IL-5 and IL-13 in response to innate cytokines including IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP81–85.

TLRs in human ILC2

It has been described that human circulating ILC2s express tlr1, tlr4, and tlr6 mRNA levels and that stimulation with mixed TLR ligands (Pam3csk4, LPS, and chitin) induces CD154 expression and secretion of IL-5 and IL-13, but not IL-4, thereby promoting IgE antibody production by autologous B lymphocytes86. Moreover, other authors showed that although LPS does not modify TLR4 expression levels, activation with a TLR2 ligand, zymosan, increased TLR2 expression in ILC2 from septic patients but not in ILC2 from healthy volunteers77. In addition, in vitro studies with LPS from multiple species of Gram-negative bacteria demonstrated that TLR4 is a potent activating receptor of human ILC2s, but not murine ILC2s, promoting their proliferation and the secretion of type 2 effector cytokines IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13. This ILC2 activation via the LPS-TLR4 signalling axis does not depend on the typical IL-33-ST2 pathway. Instead, NF-kB and Janus kinase (JAK) pathways are involved87. Studies in patients with respiratory tract diseases identified TLR4-expressing ILC2s in human nasal polyp tissue, which displayed markedly higher expression of memory cell markers IL-17RB, CD44, IL-5, and IL-13 compared with TLR4⁻ ILC2s88 (Fig. 4 and Table 1).

TLRs in murine ILC2

On the other hand, in vivo experiments indicated that the appearance of ILC2 in the peritoneum depends on an in situ differentiation of bone marrow-derived hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and/or ILC2 precursors when mice were treated with low-dose zymosan. However, the enhanced inflammatory environment under high-dose conditions seems to be capable of inducing the development of ILCs in an independent manner from BM-derived HSPCs89. Furthermore, Pam3csk4 (PAM) activates TLR2/NF-κB/AP-1 axis in ILC2s and induces type 2 cytokine secretion82. Co-stimulation with PAM and House Dust Mite (HDM), which is a major cause of asthma, enhanced both the absolute number of ILC2s and allergic cytokine expression in mouse in vivo experiments, leading to critical asthmatic features such as eosinophilic inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness in a TLR2-dependent manner82. Moreover, additional in vivo studies have demonstrated that ILC2s can undergo an alternative activation pathway directly mediated by TLR4 signalling, independently of the classical IL-33–ST2 axis. These findings suggest that TLR4 engagement represents a novel mechanism of ILC2 activation and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target in microbial infection–related inflammatory responses driven by ILC2s87. In line with this, exposure to HDM allergens has been shown to directly stimulate ILC2s via TLR4-dependent activation of ERK, p38, and NF-κB signalling pathways90. In another study, re-exposure to HDM was shown to enhance the accumulation of TLR4-expressing, IL-13⁺ ILC2s in the murine lung88. This mechanism reveals how environmental antigens can amplify type 2 immune responses through ILC2s, thereby promoting sustained inflammation in allergic airway disorders90 (Fig. 4 and Table 1). Notably, although functional data are currently lacking, transcriptomic analysis from the ImmGen resource indicates that Tlr1 is highly expressed in intestinal ILC2 at steady state, suggesting a potential, yet unexplored, role in ILC2 biology (Table 1).

ILC3s and LTis

ILC3s are relevant innate effectors implicated not only in the defence against extracellular pathogens but also in epithelial barrier maintenance. These cells can be activated by direct triggering of environmental sensors or in response to cytokines produced by epithelial cells and antigen-presenting cells91.

ILC3s secrete IL-22 and IL-17A and are characterised by the expression of the transcription factor RORγt, which is critical for their development and function. They are divided into two subsets, natural cytotoxicity triggering receptor 1 (NCR)+ ILC3s and NCR- ILC3s, depending on NKp44 (human) and NKp46 (mouse) expression92,93. These cells are involved in maintaining intestinal tissue balance and promoting intestinal stromal cell proliferation94. In addition, ILC3s play a critical role in the development and progression of different pathologies such as cancer92.

TLRs in human and macaques ILC3

Human ILC3, previously named as CD127+ LTi-like ILC cells95, express various TLRs, including TLR1, TLR2, and TLR6; however, only TLR2 agonists, when combined with cytokines like IL-2, IL-15, and IL-23, reliably boosted cytokine production in human CD127+RORγt+ cells95. Moreover, NF-kB activation contributes to TLR2-stimulated cytokine secretion95 (Fig. 4).

On the other hand, studies in rhesus macaques showed that during HIV/simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) infections, there is a persistent gut microbial translocation, leading to a decrease in ILC3 cells in lymphoid tissues, impairing ILC3 function and damaging lymphoid tissue. Microbial products in HIV/SIV infection can trigger ILC3 apoptosis via TLR-dependent pathways. Therefore, all of this contributes to persistent immune activation during HIV infection96.

TLRs in murine ILC3

Furthermore, it has been shown that a TLR2 agonist with IL-2 significantly boosted IL-22 secretion in colon explants from RAG1KO WT mice but not in those from RAG1KO HIF-1α RORγt Cre mice, suggesting that TLR2 signalling via HIF-1α triggers IL-22 production by ILC3s97. However, it was previously demonstrated that mouse CD127+ LTi-like ILC and NKp46+RORγt+ cells neither exhibit TLR2 transcripts or protein, nor respond to TLR2 stimulation95. Therefore, in mice during an enteric infection such as Citrobacter rodentium, TLR2 stimulation is not likely to directly affect ILC3. Instead, it induces proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-18, which upregulates HIF-1α in ILC3 to produce IL-2297. On the other hand, in vitro experiments demonstrate that stimulation with Poly (I:C) could promote IL-22 production by NCR−ILC3s. In particular, ligands for TLR3 and TLR9 showed the ability to directly activate NCR−ILC3s98 (Fig. 4).

In summary, human ILC3s respond directly to TLR2 stimulation, enhancing cytokine production, whereas mouse ILC3s lack TLR2 expression and rely on TLR2-indirect pathways to produce IL-22. Mouse ILC3s can also be directly activated by TLR3 and TLR9, broadening their cytokine response. These species-specific mechanisms emphasise the need for tailored approaches in ILC3-related immune research and therapies (Table 1).

TLRs in LTis

LTi cells are required for embryonic development of secondary lymphoid tissue in the mouse99,100. More recently, human LTi cells have been described in both fetal and postnatal tissue. These cells express CCR6 and RORγt and can produce cytokines such as IL-17, IL-22 and lymphotoxin11,101. Murine LTi cells express CCR6, CD4, CD90.2 and CD117 as well as receptors for IL-1, IL-2, IL-7 and IL-2311,102.

Shiu J et al. demonstrated that mouse LTi CD4+ cells are present in the stomach and promote lymphoid follicle formation in response to infection but are inhibited by the expression of a negative regulator of TLR signalling, IRAK-M102. In addition, in vitro studies showed that LTi cells (described by the authors as such) express TLR2 and TLR4 mRNA, as well as TLR2 protein. Upon stimulation with the TLR2 ligand zymosan or the TLR4 ligand LPS, these cells upregulated the expression of OX40L, CD30L, and TRANCE103.

ILCs as targets for TLR agonists

TLR agonists, known for their powerful adjuvant properties, are increasingly being studied in vaccine research because of their potential to enhance immune responses and thus vaccine efficacy104,105. TLR agonists can be natural or synthetic. Examples of natural ones include lipoproteins and peptidoglycans for TLR2, double-stranded RNA for TLR3, LPS for TLR4, single-stranded RNA for TLR7 and TLR8 and unmethylated CpG motifs in bacterial DNA or viruses for TLR9104,106. On the other hand, synthetic TLR agonists, such as Pam3CSK3 for TLR2, Poly I:C for TLR3, monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA) for TLR4, imidazoquinoline-like molecules for TLR7/8 and unmethylated CpG DNA for TLR9, have been described. These synthetic TLR agonists mimic the molecular patterns and immunostimulatory activities of natural ligands104,107,108.

TLR agonists have been shown to be highly effective in the activation of innate immunity as components of various vaccine adjuvants and immunomodulatory agents used against infectious diseases and cancers109. Currently, there is a strong effort to discover potential therapeutic applications of TLR agonists as enhancers in the stimulation of NK cell effector immune function for cancer immunotherapy and infectious disease therapy20 (Fig. 3).

NKs are activated directly by TLR agonists (bacteria-associated peptidoglycan, LPS, virus-derived dsRNA, and DNA with CpG motifs) through TLRs or indirectly by NK cell-activating cytokines secreted by coexisting dendritic cells and macrophages at the site of infection20. This interaction implies that NK cells may have an important role in both infectious disease therapy and cancer immunotherapy by secreting different cytokines, chemokines, and cytotoxic granules20. For instance, SMU-Z1, a TLR1/2 agonist, has been shown to both reactivate latent HIV-1 and enhance NK and CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity without global T cell activation, highlighting its potential as a dual-function immunomodulator in antiviral strategies44. Other in vivo studies showed that SC1, a novel TLR7 agonist, activated NK cells in a TLR7- and IFN-α-dependent manner and, therefore, SC1 reverses NK cell anergy, resulting in the effective lysis of tumour cells110. Furthermore, systemic administration of a TLR7 agonist (R848) with obinutuzumab improved the response in syngeneic human CD20-expressing models of lymphoma, preventing tumour recurrence111. Although R848 activates NK cells in vivo, the described mechanism relies on the indirect ‘priming’ or preparation of NK cells through IL-12 produced by accessory dendritic cells, which in turn drives a robust IFN-γ release by the NK cells111. Regarding TLR8 agonists, VTX-2337 is currently in phase II clinical trials for multiple oncological indications112. VTX-2337 promotes TLR8 stimulation and inflammasome activation (specifically through the release of IL-18 by monocytes, which indirectly augments NK cell activity) that potentially complements FcγRIII engagement and increases clinical responses in squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck patients treated with cetuximab113.

Polysaccharide krestin, which is a mushroom extract from Trametes versicolor, is a potent TLR2 agonist. In vitro and in vivo studies showed that treatment with PSK can activate human NKs and improve the clinical response to trastuzumab in a breast cancer model. Trastuzumab is a humanised monoclonal antibody that targets HER2, and it was the first HER2-targeted therapy approved by the Food and Drug Administration20,114.

TLR agonists are being explored as immunoadjuvants in breast cancer vaccines and adoptive cell therapies to enhance anti-tumour immune responses. Through activation of TLRs on antigen-presenting cells, they facilitate improved antigen presentation, upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules, and cytokine release, collectively strengthening T cell activation against tumour antigens. Notable examples include poly(I:C) for TLR3, imiquimod and resiquimod for TLR7/8, and CpG oligodeoxynucleotides for TLR9. Combinatorial approaches with immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4) are under clinical evaluation, aiming to synergise innate immune activation with checkpoint blockade to overcome immunosuppression and improve therapeutic outcomes115–119.

In vivo assays with TLR3 knockout mice showed that endogenous TLR3 stimulation is necessary to acquire full function of NKs and the immune protection against metastasis120. Consistently, recent findings demonstrated that, beyond its role as an exogenous adjuvant, TLR3 contributes to cancer immunosurveillance through endogenous sensing mechanisms that sustain NK cell responsiveness. In the absence of TLR3, NK cells display impaired cytokine production and reduced control of metastatic spread, underscoring a pivotal role of this receptor in the suppression of experimental tumour metastasis even without administration of its agonist120. In melanoma and breast cancer, TLR3 agonists have been evaluated in phase I/II trials as adjuvants for therapeutic vaccination121.

Due to the molecular heterogeneity of breast cancer and the multifaceted role of TLRs in the tumour microenvironment, tailored strategies based on tumour-specific features (such as TLR expression patterns, immune infiltration, and mutational burden) are likely required to optimise clinical benefit115,122. Although certain TLRs, like TLR2 and TLR4, have demonstrated both tumour-promoting and tumour-inhibiting effects, others such as TLR3 and TLR5 stand out as potential therapeutic targets because of their clearer roles in stimulating anti-tumour immune responses. This functional diversity highlights the intricate nature of using TLRs in breast cancer treatment, making it essential to thoroughly understand their underlying mechanisms115.

Furthermore, an upregulation of TLR expression on NK and NKT-like cells has been consistently observed in patients with gastric cancer, particularly in more advanced tumour subtypes. Notably, increased expression of TLR2, TLR3, TLR4, and TLR9 has been reported in patients with gastric cancer compared to healthy volunteers, suggesting that these receptors play a critical role in the immune response to the disease. Importantly, this elevated TLR expression correlates with disease progression, highlighting the potential of TLR profiling, particularly in peripheral NK cells, as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker123. Building on this, recent studies have shown that NK cells can be activated through both direct and indirect mechanisms by TLR7/8 agonists encapsulated in virus-like particles (TLR7/8a VLPs), enhancing both NK-mediated cytotoxicity and T cell activation124. By bridging innate and adaptive immunity, these approaches position TLR-targeting strategies as promising tools to improve the efficacy of current immunotherapies in solid tumours123.

Moreover, preclinical studies in tumour models have provided additional mechanistic insights into the therapeutic potential of TLR7/8 activation. In particular, TLR7/8 agonists have been shown to reprogramme NK cell dynamics, enhancing their effector functions and delaying tumour progression in melanoma models. These findings support the concept that TLR engagement can reshape innate immune responses within the tumour microenvironment and potentiate antitumor immunity125.

In this context, TLR agonists, particularly those targeting endosomal receptors such as TLR8 and TLR9, are under active investigation as anticancer agents, both as monotherapies and in combination with conventional treatment modalities. Ongoing phase I and II clinical trials are evaluating their efficacy in combination with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapies, reflecting their ability to modulate the tumour microenvironment and enhance antitumor immune responses115,123,126.

Specifically, TLR9 agonists have demonstrated the capacity to activate NK cells and promote Th1-skewed immune responses, contributing to enhanced antitumor activity. For example, the synthetic oligonucleotide IMO-2055 activates TLR9-dependent pathways and has shown favourable safety profiles in combination with chemotherapy in colorectal cancer patients, supporting its potential in combinatorial immunotherapeutic strategies127,128.

Similarly, TLR8 and TLR9 agonists such as R848 and ODN 2006 have been shown to enhance NK cell cytotoxicity against leukemic cells, highlighting that TLR stimulation can directly potentiate NK cell effector functions, including proliferation, cytokine production, and cytotoxicity. Notably, R848 primarily signals through TLR8 to enhance the activity of CD56brightCD16− NK cells, including those derived from ovarian cancer patients, supporting its role as a potential immunotherapeutic adjuvant59,129.

Beyond TLRs, modulation of the cGAS–STING axis has also emerged as a strategy to enhance NK cell–based immunotherapies. Expression of the viral nuclease Poxin suppresses cGAS–STING–mediated type I IFN responses, limiting apoptosis and thereby enhancing the persistence, cytotoxic activity and anti-tumour activity of NK and CAR-NK cells in preclinical models70.

Regarding other ILC subsets, there is limited information on the modulatory potential of TLR agonists, although such insights could provide a basis for their use in improving vaccination outcomes (Fig. 4). For instance, the synthetic lipid A analogue CRX-527 has been shown to directly activate TLR4 on ILC2s, promoting their proliferation and the production of type 2 cytokines IL-5 and IL-13. This response occurs independently of IL-33 but depends on NF-κB and JAK signalling pathways. Similarly, lipid A derivatives such as MPLA are widely employed as vaccine adjuvants to boost antigen-presenting cell function and enhance adaptive immune responses87.

Based on the presented summarised data, TLR agonist activation on ILCs represents a promising frontier in cancer immunotherapy and the development of antimicrobial therapies.

Conclusions and perspectives

ILCs exhibit species-specific patterns of TLR expression and activation. Both human and murine NK cells express multiple TLRs and respond directly to their ligands by producing cytokines. Murine ILC1s predominantly express TLR4, while human ILC1s display variable expression, including TLR1, TLR2, and TLR4, depending on the context. ILC2s in both species express TLR2 and TLR4, with human ILC2s also expressing TLR1 and TLR6. Human ILC3s respond directly to stimulation via TLR1, TLR2, and TLR6, whereas murine ILC3s primarily rely on indirect pathways but can also be directly activated through TLR3 and TLR9. In contrast, TLR expression in LTi cells remains poorly defined, although TLR-mediated signalling has been reported. Finally, it is essential to reiterate that the activation of NK cells and ILCs by TLR agonists relies on a complex interplay of both direct and indirect mechanisms. While these cells can directly respond to PRR engagement, they are powerfully driven by indirect soluble signals, such as cytokines derived from other TLR-stimulated immune or stromal cells. In this framework, direct PRR-mediated activation might represent a complementary signalling pathway whose physiological relevance is likely context-dependent.

In this review, we provide an updated overview of TLR involvement in ILC function, emphasising species-specific differences, molecular mechanisms, and experimental findings. Additionally, this review explores the therapeutic potential of TLR agonists in NK-mediated immunotherapy, highlighting their relevance in the development of novel treatments for infectious and inflammatory diseases.
