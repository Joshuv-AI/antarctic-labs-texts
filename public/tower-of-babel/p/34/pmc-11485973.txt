Inuit mental health service utilisation in Manitoba: results from the qanuinngitsiarutiksait study
Authors: Josée G Lavoie, Wayne Clark, Leah McDonnell, Jeevan Toor, Nathan Nickel, Polina Anang, Michael Arvaarluk Kusugak, Tagaak Evaluardjuk-Palmer, Nuqaalaq Brown, Grace Voisey Clark, Sabrina Wong, Julianne Sanguins
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite decades of Inuit accessing services in Manitoba, Inuit-centric services remain scant and have only begun to emerge. This article reports on Inuit utilisation of mental health services in Manitoba. In this study, we focused on two interrelated cohorts: Inuit living in Manitoba and Inuit from the Kivalliq region who come to Winnipeg to access specialised services. We used administrative data routinely collected by Manitoban agencies. The study was conducted in partnership with the Manitoba Inuit Association, and Inuit Elders from Nunavut and Manitoba. Our results show that mental health-related consults represent between 1 in 5 and 1 in 3 of all consults made by Inuit in Manitoba. Rates of hospitalisation for mental health conditions are considerably lower than those of residents from the Manitoba northern health authority. Given that Nunavut has the highest rate of suicide in the world, our results suggest underserved needs rather than lower needs. Kivalliq and Manitoba Inuit utilise mental health services in Manitoba extensively, yet these services for the most part remain western-centric. Epistemological accommodations in the provision of mental health services have yet to be implemented. This is now the focus of our work.KEYWORDS: Nunavut, Indigenous, primary healthcare, suicide, psychiatry

Background

The mental health of the Canadian Inuit has attracted considerable attention in the literature, even in comparison to other Indigenous peoples [1]. This focus has presumably been in response to the disproportional rates of suicide documented among Canadian Inuit [2–8]. Many reasons have been offered to explain this situation, including the disastrous history of colonialism that displaced traditional economies, knowledge systems and undermined family and individual self-reliance [9,10]; and the continuous marginalisation of newly created Inuit communities. To date, Inuit have been provided limited opportunities to secure benefits from Canadian society and from extractive industries active in Inuit territories [11]. Uncertainty associated with climate change is the latest of a series of challenges Inuit have to face, as a result of decisions made outside of their input and control [12–15]. Although the Aqqiumavvik Society of Arviat Nunavut has done considerable work towards the development of Inuit constructs of mental health and well-being [16,17] and trauma-informed care as the basis for programming, this work has yet to result in the development of Inuit-centric therapeutic pathways and tools to address the distress documented.

The Qanuinngitsiarutiksait: Developing Population-Based Health and Well-Being Strategies for Inuit in Manitoba project was pursued at the request of the Manitoba Inuit Association (MIA). It is a Canadian Institutes for Health Research-funded partnership (since 2016) co-led by university-based researchers from the University of Manitoba and MIA, further supported by researchers from the University of British Colombia and the Manitoba Metis Federation. The word Qanuinngitsiarutiksait means tools for the well-being/safety of Inuit/people. The overall study documented the experience and needs of Inuit accessing health and other services in Manitoba and developed strategies to facilitate transitions to and from Nunavut. This includes Inuit living in Manitoba, Canada, estimated to be at least 1500, and Inuit from the Kivalliq region of Nunavut (just under 10,000 individuals) who come to Winnipeg to access specialised services (approximately 16,000 consults per year). Specific objectives included 1) developing detailed profiles of Inuit accessing services including length of residence (permanent, short-term, or long-term relocation), types of services accessed, unmet needs, costs, and challenges associated with relocating to and accessing services in Manitoba; and 2) exploring solution-oriented options with impacted families, MIA, and allied health agencies presently serving Inuit in Manitoba. The study was conducted under the careful guidance of Inuit Elders, co-authors of this paper. A number of analyses have already been published [18–24]. This article focuses on mental health service utilisation in Manitoba, among two cohorts: Inuit living in the Kivalliq region and Inuit living in Manitoba. Our purpose is not to compare the cohorts, but rather to show needs in support of the development of Inuit-centric services in Manitoba. Overall, health service utilisation data has already been published [18].

Conceptualising Inuit mental health

Inuit law is informed by three relational terms: tirigusuusiit, which is at times translated as the observance of “taboos”, but that can perhaps best be understood as observing conducts of respect for the natural environment; piqujait, meaning obligations to respect rules made by those in Inuit society who are respected knowledge holders; and maligait, which refers to observance of rules as asked. Inuit law is focused on the correction of behaviours: integration into society is prized, and coercion and punishment are rarely used [25]. The objective is to maintain balance and harmony. Kirmayer and colleagues describe the notion of the Inuit self as in constant relation with others and with the physical environment [26]. Vallee further describes Inuit living in a close relationship with the spirit world, including some co-habiting with the spirit of loved ones, without signs of mental illness [27]. Outward directed aggression was historically identified as rare among Inuit [27]. Emotional control is paramount to Inuit identity. Inuit recognise that words can hurt someone’s feeling to the point of causing illness for that individual, and disharmony:

When the mind is in turmoil, you can have difficulty sleeping and lose your appetite. When you are under stress, the mind can be very powerful. It can even prevent you from sleeping. Even if you try not to be depressed, if you think too much, your mind can become heavy, and you can completely lose your appetite. That is a known fact. [Elder Akisu Joamie, cited in [28], p. 245]

The Western labelling of individuals’ mental health experience (“depressed”, “schizophrenic”) as a way to focus therapeutic support, is not shared by Inuit.

Mental health problems were attributed to four broad classes of explanation: (1) physical or organic effects of the environment or human behavior; (2) psychological or emotional factors related to child-rearing, interpersonal relations, and mental functioning; (3) various forms of spirit possession or intrusion; and (4) cultural change and social disadvantage. People readily employed multiple explanations to some mental health problems, the choice of explanations and their relative emphasis reflecting pragmatic concerns that shift over time [29] p. 79].

Inuit portray mental illness as independent of the individual and usually transitory. Causes of mental illness could be physical or organic, emotional, or psychological, and spiritual through spirit possession. Physical causes centred around a lack of traditional food. Environmental changes associated with extractive industries, pollution and climate change undermine Inuit’s relationship to traditional food and their environment.

Colonialism resulted in the imposition of foreign value structures through the displacement of local subsistence economies in favour of often unattainable wage employment [30]; a legal system which is predicated on denunciation, punishment and ostracism [10]; western education, including residential school, reflecting values of individualism, competition and self-reliance [31] over inter-dependency; Christianity with its focus on shame and the rejection of other belief systems as wrong, and at times, “evil” [26]; and the medical system, with its nearly exclusive focus on individual physical health with little consideration for spiritual, mental, emotional and contextual dimensions [32]. While Inuit have long been familiar with trauma and had effective mechanisms to support recovery, healing from the relentless impact of colonial trauma has been identified as essential to re-establish harmony [32], but challenging.

Problematising indigenous reliance on Western mental health services

Multiple studies documented an over-diagnosing of psychosis/schizophrenia in minority populations in comparison to White populations, particularly among Black (UK and African American) and migrant communities (Europe based), with these communities receiving diagnoses up to four times that of White communities [33–37]. As explanations, researchers point to, (1) a mix of socio-contextual factors potentially interacting with biological ones, but which remain neglected in treatment/diagnosis [35,38]; (2) contextual/environmental factors impacting symptoms which are then perceived as psychosis/schizophrenia; and (3) practitioners’ ethnic bias/implicit racial bias, rooted in colonialism and structural racism [35,37,39–42]. In the African-American context, it was suggested that “cultural mistrust” rooted in historical tensions between the Black community and a primarily white provider community could also reproduce paranoia in healthcare settings [43]. In minority/underserved populations, misdiagnosis of psychosis/schizophrenia can lead to adverse side effects from medication leading to a spiral of further problems [44].

In the Indigenous context, research is beginning to be conducted with Māori, reflecting findings similar to the Black and migrant communities, that diagnosis of first episode psychosis is significantly higher than their non-Indigenous counterparts [45–47]. One study adjusted for sex, age, deprivation, and location (urban/rural) found that the ethnic differences in incident rate were reduced but that younger Māori were still more than twice as likely to be diagnosed with first episode psychosis than young non-Māori. One study found a link between higher subclinical psychotic-like experiences and adverse factors (childhood trauma, discrimination, financial stress) among Māori [48]. Another study found higher trend of diagnosis among the Indigenous population in Northern Australia [49]. The literature thus suggests caution in the interpretation of rates of medical consults from Indigenous communities, as misdiagnoses and overmedicalization can artificially distort rates. Despite these findings, culturally appropriate diagnostic measures have not yet been taken up.

Access to culturally appropriate health and mental health services

Nunavut is divided into three regions: the Qitirmiut, situated to the northeast, with a population of 6,710; the Kivalliq, located north of Manitoba with a population of 11,472; and the Qikiqtaaluk, previously known as Baffin Island, with a population of 18,725 [50], 2015 data. The Iqaluit-based Qikiqtani General Hospital stands as the hospital in Nunavut and mainly serves the healthcare needs of the Qikiqtaaluk area [51]. Individuals from these areas who need advanced medical care must usually leave the territory, to destinations defined by air travel routes. Individuals from the Qitirmiut area tend to seek services in Edmonton, Alberta, while those from the Kivalliq area go to Winnipeg, Manitoba; and those from the Qikiqtaaluk area usually go to Ottawa, Ontario [18].

The continuum of mental health services includes mental health and wellness promotion, counselling, and treatment of acute conditions. The literature distinguishes between different approaches used to accommodate culture in programming. Programmes rooted in the culture of the people who will benefit from the programming are referred to as culture as treatment programs [52]. In this case, this could mean working with Inuit Elders to identify knowledge and approaches and providing land-based treatment. These programmes are not necessarily devoid of Western-based treatment modalities; however, such modalities are integrated within a culture-specific framework. It is generally acknowledged that using culture as treatment produces good results [53–55], despite limited empirical evidence [52]. In contrast, programmes that incorporate cultural components into western-informed programmes to modify then and improve cultural appropriateness are referred to as culture in treatment programs [52]. Strategies might include translating a Western-informed treatment or pamphlet or furnishing a room with artwork or posters that represent the culture of the people they serve. Culture as treatment models have been enthusiastically endorsed as a form of cultural affirmation [52,55]. In addition, we argue that trust and credibility are key factors in therapeutic relationships and that culture as treatment models may better resonate with Indigenous participants.

The Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, the national Inuit political organisation, developed an Inuit-centric mental wellness plan, predicated on the following principles:

Still, access to mental health services informed by Inuit culture, whether culture as treatment or culture in treatment, remains somewhat aspirational. Services delivered by Western-trained professionals are the norm.

Mental health, wellness promotion, and counselling are available in Nunavut, a territory where the population is 85% Inuit, but where, despite continuous efforts, 53% of the positions within the health field are held by Inuit, primarily as administrative support [57]. Elders continue to act as counsellors to family and community members experiencing anguish and loss. The extent to which this occurs has yet to be documented. All Nunavut regions have Elders groups, who are consulted on programme development. Community-based wellness services and youth centric programmes are available in some communities through funds from several federal programmes [58]. Helplines are in place, providing support in Inuktitut and English from 19:00 to midnight. The Nunavut Department of Health and Social Services (DHSS) provides the overall direction for mental health and addictions programming. Community-based mental health workers are in place, although staffing these positions with Inuit has been a challenge, as these positions can result in considerable vicarious trauma, and some community members may prefer to trust providers that are not from their community, to ensure privacy and confidentiality. A first point of contact with clinical care is through Community Health Nurses working at community-based health centres. These professionals are often transient non-Inuit with limited training in mental health. They are supported by family physicians, either in person in those larger communities with resident family physicians or by telephone in between family physician visits, non-resident mental health nurses, resident social workers, and community health representatives (Inuit trained in a paraprofessional health promotion role). There is no psychologist resident in Nunavut. Some visiting psychiatrists make scheduled visits and provide ongoing and diagnostic care [58].

Acute care is provided in southern healthcare settings in Edmonton for the Kitikmeot, Winnipeg for the Kivalliq and Ottawa for the Qikiqtani regions. In addition, the Manitoba-based Selkirk Mental Health Centre provides specialised inpatient mental health and acquired brain injury treatment and rehabilitation services. Access to Inuit-centric mental health services that is trauma-informed and integrate context and Inuit values, does not exist, to our knowledge. The Akausisarvik Mental Health Treatment Centre, located in Iqaluit, is a 15-bed residential facility, and possibly the only acute care facility providing Inuit-centric mental health services in the country. It opened in 2013. Programming details could not be located.

Creating a continuum of Inuit-centric mental health promotion, counselling, and clinical care services, in the context of a population of 38,780 people dispersed across 25 communities, with limited infrastructure and housing pressures, is a daunting task. Conceptual work integrating western and Inuit understanding of mental health has existed for decades [26,29,59–61], however interest in having this work informs therapeutic strategies in Manitoba [62] is only emerging [63,64].

Methods

Inuit from the Kivalliq versus Inuit living in Manitoba

Discussions with Elders working on this project suggest that a dichotomised approach to understanding the two cohorts are inappropriate. The Inuit community remains closely knit, with strong relationships between relatives wherever they live. Still, while strong cultural bonds remain, opportunities for intergenerational sharing of teachings are likely to be more limited in urban environments. Opportunities for Inuktitut language acquisition and retention are likewise more limited. Still, creating sharp boundaries between the two cohorts may be misguided, given the substantial degree of mobility between Nunavut and Manitoba (and other locales) related to access to housing pressures in Nunavut and better access of employment, education, and services in Manitoba.

Data source and scope

To identify Inuit in health administrative datasets, we used two interrelated approaches. For Inuit living in Manitoba (cohort 1), we assumed that all of those who ever had a Nunavut Health Care Number (NHCN) were Inuit. Upon relocation to Manitoba and after a lapse of 3 months, Inuit are expected to change their healthcare card to a Manitoba card. The two numbers are independent, and Manitoba does not keep a record of the previous NHCN. To create cohort 1 (Inuit living in Manitoba), Manitoba health did a probabilistic linkage of information on the NCHN and Manitoba health card. A flag was added to the resultant dataset, records were anonymised, and transferred to the Manitoba Centre for Health Policy (MCHP).

Variables

We focus on selected two sentinel mental health conditions, namely 1) schizophrenic disorders, paranoid conditions and 2) depression (defined as persistent depressed mood and long-term loss of pleasure or interest in life, often with other symptoms such as disturbed sleep, feelings of guilt or inadequacy, and suicidal thoughts), which have been shown to be responsive to community-based interventions, when such services are available and responsive. These types of conditions are known as Ambulatory Care Sensitive Conditions (ACSC) and have been used in the past work [65,66]. While mental health is often not included in definitions of primary healthcare, services provided in Nunavut include out-patient mental health and counselling. We also include injury and poisoning: we recognise that injury and poisoning, as a category, does not distinguish between accidental injuries (associated with car accidents, traditional harvesting activities, workplace injuries) and intentional injuries (attempted suicide). Likewise, poisoning includes intentional and accidental overdoses associated with prescription or illicit substance misuse, including misuse of prescription drugs because of dementia or food poisoning. As a category, injuries and poisoning are imprecise, yet we believe that it adds texture to our analysis. We attempted to access data that might more precisely point to addiction, however, at the time of analysis, the quality and specificity of this data was insufficient.

Analysis

Because of the size of our sample, and because of challenges in determining a denominator for Kivalliq Inuit, where a significant portion of primary care services are accessed in the Kivalliq and are not included in the data we can access, we present unadjusted descriptive statistics. We present age and sex adjusted rates per 1000 of hospitalisation for Kivalliq Inuit and compare these rates to those of Manitoba residents, of the northern regional health authority and those living in smaller communities.

Discussion

The Qanuinngitsiarutiksait study is the first attempt at documenting mental health services utilisation in Manitoba by Kivalliq Inuit and by Inuit living in Manitoba.

We are aware that our study has several limitations. We report on challenges associated with each cohort separately. For the Inuit from the Kivalliq region accessing care in Manitoba, first, we realise that the population of Nunavut is 84.9% Inuit. This goes up to 91.1% for the Kivalliq region [67]. Thus, if all Kivalliq residents utilise Manitoba Health services equally, we could assume at nearly 1 consult for every 10 is from a non-Inuit person. Yet, we also know that a majority of non-Inuit residents of Nunavut moved to Nunavut for employment and are likely to have higher socio-economic status. In contrast, Inuit are known to shoulder a significantly higher burden of chronic diseases, and to experience higher mortality rates [68]. Consequently, we anticipate that the number of consults and hospitalisations by non-Inuit would be considerably less than 1 in 10, and insignificant. Second, our definition of mental health services is not comprehensive, and excludes problematic substance use. This exclusion reflects a methodological limitation: currently, the data on health services utilisation for problematic substance use is somewhat unreliable. For example, we know that not everyone with a substance disorder will seek care and get diagnosed, resulting in numerous false negatives. Diagnostic biases are also suspected, where Indigenous people are more likely to be diagnosed with a substance abuse disorder compared to non-Indigenous people who might be more likely to be diagnosed with a mood or anxiety disorder. Third, we are aware that some Inuit children are accessing mental health services at the Selkirk Mental Health Centre. This Centre provides mental health stabilisation and residential care to children in crisis, and hosts Inuit from Kivalliq and elsewhere in Nunavut. Our data does not reflect the utilisation of these services.

We also note some limitation to the cohort of Inuit living in Manitoba. First, we are aware of instances where Inuit families from the Kivalliq moved to Winnipeg, returned to Nunavut and came back eventually. Our count thus includes the same individual counted separately. Second, the method we used to identify Inuit was to include anyone living in Manitoba who previously had a Nunavut healthcare number. As a result, we may have missed some Inuit families who were living in Manitoba before the creation of Nunavut. Second, our final cohort is small, and limited the number of analyses we could undertake. For example, we could not define a denominator for this cohort, thus preventing us from calculating rates.

In addition to these limitations, we believe that rates of referrals to Western-based mental health services are a fragment of mental health care needs. We recognise that some Inuit won’t seek the support of Western-trained mental health providers and favour instead reaching out to Elders. Still, we remain confident that the cohorts created are representative and that our results are important steppingstones to the creation of more responsive Inuit mental health services. Finally, while MCHP offers a sophisticated data platform and analysts intimately knowledgeable of the data and its limitation, administrative data remain blunt instruments, subject to diagnostic biases and data entry errors.

Our results nevertheless show that Manitoba-based mental health service utilisation by Inuit is significant: mental health-related consults represent between 1 in 5 and 1 in 3 of all consults made by Inuit in Manitoba. Our results show that hospitalisation for mental health concerns is comparable to those of Manitobans from small communities. Rates are considerably lower than those of Manitobans from the northern health authority. This result should be interpreted with caution. Katz et al. [69] documented that suicide rates for First Nations living on-reserve in the northern health authority were 65 per 100,000 (2012–16). In comparison, the rate of suicide in Nunavut was 99 per 100,000 in 2011 [70]. Thus, the lower rates of hospitalisation for mood disorders might reflect unmet needs, and a lack of referral or a refusal to seek Western-informed care, rather than lower needs. Conversely, rates of hospitalisation for schizophrenia in Indigenous communities may more readily reflect Western biases towards Indigenous culture and inappropriate diagnostics. Shifting rates from year to year might also reflect barriers to access.

Noteworthy, a diagnosis of psychosis/schizophrenia is heavily reliant on consultation format – which may not align with the aspects culturally relevant to the patient and are often too rapid to gain a “full picture” [71,72]. In the Canadian First Nation context, Ryder suggests that spiritual aspects of culture can be confused with/misunderstood as psychosis/schizophrenia [73].

Finally, some literature is emerging around the idea of over-medicalisation and/or over-pathologizing life experiences in psychiatry [71,74]. This link was also specifically made in the Indigenous context of the Māori, where it was suggested that adverse experiences considered “normal” (those relating to systemic racism and colonial impacts) to the community were pathologized, along with culturally-related spiritual experiences [48]. We note that 25–30% of all consults by Inuit were for mental health services and wonder whether health concerns might have been misdirected to mental health consultations, based on misunderstandings. Further research will be required to shed light on these findings.

In the context of our findings, this study points to a need for prudence in the interpretation of rates of mental illness. The overrepresentation of Kivalliq Inuit, residents of small communities and of the northern RHAs may point to needs, and to cultural biases leading to the overmedicalization of cultural expressions of distress. These findings confirm our assumption that culturally appropriate Inuit-centric diagnostic and treatment services as badly needed, to address potential misdiagnoses and over medicalisation of Inuit, and ensure that Inuit requiring mental health support can access effective programming that also instil trust.

Conclusions

Our Qanuinngitsiarutiksait study makes a significant contribution to our understanding of mental health care utilisation and needs. Kivalliq and Manitoba Inuit utilise mental health services in Manitoba extensively, yet these services for the most part remain western-centric. To date, epistemological accommodations in the provision of mental health services, i.e. culture in treatment, have been limited. Opportunities to mobilise culture as treatment [75–77] as a decolonised approach to mental health care service provision is not apparent in mental health and wellness programming accessible in Manitoba.

Based on discussions with Inuit Elders on this project and comments from the community, we believe that such services should be based on Inuit traditional knowledge of conflict resolution. Fortunately, seminal work is being undertaken by the Arviat-based Aqqiumavvik Society, which has been documenting traditional Inuit knowledge for restoring harmony and maintaining family and community cohesion in challenging times [16,17,32,78,79]. We are currently collaborating with MIA on designing Inuit-centric mental health interventions to respond to the needs of our study, in partnership with Elders from the Aqqiumavvik Society located in Arviat Nunavut.
