Surgical excision of recurrent renal angiomyolipoma after previous embolization and nephron-sparing surgery - A case report
Authors: Ajit Khadga, Mahesh Bahadur Adhikari, Bipin Maharjan, Prashant Mishra, Deepak Kumar Yadav, Ashok Kumar Thakur
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRenal angiomyolipoma is the most common benign kidney tumor, representing 1–3 % of solid renal tumors. Despite its benign nature, it can be associated with lethal hemorrhage, and can also show signs of local extension mimicking malignant tumors. The cornerstone of treatment remains angioembolization and nephron sparing surgery.Presentation of caseA 51-year-old Nepalese woman presented to the emergency department with acute abdominal pain and signs of hypovolemic shock. She was managed with resuscitation, followed by angioembolization of a left renal artery pseudoaneurysm. A few months later, she underwent laparoscopic nephron-sparing surgery. One year later, she had excision of a recurrent left angiomyolipoma. Both the initial and final histologic examinations confirmed the diagnosis of classic variant renal angiomyolipoma.DiscussionAlthough transcatheter angioembolization is the treatment of choice for bleeding angiomyolipoma, there is a higher likelihood of re-embolization and the need for surgical excision. Therefore, surgeons should carefully consider treatment options and discuss the pros and cons of each with the patient and their family, taking into account the available infrastructure and expertise.ConclusionWe report a rare and interesting case of acutely bleeding angiomyolipoma. The patient was successfully treated with angioembolization followed by laparoscopic nephron-sparing surgery. However, the tumor recurred a year later, necessitating conversion to open nephron-sparing surgery.

Introduction

Angiomyolipoma (AML) is typically a solid “triphasic” tumor composed of varying amounts of three elements: dysmorphic blood vessels, smooth muscle components, and mature adipose tissue [1]. The majority of AMLs are isolated and occur sporadically (80 %). They are typically identified in adults, with a mean age at symptomatic presentation of about 40 years, and show a strong female predilection (F:M::4:1) [2]. Selective transcatheter embolization (TE) has been the first-line treatment for acutely bleeding AML. While there is minimal data in the acute setting, embolization generally appears to be associated with fewer complications, though it may carry an increased risk of repeat intervention [3]. We present the case of a 51-year-old female who underwent excision of a recurrent left renal AML after angioembolization and laparoscopic nephron-sparing surgery a year ago. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [4].

Case presentation

A 51-year-old female presented to the emergency department with a complaint of acute left flank pain and abdominal distension for a few hours, associated with nausea and difficulty breathing. She has no comorbidities or past surgical history. On examination, her general condition appeared ill. Her blood pressure was 80/60 mmHg, pulse rate was 120 bpm, oxygen saturation was 92 %, and she was afebrile. The abdomen was tender and distended. Initially, she was managed in the ER with intravenous fluids, antibiotics, and analgesics. Once her vital signs improved, a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis was performed, revealing a large predominantly exophytic fat density lesion measuring 8.08 × 6.35 × 6.33 cm arising from the postero-superior aspect of the left kidney. Additionally, a large perinephric, left pararenal, and pelvic hematoma was observed (Fig. 1a). A saccular aneurysm measuring 11.3 x 9.4 mm, along with two small fusiform aneurysms measuring 3.6 x 3.5 mm and 3.6 x 3.3 mm, were observed in the superior segmental branch of the left renal artery (Fig. 1b).Fig. 1Preoperative imaging. After embolization, the patient was transferred to the ICU. On the third day of admission, she was shifted to the ward and discharged on the fifth day. On follow-up ultrasound after two weeks, an echogenic mass was observed arising from the upper pole of the left kidney (73.2 × 73.1 mm) and a hypoechoic collection was noted in the posterior aspect of the left kidney (57.9 × 26.8 mm). On follow-up after three months, her ultrasound scan showed an echogenic mass measuring 69 × 55 mm in the upper pole of her left kidney. Subsequently, a CECT revealed a well-defined predominantly fat density exophytic solid lesion measuring 6.63 (CC) × 6.18 (T) × 6.15 (AP) cm arising from the posterior aspect of the upper pole cortex of the left kidney (Fig. 3), suggesting left renal angiomyolipoma.Fig. 3Axial view of computed tomography scan showing, left renal angiomyolipoma three months post angioembolization.Fig. 3

Axial view of computed tomography scan showing, left renal angiomyolipoma three months post angioembolization.

After obtaining consent, she underwent laparoscopic left nephron-sparing surgery (NSS). Intraoperatively, a yellowish fibrofatty mass measuring 6 × 5 cm was observed arising from the upper pole of her left kidney. Intraoperative ultrasound confirmed the complete excision of the angiomyolipoma. Postoperative recovery was uneventful; the drain was removed on the third postoperative day, and she was discharged on the fourth day.

Histopathological examination revealed a tumor composed predominantly of mature adipose tissue, interspersed with thick-walled blood vessels showing hyalinization, along with smooth muscle fibers. Atypical cells and necrosis were not observed.

The patient failed to follow up at six months and presented after a year with left flank discomfort. An ultrasound scan revealed a well-defined, homogeneously hyperechoic lesion measuring 7.1 × 6.7 × 6.2 cm in the upper left pole of the kidney. A contrast-enhanced CT scan showed a well-defined, predominantly fat density exophytic solid lesion measuring 5.71 cm (CC) × 5.4 cm (transverse) × 5.5 cm (AP), arising from the posterior aspect of the upper pole cortex of the left kidney. (Fig. 4), suggestive of recurrent left AML.Fig. 4Sagittal view of computed tomography scan showing, large recurrent angiomyolipoma arising from left upper pole of the kidney.Fig. 4

Due to dense adhesions, the procedure was converted to an open surgery with a 5×cm left subcostal incision. The postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on the fifth day after surgery. Histopathological examination revealed no pleomorphism, mitosis, or necrosis. It showed a well-circumscribed, partly encapsulated tumor composed of triphasic components, including an admixture of mature adipose tissue (Fig. 7a) and smooth muscle fibers arranged in fascicles and bundles (Fig. 7b), along with numerous varying blood vessels (Fig. 7c). The diagnosis was a benign mesenchymal tumor, specifically a classic variant of angiomyolipoma.Fig. 7Microscopic view of the triphasic components of AML: (a) adipose tissue, (b) blood vessels, and (c) smooth muscle fibers.Fig. 7

Discussion

Retroperitoneal hemorrhage associated with the spontaneous rupture of angiomyolipoma is the most life-threatening presentation of the disease. After renal cell carcinoma, angiomyolipoma is the second most common renal cause of retroperitoneal hemorrhage. Most patients present with severe flank pain and often with a palpable mass, while up to 20 % to 30 % are present in hypovolemic shock [5].

In an influential case series and literature review of 253 patients, Oesterling et al. reported that 64 % were symptomatic, and 40 % presented with hemorrhage. These numbers became more dramatic in tumors larger than 4 cm, with 82 % being symptomatic and 51 % presenting with hemorrhage [6]. In contrast, contemporary series have reported much more modest rates of 10 % being symptomatic and only 2 % risk of hemorrhage [7].

Classical AML by non-contrast CT is based on the presence of fat in a renal mass, defined in Hounsfield units (HU) as −10 (−15 to −30 HU) [8].

Likewise, renal AML can be classified as fat-rich, fat-poor, and fat-invisible using CT or MRI [9]. It should be kept in mind that this finding can also be seen in renal cell carcinoma (RCC), lipoma, liposarcoma, oncocytoma, Wilms tumor, and teratoma; therefore, other characteristics such as the contrast enhancement pattern and the presence of calcifications must also be considered [8]. Some research centers have reported cases of AML concomitant with renal cancer in 1 % of the patients [10].

CT is highly sensitive for detecting mass and hematoma, and it is the imaging modality of choice when hemorrhage is suspected [5]. Pseudoaneurysm on CT may be predictive of severe hemorrhage [11].

Selective arterial embolization (SAE) has become the first line of management, being a safe, minimally invasive alternative, with a success rate of 90–100 %, and emergency control in 96–100 % of the patients [12]. It has a grade C indication in prophylactic management of hemorrhage at a growing AML of >0.25 cm/year, and/or the presence of aneurysms of >5 mm [13]. The recurrence rate in the results of 16 series is 25 %, with a reintervention requirement in up to 37 % of the patients managed with this technique [14]. The role of SAE prior to partial nephrectomy reduces, according to Tan et al., blood loss, hospital stay, and residual tumor in renal AML cases of >7 cm [15].

Boorjian et al. described the largest series of sporadic AML and open nephron sparing surgery, at 8 years of follow-up, a recurrence rate of 3.4 %, and 12 % de novo chronic kidney disease (CKD), respectively [16].

The Association of Urology in 2019 concluded that nephron sparing surgery had similar morbidity to SAE but seemed to be the most effective option to prevent recurrence and the need for secondary treatment [17].

Active surveillance with ultrasound or CT scans at 6 months, 12 months, and then annually is recommended [18]. It is important for clinicians to be aware of the malignant potential of epithelioid variants of AML and the need for long-term follow-up, as there have been case reports of recurrence even 12 years after the initial surgery [19].

Conclusion

This case highlights the challenges associated with managing recurrent angiomyolipoma, particularly after initial treatments such as angioembolization and laparoscopic nephron-sparing surgery. While embolization is effective in controlling acute bleeding, the potential for recurrence underscores the need for careful monitoring and consideration of surgical options. Given the lack of consensus on follow-up protocols, patients—especially those with epithelioid variants and AMLs exhibiting high-risk features—may require extended surveillance with ultrasound or CT scans.
