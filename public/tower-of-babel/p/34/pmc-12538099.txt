Cell-penetrating antibody enhances nuclear delivery of triplex-forming oligonucleotides targeting HER2-positive cancers
Authors: Anthony Minnah, Nicole M García Tubéns, Adam Krysztofiak, Yanfeng Liu, Peter M Glazer, Faye A Rogers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNucleic acid therapies (NATs) such as triplex-forming oligonucleotides (TFOs) offer innovative cancer treatment options to selectively target amplified oncogenes, such as HER2. However, challenges in intracellular and nuclear delivery hinder their clinical translation. Here, we explore the use of a lupus-derived anti-DNA monoclonal antibody, 3E10, as a delivery system for HER2-targeted TFOs. 3E10, which naturally localizes to tumors by binding extracellular DNA in necrotic regions, effectively transports TFOs into the nucleus. Moreover, the D31N mutant of 3E10 demonstrated superior binding and delivery efficiency in both in vitro and in vivo HER2-positive breast cancer models. These results establish a promising platform for TFO-based precision cancer therapies, leveraging the tumor-targeting properties of 3E10 to enhance bioavailability.Keywords: MT: Delivery Strategies, nucleic acid delivery strategies, triplex-forming oligonucleotides, tumor delivery, antibody-DNA conjugates, HER2-positive breast cancer

Introduction

Nucleic acid therapies (NATs), such as antisense oligonucleotides, small interfering RNA, and small hairpin RNA, are emerging as innovative approaches for treating certain cancers.1,2 These drugs offer significant advantages over traditional therapies as they can be designed to be highly specific to the target, thereby limiting off-target side effects. Moreover, their specificity provides an opportunity to target genes previously deemed “undruggable.” However, the intracellular delivery of these therapies to tumor cells is hindered by several challenges, including their negative charge, which limits cellular uptake; susceptibility to nuclease degradation, which reduces stability in biological fluids; and rapid systemic clearance, which restricts bioavailability.3

Among NATs, triplex-forming oligonucleotides (TFOs) have emerged as a promising alternative to target amplified oncogenes.4 TFOs bind in the major groove of duplex DNA and distort the helical structure, which impedes replication fork progression leading to DNA double-strand breaks. In normal cells, triplex-induced DNA damage is quickly repaired by the nucleotide excision repair pathway (NER). However, in tumor cells characterized by oncogene copy number gains, the formation of multiple triplex structures creates excessive DNA damage that tilts the balance in favor of inducing apoptosis to preserve genomic integrity.5 This therapeutic strategy provides an alternative approach to treat cancers characterized by gene amplification through manipulation of the DNA damage response rather than the targeting of the overexpressed protein products.

Here, we explore the delivery of TFOs with a cell-penetrating anti-DNA monoclonal antibody, 3E10. In this study, we investigate the use of 3E10 as an effective method for nuclear delivery of HER2-targeted TFOs, HER2-205 and HER2-5922, into cells with HER2 gene amplification. By characterizing the binding affinities and DNA damage induction potential of four distinct complexes (3E10-wild type [WT]: HER2-205, 3E10-WT: HER2-5922, 3E10-D31N: HER2-205, and 3E10-D31N: HER2-5922), we establish a novel delivery platform for improving TFO-based cancer therapy.

Results

3E10 antibody-based delivery system for HER2-targeted TFOs

Recent studies using the HER2-targeted TFOs, HER2-205 and HER2-5922, demonstrated that these molecules can induce copy-number-dependent DNA damage in cell lines (Figure 1) and effective tumor growth suppression in HER2-amplified breast cancer xenograft models.6 Despite the therapeutic potential of TFOs for treating HER2-positive breast and ovarian cancers, their clinical translation can be limited by inadequate nuclear delivery, the location of their target site. Efforts to improve TFO cellular bioavailability, specifically strategies that enhance their nuclear uptake, hold promise for improving drug efficacy. Addressing these delivery obstacles is crucial to unlocking the therapeutic potential of TFOs, which prompts the exploration of novel strategies to enhance their efficacy in clinical applications. 3E10 is a lupus anti-DNA autoantibody that can cross the nuclear membrane and inhibit DNA repair.7,8,9,10 Previous studies have shown that this humanized antibody is a reliable delivery vehicle due to its specific nuclear localization.9,10,11 Its mechanism of nuclear penetration involves two main functions. First, the antibody’s cationic nature allows it to bind to extracellular DNA. This interaction facilitates its entry through the equilibrative nucleoside transporter 2 (ENT2), a nucleoside transporter found in both the cellular and nuclear membranes7,11,12 (Figure 1C). This process relies on the nucleoside salvage pathway and makes its entry mechanism independent of endocytosis. Interestingly, the 3E10 antibody predominantly localizes to tumors due to its natural affinity for extracellular DNA, which is abundant in necrotic tumor microenvironments, enhancing its tumor specificity.8 To further enhance its binding ability, another variant of the 3E10 antibody with a mutational change of the aspartic acid at residue 31 to asparagine (D31N) has been developed, which creates an increased binding affinity for both single-stranded DNA and double-stranded DNA11,13 (Figure 1D).

3E10 antibody binds TFOs with nanomolar affinity

We explored the ability of the 3E10 antibody to form complexes with the single-stranded TFOs in vitro. We first established the optimal conditions for complex formation, demonstrating that binding was best facilitated in water at room temperature for 30 min. This observation aligns with our hypothesis that binding occurs primarily through electrostatic interactions. Subsequently, we used electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) to examine the binding affinity of the 3E10 antibody to our HER2-targeted TFOs. We measured the fraction of TFOs bound, held at a constant concentration (1 μM), against varying concentrations of the antibody. EMSA results showed strong binding affinities for the WT and D31N antibody variants with the HER2-205 and HER2-5922 TFOs. Indeed, nearly all TFOs reached 100% binding at the 1:1 complex ratio (Figure 2A). Comparative analysis of the Kd values revealed that the D31N antibody (381 nM) had a higher affinity for the TFOs than the WT antibody (565 nM), a statistically significant result (Figures 2A and 2B). These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that the D31N variant has an expanded positively charged pocket that enhances its anti-DNA activity compared to the WT antibody.11 Further, the affinity of both antibodies for the HER2-205 TFO was higher than that for the HER2-5922 TFO (Figures 2A and 2B), with this difference being particularly pronounced for the WT antibody. We hypothesize that the shorter HER2-205 TFO (24 bases) permits tighter binding compared to the longer HER2-5922 TFO (30 bases).

Next, we performed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements to analyze the binding kinetics of the antibody-TFO complexes. To measure the on rate of the complex formation, we affixed the antibody on the surface of the chip and passed a solution of the TFOs over the sensor. The TFOs were then replaced with a buffer solution to measure the off rate of the complexes, with the process repeated for increasing concentrations of each TFO (Figure 2C). The data were modeled as a first-order reaction, keeping the amount of antibody captured on the sensor constant while varying TFO concentrations. SPR analysis demonstrated that, compared to the WT antibody, the D31N antibody had a higher affinity (lower Kd) for the TFOs as observed in a higher binding rate (ka) and lower unbinding rate (kd) (Figures 2D–2F). These findings demonstrate that the D31N antibody exhibits significantly higher binding affinity and enhanced kinetics for TFOs compared to the WT antibody.

3E10 antibody delivers TFOs into cancer cells and tumor xenografts

After confirming 3E10:TFO binding affinity, we proceeded to analyze the efficiency of TFO delivery into the nucleus by WT and D31N variants in human cancer cell models. For this purpose, we utilized cell lines with HER2 gene amplification, including the breast cancer BT474 (Figures 3A–3D) and ovarian cancer SKOV3 (Figures S1A–S1D) cell lines. Cells were treated with complexes of dye-labeled HER2-targeted TFO (TAMRA-HER2-205) and 3E10 antibody variants at several ratios relative to the TFO. Delivery efficiency was assessed by measuring mean fluorescence intensity using confocal microscopy 24 h after treatment. Overall, both 3E10 antibody variants, WT (Figures 3A and S1A) and D31N (Figures 3C and S1C), showed delivery of HER2-205 to the nucleus using multiple antibody:TFO ratios. Interestingly, increasing the concentration of the antibody facilitated higher nuclear uptake (Figures S1E and S1F). As such, the 4:1 antibody:TFO ratio demonstrated the highest level of uptake for both 3E10 variants (Figures 3B–3D, S1B, and S1D) compared to untreated cells. This suggests that multiple antibody molecules can bind to a single oligonucleotide and facilitate a higher nuclear delivery rate. The D31N variant has been characterized to have a stronger affinity to DNA (Figures 2B and 2F) as well as increased cellular and nuclear penetration compared to the WT 3E10 antibody,11 which resulted in a significantly increased nuclear uptake of TFOs (Figures 3B–3D, S1B, and S1D). Next, we evaluated the ability of the delivered TFOs to induce DNA damage in BT474 cells as measured by the phosphorylation of H2AX at serine 139 (γH2AX). Consistent with the binding assay, HER2-205 was more effective at inducing damage compared to HER2-5922 (Figures 3E and 3F). However, the levels of induced damage were similar for delivery with the WT and D31N variants.

To further evaluate the potential of the 3E10 antibody as a delivery platform for TFO-based cancer therapies, we used a subcutaneous BT474 breast cancer xenograft model in athymic nude mice. Complexes of 3E10 antibody and TAMRA-HER2-205 at a ratio of 3:1 (antibody:TFO) were administered retro-orbitally, and uptake was assessed by measuring fluorescence intensity in tumor sections 24 h after treatment (Figures 3G and S1G). TFO complexes with both 3E10 antibody variants showed significant tumor uptake in comparison to TFO-only or non-treated controls (Figure 3H). Moreover, the D31N variant showed similar results to the cell studies, demonstrating a better TFO tumor delivery than the WT antibody variant.

In summary, our data show that 3E10 antibodies create stable complexes with TFOs, which can penetrate the cell nucleus and thus deliver the molecules to the therapeutically desired cellular compartment. Additionally, these complexes showed improved TFO delivery into tumors using a human cancer xenograft mouse model. The D31N variant, due to its higher DNA binding and cell-penetrating properties, showed overall superior TFO delivery capabilities in both in vitro and in vivo cancer models. Overall, our results suggest that the 3E10 antibody can be an attractive platform for the delivery of therapeutic oligonucleotides in cancer therapy.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the potential of 3E10 WT and its D31N variant as delivery platforms for TFOs in HER2-amplified cancers, addressing a critical barrier in the clinical translation of NATs. Our findings highlight that 3E10 forms stable complexes with HER2-targeted TFOs and efficiently delivers them to the nucleus of HER2-amplified cancer cells, with the D31N variant exhibiting superior binding and delivery efficacy. Additionally, in vivo studies confirmed the ability of 3E10 to deliver TFOs to tumor tissues, further validating its potential as a therapeutic delivery system.

The use of 3E10 offers several advantages over traditional HER2-targeted antibody-drug conjugates. Its ability to bind TFOs through simple mixing circumvents the need for complex conjugation strategies, simplifying drug preparation and potentially reducing costs. Furthermore, its nuclear delivery capability broadens the applicability of 3E10 to other NATs, enabling therapeutic targeting of a wide range of intracellular and nuclear pathways. This versatility positions 3E10 as a platform technology with far-reaching implications in cancer therapy. While the HER2-205 and HER2-5922 were selected for their previously demonstrated activity, further optimization of TFO design could enhance binding affinity and synergistic efficacy with 3E10. Nonetheless, this work underscores the promise of combining NATs with monoclonal antibodies like 3E10 to overcome delivery barriers and unlock the full therapeutic potential of nucleic acid-based cancer therapies.
