Risk Factors for Pouch Neoplasia in Patients With Inflammatory Bowel Disease: A Case-Control Study
Authors: Shanshan Wang, Huaibin Mabel Ko, Dana J. Lukin, Marco Bertucci Zoccali, Usama Ahmed Ali, Ravi P. Kiran, Ellen J. Scherl, Bo Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsPatients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) undergoing total proctocolectomy with ileal pouch-anal anastomosis remain at risk of pouch neoplasia. Prior studies, based on cohorts ending in the 2010s, identified prior colorectal dysplasia and carcinoma as independent risk factors. We aimed to reassess contributors from a contemporary, prospectively maintained registry.MethodsPatients with an ileal pouch and underlying IBD were identified from tertiary centers (2019–2026) for a case-control study. Seventeen patients with pouch neoplasia were randomly matched 1:3 to 51 patients without pouch neoplasia. Pediatric patients (<18 years) and non-IBD pouches were excluded. Univariate and multivariable analyses were performed.ResultsAmong 748 patients, 17 (2.3%) had pouch neoplasia, including 1 (5.9%) with anal intraepithelial neoplasia grade 1, 3 (17.6%) with low-grade dysplasia, 4 (23.5%) with high-grade dysplasia, and 10 (58.8%) with cancer. Five patients were diagnosed with anal squamous neoplasia, of whom 4 (80.0%) tested positive for anal human papillomavirus. On multivariable analysis, dysplasia and carcinoma in the colectomy specimen were identified as independent risk factors for pouch neoplasia (adjusted odds ratio, 6.2; 95% confidence interval, 1.1–36.7; P = .047; and adjusted odds ratio, 12.3; 95% confidence interval, 2.5–60.8; P = .002, respectively). An older age at pouch construction, a longer duration from IBD diagnosis to pouch construction, and concurrent pouchitis were significantly associated with pouch neoplasia on univariate analysis but did not remain significant on the multivariable model.ConclusionThe presence of dysplasia or carcinoma in the colectomy specimen remains the strongest risk factor for pouch neoplasia. An unusual prevalence of anal squamous neoplasia was observed, with a notable presence of human papillomavirus infection.

Introduction

Patients with ulcerative colitis, less frequently Crohn’s colitis or indeterminate colitis, may require total proctocolectomy and ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (IPAA) for medically refractory colitis or colitis-associated neoplasia (CAN). The cumulative risks of undergoing total proctocolectomy in patients with ulcerative colitis were reported at 13.1% and 18.9% at 5 and 10 years from diagnosis.1 Although proctocolectomy, with or without mucosectomy, significantly reduces the risk of CAN, it does not eradicate it, with the cumulative incidence of pouch neoplasia ranging from 4.2% to 6.9% at 20 years following pouch construction.2,3

Pouch neoplasia in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is uncommon, which has occasionally led to questions regarding the necessity of routine surveillance with pouchoscopy.4 However, large case series have demonstrated that pouch cancer is associated with a poor prognosis, especially when diagnosed at an advanced stage.5,6 The American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy and the American Gastroenterological Association recommended surveillance pouchoscopy with intervals stratified by risk factors.7,8 Annual pouchoscopy was advised for patients with pre-colectomy CAN, a consistently recognized risk factor, while pouchoscopy every 1–3 years was suggested for those with proposed risk factors, including primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), family history of colorectal cancer (CRC), chronic pouchitis, Crohn’s disease (CD) of the pouch, chronic cuffitis, or histological findings of type C mucosa.

Previous recommendations were largely derived from cohorts studied through the 2010s.2,3,9,10 Since then, several advances may have altered the risk profile for pouch neoplasia: an expanded understanding of pouch disorders (structural and functional conditions beyond the classic inflammatory pouchitis), the introduction of biologic agents in the management of chronic pouchitis or Crohn’s-like condition of the pouch, and the expanded role of endoscopic therapies in treating pouch complications such as strictures and fistulas, which are associated with increased neoplastic risk in colonic IBD.11 Studies published in the past decade have reported 13 and 10 cases of pouch neoplasia from the Cleveland Clinic4 and Mayo Clinic,12 respectively, underscoring the limited ability to robustly evaluate risk factors for pouch neoplasia given the rarity of this condition. Therefore, we aimed to contribute additional data to the literature by reassessing the risk factors for pouch neoplasia in a contemporary cohort.

Patients and Methods

Data Source

Medical records were extracted from a prospectively maintained, institution board review–approved Pouch Registry at Columbia University Irving Medical Center/New York-Presbyterian Hospital in patients who had not withdrawn research authorization to complete clinical and diagnostic data collection. A 1:3 matched case-control study was performed to identify risk factors for pouch neoplasia.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The case group included consecutive patients with IBD and an ileal pouch who were diagnosed with pouch neoplasia involving the prepouch ileum, pouch body, cuff, or anal transition zone (ATZ) between December 2019 and April 2026. The pouch neoplasia includes neoplasia of glandular or squamous cell origin, lymphoma, and neuroendocrine tumors. Control subjects were randomly selected from the Pouch Registry and had no evidence of pouch neoplasia at the last follow-up.

The following were excluded from both groups: patients under 18 years of age; ileal pouches created for gastrointestinal disorders other than IBD; histologic diagnoses of indefinite for dysplasia without subsequent confirmation of dysplasia or cancer; and metastatic colorectal disease without an identifiable primary lesion within the pouch. Additionally, patients with a non-pelvic continent pouch (Kock pouch) were excluded from the control group, given their markedly lower neoplasia risk,13 which would have introduced heterogeneity into control selection.

Demographic, Clinical, and Endoscopic Variables

The demographic and clinical variables included sex, age at diagnosis of IBD, smoking status, family history of CRC and IBD in first-degree relatives, history of PSC, IBD subtype, extent of colonic involvement, age at pouch construction, duration from IBD diagnosis to pouch construction, indication for colectomy (medically refractory colitis or CAN), dysplasia or carcinoma in the colectomy specimen, pouch configuration (J pouch, S pouch, or H pouch), staged pouch construction (entailing protecting diverting ileostomy, construction of the pouch, and stoma closure later), mucosectomy (the removal of rectal stump), and duration from pouch construction to detection of pouch neoplasia. For the control group, the interval from pouch construction to the last follow-up was calculated to match the pouch-to-neoplasia duration of the case group. Pouch-related disorders, such as a history of chronic pouchitis (>4 episodes of acute pouchitis per year or persistent symptoms >4 weeks despite therapy), refractory cuffitis (nonresponsive to topical mesalazine or corticoids), CD of the pouch (presence of fistula or abscess in the pouch body or prepouch ileum, prepouch ileitis, or noncaseating granuloma on histology), stricture at pouch-anal anastomosis, and perianal fistula of the pouch, were collected. In some patients, neoplasia involved 2 contiguous locations. The location with the largest affected surface area or higher neoplastic histopathological grade was considered the predominant location of neoplasia, and the less affected site was considered the synchronous lesion. Pouch neoplasia locations included the ATZ, cuff, pouch body, and prepouch ileum. Endoscopic findings at the time of neoplasia detection or from the most recent pouchoscopy in control patients were documented, including evidence of pouchitis and cuffitis. Endoscopic pouchitis and cuffitis were defined by the presence of endoscopic features included in the Pouch Disease Activity Index,14 such as edema, granularity, friability, loss of vascular pattern, mucous exudates, or ulceration. At our institution, all patients with active follow-up underwent pouchoscopy at least every 3 years for surveillance, except for 58 patients who presented for a second opinion. Yearly surveillance and disease-monitoring pouchoscopy was performed for patients with pre-colectomy CAN and every 1–3 years for those with PSC, chronic pouchitis, CD of the pouch, or chronic cuffitis. The practice pattern was consistent with American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy guidelines.7

Histologic Assessment

“Pouch neoplasia” is a term generally used in the manuscript or previous publication, defining neoplastic lesions in the pouch body and its proximities, including the prepouch ileum, cuff, or ATZ. The histologic phenotypes of pouch neoplasia, therefore, can be low- or high-grade dysplasia of glandular source, adenocarcinoma,15 anal intraepithelial neoplasia (AIN) grades 1, 2, 3, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC),16 lymphoma, and neuroendocrine tumor. To maintain clarity and consistency, the term neoplasia was used throughout to collectively refer to both cancer and dysplasia, whereas features specific to dysplasia or carcinoma were reported separately. The control group consisted of patients with no histologic evidence of dysplasia or cancer on the last follow-up date.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables, including means and standard deviations for continuous factors and frequencies for categorical factors. Comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the independent t-test for continuous variables. Variables with a P value <.05 in univariate analyses were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to identify factors independently associated with pouch neoplasia, using a backward stepwise selection. Both indications for colectomy and the presence of neoplasia in the colectomy specimen were evaluated as candidate variables in univariate analyses. Given the overlap whereby neoplasia in colectomy specimens includes pre-colectomy CAN and incidental neoplasia found in colectomy specimens for medically refractory disease, the latter variable was selected for inclusion in the multivariable model. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using logistic regression. Statistical significance was defined as a P value <.05. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software version 31 (IBM, Chicago, IL).

Discussion

Pouch neoplasia, although low in incidence, may carry a poor prognosis.5,6 Therefore, defining risk factors is crucial to guide surveillance strategies and clinical management. In this case-control study, which included 17 patients with pouch neoplasia matched to 51 controls, dysplasia and carcinoma in the colectomy specimen were identified as independent risk factors for pouch neoplasia.

Colorectal neoplasia prior to pouch construction has consistently been shown to be associated with the development of pouch neoplasia.2,3,12 In a cohort of 3203 patients followed at the Cleveland Clinic between 1984 and 2009, Kariv et al2 identified 38 cases of pouch neoplasia and reported that pre-colectomy colitis-associated dysplasia and carcinoma were independent risk factors for pouch neoplasia. Similarly, Derikx and colleagues3 found 25 cases with pouch neoplasia in the Dutch registry of 1200 patients (1991–2012) and also outlined that prior colorectal dysplasia and carcinoma significantly increased the risk of pouch neoplasia. More recently, Urquhart et al12 reported 10 cases among 1319 patients at the Mayo Clinic and described that pre-colectomy rectal dysplasia was associated with pouch neoplasia. In alignment with these findings, our study further confirmed that dysplasia and carcinoma in the colectomy specimen remain the strongest risk factors for pouch neoplasia.

Following proctocolectomy, the ileal pouch construction can be completed either with a stapled anastomosis with a rectal cuff or a handsewn anastomosis with mucosectomy. The remnant rectal mucosa (cuff) above the ATZ may be susceptible to inflammation (cuffitis) and malignant transformation.17 Whether mucosectomy confers a protective effect against pouch neoplasia remains debatable. A meta-analysis showed an 8-fold higher risk of pouch cancer in the anorectal mucosa in stapled anastomosis compared with mucosectomy.18 However, mucosectomy did not appear to eradicate the neoplasia risk. Across studies, 20% to 50% of patients with neoplasia in the ATZ had undergone mucosectomy.2,4,6 This may be attributable to the technical difficulty of achieving complete rectal mucosa excision, with residual islets of rectum reported in 20% of patients following mucosectomy.19 We observed no significant difference in mucosectomy rates between patients with and without pouch neoplasia (35.5% vs 51.0%, P = .401).

Inflammatory disorders such as pouchitis, cuffitis, and CD of the pouch frequently occur after IPAA.13 Similar to colonic IBD,20 an inflammation-dysplasia-carcinoma sequence has been proposed in the carcinogenesis of the pouch;21 however, subsequent studies have struggled to validate this hypothesis. While chronic pouchitis has been associated with mucosal atrophy, its relationship with the development of pouch dysplasia remains unclear.9 Banasiewicz et al22 reported an association between chronic pouchitis and an increased risk of LGD, but the robustness and reproducibility of these findings were limited by the small number of LGD cases (n = 5). In contrast, large cohort studies,2,3,12 as well as our findings, have not demonstrated chronic pouchitis as an independent risk factor for pouch neoplasia. Nevertheless, the high prevalence of chronic pouchitis in both the case and control groups, likely reflecting referral bias inherent to large cohorts from tertiary centers, may have reduced the statistical power to detect such an association.

The endoscopic appearance of pouch neoplasia is often heterogeneous and nonspecific, frequently manifesting as inflammation, erosion, or ulceration,2,3,5 which overlap with the features used to assess endoscopic pouch disease activity. Despite the widespread use of the Pouch Disease Activity Index,14 no validated instrument is currently available for standardized endoscopic assessment of pouch inflammation, and interobserver variability persists even among experts.23 We deliberately evaluated the association of concurrent endoscopic inflammation of the pouch and cuff with the detection of pouch neoplasia. Endoscopic pouchitis was significantly more common in the case group than in the control group. Although it was not identified as an independent risk factor after adjusting for covariates, this finding suggests careful inspection and adequate biopsy sampling when focal and patchy inflammatory or morphologic abnormalities are observed during pouchoscopy.

Primary sclerosing cholangitis has been proposed as a risk factor for pouch neoplasia, given its association with increased incidence of CAN and chronic pouchitis;24 however, the available evidence remains conflicting.10,25 More recently, the Mayo Clinic reported a significant association between PSC and pouch neoplasia;12 however, multivariate analysis was not performed due to a low event rate (n = 10). Consistent with other large cohort studies,2,3 we did not observe a significant association between PSC and pouch neoplasia.

Although direct comparisons across studies should be interpreted with caution, a higher proportion of lesions originating from anal epithelium (29.4%) was observed in our cohort, including 1 case of AIN and 3 cases of SCC, than the 2.3% rate reported previously.5 The true prevalence of anal HPV in patients with IBD remains unclear, with reported rates ranging from 27.0% to 89.1% across small studies,26, 27, 28 and no published data exist for IBD patients with an ileal pouch. In a study of 18 IBD patients, including 3 with IPAA, anal HPV infection was associated with anal squamous neoplasia. Consistent with this prior report, 4 of 5 (75%) patients tested positive for HPV by in situ hybridization. HPV is not routinely tested in ATZ specimens, and no control group was available; nevertheless, our observations point to a significant role for HPV in the development of anal squamous neoplasia.

Our findings have important clinical implications. The natural history of pouch neoplasia, including neoplasia of squamous cell, has not been fully defined. Despite endoscopic surveillance, dysplasia was still missed before the diagnosis of cancer, and different strategies have been proposed. In our cohort, one-third of pouch neoplasia cases escaped endoscopic surveillance and presented as cancer without preceding identifiable precancerous lesions; among the 5 patients with squamous cell neoplasia, disease was detected at the precancerous stage in only 3. Endoscopic evaluation of the ATZ, cuff, or pouch body is often complicated by concurrent inflammation and the subtle, nonspecific appearance of pouch neoplasia. Currently, no standardized surveillance biopsy protocol is provided by guidelines.7,8,24 To reduce sampling error, in addition to targeted biopsies of the suspicious area (nodularity, erythema, erosion, and ulceration), a more rigorous protocol incorporating 4-quadrant random biopsies of the cuff and/or ATZ, as well as in the involved pouch body segment, may be needed. This approach is analogous to the Seattle protocol used for Barrett’s esophagus surveillance.29,30 Skin tags and perianal dermatitis should also be carefully examined and biopsied when appropriate, as anal neoplasia can mimic benign conditions.31

Some limitations in our study should be acknowledged. As a tertiary center-based investigation, our findings may be subject to referral bias. The low prevalence of pouch neoplasia limited the statistical power, potentially increasing the risk of type II errors. To reduce the risk of overfitting, we applied a more stringent univariate selection threshold (P < .05 rather than P < .10) when building multivariable models. The wide CI for certain estimates reflects the underlying uncertainty inherent to small-sample analysis. We also acknowledge the histologic heterogeneity of the case group. However, we believe that adenocarcinoma, SCC, neuroendocrine tumor, and lymphoma arising in the setting of IBD may share common pathogenetic pathways. Therefore, non-Hodgkin lymphoma and neuroendocrine tumors were included, as these histologic types have been associated with colonic IBD32,33 and reported in patients with an ileal pouch and underlying IBD.34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39 Type C mucosa (severe mucosal atrophy) was first described by Gullberg et al21 as a result of chronic inflammation and a potential contributor to malignant transformation. The term has hardly been used in the subsequent literature. In clinical practice, the term was not routinely reported in contemporary histologic examinations and therefore was not used in our cohort. Nevertheless, our study provides clinically relevant insights for future research. Chronic pouchitis may be an inadequate surrogate for validating the inflammation-dysplasia-carcinoma sequence in pouch neoplasia. Multicenter studies with a larger number of cases, incorporating detailed histologic examination and molecular analyses, may be warranted to elucidate the underlying drivers of pouch neoplasia. HPV was detected in three-fourths of the patients with AIN/SCC, highlighting the need for broader HPV testing to further clarify its role in ileal pouch neoplasia through comparative analyses. Importantly, one-third of patients with pouch neoplasia presented with pouch cancer without previously detected precancerous lesions, underscoring the limitations of current endoscopic surveillance. Improvements in endoscopic imaging, including the integration of artificial intelligence, may be helpful to enhance early detection of pouch neoplasia.

Conclusion

Dysplasia and carcinoma identified in the colectomy specimen prior to pouch construction remain independent risk factors for the development of pouch neoplasia, necessitating rigorous endoscopic surveillance in this high-risk group. Four-quadrant biopsies of the ATZ and/or cuff or pouch body should be considered when inflammatory changes are associated. The unusual frequency of anal squamous neoplasia calls for careful anal and perianal examination, combined with immunohistochemical analyses. Despite surveillance endoscopy, pouch dysplasia may still be missed, underscoring the need for improvement in surveillance techniques and strategies.
