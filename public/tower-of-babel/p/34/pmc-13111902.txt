Guess Who: Delineating Post‐Metamorphic Amphibian Life Stages and Sexes Using a Standardised Visible Approach
Authors: John Gould, Loren Louise Fardell, Alex Callen, Colin McHenry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAmong animals, adulthood is synonymous with sexual maturity and often defined by the capacity of an individual to store and/or use functional gametes. Yet, under field conditions, where evidence of gametes cannot always be easily ascertained, researchers rely on other cues of maturity, such as size and sex‐based traits, which also enable for the differentiation of the sexes. In this study, we used a multi‐season data set to help define adulthood for the green and golden bell frog, Randoidea [Litoria] aurea males and females that improves upon past methods using externally visible attributes. Our decision‐making approach is nuanced as it considers both the presence and level of expression of visibly detectable sex attributes, namely nuptial pad colouration and gravidity level, with expression levels of these traits also used to define minimum size thresholds of maturity for each sex independently. This protocol allows individuals with high expressions of sex‐based traits of maturity to be assigned as adults even if they are below minimum size thresholds, thus accounting for precocious development, and for individuals above these thresholds to be assigned as adults regardless of trait expression, thus accounting for temporal variability in these traits within the adult life stage that are related to hormone cycles. We believe this decision‐making pipeline can be applied to other amphibians by adjusting trait thresholds to species‐ or population‐specific specifications.

Introduction

Animals develop through a series of life stages defined by researchers based on morphological, ecological and behavioural changes (Hone et al. 2016; McEdward and Janies 1993; Smirnov and Vassilieva 2022). Issues may arise when the attributes used or their definitions differ between studies, resulting in difficulty in comparing between populations or species (Hone et al. 2016; Lauri 2002; McEdward and Janies 1993). Additionally, some transition points between life stages may be less clear, such as the boundary between juvenile and adult stages (Hone et al. 2016; Vidal and Shea 2023). Therefore, there is a need for robust criteria that can consistently differentiate between life stages, which should be applicable within populations over time, between populations of the same species, and, if possible, across species within major groups.

Sexual maturity is defined as the stage at which an individual has completed the development necessary for the production of functional gametes (Minelli 2011; Olmstead et al. 2009; Vidal and Shea 2023). Determining the point at which individuals transition into the adult life stage is critical for obtaining accurate information on key population parameters (Caswell 2000; Kendall et al. 2019), including population size and differences in the sizes between adult sex cohorts (Pellet et al. 2007). Thus, definitions of adulthood can have considerable influence on management by determining whether populations are in decline, particularly in the absence of genetic analyses to determine effective population size (Hoffman et al. 2004), as well as the number of adult females within the population. Reducing the occurrence of misclassifications relies on the use of reliable diagnostic features of maturity that balance the risk of assigning juveniles as adults and vice versa, as well as differentiating between the sexes at the adult stage. Under field conditions, this typically involves the use of visible features of maturity and sex as opposed to genetic or hormonal analyses or the detection of functional gametes, which is difficult to achieve without expertise or using invasive techniques and extended handling times that may be considered too much of a risk for species in decline (Graham et al. 2016; Reyer and Bättig 2004). Thus, there is a need for rapid identification of maturity and sex in the field to monitor populations in situ, which balances the risk of misclassification with the potential for causing injury/harm or impacting natural behaviours. Methodological improvements that consider these are particularly important for threatened species, because they are less resilient to the impacts associated with monitoring and as management decisions on their behalf depend on reliable data to determine population trends.

Among anuran amphibians, there are five key life stages for species with a generalised life history (i.e., non‐direct development): egg/embryo, tadpole, metamorph, juvenile and adult (Wells 2019). The transition from juvenile to adult life stages is the most challenging to define based on visual assessments, as the amphibian body gets larger but does not undergo extreme physical changes or reorganisations apparent with the previous life stage boundaries (Gosner 1960). An amphibian can be inferred to reach adulthood when it shows signs of sexual maturity, which can be based on the presence of secondary sexual characteristics (Sarasola‐Puente et al. 2011). For males, this can occur physically with the development of nuptial pads, which become thickened and rough during the breeding period to aid males in gripping females during amplexus (Wells 2019), or breeding colouration (Christy 2001; Graham et al. 2016; Orton et al. 2020; Pyke and White 2001). It can also occur behaviourally with calling, movement to breeding sites and amplexus (Felton et al. 2006; Goldingay and Newell 2005; Wells 2019). For females, physical signs of maturity are fewer (Standaert 1967), but behaviours may include movement to breeding sites and egg laying. Physical features among females, besides the absence of nuptial pads, are related to primary sex characteristics, including the development of the ovaries (Dumont 1972; Kelley 1996), which can be externally visible in some species with the aid of translumination, referred to as candle‐lighting (Gillette and Peterson 2001; Reyer and Bättig 2004). However, using visible indications of sex to then also define adulthood is challenging as these traits begin to form prior to maturity (Kelley 1996), meaning their presence is a positive indication of sex but not always of adulthood.

Immature amphibian gonads develop post‐metamorphosis, resulting in the increased circulation of sex hormones that start to develop the primary and secondary sex characteristics in the juvenile life stage (Kelley 1996). For example, testosterone is elevated 8 weeks post‐metamorphosis in Xenopus tropicalis and this coincides with the first observation of nuptial pads (Olmstead et al. 2009), yet reproductive maturation is estimated to begin at 18 weeks (Hirsch et al. 2002), and maximum reproductive capacity at 22 weeks (Olmstead et al. 2009). In X. tropicalis females, estradiol begins to be produced by the ovaries 16 weeks post‐metamorphosis, and this triggers oocytes to begin growing through stages I to VI (Dumont 1972), while female maturation is estimated as early at 24 weeks (Hirsch et al. 2002), and maximum reproductive capacity at 30 weeks (Olmstead et al. 2009). Thus, there are periods where sex characteristics are forming and detectable, including immature nuptial pads and growing eggs in juvenile females, yet individuals may not yet be reproductively competent, active or at the very least peaking (Olmstead et al. 2009; Smith et al. 1994). Levels of expression of sex‐based traits rather than their mere presence may enable the threshold point between juvenile and adult life stages to be more accurately determined, as we would not expect fully developed nuptial pads and fully gravid females until after individuals were reproductively competent and thus truly adults. Yet, few studies have quantified these changes in the visible expression of such traits (Graham et al. 2016; Olmstead et al. 2009).

Even once maturity has been reached, the level of expression of sex characteristics can be temporally variable. For example, nuptial pad expression is influenced by changing levels of sex hormones which can be cyclical, typically regressing and fading during the non‐breeding period (Kelley and Pfaff 1976; Olmstead et al. 2009; Pyke and White 2001). Likewise, female ovaries typically contain an asynchronous group of oocytes at all stages of development (Dumont 1972; Gould, Clulow, et al. 2023), with the presence of late stage VI eggs gradually increasing and then abruptly decreasing after each oviposition event (Gould, Callen, et al. 2023; Sato and Tokmakov 2020). These issues may cause individuals to be falsely classified without the consideration of supplementary attributes, such as size.

Among amphibians, individuals are commonly defined as mature once they reach a species‐specific size, determined by correlations of size with traits related to maturity (Guarino and Mezzasalma 2025). This is because size in this group is positively correlated with age, as they possess indeterminate growth that continues yet slows even after maturity is reached (Gould et al. 2022). Yet, studies that define maturity based solely on size thresholds established from past studies run the risk of misclassifications if development rates differ between individuals, even within the same cohort (Pyke and White 2001; Sarasola‐Puente et al. 2011). Given the need to classify both life stage (juvenile vs. adult) and sex (adult male vs. adult female), a rigid and singular definition of maturity should not assign the same size threshold to both sexes of a species (Klop‐Toker et al. 2016; Valdez et al. 2016), as amphibians commonly display sexual dimorphism and sex‐based differences in maturation (Shine 1979). Applying even sex‐specific size thresholds of adulthood without simultaneously taking into account other features may not allow for distinguishing between the sexes even if there is size dimorphism, as there is often some degree of overlap (Graham et al. 2016). This also disregards individual variability in maturation rates within or across populations, which is determined by the interplay between genetics and environmental conditions (Acevedo et al. 2022). A nuanced approach is required that identifies when each individual reaches sexual maturity based on a combination of sex‐specific size thresholds and levels of expression of sex‐based attributes of maturity that are visually detectable.

The green and golden bell frog, Ranoidea [Litoria] aurea, has received considerable research attention given its abrupt range contraction as a result of the introduction of the amphibian chytrid fungus and Gambusia fish (Mahony et al. 2013; Penman et al. 2008; White and Pyke 2008). The classification of adulthood in this species has differed considerably between studies based on a combination of size thresholds and secondary sex characteristics (Beranek et al. 2022; Gould, Callen, et al. 2024; Hamer et al. 2008; Pyke and White 2001; van de Mortel and Goldingay 1998), making it a suitable model for combining past classifications and determining more nuanced approaches that may be applicable to amphibians in general. In this study, we use a multi‐season data set to define adulthood for R. aurea males and females using externally visible attributes. Our decision‐making approach is nuanced as it considers both a minimum size threshold of adulthood for each sex independently based on the minimum size where a majority of individuals show high expressions of sex‐based traits, in conjunction with the presence and level of expression of visibly detectable sex attributes, namely nuptial pad colouration and gravidity level, as well as reproductive behaviours such as calling and amplexus.

Materials and Methods

Survey Design and Animal Capture

Across 3 consecutive years, we surveyed between 60 and 80 freshwater ponds on Kooragang Island, NSW, Australia (32.85734° S, 151.72561° E) for the presence of post‐metamorphic (i.e., juvenile and adult) R. aurea individuals. Upon detection, individuals were captured and processed as per Gould et al. (2021). We measured snout‐vent length (SVL) to the nearest 0.1 mm using dial callipers and recorded the expression of visibly detectable attributes of sex and maturity. We excluded the use of the term ‘sub‐adult’ which can be defined by the attainment of some features used to define adulthood (Hone et al. 2016), and only referred to transitions between juvenile and adult life stages to avoid ambiguity.

Visual Thresholds for Sexual Maturity in Males

Among R. aurea males, the nuptial pad is a raised, fleshy and keratinised pad of tissue on the outer side of the infer phalange of the front foot (Hamer and Mahony 2007). Given that the nuptial pad starts to develop in the juvenile stage in amphibians and remains present thereafter but with variable levels of expression (Christy 2001; Pyke and White 2001), its presence is a positive indication of sex but not always of sexual maturity. This is because the colouration of the nuptial pad is temporally variable (Figure 1), changing with (1) maturity level, likely starting off as uncoloured/white in juvenile males and the first appearance of colour change generally coinciding with transitions towards sexual maturity (Christy 2001), and (2) breeding activity once adulthood is reached, becoming dark during the breeding season and fading back to a uncoloured/white during the non‐breeding season or between breeding episodes via a process of shedding (Figure 2). This darkening of the pad is likely to occur in response to heightened sex hormones (androgens) that increase melanin production, which itself may not have a functional benefit but accompanies keratinisation of the pad that increases surface roughness, the function of which is to improve the male's grip during amplexus (Wyk et al. 2003; Qiu et al. 2024). Thus, a complex situation arises where dark nuptial pads are a positive indication of male sexual maturity, but individuals with light nuptial pads may be (1) sexually mature males with faded nuptial pads or (2) immature males. Individuals without nuptial pads may be (1) immature males (Hamer et al. 2008), (2) immature females or (3) mature females. Females do not have a nuptial pad but may possess dark skin in the same region that can be misidentified without sufficient training (Figure 1).

A visual assessment of sexual maturity in males can be based on the detection of nuptial pads irrespective of colouration or animal size. However, this runs the risk of falsely recording juvenile males that are just starting to form nuptial pads but are still not sexually mature. An alternative is to classify males with coloured nuptial pads only as mature, yet this will falsely classify adults that happen to have faded nuptial pads. A more nuanced approach is to determine the minimum size class where darkened nuptial pads start to be detected in the population or comprise the majority of individuals with nuptial pads. Males with nuptial pads at or above one of these chosen size classes can then be automatically classified as sexually mature regardless of nuptial pad colouration, while individuals below the size class should only be classed as sexually mature on a case‐by‐case basis based specifically on nuptial pad expression.

Based on this, for all captured individuals, we noted whether nuptial pads were present as a positive indication of the male sex and scored the colour of the nuptial pads from 1 to 3: (1) white without any traces of dark colouration to very little colouration; (2) grey−brown or splotchy colouration; and (3) dark black. We then grouped individuals based on SVL size, set to 1 mm bins (Jang et al. 2011), and determined the minimum size class where nuptial pads were: (1) first detected at any colour, (2) first detected with dark colouration and (3) dark in a majority (> 50%) with nuptial pads.

A universal issue with setting minimum size thresholds of adulthood is that it applies to individuals within a group or population (e.g., sex cohort) despite inter‐individual variability. As such, there will always be a risk of misclassifying adults as juveniles if they happen to be small and mature earlier (Error Type A), and misclassifying juveniles as adults if they happen to have slower maturation (Error Type B). In order to reduce bias in these error rates, we selected the first size class at which a substantial proportion (at least 50%) of individuals exhibited a high level of expression of sex‐based traits of maturity. This point minimises the severity of asymmetric error rates by anchoring the size threshold away from any extremes and to a position more centralised within this transition zone between juvenile and adult life stages. It is also more reflective of population‐level patterns as it is defined by multiple individuals. This contrasts with selecting an outlier data point to define the minimum size threshold, such as the smallest individual with any level of expression of maturity‐based traits, which weights the entire decision‐making process on a data point of one.

We grouped individuals with medium (level 2) and dark (level 3) nuptial pads together and defined them as being coloured, and kept individuals with light nuptial pads separate. While the level of darkening of the nuptial pad is a continuum, we set three different levels of expression in order to capture an intermediate level of expression, which represents a transitional state between little colour to a fully expressed colour. However, we grouped individuals with this transitional state as being adult males alongside those with dark nuptial pads, given that any degree of darkening of this tissue would be expected for individuals that have started to show signs of greatly increased hormone levels, and thus more likely to have viable gametes than not, or just after shedding has occurred for mature males post breeding. While any categorical cutoff involves a degree of subjectivity, the chosen threshold reflects a biologically meaningful distinction between individuals with little or no development of nuptial colour and those that clearly show development of colour to some extent. Individuals that were gravid and thus identifiable as female were removed from this analysis.

Visual Thresholds for Sexual Maturity in Females

The presence of ovarian masses within the body cavity is a positive indication of the female sex (Figure 3). This is detectable via candle‐lighting, whereby growing eggs within the ovaries block light transmitting through the body cavity (Reyer and Bättig 2004); adult males do not show light blockage when candlelit except for the central region corresponding to the gut (Figure 3). Later stage eggs are much larger and often darker than earlier stages in amphibians (Dumont 1972), meaning they block relatively more light. Thus, gravidity, assessed visually via candle‐lighting, reflects the presence and relative abundance of developing oocytes across mid to late oocyte stages. This process does not detect the presence of ovarian tissue itself, which would not block significant light in the absence of later‐stage eggs.

Images show adult females with different levels of light blockage from the ovaries ranging from (A1) none (non‐gravid; score = 0) to (A2) low gravidity filling the lower quarter of he body cavity (score = 1), to (A3) medium‐low gravidity filling half the body cavity (score = 2), to (A4) medium‐high gravidity filling three‐quarters of he body cavity (score = 3), to (A5) high gravity filling most of the body cavity (score = 4), and (B1) adult male showcasing light blockage only from the gut/internal organs. Schematics of the different levels of gravidity are shown below each photograph.

During a new wave of oogenesis, ovaries with fewer mid to late‐stage eggs initially fill the posterior portion of the body cavity closer to the vent but will gradually fill more of the body cavity up to the chest as eggs grow and become larger, blocking increasingly more light (Gould, Callen, et al. 2023) (Figure 3). Given that the first bout of egg growth likely starts to occur in juvenile females (Olmstead et al. 2009), low level gravidity causing light blockage from the lower portions of the belly may not be a positive indication of sexual maturity, as the first full wave of egg growth has not yet occurred and/or no eggs have yet reached a stage of development to acquire fertilisation competence (‘ovulation ready’) (Sato and Tokmakov 2020). Likewise, levels of gravidity are temporally variable in adult females dependent on when they last bred. Females that have recently oviposited may have no/few late‐stage eggs remaining in the body cavity and thus are visually non‐gravid, but new waves of egg maturation will cause gravidity levels to increase over time as late‐stage eggs are replenished (Gould, Callen, et al. 2023).

Higher levels of gravidity are thus more likely to be a positive indication of female sexual maturity than low levels, but not always given this temporal variability before and after reproduction. Thus, individuals with lower levels of gravidity may be: (1) sexually mature females that have recently oviposited but already started to show new egg development, or (2) immature females that have started to show egg development, while those with no eggs may be (1) immature females, (2) immature males (if nuptial pads are also not present) or (3) sexually mature females that have recently oviposited and currently without evidence of new egg development. Thus, visually identifying sexual maturity in females must take into account the possibility that adult females may possess no developing eggs (i.e., visually non‐gravid).

A visual assessment of female sexual maturity may be based on gravidity at any level, but this runs the risk of falsely recording juvenile females that have just started to show signs of egg growth. An alternative is to only classify females with higher levels of gravidity as adults, but this runs the risk of misclassifying adults that have recently oviposited as juveniles. A more nuanced approach is to determine a size threshold where any individual is classified as an adult female irrespective of their gravidity level and extent of light blockage through the body cavity, so long as there is an absence of nuptial pads. This threshold may be based on the size class where the first highly gravid female is detected or a majority of females have high versus low levels of gravidity. Those individuals above this size threshold are automatically assigned adult female regardless of gravidity (so long as nuptial pads are absent). Individuals below this size threshold are classified based on their gravidity level, with those with high gravidity levels classified as adults, those with low gravidity levels classified as juvenile females and those non‐gravid classified as sexless juveniles. This method allows for some consideration of the fact that at smaller size classes, individuals with few or no late‐stage eggs are more likely to be immature females as opposed to adult females that have recently oviposited. Above this threshold, individuals without late‐stage eggs are more likely to be adult females that have recently oviposited than juveniles.

We inspected all captured individuals without nuptial pads for gravidity via candle‐lighting. To do so, individuals were inverted and a headtorch (Ledlensor H15R; lumens > 1000) pressed against the dorsum to back‐light it (Figure 3). The amount of light that penetrated the body cavity, excluding the central region where the digestive system is located, was used as a visual measure of gravidity, with less light passing through to the ventral surface indicative of there being more enlarged ovaries and thus more eggs at later stages of growth present. For all captured individuals, we measured the level of gravidity on a scale from 0 to 4; being (0) no light blockage, non‐gravid; (1) light blocked by eggs in the lower quarter of the body cavity; (2) light blocked by eggs in the lower half the body cavity; (3) light blocked by eggs in the lower three quarters of the body cavity and (4) light block by eggs across all quarters of the body cavity (Figure 3). We then grouped individuals based on SVL size, set to 1 mm bins, and determined the minimum size class where gravidity was (1) first detected at any level, (2) first detected at a higher level, and (3) high in a majority ( > 50%) of females that were gravid. To do so, we grouped individuals with gravidity levels of 2 and above as ‘high’ in gravidity and those with a gravidity level of 1 as ‘low’ in gravidity. This threshold was selected because it was visibly difficult to distinguish gravity scores of 3 and 4, and reflects a biologically meaningful distinction between little or some egg development from extensive egg development. As fieldwork protocols followed those of Gould et al. (2021), we typically only candlelit individuals without nuptial pads that were above 48 mm in SVL. Thus, a potential limitation of the current study is that the presence of gravidity in smaller individuals may have been missed. However, a small number of individuals below 48 mm that were candlelit by mistake lacked signs of egg maturation at any level, suggesting that most gravidity data was captured. Individuals with nuptial pads were removed from analysis, leaving only individuals that were females or unsexed juveniles.

Additional Measures of Adulthood

Other attributes among R. aurea that can be used to differentiate between the sexes and sexual maturity in males in particular is skin colour (Gould and McHenry 2024). Adult males tend to showcase yellowing of the dorsal surface, white dorsal stripes, throat and belly (Figure 4). In contrast, females showcase no yellowing of the dorsal surface, along with whiter striping, throat and belly regions (Figure 4). However, we have noted this colour difference can be subtle or difficult to detect between the sexes, with yellowing absent in some adult males. Thus, we suggest this attribute should only be used to assist in differentiating between the sexes, but not used for actual validation purposes. While we inspected males for yellow throats, we only determined male sex based on the presence of nuptial pads.

In addition to physical signs of adulthood, amphibians may showcase behaviours that can be used to infer maturity. For both sexes, this includes movement to breeding sites if they are physically separate from sites used for non‐breeding activities, in addition to calling and engagement in amplexus for males and egg‐laying for females. Calling cannot be used as an indication of maturity for female amphibians, as they do not engage in this behaviour, as well as amplexus, as immature females may not be able to avoid clasping males.

In this study, we recorded whether R. aurea individuals were calling or in amplexus (Figure 5) prior to capture, as a positive indication of the male sexual maturity. As R. aurea adults remain at breeding sites for non‐breeding activities, this could not be used as a positive indication of maturity for either sex. Egg laying was not detected at any time throughout the study period.

Comparing Classification Systems

We created a decision tree for assigning adulthood to male and female R. aurea that takes into consideration reproductive behaviour, morphology and size. Given that the expression of reproductive behaviours is a definitive indication of sexual maturity, we choose for such traits to take precedence in defining adulthood, followed by sex‐based size thresholds, and finally the expression of sex characteristics. We subsequently compared our decision‐making approach to classification systems used in past studies. To do so, we applied each classification system, including our own, to our Kooragang Island data set to determine how counts of adult males and females differed. For past criteria that set a specific size threshold to define adulthood, we applied this value directly. However, if past criteria provided protocols for determining such size thresholds, this was applied instead. An exception was the size threshold calculations established by Beranek et al. (2022), which rely on recapture data and cannot be reproduced in their absence.

Results

Fieldwork Data

Survey nights in each study year occurred across the summer breeding period, from September through till the end of April. The number of survey nights with at least one animal capture was 72 in 2021, 66 in 2022 and 40 in 2023. The mean number of individuals captured and sexed per night was 25 (SD = 19) in 2021, 20 (SD = 16) in 2022 and 7 (SD = 5) in 2023.

Males

In total, 1494 out of 3447 individuals (43%) captured during surveys on Kooragang Island exhibited nuptial pads and thus could be confidently classified as male. Individuals with nuptial pads ranged in sizes between 40 and 73 mm (mean = 56.7 mm, SD = 3.9) and had nuptial pads that were either light (N = 318), medium (N = 225) or strong in colour (N = 951). The minimum size class where individuals had nuptial pads of any kind, irrespective of their colour, was 40 mm (one male), while the minimum size class where coloured pads were detected was 43 mm (one male) (Figure 6). The minimum size class where a majority of males had coloured ‘dark’ versus light nuptial pads was 45 mm (2 vs. 1 male). Both light and dark nuptial pads were exhibited by males across nearly all size classes. However, light nuptial pads were relatively less common, comprising 26% of males in each size class on average.

We captured two males that were in amplexus with female partners. Both individuals had dark nuptial pad colouration and were 57.0 and 72.9 mm in size. We also detected 64 males calling prior to capture, with sizes ranging between 52.2 and 61.6 mm (mean = 58.0 mm, SD = 3.79), and with nuptial pad pigmentation ranging from light (N = 4) to dark (N = 60). We also noted that males with dark nuptial pads showed yellowing of the throat and a general yellowing of the dorsal skin, when compared to adult females that had white throats and no yellowing of the dorsum.

Females and Gravidity Levels

In total, 1075 out of 1403 individuals inspected for eggs and without nuptial pads (77%) were gravid to some extent and thus could be automatically classified as female. When considering only gravid females, sizes ranged between 48 and 87 mm (mean = 66.3 mm, SD = 5.08), with gravidity levels low (N = 238) and high (N = 837). The smallest size class where gravidity of any level was detected was 48 mm, with no gravidity at any level detected in smaller individuals (N = 6) (Figure 7). The smallest size class containing individuals with high gravidity levels was 50 mm. However, it was only at 57 mm that a majority of individuals with eggs were high versus low in gravidity levels (7 vs. 5 individuals); this majority was found in all larger size classes as well. Nearly all size classes at and above 57 mm also had individuals that were non‐gravid and without nuptial pads, averaging 10.7% of each size class. We detected two females in amplexus with males, one of which was non gravid and 67.7 mm in size and the other high in gravidity and 73.9 mm in size.

Comparison of Classification Systems

We have provided a decision tree for assigning sex and life stages to individuals post‐metamorphosis (Figure 8). For our adult classification system, we chose to define a probabilistic threshold for adulthood based on the minimum size class where a majority ( > 50%) of individuals showed high versus low expression of sex‐based traits of maturity (i.e., dark nuptial pads or high gravidity), which was 45 mm for males and 57 mm for females. Above the thresholds, individuals were assigned adult male irrespective of nuptial pad expression, and those without nuptial pads assigned adult female irrespective of gravidity level. Individuals with nuptial pads below 45 mm were only assigned adult male if their nuptial pads were dark, while individuals below 57 mm were only assigned adult female if they did not possess nuptial pads and were highly gravid. Additionally, males were automatically assigned adult if they were detected calling or in amplexus, irrespective of their size or nuptial pad colour expression.

Classification tree for differentiating post‐metamorphic life stages and sexes among amphibians using the green and golden bell frog, Randoidea aurea, as an example. Icons indicate life stage (large = adult, small = juvenile), icon colour indicates sex (blue = male, pink = female) and arrow colour indicates attribute presence (green = yes, red = no).

We found that our multifactor classification scheme differed from those applied in past studies on R. aurea (Table 1). Only three in nine studies (33%) used multiple attributes to define adulthood for males (Pyke and White 2001; Klop‐Toker et al. 2016; Valdez et al. 2016), such as nuptial pad presence and size thresholds, and only two in nine studies (22%) differentiated between dark and faded nuptial pads as part of the classification (Goldingay and Newell 2005; Beranek et al. 2022). Most studies (seven in nine; 78%) used size alone to define adulthood in females, but used the absence of nuptial pads in order to do so.

Discussion

We provide detailed visual criteria for defining adulthood in both R. aurea sexes and for differentiating between the sexes using a combination of sex‐specific size thresholds and sex‐specific traits with defined expression levels, thus developing a nuanced process that extends upon past classification systems. Our work is novel because we establish minimum size thresholds of maturity for each sex independently by considering the first size class where a majority of individuals present within a specific level of expression of sex‐specific traits that are hallmarks of maturity, namely dark nuptial pads in males and high gravidity in females. These thresholds enable (1) individuals above a certain size to be classified as adults regardless of their level of expression of such traits, thus accounting for seasonal variability related to hormone cycles in adults, and (2) individuals falling below a certain size to be classified as adults but only if they show a high degree of expression of such traits. This decision‐making pipeline has the capacity to be applied to other amphibians, particularly anurans (frogs and toads), simply by adjusting trait thresholds to species or population specifications.

In order to determine changes in population parameters such as population size, there is a need to reliably differentiate between the sexes and post‐metamorphic life stages. As monitoring typically occurs during the breeding period when amphibians are active and detectable (e.g., Beranek et al. 2022; Gould, Callen, et al. 2024), rapid and non‐invasive methods of classification, such as those that are morphology‐based, are better suited for fieldwork where there are time and logistical constraints. More importantly, they also reduce stress and the potential for inflicting harm, given reduced handling times, use of invasive techniques or exposure to equipment or multiple handlers that has the potential to transfer disease (Mendez et al. 2008; Swierk and Langkilde 2018). For threatened species, this is particularly critical, as it minimises potentially negative impacts on populations in situ if injury, stress or changes to natural behaviours diminish the breeding capacity or opportunities for handled individuals (Swierk and Langkilde 2018). We also believe it is more effective to use sex‐specific traits rather than differences in a single trait to distinguish between the sexes, such as tympanum size (Martof 1956), as the risk of misclassifying sex is immediately reduced if the sexes do not share the same traits used for classification.

It is difficult to detect the presence of nuptial pads that have only just started to form in juvenile males because they are small and colourless, but our findings show it is possible when researchers are sufficiently trained and thus their emergence in juvenile males needs to be accounted for in classification systems. Conversely, the detection of large males without dark nuptial pads clearly shows that the expression level of secondary sex characteristics alone is not an adequate definition of sexual maturity as they may show temporal variability within this life stage (Pyke and White 2001). This supports the efficacy of a minimum size threshold above which levels of expression of sex‐based traits of maturity can be ignored when assigning adulthood to larger individuals. Additionally, there is no need for an upper size threshold for defining individuals as males versus females, as males can be easily distinguished from females based on the presence of nuptial pads, which are present throughout adult life once formed. However, future studies should explore temporal changes in sex character expression with season, size and age, by conducting mark‐recapture studies on identifiable individuals.

It is apparent that females are the most misclassified sex in R. aurea, and there are a number of reasons for this. For example, the minimum female adult size threshold has been set based on the smallest size where the opposite sex displays sexual attributes of maturity rather than female‐specific attributes (Hamer et al. 2008; Hamer and Mahony 2007), which ignores sex‐based differences in maturation by defining adulthood for both sexes based on an extreme male data point. We found that this caused a potential over‐estimate of adult female numbers when applied to real‐world population data, as it sets the minimum size threshold for this sex too low. In other cases, the same size threshold for adulthood has been set for both sexes, which also ignores sex‐specific differences in maturation, and this also led to overestimates of female population size (Klop‐Toker et al. 2016; Valdez et al. 2016). The largest differences in female counts relative to the use of our own criteria were obtained from Pyke and White (2001) and Goldingay and Newell (2005), likely because their minimum size thresholds for female adulthood were much larger and without accounting for gravidity or other traits of maturity below this size. We also found that studies did not consider gravidity to define female adulthood in their methods, and instead used the absence of nuptial pads in combination with a minimum size threshold; an exception was Beranek et al. (2022). The misclassification of females may be problematic for other species if minimum size thresholds have been set without adjustments using sex‐based traits of female maturity.

Problematic classifications among males included individuals being excluded from adult cohorts if they are below a size threshold even if they possess dark nuptial pads (Klop‐Toker et al. 2016; Pyke and White 2001; Valdez et al. 2016), which omits precocious males and variability in maturation within populations. Male maturity has also been based on nuptial pad presence without consideration for nuptial pad colouration (Gould et al. 2021; Klop‐Toker et al. 2016; Valdez et al. 2016), despite the fact that colourless nuptial pads likely begin to form in the juvenile life stage. However, we found these did not result in large underestimates of adult male numbers relative to our criteria given that the minimum size thresholds were similar. Other studies defined adulthood only based on the presence of dark nuptial pads (Goldingay and Newell 2005), which we found led to significant under‐estimates given that it likely omitted adult males that happen to not be in breeding condition. More recent methods have defined adulthood for males based purely on the presence of nuptial pads without a size threshold restriction, which removes the risk of erroneously identifying adults as juveniles if they are small (Gould, Callen, et al. 2024; Gould et al. 2021), but without considering the risk of misidentifying juveniles that begin to form sex‐based traits before maturation is reached. Only one study explicitly states the use of other sex traits to identify adults, such as calling and amplexus (Goldingay and Newell 2005), which should be considered in future studies to provide additional evidence of maturation; on the caveat that such behaviours are short lived and not always expressed by adults.

What is apparent from comparisons of past studies with our own is that there is much less variability in male counts when applying various criteria relative to females, indicating that individuals of this sex are, for the most part, correctly allotted into juvenile and adult cohorts. This is achieved primarily because of the ease of detection of nuptial pads, that is a robust visual identifier in this species, although not others (Standaert 1967). However, a potential issue in some species is that changes in nuptial pad expression with hormone cycles are not described in detail, making it hard to discern if they simply fade or truly vanish (Graham et al. 2016; Olmstead et al. 2009). There is a risk that future studies might ignore faded nuptial pads and thus incorrectly classify the maturity and sex of individuals that are indeed adult males if they rely on past studies that do not provide an adequate description of nuptial pad variability. Studies using nuptial pads to differentiate the sexes and assign maturity should explain how this tissue structure varies temporally to reduce this type of error.

Our classification system is most similar to that followed by Beranek et al. (2022), where adult males are identified by the presence and colouration of nuptial pads, and adult females by being gravid or above a size threshold if females are non‐gravid. This is apparent as there was minimal discrepancy in both male and female counts from our data set when applying our criteria versus theirs. However, Beranek et al. (2022) did not set a size threshold for male adulthood, which could increase the chance of misassigning adults as juveniles if they show nuptial pads that aren't dark. Similarly, Beranek et al. (2022) did not account for gravidity level when classifying female adulthood, which could increase the chance of misassigning juvenile females as adults if they start to show egg maturation.

There may be caveats to the use of visible features of sex to define maturity. For example, the visibility of eggs via candle‐lighting can be made challenging if the lighting used is not sufficiently strong to transmit through the body cavity, especially in larger females that are thicker. This issue can be reduced by using sufficiently powerful headtorches ( > 1000 lumens); however, care must be taken to ensure light is not shined into the eyes of the frog and that the light source does not produce heat that could cause burns. Gravidity can also be masked by or mistaken for gut content (Gillette and Peterson 2001), particularly after a large meal has been eaten. However, this issue can be reduced with training, as gut content, even large meals, does not cause light blockage from the outer regions of the posterior body cavity in the same manner as ovarian tissue and often causes asymmetry in light blockages to one side of the body cavity. The size of ovarian masses relative to the body cavity may also change in appearance dependent on how much an individual has inflated its lungs, which must also be considered (Reyer and Bättig 2004), but can be easily dealt with by illuminating the frog several times and only recording gravidity when inflation levels are relatively low. Other attributes may also be used to distinguish between the sexes, such as colouration (Gould and McHenry 2024), but we believe this should be used as supporting information only as it is not always consistent between individuals, even within populations. Body condition may be used to determine sex and gravidity level (Reyer and Bättig 2004), however, this is not reliable as it can be difficult to tease apart from the effect of resource acquisition and time since last feeding. We did not consider using relative hindlimb length and width as additional traits to determine sex or maturity as we wanted to use sex‐specific traits that could be easily and visually detected in the field, in contrast to traits that require measurement and are shared between sexes. Other techniques, such as ultrasound, may be used to detect the presence of mature eggs (Reyer and Bättig 2004), but this requires expertise and special equipment, along with extended handling times.

Conclusion

The size at which sexual maturity is reached may differ between individuals based on genetics and/or environmental factors within cohorts, between generations, between sexes, across populations and for different species. This means that setting a minimum size threshold for adulthood without consideration of other attributes of sexual maturity may lead to high rates of misclassifications between juveniles and adults. Adulthood should be determined based on a minimum size threshold where a majority of individuals consistently start to express high levels of sex‐based traits of maturity. Our approach takes this into consideration, thus making it sufficiently flexible to be applicable to a wide range of anuran amphibians.
