Structurally-colored adhesives for sensitive, high-resolution, and non-invasive adhesion self-monitoring
Authors: Quanqian Lyu, Miaomiao Li, Lianbin Zhang, Jintao Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymeric adhesives are critical in many applications, from daily life to implantable devices and soft robotics. Monitoring adhesion in a real-time and convenient manner before premature failure is essential yet challenging. Herein, we present structurally-colored adhesives for sensitive, high-resolution, and non-invasive adhesion self-monitoring via distinct color change for detecting subtle deformation and debonding. The structurally-colored adhesives are designed by integrating one-dimensional photonic nanochains into exemplified acrylate-copolymer-based adhesives and demonstrate distinct color-changing capability through a unique tilting mechanism of nanochains under shear. Our structurally-colored adhesives can be customized as pressure-sensitive and structural adhesives, exhibiting ultrafast response (<60 ms), high sensitivity, and high resolution (~120 μm). Moreover, predicting adhesion states and premature failure can be achieved assisted by imaging systems and machine learning algorithms with an average accuracy of up to 97.2%. Our structurally-colored adhesives are expected to offer a practical paradigm for structural health monitoring in the Internet of Things era.Subject terms: Composites, Colloids

Introduction

Recent years have witnessed a rapid development of polymeric adhesives, which are used for temporary or permanent bonding of different solid surfaces1–4. Polymeric adhesives have shown broad and innovative applications, from daily life to implantable devices, wearable electronics, and soft robotics5–10. However, during the service of these adhesives, environmental effects (e.g., high temperature or humidity), loading circumstances, and false bonding operations can deteriorate adhesion performance, ultimately resulting in premature failure11,12. Monitoring the adhesion is essential to the service of polymeric adhesives, especially in preventing adhesion failures and the resulting catastrophic consequences. Some techniques have thus been employed to monitor adhesion by detecting strains or damages within adhesives, e.g., ultrasonic testing13,14, X-ray computed tomography15,16, and electrical or optical monitoring techniques through embedded electrically conducting components or optical fiber sensors17,18. However, the bulky size of the external equipment involved in these strategies makes it almost impossible to conduct on-site measurements in crude environments or probe in-situ mechanical changes. Moreover, it is even more challenging to accurately identify localized mechanical or adhesion changes, i.e., strain, stress, and even their distribution, within the polymeric adhesive, which is a long-standing roadblock for adhesion monitoring.

Recently, some self-monitoring materials that can indicate deformations, damages, or other mechanical events via perceptible optical signals have attracted increasing attention as they provide direct or relevant information on localized structural changes19–23. The perceptible optical signals allow for user-friendly readout and analysis of material properties through the naked eyes or imaging systems. To achieve such optical signals for visualization, mechanosensing moieties, including dye-filled capsules19,20, mechanophores21,22, and mechano-catalysts23, are usually incorporated into composites or polymer matrices. Upon mechanical load or even damage, optical outputs are triggered by changes in fluorescence, luminescence, or color. However, the load (or thus-resulted deformation) can only be indicated when greater than a critical threshold required to activate the mechanosensing moieties, limiting their application to continuous monitoring of undiscernible deformations of adhesives. Notably, besides the strain or damage within polymeric adhesives, interfacial bonding between the substrate and adhesive is also an essential aspect of adhesion. Unfortunately, in the case of interfacial debonding, the polymeric adhesive bulk usually remains unchanged, and the mechanosensing moieties cannot be effectively activated to indicate the adhesion performance. Therefore, these diverse behaviors involved in adhesion make self-monitoring more challenging. In addition, introducing mechanosensing moieties often requires additional, nontrivial chemical synthesis and the alternation of the adhesive matrix. Therefore, developing innovative adhesion self-monitoring strategies with high sensitivity and resolution remains highly desirable.

Nature has provided fascinating examples of directly converting structural deformation into perceptible color signals24–26. For example, chameleons can alter their skin colors to achieve camouflage and deterrence by adjusting the spacing between the guanine nanocrystal arrays within the iridophore cells by stretching or relaxing the skin. These guanine nanocrystal arrays within the skin form tunable photonic crystals that can generate structural color from the interaction between incident light and periodic photonic structures26,27. The unique color-changing mechanism of chameleon skin contributes to one of the most precise appearance controls in living organisms, hence inspiring the design of innovative self-monitoring materials.

Inspired by this, herein, we present structurally-colored adhesives with brilliant structural color for sensitive, high-resolution, and non-invasive adhesion self-monitoring via distinct color change. Such structurally-colored adhesives are obtained by embedding one-dimensional (1D) photonic nanochains of carbon-coated colloidal Fe3O4 (Fe3O4@C) particles into acrylate-copolymer-based adhesives (Fig. 1a). The unique nanochains of Fe3O4@C particles with high refractive index endow the adhesive with brilliant structural color even with low content. Photocured acrylate copolymers of poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether acrylate (PEGA) and N-hydroxyethyl acrylamide (HEAA) are chosen as an example adhesive matrix for their easily customizable adhesion performance to most common substrates. We demonstrate two typical adhesives: structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesives and structural adhesives. The structurally-colored adhesives can respond to almost all deformations (e.g., compression, tension, and shear) that are coupled with adhesion states. Especially we show that the tilting of the unique photonic nanochains under shear load can generate distinct color changes for localized adhesion visualization with high sensitivity (Fig. 1b), featuring ultrafast response (<60 ms) and high resolution (~120 μm). Moreover, with the assistance of imaging systems and machine learning (ML) algorithms, prediction of adhesion states and warning of premature adhesion failure can be achieved, avoiding disaster consequences (Fig. 1c). In particular, our structurally-colored adhesives outperform existing assessing techniques by fairly large margins in simplicity, in-situ, non-invasive, non-contact, and low cost. Our work provides a user-friendly method for adhesion self-monitoring and demonstrates the excellent assistance of artificial intelligence in polymeric adhesives.

Results

Design of structurally-colored adhesives for adhesion self-monitoring via distinct color change

We aimed to achieve adhesion self-monitoring through an effective yet easily perceptible optical signal and developed structurally-colored adhesives that can generate distinct color changes in response to diverse interfacial bonding or mechanical changes of adhesives. Our structurally-colored adhesives were prepared by embedding 1D photonic nanochains of Fe3O4@C colloidal particles in an adhesive matrix of acrylate copolymers. An adhesive precursor, consisting of Fe3O4@C particles dispersed in a liquid monomer mixture of PEGA and HEAA with a photoinitiator, was exposed to a magnetic field (Fig. 2a), which aligned the Fe3O4@C particles into photonic nanochains28,29. After UV light-initiated polymerization, the nanochains were immobilized in the adhesive matrix. The resultant adhesives exhibited brilliant structural colors because of the high refractive index (~1.93) of Fe3O4@C particles and photonic nanochain arrangement (Fig. 2b, c), even with a low loading content of 0.3 wt% in the adhesive matrix30. The structural color can be tuned by adjusting the size of Fe3O4@C particles and by manipulating the magnetic field intensity (Supplementary Fig. 1). The resultant structurally-colored adhesives exhibited comparable mechanical and adhesive properties to those of the adhesive matrix thanks to the low particle content. With a layer thickness of ~1.0 mm, it could tightly bond the two glass substrates, lifting a load of 3.0 kg (Fig. 2b).

Fe3O4@C particles can be directly dispersed in the monomer mixture to form dispersions without any solvent due to the hydrogen bonding (H-bonding) interactions between the monomers and carboxyl groups on the carbon shells30,31 (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). The mechanical and adhesion properties of the acrylate-copolymer-based adhesive matrix can be readily customized from pressure-sensitive adhesive to structural adhesives via the molar fraction of HEAA in the matrix (details in Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Note 1). The copolymers are denoted as Fx, where x represents the molar fraction of HEAA in the mixture of the monomers. For example, F30 was soft with a low modulus and could sufficiently wet and bond to substrates as a pressure-sensitive adhesive. F60 exhibited excellent stretchability. F80 was tough and had a high modulus, which is expected to be utilized as a structural adhesive with strong cohesive strength. These copolymer adhesives can adhere to various substrates with adhesion strengths ranging from 40.0 kPa to 4.0 MPa thanks to the multiple interactions with the substrates, i.e., van der Wal and H-bonding interactions (Fig. 1a). Structurally-colored adhesives with embedded nanochains (denoted as Fx-Fe3O4@C) exhibited mechanochromic capabilities similar to chameleon’s skin and thus can respond to various deformations, including shear, tension, and compression. Such ability allows for visual self-monitoring of the strain of the structurally-colored adhesives under different loads.

Notably, our structurally-colored adhesives exhibited a distinct color change during the shear deformation, which can be used to assess the critical information related to adhesion states, e.g., effective bonding area and displacement of adhesively-bonded joints. In one example, two glass plates were bonded by a structurally-colored adhesive layer (F60-Fe3O4@C). When the top glass plate moved with a tiny displacement of ~2.0 mm toward the observer (with the lower plate remaining in position), there was a significant redshift (492 to 631 nm) in its structural color (Fig. 2d–f). This corresponded to a 200% shear strain in the adhesive layer. Unlike the conventionally reported blueshift of the reflection peak of the responsive structurally-colored composites caused by tensile and compression32–38, the unique and significant redshift in the structural color derives from the tilting of the nanochains in shear strain.

To illustrate the unique mechanism of the significant color redshift induced by the tilting of the nanochains, we correlated the tilting angle of nanochains to the reflection peak wavelength. The nanochains in the polymeric matrix during the shear process of the structurally-colored adhesive were characterized by optical microscopy. For the rectangular sample positioned between two plates, a tilting angle (α) between the Fe3O4@C nanochains and normal direction is generated when the top glass plate moves a displacement of x (with the lower glass plate fixed). Thus, α can be calculated from facile geometrical considerations (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 5). The cross-sectional view shows that the nanochains tilted toward the shear direction, and the tilting angle α increased to 29° as the shear strain increased to 200% (Fig. 2h). Due to the relaxation of polymer chains or inhomogeneous distribution of shear strain, we presume that the tilting of the Fe3O4@C nanochains is merely a fraction of the maximum theoretical value, which can be corrected with a parameter k (0 ≤ k ≤ 1). The tilting angle of the nanochains is measured during the shear process of the adhesive (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Table 1), corresponding to a corrected parameter k of 0.27. Mechanical and adhesion properties of structurally-colored adhesives

Our structurally-colored adhesives are readily customized in broad types of adhesives based on the adhesive matrix. Three green structurally-colored adhesives (i.e., F30-Fe3O4@C, F60-Fe3O4@C, and F80-Fe3O4@C) were prepared using Fe3O4@C of 130 nm (Fig. 3a). The loading content, as low as 0.3 wt% of the Fe3O4@C in the adhesive matrix, maintained its mechanical and adhesive properties (Supplementary Fig. 4, 7). Correspondingly, F30-Fe3O4@C had a softness similar to that of F30 with a compressive strength of only 0.18 MPa under 60% strain (Fig. 3b). The H-bonding interactions between the polymer chains enhanced the strength and stretchability of the composites as the content of HEAA increased to 60%. As the content of HEAA exceeded 60%, the mobility and flexibility of the copolymer chains were suppressed. Therefore, the F60-Fe3O4@C had the maximum elongation (~900%) among the samples with excellent tensile-induced color change properties (Supplementary Fig. 8). The F80-Fe3O4@C exhibited a glassy polymer-like mechanical performance with the typical yielding at strain (~50%) followed by forced elastic deformation and further stress hardening (Fig. 3c). The temperature sweep of the F80-Fe3O4@C showed that the loss factor (tanδ) exhibited two broad peaks at −43.5 °C and 74.9 °C corresponding to two Tgs, ascribed to the PEGA-rich phases and HEAA-rich phases, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9). The F80-Fe3O4@C exhibited good stability and stiffness to bear 3000 times its weight at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 10). These results suggest that the F30-Fe3O4@C tends to make intact contact with the substrates under external pressure and serves as pressure-sensitive adhesive, and F80-Fe3O4@C can serve as a structural adhesive due to its high toughness of ~24 MJ m‒3 (Supplementary Table 2).

To further verify their adhesion performances, the frequency sweep rheology of the F30-Fe3O4@C was first performed from 0.1 to 628 rad s−1 at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 11). At the bonding frequency, G′ needs to fall below the Dahlquist criterion (G′ ≤ 3 × 105 Pa) to promote sufficient wetting on the substrate39. The F30-Fe3O4@C showed a shear G′ of 1.2 × 104 Pa at the bonding frequency that conformed with the Dahlquist criterion40. A viscoelastic window was constructed by plotting G′ and G″ at bonding and debonding frequencies on a plot of G′ vs. G″ (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, the adhesion performances of F30-Fe3O4@C were further assessed via lap-shear tests on various substrates (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 12). The pressure-sensitive adhesive of F30-Fe3O4@C could still maintain 70.6% adhesion efficiency after 10 adhesion-peel cycles (Supplementary Fig. 13). The typical lap-shear curves of glass joints bonded by three structurally-colored adhesives (area 10.0 × 10.0 mm2) through in situ UV light curing at room temperature are shown in Fig. 3f. For the joint bonded by the F80-Fe3O4@C, neither cohesive nor interfacial failure occurred even though the glass substrates were broken as the shear force exceeded 200.0 N. In addition, the glass substrates were also bonded with various substrates (including glass, copper, steel, aluminum, wood, PMMA, and PET) via F80-Fe3O4@C adhesives to test the adhesion performance and corresponding adhesion strength. F80-Fe3O4@C demonstrated excellent adhesion effects to hydrophilic substrates with the average lap-shear strength to glass exceeding 4.0 MPa (Fig. 3g). Furthermore, the transparency of the adhesive layers is significantly influenced by their thickness (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15, and Supplementary Note 3). For example, thinner adhesives (e.g., 0.2 mm and 0.5 mm) allow a small amount of light to pass through, and the reflection of the transmitted light by the colorful backing may interfere with the structural color of the adhesives. To ensure that the structural colors of the adhesives remain unaffected by the background environment, and after considering their adhesion properties (Supplementary Figs. 16–18 and Supplementary Note 4), an optimized thickness of 1 mm is used for all subsequent experiments.

Notably, real-time adhesion self-monitoring is the key to preventing premature adhesion failure. Our structurally-colored adhesives demonstrated ultrafast response to mechanical stimuli. We examined the response time (tR), here defined as the time for a significant shift in λ (i.e., 50 nm) upon shear force stimuli, of structurally-colored adhesives by simultaneously imaging the sample and recording the reflection spectra (Fig. 3h, i). After the quick introduction of a shear force for 100 ms, corresponding to ~200% shear strain on the F30-Fe3O4@C, the color immediately shifted from green to yellow and eventually disappeared, with the λ shifting from 515 nm to 601 nm (Fig. 3h, i). Thus, tR can be calculated as ~60 ms as determined for the time-course response of the reflection peak (Fig. 3i). After removing the shear force, the λ of the sample returned to its initial value. This ultrafast response is attributed to the H-bonding interactions between the polymer matrix and nanochains, allowing for immediate tilting of the nanochains upon the shear force.

Structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive

Distinct color change and ultrafast response allow for real-time self-monitoring of adhesion states. As a proof-of-concept, structurally-colored adhesives prepared from F30-Fe3O4@C were exploited as pressure-sensitive adhesives that could visualize adhesion state via color change, thus helping users to operate in a compliant way and monitor interfacial bonding in service. Pressure-sensitive adhesives are usually used for temporary bonding, and their complete wetting on substrates with the help of pressure is a prerequisite for adequate bonding41,42. However, inadequate wetting often results in cracks at the adhesive-substrate interface, which can cause the propagation of cracks in the early stage of interfacial bonding failure43,44. Therefore, visualization of the applied pressure can help operate pressure-sensitive adhesives in a more standardized way (Fig. 4a).

Our structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesives could respond to compressive strain. Similar to chameleons adjusting the spacing between the guanine nanocrystal, the compression reduced the lattice spacing (d) within the nanochains, resulting in a blueshift in color (Supplementary Fig. 19 and Supplementary Note 5). First, we explored the relationship between preload pressure, color, and adhesion strength. As the preload pressure increased from 0.0 to 40.0 kPa, the compressed area of the structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive changed from yellow to blue, corresponding to a reflection peak wavelength shift from 603 to 495 nm (Fig. 4b, c and Supplementary Fig. 20). Meanwhile, the resulting adhesion strength of the structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive increased from 20.6 ± 5.1 to 50.2 ± 9.7 kPa. At a preload of 5.0 kPa, the color of the structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive changed from yellow to green (603 to 586 nm), thus corresponding to an adhesion strength of 24.4 ± 5.2 kPa. As the preload increased to 20.0 kPa, the adhesive’s appearance changed to blue (510 nm), and the adhesion strength increased to 43.0 ± 8.3 kPa (Fig. 4c), which is sufficient to generate reliable adhesion to common substrates. These results demonstrated that our structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesives possessed high sensitivity for assessing local adhesion strength, with ~1 kPa per 3.6 nm shift in reflection spectra. Moreover, our structurally-colored adhesives with embedded photonic nanochains exhibited high-resolution strain mapping capability because each photonic nanochain can essentially serve as a “sensing pixel” to reveal localized strain changes, as demonstrated in a lap-shear test of structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive partially bonded PET sheet. As shown in Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 21, when moving the PET sheet slightly, a clear boundary was observed between the bonded (orange) and unbonded (green) area with a resolution accuracy of up to 120 μm was obtained (Supplementary Fig. 22 and Supplementary Note 6). These sensitive and high-resolution color changes allow accurate self-monitoring of adhesion.

Based on the multi-strain responsive characteristics of 1D nanochains, the structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesives can visualize the wetting and interfacial crack propagation processes that affect the adhesion state. We used a piece of structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive sample (originally yellow-colored, 25.0 mm × 20.0 mm) to bond a PET sheet and a glass plate via a randomized preload process. The preload process and adhesion states were assessed via color changes (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 23, and Supplementary Movie 1). The pressed areas (Area I and Area II) showed a blueshift (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 24), indicating the adequate bonding of the structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive on the substrates. The blue appearance in Area I implies a higher adhesion generated than in Area II during the preload process. On the other hand, improper pressing during the randomized preload process may generate cracks at the edge of the interface, as indicated in Area III, suggesting the inadequate bonding between the structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive and the substrates in the initial stage. When the weight was lifted, the structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive was subjected to a shear force (4.0 N). The bonding area showed a redshift in color, while the debonding area remained unchanged (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 25), corresponding to the 70 s timepoint in Fig. 4e. These results were attributed to the bonding area being subjected to shear force while the debonding area was not. Under the shear force, the crack propagated along the interface, causing an increase in the debonding area, eventually leading to interfacial failure at 96 s (Fig. 4f).

More importantly, early warning of premature interfacial bonding failure can be achieved by monitoring the appearance of the structurally-colored adhesives and combining image analysis. To this end, we recorded the bonded sample mentioned above via an imaging system in real-time, and by extracting RGB values in the images, the bonding and debonding areas were precisely identified. We found that the bonding areas exhibit a higher R-value and lower G-value (RGB value: 235, 143, 23) than those in the debonding areas (RGB value: 197, 196, 27) due to the shear-induced color change (Fig. 4g). As the crack propagated, the bonding area decreased, as evidenced by the reduced area with a higher R-value and lower G-value. For instance, before 83 s, the percentage of bonding area decreased at a relatively constant rate of 0.24% s−1 with crack propagation. After 83 s, the bonding area decreased rapidly, and a separation between the adhesive and the substrate occurred (i.e., the interfacial failure) at 96 s (Fig. 4h). The moment when the constant debonding rate ended (83 s in this example) could be recognized as a critical warning point, before which one can take action to avoid the negative consequences of interfacial bonding failure. In contrast, if good wetting between the structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive and the substrates had been generated at the preloading stage, the adhesion area remained constant under shear force without crack propagation (Supplementary Fig. 26, 27). Therefore, one can readily determine from the appearance of the structurally-colored adhesive whether the applied force is sufficient and proper to achieve reliable adhesion, which is essential for the standard use of pressure-sensitive adhesives. Collectively, we demonstrated that the self-monitoring structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesives could accurately provide visual feedback for the adhesion quality and warning of interfacial bonding failure.

Structurally-colored structural adhesives

Structural adhesives are usually employed in strengthening techniques; however, environmental conditions and high shear stress can cause their deformations or even cohesive failures (i.e., fractures), resulting in debonding of adhesively-bonded joints45,46. For example, we constructed adhesively-bonded joints (10 mm × 25 mm × 1 mm) by structurally-colored adhesive of F80-Fe3O4@C between two glass substrates. At room temperature, the adhesive exhibited a strong bonding capability to lift a load of ~12.0 kg and displayed a brilliant green color (Fig. 5a), and even when the load further increased to break the glass substrates (the corresponding shear stress 4 MPa), the adhesively-bonded joints remained stable. However, at 70 °C, fracture in the adhesive bulk was observed as shear stress increased to 0.8 MPa (Supplementary Fig. 28). Our structurally-colored adhesives allow for self-monitoring of the subtle deformation of the structural adhesive via distinct color change, and thus, premature failures and disastrous consequences can be effectively avoided. To primarily verify such capability, we monitored the appearance of adhesively-bonded glass joints subjected to a load of ~3 kg (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Movie 2). At 50 °C, the shear displacement of the adhesively-bonded joint slowly increased to ~1.5 mm at 60 min. Although such subtle displacement is difficult to distinguish with the naked eye, especially at a long distance, with the help of the structurally-colored adhesives, the imperceptible deformation was “amplified” through distinct color changes and was readily identified even remotely. Compared with conventionally reported mechanochromic photonic composites47–49, our structurally-colored adhesives with shear strain response capability are more sensitive to sensing tiny deformations. For example, for a sample with a size of 10 mm × 25 mm × 1 mm, an elongation of 10 mm corresponds to 100% tensile strain, while a shear displacement of 1 mm can induce 100% shear strain. The ability to sense tiny deformations is critical for early warning of premature adhesion failure.

As discussed above, the reflection peak λ and shear displacement of the structurally-colored adhesive F80-Fe3O4@C have a good linear relationship, making it possible to quantitatively assess the shear displacement of adhesively-bonded joints via color change. We correlated the λ and shear displacement of the structurally-colored F80-Fe3O4@C adhesives (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30). As expected, a linear relationship between the λ and shear displacement (less than 2.4 mm) of the structurally-colored adhesive was obtained with a mechanochromic sensitivity of 63.7 nm per displacement (mm). Notably, when the shear displacement exceeded 2.4 mm, although λ remained unchanged, the reflection intensity gradually decreased (Supplementary Fig. 30), and the structural color disappeared due to the limitation of directional diffraction. The structurally-colored adhesives possess a high sensitivity of ~200 μm for visualizing a displacement by the naked eyes, ensuring the precise identification of the adhesion states via color changes (Supplementary Fig. 29 and Supplementary Note 7). To assess displacement via color change quantitatively, we monitored the reflection spectra of structurally-colored adhesive-bonded glass joints for 60 min through creep tests in a lap-shear model under different temperatures from 25 to 60 °C (Fig. 5d). Accordingly, the shear displacement can be calculated using λ with the fitted equation in Fig. 5c (Fig. 5e, empty dots). At 25 °C, the reflection peak λ remained unchanged after bearing a constant shear stress of 0.12 MPa for 60 min, indicating a negligible displacement. High temperature weakened the cohesion strength of the adhesives. At 60 °C, a significant shift in λ to 654 nm was witnessed, implying a significant displacement for the structurally-colored adhesive-bonded glass joints. As expected, the calculated displacements matched well with the measured ones at different temperatures (Fig. 5e, solid lines). These results demonstrate that our structurally-colored adhesive greatly simplifies the assessment of adhesion states by color change to avoid premature adhesion failure by immediate measures (Supplementary Movie 2). The adhesive-bonded joint can recover its original state after removing the load due to its shape memory property, implying the recyclability of our structurally-colored adhesives (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32, and Supplementary Note 9). In addition to high temperatures, the decrease in adhesion strength caused by humidity environments can also be visualized via color changes (Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34, and Supplementary Note 10). Our structurally-colored adhesives improve the ability to visualize the adhesion state of adhesives in various environmental conditions (e.g., high temperature and high humidity)50.

ML techniques have provided abundant opportunities for predicting adhesion states under harsh conditions, thus avoiding catastrophic consequences51. To prove such a concept, we constructed a computer vision system to record digital images of structurally-colored F80-Fe3O4@C adhesive-bonded joints and then predicted their shear displacement with a classic convolutional neural network (CNN) of LeNet-5 for early warning of premature adhesion failure (Supplementary Fig. 35). The creep behavior of the sample was measured and recorded at four different temperatures between 25 and 60 °C under a shear stress of 0.12 MPa. The influence of the temperature on the creep kinetics was visible in each RGB curve (Supplementary Figs. 36 and 37). At each temperature, the sample was recorded for 1 h at intervals of 0.5 s, corresponding to 7200 training images. These images were divided into two parts: 3600 images for training and the remaining 3600 images for testing. In the supervised training process of the CNN, the images were labeled based on displacement-RGB analysis (Fig. 6a). After 100 training epochs, the resulting predictions nicely resemble the actual values with an average accuracy of 91.4% (Supplementary Fig. 38). Furthermore, an average precision of 97.2% could be reached after 1000 training epochs (Fig. 6b). The monitoring system allows for the prediction of adhesion failure in practical applications. The structurally-colored F80-Fe3O4@C adhesive was used to bear a bag (~3.0 kg) and exposed to a high-temperature environment (~60 °C). With continuous exposure to such an environment, the appearance of the adhesive changed from blue-green (Pristine) to green-yellow and yellow, and the monitoring system successfully gave corresponding predicted shear displacements of 0.8 and 1.6 mm, offering early warning of potential failure (Fig. 6c). Therefore, the final cohesive failure and the resulting potential loss or disaster can be avoided by removing the load and extreme environments in time. Our results indicate the ML-assisted monitoring system can autonomously and precisely predict shear displacements, allowing for a non-invasive, real-time, in-situ, and user-friendly technique for early warning of premature adhesion failure.

Structurally-colored adhesives assisted by ML and image analysis for non-invasive and real-time monitoring of adhesion states and warning of premature adhesion failure.

a The ML-assisted image analysis for early warning of premature adhesion failure. b Prediction of the shear displacement of the joint bonded by structurally-colored adhesive at different service temperatures. c Non-invasive and real-time monitoring of adhesion performance of the structurally-colored structural adhesive and corresponding predicted shear displacements for early warning of premature adhesion failure.

Discussion

In summary, we have proposed structurally-colored adhesives with sensitive, high-resolution, and non-invasive adhesion self-monitoring capability to report whether they were reliable and when they failed. Our structurally-colored adhesives exhibited excellent mechanochromic properties that could respond to diverse deformations or strains where the unique shear-induced color change was carefully studied and shown to be primarily decided by the tilting of the photonic nanochains. We prepared structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesives and structural adhesives, enabling self-monitoring of adhesion states and early warning of premature adhesion failure. In particular, we demonstrated the practical application of structurally-colored adhesives assisted by ML and image analysis to avoid potential loss from premature adhesion failure. The advantages of responsive structurally-colored materials were effectively utilized to amplify imperceptible deformation into distinct color changes, greatly simplifying the assessment of adhesion state. Overall, we present an attractive concept for self-monitoring the adhesion state of polymeric adhesives and how digital technology enables advanced applications of classical materials. Furthermore, we envision that other factors that may affect adhesion, e.g., humidity, radiation, and aging, can also be assessed by our strategy.

Methods

Materials

Poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether acrylate (PEGA, Mn = 480 g mol−1) and N-hydroxyethyl acrylamide (HEAA) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. The 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (HMPP) and ferrocene were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Acetone and hydrogen peroxide were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Preparation of acrylate copolymers

The precursors were prepared by mixing PEGA and HEAA with 100: 0 to 0:100 molar ratios and 0.5% photoinitiator (HMPP) of the total molar amount of monomers. The mixture was stirred for 2 h, then poured into a rectangular or dumbbell-shaped polytetrafluoroethylene mold, followed by exposure to UV light (wavelength 365 nm, 2 W) for 10 min for the polymerization.

Preparation of structurally-colored adhesives

Monodispersed Fe3O4@C particles were synthesized via the solvothermal method52. Taking the F30-Fe3O4@C as an example, 5.25 mmol PEGA, 2.24 mmol HEAA, and 6 μL HMPP were stirred for 2 h to form a PEGA-HEAA mixture. 8 mg of Fe3O4@C particles were added to the PEGA-HEAA mixture, followed by 2 h of sonication to obtain a uniform precursor. The precursor was filled into the gaps between two adherent substrates (e.g., glass slides) or poured into a rectangular or dumbbell-shaped polytetrafluoroethylene mold and exposed to a magnetic field, followed by UV light irradiation (wavelength 365 nm, 2 W) for 10 min. Both sides were sequentially exposed to UV light to ensure sufficient polymerization. Similar procedures are adopted for preparing the F60-Fe3O4@C and F80-Fe3O4@C samples, with the PEGA and HEAA ratios changed correspondingly. After curing, the structurally-colored composites can be peeled off from the mold for tests.

Preparation of structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesives

The precursor was prepared as the F30-Fe3O4@C sample. The precursor was then poured into the mold with surfaces covered with PET films and exposed to a magnetic field, followed by UV light irradiation (wavelength 365 nm, 2 W) for 10 min. After curing, the surfaces of the structurally-colored pressure-sensitive adhesive sample were protected by PET films. The PET films were removed before use.

Preparation of joints bonded by structurally-colored structural adhesive

Two glass plates were placed in parallel and separated vertically by a distance of 1 mm with an overlapping area of 2.5 cm when viewed from above. The precursor of F80-Fe3O4@C was integrated into the overlapping area of two glass plates and exposed to a magnetic field, followed by UV light irradiation (wavelength 365 nm, 2 W) for 10 min.

Characterizations

Unless otherwise stated, tensile tests, compression tests, and lap-shear tests were performed using a CARE IBTC-300SH (CARE Measurement and Control, China) at 25 °C, and all of the tests were carried out at a loading rate of 40 mm min−1. Young’s modulus was calculated from the initial slope of the corresponding stress–strain curve. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) images were obtained by a Hitachi SU8010 (Hitachi, Japan) instrument. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained by a JEOL JEM2100 electron microscope (JEOL, Japan). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were obtained on an Equinox 55 spectrophotometer (Bruker, Germany). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) data were obtained by a Diamond DSC (PerkinElmer, America) under flowing N2 with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 from −75 to 150 °C. UV‒vis spectra were obtained by a UV‒vis spectrometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Rheological tests were performed on a TA DHR-2 instrument with parallel plate geometry at 25 °C in the 0.1–628 rad−1 ω range. Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) measurements were performed by a Diamond DMA (PerkinElmer Instruments) at a frequency of 1 Hz and strain amplitude of 0.5% in the temperature range from −100 to 120 °C, and the heating rate was 10 °C min−1. Reflectance spectra were recorded by a high-power tungsten light source (Ocean Insight HL-2000) and a USB4000 fiber optical spectrometer (Ocean Optics). The reflection spectra were calibrated by a clean silicon wafer. The spectra related to compression and tensile strain response were recorded at a viewing angle of 0°. Unless otherwise specified, the spectra related to shear strain response were recorded at a viewing angle of 30°. All photographs were obtained by a Canon PowerShot SX6 camera.

Training process

All the training and test processes were conducted on a local server with an Intel-12900k CPU and an NVIDIA RTX T-1000 GPU. CUDA 11.7 and PyTorch 12.1 are used to establish and optimize the neural network. All images undergo a series of preprocessing steps before training. An image recognition algorithm constructed based on OpenCV was utilized for acquiring images of the structurally-colored structural adhesive region in an hour of monitoring video at intervals of 0.5 s. The acquired images were resized to 285 × 200 × 3 pixels, and their RGB and HSV values were normalized into a range between 0 and 1, respectively. The training set consists of 3600 images, and the validation set consists of 3600 images for each temperature. The shear displacement prediction model was trained using PyTorch 12.1. The network structure is shown in Supplementary Fig. 39. Details, including the image acquisition (https://github.com/Bigyellow722/opencv-video-learn.git), and the construction of the model (https://github.com/Bigyellow722/ML-videos-learn), are available online.
