Preparation and characterization of BSA-loaded liraglutide and platelet fragment nanoparticle delivery system for the treatment of diabetic atherosclerosis
Authors: Mingping He, Ming Fang, Limin Fan, Alimujiang Maimaitijiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic atherosclerosis is one of the main causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, but its therapeutic options are limited. Liraglutide (LIR), a synthetic analog of GLP-1 approved as an anti-obesity drug by the FDA, has been reported as a promising drug for diabetic atherosclerosis. However, the main problem with LIR is its use that requires regular parenteral injections, which necessitates the improvement of drug delivery for increased efficiency and minimization of injection numbers.ResultsThe objective of our present study was to prepare and characterize nanoparticles (BSA@LIR-PMF) for targeted drug delivery using LIR-encapsulated platelet membrane fragments (PMF) coated bovine serum albumin (BSA). We used various methods to characterize the prepared nanoparticles and evaluated their efficiency on diabetes-induced atherosclerosis in vitro and in vivo. The results showed that the nanoparticles were spherical and had good stability and uniform size with intact membrane protein structure. The loading and encapsulation rates (LR and ER) of BSA@LIR-PMF were respectively 8.29% and 90.39%, while the cumulative release rate was around 77.06% after 24 h. Besides, we also examined the impact of BSA@LIR-PMF on the proliferation, migration, phagocytosis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, lactate and ATP levels, and lipid deposition in the aortas. The results indicated that BSA@LIR-PMF could effectively inhibit ox-LDL-stimulated abnormal cell proliferation and migration, reduce the level of ROS and lactate concentration, and enhance the level of ATP, thereby improving oxidative phosphorylation in ox-LDL-treated cells.ConclusionBSA@LIR-PMF significantly inhibited diabetes-induced atherosclerosis. It was anticipated that the BSA@LIR-PMF nanoparticles might be used for treating diabetes-associated cardiovascular complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02775-z.

Introduction

Diabetes, a chronic abnormal metabolism-related disease characterized by hyperglycemia, can set off complications in multiple organs, including the heart and blood vessels [1–5]. Previous studies proved that both types of diabetes mellitus can instigate the pathological processes leading to atherosclerosis or exacerbate atherogenic progression [6, 7]. In addition, platelet activation has also been reported to play a vital role in the instigation of thrombotic events encountered in both atherosclerosis and type 2 diabetes (T2DM) [8–11]. The development of treatment strategies and drugs taking into account platelets could pave the way for new efficacious therapies and preventive options for monitoring obesity and T2DM patients.

The United States Food and Drug Administration (US-FDA) has permitted five drugs for long-term use in the treatment of obesity, among which liraglutide (LIR) has been found promising for treating diabetic atherosclerosis. Findings from previous studies signposted that LIR could reduce atherogenesis in T2DM patients by improving the state of inflammation and intimal function in individuals suffering from impaired glucose tolerance [12]. LIR therapy was also found to hinder endothelial dysfunction, leading to reduced inflammation and vascular remodeling [13]. However, LIR therapy has some challenges, such as the inconvenience of subcutaneous injection and the high cost, which makes it unaffordable for many patients. Oral formulations are being developed to overcome the injection issue, but their effectiveness and safety are still being investigated. In this context, some researchers developed LIR-loaded PLGA nanoparticles, and their results indicated that the PLGA nanosystem protected LIR from enzyme degradation and enhanced its permeability across the intestinal epithelium, making it a promising carrier for oral GLP-1 analog delivery [14]. However, despite the development of new oral nano-formulations for LIR delivery, it is still important to develop nanoparticles that provide a sustained release of LIR into the target location via subcutaneous injection, which could help reduce the number of doses and improve the therapeutic efficacy Scheme 1.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of Preparation and characterization of BSA-loaded Liraglutide and platelet fragment nanoparticle delivery system for the treatment of diabetic atherosclerosis

In recent years, platelet membranes have been reported to hold unique targeting and precise drug delivery ability characteristics and are therefore regarded as an ideal model for use in the fabrication of nanoparticles for antithrombotic therapy as nano-carriers [15, 16]. Previous studies showed that Platelet Membrane-Coated Nanocarriers encapsulating anti-CD47 antibodies can be used as a targeted drug delivery platform for atherosclerosis therapy [17]. It was also reported that nanoparticles coated with platelet membranes and loaded with Rapamycin could prevent the development of atherosclerotic plaques and stabilize them in mice lacking apolipoprotein E (ApoE(-/-) mice) [18]. It was also indicated that nanoparticles functionalized with platelet membranes are effective in treating atherosclerosis [19]. These studies suggest that the platelet-based nanoparticles containing LIR could have improved therapeutic effects compared to conventional therapies with LIR. However, there has been no such development so far.

BSA is a safe protein that can be incorporated into the preparation to keep nanoparticles from aggregation and help stabilize them[20]. Thus, combining the BSA and platelet membrane fragments (PMFs) to develop nanoparticles with a range of properties could empower various therapeutic applications, including treating or mitigating obesity or atherosclerosis in diabetic patients. An approach tailored in this way can also help reduce the risk of side effects caused by traditional drug delivery approaches, while also improving the therapeutic efficiency. However, there is no study conveying the incorporation of LIR, BSA, and PMF, especially in treating diabetic atherosclerosis.

Therefore, in the present study, to verify whether BSA and PMF-loaded LIR may have an important implication for the treatment of obesity/atherosclerosis in diabetes, we prepared a novel drug delivery platform utilizing BSA@LIR nanoparticles covered with platelet membrane fragments (BSA@LIR-PMF) and applied in the treatments of atherosclerosis in diabetic mice using a targeted drug delivery strategy.

Methods

Main instruments and reagents

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) and MES solution were purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA); sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and DL-Dithiothreitol (DTT) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China); liraglutide was purchased from MCE Company (St. Louis, MO, USA); Liraglutide (LIR) detection kit (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) was purchased from Wuhan Cloud Clone Technology Co., Ltd.(Wuhan, China); cell and tissue lysate were purchased from Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China); CO2 incubator (Thermo Company, St. Louis, MO, USA); inverted microscope (Olympus Company, Japan); confocal laser microscope (Zeiss, Germany); transmission electron microscope (FEI, USA); particle size analyzer (Malvern Company) and UV spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Company).

Preparation of platelet membrane fragments

Platelets were manually concentrated from fresh mouse whole blood and stored at − 80 °C. The following steps were taken to prepare platelet membrane fragments (PMFs). Briefly, the platelets were thawed three times until they reached room temperature. Then, a centrifugation process was carried out for 3 min at 4000 g to separate the platelet membranes. The pellets were thereafter suspended in a PBS buffer containing 2 mM of protease inhibitor. Finally, a sonication process at 40 kHz and a power of 100 W was performed for 5 min, resulting in a suspension of PMFs.

Preparation of nanoparticles

To prepare BSA nanoparticles, 120 mg BSA, 60 mg sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and 4.4 mg dithiothreitol (DTT) were weighed and added to a 5 mL sample bottle. Next, 3 mL of ultrapure water was added and the solution was placed in a 90 °C oil bath at 180 r/min for 2 h. Next, 0.97 g PMF was dissolved in 50 mL ultrapure water, and the pH value was adjusted to 4.25–4.8 with 0.1 mol/L NaOH. Then, 25 μL of activated BSA solution (40 mg/mL) was taken and added into a 24-well plate, followed by adding 975 μL PMF. The mixture was placed at 37 °C with shaking at 800 r/min for 4 h. The synthesized nanoparticles were washed 2 to 3 times with a 100 kd ultrafiltration tube at 4 000 r/min for 10 min and finally stored at 4 °C for later use. For Preparing BSA@LIR nanoparticles, 25 μL of activated BSA solution (40 mg/mL) was added to a 24-well plate, then 950 μL PMF was added, followed by mixing under shaking conditions. Next, 25 μL of LIR solution was added, followed by synthesis at 37 °C under shaking at 800 r/min for 4 h. The nanoparticles that were synthesized underwent washing two to three times using a 100 kd ultrafiltration tube at 4,000 r/min for 10 min. Afterward, the BSA protein concentration was measured utilizing a BCA protein kit, and it was stored at a temperature of 4 °C. For the preparation of BSA@LIR-PMF nanoparticles, 1 mg/mL BSA@LIR solution and 1 mg/mL PMF solution were prepared, and mixed according to the volume ratio of 1:1. Next, the above-mixed solution was passed through 0.22 μm polycarbonate. The membrane was extruded back and forth 20 times, and the sediment was resuspended for later use.

Characterization of the nanoparticles

The micromorphology of BSA, BSA@LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) was used to compare the hydration kinetic diameter and Zeta potential of the nanoparticles before and after drug loading and coating. The LIR detection kit (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) was utilized to detect the encapsulation rate and loading rate of LIR, and the release of LIR in BSA@LIR-PMF.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

To prove that the modified BSA@LIR-PMF has intact membrane proteins, RIPA lysate was used to cleave PMF and BSA@LIR-PMF followed by centrifugation (14 000 r/min, 5 min) to obtain the extracted membrane proteins, and then the protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein concentration assay kit. The sample was mixed with loading buffer and boiled for 5 min. PMF and BSA@LIR-PMF were loaded into a 15% SDS-PAGE gel (35 μg per well) run at 120 V for 1.7 h and subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue for 60 min.

ATr5 culture and treatment

The ATr5 cell lines were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai. These cell lines were cultivated in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) from Gibco, Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA. The medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) from GIBCO Industries, USA. The cells were then incubated in a humidified chamber at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Afterward, the cells were treated with ox-LDL (10 μg/ml) and high glucose condition (30 mmol/L) for 24 h for sample collection and subsequent testing.

Cytotoxicity of nanoparticles

The cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 5000 cells per well with a final volume of 200 μL. They were then cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and saturated humidity. Once the cells were completely attached, 100 nM BSA and BSA@PMF were added in sequence (Subsequent in vitro experiments were conducted using the same concentrations). The cells were then incubated for various periods (4 h, 8 h, 12 h). Following this, 20 μL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, China) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator. The microplate reader was preheated in advance, and the absorbance value was measured at OD450 and saved for further analysis.

Cell phagocytosis

The experiment involved seeding cells in a 6-well plate with a density of 4 × 105 cells per well and a final volume of 2 mL. The cells were cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and saturated humidity until they were completely attached. Then, isothiocyanate was used to label BSA@LIR-PMF with fluorescein (FITC) (Aladdin, China). The labeled nanoparticles were incubated with cells for 4 and 8 h, stained with DAPI (1:1000) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 15 min, and washed with CLSM (ZEISS, Germany) to monitor the cellular uptake of nanoparticles. Finally, the fluorescence intensity was measured using Image J (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD), and the results were expressed as mean FITC fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Cell proliferation

Cells were plated into a 96-well plate at an initial density of 5000 cells per well and a final volume of 200 μl. The cells were cultured for 12 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and saturated air humidity. Afterward, they were exposed to ox-LDL and high glucose condition (30 mmol/L) for 24 h.[21] Then separate sets of cultures were used that included BSA@PMF in a preheated microplate reader and, the OD450 reading was performed.

Cell migration

There were about 4 × 105 cells seeded in each of the 6 wells. 2 mL of the volume was used. The cells were then cultured for 12 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and saturated humidity. Thereafter, the cells underwent the treatment of ox-LDL and high glucose (30 mmol/L) for 24 h. The culture medium was subsequently removed and then BSA@PMF, LIR, BSA@LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF were added to the wells. It remained for 12 h. Then, we carefully removed the chamber with tweezers, and the upper chamber liquid was dried and fixed at room temperature with methanol for 30 min. Afterwards, the fixed liquid in the upper chamber was absorbed and crystal violet solution was used for staining at room temperature for 30 min. Next, after rinsing and soaking in PBS several times, the liquid from the upper chamber was removed, followed by transfer to a glass slide for sealing with neutral resin. Finally, 5 random fields were chosen to count under a microscope.

Energy metabolism analysis

PBS was used as the homogenization medium to break the cells, and then the supernatant was taken by centrifugation. The intracellular lactate level was detected using the lactate test kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China), and the colorimetric measurement wavelength was 530 nm. Similarly, the intracellular ATP level was measured using the ATP content assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China), and the colorimetric measurement wavelength was 636 nm.

Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

The degree of cellular oxidative damage was evaluated by measuring the content of ROS using 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). The cells were seeded in a 48-well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 12 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and saturated humidity. After 24 h of exposure to ox-LDL, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were treated with BSA@PMF, LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF for 12 h. Then, the cells were incubated with 20 μM DCFH-DA solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by green fluorescence imaging using a fluorescence microscope (infinite M200, TECAN, Switzerland). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of dichlorofluorescein (DCF) was analyzed using Image J. Untreated cells were used as controls.

Detection of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR)

The rate of dissolved O2 around cultured cells was measured using the Seahorse Bioscience X96 model from Agilent Technologies, which is an extracellular flux analyzer. The XF96 V3 cell culture microplate developed by Seahorse Bioscience was used for culturing ATr5 cells in DMEM supplemented with 0.5% FBS at a density of 0.8 × 104 cells per well. The oxygen consumption rates (OCR) were measured using the mitochondria stress test kit, which involved injecting various compounds into the microplate. The glycolytic activity was assessed using the same XF-96 analyzer, which provided extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) associated with glycolysis, maximum glycolytic capacity, and non-glycolytic ECAR after injecting D-glucose (2 g/L), oligomycin (1 μM), and 2-Deoxyglucose (100 mM) onto the microplate.

Establishment of diabetic atherosclerosis mouse model

The Animal Care and Use Committee of Huashan Hospital, School of Medicine, Fudan University, Shanghai approved our study, which utilized C57BL/6 mice and APOE-/- weighing 20 ± 2 g and aged 6 weeks. These mice were procured from Charles River Laboratories Animal Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). They were provided with food and water ad libitum in a controlled environment for a week, with a 12 h light–dark cycle at 25 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity.

Male APOE-/- mice aged 6 weeks were purchased and fed a high-fat and high-cholesterol diet for 8–12 weeks. After that, the mice were injected with streptozotocin (STZ) (50 mg/kg, Sigma) solution for five consecutive days for induction of diabetes. After 4 weeks of treatment with BSA@PMF, LIR, BSA@LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF by tail-vain injection at a nanoparticle concentration of 332 µg/ml (6 µg per animal), the mice were sacrificed and their aortas were collected for subsequent testing.

Oil red staining of the aorta

To prepare the Oil Red O working solution, we weighed 0.5 g of Oil Red O powder and added 100 ml of 98% isopropyl alcohol. The mixture was left in a 90 °C water bath for 1 h and filtered to obtain the Oil Red O saturated liquid. The saturated liquid was then mixed with double-distilled water at a ratio of 3:2 and left to stand for 10 min. After 10 min, the mixture was filtered to obtain the Oil Red O working solution. Both the saturated solution and working solution were stored in a light-protected container. To stain the blood vessels, the glass dish was filled with the Oil Red O working solution until the blood vessel was completely submerged. The dish was then placed in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min. The staining solution was discarded, and 75% ethanol was added for differentiation twice, each for 5 min. The ethanol was then washed away with physiological saline or PBS. Finally, the blood vessels were immersed in physiological saline or PBS in the dish and pictures were taken.

Animal imaging

After administering isoflurane anesthesia, the mice were positioned in a dark box for recording. The imaging system used was a small animal multispectral in vivo imaging system. Fluorescent dyes were used to label nanoparticles (BSA@LIR, BSA@LIR-PMF), which were then injected into the mouse body through the tail vein at a volume of 100 μL/mouse. The imaging system captured the fluorescence emitted by the animal at a fixed time, allowing researchers to analyze the distribution of fluorescent drugs.

Histological analysis

All aorta tissue specimens of mice were analyzed by HE staining. Briefly, paraffin Sects. (5 μm) were prepared and, after deparaffinization with xylene for 10 min and rehydration in an ethanol gradient, hematoxylin (1%) was used to stain the sections for 5 min. Next, the slides were washed, followed by differentiation with 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 5 s. Next, the slides were stained with 1% eosin alcohol for 1 min. After staining, the specimens were dehydrated in an alcohol gradient and counterstained in xylene. Finally, the specimens were mounted and observed under a light microscope to analyze histological features.

For Masson staining, after being deparaffinized, the sections were refixed in Bouin’s solution at 56–60 °C for 1 h. Next, the sections were stained using hematoxylin, washed thoroughly, and treated with Masson Ponceau acidic fuchsin solution, aniline blue, and glacial acetic acid aqueous solution. Finally, the sections were treated with alcohol, mounted with a coverslip, and examined under a microscope.

Oil red staining of aorta sections

To prepare the frozen sections for microscopic examination, the sections were placed into a dye vat filled with Oil Red O for 10 to 15 min and then washed with distilled water at a temperature of about 37 °C for 5 to 20 s. They were then stained with light hematoxylin counterstaining solution for 3 to 5 min and washed with water for about 30 to 60 s. The reagent three-aqueous mounting agent was added dropwise to the surface of the glass slide before the surface water was completely dry and the slide was sealed (the aqueous mounting agent was heated to a liquid state in 60 °C warm water before sealing the slide). Finally, a microscopic examination of the prepared sections was performed.

Immunofluorescence assay

The aortic tissue sections were prepared, dewaxed, hydrated, and soaked in 3% H2O2 methanol for 10 min to remove endogenous catalase. After high-pressure antigen retrieval, serum blocking was performed before incubation with Anti-Cleaved Caspase-3 (Abcam, ab32042) at a dilution ratio of 1:100, Anti-alpha smooth muscle Actin (Abcam, ab7817) at a final concentration of 1 µg/ml, and Anti-CD31 (Abcam, ab222783) overnight. After washing with PBS, Biotinylated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (Abcam, ab64256) was added at a dilution ratio of 1:200 and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. DAPI counterstaining was performed for 15 min, and then the slides were mounted. The samples were observed and pictures were taken under a fluorescence microscope (infinite M200, TECAN, Switzerland). ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) was used for quantitative analysis to obtain the cumulative optical density IOD.

Biochemical indicators

Serum was separated from arterial blood, and plasma total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol concentrations were measured using a lipid meter (PTS, USA).

Biosafety assessment

First, we collected the samples and prepared paraffin sections using a series of steps, including collection and fixation, washing, dehydration, transparency, waxing, embedding, slicing, unfolding, fishing, patching, and baking. Next, we dewaxed the sections using two rounds of xylene treatment, followed by a hydration process involving a series of alcohol washes. After hydration, we dyed the sections using hematoxylin and eosin staining, with a brief differentiation step using hydrochloric acid alcohol. Finally, we dehydrated the sections again using a series of alcohol washes and made them transparent using xylene, before sealing the slides.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissue samples using the Trizol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) method, and cDNA was obtained by a reverse transcription kit (Takara Bio Inc, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc, Japan) as follows: an initial denaturation was performed at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30–60 s. Relative expression of mRNA (normalized to actin) was calculated by the 2-ΔΔCq method.

Western blot

Protein was extracted from both cells and tissue samples using RIPA lysis buffer from Beyotime, China. Subsequently, protein quantification was performed using the BCA protein concentration assay kit, also from Beyotime, China. The sample was then loaded together with PageRuler™ pre-stained protein ladder from Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, and underwent SDS gel electrophoresis, transfer, and blocking. The sample was then combined with CHOP (D46F1) Rabbit mAb (1:1000) (#5554, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), eIF2α Antibody (1:1000) (#9722, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), GRP78 (BiP) Antibody (1:1000) (#3183, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), PGC-1a Antibody (1:1000) (ab191838, Abcam, USA) and CPT1 Antibody (1:1000) (ab189182, Abcam, USA) and incubated overnight at room temperature. Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (ab205719, Abcam, UK) or Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (ab205719, Abcam, UK) was incubated for 2 h and developed by a chemiluminescence imaging system from Shanghai Ouxiang Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China. Finally, the gray value of the strips was measured by Image J from the National Institutes of Health in Bethesda, MD.

Statistics

Data was expressed as mean ± SD using GraphPad Prism. The differences among the groups were performed using one-ANOVA followed by Turkeys’ posttest. The significance cutoff was 0.05.

Results

Characterization of the prepared nanoparticles

The BSA, BSA@LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF groups are all spherical nanoparticles with a uniform particle size (Fig. 1A). The individual BSA nanoparticles range in size from 25–35 nm, while after loading drug LIR, their size range is 35–50 nm. Many nanofragments appeared in the nanoparticles BSA@LIR-PMF wrapped with the platelet membrane, and the nanometer size range of BSA@LIR-PMF was 37–50 nm. The size of the nanoparticles wrapping the platelet membrane fragments is slightly reduced compared to individual nanoparticles, which may be due to the back-and-forth extrusion during the preparation process of BSA@LIR-PMF. The shape of the nanoparticles becomes more regular during the back-and-forth extrusion process, and some nanoparticles may be crushed. The hydration kinetic diameter of BSA alone is 28.2 nm, while the drug-loaded BSA@LIR’s particle size increased slightly from 28.2 nm to 32.7 nm. After further wrapping the nanoplatelet film, the particle size value of BSA@LIR-PMF changed to 55 nm, and the particle size distribution broadened (Fig. 1B). This may be due to the presence of platelet membrane fragments, which is consistent with the TEM results. After LIR is combined with BSA nanoparticles, the zeta potential value is significantly reduced, which means the drug LIR was successfully loaded on the surface of BSA nanoparticles. After wrapping the platelet membrane, the zeta potential value of BSA@LIR-PMF further decreased, which may be due to the adsorption of platelet membrane fragments on the surface of BSA@LIR. BSA@LIR-PMF in PBS (pH = 7.4) has good stability in the solution, and the intact membrane protein structure is still retained (Fig. 1C, D). The Encapsulation efficiency and loading efficiency of BSA@LIR-PMF are 90.39% and 8.29%, respectively (Fig. 1E). BSA@LIR-PMF was swollen in PBS (pH = 7.4), and the cumulative release rate in 24 h was about 77.06% (Fig. 1F). The same protein bands were detected in the PMF and BSA@LIR-PMF groups, and the platelet membrane was coated after drug LIR and BSA nanoparticles. Effects of BSA@LIR-PMF nanoparticles on cell proliferation, migration, phagocytosis, and ROS levels

The result of CCK8 analysis showed that BSA and BSA@PMF nanoparticles were non-toxic to cells in terms of cytotoxicity (Fig. 2A). The cell proliferation result analysis indicated that cells in the model group (ox-LDL (10 ug/ml) and high glucose condition (30 mmol/L) for 24 h) showed abnormally increased proliferation compared to the control group (Fig. 2B). However, treatment with BSA@LIR, LIR, BSA@LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF inhibited the abnormal proliferation of cells (Fig. 2B). Among these treatments, BSA@LIR-PMF showed the most significant effect (Fig. 2B). The cell migration result analysis showed that cell migration increased significantly in the model group compared to the control group. (Fig. 2C). However, treatment with LIR, BSA@LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF effectively alleviated the level of ox-LDL-induced cell migration (Fig. 2C). On the other hand, BSA@PMF showed no obvious effect (Fig. 2C). The results indicated that LIR, BSA@LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF can all inhibit the abnormal migration induced by ox-LDL, and BSA@LIR-PMF has the most significant effect (Fig. 2C). According to the cell phagocytosis analysis result, cells started to take up BSA@LIR-PMF nanoparticles after 4 h, and the uptake amount increased with time (Fig. 2D, E). Effects of nanoparticles on lactate and ATP levels, oxidative phosphorylation, and glycolysis in vitro

The lactate level of cells in the model group increased and the ATP level decreased when compared to the Control group (Figs. 3A, B). However, the treatment with BSA@PMF, LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF significantly reduced the lactate level of cells and increased the ATP level (Fig. 3A, B). Amongst these treatments, BSA@LIR-PMF had the most significant effect (Fig. 3A, B). Additionally, cellular energy metabolism levels were measured using the Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. Glycolysis was measured by analysis of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation was measured by live cell real-time oxygen consumption rate (OCR). Compared with the control group, the model group had a decrease in OCR and an increase in ECAR. Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that LIR impedes the development of plaques in atherosclerosis via regulating vascular inflammation [13, 22, 23]. Nevertheless, the application of LIR is limited because it requires regular injections. Nanotechnology introduces innovative approaches towards enhancing drug efficacy in treating different conditions like diabetic atherosclerosis. In the present study, to improve the efficiency of the LIR injection and minimize the unpleasant effects, we developed BSA@LIR-PMF nanoparticles, which were found to be efficient in the treatment of diabetic atherosclerosis. Especially, BSA@LIR-PMF significantly alleviated the abnormal changes in energy metabolism (OCR and ECAR), endoplasmic reticulum-related oxidative stress, and cell migration and proliferation in vitro. BSA@LIR-PMF was significantly deposited in the atherosclerotic lesion sites and markedly alleviated the lipid deposition, fibrosis, and endoplasmic reticulum-related oxidative stress in the aorta of mice. This indicates the lipid-lowering potential of the BSA@LIR-PMF nanoparticles, making them an effective nanomedicine for treating atherosclerotic plaque.

In this study, the prepared nanoparticles were characterized to evaluate their properties and potential for drug delivery. The prepared nanoparticles were BSA, BSA@LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF. The individual BSA nanoparticles were found to have a size range of 25–35 nm, while after loading the drug LIR, the size range increased to 35–50 nm. The BSA@LIR-PMF nanoparticles wrapped with the platelet membrane showed nanofragments and a size range of 37–50 nm. The size of the nanoparticles coated with the PMFs was slightly reduced compared to individual nanoparticles due to the back-and-forth extrusion during the preparation process of BSA@LIR-PMF. The zeta potential value of BSA@LIR-PMF further decreased after coating with PMFs, and the encapsulation rate and loading rate were found to be increased, respectively. The nanoparticles showed good stability in the solution and retained the intact membrane protein structure. The cumulative release rate of BSA@LIR-PMF was about 77.06% in 24 h, and the nanoparticle size remained at about 55 nm within 48 h. Overall, these results suggest that the prepared nanoparticles have desirable properties for drug delivery and can be further studied for their potential applications in medical treatments.

The present study has found that the platelet-coated nanoparticles (BSA@LIR-PMF) have a higher targeting effect than the nanoparticles without coating platelets (BSA@LIR). After 12 h of circulation, BSA@LIR-PMF was observed to accumulate in the lesion site. After 12 h, there was a slight increase in the accumulation of BSA@LIR at this location. Additionally, there was more accumulation observed with BSA@LIR-PMF than with BSA@LIR nanoparticles. The ability of BSA@LIR-PMF to target atherosclerotic plaques was indicated by the accumulation at lesion sites. This marked targeting ability may be mainly attributed to the fact that BSA@LIR-PMF truly mimics the platelet inherent affinity to plaques. Mimicking the biological functions of platelets, BSA@LIR-PMF nanoparticles could modify platelet behavior, thus resuming homeostasis in the lesion site. Additionally, the ability of BSA@LIR-PMF may help in tailored delivery of LIR to plaque sites. Our study also corroboration of previous studies indicating that platelets possess a natural ability to target atherosclerotic lesions and are abundantly present in atheroma burden, particularly in vulnerable plaques [17, 18, 24]. To imitate the inherent attraction of platelets towards plaques, the researchers utilized platelet membranes to produce nanoparticles that mimic the surface characteristics of actual cells [17, 18, 24], as demonstrated in this study. Previous studies have utilized traditional methods for designing platelet-mimetic platforms, which involved decorating the particle surface with various peptides or ligands [25–28]. Adhesive peptides or ligands were found on platelet surfaces, which bind to over-expressed antigens at the disease sites [25–30]. During atherosclerosis, platelets are recruited to migrate toward the ruptured lesion wall; adhesive interactions take place between adhesion molecules and factors such as inflamed endothelial cells, fibrinogen, collagen fibers, or pieces that break off [31]. Platelet-mimetic nanoparticles based on specific peptides or ligands struggle to effectively target atherosclerotic plaques due to complex interactions present in atherosclerosis. On the contrary, platelet membrane-cloaking nanoparticles use the natural adhesion properties of platelets for drug targeting, providing an alternative to traditional methods [32]. This method avoids the need for a single targeting ligand and can better replicate the complex interactions seen in atherosclerosis. In the present study, BSA@LIR-PMF had the surface properties of platelets and was naturally recruited to atherosclerotic lesions, indicating its strong affinity to plaques.

The current research found that administering free LIR intravenously reduced atherosclerosis progression in ApoE-/- mice by decreasing lesions in the entire aorta and at the aortic roots. The anti-atherosclerotic activity was significantly enhanced by targeted delivery of LIR through BSA@LIR-PMF. By decreasing fibrosis, lipid deposition, and abnormal metabolism-induced reticulum endoplasmic-related oxidative stress, BSA@LIR-PMF effectively stabilized the atherosclerotic plaques and significantly attenuated the progression of atherosclerosis. The progression of atherosclerosis and instability of vulnerable lesions can be attenuated by reducing oxidative stress and ROS. This study found that CHOP, GRP78, and eIF2a expression increased in the model group, but BSA@PMF, LIR, BSA@LIR, and BSA@LIR-PMF treatment significantly reduced CHOP, GRP78, and eIF2a gene expression, indicating that these nanoparticles can effectively inhibit endoplasmic reticulum stress and thereby improve diabetic atherosclerosis, among which BSA@LIR-PMF exhibited the most significant effect. CD31 and α-SMA are implicated in endothelial cell function and smooth muscle cell activation, respectively, while PGC-1 and CPT1 are implicated in regulating energy metabolism and fatty acid oxidation. The efficiency of BSA@LIR-PMF in targeting these proteins indicated the potential of BSA@LIR-PMF in reducing plaque formation, stabilizing existing plaques, restoring metabolic balance, and improving overall vascular health, which could help reduce the risk of cardiovascular events. Further research is required to evaluate other potential effects of LIR on plaques due to its multifaceted activities. Importantly, the administration of BSA@LIR-PMF over a long period did not result in any significant adverse effects, indicating its safety for the treatment of chronic diseases[21].

Conclusion

In conclusion, BSA@LIR-PMF has shown promising results for the treatment of atherosclerosis by effectively targeting atherosclerotic plaques, halting its progression, and stabilizing them. BSA@LIR-PMF is a potential nano-drug delivery platform for clinical translation.
