Detection and diphtheria toxin status of Corynebacterium ulcerans in canine and feline clinical specimens in Japan
Authors: Sakiko DOI, Yuko GOTO-KOSHINO, Yasuyuki MOMOI, Yumi MORIYAMA, Yuzo TSUYUKI, Masaaki IWAKI, Miyuki KIMURA, Mitsutoshi SENOH
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCorynebacterium ulcerans (C. ulcerans) is capable of producing diphtheria toxin and causing zoonotic diphtheria, with companion animals recognized as an important infection source. We evaluated the occurrence and diphtheria toxin status of C. ulcerans in canine and feline clinical specimens in Japan. Out of 244 Corynebacterium isolates, 18 were identified as C. ulcerans using the matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) and C. ulcerans-specific PCR, including 1 from a dog and 17 from cats. The nasal cavity was the most common isolation site (11 of 18 C. ulcerans isolates). Real-time PCR results from DNA extracted from bacterial colonies showed high concordance with MALDI-TOF-MS findings. Direct application of real-time PCR to clinical samples without prior culture may be considered in the future to enable more rapid identification of C. ulcerans. The tox gene was detected by PCR in 11 feline-derived isolates, and 9 of these demonstrated diphtheria toxin production in a toxigenicity assay evaluating cytotoxicity in Vero cells. Multilocus sequence typing revealed that 15 of the 18 isolates belonged to the same sequence type (ST337). These findings suggest that toxigenic C. ulcerans is more frequently isolated from diseased cats than from dogs in Japan, and that these strains exhibit low genetic diversity. Detection of the tox gene by real-time PCR would be useful for preventing human diphtheria, although toxin gene presence does not necessarily correlate with actual toxin production.Keywords: clinical specimen, Corynebacterium ulcerans, diphtheria toxin, multilocus sequence typing, real-time PCR

INTRODUCTION

Corynebacterium ulcerans (C. ulcerans) is a facultative anaerobic gram-positive rod-shaped bacterium [19, 26]. C. ulcerans can produce diphtheria toxin (DT) upon acquisition of the tox gene via phage lysogenization [26]. After acquiring the tox gene, C. ulcerans–like C. diphtheriae and C. pseudotuberculosis–can become a causative agent of diphtheria, which is a notifiable disease in multiple countries, including the United States [7], and the United Kingdom [23]. Meanwhile, only diphtheria caused by C. diphtheriae is defined as category 2 infectious disease in Japan [22].

The clinical symptoms of diphtheria caused by C. ulcerans are indistinguishable from those caused by C. diphtheriae, including fever, sore throat, cough, and pseudomembrane formation, and can be fatal in severe cases. Non-respiratory manifestations such as swollen cervical lymph nodes and skin lesions have also been reported [9, 27]. While the number of reported C. ulcerans-infected cases has remained stable in Belgium [14], an increase has been observed in Japan and the United Kingdom since 2000 [13, 25, 27]. The rising risk of diphtheria caused by C. ulcerans, rather than C. diphtheriae, in some countries may be associated with differences in vaccination programs [24].

C. ulcerans was originally known as a causative agent of bovine mastitis [9] and was primarily transmitted to humans through raw milk from infected cows with mastitis or arthritis. However, various other animal species have since been identified as hosts, including goats, pigs, dogs, cats, and several wild animals [3, 9]. In recent years, companion animals such as dogs and cats have been suggested to be the primary source of human C. ulcerans infections, surpassing the risk associated with raw milk exposure or international travel. Recent reports show that most patients infected with C. ulcerans–between 83% to 100%–had contact with animals, mainly companion dogs and cats [5, 6, 27]. Genetic sequencing analysis revealed that the bacteria isolated from patients and their domestic animals were closely related, indicating zoonotic transmission [13, 15, 18].

Therefore, investigating the prevalence and the toxigenic status of C. ulcerans in dogs and cats—which serve as reservoirs for human infection—is important from a public health perspective. This is particularly relevant for pet owners and veterinarians, who are at increased risk of exposure. However, few large-scale studies have assessed the prevalence and toxigenic potential of C. ulcerans in clinical specimens from companion animals. In this study, we investigated the occurrence and DT production of C. ulcerans in clinical specimens from dogs and cats submitted for bacterial culture testing in Japan.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Clinical samples

Clinical specimens from dogs and cats submitted for bacterial culture testing by veterinary clinics across Japan to the Sanritsu Zelkova Testing Center (Tokyo, Japan) were used in this study. Samples were collected during the following periods: July–August 2023; November 2023–January 2024; and March–May 2024. The total number and the geographical distribution of clinical specimens submitted during these periods was unavailable.

Species identification of cultured bacteria

Specimens were transported under refrigeration in liquid Amies culture medium from each veterinary clinic to the testing center. Upon arrival, the specimens were inoculated onto solid media (Columbia agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood and colistin–nalidixic acid) and cultured at 35°C in a CO2-enriched atmosphere.

Bacterial colonies suspected to belong to the genus Corynebacterium were further identified to the species level using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (MALDI Biotyper; Bruker Daltonics, MA, USA). A single pure cultured colony from each clinical specimen was subjected to MALDI-TOF-MS. A MALDI-TOF MS score of ≥2.000 was considered reliable for species-level identification, while scores between 1.700 and 1.999 were considered reliable only at the genus level. Scores below 1.700 were deemed unreliable. Only isolates confirmed as Corynebacterium spp. by MALDI-TOF MS were included in this study.

Although CLSI M100 and M45 guidelines recommend the broth microdilution method as the reference for Corynebacterium spp., antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the disk diffusion method in this study due to practical limitations in our routine diagnostic workflow. Due to the lack of established disk diffusion breakpoints for Corynebacterium spp. in these guidelines, the interpretive criteria for Staphylococcus spp. were applied to classify the isolates as Susceptible (S), Intermediate (I), or Resistant (R). Bacterial isolates were cultured on Mueller-Hinton agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood (Becton, Dickinson and Co. [BD], Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Sensi-Disk antibiotic disks (BD) were applied, and following incubation at 35–37°C for 16–18 hr, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured. The antibiotics tested included: benzylpenicillin (PCG), gentamicin (GM), minocycline (MINO), erythromycin (EM), cefotaxime (CTX), ceftriaxone (CTRX), cefepime (CFPM), clindamycin (CLDM), levofloxacin (LVFX), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (ST), and imipenem (IPM). Quality control was performed using Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923.

Toxigenicity assay

Diphtheria toxin production was quantified using a Vero cell cytotoxicity assay [16]. Serially diluted bacterial culture supernatants were added to Vero cell cultures in 96-well microplates, with or without the addition of standard diphtheria antitoxin, to confirm that the observed cytotoxicity was due to diphtheria toxin activity. Cytotoxicity was assessed based on the morphological changes in Vero cells after 20 hr of incubation.

DNA extraction and real-time PCR

A portion of pure bacterial colonies was collected using a sterile pipette tip, suspended in 200 μL of sterile water, and used for DNA extraction with the MagDEA Dx SV kit in combination with the MagLEAD 6gC automated extractor (Precision System Science Co., Ltd., Matsudo, Japan). The extracted DNA was then used for real-time PCR targeting the rpoB gene of C. diphtheriae, the rpoB gene of C. ulcerans, the A subunit of the tox gene [28] and the 16S rRNA gene of various bacterial species [2] (Table 1).

Primers and probes used for real-time PCR

Real-time PCR was performed using the Luna Universal Probe qPCR Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 sec and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 sec. The reaction mixture contained Luna Universal Probe qPCR Master Mix, forward and reverse primers (900 nM each), a dual-labeled fluorescent probe (200 nM), template DNA, and nuclease-free water, with a final volume of 20 μL.

Bacterial species identification by rpoB gene sequencing

Six bacterial isolates that could not be identified by the MALDI-TOF MS were subjected to sequence analysis of the rpoB gene for species identification [4]. An 845-bp fragment was amplified using the primers rpoB-Amp-F (5′–AAGCGCAAGATCCAGGAC–3′) and rpoB-Amp-R (5′–TCGAACTCGTCGTCATCC–3′) and then subjected to Sanger sequencing. The obtained sequences were analyzed using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST).

MLST analysis

We also evaluated the genetic diversity of the C. ulcerans isolates using multilocus sequence typing (MLST). The seven target genes analyzed were atpA, dnaK, fusA, rpoB, dnaE, leuA, and odhA. For atpA, dnaK, fusA, and rpoB, amplification was performed using one of the two previously reported primer pairs for each gene [4]. Primers for the remaining three gene loci were designed in this study: dnaE (dnaE-Allele2-F: CATGCTTGTGCCGTGATTAT, dnaE-Allele2-R: TCCTTGGAATACCCATTGGA; expected amplicon size, 631 bp), leuA (leuA-Allele2-F: CCGATTACCCAGACACCAAC, leuA-Allele2-R: CCAGACCTCTTCGCGGAGAT; expected amplicon size, 589 bp), and odhA (odhA-Allele2-F: ATTGCTACCCACGGACTCAC, odhA-Allele2-R: GTCTAGGATGTCTTGCTTGG; expected amplicon size, 623 bp). Amplification was performed using AmpliTaq Gold 360 Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 sec, annealing at the respective temperature for 30 sec, and extension at 72°C for 60 sec, with a final extension step at 72°C for 7 min. The annealing temperature was set at 56°C for dnaE, leuA, and odhA, and at 46°C for atpA, fusA, rpoB, and dnaK, respectively. The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 12.5 μL of AmpliTaq Gold 360 Master Mix, forward and reverse primers (200 nM each), template DNA, and nuclease-free water, for a total volume of 25 μL. Each PCR product was purified and subjected to sequencing analysis. Seven nucleotide sequences from each bacterial isolate were deposited in the Corynebacterium diphtheriae complex database (INSTITUT PASTEUR, https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr) and analyzed for sequence types (STs) (ID numbers 6716–6733). Two isolates were submitted as novel STs.

RESULTS

Species identification of Corynebacterium isolates

A total of 244 isolates (192 from dogs and 52 from cats) were identified as belonging to the genus Corynebacterium by MALDI-TOF MS analysis and were included in this study. In six isolates, species could not be identified by MALDI-TOF MS analysis, so rpoB gene sequencing was performed. Three isolates were identified as C. auriscains, C. amycolatum, and C. urealyticum, respectively. However, the bacterial species could not be identified in the remaining three isolates (Table 2). Among the identified species, C. auriscanis was the most frequently isolated species in dogs, while C. ulcerans was the most frequently isolated species in cats. Neither C. diphtheriae nor C. pseudotuberculosis was isolated from either dogs or cats. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed for all C. ulcerans isolates, and all were found to be susceptible to all 11 antimicrobial agents tested.

Real-time PCR identification

Real-time PCR was performed on these 244 isolates, all of which were amplified using primers and a probe targeting bacterial 16S rRNA. No isolates showed amplification with C. diphtheriae-specific primers and probe, while all 18 C. ulcerans isolates identified by MALDI-TOF-MS showed amplification using C. ulcerans-specific primers and probe. Three unidentified isolates, along with 223 non-ulcerans Corynebacterium isolates, showed no amplification with the C. ulcerans-specific primers and probe.

Anatomical isolation sites

Sampling sites for the Corynebacterium isolates included in this study were identified in 181 of the 244 specimens. Of these, 91 were from the ear (e.g., otorrhea and external auditory canal), 17 from the nose (e.g., nasal discharge and nasal exudate), and 25 from the eye (e.g., ocular discharge and cornea) (Table 3). Of the 18 C. ulcerans isolates, 11 originated from nasal specimens, one from the ear, and six were of unknown origin due to a lack of clinical information (Table 4).

tox gene detection and toxin production assay

The presence of the tox gene was examined by real-time PCR in 18 C. ulcerans isolates. Eleven of the 18 isolates carried the tox gene and therefore considered potentially toxigenic; all of these originated from cats. The tox gene was not detected in six C. ulcerans isolates originating from cats and one isolate from a dog. In addition, none of the other 226 Corynebacterium isolates, excluding C. ulcerans, harbored the tox gene. Subsequently, a toxigenicity assay using Vero cells was performed to assess DT production in the C. ulcerans isolates. Nine isolates produced DT, all of which carried the tox gene. In contrast, no DT production was observed in any of the seven isolates lacking the tox gene. Two tox gene-positive isolates did not produce DT, as determined by the toxigenicity assay (Table 4).

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST)

MLST analysis was conducted on 18 C. ulcerans isolates by sequencing seven housekeeping genes: atpA, dnaE, dnaK, fusA, leuA, odhA, and rpoB. Of the 18 isolates, 15 were classified as ST337. One isolate was classified as ST325, and the remaining two were newly assigned as ST1079 and ST1080, respectively (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Using the clinical specimens from dogs and cats submitted for bacterial culture testing, we evaluated the occurrence of C. ulcerans in Japan. Of the 18 C. ulcerans isolates identified, one originated from a dog (1 out of 192 Corynebacterium spp. isolates; 0.5%), and 17 from cats (17 out of 52 Corynebacterium spp. isolates; 32.7%). Although the collection site was unclear for some isolates, 10 of the 18 C. ulcerans isolates (56%) were derived from the nasal cavity of cats, suggesting that the nasal cavity is a primary colonization site for C. ulcerans, particularly in cats with respiratory disease.

A previous report from Ireland showed low detection rates of C. ulcerans in healthy animals (<0.5%), whereas higher detection rates were reported in animals with respiratory symptoms (0.53% in dogs and 6.25% in cats) [1]. Two previous reports from Japan evaluating C. ulcerans infection in healthy dogs have yielded contradictory results. One study reported that C. ulcerans was isolated from one out of ten hunting dogs, whereas it was not detected in any of the 218 companion dogs [10]. Another study conducted in dogs under institutional custody reported that 7.5% of clinically healthy dogs were infected with C. ulcerans, likely due to in-facility transmission [11]. Therefore, the prevalence of C. ulcerans in healthy dogs remains unknown in Japan. The present study analyzed clinical specimens collected for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Although direct comparison of overall detection rates is not possible because the total number of tested clinical specimens was unavailable in this study, the relatively frequent detection of C. ulcerans in cats with respiratory symptoms and the higher number of detections in cats than in dogs were consistent with previous reports.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that C. ulcerans was highly susceptible to multiple classes of antibiotics: β-lactams (PCG, CTX, CTRX, and CFPM), macrolides (EM), tetracyclines (MINO), fluoroquinolones (LVFX), aminoglycosides (GM), lincosamides (CLDM), sulfonamides (ST), and carbapenems (IPM), although the antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the disk diffusion method rather than the CLSI-recommended broth microdilution method. High susceptibility to multiple antibiotics has also been reported in previous studies, though treatment with these agents did not necessarily result in eradication of the infection [1, 17]. Elimination of C. ulcerans should be considered both for the treatment of infected animals and from a public health viewpoint. However, based on previous reports, treatment strategies that are not solely guided by antimicrobial susceptibility testing may be preferable. According to CDC clinical guidance, erythromycin and penicillin are recommended for the treatment of diphtheria in humans—regardless of antibiotic susceptibility—which may provide useful insights for future therapeutic approaches in dogs and cats [8].

The real-time PCR system successfully detected C. ulcerans, showing complete agreement with the results of MALDI-TOF MS. Given the high consistency between real-time PCR results and those of MALDI-TOF MS, it is considered that the C. ulcerans-specific real-time PCR assay may be a useful tool for detecting C. ulcerans isolates from canine and feline cases in Japan. In this study, DNA extracted from purely cultured colonies was used for real-time PCR, which may have led to a higher detection rate than would be expected from DNA extracted directly from clinical specimens. However, as a clinical diagnostic tool, real-time PCR using DNA extracted directly from clinical samples should be further explored, as it may enable more rapid detection of infection and the tox gene compared to bacterial culture. This approach could offer advantages from a public health perspective. The effectiveness of real-time PCR using clinical specimens warrants further evaluation.

Real-time PCR results revealed that 11 of 18 C. ulcerans isolates (61%) carried the tox gene. Among these, nine isolates were confirmed to produce DT by a toxigenicity assay and were thus considered toxigenic C. ulcerans. However, two tox-positive isolates did not produce DT in the toxigenicity assay. It has been reported that some C. diphtheriae isolates possess the tox gene with nucleotide mutations or deletions and do not express DT, despite being genetically tox-positive [21]. These isolates are referred to as non-toxigenic tox-bearing (NTTB) strains, and it is possible that some C. ulcerans isolates may also fall into this category. Since sequence analysis of the tox gene was not performed in the present study, the discrepancy between the presence of the tox gene and the absence of DT production remained unexplained. Since the mechanism responsible for DT production in tox-positive isolates has not yet been clarified, it is possible that the tox-positive isolates that did not produce DT in vitro (isolates 87 and 110) could produce DT in infected hosts. As DT is considered the main virulence determinant, the ability to predict DT production in C. ulcerans is of significant clinical relevance. Given that current data are insufficient to resolve this issue, it may be appropriate—at least for now—to consider all tox-positive C. ulcerans isolates as “toxigenic” and potentially capable of causing zoonotic diphtheria.

Fifteen of the 18 C. ulcerans isolates in this study were classified as ST337. In contrast, none of the isolates reported in Ireland, and only one isolate in France, belonged to ST337. Most of the C. ulcerans isolates from Japan deposited in the Pasteur database—both human- and cat-derived—were classified into ST337. These findings suggest that regional differences in the distribution of C. ulcerans may exist between Japan and other countries, and that the genetic heterogeneity of strains within Japan is low. Three of the 18 C. ulcerans isolates were classified into sequence types other than ST337, namely ST325, ST1079, or ST1080. ST325 has mainly been reported in Europe, whereas the remaining two (ST1079 and ST1080) were newly assigned in this study. One isolate was derived from a dog (ST1080), and two were from cats (ST325 and ST1079). The ST325 isolate was found to carry the tox gene. Although ST325 C. ulcerans has not been reported in Japan in the available database, a recent study reported that ST325 was the second most frequently isolated sequence type in Japan [12]. In that report, the level of toxin production was not statistically different between ST337 and ST325. The relationship between sequence type and pathogenicity requires further investigation.

Detailed clinical data of the animals, including symptoms, treatment procedures, and outcomes, could not be obtained in this study. Nonetheless, understanding the clinical characteristics of C. ulcerans infection in dogs and cats is essential for preventing zoonotic transmission, both in household settings and in veterinary clinics. As respiratory symptoms are often associated with C. ulcerans infection in companion animals—particularly in cats [1, 20]—increased awareness of this pathogen may contribute to improved diagnosis and treatment, as well as a reduced risk of animal-to-human transmission.
