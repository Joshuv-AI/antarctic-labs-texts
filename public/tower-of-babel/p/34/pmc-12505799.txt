Can isokinetic muscle strength training with hip abduction and adduction improve muscle strength, balance, and gait in patients with functional ankle instability? A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Bin Wang, Fengming Chu, Qiaojun Zhang, Feilong Zhu, Ming Zhang, Xiaohui Lei, Lei Lu, Kai Wan, Siduo Zhang, Peijia Fu, Ben Ma, Hongfei Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have confirmed that patients with functional ankle instability (FAI) have inadequate hip abductor muscle strength. Muscle strength training is a commonly used intervention in people with FAI. The effects investigated by previous studies have shown conflicting results.However, whether hip adduction and abduction isokinetic muscle strength training can improve muscle strength, balance, and gait in patients with FAI remains unclear.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to observe whether muscle strength, balance, Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT) score, plantar pressure, and gait can be improved in patients with FAI via isokinetic muscle strength training of the peri-ankle muscle groups combined with hip abduction and adductor muscle groups.ParticipantsFAI (n=70).InterventionsThe 70 FAI patients were randomly divided into an ankle isokinetic strength training (AIT) group and a hip isokinetic strength training (HIT) group. The AIT group underwent inversion/eversion and dorsiflexion/plantar flexion isokinetic concentric strength training; the HIT group underwent hip abduction and abduction isokinetic strength training based on ankle isokinetic strength training for six weeks.Main outcome measuresBefore and after training, isokinetic concentric force tests around the ankle and hip adduction and abduction, the Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT), and plantar pressure and gait assessments were performed.ResultsAfter 6 weeks of training, significant changes in muscle strength were observed in ankle inversion, eversion, dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, and abduction for both the AIT and HIT groups (Group*Time, P < 0.05). Furthermore, the HIT group exhibited greater increases in muscle strength compared to the AIT group (Group, P < 0.05). Additionally, both groups showed varying degrees of improvement in dynamic balance, CAIT scores, and gait patterns (Time, P < 0.05), with the HIT group demonstrating superior improvement compared to the AIT group (Group, P < 0.05).ConclusionsIsokinetic strength training through the peri-ankle muscles combined with the hip adductor and abductor muscles was more effective than peri-ankle strength training alone in improving muscle strength, balance, plantar pressure, gait, and self-reported scores in FAI patients.Trial registrationThis study is a randomized controlled clinical trial and has been registered in the China Clinical Trial Registry on 07/04/2022 with registration number ChiCTR2200058341.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09141-6.

Introduction

Ankle sprain is a common clinical disease with the highest incidence involving joint and ligament injuries [1]. According to epidemiological surveys, there are 2 to 7 cases of acute ankle sprain per 1,000 people annually, resulting in approximately 2 million injuries per year [2]. Approximately 74% of patients with acute ankle sprains have been reported to experience a range of residual symptoms after the first ankle sprain, such as pain, sensory instability, muscle weakness and swelling around the ankle [3]. The recurrence rate of lateral ankle sprain was the highest among all ankle sprain types [4, 5]. Freeman et al. [6] defined a population with self-reported loss of ankle control and repeated inversion sprains without joint laxity as having functional ankle instability (FAI). The pathogenesis of FAI has not yet been determined, and its clinical symptoms mainly include the following manifestations: insufficient muscle strength [7, 8], postural control disorders [9, 10], repeated ankle sprains, proprioceptive dysfunction, and neuromuscular control disorders [11, 12]. These conditions have a serious negative impact on the patient’s motor level and quality of life [13].

Previous studies have shown that patients with FAI have muscle weakness and neuromuscular control dysfunction in the frontal plane, especially ankle inversion and eversion, and hip abductor weakness seems to have become a common dysfunction in patients with FAI [7, 14, 15]. It has been shown that performing peri-ankle muscle strength training significantly improves function and enhances stability in patients with FAI [16]. The hip, knee, and ankle joints form the kinetic chain of the lower extremity. When one joint function abnormally, the others compensate for the damaged joint. Prolonged compensation may lead to abnormal function in the other joints [17]. Koldenhoven et al. [18] observed a significant increase in gluteus medius activation while standing in patients with ankle instability using surface electromyography, suggesting that the hip joint is under compensatory control while standing, possibly to better maintain stability and compensate for the balance dysfunction caused by injury to the ankle joint. Friel et al. [14] tested the strength of the hip abductor and extensor muscles in patients with FAI using a hand-held dynamometer and found that the hip abductor strength was significantly lower than that of the healthy side. In the joint movements of the lower limbs, the hip joint is of great importance in controlling posture, maintaining lower limb stability, and preserving dynamic balance as well as gait [19]. The deficits in hip strength in patients with FAI, and its importance in balance control, suggest that incorporating hip strengthening into treatment protocols for this population may be potentially beneficial. It has been reported that the phenomenon of foot inversion can be observed in patients with FAI during both walking and jogging, as shown by greater lateral plantar pressure in these patients than in patients in the internal test [20, 21]. Therefore, these plantar pressure distributions may also be an important indicator of dynamic functional stability in FAI patients. At the same time, the intervention program should include a self-reported score to assess effectiveness in avoiding recurrent sprains, rather than focusing only on muscle strength and balance. To the best of our knowledge, no study has analyzed the dynamic balance ability, self-reported score, plantar pressure, or gait status of patients with FAI using isokinetic muscle strength training involving hip abduction and adduction.

The primary objective of this study was whether 6 weeks of isokinetic concentric training of the hip joint in combination with the peri-ankle muscle groups was more effective than isokinetic concentric training of the ankle joint alone in improving lower limb motor function in patients with FAI. We hypothesized that both AIT and HIT groups would be effective in improving muscle strength, dynamic balance, self-reported scores as well as plantar pressure distribution and gait performance in patients with FAI; We also hypothesized that both training modalities in the HIT group would have a more positive impact on improving lower limb motor function in patients with FAI than in the AIT group.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study was a randomized controlled trial in which 70 participants had FAI (32 with ankle inversion and eversion isokinetic training and 33 with hip adduction and abduction isokinetic training; four patients were unable to complete all training plans due to the pandemic and withdrew from the trial, while one patient withdrew due to another ankle sprain; Table 1), one was male or female, and one was older than 18 years. All patients signed informed consent to participate in the study. The inclusion criterion for all FAI patients was the exclusion of mechanical ankle instability based on the diagnostic criteria for chronic ankle instability established by the International Ankle Consortium [22]. The inclusion criteria for the participants were as follows: (1) a significant ankle sprain at least once in the 12 months before inclusion, resulting in swelling, pain and inability to bear weight for at least one day; (2) a recent ankle sprain 3 months before inclusion; (3) an experienced physiotherapist performed an anterior drawer and talar tilt test with negative clinical manifestations; (4) no serious injuries such as fractures or surgeries in the lower limb joints; and (5) a Cumberland ankle instability tool (CAIT) score < 24 [23]. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) bilateral ankle sprain; (2) lower limb fracture or surgery; (3) positive ankle joint tilt table test and anterior drawer test results; and (4) other neuropathy or muscle abnormalities.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Rehabilitation Hospital (ethics approval code: XKYL2022004) and was registered in the China Clinical Trial Registry on 07/04/2022 with registration number ChiCTR2200058341. Patients were recruited from Xuzhou Rehabilitation Hospital, Xuzhou Central Hospital, and different communities in Xuzhou over a 12-month period using instant messaging tools (WeChat, QQ) and paper notifications between April 2022 and April 2023.

Study design

Subjects were randomly assigned to 2 groups of 35 subjects each. Hidden allocation was achieved by means of a computer-generated table of random numbers. The random numbers were placed in sealed envelopes. The researcher opens the envelope and treats the group as assigned. Subjects were randomly assigned to two groups (AIT, receive ankle isokinetic muscle strength training. HIT, add hip isokinetic muscle strength training to AIT), with 35 participants in each group. The concentric/concentric contraction mode was selected according to the A8-2 training manual with angular velocity of 60°/s (see Appendix A). Before and after the 6-week training, all subjects underwent ankle inversion, eversion, dorsiflexion, and plantar flexion tests, as well as hip adduction and abduction isokinetic concentric strength tests, Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT), the CAIT score, plantar pressure distributions, and gait analysis. Prior to the start of the trial, the order of the various measures was randomized and assigned to different subjects. A 30-minute rest was taken at the end of each test index to avoid patient fatigue affecting the test results. All tests and exercise protocols were done at Xuzhou Rehabilitation Hospital Sports Medicine. Patients were assessed before and after training by trained physiotherapists who strictly adhered to the assessment benchmarks. The assessor was not aware of the type of treatment each subject received.

Strength testing

A multi-joint isokinetic muscle strength testing and training system (Guangzhou Yikang Medical Equipment Industrial Co., Ltd., A8-2 type, China) was used. The test methods and procedures were adjusted and set according to the general clinical practice and experience and the stature of the subject based on the equipment instruction manual. All the subjects rested for 5 min before formal testing to avoid fatigue. For the tests of ankle inversion, eversion, dorsiflexion, plantar flexion and hip adduction and abduction, we used angular velocities of 60°/s and 120°/s. Previous studies have shown no difference in isokinetic muscle strength between the dominant and minutes sides; therefore, we did not consider the dominant and nondominant sides of the unstable ankle when testing [24]. For comparisons of different body weights and populations, we normalized the peak torque and selected the relative peak torque (ratio of peak torque to body weight) for statistical analysis to control for the effect of body weight on peak torque [25].

Isokinetic concentric strength tests for ankle inversion, eversion, dorsiflexion, and plantarflexion were performed according to the test sequence set by the patient randomization protocol (Fig. 2). Balance testing

The SEBT is used to assess dynamic balance in patients with FAI, and previous studies have confirmed that the SEBT has better sensitivity and high test-retest reliability [26, 27]. Prior to the formal test, each subject was instructed to learn the correct test method while performing four exercises in each direction to ensure that each subject understood and mastered the test method [28]. For formal testing, subjects stood barefoot with the other leg extending to the limit in eight directions: anterior, anteromedial, medial, posteromedial, posterior, posterolateral, lateral, anterolateral, and then back to the origin. Three trials were performed in each direction, and the longest reach distance was recorded.

To avoid being affected by leg length, we used standardized processing [29]. Length (anterior superior iliac spine to distal medial malleolus) was measured by an experienced physiotherapist.

CAIT scoring

The CAIT questionnaire included 9 questions, divided into left ankle and right ankle, for a total possible score of 30 points; these questions have high reliability and validity in the evaluation of FAI [30]. The lower the evaluation score was, the worse the stability of the tested ankle joint. Therefore, this scale can be used to assess whether the ankle joint is stable or to diagnose the degree of ankle joint functional instability [31].

Plantar pressure distributions and gait analysis testing

The plantar pressure and gait parameters were tested by the GaitScan (The Orthotic Group, Markham, Ontario, Canada). The reliability and validity of the GaitScan have been demonstrated by previous tests [27]. First, the subjects walked through the plantar pressure plate at a naturally comfortable speed, and gait and plantar pressure data were collected. All the data were measured 3 times, and the average of the three test results was used for statistical analysis.

The GaitScan instrument provides the following results: ①plantar pressure indicators; (1) percentage of pressure distributed across the whole foot (the ratio of healthy side (HS) impulse to affected side (AS) impulse); (2) percentage of plantar pressure in the medial heel and lateral for the rearfoot; (3) percentage of plantar pressure in the first, second, third, fourth and fifth metatarsals; and (4) the ratio of the lateral heel (LH) impulse to the medial heel (MH) impulse. ② gait cycle indicators: The start and end times of each phase of the gait cycle were divided into three phases (loading response phase, midstance phase and propulsion phase).

Strength training protocols

Isokinetic muscle strength training was performed using the Multi-Joint Isokinetic Muscle Strength Testing and Training System. The training positions for the AIT and HIT groups were the same as those selected in the test. The AIT group performed isokinetic muscle strength training of the ankle inversion, eversion, dorsiflexion, and plantarflexion muscle groups using concentric/concentric contraction patterns and angular velocities of 60°/s according to the A8-2 Training Manual. The HIT group performed isokinetic muscle strength training of the ankle internal rotation, external rotation, dorsiflexion and plantar flexion muscle groups, isokinetic muscle training of the hip adductors and abductors was performed using concentric/concentric contraction mode and an angular velocity of 60°/s. In isokinetic muscle strength training, each exercise involved 10 repetitions per set, performed across 5 sets. The training protocol was conducted 3 days per week for 6 weeks (Table 2). Data analysis

The sample size was estimated with G*Power 3.1.9.2 software using hip muscle strength and balance testing data based on previous studies [14, 32]. Consequently, with a power of 80% (β = 0.2), alpha = 0.05 and an estimated effect size = 0.7, we estimated that 26 participants in each group (52 total) were needed to detect statistically significant differences. Considering attrition and loss to follow-up, we recruited a total of 70 patients with FAI and eventually completed the trial with 65 patients.

All the statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, Illinois). The level of significance was set at P < 0.05. The chi-square test was used for sex comparisons. Age, height, weight, and BMI were compared by two independent sample t tests. The two-way ANOVA method is used to analyze the main effects of population and time and the interaction of these factors on variables. if the interaction was observed, we will determine whether there is a separate influence of time or group. Otherwise, we determine whether there is a main effect. For continuous variables, the mean difference between groups was calculated along with the 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The effect in the analysis of variance is calculated (η²).

Strength analysis

The results of isokinetic muscle strength tests for ankle and hip joints before and after 6 weeks of training at angular velocities of 60°/s and 120°/s are shown in Table 3. Except for Add RPT (Nm/Kg) at 60°/s and 120°/s, which did not show an interaction effect (P = 0.409) and (P = 0.837) respectively, all other five indicators Inv RPT (Nm/Kg) 60°/s (P = 0.007), Inv RPT (Nm/Kg) 120°/s(P = 0.001), Eve RPT (Nm/Kg) 60°/s(P = 0.001), Eve RPT (Nm/Kg) 120°/s(P < 0.001), Dorsi RPT (Nm/Kg) 60°/s(P = 0.006), Dorsi RPT (Nm/Kg) 120°/s(P < 0.001), Plantar RPT (Nm/Kg) 60°/s(P = 0.002), Plantar RPT (Nm/Kg) 120°/s(P = 0.023), Abd RPT (Nm/Kg) 60°/s(P < 0.001) and Abd RPT (Nm/Kg) 120°/s(P < 0.001) showed a significant group*time interaction effect. Subsequent simple effects analysis was conducted for time and group. The analysis of time effects showed that all five indicators mentioned above improved significantly compared to pre-treatment, and the differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05). The analysis of group effects showed that the HIT group exhibited significant improvement compared to the AIT group in terms of muscle strength in ankle inversion, eversion, plantar flexion, dorsiflexion, and abduction, with differences of 0.06, 0.04, 0.03, 0.06, 0.07, 0.04, 0.05, 0.29, and 0.14 respectively (P < 0.05). In conclusion, the HIT group demonstrated superior improvement in muscle strength compared to the AIT group.

Dynamic balance analysis

The results of the dynamic balance ability test are shown in Table 4. We found significant interaction effects in Anteromedial (ηP = 0.007), Posteromedial (P = 0.034), and Posterolateral (P < 0.001) directions after 6 weeks of training. Although there were no interaction effects in the remaining five directions, both time effects and group effects were significant (P < 0.05). The analysis of time effects showed that both the HIT group and AIT group increased their distances in all eight directions, and the differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05). The analysis of group effects showed that the HIT group exhibited greater increases compared to the AIT group, with differences of 0.07, 0.04, 0.06, 0.07, 0.04, 0.20, 0.05, and 0.05 respectively, and the differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05). In conclusion, the HIT group demonstrated superior improvement in balance function compared to the AIT group.

CAIT score analysis

Based on the results from Table 5, we found that there was no interaction effect for CAIT (P = 0.532) after 6 weeks of training. The analysis of time effects showed that both the HIT group and AIT group demonstrated improvements in CAIT scores, and the differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05). The analysis of group effects revealed that the HIT group had a higher CAIT score compared to the AIT group, with a difference of 1.90, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05).

Plantar pressure distributions and gait analysis

After 6 weeks of treatment, the analysis of time effects showed that both groups demonstrated varying degrees of improvement in plantar pressure, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05), except for the 3rd Metatarsal. The bilateral whole foot pressure ratio and one-foot medial heel pressure ratio tended to approach 1, with increased plantar pressure in the 1 st and 2nd heels and decreased plantar pressure in the lateral heel, 4th, and 5th heels. The analysis of group effects revealed that the HIT group had greater changes compared to the AIT group (0.15, 0.11, 2.62, 2.66, 3.17, 2.67, 3.23, and 2.90), except for the 3rd Metatarsal. After 6 weeks of treatment, the HIT group and AIT group showed significant changes in the admission-to-push-off time ratio (Tables 6 and 7).

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that there is a lack of muscle strength in the frontal plane muscle group of the hip and ankle joints in patients with FAI, as well as delayed neuromuscular activation [14, 33, 34]. The aim of this study was to investigate the improvement of muscle strength at the ankle and hip joints in FAI patients after hip joint combined with ankle joint isokinetic training. We found improvements in muscle strength of all joints, dynamic balance, self-reported scores, and plantar pressure distribution and gait in the AIT and HIT groups compared to the pre-training period, and in the HIT group, which performed more favorably on various measures after hip plyometric training. This finding is consistent with our previous hypothesis.

Muscle strength is an important factor in maintaining normal movement and intact biomechanics of the joint [35]. Studies have shown that patients with FAI have a loss of ankle inversion and eversion muscle strength [36–38]. Therefore, increasing muscle strength around the ankle joint has become a rehabilitation treatment for restoring dynamic balance ability and normal gait function in patients with FAI [39–41]. Sekir et al. [39] used isokinetic concentric force training for ankle inversion and eversion muscle groups in patients with FAI and reported that isokinetic concentric force training for six weeks resulted in ankle muscle strength reaching the muscle strength level of the uninjured ankle joint in patients with FAI. In previous trials, researchers have tested the strength of the ankle valgus and valgus muscle groups at different speeds, defining between 30–120°/s as slow, with the data representing muscle strength, and 120°/s and above as fast, with the data representing explosive muscle strength [42].

In this study, isokinetic concentric training was used to train the muscle tissues around the ankle joint for 6 weeks in the AIT group, and muscle strength of the ankle inversion, eversion, plantar flexion and dorsiflexion muscle was improved in FAI patients at both 60 °/s and 120 °/s angular velocities. This demonstrates that isokinetic training at an angular velocity of 60 °/s not only improves muscle strength but also muscle explosiveness in FAI patients.

The hip, knee, and ankle joints are an overall lower extremity dynamic chain, and distal ankle injuries may lead to decreased proximal hip function [43]. Most ankle sprains are inverted, so the ankle inversion and eversion muscle strength and joint control are impaired. An increase in the number of sprains will cause insufficient muscle strength in the abductor muscle group of hips [14, 32]. In this study, the HIT group performed Isokinetic concentric training on the basis of ankle internal/external rotation and dorsiflexion/plantarflexion in the hip abductor and adductor groups for a period of 6 weeks, and the muscle strength of the hip abductor muscle group of the patients in the HIT group changed significantly, and at the same time, we found that the muscle strength of the peripheral muscles around the ankle joint in the HIT group was more changed compared with that of the AIT group. During hip adduction/abduction isokinetic muscle training, where the entire lower extremity is maintained in extension, significant changes in hip abductor muscle strength occurred when the muscle groups around the hip joint were activated. At the same time the muscle groups around the distal ankle joint may also remain somewhat activated, which may account for the difference in ankle muscle strength in the HIT group compared to the AIT group. Therefore, in the rehabilitation training process for FAI patients, not only should the local muscle groups of the ankle joint be considered, but the hip joint muscle groups should also be included in the rehabilitation treatment process.

Several studies have shown that patients with FAI have defects in dynamic balance ability during daily activities, along with poor postural control, and cannot return to their original balance state or establish a new balance in time when the body center loses balance, resulting in repetitive sprains of the ankle joint [10, 44]. In this study, after muscle strength training for FAI patients in the AIT and HIT groups, the dynamic balance ability of patients in both groups improved, but the balance ability of patients in the HIT group improved. When the body’s center of gravity is disturbed, ankle and hip strategies typically control anterior-posterior sway to maintain stability [45]. However, preserving postural balance requires coordinated movements of the ankle and hip joints in both sagittal and frontal planes to collectively maintain multidirectional whole-body stability [46]. Following ankle injury, delayed or impaired sagittal-plane ankle strategies often lead to compensatory reliance on hip strategies for balance recovery. Strengthening hip muscle strength can enhance this compensatory mechanism by improving hip strategy responsiveness. This adaptive shift not only stabilizes proximal joints but also reduces excessive loading on the injured ankle during lateral perturbations, indirectly promoting ankle rehabilitation [47]. Since the FAI patients in the HIT group received isokinetic muscle strength training of the frontal plane muscle groups of the hip on top of the ankle muscle strength training, this training protocol significantly strengthened the FAI patients’ control of the joint. This dual approach not only enhanced hip stability but also induced greater ankle muscle strength gains compared to the AIT group. These synergistic effects likely explain the HIT group’s superior performance in the SEBT.

It is reasonable for FAI patients to expect an improvement in strength after muscle training; however, a good training program should not only focus on muscle strength and balance but also focus on whether the patient’s ankle joint function is improved. Our results showed that patients in both the AIT and HIT groups improved their self-reported functional scores, and the functional improvement in the HIT group was greater than that in the AIT group. When there is a lack of strength around the ankle during sprain, patients with FAI are not able to control the ankle to return to its normal position, so repeated ankle sprains occur [40]. In the AIT group, peri-ankle muscle strength was increased, ankle instability was reduced, and self-reported functional scores were increased in FAI patients. When a sprain occurs, if the strength of the ankle joint is insufficient to maintain balance, the body uses the hip strategy to maintain stability. As mentioned above, patients with FAI also have deficient peri-hip strength. In the HIT group, after training, the strength of the hip abductor muscle group increased, and hip abduction effectively increased the support area, which is conducive to avoiding ankle sprains.

Several studies have shown that patients with ankle instability exhibit increased lateral pressure distribution and lateral deviation of the center of pressure (COP) trajectory during walking [48–50]. Similar results were found in this study. In patients with FAI, the plantar pressure in the lower limb was unevenly distributed, and the weight-bearing ability of the unaffected individuals was greater than that of the affected individuals. One possible explanation is that eversion sprains cause varying degrees of damage to the lateral collateral ligaments of the ankle joint; therefore, the ankle joint is in a state of dysfunction [51]. Decreased control and perception of the ankle joint require compensation for the eversion muscle force. Over time, the eversion muscle force compensates for the loss of force, and the lateral stress on the foot increases [52]. Through peri-ankle muscle strength training, the plantar pressure ratio of both feet and the pressure ratio of the lateral and medial hindfoot on the affected side increased toward 1 in the AIT group, and the same finding was observed in the HIT group; however, this trend was greater than that in the AIT group. We believe that isokinetic muscle strength training through hip adduction and abductor muscle groups reduces the transverse deviation of FAI patients in the frontal plane, decreasing the difference in plantar pressure in the lateral direction and decreasing the weight-bearing balance of both lower limbs.

Previous studies have shown abnormalities in gait patterns in patients with ankle instability [20, 53]. In this study, we found that through peri-ankle muscle strength training combined with hip abductor muscle group isokinetic muscle strength training, foot pedaling and accelerating forward function were significantly enhanced in FAI patients during walking, the midstance phase duration was shorter, and the propulsion period was more increased in FAI patients than before training. Our training program is effective because strengthening the muscle strength of the hip abductor muscle group reduces the excessive swing of the center of gravity in the frontal plane of FAI patients and increases the transverse stability of FAI patients so that the ability of the foot and ankle to advance forward is improved [54].

Study limitations.

In this study, we did not test the muscle strength, balance, or gait parameters of normal subjects; therefore, we cannot determine whether 6 weeks of hip adduction or abductor muscle strength training would lead FAI patients to reach the normal level. At the same time, we did not include the hip sagittal muscle group of FAI patients in the study plan in the experimental design, so we cannot clearly know whether to add the sagittal muscle group muscle strength training, the improvement effect of muscle strength, balance and gait is better.

Conclusion

Isokinetic muscle training of the periprosthetic muscle groups in combination with isokinetic muscle training of the hip adductors/abductors has greater potential to improve muscle strength, dynamic balance, self-reported scores, plantar pressure distribution, and gait performance of the ankle and hip muscle groups in patients with FAI compared to ankle training alone.
