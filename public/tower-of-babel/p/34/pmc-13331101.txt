Guarding epithelial integrity: origins of adaptive immunity and cancer immunosurveillance over time and space
Authors: Xiao-Song Wang, Christina Curtis, Michael T Lotze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile conventionally viewed as a defense against external pathogens, we posit that the adaptive immune system likely evolved primarily to preserve internal epithelial order. We propose that adaptive immunity, with combinatorial diversity emerging ~0.5 billion years ago during the Cambrian explosion, was based on extant and established complex cellular interactions, derived over the previous 3.5 billion years of life’s history. These coordinate cellular processes originated to police epithelia against aberrant or rogue cells—inherent to complex multicellularity. Primordial surveillance relied on maintaining epithelial integrity and specialized immune sentinels. Early deposition of tissue-resident γδ T and natural killer cells detects abnormalities and emergent stress signals as damage-associated molecular pattern molecules (DAMPs), independently of classical major histocompatibility (MHC) presentation to αβ T cells. Trained immunity in tissue-resident macrophages, stroma, and epithelia reciprocally respond and are tailored by nascent and highly selected tissue-resident memory cells.Keywords: Adaptive, Immunotherapy

Three functions of epithelial cells

Epithelia are dynamic immunological organs that maintain tissue integrity through three coordinate functions. First, they establish a sophisticated physical and chemical barrier, with tight junctions and mucous layers that segregate the host from topical or luminal microbes and toxins.1 Second, they mediate selective transport; for example, intestinal epithelial cells absorb essential nutrients, shedding themselves when effete, as well as associated debris, while in the gut, enabling transcytosis of immunoglobulins.2 Third, they operate as an active immune interface, deploying pattern recognition receptors to sense tissue damage and orchestrate tailored immune responses.3 These functions converge into an “epithelial-immune reciprocal learning system”, whereby epithelial stem cells retain an epigenetic memory of prior inflammation, enabling faster, stronger responses to subsequent alterations coordinately with immune memory.4 Conversely, endothelial cells, in addition to delivering nutrients and immune cells, dispose of detritus and regulate, within the lymphatic and vascular systems, tissue turgor, inflammation, and homeostasis, enabling organ function.

An internal guardian: policing the “self”

The adaptive immune system is conventionally viewed as the body’s defense against external threats such as bacteria and viruses. However, accumulating evidence suggests its most ancient and fundamental role may not be solely reacting to pathogens but rather preserving internal order. From an evolutionary perspective, adaptive immunity can be understood as a mechanism by which multicellular organisms maintain their cellular integrity and homeostasis.5 It functions as a continuous internal surveillance system, identifying and eliminating aberrant cells—including mutated cells and virally infected cells—that threaten the individual organismal stability.6 This reframing posits the adaptive immune system’s ability to fight pathogens as a secondary adaptation, built on a foundation of policing the “self” and “normality”. This includes the ability of immune cells to detect metabolic disorders because of mitochondrial-nuclear mismatch,7 8 disorders of proteostasis from dysfunctions in the proteasome or autophagy, and alterations in ionic flux due to breaks in various junctional or positional programs. Innate immunity, in contrast, evolved more broadly to recognize pathogens by sensing pathogen-associated molecular pattern molecules through pattern-recognition receptors. These likely having in part their origin from Asgard archaea,9 include various toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD1-like receptors, C-type lectin receptors, AIM2-like receptors, RIG-I-like receptors, defensins, and the cyclic guanosine monophosphate-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway. Activation of these systems induces type 1 interferon programs and other antimicrobial responses, providing host defense through evolutionarily hard-wired recognition mechanisms that encode species-level, rather than individual, immune memory,10 detecting stress and infection.

Beyond immune-mediated surveillance, cell competition represents a conserved cell-autonomous mechanism for tissue quality control, whereby cells with reduced fitness are actively eliminated by their neighbors through mechanical extrusion and apoptosis induction, operating independently of the immune system. Natural killer (NK) cells further complement adaptive sentinels by recognizing “missing-self” signals (downregulated major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I) and stress-induced ligands through activating receptors such as NKG2D. Together, γδ T cells, cell competition, and NK cells constitute a comprehensive defense system for initially maintaining tissue integrity.

Forged in the Cambrian: the rise of internal surveillance

The evolutionary pressures that forged this internal surveillance system intensified during the Cambrian explosion, approximately 540 million years ago. This era saw a rapid diversification of complex, long-lived multicellular life. With increased age, size and cellular specialization came a new and critical internal threat: the emergence of “rogue” cells that could cheat the cooperative system, leading to deleterious cells capable of breaking the barrier function, allowing ingress of pathogens, or over time, leading to cancer. The survival of these new, complex organisms depended on suppressing such internal rebellions. The first identifiable adaptive immune systems, which appeared in jawless vertebrates around 540 million years ago, were likely driven by this intense selective pressure.11 The so-called immunologic big bang12 in agnathans introduced novel forms of combinatorial receptor diversity, enabling individualized recognition of aberrant cellular states. From this perspective, the primordial function of adaptive immunity was to constrain the expansion of selfish cellular mutations that threaten organismal fitness, with pathogen defense representing a later evolutionary co-option.13 This suggests that the adaptive immune system’s original mandate was to preserve the cooperative harmony of the organism’s own cells. Indeed, even rudimentary allorecognition systems in simpler organisms, which distinguish self-tissue from non-self, likely served as evolutionary precursors to the sophisticated surveillance mechanisms required to maintain multicellular integrity.13

Remarkably, the variable lymphocyte receptors (VLR)-based adaptive immune system in jawless vertebrates independently converged on the same organizational logic as the recombination activating genes (RAG)-dependent system in jawed vertebrates, providing compelling support for this hypothesis. In lampreys, VLRB-bearing cells function analogously to B cells and secrete multimeric antibodies, while VLRA/VLRC-bearing cells resemble T cells and are restricted to cell-surface expression. Recent work identified a VLRB-GPIbβ-GPIX signaling complex in lamprey VLRB cells that transduces activation signals via an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) within glycoprotein 1b beta (GPIbβ), paralleling the B Cell Receptor (BCR) signaling architecture of jawed vertebrates.14 This convergence implies that the fundamental logic of lymphocyte activation, centered on ITAM-mediated signal transduction via the Src-Syk-PLCγ axis, was established in the common vertebrate ancestor. The VLR system arose through a gene conversion-like mechanism mediated by APOBEC family cytidine deaminases,15 entirely independent of RAG-mediated V(D)J recombination. Both systems converged on separate T-like and B-like lineages, dedicated primary lymphoid organs, and somatic receptor diversification. This suggests that the selective pressure driving adaptive immunity was a fundamental challenge shared by all complex multicellular organisms—the need to police internal cellular integrity—rather than species-specific pathogen landscapes.

The discovery of a thymus-equivalent structure (the “thymoid”) in lamprey gill epithelium, where VLRA and VLRC cells develop, demonstrates that dedicated lymphocyte selection organs arose independently of the RAG-based system.16 The thymoid expresses thymic markers (foxn1, Notch ligands) and supports VLRA gene rearrangement and apoptosis of non-functional clones—features reminiscent of gnathostome thymic selection. This gill-associated origin of the thymic microenvironment suggests that the earliest lymphocyte selection occurred at a mucosal-environmental interface. Notably, γδ T cells develop in the thymus but precede αβ T cells in fetal ontogeny across species, consistent with their status as an evolutionarily ancient surveillance lineage.17 The thymic dependence of γδ T cells is therefore not at odds with their primordial sentinel function but rather reflects the early co-option of pharyngeal epithelial microenvironments for lymphocyte selection.

Cancer immunosurveillance: an ancient mandate

This evolutionary model provides a powerful context for the theory of cancer immunosurveillance, originally articulated by Lewis Thomas and Sir Frank Macfarlane Burnet is now supported by extensive experimental and clinical evidence. Immunodeficient mouse models18 and patients on immunosuppressive therapies exhibit significantly higher cancer rates.19 In parallel, aging is intrinsically linked to a decline in “immune fitness”—the system’s ability to effectively eliminate malignancy. This gradual erosion of immune surveillance directly correlates with an increased risk of cancer20 occurring later in life. Moreover, tumors arising in immunocompetent hosts frequently bear molecular and antigenic signatures of immune pressure, reflecting a process termed immunoediting. During this process, highly immunogenic tumor cells are selectively eliminated, allowing immune-evasive variants to survive and expand.21 Indeed, selection for oncogenic states that reduce antigen presentation, suppress T-cell recruitment, and inhibit endothelial activation are part of what makes an oncogene “oncogenic”. A striking illustration of this principle comes from studies of Non-Obese Diabetic/Severe Combined Immunodeficient (NOD SCID gamma, NSG) retired ex-breeder mice, which lack functional T cells, B cells, and NK cells. In these animals, up to 88% of aging females develop spontaneous tumors, approximately half of which are malignant.18 Critically, NSG mice are also deficient in γδ T cells, meaning they lack all arms of the internal surveillance hierarchy described here—from innate NK cell surveillance through adaptive γδ and αβ T-cell responses. The high spontaneous tumor rate therefore represents the consequence of losing the entire spectrum of internal guardians, supporting the thesis that when all layers of internal surveillance are ablated, malignancy becomes inevitable due to time-dependent random mutations. This highlights the constant “pruning” of nascent tumors performed by a competent immune system, often in the context of inflammation promoting inception and initiation of an epithelial big bang with multiple mutations.22

Redefining “Self”: the hierarchical sensors of internal disorder

Maintaining tissue homeostasis relies on the generation of “danger signals” or damage-associated molecular pattern molecules (DAMPs)23 24—immunogenic molecular cues not released by healthy cells but induced or released in response to cellular stress and tissue injury.25 Genomic errors, such as point mutations and small insertions or deletions, are converted into immune danger signals when DNA repair capacity is exceeded. Base excision repair corrects base damage and mismatch repair repairs small insertions/deletions by generating single-strand break (SSB) intermediates. Persistent SSBs overwhelm the DNA damage sensor poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1), whose hyperactivation initiates the release of the nuclear DAMP, high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1).26 This molecular switch involves PARP1-catalyzed poly(ADP-ribosylation) of HMGB1, which drives HMGB1 out of the nucleus, promoting autophagy, and broadcasting extracellularly a signal of DNA damage response exhaustion. Destabilizing mutations that impair protein folding can cause misfolded polypeptides to accumulate within the cell. This “proteotoxic stress” can also act as an internal danger signal, triggering the integrated stress response via the protein kinase R-like Endoplasmic Reticulum kinase (PERK), which in turn upregulates surface stress ligands, effectively flagging the defective cell for induced senescence or rapid elimination by immune sentinels.27 Oncogenic programs are not only sources of cellular stress or DAMPs but can evolve into active regulators of epithelial-immune communication. Tumor-intrinsic states (eg, metabolic rewiring, tumor cell cytokine/chemokine regulation) can suppress or distort immune sensing, driving immune escape.

The sentinels maintaining internal order

Evolution appears to have equipped the adaptive immune system with specialized sentinels—γδ T cells—which function as “pre-wired” stress-sensing T cells to detect such internal “danger” signals. When epithelial cells undergo stress, they broadcast a universal “distress code” by upregulating cell-surface ligands for cytotoxic receptors. Unlike conventional αβ T cells, which require MHC presentation and limiting cancer development,28 γδ T cells recognize these conserved patterns of cellular stress and deviation from normality29 30 without the need for classical antigen presentation. γδ T cells sense cellular distress through a dual recognition architecture: their T-cell receptors bind membrane proteins perturbed by metabolic phosphoantigens or lipids, while NK-type receptors such as CD226, and NKG2D as well as TLRs, independently recognize DAMPs and stress-induced ligands (eg, MICA/B, UBLP1-6) on compromised cells.31 Unlike αβ T cells, many γδ subsets are generated in developmentally ordered “waves” during fetal/neonatal life with limited ongoing thymic output in adults. The thymic dependence of γδ T cells may reflect an evolutionarily ancient use of the thymus as an organ for calibrating self-reactive sentinel cells. In this case, the thymus appears to serve more as a developmental staging ground where pre-programmed effector fates are imprinted, often before any encounter with stressed epithelium. Differential mitochondrial-nuclear matching, varying from individual to individual and allowing variable biochemical constituents in both identity and concentration may represent a need for variable selection in the thymus and to be differentiated from other more hard-wired innate mechanisms.

Among γδ T-cell subsets, circulating Vδ2+cells decline markedly with age while Vδ1+cells remain numerically stable.32 Vγ9Vδ2+cells sense phosphoantigens via butyrophilin BTN2A1/BTN3A1, both Vδ1+ and Vδ2+subsets participate in NKG2D-mediated recognition of stress ligands such as MICA/B and ULBPs, and Vδ1+cells additionally recognize CD1d-presented lipid antigens.33 Thus, age-related Vδ2+decline removes a major phosphoantigen-driven surveillance arm, while functional changes in residual Vδ1+cells may further compromise lipid-dependent sensing. Which recognition mode dominates in age-related immunosurveillance failure remains an important open question. Notably, the abundance and functional competence of tissue-resident γδ T cells decline with age. Increased γδ T-cell infiltration correlates with improved clinical outcomes across multiple epithelial malignancies.34 Together, these observations underscore γδ T cells as critical sentinels that enforce tissue integrity and constrain malignant evolution. The development of various breast cancer phenotypes, for example, are sculpted by such γδ T cells as well as the subsequent T-cell receptor (TCR) αβ adaptive immune system.6

Testable questions arising from this hypothesis

Given this formulation, what consequences could emerge? Over one’s lifespan, reciprocal interaction between the adaptive immune cells deploying and emerging from the thymus and bone marrow allows differential expression of the individual repertoires over time and space in various epithelial sites. We would predict that the steady attrition of γδ T cells within tissues35 over time would be replaced with tissue resident memory αβ T cells. There are several issues oriented towards cancer prevention, primarily those related to limiting chronic inflammation as well as the detection of occult inflammation in specific epithelia, that are also worthy of attention. Early changes in cell-free DNA that can be identified with the tissue of origin with identifiable methylation patterns, associated with changes in peripheral TCR and BCR repertoires over time and space could be assessed. Early attrition of immune cells found in the periphery along with measures of immunologic reserve, consistent with immune responses to epithelial perturbations, provoked by enhanced splenic delivery36 could be monitored with assessment of diversity of all seven chains of the adaptive immune antigen receptors, the adaptome. The dynamic changes associated with response to viral infection such as COVID-19,37 could also be observed during periods of heightened tissue remodeling, for example, during menstrual cycles in women. Use of machine learning/artificial intelligence to monitor thymic health on computerized chest axial tomograms over time could reveal emergent vulnerabilities in the host and define response to immunotherapy.38

Conclusion: a primordial surveillance program revisited

In conclusion, viewing the adaptive immune system through an evolutionary lens reveals that its limiting of cancer may not be an emergent secondary strategy but manifests its most ancient mandate, policing epithelia. Long after innate immunity excelled in combating external microbes, the adaptive immune system was forged to keep the peace within, ensuring the stability and integrity of multicellular organisms. This perspective underscores that immunity is what “maintains the unique identity of the organism across its life”.13 This provides a powerful conceptual foundation: our work aims to restore deeper understanding of a fundamental, ancient guardian that has been uniquely protecting us from within since the dawn of long-lived organisms.
