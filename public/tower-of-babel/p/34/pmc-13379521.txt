Factors Associated With Reoperation After Minimally Invasive Tubular Transforaminal Lumbar Interbody Fusion: A Cohort Study of 756 Patients
Authors: Aaron Lerch, Anthony Minh Tien Chau, Silvia Garcia Martin, Amir Amiri, Antonio Tsahtsarlis, Jason Papacostas, Jason McMillen, Amelia Jardim, Robert Campbell, Brendan Davis, Mark Midwinter, Damian Amato, Martin Wood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study DesignRetrospective Cohort Study using Nested Matched Case-Control Analysis.ObjectivesHigh-volume data to describe the complication profile and factors associated with reoperation after minimally invasive tubular transforaminal lumbar interbody fusions (TLIFs) in the short-term (<30 days) and long-term (>30 days).MethodsAll tubular TLIFs (2011-2024) were retrospectively reviewed, performed by eight neurosurgeons at our single centre. Two matched controls per reoperation case were manually selected. Each control had undergone the same primary procedure within six months of the index case, at the same levels and, if possible, by the same surgeon. Variables included demographics, comorbidities, frailty indices, private/public status, workplace injury, preoperative antithrombotics, preoperative laboratory results, the symptomatic indications, surgical factors, and clinical outcomes. Univariate logistic regression identified candidate variables for multivariate analysis.ResultsFrom 756 patients, there was a 6.6% reoperation rate (n=50), with mean follow-up of 1.5 (±1.7) years. Short-term reoperation was predominantly for cage migration and multivariate analysis revealed no independent predictors. Long-term reoperation was mainly for cage migration and was associated with private-funding, WorkCover status, osteoporosis/osteopenia, and previous lumbar surgery. Combined timeframe reoperations were associated with WorkCover status, osteoporosis/osteopenia, diabetes, and previous lumbar surgery.Patients who had reoperations fared worse than their matched controls. Additionally, a worse patient outcome was associated with diabetes, a higher Modified Charlson Comorbidity Index, and previous lumbar surgery.ConclusionThis retrospective case-control series reports our complication profile and factors associated with reoperation and poor patient outcomes after tubular TLIFs. Reoperation was associated with osteoporosis/osteopenia, diabetes, private-funding, WorkCover status, and previous lumbar surgery at another spinal level.

Introduction

With increasing lumbar fusion rates, surgical techniques have evolved accordingly to optimise patient outcomes, namely minimally invasive spinal (MIS) approaches.1-3 Tubular retractor systems offer smaller incisions with reduced iatrogenic tissue damage, decreased estimated blood loss, shorter lengths of stay, less postoperative pain, and faster recovery times. 4 One of the most popular lumbar fusion techniques is the transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), which allows for a posterior and safe MIS approach with good long-term outcomes.3,5

Nevertheless, reoperation remains an issue, with 2.7-14.5% of all tubular TLIF patients requiring a return to theatre.6-8 Such reoperations have been associated with higher complication rates, lower patient satisfaction, worse long-term outcomes, and increased cost burden.9,10 This reflects an important part of the surgical complication profile, including nerve root injury or continued compression, infection, haematoma, and implant failure.8,11

Previous studies have examined MIS TLIF reoperation rates, however, these often lack breadth in potential risk factors, have small sample sizes, and limited timeframes.7-9,11 Population studies have attempted to rectify this, however, remain nonspecific for translation into clinical practice with inclusion of multiple fusion or approach techniques, and assess a small amount of clinically-relevant risk factors.6,12-14 The present study aims to fill this gap by considering a large variety of factors and their implication on reoperation status in both the short and long-term after tubular MIS TLIFs.

The primary research question was to determine the incidence and indication profile of reoperation after elective tubular MIS TLIF, and which factors were associated with reoperation. We hypothesised that reoperation may be associated with increased age, metabolic comorbidity, and prior lumbar surgery. Secondary aims were to compare short- (<30 days) and long-term (>30 days) reoperation profiles, as well as to assess factors associated with poor clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Cohort

A retrospective single-cohort study with nested matched case-control analysis was conducted for all tubular MIS TLIF procedures performed by eight neurosurgeons in a single institution from 2011 to February 2024. TLIFs were indicated for elective degenerative conditions per clinician expertise, including intractable lower back pain and radiculopathy with neural compression or spondylolisthesis after failed conservative management. All emergent, tumour, and trauma cases were excluded.

A case of reoperation was defined as any patient requiring any subsequent procedure at the same spinal level. The patient’s first TLIF at our centre was used as the index case. For each case, two controls were manually selected from the full tubular MIS TLIF cohort. Eligible controls had undergone the same primary procedure within six months of the index case, at the same level/s and, if possible, by the same surgeon. When two or more eligible controls were available, selection was prioritised in the order of closest date of surgery. This matching was blinded to outcomes, based purely on the criteria, and conducted before data-extraction commenced. Short-term reoperation was defined as within 30 days and long-term thereafter. Ethics approval was formally obtained from the Mater Ethics Committee and Mater Research Governance Committee (MSSA/MRGO/104172). Patients consented to the procedure, and data were collected retrospectively. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Patient records and operation notes were assessed until April 2024. Baseline parameters, including demographics and comorbidities were recorded on admission. Osteoporosis was defined per WHO criteria with a bone mineral density T-score ≤-2.5 SD below the young adult mean, with a T-score ≤-1 defining osteopenia. 15 Two validated frailty indices were included: the 5-factor modified Frailty Index (mFI-5) and the modified Charlson Comorbidity Index (mCCI).16,17 The mCCI was modified to exclude age, as this was assessed independently, and has been validated as a survival prediction tool. 18 The healthcare sectors of private/public-funding were assessed, alongside WorkCover status (whereby patients receive financial compensation for work-related injuries). Preoperative sensory changes included any numbness or paraesthesia, and motor changes included weakness consistent with the spinal level of the pathology. Operative duration was defined as from the start of general anaesthesia until the patient left theatre. Clinical outcomes were assessed per the MacNab criteria, which is well-validated for use in spinal surgery follow-up, and was utilised in this study as it was consistently available across the study period. 19 This classifies outcomes as excellent, good, fair, or poor based on residual pain, functional limitation, persistent radicular symptoms, and classically by the need for further operative intervention. We adapted this last point of the criteria (Table 1) so that the index reoperation for cases did not automatically class them as ‘poor’, which would have introduced circularity as reoperation itself was the primary study endpoint. MIS TLIF Surgical Technique

This current series only includes patients undergoing MIS TLIF before our unit adopted robot-guided pedicle screws. We have reported our unit’s standardised surgical technique over 11 years ago. 20 The operation is performed under general anaesthesia, with electrodes in the lower limbs for continuous electromyography, and prone positioning on a Jackson table. A reference pin array is inserted into the posterior iliac crest contralateral to the side of the facetectomy, and 3D images acquired with the O-arm intraoperative CT scanner (Medtronic, Minnesota).

Short bilateral Wiltze incisions allow navigated manual insertion of pedicle screws, with entry at the junction between the transverse process and lateral facet joint. A navigated Jamshidi needle connected to continuous stimulation traverses the pedicle. The length and width of the screw is then calculated on the navigation software, and then the Jamshidi needle is replaced with a K-wire.

METRx tubular dilators (Medtronic) are placed using a MAST dilator directly over the facet joint, with a trajectory through the foramen and along the angle of the disc space. Monopolar cautery exposes the facet joint, and a high-speed drill is used to expose the exiting nerve root. A bone dust collector connected to the suction tube collects bone for grafting. The nerve root is directly decompressed removing soft ligamentous tissue with a Kerrison rongeur from its junction with the thecal sac, to out past the foramen laterally.

The disc space is entered via an annulotomy, disc material is removed unilaterally and end plates prepared. Trial cages are placed to ascertain optimal cage height, whilst length is based on navigation. Bone graft (autologous bone dust combined with synthetic ceramic) is packed into the disc space (typically 5mL or more), and the cage filled with graft is then inserted under direct vision with x-ray confirmation.

Expandable cages were expanded until the torque mechanism was completed. Most patients underwent unilateral TLIF, but if bilateral cages were inserted, this process was repeated on the contralateral side.

Finally, navigated pedicle screws with percutaneous towers are then inserted over the K-wires. Connecting rods and set screws are inserted and torqued off. An O-arm spin is performed to ensure good hardware placement. The percutaneous towers are disconnected and the incisions are closed with vicryl and monocryl.

Preoperative and Postoperative Care

All patients had preoperative magnetic resonance imaging. Within a week of the procedure, antithrombotics were ceased and routine blood tests taken. All patients were reviewed postoperatively, with consults at the 6-week mark and typically at 12 months. Upon symptom resolution, patients were discharged from further follow-up with instruction to re-present if symptoms reoccurred.

Data Review

Factors were assessed between cases and controls and across dichotomised MacNab outcomes (classed as excellent/good and fair/poor) with IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). 21 Missing data were not imputed, with the extent of missing variables reported in Supplementary Table 1. A series of univariate binary logistic regressions were completed, with thresholds of 50% and P<0.2 set as the cut-off, as per standard convention for risk-factor candidate selection. These candidates were then assessed with a multivariate backward conditional logistic regression with threshold for significance set at P<0.05. Given the exploratory nature of this retrospective analysis and the correlation between multiple variables, formal correction for multiple comparisons was not applied. Accordingly, statistically-significant results should be interpreted as associations and require external validation. The cumulative incidence of reoperation was derived as 1 – Kaplan–Meier survival estimate, with censoring at last follow-up. As the matched analysis was case-control in design, logistic regression was used for associative testing, however, any time-to-event inferences were limited by variable follow-up and event number. Due to the reduced sample size in the short-term reoperative cohort, some changes to the multivariate models were required. During multivariate modelling for short-term reoperations, MFI-5 was found to be collinear with diabetes and hypertension, as it includes these two factors in its calculation. To reduce redundancy and stabilise the model, we retained MFI-5 (as the broader comorbidity index) and excluded diabetes and hypertension per statistical advice. No such issue was observed modelling the combined timeframe cohort. For MacNab outcomes in the short-term, there was quasi-separation with sensory changes and previous surgery at the same spinal level, both were required to be excluded to produce a satisfactory model.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 756 patients underwent tubular MIS TLIF, of which 50 patients (6.6%) required reoperation, and 10 of whom were within the first 30 days. Figure 1 shows cumulative reoperation, half within 189 days. Each case was matched with two samples, giving a total study size of 150 patients. The mean age was 60.1 years, with 81 females (54%) and 86 private patients (57.3%). Rates and Reason for Reoperation

The most common reason for reoperation was revision of the instrumentation (62% of cases), as per Table 2. Within that category, cage migration (14 patients) was the most common indication, with eleven in the posterior direction, two lateral, and one anterior. Five out of these 14 migrated cages occurred within 30 days. No association was noted with cage type (expandable versus static). Dislodgement of a locking cap or spacer (14%) and screw loosening/misplacement (16%) was seen more after 30 days. One case required an extension of the hardware due to incorrect fusion of the wrong level. The decompression was augmented in 22% of reoperations due to residual neural compromise via canal/recess stenosis. This was followed by washouts (8% for haematoma or infection) and, in 6% of cases, exchange to anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF). One of the three ALIFs was performed within 30 days as the TLIF cage had extruded into the abdomen and required retrieval and anterior fusion. Overall, the multivariate models demonstrated acceptable discrimination with high specificity (90-92.2%) but modest sensitivity (43.6-60%) which limits use for individual-level prediction. These findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than a clinical risk-prediction tool without further validation.

Follow-Up Consequences

As per Supplementary Table 2, most factors were associated with the requirement of reoperation. Being postoperative factors, i.e. not predictive risk factors of reoperation, they were excluded from multivariate analysis. Patients were censored at last documented follow-up. As follow-up was variable and relatively short for some patients, late reoperation risk may be underestimated.

Patient Outcomes

The revised, modified MacNab scores at follow-up in the combined timeframe for patients who had a reoperation were 10% excellent, 28% good, 22% fair, 40% poor and for controls was 47% excellent, 33% good, 20% fair, 0% poor.

Discussion

This high-volume single-centre cohort study reports factors associated with reoperation and poor patient outcomes after tubular MIS TLIF, with reoperations stratified into timeframes. Our overall reoperation rate was 6.6%, with mean follow-up 1.5 years (SD=1.7), within reported literature ranges (Table 7). Importantly, 42 of 50 reoperations were for revision of instrumentation or residual neural compression, corresponding to 5.5% of the total cohort. These events may reflect further procedure-specific technical factors that were not included in this study, including cage positioning, hardware placement, decompression adequacy, endplate preparation, and patient anatomical/bone-quality factors. As the cumulative incidence curve (Figure 1) suggests ongoing reoperation risk beyond the mean follow-up period, the reported long-term rate should be considered a minimum estimate rather than a definitive lifetime reoperation risk.

Nevertheless, the indications for reoperation are consistent with those found in other studies. Posterior cage migration (0.7-6% in the literature) and general instrumentation failure remain inherent issues of the minimally invasive nature of this posterior approach, largely attributable to limited contact between the cage and vertebral body.7-9,32 Such complications can be minimised by avoiding endplate injury, assessing disc shape to allow anterior placement of cage intraoperatively, and carefully considering cage size. 40 Theoretically, expandable cages may prevent migrations, however, this association was not noted in our study. 41 Our infection rates were low at 0.1% (compared to 0.6-4% in the literature), alongside far fewer CSF leaks than the 1.3-8% reported in the literature, with only three intraoperative dural tears noted, all of which were managed conservatively.8,22

Overall factors associated with reoperation were private and WorkCover status, reduced bone density, diabetes, and previous lumbar surgery not at the index level. These aforementioned factors were noted for long-term reoperation, with diabetes additionally noted in the combined timeframes. No independent short-term associations were identified, however, the short-term subgroup contained only 10 events which limits model stability and increases the risk of a type two error or overfitting.

The mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Osteoporosis is known to contribute to hardware malfunction by promoting poor implantation and cage subsidence. Additionally, it is a risk factor for further degenerative spondylolisthesis, and therefore, has already been associated with increased reoperations.42,43 At our centre, patients at risk of osteoporosis undergo dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry screening to help guide decision-making. Previous lumbar surgery at adjacent levels likely reflects a generally worse condition of the spine, which would perpetuate adjacent segment disease, a common indication for reoperation in other papers.6,9,11 The split of patients into private and public sectors is only relevant for some healthcare systems and is rarely reported in the literature. This association likely reflects numerous unmeasured confounding factors, including socioeconomic status, healthcare access, patient expectations, compensation status, and health literacy. 44 Although public patient status was observed to be associated with reduced rates of reoperation, this finding should therefore be viewed as a context-specific health-system association, rather than a causal predictor. WorkCover patients receive financial compensation for missing work, with this cohort repeatedly linked with worse outcomes and a higher reoperation rate in the literature.45,46 Finally, diabetes has been proposed to impact healing, however this may not fully account for its role in the short-term setting.9,47 Whilst frailty indices have been linked with reoperation, due to their representation of the overall health of the patient, we did not note any such statistical association.13,14,47

Alternative techniques such as open were noted to share diabetes and osteoporosis as associated factors, however, noted additional factors such as male sex, smoking and alcohol consumption.8,14 Comparison with an endoscopic approach is difficult, with a recent meta-analysis by Zhu et al. noting only 3 cases of reoperation. 39

The association of patients who had reoperations with a longer time until the most recent follow-up appointment likely reflects the chronic nature of TLIF complications. Thirty-two percent of these reoperations occurred after the standard follow-up of 12 months (Figure 1). However, this may also represent a bias of capturing complications that re-present, although the opposite may also be true whereby dissatisfied patients are lost to follow-up.

As a secondary aim of this study, we assessed the impact of the recorded parameters on patient outcomes. As expected, cases of reoperation skewed towards worse MacNab outcomes than their control counterparts. Across the stratified cohorts, fair/poor outcomes were associated with diabetes, the mCCI frailty index, and previous surgery at the same level. These are similar to those for reoperation, albeit with previous surgery at the same level rather than different levels, possibly reflecting a dissatisfaction with an unresolving pathology.

Limitations

Few studies have reported such a wide array of potential risk factors for MIS TLIF reoperation, however, some limitations remain. Due to the nature of case-control matching, the level and number of levels could not be assessed as potential factors. Other studies have noted multilevel procedures requiring more reoperations.9,13 Manual control selection, although blinded to outcomes and performed before data extraction, may introduce selection bias with some potential that not all non-reoperation patients are fully represented. Although our centre has extensive follow-up and two emergency departments (public and private) which capture the majority of emergent re-presentations, there remains the possibility of an unknown portion of patients who were lost to follow-up. This, however, is minimised in the context of short-term reoperations within 30 days and high attendance rates to early reviews; nevertheless, it may introduce bias as less-satisfied patients may seek follow-up elsewhere. In terms of statistical limitations, the number of variables assessed increases the risk of type one errors from the multiple comparisons, although formal correction was not applied given the exploratory retrospective design and correlation with clinical variables. Thus, the significant findings should be noted as potential associations, with further external validation required. Although Kaplan-Meier modelling accounted for censoring at last follow-up, the matched case-control design limited formal time-to-event analysis of associated factors. Therefore, late reoperation risk may be underestimated and its associations should be interpreted in context of differential follow-up. Additionally, factors assessed could only be selected from those recorded by hospital staff, which in future will be minimised by using a prospective questionnaire. The associations between funding status, WorkCover status, and reoperation may reflect system-level confounding rather than biological risk, which requires further studies incorporating health-system factors (for example socioeconomic status, healthcare access) to better delineate these findings. Finally, the extensive dataset collected does still omit some variables of interest, including pain visual analogue scores, Oswestry Disability Index, and fusion rates (which may allow for further assessments of reoperation mechanisms, such as pseudoarthrosis or adjacent segment disease), all of which will be amended in future research through the prospective questionnaire and set follow-up protocols.

Conclusion

We present one of the largest single-centre experiences with MIS TLIFs, with an overall reoperation rate of 6.6% and a mean cohort follow-up of 1.5 years. Private status, WorkCover status, osteoporosis/osteopenia, diabetes, and previous lumbar surgery at a different level were all associated with reoperation. Worse patient outcomes were associated with similar factors, namely diabetes, a higher mCCI frailty index, and previous surgery at the same level. This study reports our complication profile and identifies associated preoperative factors that may help inform decision-making in the preoperative counselling phase after further external validation.
