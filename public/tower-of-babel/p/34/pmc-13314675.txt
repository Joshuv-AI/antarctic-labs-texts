Neurophysiological and Cognitive Effects of Transcendental Meditation: Evidence from EEG Biomarkers and Reaction Time
Authors: Vandana Daulatabad, Prafull K, Estelle H, Naveen R, Afna A, Anala B, Neeraja V, Dipti Magan, Hemant Bhargav, Nitin John
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscendental meditation (TM) is broadly trained mind-body intervention for its prospective benefits on physical and mental health. However, evidence exploring its effects on brain connectivity and cognitive performance remains inadequate.PurposeThis research aimed to evaluate the influence of TM on EEG functional connectivity and audiovisual reaction time in healthy adults.MethodsThis prospective single group study was undertaken after institutional ethical approval among 34 healthy adults aged between 25-50 years who completed a 12-week transcendental meditation intervention (20 minutes/day, 5 days/week). Physiological parameters including pulse rate and blood pressure were recorded using standardized protocols. Neurophysiological assessment was performed using a 32-channel EEG system and functional connectivity was analysed using Weighted Phase Lag Index (WPLI). Cognitive performance was assessed by audio-visual reaction time. Pre-post-intervention differences were analysed using paired t-test and effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d.ResultsTM practice significantly reduced systolic and diastolic blood pressure, along with significant reduction in pulse rate with a small effect size (p = 0.021, d = 0.29). EEG analysis showed increased functional connectivity across fronto-central and occipital regions. Visual and auditory reaction times improved significantly, with the largest effect observed in auditory reaction time.ConclusionTM enhances cardiovascular regulation, brain connectivity and cognitive processing speed, reflecting improved neural efficiency and autonomic balance. It may serve as a simple, non-pharmacological approach for improving cognitive performance and well-being.Keywords: Transcendental meditation, cognitive performance, reaction time, electroencephalogram, Weighted Phase Lag Index

Introduction

Meditation involves concentrating our mind on a specific object, thought or activity to achieve a peaceful and steady mental state. 1 This practice includes deliberate mental and awareness exercises such as observing, concentrating, visualising or engaging in mindful movement. 2 These activities are grounded in the essential ability to be conscious of our own awareness. Meditation encompasses a variety of practices, including mindfulness, focused attention, body scanning, loving, kindness, transcendental meditation (TM), tai-chi and advanced states like the jhanas.3, 4 TM was introduced by Maharishi Mahesh Yogi (in the 1950s), a follower of Swami Brahmananda Saraswati. This technique, with its roots in the ancient Vedic traditions of India, emphasises preventing distracting thoughts by the use of a specific mantra to foster deeper relaxation and personal growth. 5 TM gained global popularity for its simplicity to practice and its potential to enhance mental and physical health. Ancient contemplative practices, particularly those originating from the Global South, are increasingly being examined for their health advantages. To date, research has primarily concentrated on mindfulness, which is prevalent in Western clinical settings.6-9 Recently, TM has also attracted scientific interest for its effectiveness to reduce stress, enhance mood and support overall well-being of an individual. 10 However, there is limited evidence regarding TM’s effects on neurophysiological and cognitive functions within the Indian context. Therefore, our study aims to bridge this research gap by assessing the impact of TM on neurophysiological and cognitive health and exploring its potential as a complementary strategy for promoting mental health and enhancing performance.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed a single-group prospective design to evaluate the impact of TM practice. A convenience sample of healthy adult volunteers was recruited for the study. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. The study workflow is depicted in Figure 1.

Project Workflow.

Study Setting

The study was conducted in the Department of Physiology at AIIMS, Bibinagar, on receiving approval from the institutional ethical committee (IEC; AIIMS/BBN/IEC/AUG/2024/548-R). The TM programme was delivered by an experienced and certified TM instructor.

Study Participants

Healthy adults aged 25–50 years, who met the inclusion criteria and consented to participate, were recruited through oral communications and WhatsApp groups. Initially, 56 participants were enrolled in the study. The inclusion criteria required participants to be willing to learn and practice 12 weeks of TM and the ability to read and comprehend the research guidelines. Major psychiatric, neurological or chronic medical conditions, usage of psychotropic drugs, substance misuse or dependency and past practice of any meditation technique within the preceding 6 months were among the exclusion criteria. Participants who were unable to refrain from caffeine-containing beverages (e.g., coffee, tea, energy drinks) were excluded. Participants’ daily attendance was monitored, and only those with at least 80% attendance during the intervention period were included in the final analysis. Of the initial 56 participants, 34 (13 males and 21 females) met the attendance criteria and were included in the final data set. Baseline assessments were conducted before the intervention and repeated after the 12-week TM programme. Participants practiced TM for 20 min once daily, 5 days per week, at the Department of Physiology. To sustain engagement and minimise monotony, weekly motivational sessions on Indian philosophy were conducted. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual daily routines and refrain from initiating any new wellness or meditation practices during the study period.

Assessments

Physiological Measures

Pulse and blood pressure (BP) measurement: Each participant, upon arrival at the Department of Physiology on the designated day and time, was instructed to lie down in a calm and quiet room for a duration of 10 min. Subsequently, participants were asked to assume a comfortable sitting position, and the pulse rate was measured for a full minute. Following this, a BP cuff was applied to the non-dominant arm, and a digital sphygmomanometer was employed to record the BP.11, 12

Neurophysiological Measures

Electroencephalogram (EEG) data were acquired using the Neuro Max NMX 32 EEG System, a digital 32-channel amplifier. 13 Participants were instructed to have sufficient sleep and to shampoo and wash their hair and scalp the night before the EEG session. During the recording, participants were seated in a comfortable, temperature-controlled, sound-attenuated room with low ambient lighting. Prior to electrode application, the scalp was prepared with an abrasive skin prep gel to lower impedance and ensure optimal conductivity. In total, 32 silver cup electrodes were positioned according to the international 10–20 system, covering standard scalp locations for comprehensive brain activity monitoring. A linked-ears reference montage (electrodes A1 and A2) was used, and a ground electrode was placed on the forehead. Electrode-skin impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ throughout the session, as verified by the EEG system’s built-in impedance monitoring tool, to ensure high-quality signal acquisition. EEG signals were digitised at a sampling rate of 512 Hz. A band-pass filter from 0.5 to 35 Hz was applied to capture the relevant EEG frequency range while excluding slow drifts and high-frequency noise, and a 50 Hz notch filter was used to eliminate electrical interference. Continuous real-time monitoring during data collection allowed to annotate any artefacts. After each recording session, all data were visually inspected to verify signal quality and the effectiveness of artefact removal or marking.14, 15 Finally, the EEG data files were stored in a secure digital format for subsequent offline analysis. The Weighted Phase Lag Index (WPLI) was used for identifying task-related functional networks in electroencephalography recordings (as depicted in Figure 2) during cognitive tasks for analysis.16, 17

Cognitive Measures

Two distinct devices were used to measure auditory reaction time (ART) and visual reaction time (VRT). The first was a digital Reaction Time Apparatus (model no. 501-004TR) from Psychotronics, Bangalore, which had a built-in chronoscope and timer with a 1 ms accuracy. A beep sound stimulus was used to record ART, while green and red light stimuli were used to evaluate VRT. The device was made up of distinct interfaces with keys for stimuli and responses for both the participant and the examiner. The quickest reaction time was taken into account for analysis after each participant completed three trials. 18 ADI Power Lab 8 was the second tool utilised. Before the final recordings, participants were given a 1-min familiarisation trial and standardised instructions. VRT and a ‘click’ auditory stimulus were used to measure ART. The first was a digital Reaction Time Apparatus (model no. 501-004TR) from Psychotronics, Bangalore, which had a built-in chronoscope and timer with a 1 ms accuracy. A beep sound stimulus was used to record ART, while green and red light stimuli were used to evaluate VRT. The device was made up of distinct interfaces with keys for stimuli and responses for both the participant and the examiner. Each task lasted for 2 min with a 1-min break between trials. 19

Data Collection Procedure

All the tests were done in a discreet setting under the observation of the research team. Participants were comfortably seated and utilised identical equipment for both initial and follow-up assessments. Compliance with the TM practice was tracked through daily attendance records. Participants’ confidentiality was upheld throughout the study.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS-29. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarise participants’ demographics. Differences in pulse, BP, EEG and audio-visual reaction time between pre- and post-TM intervention were examined using paired t-tests. A p value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of pre- and post-intervention EEG values revealed an increase in EEG activity across several cortical regions following the TM intervention. Statistically significant values (p < .05) were observed in multiple frontal, central, temporal and occipital channels, including FP2, F4, A2, T4, C4, CZ, C3, T3, OZ and O1. Additional significant changes were also noted in BP3, BP7, BP8, DC1 and DC2 channels. Among these, the DC1 channel showed the strongest effect size (Cohen’s d = −0.53), followed by DC2 (d = −0.51) and OZ (d = −0.46), indicating moderate effects of the intervention on EEG activity. Although several other channels, such as F8, F3, F7, T5, O2 and P3, demonstrated increases in post-intervention values, these changes did not reach statistical significance (p > .05) (Table 2). Overall, the findings suggest modest-to-moderate neurophysiological changes in cortical activity following the meditation intervention.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated a significant decrease in pulse, systolic and diastolic BP following TM intervention, suggesting improved cardiovascular regulation associated with meditation practice reducing sympathetic activity and increased parasympathetic dominance. TM influences neural circuits involved in stress regulation and emotional processing, particularly within the prefrontal cortex and limbic regions such as the amygdala, thereby contributing to improved cardiovascular control. 20 Similar findings by Schneider et al. demonstrated significant reductions in BP among hypertensive individuals practising TM, highlighting its potential as a complementary lifestyle intervention for cardiovascular risk reduction. 21 Likewise, a meta-analysis by Nidich et al. reported decreases in both systolic and diastolic BP in individuals practising TM. 22 TM reduces sympathetic nervous system activity and downregulates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to reduced cortisol levels and anxiety that improves stress regulation. 23 A meta-analysis reported that TM practice resulted in a mean reduction in BP, although the effect was most evident within the first 3 months of practice. 24 Taken together, these findings support the role of TM as a potential non-pharmacological strategy for improving cardiovascular and autonomic function.

Our study findings depict increased EEG activity across several cortical regions following the TM intervention, with significant changes observed in frontal, central, temporal and occipital channels, including FP2, F4, A2, T4, C4, CZ, C3, T3, OZ and O1. Notably, DC1, DC2 and OZ exhibited moderate effect sizes, indicating neurophysiological modulation after meditation practice. The predominance of changes in the fronto-central regions suggests enhanced functional connectivity within neural networks associated with attention, cognitive control and focused awareness. 25 In our study, the reduction in audio-visual reaction time was accompanied by increased activity in frontal and central cortical regions, suggesting improved cognitive processing, anticipation and sustained attention during task performance. 26 Although correlation analysis between EEG connectivity and reaction time was not performed, the concurrent improvement in both parameters suggests a possible association between enhanced functional connectivity and cognitive processing speed, which warrants further investigation using advanced spectral and connectivity analyses. Neuroimaging studies demonstrated enhanced prefrontal cortex activation and improved psychomotor performance during attention tasks following meditation practices. 27 A functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study also demonstrated altered brain activity in attention networks during meditation practices. 28 Furthermore, meditation has been shown to improve psychomotor vigilance and reaction time, suggesting enhanced cognitive efficiency and alertness following meditation practice.29, 30 Collectively, these findings support the present results, indicating that TM may enhance neural efficiency in fronto-central networks involved in attention, cognitive control and psychomotor performance, resulting in faster and shorter audio-visual reaction time.

In our study, pre-intervention VRTs were longer with line-based stimuli compared with coloured light stimuli from the digital reaction time apparatus, probably due to strong contrast and sharp edge detection, which activates the primary visual cortex (V1). 31 Among the visual stimuli, green light produced faster responses than red light, consistent with earlier studies.32–34 Similar observations were reported by Swati et al. and Daulatabad et al.35, 36 Max-Planck’s corpuscular theory suggests that green light may trigger visual receptors more efficiently than red light due to its higher energy and shorter wavelength. 37 Because beep sounds have a sharper beginning than clicks, ART improved most noticeably with beep stimuli. 38 Both before and after TM practice, ARTs were faster than VRTs, in-line with other research.39, 40 This is because auditory signals reach the brainstem in 8–10 ms, whereas visual signals take 20–40 ms because of longer retinal-thalamic-cortical routes. 41 The post-intervention reduction in reaction time observed in this study may reflect enhanced neural efficiency and faster central information processing. This improvement corresponds with the increased EEG activity observed in fronto-central regions, indicating enhanced neural connectivity in attention-related networks. Additionally, the reduction in systolic and diastolic BP following TM suggests improved autonomic balance with increased parasympathetic activity and vagal tone. 42 TM enhances cognitive performance through limbic deactivation and improved attentional control. 43 Increased cortical arousal and attentional regulation may therefore contribute to shorter reaction times, important for optimal performance in tasks requiring rapid responses. 44 Our findings are also consistent with previous studies that reported faster reaction times and improved information processing in individuals practising TM.45–47

Limitations

The study has certain limitations. Lack of a control group limits causal inference, and participant attrition may introduce bias. Practice effects could influence reaction time results, and exploratory EEG analysis without multiple comparison correction.

Conclusion

The present study concludes that regular TM practice is associated with significant improvements in cardiovascular, neurophysiological and cognitive parameters. The observed reductions in BP suggest improved autonomic balance with enhanced parasympathetic activity, while the increase in EEG activity across fronto-central and related cortical regions indicates improved neural connectivity in networks involved in attention and cognitive control. These neurophysiological changes were accompanied by shorter audio-visual reaction times, reflecting enhanced information processing speed, attentional efficiency and psychomotor performance. Despite a few limitations, these study findings do provide preliminary evidence supporting the beneficial effects of TM on EEG parameters and cognitive performance in healthy adults. Additional research in bigger population is required that can help comprehend the distinctions in detail and develop spot-on approaches. Collectively, these findings suggest that TM promotes a state of relaxed alertness, facilitating both physiological regulation and improved cognitive functioning. Given its non-pharmacological, low-cost and easily accessible nature, TM may serve as a valuable harmonising strategy for encouraging mental well-being, stress reduction and cardiovascular health. Wider incorporation of meditation-based practices in educational institutions, workplaces and community health programmes may contribute to improving overall public health and cognitive performance in modern high-stress environments.

Suggestions

TM is a simple, safe and cost-effective practice that may improve cardiovascular health, brain function and cognitive performance. Its integration into educational institutions, workplaces and community health programmes could enhance stress resilience, attention and overall well-being. Given its non-pharmacological nature and ease of practice, TM represents a practical lifestyle intervention for improving mental clarity and reducing stress-related disorders.
