Prevalence and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of Klebsiella Pneumoniea isolated from cases of bovine mastitis in Maya City, Eastern Hararghe, Ethiopia
Authors: Bilisumma Mohammedyakob Kedir, Chala Mohammed, Abdallahi Abdurahman, Teshita Edaso Beriso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA cross-sectional study was carried out from November 2023 to May 2024 on lactating dairy cows in Maya city, in the eastern part of Ethiopia, with the aim of estimating mastitis prevalence, isolate Klebsiella pneumoniea and determine antibiotic resistance profiles of the isolates. A total of 281 animals were selected for screening of mastitis. The city was selected purposively, while kebeles, households/dairy farms, and dairy cows were chosen by the simple random sampling method. The California mastitis test (CMT) was used to screen for subclinical mastitis. Milk samples from CMT-positive and clinical mastitis cases were aseptically collected and used to isolate and identify K. pneumoniea, a common cause of mastitis. The isolates were tested for antimicrobial susceptibility using the disk diffusion method against 7 selected drugs. The overall mastitis prevalence at the cow and quarter levels was 48.4% and 28.11%, respectively, while the isolation rate of K. pneumoniea was 11.7% and 12.6%, respectively. Potential risk factors like parity, house floor, and previous history of mastitis were associated with prevalence of mastitis. Additionally, parity, management system, and teat lesion risk factor were statistically associated with isolates of K. pneumoniea. The K. pneumoniea isolates showed 100% susceptibility to ciprofloxacin, 60% to chloramphenicol, and 29% to cephalothin. In contrast, the highest (100%) resistance was observed for amoxicillin, ampicillin, and ceftriaxone, followed by 60% for vancomycin. In conclusion, the study indicated an increase in prevalence of mastitis, with K.pneumonia isolates showing high resistance to the commonly used antibiotics. Routine mastitis control measures combined with antimicrobial therapy guided by culture and susceptibility testing are recommended to reduce mastitis prevalence and limit antimicrobial resistance.Keywords: Antimicrobial susceptibility, California mastitis test, Klebsiella pneumoniea, Prevalence, Risk factors

Introduction

Ethiopia possesses 70 million cattle. Out of this total cattle population, the female cattle constitute about 56%, while the males are 44%, and about 97.4% of the total cattle in the country are local breeds. The remaining are hybrid and exotic breeds that accounted for about 2.3% and 0.31%, respectively [1]. However, milk production does not satisfy the country’s requirements due to a multitude of factors [2]. Among these factors, bovine mastitis is the second most frequent disease next to reproductive disorders and one of the major causes of reduced milk production and economic failure in Ethiopia. It affects both the quantity and quality of milk [3].

Mastitis is a multi-etiological complex disease, which is defined as inflammation of the parenchyma of the mammary gland characterized by physical and chemical changes in milk and pathological changes in glandular tissue [4]. Mastitis remains the most common infectious disease in adult dairy cattle and the disease that most affects the dairy industry. A major portion of this cost is not attributable to cows with clinically apparent mastitis but, rather, is the result of subclinical mastitis (SCM) [5]. This is explained by the fact that SCM is more difficult to diagnose and therefore usually persists longer in the herds, causing production losses [6]. More than 140 various microorganisms can cause mastitis [7]. Among various infectious agents, bacterial pathogens have been known to be widely distributed in the environment of dairy cows, constituting a threat to the mammary gland [8]. The bacteria that cause mastitis in dairy cows are classified into contagious pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus agalactiae, and Mycoplasma bovis, and environmental pathogens, such as streptococci (Streptococcus dysagalactiae and Streptococcus uberis) and coliforms (E. coli and Klebsiella species) [9, 10].

Klebsiella spp. are important opportunistic pathogens commonly defined as environmental clinical mastitis agents [11], from Klebsiella spp. K. oxytoca and K. pneumoniea are the species that are responsible for causing clinical mastitis [12]. K. pneumoniea is a gram-negative organism and one of the most important members of the Klebsiella genus belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family [13, 14], and it is a facultative anaerobic, non-motile, lactose-fermenting, and rod-shaped (0.3–1 μm in width and 0.6–6 μm in length) organism arranged singly, in pairs, or in short chains that possess a prominent polysaccharide capsule, which gives the colonies their appearance on agar plates [14, 15].

Klebsiella pneumoniea colonies appear large, mucoid, and reddish-pink with diffusing red pigment on MacConkey agar, indicating fermentation of glucose and acid production [15]. This species of bacterium is frequently isolated from various infections in animals and humans. In humans, it causes infections mainly in hospitalized patients, while in animals, infections of the urinary tract, respiratory tract, and sepsis are the most common clinical manifestations, and it causes the most severe cases among the gram-negative bacterial causes of mastitis [16].

In dairy cattle, Klebsiella spp. are transmitted by contact with teats, mainly through manure, bedding, and other farm equipment, and invade mammary epithelial cells and persist for a long time [17]. Mastitis caused by this bacterium may be mild, moderate, or severe depending upon the symptoms, and because the bacterium deeply invades the udder tissue and damages the glandular tissue, it will compromise the secretory capacity of the mammary gland. Consequently, Klebsiella pneumoniea infections become chronic infections, causing cows to experience a long-term reduction in milk production [12, 18].

Mastitis caused by K. pneumoniea is more severe due to its poor bacteriological cure rates or poor response to antibiotic therapy following administration of antimicrobials, rapid evolution to toxic shock and death of the animal, and also it can result in significant economic losses due to its long-term reduction in milk production [11, 19]. Cows with Klebsiella spp. mastitis are more likely to die or to be culled than are cows with other types of mastitis [20]. Vaccination has been reported to provide some protection against the risk of culling but does not affect the incidence or severity of clinical mastitis due to Klebsiella spp. [20, 21].

Despite Klebsiella mastitis being clinically impairing in cows, responding poorly to antibiotic therapy, and costly to the industry, only a few studies describe K. pneumoniea isolated from mastitis cases. The epidemiological data addressing the prevalence of K. pneumoniea from dairy cows in Ethiopia is not well documented and published. To the best of the researcher’s knowledge, there is a paucity of information on the prevalence and antimicrobial susceptibility test of K. pneumoniea in Maya City in particular and in our country in general as well. Therefore, this study was conducted to estimate the prevalence of mastitis, identify its associated risk factors, isolate Klebsiella pneumoniea from mastitic milk samples, and evaluate the antimicrobial susceptibility profile of the isolates obtained from lactating cows in Maya City.

Materials and methods

Description of study area

The study was conducted in the Maya city, Oromia Regional State, in the Eastern Hararghe zone of Eastern Ethiopia. Maya city is located 510 kilometers away from Addis Ababa. Aweday, Haramaya, and Adele are the sub-cities that make up the Maya City. The major production system is the mixed crop production system, and the main livestock types kept in the area include cattle, sheep, goats, camels, donkeys, and poultry. Its cosmic area is scope/longitude: 9°24’ 0” N and 42°1’0” E longitude. The area has an altitude range between 1600 and 2100 m above sea level. The rainfall of the district is bimodal, with the short rain occurring between the months of February and May and the long rain occurring between the months of June and September, followed by a dry season from October to February. The mean annual rainfall is 492 mm, ranging from 118 to 866 mm. The mean maximum and minimum temperatures are 17 and 9 degrees Celsius and relative humidity is 18 °C and 68%, respectively (Maya City Office of Agriculture and Rural Development, 2023). From a population of 79,446 cattle, there were about 16,957 lactating cows, of which 5765, 6105, and 5087 were found in the Addelle, Haramaya, and Awaday subcities of Maya City, respectively. Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted from November 2023 to May 2024 on randomly selected lactating dairy cows in Maya city, in eastern Ethiopia, to estimate the prevalence of bovine mastitis and determine associated risk factors. The study also sought to isolate K. pneumoniea, from cases of mastitis and determine antimicrobial susceptibility and resistance profiles of the isolates. A sampling frame of dairy farms and households owning dairy cows was established prior to data collection.

The sampling frame for dairy farms was prepared in collaboration with city animal health workers, while the household sampling frame was developed in coordination with local extension workers. Within each selected farm or household, individual dairy cows were selected using simple random sampling. In cases where a selected farm or household declined participation, it was replaced by the next eligible farm or household on the sampling list.

From the list of registered dairy farms, five farms were selected based on owners’ willingness to participate in the study, and these farms contributed 15% of the total sample size. From each selected dairy farm, an average of nine (9) lactating cows were randomly selected in accordance with the predefined inclusion criteria. The remaining 85% of the total sample size was obtained from household-owned dairy cows. Households were selected using a simple random sampling technique from a sampling frame developed for the selected kebeles. On average, four (4) dairy cows were sampled per household.

Study population

Lactating dairy cows from five farms and individual household owners that were selected randomly from the Haramaya, Adele, and Awaday subcities of Maya City are the study population of the current study. Most of the cows were local (Borana and Arsi breeds) and crossbreeds (Holstein-Friesian x Zebu), with few Holstein-Friesians. With regard to management, the herds were managed under intensive, semi-intensive, and extensive management systems. The intensively managed cattle were kept indoors and received concentrate feeds (grain mixes) in addition to hay and crop residues (such as corn stalks, wheat/barley straw, and other leftovers from grain threshing). The semi-intensively managed cattle grazed freely on pasture but received supplementary feeds in the morning and evening when they were milked. Lactating cows under antibiotic treatment were excluded from the study animals. The study cows were categorized into different age groups: young (3–5 years of age), adult (5–8 years of age), and old (above 8 years of age). According to Shewafera et al. [22]. , parity was grouped into few (1–3 calves), moderate (3–6 calves), and many (6 and above calves). The lactation stage of the cow was also categorized into early-stage lactation (1–3 months), mid-lactation (4–7 months), and late lactation (above 7 months), while the body condition score was categorized as good, medium, and poor [23, 24].

Sample size determination and sampling techniques

The desired sample size for the study was calculated based on the formula developed by Thrusfield [25] for simple random sampling. The number of the sample was determined using a 95% level of confidence, 0.05 desired absolute precision, and by taking 24.04% of the expected prevalence that can be taken from a previous study conducted by Shewafera et al. [22].

Pexp = expected prevalence 24.04% and d2 = desired absolute precision 0.05. Based on the stated formula and the substituted parameters, a total sample size of 281 lactating cows was determined. This sample was allocated proportionally among the three study towns, with 125 cows from Haramaya, 99 from Adele, and 57 from Awaday.

The study towns (Haramaya, Awaday, and Adele) and their respective kebeles were purposively selected based on the prevailing number of dairy farms cows, whereas households/dairy farms and individual lactating cows were selected using a simple random sampling technique.

Study protocol

Screening of mastitis

Clinical inspection of the udder

Each cow was clinically observed for the manifestation of general clinical signs related to the udder and teats and the presence of any gross abnormalities like fibrosis, inflammatory swellings, pain, visible injury or lesion, atrophy of the tissue, and teat blindness. The milk sample was observed for any change regarding color, odor, and consistency. The presence of clots, flakes, blood, and other consistency changes were part of the indicators for clinical mastitis along with udder and teat morphological changes [8].

California mastitis test (CMT)

For cows with no clinical indications of mastitis, the CMT was used to test for subclinical mastitis in accordance with the National Mastitis Council Guidelines. From each quarter, about 2 ml of milk was placed in each of the four shallow cups in the CMT paddle. Each cup was filled with an equal amount of a CMT reagent (4% NaOH in distilled water and 1% bromothymol blue), which was carefully mixed by spinning the paddle horizontally for up to 30 s. Gel formation was assessed immediately after testing. Milk samples from clinically mastitic and CMT-positive (subclinical) cows were collected at both cow-level and quarter (teat)-level and subsequently subjected to microbiological examination [26].

Sample collection and transport

Milk samples were collected from each clinically and subclinically affected cow. Briefly, each teat end was cleaned and disinfected with ethanol (70%) before sampling. The first three fore-milks were discarded to reduce contamination of samples by teat-end-dwelling microbes, and about 15 ml of milk were collected using a sterile universal bottle with tight-fitting cups. Then samples were transported to the Microbiology Laboratory, College of Veterinary Medicine, Haramaya University, using an icebox. The samples were kept at + 4 °C until processed for isolation, in case immediate inoculation of samples on media was not convenient [27].

While sample collection, overall biodata and husbandry of dairy cows were collected. Generally, it includes age (young, old, and adult), parity (few, moderate, and many), stage of lactation (early, medium, and late), breed (HF, Cross, and Local), house floor type (concrete or muddy), previous mastitis history (yes or no), and milking personnel hygiene (good or poor). In addition to the surveys, farm hygiene and body condition score (good, medium, or poor) were carried out through direct observational evaluations.

Isolation and identification of Klebsiella Pneumoniea

The milk samples were cultured on a MacConkey agar selective media and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The isolate based on colony character (pinkish, mucoid) was transferred and cultured on EMB. On EMB agar, Klebsiella spp. produced large, mucoid, pink-to-purple, and nonmetallic-green sheen colonies [15, 26, 28]. Then the isolate was transferred and aseptically cultured on nutrient agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. For further identification of bacterial colonies, the Gram staining in which encapsulated, Gram-negative, short, plump, straight rods with a pinkish background were seen. Additionally, catalase and KOH tests were done using 3% of H₂O₂ (hydrogen peroxide) and 3% of KOH (potassium hydroxide), mixing them with the pure isolates from nutrient agar on the glass slide, and the results were positive for the catalase test (bubble formation) and positive for the KOH test, in which a viscous string was seen. Secondary biochemical tests (Indole, Methyl Red, Vogues-Proskauer, Citrate Utilization, and Triple Sugar Iron (TSI)) were performed for identification of K. pneumoniea [29]. A biochemical test result of fermented sucrose, lactose, and glucose with the production of acid within 24–48 h of incubation, a positive reaction (pink-red color over the surface of the medium) in the VP test, and a negative reaction (yellow color over the surface of the medium) in the MR and Indole tests (yellow ring) was identified as K. pneumoniea [30].

Antimicrobial susceptibility tests

Klebsiella pneumoniea were subjected to in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility testing according to the procedures recommended by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [31]. For susceptibility testing, direct colony suspension of young isolates (incubated for 18 h) was adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland standard. Susceptibility to antimicrobial agents was determined for isolated strains by the disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar. The antibiotic discs used for the study were ampicillin (2 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), amoxicillin (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), vancomycin (30 µg), and cephalothin (30 µg) (Oxoid, England). Which were placed on the surface of Mueller Hinton agar (MHA), and then the plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. Finally, the inhibition zone around each disc was measured against each antibiotic, and multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories [32].

Data analysis

The collected data was entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet and filtered before being analyzed using STATA 14 software. Descriptive statistical analysis was used to summarize and present collected data. The prevalence of mastitis was calculated by dividing the total number of mastitic animals by the total number of animals included for study. The prevalence of K. pneumoniea isolate was calculated as the proportion of positive cows against the total number of mastitic animals. The association between different risk factors with the prevalence of mastitis and K. pneumoniea isolate were analyzed, in univariable and multivariable logistic regression. Univariable logistic analysis will be used to analyze the unconditional association of presumed predictors. Predictors with liberal P value (p < 0.25) will be considered for multivariable regression analysis after multicollinearity assessment. To this end Goodman and Kruskal’s Gamma statistic was used to check multicollinearity in a cross-tabulation for all selected predictors.

All the non-collinear predictors (gamma values between − 0.6 to + 0.6) were subjected to multivariable logistic regression analysis, and using backward elimination technique, the final model was developed. Confounding effect was also checked using the changes in the proportion of OR. Then, a covariate was considered to be a confounder and comprised in the model if its inclusion altered the OR of the estimated risk at least by 20%. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered a statistically significant association. The antimicrobial susceptibility test was calculated by dividing the total number of K. pneumoniea isolates that were susceptible to a specific drug by the total number of isolates found in the study after measurement of the zone of inhibition for each drug, and multidrug resistance was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories [32].

Result

Mastitis prevalence at cow level

A total of 281 lactating dairy cows (1,124 quarters) were examined for mastitis in the study area. The overall cow-level prevalence of mastitis was 48.75% (137/281; 95% CI: 42.91–54.62). Of these, 7.11% (20/281; 95% CI: 4.60–10.80) were diagnosed with clinical mastitis based on physical examination, while 41.64% (117/281; 95% CI: 39.50–47.50) were identified as having subclinical mastitis using the California Mastitis Test (CMT) during the study period.

Mastitis prevalence at quarter level

From the total of 1124 quarters examined, the overall prevalence of mastitis at the quarter level was 28.11% (95% CI: 24.12–32.73). Accordingly, the prevalence of clinical and subclinical mastitis at the quarter level was 5.24% and 22.85%, respectively. However, 2.75% (31/1124) of the teats were blind (Table 1).

Result of antimicrobial susceptibility test

A total of 40 isolates (n = 40) were tested against 7 commonly used antimicrobial drugs, such as ampicillin (10 µg), amoxicillin (2 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), vancomycin (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), cephalothin (30 µg), and ceftriaxone (30 µg), following CLSI guidelines. The antibiotic sensitivity profiles of the isolates showed susceptibility to the isolates for ciprofloxacin (100%), chloramphenicol (60%), cephalothin (27.5%), and vancomycin (20%). Among all antimicrobials tested, Klebsiella isolates were 100% resistant to ceftriaxone, ampicillin, and amoxicillin (Table 5).

Discussion

The current findings provide substantial information regarding the prevalence of mastitis in dairy cows of Maya City, Eastern Hararghe, Ethiopia. The isolation and identification of K. pneumoniea from clinical and subclinical mastitis cases in the current study are consistent with previous studies in various parts of Ethiopia and other countries, indicating that the bacterium remains a significant threat to animal health and milk quality. The antimicrobial susceptibility patterns revealed in this study indicate significant resistance to commonly used antibiotics. In the present study, the overall prevalence of mastitis was found to be 48.4%, of which 41.3% and 7.11% were subclinical and clinical mastitis, respectively. This finding was in line with the previous finding of 49.2% in Haramaya [33], 44.5% in Somalia [34], 46.7% in Adama town [35], 53.25% in Dire Dawa town [36], and 54.2% in Southern Ethiopia [23], respectively. However, the finding was higher than those studies reported: 17.1% in the Gamo zone, Southern Ethiopia [37]; 23.18% in the West Harerghe zone [38]; 24.1% in central Ethiopia [39]; and 24.04% in Dire Dawa [22]. In contrast, the present prevalence was lower than a previous report of 65.6% in Asella [24], 73% in the Mid Rift Valley [40], 73.3% in Modjo [41], and 74.7% reported by Zeryehun et al. [42] in Addis Ababa. The variability in the prevalence of bovine mastitis between the current study and previous reports could be suggested by the complexity of the disease, which involves the interaction of several factors, mainly the difference in management and husbandry practices, environmental conditions, study design and sample size, and factors related to the causative agent and host.

In the current study, a 7.11% prevalence of clinical mastitis (CM) was observed. This was in agreement with the findings of Abebe et al. [23]. , Lakew et al. [19]. , and Mbindyo et al. [43]. , who reported 6.1% in Southern Ethiopia, 6.77% in and around the Haramaya district, and 6.8% in Kenya, respectively, and the current finding is comparable with the finding of 9.9% in Ambo [44] and 10.7% in Eastern Harrarghe [42]. However, the present finding was higher than the previous reports of Belay et al. [37]. , 1.9% in the Gamo zone, southern Ethiopia, and 2% by Zerfu et al. [45]. , in the western Hararghe zone, Ethiopia. In contrast, this finding was lower than the finding of Tuke et al. [46] and Zeryehun et al. [42]. , who reported 16.67% in Southern Ethiopia and 19.6% in Addis Ababa, respectively. The variation in prevalence might be attributed to the differences in the management practices of clinical mastitis at different study areas and the availability of veterinary services. Animals, pathogens, and the environment, which are the most important determining factors that influence the occurrence of clinical mastitis, also may contribute to this variation [47].

The prevalence of subclinical mastitis reported in the current study (41.6%) was consistent with the findings of Belina et al. [48], who reported 40.7% in selected zones of Oromia regional states, Ethiopia, while the current finding was lower than those reported by Abebe et al. [23]. , from Southern Ethiopia (48.1%), and by Fesseha et al. [41], from Modjo Town, Central Ethiopia (71.02%). However, it is greater than the figure reported by Dabele et al. [49]. from the West Shewa Zone of Ethiopia (27.33%). This could be because subclinical mastitis is not visible and silent, and it is more difficult to diagnose than clinical mastitis, which is immediately observable.

The current study revealed that 28.11% of quarters were affected by mastitis, which is in line with the report of 29.4% in Southern Ethiopia [23] and 29.04% in and around the Haramaya district [24]. However, the present finding is higher than the finding of Amdhun et al. [50]. , who reported 16.1% in the Tullo District of West Hararghe, Ethiopia, and Tegegne et al. [51], (14.4%) in Southern Ethiopia, while Fesseha et al. [41] reported a higher prevalence of 36.9% in Modjo town and suburbs, central Oromia, Ethiopia, and Zeryehun et al. [52]. reported 51.7% in selected districts of the Eastern Harrarghe zone, Eastern Ethiopia. The variability in the bovine mastitis at the quarter level among the reported findings in different areas could be due to differences in the management practices, laboratory techniques used, sample size, environmental conditions, and animal-level factors.

The finding of 2.7% blind teats in this study was highly similar to the 2.7% result conducted by Mohammed-Kemal [53] in the Babile district, Eastern Hararghe zone, Ethiopia, and it is comparable with the results of 3.4%, 3.5%, and 3.9% conducted by Fesseha et al. [41]. in Modjo town and suburbs, central Oromia, Ethiopia; Tegegne et al. [51]. in Southern Ethiopia; and Tezera and Aman [54] in and around Assosa town, Benishangul-Gumuz Regional State, Western Ethiopia, respectively. The blind quarters seen in this study demonstrate the significant harm that mastitis imposes in the study area as well as the lack of culling of those chronically affected animals, which would have eliminated a potential source of mammary infections for the cows [51].

The present study revealed that a statistically significant (p < 0.05) association was observed among the risk factors, such as parity, house floor, and previous history of mastitis. In this study, cows with a previous history of mastitis were more likely to have mastitis than cows without a previous history of mastitis. A similar finding was reported in Ethiopia by Tegegne et al. [51], Abebe et al. [23]. , and Kumar et al. [55]. in India and Mbindyo et al. [43]. in Kenya. This mastitis recurrence might be due to a persistent infection of the mammary gland by a mastitis-causing pathogen [23, 56].

The occurrence of bovine mastitis and parity was significantly associated, with the risk of mastitis increasing with parity number more than in low and moderate parity cows. This result was in agreement with previous studies by Girma and Tamir [57] and Kebebew and Jorga [58] that were conducted in Ethiopia. Numerous causes, such as the body’s immune system gradually declining, structural changes to the udder and teats, and repetitive exposure to milking operations, might be the cause of the rising prevalence rate. The increasing prevalence of mastitis with increasing age is in agreement with the findings by Yusuf-Isleged [34], Girma et al. [38], and Biffa et al. [2]. , who reported that the risk of mastitis increased significantly with the advancing age of the cow. This might be because aging causes animals’ immune systems to weaken, making them more vulnerable to mastitis.

The study also showed that there was a statistically significant association (p < 0.05) of mastitis with house floor types, in those cows living in muddy soil houses had higher mastitis prevalence than those living in barns with concrete floors. This finding is in agreement with the previous findings of Shewafera et al. [22] and Tegegne et al. [51], who found the cows that lived in muddy soil barn floors were more prone to bovine mastitis than cows that lived on concrete barn floors. Given that the cow spent most of the day sleeping on the floor, floor contact with manure, bedding material, feed, dirt, mud, and water may have contributed to this. These materials can readily harbor microorganisms that cause mastitis and can enter the udder through the teat orifice.

In the present study, the prevalence of isolated K. pneumoniea from positive mastitic bovine milk samples was found to be 12.6%, which was in agreement with the previous study done by Osman et al. [16], who reported 11.94% in Egypt, and comparable with the studies of El-Sukhon [59], in Jordan; Katsande et al. [60], in Zimbabwe; Chaudhary et al. [61]. , in India; and Thompson-Crispi et al. [62], in Canada, who reported 9.7%, 15.5%, 9%, and 9.5% prevalence of isolated K. pneumoniea, respectively. However, the present finding was higher than the previous reports of Belay et al. [37]. in the Gamo zone; Etifu and Tilahun [40] in the Mid Rift Valley; Shewafera et al. [22]. in Dire Dawa; and Zerfu et al. [45] in the western Hararghe zone, Ethiopia, who reported 1.6%, 5.8%, 3.1%, and 4.6%, respectively. The variation in the prevalence of K. pneumoniea isolates between the current study and the reports of different authors might be attributed to the differences in the sample size, geographical area, and study design and also might be due to differences in management and husbandry practices.

This finding revealed a statistically significant (p < 0.05) association between K. pneumoniea isolates from mastitis milk and the risk factors, such as parity, management system, and teat lesion. The present study revealed that K. pneumoniea isolates were higher in cows with many calvings than in medium- and few-calving cows. High prevalence was observed in the current study with the intensive system compared to the semi-intensive and extensive systems. This is in agreement with the study carried out by Yusuf-Isleged [34] and opposed to that of Kebebew and Jorga [58], who report the higher prevalence in semi-intensive than intensive and extensive systems. This may be explained by the differences in the hygienic standards of the dairy environment and the methods of milking used by various management systems. Additionally, cows with teat lesions showed a significantly higher prevalence of K. pneumoniae mastitis, as teat injuries compromise the natural barrier of the teat skin and canal, facilitating bacterial entry and colonization. Damaged teat tissue may also serve as a reservoir for environmental pathogens, increasing infection risk during milking, particularly under suboptimal hygienic conditions [8].

The observed antimicrobial resistance pattern of K. pneumoniae isolates in the present study may be attributed to several context-specific factors prevailing in the study area. Although ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, and cephalothin showed relatively higher efficacy—consistent with reports from Egypt and China (Osman et al. [16]; Yang et al. [63])—the high resistance rates observed against commonly used β-lactam antibiotics, including ampicillin, amoxicillin, ceftriaxone, and cephalothin, likely reflect their widespread and often indiscriminate use in dairy farms. In the study area, these antimicrobials are frequently administered without prior culture and susceptibility testing, sometimes at subtherapeutic doses or for inappropriate durations, which can promote the selection and persistence of resistant strains. In addition, limited veterinary oversight, easy access to antimicrobials without prescription, and poor adherence to treatment protocols may further exacerbate antimicrobial misuse. The universal resistance to ampicillin and amoxicillin observed in this study is consistent with the intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms of K. pneumoniae, including β-lactamase production which is consistent with the Chinese study conducted by Yang et al. in [63].

Limitation of study

This study’s cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation of the identified risk factors. The study was only conducted in a Maya city, limiting the findings’ generalizability. Laboratory identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were based on selected antibiotics and standard culture methods, which may not capture the full spectrum of resistance patterns or emerging strains. Resource constraints also hampered molecular confirmation of K. pneumoniea, restricting deeper understanding of strain variation and resistance mechanisms.

Conclusion and recommendations

The study revealed an overall mastitis prevalence of 48.75%, demonstrating that the disease remains a major challenge to dairy farmers. Subclinical mastitis was more prevalent than clinical cases, reflecting farmers’ failure to detect infections without visible signs. Potential risk factors statistically associated with mastitis were parity, type of housing floor, previous mastitis history, teat lesions, and management systems.

Klebsiella pneumoniea was isolated from 12.6% of mastitic milk samples, and antimicrobial susceptibility tests identified ciprofloxacin and chloramphenicol as effective treatment options, whereas ampicillin, amoxicillin, and ceftriaxone were found to be ineffective. These results indicate that there is an urgent need for regular mastitis screening, improved farm hygiene, proper housing conditions, and early management of teat lesions should be strengthened to reduce mastitis occurrence. The use of highly resistant antibiotics such as amoxicillin, ampicillin, and ceftriaxone should be discouraged, while effective drugs like ciprofloxacin should be used cautiously and under veterinary supervision.
