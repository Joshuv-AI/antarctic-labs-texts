The YAP1-NPM1 nuclear complex regulates MYC and reveals a targetable oncogenic node
Authors: Abdulrahman M. Dwead, Marwah M. Al-Mathkour, Maxim Khanov, Esra Akbas, Bekir Cinar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryYes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) and nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) regulate diverse cellular functions; however, their cooperative roles in cancer remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate that YAP1 and NPM1 form a direct nuclear protein complex in prostate cancer cells and tissues, as revealed by proteomics, co-immunoprecipitation, proximity ligation, and GST-pulldown assays. Androgen signaling modulates the YAP1-NPM1 interaction in androgen receptor-positive cells. Genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of NPM1 disrupts YAP1-NPM1 protein assembly, decreases YAP1 and MYC expression, and suppresses cell proliferation and motility. Immunological and computational analyses of prostate tumor samples indicate that elevated YAP1 and NPM1 expression and interaction correlate with disease progression. These findings identify the YAP1-NPM1 axis as a key regulatory node that integrates oncogenic transcriptional programs and confers therapeutic vulnerabilities. Targeting this axis may enhance the efficacy of androgen receptor-directed therapies and provide new strategies for treating advanced prostate cancer.

Introduction

Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) is a critical transcriptional co-regulator that integrates mechanical cues and cellular signaling to control cell proliferation, survival, stemness, tissue development, and organ size.1,2,3 Under physiological conditions, the Hippo signaling cascade, comprising MST1/2 and LATS1/2 protein kinases, regulates YAP1 through phosphorylation. This modification promotes YAP1 cytoplasmic retention and proteasomal degradation, thereby inhibiting its uncontrolled transcriptional activity.4,5 Disruption of this regulatory mechanism, whether through gene amplification, loss of Hippo pathway components, or aberrant subcellular localization, leads to YAP1 nuclear accumulation and activation of broad transcriptional programs that promote oncogenesis.6,7 Such dysregulation is a hallmark of aggressive cancers across multiple tissues, including liver,8,9 lung,10 breast,11 colon,12 pancreatic,13 and prostate cancer.14,15 These findings indicate that YAP1 serves as a critical modulator of transcriptional networks and influences diverse aspects of cellular biology and disease progression, particularly under changing androgen conditions.

Unlike classical transcription factors, YAP1 lacks intrinsic DNA-binding capability and instead exerts its transcriptional activity by interacting with DNA-binding proteins, most notably the TEAD family.16 These interactions form the core of YAP1-driven transcriptional programs, recruiting coactivators and chromatin remodelers to regulate gene expression and chromatin accessibility. Beyond TEAD, YAP1 engages in context-dependent interactions with transcription factors, including androgen receptor (AR),14,17,18 and estrogen receptor (ER),19,20 and other oncogenic drivers, such as AKT21 and mTOR.22 These partnerships expand the functional repertoire of YAP1, enabling integration of diverse signaling pathways and adaptation to specific cellular environments.16,23 The oncogenic potential of YAP1 is amplified by its ability to reprogram cellular states, enhance stemness, and confer therapy resistance, making it a critical node in cancer biology and a promising therapeutic target. In prostate cancer, dysregulated YAP1 has been implicated in tumor progression and therapy resistance, including phenotypic transitions that reduce dependence on canonical AR signaling. However, the molecular partners that enable YAP1 to exert its context-specific functions in prostate cancer remain incompletely defined.

Nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) is a multifunctional RNA-binding protein that regulates various cellular processes, including cancer progression.24 Predominantly localized in the nucleus of dividing cells,24,25 NPM1 orchestrates ribosome biogenesis,26 mRNA processing,27 chromatin remodeling28 DNA repair,29 and genomic stability.30 Post-translational modifications, including acetylation,31 phosphorylation,32 polyubiquitination,33 and SUMOylation34 likely facilitate broad the functional repertoire of NPM1. Elevated NPM1 expression in proliferating cells highlights its critical role in cell cycle progression and centrosome dynamics,35 processes frequently dysregulated during malignant transformation and cancer progression. Dysregulation of NPM1 has been reported in hematologic malignancies36 and in various solid tumors, including prostate cancer.37 Recent evidence suggests that NPM1 contributes to stress adaptation, proliferation, and therapy resistance in cancer.38 Due to its capacity to scaffold protein complexes and regulate chromatin accessibility, NPM1 is positioned to coordinate transcriptional responses to both extracellular and intracellular signaling cues.

YAP1 and NPM1 have been characterized as individual oncogenic mediators in diverse contexts,23,36 yet their potential cooperation in amplifying oncogenic signaling has not been fully elucidated. The present study demonstrates that YAP1 cooperates with NPM1 to enhance oncogenic transcriptional program and promote tumor progression. This study has identified a direct and functional interaction between YAP1 and NPM1 in prostate cancer cells. This interaction primarily occurs in the nucleus and is regulated by androgen signaling in AR-positive cells, while it occurs independently of androgen in AR-negative cells. These findings indicate that the YAP1-NPM1 axis represents a previously unrecognized oncogenic mechanism and a promising therapeutic vulnerability in prostate cancer.

Results

NPM1 and YAP1 physically interact

Previous research from our laboratory has demonstrated that androgens regulate the YAP1 protein complexes, as shown by mass spectrometry and confirmed through biochemical and genetic methods under various conditions.39 To identify proteins that interact with YAP1 downstream of AR-directed androgen signaling, we reanalyzed the mass spectrometry-based proteomics dataset.39 This analysis revealed that nucleophosmin (NPM1), ATP synthase subunit beta (ATP5B), heat shock protein 70 (HSPA8), and the RNA-binding protein ALYREF are the persistent YAP1-associated proteins under both dihydrotestosterone and enzalutamide treatment conditions (Figure 1A). To assess the biological significance of their associations with YAP1, we constructed a protein interaction network, revealing NPM1, ATP5B, ALYREF, and HSPA8 as integral components of the YAP1 protein network (Figure 1B). We selected NPM1 for further investigation in the context of YAP1 due to its established roles in chromatin remodeling, transcriptional regulation, and cancer progression. Based on these observations, we aim to determine whether NPM1 interacts with YAP1, both biochemically and functionally, and whether they cooperate to promote cancer progression. To address this gap, we analyzed NPM1 expression in the AR-positive prostate cancer cell lines (LNCaP, C4-2, C4-2B, and 22Rv1) and the AR-negative prostate cancer cell lines (PC3 and ARCaP). LNCaP is castration-sensitive (androgen-dependent), whereas C4-2 and C4-2B are androgen-insensitive sublines of LNCaP, with C4-2B exhibiting bone metastatic properties.40 Western blot analysis revealed heterogeneous NPM1 protein levels across these cell lines (Figures 1C and S1A), with LNCaP and PC3 showing the highest NPM1 expression compared to C4-2, C4-2B, 22Rv1, and ARCaP. Quantitative PCR confirmed a positive correlation between NPM1 transcript and protein abundance (Figure 1D). Furthermore, co-immunofluorescence staining demonstrated nuclear colocalization of endogenous NPM1 and YAP1 proteins, supporting their potential association (Figure 1E).

To experimentally validate the protein complex formation between native YAP1 and NPM1, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) followed by western blot (WB) analysis. Co-IP combined with WB demonstrated that NPM1 was detected in YAP1 immune complexes (Figures 1F and S1B), with interaction strengths varying across cell types. No interaction was observed with an isogenic IgG control, confirming the specificity of the YAP1-NPM1 protein complexes (Figure 1F). To assess reciprocity of NPM1-YAP1 interaction, we repeated co-IP using an NPM1 antibody with the same nuclear extracts, and the result revealed that YAP1 was present in NPM1 immune complexes exclusively in PC3 cells, but not in LNCaP, C4-2, or C4-2B cells (Figures 1G and S1B). These findings indicate that the reciprocal YAP1-NPM1 interaction is context-dependent, at least under these experimental conditions.

To further corroborate the interaction between endogenous YAP1 and NPM1 in a native cellular context, we employed a proximity ligation assay (PLA) using our established protocol.39 PLA enables in situ visualization of protein-protein interactions at single-molecule resolution within the cell. PLA signals demonstrated that YAP1 and NPM1 are in close-proximity, predominantly occurring within the nuclei of LNCaP, C4-2, and PC3 cells (Figures 1H–1J). The interaction intensity correlated with the levels of NPM1 and YAP1 expression (Figures 1C–1E). This observation supports that NPM1 and YAP1 physically associate in the nucleus, validating the co-immunoprecipitation data.

We reported that a controlled expression of the STK4-encoded MST1 kinase in C4-2 cells reduced YAP1 nuclear abundance and increased enzalutamide cytotoxicity.14 Leveraging these findings, we performed PLA to assess whether the Hippo-like MST1 kinase signaling influences YAP1-NPM1 interactions in C4-2/HA-MST1 cells. Indeed, ectopic MST1 expression significantly decreased both the number and intensity of YAP1-NPM1 interaction compared to control cells (Figures 2C and S2C). Androgen treatment caused a modest but statistically insignificant reduction in YAP1-NPM1 interaction, whereas enzalutamide markedly suppressed the interaction (Figures 2C and S2C). These results confirm that the Hippo signaling pathway regulates YAP1-NPM1 interactions.

NPM1 is known to bind both RNA and DNA to regulate gene expression and diverse cellular functions.43,44 To assess whether RNA or DNA influences the NPM1-YAP1 interaction, we performed co-IP assays using cytoplasmic and nuclear extracts that were treated with or without RNase or DNase I. Co-IP followed by immunoblotting showed that RNase treatment enhanced the NPM1-YAP1 interaction in LNCaP and C4-2 cells compared to untreated controls (Figures 2D, S3A, and S3B), suggesting that RNA molecules may act as competitive inhibitor of the YAP1-NPM1 interaction. In contrast, DNase I treatment abolished NPM1-YAP1 complexes (Figures 2E, S3C, ans S3D), indicating that chromatin is critical mediator of the interaction. These effects were specific, as neither RNase nor DNase I treatment resulted in detectable YAP1-NPM1 complexes in cytoplasmic extracts, indicating that nucleic acids are also key regulators of YAP1-NPM1 interaction dynamics. Together, these findings suggest that the YAP1-NPM1 interaction is highly dynamic and regulated by intrinsic factors such as Hippo pathway activity and nucleic acids, as well as extrinsic factors including androgen signaling.

NPM1 interacts with defined YAP1 protein domains

To identify the YAP1 domains that are responsible for NPM1 binding, we performed GST-pulldown assays using recombinant GST vector (control) and GST-fused YAP1 fragments (Figures 2F–2H and S4A). GST-pulldown combined with WB revealed that the YAP1 fragment spanning residues 2–150, which includes the proline-rich (PR) domain, interacted with NPM1 in castration-sensitive LNCaP cells (Figure 2H, upper panel; Figure S4B). In contrast, the YAP1 fragment comprising residues 151–298, containing the WW and SH3 domains, mediated NPM1 binding in castration-resistant C4-2 cells (Figure 2H, lower panel). These results indicate that NPM1 associates with distinct YAP1 domains, likely in a context-dependent manner in both cell lines that are genetically similar but phenotypically different.

To assess the effect of androgen exposure on NPM1-YAP1 interactions in vitro, we repeated GST-pulldown assays using nuclear extracts obtained from LNCaP and C4-2 cells that were treated with either ethanol (vehicle) or DHT (Figures 2I and 2J). Western blot analysis revealed that androgen attenuated the interaction between NPM1 and the proline-rich (PR) and WW/SH3 domains of recombinant GST-YAP1 fusion peptides in both cell lines compared to vehicle controls (Figures 2I, 2J, and S4C). A weak but detectable interaction was also observed between NPM1 and the carboxy-terminal domain of YAP1 (residues 297–504), regardless of androgen treatment (Figures 1I, 1J, and S4C). These findings reinforce the contextual and domain-specific nature of YAP1-NPM1 interactions in LNCaP and C4-2 cells, which exhibit differential androgen sensitivities.

NPM1 regulates YAP1 expression and activity

To assess the biological functionality of the YAP1-NPM1 interaction, we evaluated the impact of NPM1 activity on YAP1 and MYC. NPM1 is recognized as an upstream regulator of MYC,45,46 while YAP1 may also crosstalk with MYC.47,48 However, the extent to which NPM1 influences YAP1 activity and whether YAP1 is necessary for NPM1-driven MYC expression in prostate cancer cells remains unclear. To address this, we measured YAP1 and MYC transcript levels following NPM1 knockdown using siRNA. NPM1 depletion in LNCaP, C4-2, and PC3 cells significantly reduced YAP1 and MYC mRNA levels compared to scramble control siRNA (Figures 3A–3C and S5A–S5C). This reduction in transcripts was accompanied by decreased YAP1 and MYC protein levels in LNCaP and PC3 cells (Figures 3D and 3E). Additionally, YAP1 knockdown reduced MYC expression without affecting NPM1 mRNA levels across all three cell lines (Figures S5D–S5I). These findings suggest that YAP1 serves as a critical mediator of NPM1-driven MYC regulation. To further clarify the functional relationship between NPM1 and YAP1, we assessed the expression of CCN1, CCN2, and ANKRD1; these are well-established YAP1 target genes.41 Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that silencing NPM1 significantly reduced the expression of the YAP1 target genes in both in LNCaP and PC3 cells compared to the scramble siRNA control (Figures 3F–3H). Notably, NPM1 depletion decreased CCN1, CCN2, and ANKRD1 transcript levels even in the presence of androgen (Figures 3I–3K). These findings align with previous reports that androgens modulate these genes in a YAP1-dependent manner, as androgen failed to restore their expression when YAP1 was depleted.41 Taken together, these results indicate that the dynamic NPM1-YAP1 nuclear complex underlies their functional interaction in both AR-positive and AR-negative cells.

YAP1-NPM1 cooperativity is required for cell growth

To investigate the effects of NPM1 on cell growth, we performed colony-forming assays in LNCaP cells and found that NPM1 knockdown significantly reduced colony numbers compared to the scramble control (Figures 4A and 4B). Building on this observation, we tested the effects of two structurally distinct pharmacological inhibitors of NPM1, NSC348884, and nucleozin, on LNCaP, C4-2, C4-2B, and PC3 cells. To determine how NSC348884 and nucleozin inhibit cell growth, we performed cell cycle analysis after treatment with DMSO, NSC348884, or nucleozin. In LNCaP cells, both inhibitors increased the proportion of cell cycle in the G1 phase, whereas in C4-2 cells they reduced the S and G2/M phases compared to the DMSO mock control (Figures 4E and 4F). This suggests that NPM1 regulates cell cycle differently in LNCaP and C4-2 cells, likely due to their distinct responsive to androgen signaling and YAP1-NPM1 interaction profiles. In PC3 cells, NSC348884 and nucleozin similarly increased G1 enrichment while decreasing S and G2/M phases (Figure 4G). These findings indicate that NPM1 inhibition suppresses cell growth by altering cell cycle progression.

To determine how blocking NPM1 slows prostate cancer cell growth, we first assessed whether small-molecule NPM1 inhibitors interfere with its interaction with YAP1. We used a proximity ligation assay to measure YAP1-NPM1 complexes in LNCaP cells treated with NSC348884 or nucleozin. In cells treated with DMSO, we observed numerous PLA puncta, indicating a strong interaction between the two proteins. When we added either inhibitor, the PLA signal dropped sharply, suggesting that targeting NPM1 weakens its interaction with YAP1 in cells. Next, we examined whether this interaction affects cell growth under conditions in which we independently altered YAP1 and NPM1 levels. Transient YAP1 expression led to greater cell growth than the control, consistent with its known role in promoting proliferation. However, this effect disappeared when NPM1 was silenced, and reducing NPM1 alone also lowered cell growth. These results show that NPM1 is not only a binding partner of YAP1 but also required for YAP1 to promote cell growth.

NPM1 and YAP1 expressions correlate with prostate cancer progression

To establish the physiological and translational relevance of the NPM1-YAP1 interaction in culture, we analyzed the publicly available TCGA-prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) dataset (n = 494), a male as sex variable. NPM1 transcripts were significantly elevated in primary prostate cancer specimens compared to normal prostate tissues (Figure S6A), and notably higher NPM1 expression correlated with disease progression (Figure S6B). Additionally, NPM1 and YAP1 transcripts showed a strong positive correlation in prostate cancer specimens (Figure S6C). Both NPM1 and YAP1 also correlated positively with MYC transcripts in prostate tumor tissues (Figures S6D and S6E), indicating that the functional interplay among NPM1, YAP1, and MYC is clinically relevant and likely play a critical role in prostate cancer biology.

To experimentally validate the above observations, we conducted immunohistochemical analysis of NPM1 and YAP1 proteins in prostate cancer tissue samples (n = 26) with varying Gleason grades and in non-cancerous prostate tissue sections. This analysis revealed a significant increase in NPM1 immunoreactivity in tumors relative to normal tissues (Figure 5A). YAP1 immunoreactivity also increased in tumor samples; however, the increase was not statistically significant compared to normal tissues, likely due to variability in its expression among limited samples (Figure 5B). However, both proteins showed significantly robust expression in high-grade prostate adenocarcinoma (GS > 7) compared to low-grade (GS ≤ 7) tumor samples (Figures 5C and 5D). NPM1-YAP1 interaction contributes to invasive cancer cell behaviors

Cell migration is essential for various cellular processes, such as tissue homeostasis, wound healing, immune responses, fibrosis, and cancer cell metastasis.50 Recent studies have shown that NPM1 regulates cell migration in solid tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma and bladder cancer.51,52 while YAP1 activity is also critical for cell migration and invasion.14 Data presented in Figures 3, 4, and 5 demonstrate that the cooperative interaction between NPM1 and YAP1 is critical cell growth and tumor progression. To assess the role of NPM1 in prostate cancer cell migration, we performed scratch wound assays using AR-positive LNCaP or AR-negative PC3 cells. To further clarify the role of NPM1 in cell migration and determine whether its silencing alters cell motility in response to epidermal growth factor (EGF) under three-dimensional culture conditions, we performed Transwell migration assays using collagen-coated porous membranes that mimic the sub-basal matrix. EGF, a potent inducer of cell migration and cytoskeletal organization, facilitates cellular processes, such as wound healing and tumor invasion.53 The results show that NPM1 depletion significantly reduced the migration ability of LNCaP and PC3 cells in collagen-coated Boyden chambers, even in the presence of EGF (Figure 6B). These findings indicate that NPM1 activity is essential for cell migration in response to external stimuli.

The data indicate that the NPM1-YAP1 interaction is critical for regulating cell fate, particularly cell growth and motility, which are essential for cancer progression. To examine the presence and pattern of spatial NPM1-YAP1 interactions within the complex tumor microenvironment, we performed proximity ligation assays on low-grade (GS < 7) and high-grade (GS > 7) prostate cancer tissue sections (n = 8, Figures 6D and 5E). High-grade tissues exhibited a significantly stronger NPM1-YAP1 interaction, with marked increases in both the number and intensity of interaction compared to low-grade tissues (Figures 5D and 5E). Notably, the interactions occurred primarily within the cell nuclei, consistent with observations in cell culture. These findings highlight the physiological significance of NPM1-YAP1 interactions that may contribute to invasive tumor progression and represent potential therapeutic targets.

Discussion

Prostate cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, yet the molecular circuits driving its progression are incompletely defined.54,55 In this study, we identify previously unrecognized and dynamically regulated interactions between YAP1 and NPM1 in prostate cancer cells. Our findings establish the YAP1-NPM1 axis as a critical regulatory module within the Hippo signaling pathway (Figure 6F). Using multiple complementary experimental approaches, we demonstrate that androgens modulate YAP1-NPM1 complex formation in androgen-responsive cells, whereas in AR-negative cells this interaction occurs independently of androgen signaling. We further uncover a regulatory mechanism in which DNA and RNA regulate YAP1-NPM1 interaction dynamics, indicating that a multilayered regulatory network governs this interaction downstream of, or parallel to, androgen signaling. Our study reveals that YAP1 is a key mediator of NPM1-dependent MYC expression, and that NPM1 appears to be required for YAP1-driven cell growth. Consistently, elevated expression and enhanced interaction of YAP1 and NPM1 are associated with prostate cancer progression, indicating the clinical relevance of this axis. Mechanistically, NPM1 interacts with distinct regions of YAP1 in a context-dependent manner; this may be critical for the modulation of YAP1-driven transcriptional programs and cell growth by NPM1. Collectively, these findings position the YAP1-NPM1 axis as a key regulator of invasive cancer growth and highlight its potential as a therapeutic vulnerability in prostate cancer.

Our findings indicate that pharmacological inhibition of androgen signaling alters the YAP1-NPM1 interaction, suggesting that AR regulates both the formation and functional output of the YAP1-NPM1 complex, including oncogenic transcriptional programs, such as MYC expression. Domain-mapping analyses further demonstrate that NPM1 engages distinct regions of YAP1 in AR-positive cells in an androgen-dependent manner: NPM1 preferentially binds the proline-rich domain of YAP1 in androgen-responsive cells, whereas interaction through the WW/SH3 domain predominates in androgen-insensitive cells. This context-dependent binding underlines the adaptability of the YAP1-NPM1 interaction and may contribute to cellular and molecular heterogeneity in prostate cancer. These findings are consistent with our previous observation that SCML2 and AR interact with YAP1 via the WW/SH3 domain downstream of the Hippo pathway,14 supporting the existence of a broader signaling network integrating androgen and Hippo signaling. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which AR and SCML2 regulate YAP1-NPM1 interactions and functions downstream of androgen signaling,56,57 and to determine whether therapeutic disruption of this axis can suppress invasive growth and therapy resistance in advanced prostate cancer.

Our study also reveals a previously uncharacterized regulatory mechanism in which DNA and RNA differentially regulate YAP1-NPM1 interactions within chromatin-associated complexes. We showed that DNase I treatment disrupts the YAP1-NPM1 association, indicating that chromatin integrity is essential for their interaction. In contrast, RNase treatment enhances YAP1-NPM1 binding, suggesting that RNA acts as a competitive inhibitor of YAP1 binding to NPM1. This observation is consistent with the established role of NPM1 as an RNA-binding protein possessing multiple RNA-interaction domains,58 as well as evidence showing that YAP1-containing protein complexes include several RNA-binding proteins.39 Occupation of NPM1 binding sites by RNA molecules may limit its interaction with YAP1, whereas RNA removal frees these sites, thereby promoting increased YAP1-NPM1 association. Collectively, these findings indicate that RNA actively modulates protein-protein interactions, adding a dynamic regulatory layer to chromatin-associated signaling pathways. Our data support a model in which chromatin and RNA cooperatively shape YAP1-NPM1 interactions and downstream transcriptional networks.59 These insights advance our understanding of how nucleic acids coordinate protein interaction networks and regulate gene expression. Future studies are warranted to further investigate these mechanisms in aggressive cancers and to evaluate their therapeutic potential.

We have demonstrated that disruption of NPM1 impairs the growth and migration abilities of prostate cancer cells. Nucleozin, an indirect NPM1 inhibitor, significantly reduces cell proliferation. In contrast to some conflicting reports, the present data confirm that NSC348884, a direct NPM1 inhibitor, effectively disrupts NPM1 function.60,61 Mechanistically, NSC348884 likely prevents NPM1 oligomerization, a process essential for the biological activity of NPM1. This inhibition activates apoptotic pathways and inactivates survival pathways, both of which are regulated by NPM1.62 Consistent with this mechanism, NSC348884 reduced the growth of LNCaP, C4-2, C4-2B, and PC3 cells in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, its efficacy correlated with NPM1 and YAP1 protein levels. IC50 values were in the nanomolar range for LNCaP and PC3 cells, which exhibit high NPM1 and YAP1 expression, and in the micromolar range for C4-2 and C4-2B cells, which have lower NPM1 and YAP1 expression. This differential sensitivity may reflect variations in NPM1-YAP1 interactions under androgen-regulated versus dysregulated AR signaling. Both NSC348884 and nucleozin inhibited the NPM1-YAP1 interaction, indicating that disruption of this axis underlies their growth-suppressive effects. These findings provide a rationale for developing targeted therapies against the NPM1-YAP1 axis in prostate cancer and other malignancies, as both proteins play prominent roles in the pathobiology of various cancer types across multiple organ sites.

Moreover, NPM1 is a critical regulator of cell migration, and its dysregulation is strongly associated with tumorigenesis and progression.63,64,65 Elevated NPM1 expression correlates with increased proliferation, oncogenic transformation, and aggressive tumor growth. Mechanistically, NPM1 promotes oncogenesis through interactions with proteins involved in proliferation and survival, notably by regulating MYC.45,46 Our findings uncover YAP1 a critical regulator of NPM1-driven MYC expression, although the precise mechanism underlying this regulation remains to be fully elucidated. Previous studies have shown that mutant NPM1 enhances cell adhesion, migration, and invasion in colon cancer and promotes survival through ERK/MAPK activation,66 pathways that also intersect with YAP1 signaling.67 In addition, NPM1 is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to aggressive solid tumors, where it promotes drug resistance in hypoxic liver cancer,68 enhances migration in chemoresistant bladder cancer,69 and drives proliferation in KRAS-mutant lung cancer.70 Similarly, elevated YAP1 functions as a pivotal oncogene across multiple contexts, such as sustaining tumor growth in KRAS-driven pancreatic cancer71,72 and mediating resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors,73 including facilitating amino acid uptake, morphological adaptation, and survival in KRAS-associated colorectal cancer.74 Together, these findings suggest that NPM1 and YAP1 converge on shared oncogenic pathways and cooperate to sustain tumor growth. Their functional interconnection positions both proteins as promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cancer, particularly in prostate cancer.

Furthermore, growing evidence demonstrates that NPM1 and YAP1 play key roles in the interaction between cancer and immune cells, contributing to aggressive tumor behavior.75 NPM1 is implicated as a prognostic biomarker for immune cell infiltration in lung cancer and regulates m6A RNA modification and glycolysis.76 NPM1 could promote PD-L1 expression and suppresses T cell activity in triple-negative breast cancer38 and increases PD-L1 expression in gastric cancer through its interaction with the PD-L1 promoter.77 YAP1 is also associated with PD-L1 expression, affects survival in small-cell lung cancer,78 and drives immune evasion in BRAF inhibitor-resistant melanoma.79 These findings highlight the immunoregulatory functions of both proteins across multiple cancers. Here, we showed that NPM1 regulate YAP1 expression and activates its downstream targets. This suggests that the NPM1-YAP1 axis contributes to cancer progression by influencing both oncogenic gene expression and modeling tumor microenvironment. Further research warrants to determine whether this axis promotes metastasis by modulating immune cell infiltration in prostate cancer, especially regarding androgen signaling and Hippo pathway regulation. Collectively, these insights identify NPM1-YAP1 as a central link between oncogenic and immunoregulatory pathways and offer new therapeutic opportunities for advanced cancer.

In conclusion, we identify the YAP1-NPM1 axis as an androgen-responsive driver of oncogenic transcription in prostate cancer. Pharmacological disruption of NPM1 impairs YAP1 activity, decreases MYC expression, and suppresses malignant phenotypes, revealing a previously unrecognized therapeutic vulnerability within protein-protein interaction networks. Targeting NPM1 or the YAP1-NPM1 interface may therefore complement established androgen-directed therapies and has the potential to delay disease progression and therapy resistance. Although further mechanistic and in vivo studies are required, our findings highlight the YAP1-NPM1 axis as a promising translational target, particularly in combination with AR pathway inhibitors or emerging YAP1-directed therapies.

Limitations of the study

Although the data indicate a biochemical and functional interaction between YAP1 and NPM1 that promotes proliferative and invasive phenotypes, these findings must be interpreted with caution due to the limitations. First, while androgen signaling appears to modulate the NPM1-YAP1 interaction in AR-positive cells, the upstream signals and intermediary factors responsible for this crosstalk have not been identified. Second, the precise mechanism underlying NPM1 and YAP1 cooperativity remains unclear, as YAP1 chromatin occupancy and TEAD engagement were not directly assessed in the absence of NPM1. Third, although two structurally distinct NPM1 inhibitors yielded similar effects, the potential for off-target activity cannot be excluded; genetic rescue experiments and more selective probes are needed to confirm specificity. Fourth, the in vivo relevance of this axis has not been established, and it is not yet known whether NPM1 inhibition suppresses YAP1-dependent tumor progression in animal models. Finally, broader validation across diverse preclinical systems and patient cohorts is necessary to determine the generalizability, pharmacology, safety, and translational feasibility of targeting the NPM1-YAP1 interaction.

LNCaP (Cat# CRL-1740), C4-2 (Cat# CRL-3314), C4-2B (Cat# CRL-3315), 22Rv1 (Cat# CRL-2505), and PC3 (Cat# CRL-1435) cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The ARCaP cell line is available in the laboratory. The generation of the C4-2/HA-STK4/MST1 cell line has been previously described.56 All cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (VWR, Cat# 45000-396) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Fisher Scientific, Cat# FB12999102) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. All cell lines tested by commercial laboratory and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

Protein extraction and analysis

Total proteins were isolated from cells using ice-cold cell lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, and cocktails of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Calbiochem, Cat# 539131)) as previously described.14,41,81 Cells were washed three times with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed for 45 minutes on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C. Supernatants were collected and stored at -80°C for subsequent analysis. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce Fast Gold BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat# A53227) and a BioTek Synergy H1 spectrometer. Proteins were separated on 8-10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto a 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membrane using the Nitrocellulose Transfer Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Cat# 1704270) and Trans-Blot Turbo RTA Mini (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Membranes were incubated in blocking buffer (PBS-T containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 5% (w/v) non-fat milk) for 1 hour at room temperature with constant shaking, then washed three times with washing buffer (PBS-T or PBS with 0.1% Tween-20) for 5 minutes each. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against NPM1 (Cell Signaling Technology (CST), Cat# 3542, 1:1000), YAP1 (CST, Cat# 12395 and Cat# 14074, 1:1000), AR (CST, Cat# 5153, 1:1000), β-actin (CST, Cat# 3700, 1:3000), or GAPDH (CST, Cat# 5174, 1:2000) at 4°C overnight, followed by three washes for 5 minutes each. Membranes were then incubated with an isogenic secondary antibody conjugated to HRP anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG (CST, Cat# 7076, Cat# 7074, 1:2000) for 1 hour at room temperature and washed three times for 5 minutes each. Antibodies used in this study were listed in key resources table. Protein signals were visualized using Luminata Forte Western HRP substrate (Millipore Sigma, Cat# WBLUFO500) and Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging (Cat# 12003154).

RNA isolation and real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells at 60-70% confluency using the RNA isolation kit (Zymo Research, Cat# R1054) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Gene expression was evaluated using the GoTaq 1-Step RT-qPCR System (Promega, Inc. Cat# A6020) according to the provided protocol. For each qRT-PCR reaction, 0.1 μg of total RNA and 100 nM primers were used. The RT-quantitative PCR program included 15 minutes at 37°C for reverse transcription, 10 minutes at 95°C for inactivation of the reverse transcriptase, 10 seconds at 95°C for denaturation, 30 seconds at 60°C for annealing, and 30 seconds at 72°C for extension, repeated for 40 cycles. Transcript levels were quantified using the 2-ΔCt method and normalized to 18S ribosomal RNA,14,41 which served as the internal control. Forward and reverse primer sequences used in qPCR provided in Table S1. All qPCR reactions were performed using the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Immunofluorescence and microscopy

Cells (2 x 10ˆ4) were plated in Falcon 8-chamber slides with a glass bottom (Corning, Cat# 354108). After gentle washing with 1X PBS at room temperature, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15–20 minutes, followed by three washes with 1X PBS. Fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 minutes and washed as previously described. Permeabilized cells were blocked for 1 hour at room temperature using a blocking buffer containing 2% IgG-free bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS, followed by additional PBS washes. Cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against NPM1 (CST, Cat# 3542, 1:1000) and YAP1 (CST, Cat#12395 or 14074, 1:1000), all diluted in blocking buffer. Slides were washed three times in PBS before incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. The secondary antibodies used were Goat anti-Rabbit conjugated to Alexa Fluor™ Plus 647 (Thermo Scientific, Cat# A32733; 1:500) and Goat anti-Mouse conjugated to Alexa Fluor™ Plus 488 (Thermo Scientific, Cat# A32723; 1:500), with dilutions prepared in 1X PBS. After three additional PBS washes, cells were mounted with DAPI and Prolong Gold Antifade reagent (CST, Cat# 89615). Slides were incubated for 2 hours at room temperature in the dark before sealing the edges with nail polish. Confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 700) at 40x or 63x oil-immersion magnification was used to detect NPM1 and YAP1 protein signals.

Coimmunoprecipitation assay

Twenty-four hours following seeding and serum depletion, LNCaP and C4-2 cells were treated overnight with either vehicle (0.01% ethanol, EtOH) or dihydrotestosterone (DHT, 10 nM) in 5% DCC-serum (androgen-depleted, dextran-coated charcoal-stripped fetal bovine serum). After treatment, cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS, lysed, and centrifuged as described in the protein analysis section. Supernatants were incubated overnight at 4°C with either anti-YAP1 (Mouse 1A12, CST, Cat# 12395, 1:200) or anti-NPM1 (Rabbit, CST, Cat# 3542, 1:200) antibodies. A 50% Protein A/G-Agarose slurry (Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose: sc-2003) equilibrated with lysis buffer was subsequently added and incubated for 1 hour at 4°C. The resulting antigen-antibody-protein A/G-agarose complexes were centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 1 minute and washed three times with iced-cold lysis buffer. Samples were boiled with 30 μl of 2X sample buffer for 5 minutes at 95°C. For input controls, 20 μg of protein was resuspended in 4X sample buffer and boiled at 95°C for 5 minutes. All samples were loaded onto an 8–10% SDS-PAGE gel. The presence of NPM1 in the YAP1 immune complex, or vice versa, was analyzed by western blotting as described in the protein analysis section.

Proximity ligation assay

The Duolink proximity ligation assay (PLA), in combination with confocal microscopy, was employed to assess interactions between NPM1 and YAP1 in vitro and in vivo. The PLA was conducted according to the manufacturer's protocol (Millipore Sigma, Cat# DUO92101-1KT) and published protocol.39 In brief, 2 × 10ˆ4 cells were seeded in Falcon 8-chamber glass-bottom slides (Corning, Cat# 354108). Following androgen depletion, cells were treated with vehicle (ethanol, EtOH), dihydrotestosterone (DHT, 10 nM), or DHT plus enzalutamide (20 μM) in 5% DCC-FBS overnight (16–18 hours). Cells were washed three times with PBS for 5 minutes each, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15–20 minutes, and washed again with PBS. Permeabilization was performed with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 minutes, followed by PBS washes. Blocking was performed with 1X blocking solution (supplied in the kit) for 1 hour at 37 °C in a humidified chamber (ACD HybEZ II Hybridization System, Cat# 321710 and Cat# 321720). After blocking, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-YAP1 (mouse, 1:300, CST, Cat# 12395) and anti-NPM1 (CST, Cat# 3542, 1:300), diluted in antibody dilution buffer (supplied in the kit). Cells were washed twice in 1X Buffer A (supplied in the kit) for 5 minutes each, then incubated with PLA probes for 1 hour at 37 °C in the humidified chamber, followed by two additional washes with 1X Buffer A for 5 minutes each. Probe ligation was performed for 30 minutes at 37 °C in a humidified chamber, and samples were washed twice with 1X Buffer A for 5 minutes each. Slides were incubated with amplification solution for 1 hour and 40 minutes at room temperature, followed by two 10-minute washes with 1X Buffer B (supplied in the kit) at room temperature, and a final 1-minute wash with 0.01X Buffer B. After drying for 1 hour, coverslips were mounted using mounting media containing DAPI (4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride; Millipore Sigma, Cat# DUO82040) for 30 to 60 minutes. Slides were sealed and stored at -20°C for up to 1 year. Images were acquired using confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 700) at 40x or 60x oil-immersion magnification.

GST-pulldown assay

Plasmids encoding pGEX2-GST-vector, pGEX2-GST-YAP1-WT, and truncation mutants of GST-YAP1 (2-150), (151-296), and (297-504) were previously described.14 Expression of GST-vector and GST-YAP1 fusion peptides was induced in BL-21Lys bacteria (Millipore Sigma, Cat# CMC0015) by the addition of 1 mM IPTG for 4 hours at 37 °C. Recombinant proteins were solubilized in NETN buffer (1% NP-40, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA). GST fusion peptides were purified by affinity chromatography using Magnetic Glutathione-Sepharose beads (Millipore Sigma, Cat# G0924), and eluted peptides were stored in PBS at 4 °C until use. Recombinant GST control or GST-YAP1 mutant peptides were incubated with total lysates from LNCaP cells for 6 hours at 4 °C with constant rotation. LNCaP cell lysates served as the source of NPM1. After extensive washing to remove unbound proteins, bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by 10% SDS–PAGE. Coomassie blue staining was used to visualize GST-only or GST-YAP1 fusion peptides, and Western blotting was performed to detect bound NPM1 protein.

RNAi gene silencing

ON-TARGET small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting NPM1, YAP1, and AR were obtained from Horizon Discovery (Cat# L-015737-00-0005, L-012200-00-0005, and L-003400-00-0005, respectively). Small interfering RNA (siRNA) used in this study were listed in Table S1. Cells (2 × 10ˆ5) were seeded in six-well plates and incubated overnight. Transfection was performed at 50–60% confluence using 50 nM gene-specific siRNA in Opti-MEM reduced serum medium (Gibco™, Cat# 51985034) with DharmaFect-3 transfection reagent, according to the manufacturer's protocol (Horizon Discovery, Cat# T-2003-01). Expression levels of NPM1, YAP1, and AR were evaluated by Western blotting 72 hours after transfection with either scramble or gene-specific siRNA.

Colony formation assay

LNCaP and C4-2 cells were seeded at 1,000 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 10 days. The medium was replaced every third day.82 After incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15-20 min, followed by three washes with 1X PBS, staining with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 minutes, and then thoroughly washing with tap water. Colony formation efficiency was determined by imaging each well and counting colonies containing more than 50 cells. Dyes from the cell colonies were subsequently eluted in 10% acetic acid, and absorbance was measured at 590 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader.

Cell growth assay

Cell growth was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay system according to the manufacturer's protocol (Dojindo, Cat# CK04). Cells were seeded in triplicate in 96-well plates and transfected with either mock or NPM1 siRNA for 24 hours. Seventy-two hours after transfection, CCK-8 reagent was added for up to 4 hours to assess cell proliferation. Absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader.

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle analysis was performed using the Cell Cycle Analysis Kit (Millipore Sigma, Cat# MAK344) following the manufacturer’s protocol. LNCaP, C4-2, C4-2B, and PC3 cells (5 × 10ˆ5 per well) were seeded in 6-well plates in triplicate. For cell cycle synchronization, cells were incubated in culture medium containing 0.1% FBS for 24 hours. Subsequently, cells were treated with 1 μM NSC348884 in culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS for 24 hours; control cells were incubated with DMSO control in culture medium with 10% FBS. Cells were harvested and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 minutes. After removal of the supernatant, cells were washed with 2 mL of ice-cold 1× Cell Cycle Assay Buffer (provided in the kit). Fixation was achieved by adding 2 mL ice-cold 70% ethanol dropwise while vortexing. Fixed cells were stored at -20°C in 70% ethanol. Following fixation, cells were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded. Cells were washed again in 1× Cell Cycle Assay Buffer and centrifuged. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of Staining Solution (provided in the kit) and protected from light. The cells were incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes to allow nucleic acid staining prior to analysis. Flow cytometry was conducted using a BD Accuri C6 Plus Flow Cytometer.

Immunohistochemistry

A prostate tumor tissue microarray (TMA) was purchased from TissueArray.com (formerly known as US Biomax). The study uses male as a sex variable because prostate cancer is a male-specific disease. Tissue analyses adhered to the IRB-approved protocol. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using the IHC Application Solutions Kit (Rabbit) according to the manufacturer's instructions (CST, Cat# 13079). Paraffin-embedded tissue sections underwent sequential rehydration in xylene (three washes, 5 minutes each), 100% ethanol (two washes, 10 minutes each), 95% ethanol (two washes, 10 minutes each), and MQ-H2O (two washes, 5 minutes each). Antigen retrieval was conducted using citrate buffer (pH 6.0), and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Non-specific binding was minimized by incubating TMA slides in blocking buffer as specified by the manufacturer (CST). Slides were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against NPM1 (CST, Cat# 3542, dilution 1:200) and YAP1 (dilution 1:300), followed by washing with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were treated with the signal boost solution for 20 minutes as specified by the manufacturer (CST), then washed three times with PBST for 5 minutes each. Protein expression was detected using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen. Counterstaining was performed with 2% hematoxylin, and sections were mounted with coverslips. Stained slides were scanned using the Aperio VERSA system (Leica Biosystems) at 40x magnification under a light microscope.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration assay was performed using 24-well Transwell inserts with 8 μm pores. The inserts were coated with 50 μL of rat tail collagen (50 mg/mL; Millipore Sigma, Cat# 122-20) diluted 1:4. PC3 cells were seeded at 3 × 10ˆ4 cells per insert, and LNCaP cells at 5 × 10ˆ4 cells per insert, in RPMI 1640 medium containing 0.2% BSA. Epidermal growth factor (EGF; 10 ng/mL) was used as a chemoattractant for both cell lines. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 5 hours for PC3 cells and 24 hours for LNCaP cells. Non-migrated cells in the inner chamber were removed with a cotton swab, while migrated cells in the outer chamber were fixed and stained with 3 ng/mL DAPI. Images of five non-overlapping fields per insert were captured using an Axiovert 200 M microscope (Carl Zeiss) at 10x magnification. The number of stained nuclei was quantified automatically using ZEN (blue edition) microscopy software (Carl Zeiss). The migration index was calculated as the average number of cells per field for the test condition divided by the average number of cells per field for the medium control.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Image quantification

Python codes deposited in GitHub (see Code Availability and key resources table) were developed to quantify the immunoreactivity of NPM1 (n=26) and YAP1 (n=26) protein in primary prostate cancer tissues in TMA format. TMA core images at 40x magnification were selected from whole-slide scans, excluding those with visual defects that could compromise image-based quantification. Defects such as air bubbles and sample detachment were rare. Images were circularly cropped to eliminate artifacts outside the tissue cores. Only stained pixels within the TMA core images, identified as brown regions, were included in the analysis. Four samples (two normal and two tumor) were randomly reserved from the original dataset for image calibration. Images were converted to the hue, saturation, value (HSV) color space according to the OpenCV library (RRID:SCR_015526). Thresholds for hue and saturation were applied to select stained pixels across all samples. The optimal threshold was determined through a grid search that maximized the distinction between tumor and non-tumor regions. Calibration samples were excluded from further analysis to avoid bias. After selecting stained pixels, their values were converted to grayscale, and these grayscale values were used to quantify the signal intensities of NPM1 and YAP1. The average brightness of the extracted core for each sample was calculated from the selected stained pixels. Signal intensity for NPM1 and YAP1 was represented as a proxy for pixel darkness, calculated by subtracting the average brightness from 255, the maximum possible brightness. Samples were categorized as normal or tumor to assess differences in NPM1 and YAP1 signal intensity between tissue types. To further evaluate differences in NPM1 and YAP1 within tumor samples based on Gleason grade or Gleason score (GS), samples were grouped according to GS values: GS ≤ 7 versus GS > 7. Both GS analyses demonstrated a statistically significant (p-value ≤ 0.05) correlation for the defined signal intensity.

Data analysis

Cell cycle analysis was performed using FlowJo software (RRID: SCR_008520). Protein network construction was constructed with the GeneMania web portal (RRID: SCR_003070).83 The TNMPlot84 and the TIMER85 web tools were used to conducted computational analysis of the Cancer Genome Atlas-Prostate Cancer Adenocarcinoma dataset (RRID: SCR_014555). ImageJ (RRID: SCR_003070) was employed to quantify Western blots and PLA foci. IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism (RRID: SCR_002798). Information on datatype-specific data and code repositories is available in the Resources Availability and key resources table. Statistical significance between control and experimental groups was assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test performed in Microsoft Excel 365 or GraphPad Prism (v10). A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are representative of two to three independent experiments performed in duplicate or triplicate depending on the experiment and are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Also, data specific statistical details of experiments, including the statistical test used, can be found in the figure legends.
