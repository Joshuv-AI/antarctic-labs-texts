Dyspnea in Chemical War Victims Exposed to Sulfur Mustard: A Narrative Review of Respiratory Distress in Survivors
Authors: Mahboobeh Ghasemzadeh Rahbardar, Shakeeb H Moosavi, Mohammad Hossein Boskabady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDyspnea, a prevalent symptom among victims of chemical warfare exposed to sulfur mustard, presents substantial physiological and pharmacological challenges. Inhaling sulfur mustard in warfare may result in significant respiratory distress, emphasizing the necessity of investigating the underlying processes and treatment options for dyspnea in this population.MethodsA comprehensive search was performed on Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus with no time constraints, including keywords such as “chemical war victims”, “veterans”, “respiratory distress”, “dyspnea”, and “sulfur mustard”. Relevant articles were reviewed to collect information about dyspnea in chemical war victims.ResultsVarious studies reported the dyspnea due to exposure to sulfur mustard. Dyspnea in chemical war victims is triggered by physiological mechanisms such as airway inflammation, pulmonary edema, and oxidative stress. Pharmacological interventions including bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and oxygen therapy play an essential role in controlling dyspnea. These therapies have been found to be beneficial in reducing respiratory distress and improving the quality of life in affected individuals.ConclusionDyspnea in chemical warfare victims exposed to sulfur mustard is a complex condition that requires a multidisciplinary approach for optimal management. Understanding the physiological mechanisms that underlying dyspnea and applying appropriate pharmacological therapies are essential to achieving better respiratory outcomes in this patient population. Continued research and collaborative efforts are critical to provide care and support for people suffering from dyspnea as a result of sulfur mustard exposure.Keywords: chemical warfare, inflammation, mustard gas, oxygen, pulmonary edema, respiratory distress syndrome

Introduction

Mustard gas, also referred to as sulfur mustard is an alkylating chemical that can harm people both temporarily and permanently [1]. This particular class of highly poisonous vesicant compounds has been employed in chemical warfare. Sulfur mustard affects the skin, eyes, and respiratory system, among other organs [2, 3]. The amount and length of exposure determine the extent of the harm [1, 4]. Its lipophilic nature allows it to rapidly enter target tissues, alkylating proteins and binding to lipids and nucleic acids, leading to cytotoxicity and deoxyribonucleic acid damage, particularly in the lungs. Exposure to sulfur mustard in the lung results in acute consequences such as inflammation, airway obstruction, and bronchial tissue damage, manifesting as pharyngitis, laryngitis, chest tightness, hacking cough, edema, and rhinorrhea. Spirometric tests often reveal patterns of restrictive, obstructive, or mixed lung diseases. Crucially, the long‐term respiratory consequences include chronic bronchiolitis, lung fibrosis, airway hyper‐responsiveness, and recurrent pneumonia, with pulmonary function tests frequently showing more prominent obstructive patterns [5].

Dyspnea is a hallmark symptom among individuals with sulfur mustard‐induced respiratory disorders, significantly impacting their quality of life. Understanding the etiology and effective management strategies for dyspnea in individuals exposed to sulfur mustard during warfare is vital in providing effective care and improving outcomes for affected individuals. However, despite the long‐term sequelae of sulfur mustard exposure, a comprehensive and updated synthesis of the specific challenges posed by dyspnea and the current landscape of its management remains a critical need in the literature.

Existing reviews often focus on the broader spectrum of pulmonary complications, leaving a specific gap in detailed exploration of the persistent dyspnea experienced by survivors and the complexities of their therapeutic management. This review aims to fill that gap by specifically evaluating the physiological mechanisms underlying dyspnea in chemical war victims and critically evaluating the current evidence‐based therapeutic interventions. By providing a focused analysis of these aspects, this work aims to provide a more refined understanding and practical guidance for clinicians managing this unique patient population, thereby highlighting its specific contribution and novelty.

Methods

To gather relevant information, a systematic search was conducted on Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus without time limitations up to May 2026. The search terms employed included “chemical war victims”, “veterans”, “chemical warfare”, “dyspnea”, and “sulfur mustard”. A total of 177 articles were initially identified through this search process. Among these, 29 publications were included in the review process after a careful screening procedure. The exclusion criteria involved eliminating 148 articles due to duplication, non‐English language, or lack of relevance to the study topic.

Dyspnea and Its Underlying Mechanisms

Dyspnea is a subjective sensation of breathing difficulty characterized by multiple physical sensations with different degrees of severity. There are different types of dyspnea, and each one has three main characteristics: tightness, work, and air hunger. When pulmonary ventilation is inadequate, “air hunger” occurs. Breathing requires what is called “work” when it becomes more difficult because of high‐rate minute ventilation, inspiration resistance, or weakening of the respiratory muscles. One characteristic of bronchoconstriction, as in early asthma, is “tightness”. Dyspnea is a prevalent and disabling symptom in respiratory disorders and may also occur in other medical conditions [6, 7]. Since the peripheral and central nervous systems functions are interrelated the underlying mechanisms of dyspnea remain poorly understood. Below is a concise overview of the key mechanisms involved.

Chemoreceptors are essential sensors that inform the body to changes in respiratory function. They are sensitive to alterations in blood gases such as hypercapnia and hypoxia [8]. In addition, vagal receptors, chest wall receptors, and upper airway receptors constitute a sophisticated network that detects differences in respiratory mechanics, providing essential feedback to the central nervous system [6]. Moreover, several brain regions, including the insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and amygdala, process dyspnea by integrating sensory and affective components, the overall sense of breathlessness [6, 8].

Psychological factors further modulate dyspnea perception. Conditions such as anxiety and depression can intensify breathlessness through interactions between emotional and physiological pathways [9, 10]. Additionally, theories like the motor command theory and the mismatch theory of dyspnea provide important insights into the mechanisms that underpin dyspnea sensations. According to the motor command theory, dyspnea can be triggered by conscious awareness of increased central motor output during breathing, whereas the mismatch theory proposes that dyspnea is caused by a perceived difference between efferent motor command and afferent sensory feedback [6, 7]. Understanding these pathways supports the development of more effective clinical approaches for evaluating and managing dyspnea.

Sulfur Mustard and Dyspnea

Ten years after the first exposure to sulfur mustard gas in 1986, 197 veterans participated in a cross‐sectional clinical study at a university hospital to examine the late pulmonary effects of the gas exposure. At first, these veterans had acute respiratory symptoms, such as coughing, chest tightness, hoarseness, sore throat, rhinorrhea, and dyspnea. In addition to 86 non‐exposed veterans serving as a control group, patients without a history of exposure to any other lung‐affecting substances were also included. In 24 probable cases of pulmonary fibrosis, high‐resolution computed tomography scans of the chest, bronchoscopy, transbronchial lung biopsies, and pulmonary function tests were performed as part of the investigation. The findings showed that the veterans exposed to sulfur mustard developed varying percentages of asthma, chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis, airway narrowing due to tissue damage, and pulmonary fibrosis. This contrasts with the relative absence of these conditions in the control group, with the exception of one case of chronic bronchitis [11]. This study emphasizes the possibility of chronic lung problems after short‐term exposure to sulfur mustard.

An analysis of 220 Iraq–Iran War (1981–1989) victims in Babol, Iran, from 1994 to 1998 aimed to determine the long‐term effects on the population's respiratory systems. The victims almost all complained of cough, dyspnea, and suffocation; in six cases, hemoptysis was noted. Two‐thirds of patients, had coarse rales and wheezing, and four had respiratory distress with accessory muscle usage. While most radiographic results were normal, spirometry consistently revealed an obstructive pattern in all patients, and pulmonary function tests mostly revealed restricted and typical patterns. The study found that chronic obstructive lung disease, which can present as a variety of obstructive involvement, was the most frequent effect of chemical weapons on the respiratory system. The majority of patients remembered being exposed to mustard gas, but some were unsure of the precise type of gas they had been exposed to. The short‐ to long‐term delay between exposure and assessment varied from 7 to 13 years [12].

The goal of another research was to investigate the high‐resolution computed tomography scans results in individuals who had previously been exposed to sulfur mustard and had symptoms including coughing and/or shortness of breath. Eighty percent of 50 patients, 80% had chest X‐ray abnormalities and all of the patients had high‐resolution computed tomography abnormalities. Bronchial wall thickening, interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, and emphysema, were the main results of the high‐resolution computed tomography. Clinical severity and high‐resolution computed tomography scores for bronchiectasis, bronchitis, and interstitial lung disease showed significant associations, but not for emphysema. In 20% of cases, high‐resolution computed tomography indicated abnormalities not found on chest X‐ray [13]. According to the study, exposure to sulfur mustard can often result in bronchial wall thickening, interstitial lung disease, and emphysema. This supports high‐resolution computed tomography as the primary imaging modality for chemical warfare injuries.

The effects of sulfur mustard exposure on the respiratory system in people with subclinical or low exposure levels were investigated. A sample of 77 veterans with respiratory problems out of 200 cases was analyzed. The results of high‐resolution computed tomography scans showed a variety of abnormalities; 38.23% had no defects, while others had thickening of the septal wall, bronchiectasis, and air trapping. In 44% of instances, reports of cough, phlegm, wheezing, and dyspnea did not exhibit any apparent seasonal variation; in 26.5% of cases, symptoms worsened in the winter, and in 26.5% in the summer [14] (Table 1). According to the study, bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome and exposure to chemical warfare agents are related. Cough, phlegm, wheezing, and dyspnea were among the persistent symptoms, with differing seasonal effects. For potentially life‐saving interventions, early diagnosis in both civilian and military populations without acute symptoms is emphasized.

A variety of tests and examinations were carried out in a study that focused on severely sulfur mustard‐poisoned veterans from Khorasan, Iran, 16–20 years after exposure. The lungs accounted for 95% of the prevalent problems among the patients. There were several respiratory problems, with symptoms such as expectoration, coughing, dyspnea (85%), and hemoptysis (60%) being common. The clinical findings included stridor, crackles, and wheezing. Test results for pulmonary function showed obstructive, restrictive, mixed, and normal hypoxemia, varying in severity. With particular distributions of bronchiectatic lesions, high‐resolution computed tomography scans revealed pulmonary fibrosis and bronchiectasis in patients. Large airway constriction, especially affecting the trachea, lingula, and major bronchus of the right middle lobe, was seen by bronchoscopy in seven of the twenty‐four patients. Asthma, chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchiectasis, large airway narrowing, and pulmonary fibrosis were among the common respiratory problems [15].

The long‐term pulmonary consequences of sulfur mustard exposure in heavily intoxicated Iranian veterans were investigated in a research. The study comprised 40 male individuals who had been diagnosed with sulfur mustard poisoning 16–20 years prior. These veterans in Khorasan, Iran underwent arterial blood gas analysis, high‐resolution computed tomography of the chest, respiratory examinations, and spirometry. Lung lobe involvement was used to rate high‐resolution computed tomography abnormalities. According to the study, every patient complained of coughing, 85% had dyspnea, 60% had hemoptysis, and 95% produced sputum. Wheezing accounted for the majority of objective clinical observations, followed by crackles and stridor. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease accounted for the majority of reported respiratory difficulties, followed by bronchiectasis, asthma, large‐airway constriction, pulmonary fibrosis, and chronic bronchitis. Spirometric and arterial blood gas measurements showed strong correlations. Furthermore, a substantial association between partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood and the severity grades of high‐resolution computed tomography abnormalities was found [16]. Hence, it might be suggested that respiratory issues brought on by sulfur mustard usually get worse over time. Regular follow‐ups can benefit from the use of spirometry to measure pulmonary impairment; however, arterial blood gas and high‐resolution computed tomography are more objective methods that should be used first to diagnose respiratory issues and assess severity.

Considering the complex nature of lung pathology in sulfur mustard‐exposed patients, a research tried to explore and quantify lung pathology in 23 individuals who had been exposed to sulfur mustard gas during the Iran‐Iraq war (1980–1988), 14 years earlier. After checking out other possible lung disease reasons, such as smoking cigarettes, all 23 patients had symptoms like tightness in the chest, coughing, and dyspnea. Significant air trapping was found in high‐resolution computed tomography scans, and a large increase in residual volume was found in pulmonary function tests data. In the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, neutrophils composed the majority of the inflammatory cells. In 22 patients, it was possible to evaluate lung tissue in detail. Of the 14 lung biopsies having alveoli, nine had histological abnormalities suggestive of organizing pneumonia or bronchiolitis obliterans organizing pneumonia, and 11 cases showed airway epithelial damage. Changes indicative of organized pneumonia were seen in two patients [17]. The results indicate that sulfur mustard exposure may cause lung disorders that are chronic and clinically significant, such as organizing pneumonia and bronchial mucosal injury, even years after the first exposure.

The obesity of veterans exposed to chemical warfare agents, especially those who had chronic bronchiolitis as a result of mustard gas exposure, was assessed. On 434 Iranian veterans, a descriptive cross‐sectional study was carried out. Three age categories were used to group the patients: 30‐39, 40‐49, and 50‐59 years old. The patients average body mass index was 26.4 ± 3.9 kg/m2, with minor variations seen in different age groups. The research findings indicate that a noteworthy proportion of veterans were classified as overweight or obese, with percentages varying between 54.7% and 82.4% in various age groups. There was no relationship found between body mass index and respiratory symptoms such as cough, dyspnea, sleep disturbance, bloody sputum, or respiratory volumes [18]. Hence, it might be suggested that veterans who were exposed to chemical warfare agents had a higher prevalence of obesity and overweight than the general population. Considering the importance of obesity in long‐term lung conditions, weight control for these patients needs to be a priority. For these patients, extensive support from nutritionists, endocrinologists, pulmonologists, and psychologists is advised through collaborative treatment.

In a study that examined on a single high‐dose mustard‐exposed patients (58 males, average age 45.6 ± 6.2 years), about 18 years ago, and whose main complaints were dyspnea and cough, investigations went beyond the lungs to investigate other possible origins of symptoms. Perfusion imaging scans were performed on patients who were unable to complete exercise stress testing because of dyspnea or other problems. Interestingly, 23% of the participants had left ventricular diastolic abnormality without major valvular or conduction abnormalities, whereas 96.5% of the subjects had no severe coronary artery disease. Patients complained of chest pain that was made worse by exercise or coughing, as well as increased exertional dyspnea. Diastolic dysfunction is significant because it impacts cardiac morbidity and mortality, which are determined by structure, myocardial relaxation, and ventricular filling. A minority of patients in the study also had mild mitral regurgitation and mitral valve prolapse, which may eventually lead to diastolic or systolic dysfunction [19]. To track the development of diastolic irregularities to more severe phases, the authors suggested thorough examinations and frequent follow‐ups. They emphasized the significance of early discovery and care in this population.

An Iranian retrospective cohort study examined the possibility of late pulmonary problems in patients who experienced different degrees of early symptoms after being exposed to sulfur mustard between 1983 and 1988. Patients were divided into three categories according to the study: “mild,” “moderate,” and “severe,” depending on the initial symptoms and hospitalization status. In comparison to the moderate and mild groups, the severe group—which included individuals hospitalized because of early symptoms—exhibited noticeably greater rates of cough and dyspnea. high‐resolution computed tomography scans and pulmonary function tests showed that air trapping did not significantly change between the moderate and severe groups, and both showed a similar frequency of obstructive patterns. Bronchiolitis obliterans was shown to be the main respiratory side effect of sulfur mustard exposure; moderate and severe exposure groups showed comparable risks, although mild exposure showed a decreased risk [20]. The research indicates that in situations of sulfur mustard exposure, host characteristics may have a greater influence on the development of late pulmonary problems than on the intensity of initial symptoms

A research concentrated on abnormal laryngeal findings in a cohort of male chemical war injury patients who had been acutely exposed to sulfur mustard 20 years prior and had been experiencing chronic respiratory complaints (chronic cough, chest discomfort exertion dyspnea, sputum) for a number of years. A speech‐language pathologist assessed dysphonia in 50 male patients with persistent respiratory symptoms who were consecutively chosen because of symptom aggravation in this observational case series. Following fiberoptic laryngobronchoscopy, 46% of patients had dysphonia, including harshness and hoarseness, with 18% and 6% of patients having inflammation in the supraglottic and subglottic regions, respectively. Furthermore, in 48% of the patients, hyperfunction of the false vocal cords was observed. The results highlighted the enduring nature of chronic [21]. The findings highlighted the persistence of chronic laryngitis in a considerable number of people years after being exposed to sulfur mustard, despite undergoing various therapies over time.

When the delayed problems resulting from exposure to sulfur mustard were evaluated in veterans of the Iran‐Iraq war, the most common areas of concern were the eyes (77.61%), skin (82.84%), and lungs (100%). Furthermore, dyspnea was found to be the most common complaint among all patients, with 52.98% reporting expectoration and 72.38% reporting coughing. Asthma, bronchiectasis, chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, restrictive airway disease, and small airway disease were among the common respiratory problems that were found [22]. In order to enable early detection and intervention in the management of respiratory challenges, the study emphasizes the importance of continuous monitoring for veterans exposed to sulfur mustard. It also emphasizes the necessity of focused treatment approaches to minimize long‐term effects.

An investigation assessed the pulmonary and ocular effects on veterans of war who had previously been exposed to sulfur mustard. Based on forced expiratory volume in 1 s, 292 veterans with clinical lung involvement—which included a persistent cough, sputum, hemoptysis, and dyspnea were evaluated and divided into four groups: normal, mild, moderate, and severe. The findings showed that 32.5% of cases have ocular involvement and 96.3% involved lung involvement. Although it was small, a positive association was discovered between the degree of pulmonary and ocular involvement [23]. The research findings indicate that a modest association was found between the degree of lung and ocular involvement, which might potentially be influenced by several factors such as types of gas exposure, tissue susceptibilities, or exposure patterns within the population under investigation.

Twenty years after a single, huge exposure during the Iraq‐Iran conflict, a research examined the pulmonary consequences of sulfur mustard exposure on civilian victims in Sardasht, Iran, in comparison to a matched control group. Based on the severity of injuries sustained after exposure, participants were split into subgroups that were hospitalized and those that were not. Dyspnea was the most common respiratory symptom (93.2%) among those assessed. There were notable distinctions between the two groups' chronic cough, cough intensity, sputum, hemoptysis, dyspnea, chest pain, and pulmonary abnormalities. The majority of pulmonary findings were wheezing, with significant variations in wheezing and rhonchi between the hospitalized and control groups. Objectively, 24.3% of subjects had lung disease, indicating the long‐term effects of sulfur mustard exposure on respiratory health [24].

In the Sardasht‐Iran cohort study, which examined the long‐term (20 years) pulmonary effects of sulfur mustard exposure and focused in particular on inflammatory mediators such as interleukin‐8, interleukin‐6, C‐reactive protein, and rheumatoid factor, researchers examined 348 sulfur mustard‐exposed participants and 120 controls. Spirometry tests and clinical evaluations were performed, and the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease categorization system was used to assess the severity of pulmonary involvement. Participants who were exposed to sulfur mustard had significantly lower serum levels of interleukin‐8 and interleukin‐6 than the control group. Nevertheless, no meaningful associations were discovered between spirometry data or pulmonary symptoms (dyspnea, chronic cough, hemoptysis, and sputum) and interleukin‐8 levels. Among those exposed to sulfur mustard, wheeze and rales were linked to C‐reactive protein, but wheezing was linked to interleukin‐6 [25]. Hence, the degree of lung involvement triggered on by sulfur mustard exposure may not be reflected in the levels of these inflammatory mediators, nor may they substantially contribute to the pathophysiology or persistence of pulmonary issues after sulfur mustard exposure.

Another study examined the impact of sulfur mustard exposure on late cardiovascular effects in life in Iranian war victims with disabilities. Of the 100 males included in the study (50 patients with symptoms of sulfur mustard exposure and 50 veterans of the war without chemical injuries), the patients with chemical war injuries had serious cardiac abnormalities, such as mitral regurgitation and coronary artery disease. Four percent of the group with chemical war injuries had positive findings on an exercise stress test, which indicated underlying coronary artery disease. Furthermore, a patient was referred for mitral valve replacement due to significant mitral regurgitation despite normal coronary angiography. Twenty‐three percent of the chemical war injury group had abnormalities in their left ventricle diastolic function, compared to five percent of the control group, all of whom had normal stress test findings. Despite not being statistically significant, the exposed group (50%) experienced higher exertional dyspnea than the control group (30%). The exposed group experienced symptoms such as dyspnea and chest pain more frequently, although both groups' cardiac signs were identical [26]. The study comes to the conclusion that diastolic dysfunction is the most prevalent abnormality in both patient groups, and that cardiovascular abnormalities constitute a substantial late consequence among Iranian victims of chemical warfare. Because right ventricular failure may exacerbate patient dyspnea, management of right ventricular failure is crucial.

interleukin‐6 levels were evaluated to determine whether there was a relationship between the severity of airways disease and war veterans' pulmonary problems from sulfur mustard exposure. Thirty healthy controls, matched for age and body mass index, were placed in comparison to 50 patients with stable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and sulfur mustard poisoning. Interestingly, mean serum interleukin‐6 level was considerably higher in the patient group compared to controls, and 54% of patients had mild chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Significant relationships between interleukin‐6 level and the severity of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease was found in the study. Moreover, a negative relationship was noted between forced expiratory volume in 1 s and serum interleukin‐6 levels, indicating a possible direct relationship between increased interleukin‐6 and restricted airflow in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and sulfur mustard poisoning patients [27].

Four hundred seventy‐nine participants in a research on Kurdish people exposed to chemical weapons by the Iraqi government in the 1980s underwent clinical evaluations. Significant exposure was defined as blistering at the moment of mustard gas exposure. For comparison, the subjects, whose mean age was 43.1 ± 13.7, were split into groups that blistered and those who did not. The results showed that 46.6% of patients had air trapping on computed tomography scans, a finding that was similar for both groups, and 15.2% of patients had abnormal spirometry. Blister patients had reduced forced expiratory volume in 1 second and greater respiratory symptoms, such as coughing, dyspnea, and sputum production. The majority of patients, who were from Halabja, had the highest prevalence of severe problems. Blistering did not change computed tomography results, although it associated with poor lung function and more respiratory symptoms [28]. The findings highlight widespread objective abnormalities in people whose symptoms are connected to past chemical exposure, indicating a requirement for effective early treatment approaches to lessen the population's long‐term health effects.

Another study evaluated eosinophilic inflammation in chemical warfare victims to determine if it had any consequences for asthma, given their response to inhaled corticosteroids and airway hyper‐responsiveness. Sputum examination of 40 chemical warfare victims and 15 controls revealed considerably higher eosinophil percentages and eosinophil cationic protein levels in the chemical warfare patients. Notably, eosinophil cationic protein level associated with eosinophil counts and were greater in chemical warfare victims, with a more than 12% improvement in forced expiratory volume in 1 s. Clinical data revealed that dyspnea was the most common symptom, followed by cough in 95% of participants. The dyspnea scale revealed that Modified Medical Research Council stage 2 was the most common (45%), followed by stage 3 (25%) [29]. These results reveal eosinophil‐dependent bronchial inflammation in chemical warfare victims, suggesting that sputum eosinophil cationic protein levels could be useful in predicting corticosteroid responsiveness in this population.

The relationship between the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test questionnaire and the degree of the disease as determined by spirometric results was investigated, with a focus on victims of chemical warfare. Fifty‐five male patients who did not smoke answered the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease evaluation test and underwent spirometry. Based on the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease guidelines, patients with forced vital capacity values below 70% were divided into four severity groups. Significant correlations between forced expiratory volume in 1 s and forced vital capacity and the results of the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test questionnaire were found by data analysis [30]. These findings highlight the importance of the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test questionnaire in determining the severity of respiratory conditions in this population. They also show a noteworthy correlation between the scores on the questionnaire and disease severity as assessed by lung function in chemical warfare victims.

In order to examine the long‐term harmful effects of sulfur mustard on the upper and lower respiratory systems of Iranian veterans, researchers studied 43 male veterans who had above 25% of their disabilities from sulfur mustard poisoning 20 to 25 years after the exposure. The patients had an average age of 50.6 years, a body mass index of 26.6, and an average disability rate of 53.2%. Dysphonia, laryngeal mucosal inflammation, lower larynx positioning, post‐nasal discharge, and vocal cord limitations were among the important findings related to the upper respiratory system. The most common diagnosis for lower respiratory disorders were lung fibrosis (7.7%), bronchiectasis (44.1%), and chronic obstructive respiratory disease (84%). The degree of disability showed positive relationships with wheeze and bronchiectasis, but negative correlations with body mass index, spirometric parameter, and oxygen saturation. The delayed toxic effects of sulfur mustard primarily affected the upper and lower respiratory tracts with inflammatory and infectious implications. Significant correlations were found between risk variables such as lower‐respiratory problems and disability caused by sulfur mustard. All patients had symptoms, but subjective symptoms predominated over objective findings. Coughing (97.7%), dyspnea (97.7%), headaches (81.4%), and sputum production (88.4%) were the most common [31].

Clinical evaluations, high‐resolution computed tomography, and pulmonary function tests were performed in 106 veterans who had been exposed to sulfur mustard 18–23 years earlier as part of a cross‐sectional study. Most of the patients had coughing and dyspnea when they first arrived. According to pulmonary function tests data, the most common pattern was obstructive, which was followed by restrictive and normal patterns. The results of high‐resolution computed tomography frequently displayed patterns of mosaic parenchymal attenuation and air trapping. Asthmatic bronchitis, bronchiolitis obliterans, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were among the diagnoses. The lack of substantial correlations between pulmonary function tests and high‐resolution computed tomography findings and clinical symptoms and pulmonary function tests/high‐resolution computed tomography outcomes was noteworthy [32]. Considering the chronic and progressive nature of respiratory problems following sulfur mustard exposure, the research emphasizes the need for accurate diagnosis and customized treatment plans in order to address these concerns.

A study that examined patients suffering from mustard lung, a late pulmonary consequence triggered by exposure to sulfur mustard, assessed the function of the Body mass index‐Obstruction‐Dyspnea‐Exercise index. A total of 82 stable patients with different degrees of severity related to mustard lung were evaluated using the Body mass index‐Obstruction‐Dyspnea‐Exercise index, standard spirometry, pulse oximetry, and health‐related quality of life. Both oxygen saturation and quality of life showed significant associations with the Body mass index‐Obstruction‐Dyspnea‐Exercise score. However, there was no association discovered between the patient's age and the length of their illness [33]. Therefore, the Body mass index‐Obstruction‐Dyspnea‐Exercise index can be a useful tool in clinical practice for evaluating patients with mustard lung since it is associated with important clinical factors.

In an investigation, respiratory distress in those who had survived from exposure to sulfur mustard 62 male patients—mean age 53—showed a variety of debilitating respiratory symptoms. Of these, 61 individuals experienced dyspnea (almost 100%), which was followed by dry cough in 40, hemoptysis in 21, and productive cough in 20 cases. Radiological evaluations showed common abnormalities on chest X‐rays, such as pulmonary infiltration in 83% of cases, pleural thickening in 40% of cases, and emphysema in 26% of individuals. In addition, 85% of cases had pulmonary infiltration, 61% had bronchiolitis obliterans, and 58% had pleural thickening identified by high‐resolution computed tomography scans. Recheck radiological tests conducted 5 years later revealed additional abnormalities in high‐resolution computed tomography scans, and in almost one‐fifth of chest X‐rays, new findings were identified [34]. These findings highlight the long‐term respiratory difficulties that people exposed to sulfur mustard encounter, indicating the importance of careful observation via chest X‐rays for changing symptoms, and raising questions about the need for routine high‐resolution computed tomography scan repetition in light of the findings of the study.

Researchers examined individuals with sulfur mustard exposure who developed exertional dyspnea despite normal pulmonary function tests and imaging in a case‐control study carried out in Tehran, Iran in 2010. The study used cardiopulmonary exercise tests to assess dyspnea with an emphasis on gas exchange, comparing persons exposed to sulfur mustard who had normal imaging to healthy controls. One hundred fifty‐nine male patients in the case group had a history of exertional dyspnea and sulfur mustard, exposure, but their physical examinations, chest X‐rays, pulmonary function tests, and lung high‐resolution computed tomography scans showed normal results with minimal air trapping. The baseline pulmonary function tests features and demographics of the two groups did not differ significantly. However, there was a statistically significant difference in peak oxygen consumption/kg, oxygen consumption‐predicted, and respiratory rate peak between the patients and controls. The low oxygen consumption in cardiopulmonary exercise testing could not be explained by atypical gas exchange, even though it was possible in the case group. The study offered up a theory for the low oxygen consumption in these situations: impaired cellular oxygen consumption. It was proposed that routine evaluations of lung structure might not be fully useful in determining the etiology of exertional dyspnea in those who have been exposed to low concentrations of sulfur mustard [35].

An investigation intended to determine the efficacy of the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test in assessing the health‐related quality of life of 82 consecutive stable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease patients. The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease staging and the Body mass index‐Obstruction‐Dyspnea‐Exercise index were used to determine the severity of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Results showed that 43% of patients were in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test stage 3. A negative association was found between the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test score and forced expiratory volume in 1 s, and significant correlations were found between the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test and the Body mass index‐Obstruction‐Dyspnea‐Exercise index and the St. George Respiratory Questionnaires [36]. Thus, it might be proposed that chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test is a useful and reliable method for evaluating health‐related quality of life in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease patients who were exposed to chemical warfare, providing valuable data for patient care and clinical practice.

In 43 people with more than 25% disability due to sulfur mustard exposure approximately 25 years earlier experienced delayed respiratory problems. The three most common symptoms were expectoration, dyspnea, and chronic cough, which affected 64.7%, 88.2%, and 88.2% of patients, respectively. A predominately restrictive pattern (41.86%) and no pure obstructive pattern were seen in the 44.18% of subjects with pulmonary function tests abnormalities. A mixed pattern was shown to be significantly correlated with higher percentages of disabilities. Remarkably, a negative link was found between the forced vital capacity ratio and the percentage of people with disabilities and the forced expiratory volume in 1 s. The most prevalent abnormality found on high‐resolution computed tomography was air trapping, with bronchiectasis, lung fibrosis, and ground‐glass attenuation being commonly seen [37].

In 2016, 16 participants (10 males, 6 females; mean age 45.5 years) were interviewed for a study on civilian survivors of sulfur mustard exposure at Halabja after the chemical warfare attack in 1988. The goal of the qualitative study was to investigate the main health issues that survivors with persistent respiratory problems had to deal with. Following exposure, participants reported a drop in their physical and mental health, which mostly showed up as respiratory problems, exhaustion, sleep disorders, ophthalmic troubles, depression, and anxiety. Many had persistent issues with their vision and skin in addition to respiratory symptoms as cough, dyspnea, and sputum production. These health problems had a significant influence on day‐to‐day activities; most reported fatigue impairing their physical and mental health, possibly associated with dyspnea and psychological distress. Participants frequently reported worsening respiratory problems in the winter, being more prone to infections, and having less energy for activities like walking [38]. This study highlights the intricate relationship between physical and psychological well‐being in everyday life and illuminates the long‐term health challenges experienced by Halabja survivors of sulfur mustard exposure.

Thirty‐eight Iranian veterans with late insults from sulfur mustard exposure were the subjects of a study, which examined the delayed cardiac effects of sulfur mustard exposure. Symptoms included dyspnea, which was common in 63.15% of veterans and had varied functional classes. Other symptoms were atypical chest pain (28.94%) and typical chest pain (18.42%). Right axis deviation and partial right bundle branch block were found on the electrocardiogram. Echocardiographic evaluations revealed mild to severe tricuspid regurgitation in each patient, with a mean left ventricular ejection fraction, and patients with right ventricular dilatation. Patients had elevated pulmonary artery pressure. In 16%–44% of patients, there was evidence of both obstructive and non‐obstructive coronary artery disease, with differing degrees of vascular involvement [39]. The study emphasized the necessity for thorough monitoring and care for affected individuals, citing the cardiovascular toxicity of sulfur mustard as a delayed complication of this chemical warfare exposure.

Respiratory symptoms were evaluated in 142 chemical war victims exposed to sulfur mustard, 23–25 years after exposure and 120 control subjects using a questionnaire. pulmonary function tests were also measured in chemical war victims before and 15 min after the inhalation of 200 mg salbutamol but in the control group just in the baseline. Wheezing (66.19%), cough (64.78%), and chest tightness (54.4%) and sputum (15.5%) were reported by chemical war victims and 49.3% of them had wheeze in chest examination, which all were significantly higher than the control group. In the chemical war victims also, all pulmonary function tests values were significantly lower than the control group. All the pulmonary function tests values also improved after 200 mg salbutamol significantly. These results showed that chemical war victims, have higher frequencies and severity of respiratory symptoms and lower pulmonary function tests values, which showed reversibility after 200 mg inhaled salbutamol long term after sulfur mustard exposure [40].

In a review article, similarities and differences of respiratory symptoms, pulmonary function tests values, and lung inflammation in sulfur mustard‐exposed patients with asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease indicated that immediately after sulfur mustard exposure, patients experienced tracheobronchitis, hoarseness, cough, dyspnea, tightness, sand sore throat. Increasing cough, tachypnea, sinus pain were the main symptoms in the steady state course. Sulfur mustard exposure patients were different from asthma regarding resistance to treatment and severity, but their chest pain and nocturnal dyspnea were similar to asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Airway responsiveness in sulfur mustard‐exposed patients were similar to asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

In addition, various aspects of lung inflammation exist in these patients were similar to asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [5]. In two studies, airway hyper‐responsiveness to methacholine [41] and salbutamol [42] were reported. In few other studies also various respiratory symptoms, pulmonary function tests changes and lung inflammation in chemical war victims due to sulfur mustard‐exposure were described [43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48].

Discussion

Dyspnea following chemical exposure stems from a complex interplay of cellular and molecular processes within the respiratory system. Upon exposure to toxic agents like sulfur mustard, resident airway cells such as epithelial cells, macrophages, and neutrophils become activated, releasing pro‐inflammatory cytokines like interleukins (interleukin‐1, interleukin‐6, interleukin‐8). These interleukins play pivotal roles in orchestrating inflammation by activating immune cells and recruiting more inflammatory mediators to the site of injury. Concurrently, the generation of reactive oxygen species induced by sulfur mustard leads to oxidative damage, exacerbating the inflammatory response. This cascade of events results in epithelial injury, mucus hypersecretion, and fibrotic changes in the airways and lung parenchyma, culminating in airway remodeling, narrowing, and stiffening, contributing to airflow limitation and dyspnea. Furthermore, direct stimulation of irritant receptors in the airways by these agents triggers reflex bronchoconstriction and coughing, while the release of vasoactive mediators affects vascular tone and permeability, leading to airway edema and worsened gas exchange, intensifying the sensation of dyspnea. Understanding these intricate mechanisms at the cellular and molecular levels is vital for developing targeted interventions to alleviate respiratory symptoms and enhance the well‐being of individuals affected by sulfur mustard.

The impact of dyspnea on individuals' lives is profound, affecting various aspects such as physical well‐being, quality of life, mental health, and social interactions. It can lead to limitations in daily activities, reduced exercise tolerance, anxiety, depression, and social isolation due to the chronic nature of respiratory symptoms.

Short‐term after exposure, studies have shown correlations between dyspnea and various respiratory conditions like chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis, and airway narrowing. Years after exposure, these correlations persist, with individuals experiencing long‐term effects such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, and pulmonary fibrosis. Factors like arterial blood gas levels, spirometric measurements, and high‐resolution computed tomography abnormalities have been linked to the severity of dyspnea and other respiratory complications over time, highlighting the lasting impact of chemical exposure on respiratory health.

The strength of the studies lies in their detailed exploration of the respiratory consequences of sulfur mustard exposure, providing valuable insights into the spectrum of respiratory conditions and associated symptoms in affected individuals. However, limitations include variations in study populations, retrospective study designs, potential confounding factors, and challenges in establishing direct causality between dyspnea and specific respiratory outcomes. Standardization of assessments and long‐term follow‐up could enhance the robustness of future research in this field.

Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to track the progression of respiratory symptoms and functional impairments in sulfur mustard‐exposed victims over extended periods. Investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying dyspnea post‐exposure and exploring novel therapeutic interventions to alleviate respiratory distress are crucial areas for further investigation. Currently, management strategies for these chronic respiratory complications are largely symptomatic, aiming to improve airflow, reduce inflammation, and mitigate long‐term sequelae. Standard treatments often involve bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, and mucolytics [49]. However, the long‐term outcomes and optimal treatment regimens for sulfur mustard survivors are not yet fully established, underscoring the need for more targeted therapeutic approaches and supportive care to achieve favorable short‐ and long‐term results. In this context, emerging evidence on complementary interventions targeting respiratory symptoms warrants consideration. Recent randomized controlled trials have investigated the effects of thyme oil (Thymus vulgaris) inhalation or aromatherapy across different patient populations. A study on COVID‐19 patients reported that thyme oil aromatherapy significantly relieved dyspnea, cough, and other symptoms, while improving SpO2 and reducing respiratory rate [50]. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients, thyme oil inhalation was found to significantly reduce dyspnea, cough, and secretion, with higher SpO2 levels and lower respiratory rates observed from the third day of intervention [51]. Similarly, a single‐blind randomized controlled trial in asthma patients demonstrated that thyme oil inhalation significantly reduced dyspnea, cough, wheezing, and secretion, and improved forced expiratory volume (FEV)1 and FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratios [52]. Furthermore, a study on chemical war victims demonstrated that the daily administration of a boiled aqueous extract of Nigella sativa led to significant improvements in respiratory symptoms, chest wheezing, and pulmonary function test values over a 2‐month period compared to a placebo group. Notably, this treatment was associated with a decreased reliance on inhaler and oral β‐agonists as well as oral corticosteroids, suggesting a potential prophylactic effect of N. sativa in managing chronic respiratory complications following chemical exposure [43]. Although these studies were conducted in different clinical contexts, they provide useful insight into alternative or supportive strategies aimed at reducing dyspnea and improving respiratory function. Future research could explore the potential application of such complementary approaches in sulfur mustard survivors to address persistent respiratory symptoms and enhance quality of life. Additionally, multidisciplinary approaches integrating respiratory medicine, toxicology, and rehabilitation could provide comprehensive care for individuals affected by sulfur mustard. Conclusion

In conclusion, dyspnea in sulfur mustard‐exposed victims represents a complex interplay of inflammatory processes, structural lung damage, and long‐term respiratory complications. Understanding the mechanisms driving dyspnea post‐exposure is essential for developing targeted interventions and improving the quality of life for affected individuals. Further research efforts should aim to elucidate the correlations between dyspnea and diverse respiratory outcomes, evaluate the efficacy of current symptomatic management and emerging complementary therapies, and pave the way for more personalized and effective management strategies in this vulnerable population.
