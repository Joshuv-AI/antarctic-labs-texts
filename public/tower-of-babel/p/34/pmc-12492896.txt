Association between loneliness and self-care in elderly individuals with chronic kidney disease: a structural equation modeling approach
Authors: Elham Nejadsadeghi, Ahmad Vakili-Basir, Amir Mohamad Moghadasi, Shahab Papi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) presents a growing global challenge among aging populations. Effective self-care remains a cornerstone of disease management in elderly patients. This study investigated the association between loneliness and self-care behaviors, including basic and instrumental activities of daily living (ADLs), in elderly individuals with CKD attending selected educational and medical centers affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences.MethodsIn this descriptive-analytical study conducted in 2022, 310 elderly individuals diagnosed with CKD were assessed using validated instruments: the Chronic Kidney Disease Self-Care (CKDSC) questionnaire, basic and instrumental ADL scales, and a standardized loneliness measure. Structural equation modeling using AMOS software was employed to evaluate model fit and estimate the predictive value of loneliness on variance in self-care.ResultsThe proposed model demonstrated acceptable fit indices (p < 0.001; root mean square error of approximation, RMSEA = 0.072; comparative fit index, CFI = 0.995; goodness-of-fit index, GFI = 0.994). The final model accounted for 83% of the variance in self-care. Loneliness exerted the most substantial effect on self-care behaviors (β = 1.29, 95% CI [1.17, 1.41]; p < 0.001).ConclusionFindings indicate that loneliness may unexpectedly enhance self-care engagement among elderly individuals with CKD. These results underscore the importance of context-specific interventions that address the complex psychological dynamics influencing self-management. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to explore the mediating pathways underlying this association.Keywords: Aging, Loneliness, Activities of daily living, Self-Care, Chronic kidney disease, Structural equation modeling

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 40% of individuals aged 65 years or older, representing a growing global health concern in aging populations [1]. Aging, typically defined by chronological age, is a multifaceted biological process influenced by genetics, lifestyle, and general health status, resulting in considerable interindividual variability [2].

CKD is clinically defined by either persistent kidney damage or a sustained eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for at least three months [3]. Among the elderly, it typically develops due to age-related physiological decline, cellular and molecular damage, and other inherent vulnerabilities [1]. The disease is associated with metabolic disturbances, cardiovascular comorbidities, and diminished quality of life, necessitating sustained clinical and behavioral management [4].

Self-care plays a pivotal role in CKD management, encompassing routine practices such as dietary adherence, medication compliance, and physical activity engagement, all of which contribute to improved health outcomes and reduced hospitalization rates [5]. Among elderly patients, self-care assumes greater clinical relevance due to its capacity to slow disease progression, enhance autonomy, and reduce the emotional and financial burden of repeated hospital admissions [6–9]. Improved self-care has been linked to increased satisfaction with health management, enhanced psychological well-being, and a greater sense of personal control [7].

Although loneliness can occur at any age, it is particularly prevalent and impactful among the elderly. Many older adults describe loneliness as a distressing and overwhelming experience that negatively impacts their quality of life [10]. This emotional state may reduce self-care engagement by undermining psychological resilience and daily functioning.

Functional independence, commonly assessed through the ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs), is a key indicator of self-care capacity in older adults [11, 12]. ADLs encompass fundamental tasks such as personal hygiene, mobility, and nutrition. A decline in self-care ability directly compromises the performance of ADLs, particularly in elderly individuals managing chronic diseases such as CKD [13].

Despite the established link between loneliness and poor health outcomes, its role in shaping self-care behavior among elderly CKD patients remains insufficiently explored. Within the framework of Self-Determination Theory, increasing loneliness, diminished ADL capacity, and perceived loss of independence can undermine intrinsic motivation for self-care. This psychological erosion may manifest in reduced engagement with health-promoting behaviors, including dietary regulation, medication adherence, and physical activity. A theoretical approach emphasizing autonomy, competence, and relatedness may offer a robust lens for examining these interrelationships [14].

The present study applies this perspective to explore the behavioral dynamics of elderly individuals with CKD, providing a basis for the development of targeted psychosocial interventions aimed at improving quality of life. Building on prior research—such as Masoudi et al. (2024), which examined loneliness, self-care, and ADLs among older adults [10]—this study offers a refined contribution by focusing specifically on the CKD population, employing validated tools such as the CKDSC, ADL, and IADL scales, and applying structural equation modeling to elucidate variable interactions with greater precision.

Accordingly, the study aims to examine the relationship between loneliness, self-care, and ADL performance in elderly patients with CKD referred to selected teaching and treatment centers affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive-analytical study was conducted in 2022 among elderly individuals diagnosed with CKD who were referred to selected educational and treatment centers affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences, including Imam Reza (AS), Sina, Al-Zahra (AS), Razi, Taleghani, Shahid Madani, and Shohada hospitals. Tabriz, the capital of East Azerbaijan Province and the fourth most populous city in Iran, is located in the country’s northwest, where over 10% of the population is aged 60 years or older.

Sampling and sample size

Hospitals were selected purposively based on institutional accessibility and patient volume, and participants were randomly selected from CKD patient registries. The sample size was calculated using G*Power software, assuming an effect size of 0.25 and a statistical power of 80%, which yielded a required sample size of 310 participants [15]. This estimation was informed by the findings of Papi et al. (2023), who reported a comparable effect size under similar study conditions [16].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were those aged 60 years or older with a confirmed history of CKD for at least three months, no documented psychiatric illness, no cognitive impairment as indicated by a score ≥ 7 on the Abbreviated Mental Test (AMT), and sufficient ability to speak and communicate. Individuals who declined continued participation at any point during the study were excluded.

Measuring tools

Data were collected using the Elderly Personal Information Form, the AMT, the CKDSC, the Barthel Index, the Lawton IADL Scale, and the De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale.

To comprehensively assess functional capacity among elderly individuals with CKD, two validated tools were applied: the Barthel Index, which evaluates basic ADLs such as eating, mobility, and bathing; and the Lawton IADL Scale, which measures more complex functional tasks including medication management, shopping, and use of communication devices. The combined use of both instruments provided a multidimensional profile of functional independence, aligning with the study’s goal of exploring both complex and straightforward aspects of daily living. The psychometric robustness and cultural relevance of these scales in geriatric and chronic illness populations have been previously established [17, 18].

Data collection was conducted via two approaches: self-administration and structured interviews. Participants capable of reading and writing completed the questionnaires independently. In cases of limited literacy or cognitive capacity, trained interviewers administered the questionnaires and recorded responses. Data were collected from April to July 2022 across affiliated medical centers. Interviews were scheduled based on participants’ availability, with the option of conducting them at participants’ homes if in-person visits to medical centers were not feasible. Short rest periods were provided when participants showed signs of fatigue. All responses were anonymized using coded identifiers to maintain confidentiality. The validity and cultural adaptability of the questionnaire were confirmed through expert review and pilot testing.

Demographic profile form

This form captured participants’ age, sex, education level, marital status, employment, financial status, and duration of CKD.

Abbreviated mental test score (AMTS)

The AMT consists of 10 items evaluating orientation, memory, and attention. A score of 6 or below indicates cognitive impairment. In Persian validation studies, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.76) and strong test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.89) [19].

CKDSC

The CKDSC comprises 16 items across five subscales: medication adherence (5 items), diet control (4 items), physical activity (3 items), smoking behavior (2 items), and blood pressure monitoring (2 items). Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with total scores ranging from 16 to 80 [15]. Higher scores reflect stronger self-care behaviors. In psychometric evaluations, the Persian version demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.953) and strong test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.976), confirming its validity for use among Iranian CKD patients [20].

Barthel index

The 10-item version used in this study assesses core ADLs such as feeding, bathing, dressing, toileting, and mobility. Total scores range from 0 to 100, with categories as follows: 0–20 (total dependence), 21–60 (severe dependence), 61–90 (moderate dependence), 91–99 (partial dependence), and 100 (full independence). The Persian version has shown strong reliability, with Cronbach’s α and ICC values exceeding 0.75 [21, 22].

Lawton IADL scale

The 8-item version of the Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale was employed to assess advanced functional abilities in the elderly. The instrument includes tasks such as using the telephone, shopping, food preparation, laundry, transportation, medication management, and financial handling. Each item is scored dichotomously—1 indicating greater ability and 0 indicating reduced capacity—with total scores ranging from 0 to 8, where higher scores reflect greater independence in daily functioning. Based on the validation study by Mehraban et al. (2014), the Persian version of the Lawton IADL demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability (r = 0.993), high inter-rater consistency (r = 0.961), and acceptable internal consistency, with item-total correlations ranging from r = 0.427 to 0.606. These findings confirm its suitability for clinical and research settings in geriatric populations in Iran [23, 24].

De Jong Gierveld loneliness scale

This study utilized the 11-item version of the de Jong Gierveld Loneliness Psychometric Scale, which assesses both emotional and social loneliness. Each item is rated on a 3-point Likert scale—“no,” “somewhat,” and “yes”—with total scores of 3 or above indicating loneliness and its severity. According to Hosseini et al. (2021), the Persian version of this scale demonstrated robust psychometric properties in an older Iranian population. Internal consistency was satisfactory, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.76 for the total scale and subscale values ranging from 0.70 to 0.78. External reliability was also supported, with high intraclass correlation coefficients and strong McDonald’s Omega values, confirming stability across time and applicability in cross-cultural elderly research [25].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. For continuous variables, central tendency and dispersion were reported using either the mean ± standard deviation or the median and interquartile range, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables were described using frequency counts and percentages. Internal consistency reliability for each questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on the distribution, associations between continuous variables were examined using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the independent t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. When required, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Holm’s correction method.

Following the confirmation of the psychometric robustness of the instruments, a structural equation model (SEM) was developed to test the proposed hypotheses. The SEM allowed for the simultaneous analysis of direct and indirect relationships among variables, integrating elements of regression and factor analysis. This approach enabled the modeling of both measurement and structural components, supporting the evaluation of hypothesized causal mechanisms through multiple fit indices.

Model fit was assessed using several standard goodness-of-fit indices, including the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df), GFI, normed fit index (NFI), CFI, RMSEA, and p close. An RMSEA between 0.05 and 0.08, along with GFI and CFI values exceeding 0.90, was considered indicative of acceptable model fit [26, 27]. All assumptions for SEM—including multivariate normality, linearity, adequate sample size, and correct model specification—were verified prior to analysis.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2; R Core Team, 2022) and IBM AMOS (version 26). A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied throughout the analysis. Additionally, multicollinearity diagnostics, such as Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance values, were conducted to assess potential issues of high correlation among predictor variables.

The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that the distribution of self-care scores deviated significantly from normality (p < 0.001). Consequently, nonparametric tests were used to examine associations between demographic variables and self-care. For categorical variables with two levels, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied; for those with more than two levels, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used.

Although male participants showed slightly higher median self-care scores compared to females, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.50), suggesting that self-care did not vary by gender. However, self-care scores were significantly higher among financially independent participants compared to those dependent on others (p = 0.04). No significant association was observed between marital status and self-care (p = 0.063).

Significant associations were found between self-care and education level (p < 0.001), employment status (p = 0.028), and duration of illness (p = 0.002). Post hoc analysis using Dunn’s test revealed that elderly participants without formal education had significantly lower self-care scores than those with primary or higher levels of education. No significant difference was noted between participants with primary education and those with higher educational attainment.

Regarding illness duration, the Dunn test revealed that participants with a CKD duration of three years or more had significantly lower self-care scores compared to those with less than two years of illness. No significant difference was detected between those with illness durations of less than one year and those between one and two years.

Spearman correlation analysis revealed a strong positive association between self-care and loneliness scores (ρ = 0.82, p < 0.001), indicating that higher levels of loneliness were associated with higher levels of self-care. Given the strong correlation observed between loneliness and self-care, collinearity diagnostics such as Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) were examined and found to be within acceptable limits. These results suggest that multicollinearity did not affect the validity of the model. A weak but significant correlation was also observed between self-care and overall functional independence (ρ = 0.24, p < 0.001). When analyzed separately, correlations between self-care and the Barthel Index (ρ = 0.19) and between self-care and the Lawton Index (ρ = 0.26) were both weak yet statistically significant (p < 0.001), reflecting modest linear relationships with specific dimensions of functional independence. Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between loneliness, self-care, and ADL in elderly individuals with CKD attending selected medical centers affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. Path analysis results demonstrated that loneliness had a greater direct effect on self-care than on independence. Based on unstandardized estimates, a one-unit increase in loneliness was associated with a 1.29-point increase in self-care (95% CI: 1.17 to 1.41; p < 0.001), while the corresponding effect on independence was 0.89 points (95% CI: 0.20 to 1.53; p = 0.011). Notably, this positive association between loneliness and self-care contradicts our initial hypothesis (H2), which posited a negative relationship between the two. This unexpected result warrants further interpretation through theoretical lenses such as compensatory behavior, self-determination theory, and psychological resilience, which are discussed below.

The mean self-care score in the current sample was 41.16 ± 6.81, which is substantially lower than those reported in previous studies by Bastani et al. (66.20 ± 6.06) [20] and Wang et al. (62.12 ± 8.76) [15]. Variations in self-care scores across studies may reflect differences in sample size, cultural context, care settings, and data collection periods.

Although male participants in the present study had slightly higher average self-care scores than females, the difference was not statistically significant. This finding is consistent with reports by Soodmand et al. [29] and Kim et al. [30], both of which found no meaningful gender differences in self-care behaviors. However, contrasting findings have been reported. Avazeh et al. (2019) identified higher self-care ability among women [31], whereas Bastani et al. observed lower self-care scores in men compared to women [20]. In contrast, Nakhjiri et al. and Avanji et al. reported better self-care among men [32, 33]. These inconsistencies may be influenced by contextual factors such as knowledge level, marital or employment status, or physical and psychological health.

Financial status was significantly associated with self-care: elderly individuals with financial independence had notably higher self-care scores than those who were financially dependent on others. This aligns with the findings of Tsai, who reported a positive correlation between financial autonomy and self-care performance [34].

No significant relationship was observed between marital status and self-care in this study. This finding aligns with Soodmand et al., who also reported no association between marital status and self-care in hemodialysis patients [29]. In contrast, Tsai reported that marital status, along with disease knowledge, was positively correlated with self-care [34]. Avazeh et al. [31] found that being married, divorced, or widowed was associated with reduced self-care, with single individuals reporting the highest levels of engagement in self-care activities. These discrepancies may reflect the complex role of marital dynamics. For instance, single individuals may have fewer family obligations and more autonomy in managing personal health, whereas the presence of a spouse could either facilitate self-care through emotional support or, conversely, constrain it due to dependence or shared responsibilities.

Employment status was also found to be significantly associated with self-care behaviors. While Avazeh et al. reported no significant relationship between occupational or income status and self-care [31], findings from Avanji et al. indicated that occupation does influence self-care levels, with housewives reporting lower self-care compared to other groups. Their analysis showed that employed individuals demonstrated higher levels of engagement in self-care [33]. In contrast, Bastani et al. found that self-care varied significantly across employment categories, with freelance workers scoring lower than those who were employed, retired, or homemakers [20].

Educational status also showed a strong connection to self-care outcomes. In the present study, elderly individuals with no formal education had significantly lower self-care scores than those with primary or higher education. While an upward trend in self-care scores was observed with increasing education level, the difference between primary and higher education groups was not statistically significant. This trend aligns with the findings of Ping Shao et al., who demonstrated a positive correlation between health literacy and self-care behavior. Education is a well-established determinant of health literacy, as individuals with higher levels of education are more likely to comprehend, interpret, and apply health-related information [35]. Similarly, Avanji et al. found that educated patients reported greater self-care abilities compared to illiterate individuals [33]. Soodmand et al. also reported a significant positive association between education level and self-care capacity [29]. However, Avazeh et al. found no significant effect of education, employment, or income on self-care, highlighting potential contextual or population-specific differences [31].

The duration of illness was another important variable. Participants with a CKD duration of three years or more had significantly lower self-care scores than those with one to two years of illness. Although self-care scores were somewhat higher in the one- to two-year group compared to those with less than one year of illness, the difference was not statistically significant. Interestingly, other studies have reported findings that are in contrast to these. For instance, Jung-Won [36], Wang [15], and Tsai [34] observed higher self-care scores among patients with late-stage CKD. These differences may reflect illness perception; individuals in early stages may not experience noticeable symptoms and, therefore, may not fully recognize the importance of self-care behaviors. Conversely, Seifi et al. found that patients with less than one year of illness had higher self-care scores than those with longer disease duration [35]. Mahdilou and Ziaei-Rad also reported a non-linear relationship in diabetic patients, where those with less than five years or more than twenty years of illness exhibited higher self-care than other groups [37].

Participants with full functional independence in daily activities exhibited significantly higher self-care scores compared to those who were moderately or severely dependent on others for daily activities. However, no statistically significant differences were observed among the other functional independence categories. These findings underscore the importance of promoting independence in daily functioning as a strategy to enhance self-care and reduce caregiver burden [38, 39].

Self-care is a purposeful, learned behavior that individuals initiate to regulate internal and external factors affecting their health, function, and quality of life [40]. According to Mielenz et al., self-care patterns in older adults tend to remain stable over time, suggesting that these behaviors may reflect long-standing habits rather than recent adaptations. In this context, a stable and favorable self-care pattern may help prevent functional decline and maintain autonomy [9]. Older adults who maintain independence in daily activities often experience heightened self-esteem, confidence, autonomy, and psychological well-being [41, 42]. Life satisfaction—a cognitive dimension of subjective well-being—is closely tied to perceived autonomy and plays a critical role in psychological resilience. Higher levels of life satisfaction are associated with improved adaptation to age-related limitations and increased subjective happiness [43]. Independence, in particular, has been identified as a key determinant of life satisfaction, as it fosters self-worth and a sense of personal efficacy in older adults [44, 45].

In the current study, results from multiple linear regression indicated that when loneliness, functional independence in daily activities, and functional independence in instrumental activities were analyzed simultaneously, only loneliness remained a significant predictor of self-care. Specifically, controlling for other variables, each one-unit increase in loneliness was associated with a 1.29-point increase in self-care. These findings contrast with those of Masoudi et al., who reported a significant negative relationship between loneliness and self-care among older adults [10]. The divergent results suggest that the relationship between loneliness and self-care may be context-specific, particularly in populations with chronic illness such as CKD. Despite the strong positive correlation between loneliness and self-care (ρ = 0.82), multicollinearity diagnostics confirmed that this correlation did not compromise the model’s stability or inflate the estimated effects.

Pollak et al. reported mixed evidence in a comprehensive review on the link between loneliness and functional decline. While most studies found a positive association between loneliness and impairments in walking, IADL, motor performance, and ADL functioning, seven of the 47 studies included found no significant relationship. Differences in the scope of ADL and IADL assessments, as well as variation in loneliness measures—from single-item tools to validated scales such as the UCLA Loneliness Scale, De Jong Gierveld Scale, and the Revised Social Regulation Scale—contributed to this heterogeneity [46].

Thus, variation across studies may stem from methodological differences, cultural context, timing of data collection, or disease specificity. Notably, most prior studies examined loneliness in general elderly populations, whereas the current study focused exclusively on individuals with CKD, a distinction that may account for the contrasting patterns observed.

Emerging evidence suggests that self-compassion may serve as a potential buffer against loneliness in older adults [47]. Defined as the capacity to respond to personal suffering with empathy and supportive intention, self-compassion enables individuals to face life challenges with emotional resilience [48, 53]. By mediating the effects of lifelong disappointments and perceived failures, it fosters psychological balance in later life [49]. Moreover, self-compassion is positively associated with self-efficacy—a key factor in behavioral regulation and health maintenance [47]. Self-efficacy refers to one’s belief in the ability to execute behaviors that influence outcomes. It enhances perseverance, promotes adaptive coping, and supports independence in older adults [50, 51]. Studies have confirmed its associations with both functional independence and motivation for daily self-care [41, 52]. Although these constructs were not directly measured in the current study, they represent meaningful psychological factors that could mediate or moderate the observed relationships. Future research should consider incorporating self-compassion and self-efficacy to understand better the pathways linking loneliness and self-care in elderly individuals with CKD.

The unexpected positive association between loneliness and self-care observed in this study may reflect a compensatory mechanism of self-reliance. Compensation refers to adaptive behaviors that aim to preserve function despite loss. Among older adults, these strategies often intensify with age and are directed toward maintaining autonomy in everyday life [53]. In this framework, loneliness may serve as a psychological catalyst that stimulates engagement in self-care. However, the underlying motivational dynamics are likely complex and not fully captured by quantitative models alone. Future studies should therefore employ qualitative methodologies—such as in-depth interviews or narrative analyses—to uncover the subjective experiences of socially isolated individuals managing chronic illness [54, 55]. Similarly, the observed positive association between loneliness and independence may reflect a compensatory behavioral response, whereby socially isolated elderly individuals with CKD actively strive to maintain or even reinforce their functional autonomy.

Self-determination theory also offers a valuable lens for understanding this relationship. It posits that loneliness may trigger core psychological needs, such as autonomy and competence, which in turn activate intrinsic motivation for self-care. This perspective aligns with the broader emotional and cognitive complexity of health-related behaviors in older adults [56]. Future models should consider including autonomy and competence as mediators to better elucidate the mechanisms through which loneliness influences self-care in individuals with CKD.

Psychological resilience provides an additional interpretive layer. Older adults with CKD may activate internal coping resources when confronted with loneliness, using resilience as a means to sustain health-promoting behaviors. This resilience may not only buffer against the adverse emotional consequences of loneliness but also enhance intrinsic motivation for self-care [57–59]. Future research should investigate resilience as a mediating variable and evaluate its potential as a target for intervention in this vulnerable population.

Implications of findings

The observed positive association between loneliness and self-care among elderly individuals with CKD carries significant implications at the theoretical, clinical, policy, and research levels.

Theoretically

These findings challenge the conventional view that loneliness is solely detrimental to psychological and physical health. Instead, loneliness may act as a catalyst for activating core psychological needs, such as autonomy and competence, aligning with frameworks like self-determination theory.

Clinically

The results emphasize the need to move beyond framing loneliness as a symptom to be eliminated. Instead, loneliness should be recognized as a potential entry point for empowering patients and strengthening psychological resilience. Screening for loneliness can serve as a valuable clinical tool, not only for identifying risk but also for guiding targeted self-care interventions in older adults with CKD.

At the policy level

These findings underscore the importance of designing aging services and community-based programs that foster independence and meaningful self-care among socially isolated individuals. Rather than solely aiming to eliminate loneliness, policies should aim to transform it into a constructive experience by promoting agency, purpose, and active engagement in daily life. Supportive environments that respect the autonomy of older adults with CKD can significantly contribute to health outcomes and quality of life.

From a research perspective

Future studies should adopt longitudinal and mixed-methods designs to investigate the psychological mechanisms underlying this association. Mediating variables, such as self-compassion, self-efficacy, self-reliance, intrinsic motivation, and resilience, should be systematically explored to build more comprehensive models of self-care behavior in elderly individuals living with chronic illness.

Limits and future perspectives

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. First, the structural equation model applied was based on hypothesized relationships that may have been shaped by theoretical assumptions, potentially introducing model specification bias. In addition, the sample was drawn from a specific geographic and demographic population, which may limit the generalizability of the results to broader elderly populations with CKD.

Reliance on self-reported data is another limitation, as it may introduce measurement error, recall bias, or social desirability bias [60]. The cross-sectional design further restricts causal inference, making it difficult to establish the directionality of observed associations. Future studies should adopt longitudinal approaches to assess the temporal stability of these relationships and validate findings using independent samples.

Other methodological limitations include the absence of CKD stage classification, the lack of adjustment for depressive symptoms, and the presence of unmeasured confounding variables such as self-efficacy, resilience, or motivation. Additionally, potential selection and interview biases may have influenced participants’ responses, further affecting the internal validity.

Future research should address these limitations by employing improved sampling strategies, utilizing validated multi-method measures, and incorporating psychological covariates to enhance the understanding of these relationships. Mixed-methods designs—combining quantitative models with qualitative inquiry—are recommended to capture the nuanced motivations, experiences, and contextual factors that shape self-care behavior in elderly individuals with CKD. In-depth qualitative follow-up could provide richer insights into the mechanisms underlying the observed associations and inform the development of more tailored interventions.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complex interplay between loneliness, self-care, and ADL among elderly individuals with CKD. The findings underscore the importance of integrating emotional health and functional capacity into comprehensive self-care interventions tailored for this population. The unexpected positive association between loneliness and self-care challenges conventional assumptions and offers new insight into adaptive behaviors among older adults facing chronic illness.

These results provide a valuable evidence base for the design of targeted health, educational, and psychosocial interventions, and emphasize the need for care models that move beyond eliminating loneliness to recognize its potential compensatory role instead. Loneliness may, in specific contexts, activate psychological mechanisms that promote autonomy and engagement in self-care.

Policymakers and clinicians should consider loneliness not solely as a risk factor but also as a dynamic condition with nuanced effects. Screening for loneliness in elderly individuals with CKD could support the development of personalized self-care plans. Future research, utilizing longitudinal and mediation models, is warranted to determine whether loneliness causally enhances self-care and to clarify the psychological processes underlying this relationship.

Ultimately, the findings call for a shift in how loneliness is conceptualized and addressed in aging care—acknowledging it as a reality of later life and leveraging it as a potential driver of self-directed health behavior.
