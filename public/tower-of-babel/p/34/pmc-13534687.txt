In-vivo detection of cervical cancer lesions using hyperspectral colposcopy
Authors: Carlos Vega, Norberto Medina, Raquel Leon, Himar Fabelo, Alicia Martín, Gustavo M. Callico
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cervical cancer originates from precursor lesions in the cervix, where early diagnosis is essential to avoid progression to invasive carcinoma. Today, lesion detection relies mainly on colposcopy, a highly operator-dependent procedure with considerable variability in accuracy, particularly among less-experienced clinicians. In this study, we present a hyperspectral imaging-based approach for assisted colposcopy. We developed a custom hyperspectral colposcope covering 470–900 nm and conducted a 32-month clinical study including 116 patients and 245 hyperspectral images acquired during routine examinations. First, a dedicated algorithm automatically delineated the cervical region and segmented the tissue into ectocervix, endocervix and abnormal areas, achieving a macro-level DICE of 0.84. Subsequently, two approaches were studied for pixel-wise lesion classification: a binary model for high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion and invasive carcinoma versus healthy tissue, which achieved a mean F1-score of 0.74 on an independent test set; and a multiclass model for grading according to the Bethesda system, which showed lower generalisation (F1-score = 0.26) due to limited spectral resolution and spectral overlap. Overall, the results show the potential of hyperspectral colposcopy for non-invasive detection and delimitation of cervical lesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58325-0.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a global risk for women, especially for younger women under 45 years old and in low-income countries, where its incidence of 19.3 per 100,000 is significantly high. It is the fourth most common cancer in terms of incidence and mortality among women, with an estimated 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths worldwide in 20221. The expansion of cervical cancer screening programs in many countries in Europe, Oceania, and North America has led to a reduction in incidence and mortality rates, but there is still a high risk in younger populations, partly due to changes in population behaviour and increased transmission of human papillomavirus (HPV)2–4. Squamous cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) lesions are graded according to the degree of epithelial involvement into CIN 1 (low grade), CIN 2, and CIN 3 (high grade), the latter being the precursor of cervical cancer, specifically invasive carcinoma (IC). The development of the cancer starts several years earlier, with the HPV infection in the keratinocytes of the basal stratum of the epithelium5. Initially, a low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) appears, which can progress to a high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) with a high potential for malignancy6. The treatment of the early-stage precancerous lesions does not require complex interventions and generally results in a favourable prognosis for the patient7, which motivates the early detection of these lesions.

The predominant approach to the diagnosis of preneoplastic lesions in the cervix is the combined use of colposcopy and cervical biopsies. However, this conventional method is inherently subjective and depends on the practitioner’s expertise, leading to a notable variability in results8. Previous studies encountered low sensitivity (between 68.5% and 77%) and specificity (between 75.9% and 82%) using this method9,10, often leading to unnecessary biopsies11. The World Health Organization has adopted the Global Strategy for cervical cancer elimination, which establishes a goal of 90% of HPV vaccination for girls by the age of 15, 70% of women screened by the age of 35 and 45, and 90% of women with pre-cancer or cancer to receive appropriate treatment and management12. These goals present challenges not only in terms of vaccination but also in the screening process, as many women will require specialists and specific tests for early diagnosis, which is challenging given the limitations of current diagnostic methods13,14. In this context, the development of new screening and diagnostic methods becomes an urgent area of study.

These challenges have encouraged the development of more objective image-based tools for cervical assessment. Automated analysis of cervical images has been explored since the early 2000s 15,16. Recent deep learning (DL) approaches have substantially improved performance, with several studies reporting sensitivities and specificities above 85%17. Most of these methods analyse one or more colposcopic images acquired during the acetowhitening process. For instance, Peng et al. combined pre-acetic and post-acetic images 18, whereas Yu et al. integrated a temporal sequence of acetowhitening images together with green-filter and iodine-test images for CIN-level prediction 19. Although promising, these methods usually require the colposcope to remain static during sequential image acquisition, which may interfere with routine workflow, and they often offer limited interpretability for clinical decision-making, a serious issue for legislation and applicability in clinical practice20–22. HSI may help address these limitations by capturing spatial and spectral information simultaneously, enabling a more objective characterisation of tissue composition at the pixel level.

Hyperspectral (HS) Imaging (HSI) is a technology that combines traditional image and spectroscopy methods to capture spatial and spectral information. The use of this technology has emerged as a novel approach in the medical field, especially for cancer analysis23. HS images are represented as three-dimensional cubes, where the spatial dimensions (x,y) form the horizontal planes and the spectral information introduces the third dimension (λ). This third dimension provides hundreds of narrow and contiguous spectral bands, presented as spectral signatures for each pixel of the image, which provide specific details about the captured material, substance or tissue composition24.

Previous work has evaluated the use of HSI in cervical examination. In 2001, Ferris et al. evaluated in 136 patients a multimodal HSI system for the non-invasive diagnosis of cervical neoplasia that simultaneously acquires tissue fluorescence and reflectance25. It works based on a lamp source and a computer-controlled monochromator system for illuminating the cervix with selected wavelengths. The light is emitted and collected by a custom bifurcated fibre guide which is connected to a probe. A digital spectrometer with a filter wheel in front measures the collected light with a 5 nm spectral resolution. This system achieved 95% sensitivity and 83% specificity in the identification of CIN 2 and 3 lesions. In 2002, Mirabal et al.26 studied the use of reflectance spectroscopy in 161 patients for in-vivo detection of cervical cancer by using a spectrograph in the wavelength range between 355 and 655 nm with a spectral resolution of 2.5 nm. A preliminary study from 2016, with 6 patients enrolled, presented the use of multi-scale HSI for detecting cervical neoplasia at tissue and cellular levels27. HS images were captured with a monochrome camera, attached to an acousto-optic tuneable filter (AOTF) with a filtering range between 500 and 900 nm. Samples were captured in-vivo, during the patient examination, and ex-vivo, from tissue slices observed under the microscope. Authors showed favourable results by analysing the spectrum between 600 and 800 nm and claimed that three wavelengths (620, 696 and 772 nm) were sufficient for the tissue classification into normal, inflammation, and high-grade lesion by using an analysis in the second derivative with a custom window size of 76 nm28. Furthermore, Schimunek et al. presented in 2023 a statistical study based on the spectral signatures captured from 41 patients with a commercial HSI acquisition system in the 500–995 nm range29. The acquisition system was prepared for a wide area of vision, which resulted in only 5% to 10% of the pixels belonged to the cervix due to its relatively small dimensions. Despite this limitation, the statistical analysis revealed similar trends with significant differences in the 550–585 nm range. Our previous research, conducted in 2024, statistically evaluated the spectral signatures from various tissue types, including cancerous lesions, from a HS cervix dataset composed of 62 patients captured with a custom-designed HS colposcope30. Results revealed a significant difference in the 470–550 nm range and over 750 nm, which highlights the potential to distinguish the alterations in the cervical tissue.

In parallel, the National Institute for Health Research Health Technology Assessment (NIHR-HTA) first conducted in 2013 a systematic review of commercial technologies that complement the traditional colposcopy, such as Dynamic Spectral Imaging System (DYSIS, DYSISMedical Inc., MA, USA), LuViva® Advanced Cervical Scan (Guided Therapeutics, Inc, GA, USA) and Niris® Imaging System (Imalux, OH, USA)31. The initial assessment suggested that DYSIS, a system based on analysis of the acetowhitening process, was both less costly and more sensitive than traditional colposcopy alone. The systems of LuViva and Niris, both based on reflectance analysis, claimed competitive performance, but their studies were too limited and potentially biased to support firm conclusions. This evaluation was updated in 2018, when the NIHR-HTA reassessed adjunctive colposcopy technologies, focusing on DySISmap, a colour-coded map derived from the acetowhitening response, and ZedScan, a contact-based system using electrical impedance spectroscopy to assess cervical tissue32. The updated review reported that DySISmap increased sensitivity for detecting CIN2+ lesions compared with standard colposcopy alone at the expense of lower specificity and concluded that both DySISmap and ZedScan appeared potentially cost-effective, while also emphasising the limited and high-risk-of-bias evidence base, particularly for ZedScan. Overall, these reports highlight both the clinical interest in improving colposcopic assessment and the need for more robust and objective image-analysis approaches for cervical lesion detection and characterisation.

In this study, we present an approach for assisted colposcopy using HSI. We employed a custom-built HS colposcope, covering the visual and near-infrared spectral range (470–900 nm), to capture 116 patients and 245 HS images during routine cervical cancer screening consultations. These HS images were used to automatically identify the cervical region, reducing the region of interest and, consequently, the computational effort when using machine learning processing algorithms. Using this region, we segment the cervical tissue into key areas, including the ectocervix, endocervix and abnormal elements. The cervical region was then classified using machine learning algorithms to discriminate between different tissues. We propose two classification scenarios: the first discriminates between healthy cervical tissue and HSIL + IC lesions, while the second discriminates between healthy cervical tissue, LSIL, HSIL, and IC.

Results

Study population

This study involved 116 female participants aged 18 years or older who were treated at the Complejo Hospitalario Universitario Insular Materno Infantil in Las Palmas de Gran Canaria, Spain. The cohort represented a referral for colposcopy and follow-up population: patients attended either for further assessment after previous abnormal cervical screening findings or for surveillance after prior CIN diagnosis, treatment, or lesion regression. Eligible women were invited to participate before HS image acquisition and before any study-related procedure. The study adhered to ethical guidelines and obtained informed consent from all participants. During the clinical study, the gynaecologist collected sociodemographic and clinical data from the recruited participants and acquired a total of 245 HS images of the cervix using the HS colposcope. When feasible, two HS images were obtained per patient: (i) a baseline HS image of the cervix after removing secretions and debris before any intervention, and (ii) a subsequent HS image after the application of acetic acid and after cytology. In the subsequent experiments, spectral analysis was performed exclusively on the baseline HS image. Cytology (ThinPrep Pap Test PreservCyt™) and HPV testing (Cobas 4800) were performed in accordance with routine clinical practice, and the final diagnosis was made in accordance with standard clinical protocol.

Unsupervised segmentation of the cervical area

The unsupervised segmentation method automatically delineates the cervical region and segments it into key subregions, including the ectocervix, endocervix, and areas of potential abnormality. The cervical region was automatically delineated in all HS images to identify the region of interest and exclude surrounding tissues and external elements such as the speculum. The unsupervised segmentation was then applied only within the defined cervical area. This segmentation was evaluated on a subset of 14 test patients from the 77 selected for the study, corresponding to the same test group used in subsequent experiments. GT annotations contain the regions of the cervix where the gynaecologist was more confident in the class, while the unsupervised segmentation is performed in the whole cervical region. Conversely, P12 obtained the highest DICE performance (0.98), where, due to the cervix morphology, no endocervical tissue was visible, and all pixels were correctly classified as ectocervix.

HSIL + IC classification for CIN lesions detection

The first method is based on a binary classification approach, where the algorithm discriminates at the pixel level between HSIL + IC lesions and healthy cervical tissue. The collected HS data were used as input for several machine learning classifiers: support vector machines (SVMs) with four different kernels (linear (SVM-L), quadratic (SVM-Q), cubic (SVM-C), and radial basis function (SVM-RBF)); a decision tree (DT, maximum 50 splits); and a random forest (RF, maximum 200 trees). The SVM-RBF classifier was further evaluated using three kernel sizes: 5 (fine, SVM-RBF-F), 9.75 (medium, SVM-RBF-M), and 46 (coarse, SVM-RBF-C); resulting in a total of eight different classifiers. Figure 2 presents the validation results obtained using a fourfold cross-validation procedure. Training and validation were performed for the eight different models using the three HS data modalities generated to evaluate their classification performance: reflectance (Fig. 2a), absorbance (Fig. 2b), and second derivative (Fig. 2c). Results for the different modalities of data only present minor differences, where the best F1-score is 0.74 ± 0.06. The reflectance method shows slightly lower variability across the different folds, where the mean cross-fold standard deviation (averaged across all classifiers) in the two-class classifier is 0.09 (Fig. 2a) compared to 0.11 for absorbance (Fig. 2b) and 0.10 for second derivative (Fig. 2c). The SVM-RBF-C achieved the best performance in the three HS data modalities, followed by the SVM-RBF-M, which presents a difference between them inferior to 1% in all the different configurations. According to the results obtained in the validation set, the SVM-RBF-C using reflectance data was selected to evaluate the test set. This classifier achieved the highest F1-score and the lowest standard deviation across folds. Hence, the algorithm was retrained by combining all training and validation data (a total of 3,542,028 labelled pixels extracted from 63 HS images of 63 patients) to increase the amount of information used in the supervised classifier’s learning process. Exploring multiclass classification for CIN lesion grading

As a proof of concept, we also evaluated a multiclass pixel-wise classifier to grade cervical tissue as healthy, LSIL, HSIL, or IC according to the Bethesda system (Supplementary Fig. S1). The best validation performance was obtained with the SVM-RBF-C classifier in reflectance mode (F1-score: 0.62 ± 0.06), which was subsequently retrained on the full training and validation sets and tested on the independent cohort. However, performance decreased markedly on the test set, with an F1-score of 0.50 ± 0.28 for the healthy class, compared with 0.05 ± 0.01, 0.15 ± 0.28, and 0.21 ± 0.17 for LSIL, HSIL, and IC, respectively. Spectral comparison between training and test groups revealed inter-patient variability within the same diagnostic categories, which may have limited classifier generalisation (Supplementary Fig. S2). In addition, spectral overlap between diagnostically adjacent classes was evident, particularly between LSIL and healthy tissue and between HSIL and IC, leading to misclassification in some patients. Qualitative examples further showed that many errors arose from confusion between HSIL and IC, suggesting that the model captured clinically relevant high-grade abnormalities but was less reliable for strict subclass discrimination (see Supplementary Results).

Discussion and conclusions

This study demonstrates the potential of HSI for real-time in-vivo diagnosis and delimitation of CIN lesions and IC in the cervix. This study presents a novel approach for assisted colposcopy using HSI. We have developed a HS colposcope, which has been used for the acquisition of 245 HS images from 116 patients during a 32-month observational study period in routine cervical cancer screening consultations. Based on a preliminary data acquisition campaign, the spectral characteristics of these types of tissues were studied and published in a previous work30, providing evidence that the HSI could capture tissue-specific spectral signatures. In this study, we present a new approach that focuses on pixel-wise classification using an expanded dataset of cervical tissue and exploits the spectral information present across different cervical lesion categories.

Compared to state-of-the-art approaches, our method reports pixel-level performance metrics based on gynaecologist-annotated GT derived from biopsy-confirmed diagnoses, and generates biopsy-referenced classification maps, instead of analysing the cervix globally or by quadrants using only patient-level metrics25. Our study is methodologically closer to Zheng et al.28, who produced spatial maps to differentiate pixels into normal, inflammation, high-grade lesion, unknown, and specular reflections. However, that work did not provide any quantitative global metrics, so we cannot directly compare with them. The most comparable clinical product for in-vivo cervical lesion assessment is DYSIS, which generates a colour-coded 2D map based on the aceto-whitening of the tissue to highlight any suspicious region. Clinical studies have shown that adjunctive DYSIS can improve patient-level CIN2+ detection and biopsy efficiency, but these reports remain focused on subject-level and biopsy-level outcomes rather than on quantitative evaluation of lesion delimitation33–36. Unlike DYSIS, which uses a proprietary aceto-whitening score and does not capture full spectral data, the proposed method analyses each pixel’s spectrum, aiming to classify tissue as LSIL, HSIL, or IC (aligned with CIN1–3). Nevertheless, published DYSIS studies report results at the patient or biopsy level, and, to the best of our knowledge, do not provide quantitative metrics on lesion-delimitation accuracy33–36.

Our work introduces a new method for segmenting the cervical tissue into three key areas (ectocervix, endocervix, and abnormal elements) while also improving the cervical region delimitation approach previously presented for HS images30. The segmentation method achieved strong results, with a macro-level DICE of 0.84 in pixel-level segmentation and enabled in-depth analysis of the cervical tissue. Beyond its standalone performance, the segmentation stage constrains the analysis to anatomically meaningful cervical regions, reducing the influence of noise and improving the robustness of subsequent spectral classification. Additionally, separating the ectocervix and endocervix introduces a clinically relevant boundary, enabling more interpretable and spatially consistent classification maps that align with expert assessment.

Based on this first segmentation of the cervical area, two approaches were evaluated for spectral classification of cervical tissue: a binary model to distinguish HSIL+ IC from healthy cervical tissue, and a multiclass classifier to grade each pixel according to the Bethesda classification (healthy, LSIL, HSIL, and IC). The first method (HSIL+ IC classifier) demonstrated its potential for clinical applications, with an F1-score ranging from 0.43 to 0.98 across the test set, from the worst to the best case, respectively. This large variability could be due in part to inter-patient variability in the dataset, but mostly to issues in the HS data acquisition process, such as patient movement during acquisition or subtle illumination shifts. An in-depth analysis of the HS data revealed that acquisition artefacts, caused by the combination of patient movement and the HS acquisition technology employed by the camera, degraded spectral fidelity and produced a drop in the classification performance. This is especially noticeable in P1, which obtained the worst quantitative classification results (Fig. 3e).

The exploratory multiclass classification experiment achieved an F1-score of 0.62 on the validation set for the best-performing model, but performance declined markedly on the independent test set, yielding an overall F1-score of 0.26 across all classes. Spectral analysis revealed substantial inter-patient variability and high spectral similarity between healthy tissue and LSIL, and between HSIL and IC, which led to frequent misclassification between diagnostically adjacent classes. These errors are also consistent with routine clinical practice, where even experienced colposcopists face difficulties in visually differentiating such lesions without histopathological confirmation8,10. Further studies with larger datasets will therefore be necessary to determine whether multiclass hyperspectral classification can achieve robust and clinically meaningful performance.

For the presented method to be applicable in clinical practice, it must first reduce the current acquisition time of 45 s. Our current HS colposcope is based on a spatio-spectral scanning system, which enables high spatial and spectral resolution, but the resulting image quality is strongly degraded by any movement during acquisition. In this study, such effects were partially mitigated by excluding low-quality acquisitions with significant motion artefacts during data curation, although residual variability remains. This technological limitation of our system could be reduced in future work by selecting the most relevant bands for spectral analysis, thereby enabling the use of snapshot cameras for real-time acquisition of the HS cube in under one second. Another alternative is spectral scanning systems combined with algorithms to compensate for movement between bands, which allow for high spatial resolution and shorter acquisition times. Furthermore, an extensive dataset must be generated using this new system to train algorithms on a large population that reflects the inherent variability across different patients and lesion types.

In conclusion, HSI has the potential to evolve into a clinically valuable diagnostic support tool for the non-invasive detection and delimitation of CIN and IC. Future tools, based on this technology, can be especially relevant in the context of minimally invasive therapy, where accurate visual classification enables precise lesion delineation, guiding targeted biopsies and local excision while preserving surrounding healthy tissue. By providing objective, real-time information, HSIs could reduce interobserver variability, improve the detection of clinically significant lesions, and ultimately enhance patient outcomes within cervical cancer prevention and treatment programs.

HS clinical data collection

The HS data for this study were acquired with the HS colposcope previously developed by this group30. This system was developed using a commercially available colposcope (Optomic OP-C5, OPTOMIC, Spain), in which the manufacturer removed the infrared filter to allow halogen light to pass beyond 750 nm. Then, an HS camera (Snapscan VNIR, IMEC, Belgium), which covers the 470–900 nm spectral range, was attached to the colposcope head for acquiring HS images with 1000 × 900 pixels and 158 spectral bands. The illumination system used a 150 W halogen light source (Fibrolux-150, OPTOMIC, Spain), with a halogen bulb (OSRAM, 64,634 HLX EFR), which was connected to the colposcope head via a fibre optic guide.

The gynaecologists acquired HS images during routine cervical cancer screening consultations using a custom-developed easy-to-use application designed to be used in clinical settings. When feasible, two HS images were obtained per patient: (i) a baseline HS image of the cervix after removing secretions and debris, and (ii) a subsequent HS image after the application of acetic acid and after cytology. Only the baseline HS images have been employed in this study, as the second HS image was excluded to avoid capturing the temporal dynamics of acetowhitening, which could alter the spectral signature. In addition, cytology can induce minor bleeding or superficial abrasions in the cervical epithelium, which may alter the spectral signal and contribute to motion or reflectance artifacts observed in some patients. During a 32-month data collection period, 245 HS images were obtained from 116 patients, together with their associated sociodemographic and clinical information. The clinical study was approved by the ethical committee of the Hospital Universitario de Gran Canaria Dr. Negrín, Spain (ref. 2022-081-1). All the participants involved in this study and/or their legal tutors were informed about the research and voluntarily signed an informed consent form, allowing their participation and the anonymous publication of the results. All research methodologies were performed in accordance with current national guidelines and regulations.

Lesions were diagnosed according to the standard clinical protocol, where patients initially undergo a colposcopy examination, followed by liquid-based cytology (ThinPrep Pap Test PreservCyt™ Solution) and an HPV testing (Cobas 4800). Colposcopic images are described according to the 2011 International Federation for Cervical Pathology and Colposcopy (IFCPC) colposcopy terminology37. The grading of the lesions was carried out according to established histopathological criteria, using two standardised and globally recommended classification systems, complementary and commonly used in clinical practice. The Bethesda system38, used for cytology, classifies lesions as LSIL, HSIL, and IC, while the histological result is classified according to WHO terminology as LSIL/CIN 1, HSIL/CIN 2, HSIL/CIN 3 and HPV-associated squamous cell carcinoma. LSIL corresponds to CIN1, whereas HSIL includes CIN2 and CIN3. Lesions with invasion beyond the basement membrane are classified as IC in the WHO histological classification. The reference diagnosis was established in accordance with routine clinical practice. In patients with abnormal colposcopic findings, histopathology was the primary reference standard. In patients without visible abnormalities, classification relied on the available non-invasive clinical assessment, including colposcopy, cytology, and HPV testing. In addition, the evolution of the diagnosis based on posterior clinical evaluations was incorporated into the associated clinical data.

Participants were consecutively recruited from a specialised cervical gynaecology consultation, to which patients were referred after an abnormal cervical screening result. Eligible participants were women aged ≥ 18 years who provided written informed consent and underwent colposcopy, cytology, and HPV testing during the clinical consultation. Both first-time consultations and follow-up visits were eligible; however, each participant was included only once in the dataset, and no longitudinal follow-up analysis was performed. Pregnant women were excluded from the study. The presence of a cervical lesion was not required for inclusion; therefore, the cohort also included women referred after abnormal screening who were subsequently found to have no cervical pathology.

Of the 77 patients included in the study, 63 were assigned to the training and validation group, while the remaining 14 patients were reserved as an independent test set. The data split was performed at the patient level to avoid any data leakage between sets, ensuring that no pixels from the same patient were present in both training/validation and test stages. A fourfold cross-validation strategy was applied to the 63 training/validation patients. In each fold, approximately 75% of the patients were used for training and 25% for validation. The folds were constructed using a stratified sampling approach, ensuring that all diagnostic classes were represented in each split. Each HS image in this group was annotated at the pixel level into four distinct classes: ectocervix, endocervix, outliers, and LSIL (CIN1), HSIL (CIN2-3), and IC. Lesion annotations (CIN and IC) were performed manually by an experienced gynaecologist based on biopsy-confirmed diagnoses, biopsy location, and acetowhitening process. Biopsy location was recorded using the cervical clock-face notation39 and the lesion extent was delimited based on the post-acetic-acid capture by using the aceto-whitening as the reference (Fig. 5b). It is relevant to acknowledge that due to the 45-s acquisition time, the HS image after the acetic acid application might be affected by dynamic whitening changes. This did not affect the HS image analysis, as these images were used only for the gynaecologist’s visualisation after converting them to synthetic RGB, and their spectral signatures were not used in this study. Finally, the resulting delimitation was manually transferred onto the baseline HS image, ensuring that only pre-acetic-acid spectral information was analysed. However, because the baseline and post-acetic-acid images do not have exact pixel-wise correspondence, this step may introduce some boundary imprecision, particularly at lesion margins. The patients with a cancer diagnosis were exclusively labelled as IC and not assigned to any other class. The remaining classes (ectocervix, endocervix, and outliers) were labelled using an unsupervised method and evaluated by a gynaecologist, who made any necessary corrections. Pixels segmented as ectocervix and endocervix were divided into two groups based on patient information. Ectocervix and endocervix pixels from patients diagnosed as HPV-negative and clinically classified as normal in accordance with colposcopic examination and cytology were used as the reference for the healthy class. This has to be considered a conservative reference class rather than a histology-confirmed gold standard. All other ectocervix and endocervix pixels from patients who did not meet the previous conditions (HPV-infected, CIN 1, CIN 2–3) were grouped as potentially infected and were not used to train the classifiers during this study.

The independent test set comprised 14 patients not used during model development (see Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5). These patients were randomly selected while enforcing a balanced distribution between healthy and pathological cases (7 HSIL/IC and 7 healthy) to enable a controlled evaluation of classification performance across classes. Although this distribution does not reflect the disease’s natural prevalence, it enables a more reliable assessment of model sensitivity and specificity. These patients were manually annotated by an expert colposcopist, who identified the healthy cervical areas (endocervix and ectocervix) and the lesions (LSIL, HSIL, and IC). The lesion annotations were confirmed by a biopsy, which indicates the corresponding higher grade for the sample but does not provide spatial information, as it is captured at a single position. The test set was employed to assess the experiments conducted in this study, including both unsupervised segmentation and supervised classification.

Unsupervised segmentation of the cervical tissue

The tissue in the cervical region is not homogeneous, being divided into the ectocervix (also named exocervix), which corresponds to squamous epithelial tissue, and the endocervix, which is the inner part of the cervix and covers the internal endocervical canal. The junction between both tissues is the squamous-columnar junction, which defines the transformation zone, the region of highest risk for the development of the CIN lesions. Furthermore, cervical tissue (ectocervix and endocervix) may be affected by a precancerous lesion or an invasive carcinoma. The lesions found in the dataset were manually annotated by an expert gynaecologist based on the biopsy area and the posterior treatment. However, the remaining tissues and external elements were annotated using an unsupervised segmentation method.

The proposed unsupervised tissue segmentation method (Fig. 6b) relies on spatio-spectral clustering and the analysis of the tissue-specific spectral properties. First, pixels outside the cervical region are removed. SAM is then computed between each pixel and the mean spectral signature of all cervical pixels, and pixels with SAM values above 0.25 (empirically determined) are discarded as outliers. The remaining pixels are segmented into two classes: endocervix and ectocervix. A spatio-spectral clustering step is then applied using the SLIC algorithm41 on the reflectance HS image, producing 200 clusters that group neighbouring pixels with similar spectra. For each cluster, the centroid spectrum is extracted, and absorbance maps are generated at specific wavelengths (550 nm, 620 nm, 795 nm, and the range from 660 to 650 nm) to enable subsequent detection of the different tissues.

The endocervix is composed of glandular tissue, which presents a noticeable difference morphologically and in its spectral response compared to the squamous epithelial tissue that forms the ectocervix. Similar to the blood, it presents a large spectral contribution of the haemoglobin characteristics peaks, but it also presents a consistent higher absorption in the 620 nm band. After classifying certain pixels as endocervix, all the remaining pixels are preliminary considered part of the ectocervix. If a centroid presents a SAM error larger than the mean spectral signature plus the standard deviation, it is rejected from the ectocervix. Supervised classification of cervical pixels

This study presents a methodology for classifying the spectral signature of cervical tissue using spatial annotations for each capture (Fig. 6c). Given the dataset’s class imbalance, the classification framework was designed to reduce bias toward the most frequent categories. Various machine learning algorithms were evaluated in combination with different preprocessing methods. In addition, two classification approaches were analysed based on different diagnostic class grouping strategies: (i) a binary classification between HSIL + IC and healthy tissue and (ii) a multi-class classification to identify healthy cervical tissue, LSIL, HSIL and IC. To perform the binary classification, the dataset was divided into two categories: HSIL + IC (composed of IC, CIN 3, and CIN 2 lesions) and healthy cervical tissue (composed of the ectocervix and endocervix tissue without any lesions). This first approach aims to validate the ability of the proposed method to detect lesions in the cervical tissue and compare with previous technologies, such as DYSIS36. In the multi-class classification, the dataset was labelled as healthy tissue, LSIL (CIN1), HSIL (CIN2–3), and IC, in order to explore the limits from this method to identify the different grades of a cervical lesion.

In both approaches, three preprocessing modes were applied to the input data across all algorithms: reflectance, absorbance and second derivative mode. For testing, only the classifier corresponding to the best-performing data mode was retrained by combining the training and validation sets, and this model was subsequently evaluated on the independent patient test set.

In addition, a one-vs-all methodology was employed in both approaches, where each classifier was trained to distinguish its specific class from all other classes. For each class’s training, 25,000 pixels were selected, with a 50/50 split between the target class and the other classes. Due to the small sample size and class imbalance, training was performed using a fourfold cross-validation strategy with stratified sampling across classes, ensuring that at least one patient from each group was included in each fold. The methodology was further evaluated using 6 different classifiers: SVMs with the SVM-L, SVM-Q, SVM-C, and SVM-RBF kernels; DT with a maximum of 50 splits; and RF with a maximum of 200 trees. In the case of SVM-RBF, three different configurations were evaluated based on the kernel size: a kernel size of 9.75 (SVM-RBF-M) obtained by the Normalised Silverman’s rule of thumb method from Tapia et al.42, a fine kernel of size 5 (SVM-RBF-F), and a coarse kernel with a size of 46 (SVM-RBF-C). In total, 8 different classifier configurations were evaluated. Each classifier configuration was evaluated across the three preprocessing modes described above (reflectance, absorbance, and second derivative), yielding a total of 24 analysed configurations per classification approach. Furthermore, each trained classifier was evaluated on each data partition using its respective validation set. Model selection was based on the highest mean validation F1-score across folds, while also accounting for variability between folds. Due to the one-vs-all configuration, multiple classifiers could be activated for one input spectrum. The MATLAB R2023a (Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox) implementation of the different classifiers provides a score metric that, for the SVM, indicates the signed mean distance of the given input from the decision boundary. As this score depends on the input data, a method is proposed to normalise it across different classifiers to the range 0 to 1. The normalisation is performed based on the cumulative distribution of scores obtained from the model’s predictions on the training data. During the validation and test stages, each classifier’s score is normalised using the cumulative distribution function, and the class with the highest score is assigned to that pixel.

Evaluation metrics

The unsupervised segmentation was evaluated based on the manual annotations provided by the gynaecologist. For the evaluation, binary masks were generated for each class, and only the predictions over annotated pixels were quantitatively evaluated. From the annotations, true positive (TP), false positive (FP), false negative (FN), and true negative (TN) results were generated for each class, and metrics were calculated for each capture and averaged globally. The Dice coefficient, also known as the F1-score, is defined in Eq. (8) and provides a balanced measure that accounts for FP and FN in a single metric. For the experiments, we report the Dice coefficient for segmentation results and the F1-score for classification results, following the standard terminology in each context. Metrics were computed independently for each class, and the model’s global score is the mean of the class scores. Classification performance was further measured using precision and recall on the annotated data. Precision measures the accuracy of positive predictions by calculating the proportion of TP to the sum of TP and FP, as shown in Eq. (9).
