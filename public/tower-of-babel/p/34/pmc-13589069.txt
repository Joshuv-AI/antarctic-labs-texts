Rutin alleviates heat stress-induced intestinal oxidative damage in broilers via AMPK/PINK1-Parkin signaling pathway
Authors: Hongyan Ding, Chang Zhao, Xu Ji, Fen Zhou, Weizhe Yan, Zhenhao Ma, Daoliang Zhang, Lei Wang, Xiaoxia Shi, Wanyue Huang, Xichun Wang, Shibin Feng, Dong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxidative stress is a primary pathogenic factor in heat stress (HS)-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction, causing substantial economic losses in poultry production and compromising animal welfare. Rutin, a natural flavonoid with potent antioxidant properties and prominent intestinal protective effects, represents a promising nutritional intervention. This study investigated the protective efficacy and underlying mechanisms of rutin against intestinal oxidative damage using both in vivo and in vitro HS models. One hundred sixty 21-day-old Arbor Acres broilers were randomly assigned to four treatments: control (C), rutin (R; 500 mg/kg diet), HS (H), and HS + rutin (HR), with four replicates of 10 birds each. Broilers in the H and HR groups were exposed to 34 ± 2°C for 8 h/d to induce HS. Concurrently, chicken small intestinal epithelial cells (CIECs) were assigned to the same four groups, with HS induced at 43°C for 3 h. Mechanistic validation was performed using compound C (an AMP-activated protein kinase [AMPK] inhibitor) and Parkin small interfering RNA (siRNA). The results showed that HS induced systemic and local oxidative stress and impaired intestinal mucosal barrier function, both of which were significantly mitigated by dietary rutin supplementation. In vitro evaluations revealed that rutin preserved cellular viability, restored mitochondrial membrane potential, and enhanced tight junction protein expression. These protective effects were driven by the activation of the AMPK/PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)-Parkin pathway, which up-regulated mitophagy to clear dysfunctional mitochondria. Conversely, inhibition of AMPK or knockdown of Parkin abolished rutin-mediated mitochondrial protection and barrier restoration in CIECs under HS. In conclusion, rutin alleviates HS-induced intestinal oxidative injury and barrier dysfunction by promoting AMPK/PINK1-Parkin-mediated mitophagy. These findings demonstrate that rutin is an effective dietary antioxidant that can optimize gut health and heat resistance in commercial broilers.

Introduction

With the intensification of global warming, heat stress (HS) has become a major challenge to the poultry industry worldwide, resulting in substantial economic losses and compromised animal welfare (Saracila et al., 2021). Due to feather coverage and the absence of sweat glands, broilers have limited heat dissipation capacity and are therefore more susceptible to HS than many other livestock species (Ban et al., 2024). HS markedly increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, disrupts cellular redox homeostasis, and induces oxidative injury in multiple tissues and organs (Teyssier et al., 2023). Among these, the intestine is particularly vulnerable to HS exposure. As the primary site for nutrient digestion and absorption, intestinal integrity is essential for maintaining growth performance and production efficiency in poultry (Kikusato and Toyomizu, 2023). Previous studies have demonstrated that HS impairs intestinal function by promoting excessive ROS accumulation, damaging epithelial cells, and reducing the expression of tight junction proteins, ultimately increasing intestinal permeability and compromising barrier integrity (Kikusato and Toyomizu, 2023; Zhu et al., 2025). Therefore, alleviating intestinal oxidative stress and preserving epithelial barrier function represent promising strategies for mitigating HS-induced intestinal dysfunction in poultry.

Rutin is a naturally occurring flavonoid with well-established antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and cytoprotective properties (Wang et al., 2024). Increasing evidence suggests that rutin exerts beneficial effects on intestinal health by enhancing antioxidant defense and maintaining epithelial barrier integrity (Zou et al., 2025). In murine models of colitis, rutin improves epithelial integrity while suppressing inflammatory signaling pathways (Sharma et al., 2021). In poultry, dietary supplementation with rutin increases the activities of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), while reducing serum malondialdehyde (MDA) levels (Hassan et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2026). Rutin has also been shown to improve intestinal villus morphology and enhance immune-related indicators, including secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), immunoglobulin M (IgM), immunoglobulin G (IgG), interleukin-4 (IL-4), and interleukin-10 (IL-10) (Li et al., 2022). These findings suggest that rutin is a promising nutritional antioxidant for protecting intestinal function and improving heat tolerance in poultry. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the protective effects of rutin against HS-induced intestinal injury remain largely unclear.

Mitophagy is a selective mitochondrial quality control process that eliminates damaged mitochondria to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis and reduce potential sources of oxidative stress (Wen et al., 2024). Among the regulatory pathways involved, the PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase (Parkin) pathway plays a central role in mediating mitophagy, and disruption of this pathway leads to impaired mitochondrial clearance and increased oxidative stress (Li et al., 2023, 2024). AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a key cellular energy sensor activated under stress conditions such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, regulates multiple biological processes associated with oxidative stress and mitophagy (Xu et al., 2023). Emerging evidence indicates that HS suppresses AMPK activation in broilers (Ma et al., 2025), whereas rutin has been shown to enhance AMPK activity and upregulate PINK1/Parkin signaling in livestock and cellular models subjected to oxidative stress (Ding et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025). These findings suggest that the protective effects of rutin on intestinal health may involve activation of AMPK-dependent mitophagy.

However, whether rutin alleviates HS-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction in broilers through AMPK/PINK1-Parkin-mediated mitophagy remains unclear. Clarifying this mechanism would improve our understanding of the cellular basis underlying rutin-mediated intestinal protection and provide new insights into nutritional strategies for enhancing heat resistance in poultry. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the protective effects of rutin against HS-induced intestinal oxidative damage and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, with a particular focus on the AMPK/PINK1-Parkin signaling pathway, using both in vivo and in vitro HS models.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

All animal procedures conducted in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Anhui Academy of Agricultural Sciences (approval no. AAAS2023-2) and were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations for the care and use of experimental animals.

Summary of experimental procedures

A total of 300 one-day-old Arbor Acres broilers were raised under identical management conditions until 21 d of age. At 21 d, 160 broilers with similar body weights were randomly assigned to 4 treatment groups (Fig. 1A): control group (C; ad libitum feeding at 24 ± 2°C), HS group (H; ad libitum feeding with exposure to 34 ± 2°C for 8 h/d from 09:00 to 17:00), rutin supplementation group (R; basal diet supplemented with 500 mg/kg rutin, ≥95% purity; Shanghai Hongye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; maintained at 24 ± 2°C), and HS+rutin group (HR; basal diet supplemented with 500 mg/kg rutin and exposed to 34 ± 2°C for 8 h/d from 09:00 to 17:00). Each treatment consisted of 4 replicate cages with 10 broilers per cage. At 42 d of age, following 12 h of feed withdrawal, 2 broilers per cage with body weights closest to the cage mean were selected for sample collection. The selected birds were anesthetized, blood samples were collected via the wing vein, and jejunal tissues and mucosal samples were subsequently harvested for further analyses.

Cell culture and treatment

Chicken small intestinal epithelial cells (CIECs; Cat. No. BFN60807505) were obtained from Qingqi Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM-HG) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. To evaluate the protective effects of rutin against HS-induced injury, CIECs were pretreated with different concentrations of rutin (0, 25, 50, or 100 μM; ≥98% purity; Shanghai Hongye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 2 h at 37°C, followed by exposure to 43°C for 3 h to induce HS, according to the method described by Song et al. (2025). To investigate the involvement of AMPK and Parkin in rutin-mediated protection, inhibition experiments were performed using the AMPK inhibitor Compound C (CC) or Parkin siRNA. Briefly, cells were pretreated with 5 μM CC for 2 h or transfected with Parkin siRNA for 24 h, followed by treatment with 50 μM rutin for 2 h before exposure to HS (43°C for 3 h). After treatment, cells were harvested for subsequent analyses.

Histological analysis

Jejunal tissues were collected and fixed in paraformaldehyde fixative (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) for 48 h. Following fixation, tissues were dehydrated, cleared, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned at a thickness of 6 μm using a microtome. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were stained using a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (Servicebio.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Histological sections were examined under a light microscope, and representative images were captured using an imaging system.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). CIECs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 100 μL of culture medium. After 24 h of incubation to allow cell attachment, cells were treated with the indicated reagents for the designated durations. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 solution, and cells were incubated at 37°C for 2 h. The optical density (OD) of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Antioxidant and biochemical assays

Antioxidant parameters, including SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and MDA levels in serum, jejunal mucosa, and CIECs, were measured using commercial biochemical assay kits (Shanghai Youxuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum concentrations of D-lactic acid (D-LA) and diamine oxidase (DAO), indicators of intestinal permeability, were also determined using commercial biochemical assay kits (Youxuan) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

ROS measurement

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe. Following treatment with the indicated reagents or Rosup reagent (positive control; Beyotime), cells were gently washed once with buffer solution. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, 1 mL of 10 μM DCFH-DA working solution (Beyotime) was added to each well, and cells were incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. Subsequently, the DCFH-DA solution was removed, and cells were washed 3 times with buffer solution. Cells were then detached using trypsin, centrifuged at 500 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in buffer solution. ROS fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry in the FITC channel using a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Shanghai, China). A minimum of 10,000 events was acquired for each sample.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using a JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after treatment with the indicated reagents or carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP; positive control), CIECs were gently washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The JC-1 working solution was prepared by mixing JC-1 dye with culture medium at a 1:1 ratio, and 2 mL of the mixture was added to each well. Cells were incubated at 37°C for 20 min in the dark, washed twice with JC-1 staining buffer, and resuspended in buffer solution for flow cytometric analysis. The ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity was used to assess mitochondrial membrane potential.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection

CIECs were seeded into 12-well plates and cultured until approximately 60% confluence was reached. The culture medium was then replaced with antibiotic-free Opti-MEM medium (Servicebio). After 24 h, siRNA was diluted in antibiotic-free Opti-MEM medium to final concentrations of 10, 20, 50, and 100 nM. The diluted siRNA was then mixed with Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), which had been separately diluted in an equal volume of Opti-MEM medium. Following incubation at room temperature for 20 min, the transfection mixture was added to the CIECs and incubated at 37°C for 6 h. Subsequently, the transfection medium was replaced with complete culture medium, and cells were cultured for an additional 12 h before subsequent treatments. The siRNA targeting Parkin was synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The siRNA sequences targeting Parkin were as follows: sense, GCAUCUAAAGAAACAAUCA; antisense, UGAUUGUUUCUUUAGAUGC.

Western blotting

Protein extraction and western blotting were performed as previously described by Wen et al. (2024). Briefly, total proteins were extracted from jejunal mucosa and CIECs using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer. Protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Occludin, Claudin-1, ZO-1, Beclin-1, LC3, phosphorylated AMPKα (p-AMPKα), PINK1, and Parkin. Membranes were then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Prior to statistical analysis, data normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. For the in vivo broiler experiment, each cage was considered the experimental unit for growth performance analyses, whereas individual birds served as experimental units for serum biochemical parameters and jejunal tissue measurements. For in vitro experiments, each independent culture well was considered one biological replicate. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test when assumptions of homogeneity of variance were met; otherwise, the Games–Howell post hoc test was applied. Comparisons between 2 groups were conducted using Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, whereas P < 0.01 was considered highly significant. In figures, * and # indicate significant differences (P < 0.05), whereas ** and ## indicate highly significant differences (P < 0.01). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and graphical visualizations were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Rutin protects the intestinal barrier against HS-induced oxidative damage by enhancing antioxidant capacity in broilers

Broilers were exposed to 34°C for 8 h/d to induce HS. As shown in Fig. 1C and D, HS significantly reduced antioxidant capacity in serum, as evidenced by decreased activities of SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, and T-AOC, accompanied by increased MDA levels. These findings indicate that HS induced oxidative stress in broilers. In contrast, rutin supplementation significantly increased serum SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, and T-AOC levels while reducing MDA content in HS broilers, suggesting an improvement in antioxidant capacity under HS conditions. Similarly, rutin supplementation alleviated HS-induced oxidative stress in jejunal tissue, as demonstrated by increased antioxidant enzyme activities and T-AOC, together with reduced MDA levels (Fig. 1C and D).

H&E staining was subsequently performed to evaluate jejunal morphology. As shown in Fig. 1E and Table 2, compared with the C group, the H group exhibited significantly decreased villus height and villus height/crypt depth ratio, whereas crypt depth was significantly increased. Compared with the H group, broilers in the HR group showed significantly increased villus height and villus height/crypt depth ratio, along with reduced crypt depth. D-LA is an indicator of intestinal permeability (Jin et al., 2025), whereas elevated serum DAO levels reflect intestinal mucosal injury (Nieto et al., 2000). In the present study, serum D-LA and DAO concentrations were significantly increased in the H group compared with the C group, whereas rutin supplementation significantly reduced both parameters in the HR group relative to the H group (P < 0.01; Fig. 1B). Consistent with these findings, western blot analysis showed that rutin supplementation increased the expression of tight junction proteins, including Claudin-1, Occludin, and ZO-1, under HS conditions (Fig. 1F). These results suggest that rutin supplementation alleviated HS-induced intestinal mucosal injury and reduced intestinal permeability in broilers.

Rutin alleviates intestinal oxidative damage in CIECs

An in vitro HS model was established to investigate the mechanisms underlying the protective effects of rutin against HS-induced intestinal oxidative injury in CIECs (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2C, exposure to 43°C for 3 h significantly reduced cell viability. To determine the optimal concentration of rutin, CIECs were treated with different concentrations of rutin (0, 25, 50, and 100 μM). Compared with the control group, treatment with 50 μM rutin significantly increased cell viability, whereas 100 μM rutin reduced cell viability (Fig. 2B). However, protective effects of rutin against HS-induced injury were observed at concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 μM (Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 4A, HS increased the expression of mitophagy-related proteins, including PINK1, Parkin, Beclin1, and LC3, in jejunal tissue, whereas rutin supplementation further enhanced their expression. Similarly, in CIECs, HS induced the expression of PINK1, Parkin, Beclin1, and LC3, and rutin treatment further increased the abundance of these proteins (Fig. 4B). Rutin promotes mitophagy and alleviates intestinal oxidative damage through AMPK in HS-induced CIECs

AMPK plays an important role in regulating autophagy, including mitophagy. To further investigate whether AMPK is involved in rutin-mediated Parkin-dependent mitophagy, the expression of AMPK-related proteins was examined. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, rutin treatment increased the phosphorylation level of AMPKα in both HS broilers and HS-induced CIECs. These findings suggest that AMPK may participate in rutin-mediated mitophagy induction and antioxidant regulation under HS conditions.

To further determine the role of AMPK in rutin-mediated protection, inhibition experiments were performed using CC, an AMPK inhibitor. Based on western blot and cell viability results, 5 μM CC was selected for subsequent experiments. Compared with the H group, rutin treatment enhanced antioxidant capacity in broilers and CIECs, as shown in Fig. 1C and Fig. 2D–F. However, CC attenuated the protective effects of rutin by decreasing SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, and T-AOC levels while increasing MDA and ROS accumulation (Fig. 6G and H). Furthermore, CC abolished the rutin-induced upregulation of tight junction proteins, including Occludin, ZO-1, and Claudin-1, in HS-treated CIECs (Fig. 6I). In addition, inhibition of AMPK reduced the expression of mitophagy-related proteins, including PINK1, Parkin, Beclin1, and LC3, thereby counteracting the enhanced expression induced by rutin under HS conditions (Fig. 6D–F). The restorative effects of rutin on mitochondrial membrane potential and COX I activity were also eliminated following CC treatment (Fig. 6J and K). These findings suggest that AMPK activation is involved in the protective effects of rutin against HS-induced oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and epithelial barrier impairment, potentially through regulation of PINK1/Parkin-associated mitophagy in CIECs.

Discussion

Chicken is one of the most widely produced and consumed sources of animal protein worldwide, and the poultry industry is increasingly challenged by rising global temperatures. Modern commercial broilers are particularly susceptible to HS because of their limited heat dissipation capacity and high metabolic rate. Previous studies have demonstrated that HS adversely affects growth performance in broilers (Ma et al., 2026). Consistent with these findings, HS significantly reduced final body weight, ADFI, and ADG in the present study (Hu et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2026). Previous research has also shown that dietary rutin supplementation alleviates HS-induced growth retardation (Chen et al., 2023). Similarly, our results demonstrated that rutin supplementation effectively mitigated the reduction in growth performance caused by HS, although no significant improvement in F/G was observed.

Improved growth performance under HS conditions may have practical implications for poultry production by reducing performance losses associated with thermal stress and enhancing production efficiency. In addition, rutin supplementation decreased body temperature and respiratory rate in broilers exposed to HS, suggesting an attenuation of physiological stress responses. These effects may contribute to improved resilience to HS and more stable production performance. Maintaining stable growth performance under HS conditions is beneficial for improving production consistency and reducing economic losses associated with environmental stress in commercial poultry systems.

The intestine is among the organs most susceptible to HS, and accumulating evidence indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction-induced oxidative stress is a key pathological mechanism underlying HS-associated intestinal injury in broilers (Rostagno, 2020). Under HS conditions, intestinal blood flow in broilers is reduced, leading to impaired oxygen and nutrient utilization. Consequently, mitochondrial respiratory chain activity is disrupted, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS production, which further exacerbates oxidative stress (Akbarian et al., 2016). Oxidative stress has been shown to reduce intestinal epithelial cell viability and downregulate the expression of tight junction proteins, including Claudin-1, Occludin, and ZO-1, thereby compromising epithelial barrier integrity (Zhuang et al., 2019). Consistent with these previous findings, the present study demonstrated that HS markedly increased oxidative stress in both in vivo and in vitro models, accompanied by increased intestinal permeability and impaired mitochondrial function. These observations suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction-driven oxidative stress plays a central role in HS-induced intestinal barrier impairment. Therefore, interventions targeting mitochondrial oxidative damage may represent an effective strategy for preserving intestinal integrity and alleviating HS-induced intestinal dysfunction in broilers.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that natural antioxidants help maintain redox homeostasis in broilers exposed to HS (Yuan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Rutin, a plant-derived flavonoid, has been reported to exert potent antioxidant effects in the poultry intestine (Hassan et al., 2018). Consistent with previous findings, the present study showed that rutin alleviated oxidative stress by increasing SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, and T-AOC levels while reducing ROS and MDA accumulation in both in vivo and in vitro models. These findings suggest that rutin effectively preserves intestinal redox homeostasis under HS conditions and may serve as a promising nutritional antioxidant for mitigating oxidative damage in broilers.

In addition to inducing oxidative stress, HS disrupts epithelial junctions and impairs intestinal barrier integrity in broilers. In the present study, HS reduced the expression of tight junction proteins, including Claudin-1, Occludin, and ZO-1, while increasing serum D-LA and DAO levels, indicating increased intestinal permeability and mucosal injury. In contrast, rutin supplementation significantly enhanced the expression of Claudin-1, Occludin, and ZO-1 and reduced serum D-LA and DAO concentrations, suggesting a protective effect on intestinal barrier function. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies. In a murine colitis model, rutin increased the expression of tight junction proteins, reduced serum FITC-dextran and endotoxin levels, and improved intestinal permeability (Liu et al., 2022). Likewise, in aged laying hens, dietary rutin enhanced antioxidant capacity and increased the mRNA expression of ZO-1 and Mucin2 (Li et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings suggest that rutin protects intestinal barrier integrity under HS conditions, potentially through coordinated regulation of oxidative stress and epithelial junction stability.

Mitochondria are highly dynamic organelles in eukaryotic cells and are particularly susceptible to damage under HS conditions. Consistent with previous findings (Shi et al., 2024), HS induced mitochondrial dysfunction, as evidenced by decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and reduced COX I activity. The accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria can promote excessive ROS production and subsequent oxidative stress, which represents an important pathological mechanism underlying HS-induced intestinal injury in broilers.

Rutin has been widely reported to alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. In intestinal epithelial Caco-2 cells, rutin increased ATP levels and restored mitochondrial membrane potential following indomethacin-induced injury (Carrasco-Pozo et al., 2012). In rats exposed to AlCl₃-induced toxicity, rutin administration increased the activities of COX I and COX IV (Kessas et al., 2024). Similarly, rutin has been shown to improve hepatic mitochondrial function in HS broilers (Ma et al., 2025). Consistent with these previous findings, the present study demonstrated that rutin increased mitochondrial membrane potential and restored COX I activity in HS-treated intestinal epithelial cells. These results suggest that the protective effects of rutin on intestinal function in HS broilers may be largely attributable to its ability to preserve mitochondrial integrity and attenuate mitochondria-derived oxidative stress.

Mitophagy is an essential mitochondrial quality control mechanism that removes damaged mitochondria, thereby limiting excessive ROS accumulation and maintaining mitochondrial function (Su et al., 2023). To date, however, the effects of rutin on mitophagy in HS-induced CIECs have not been investigated. In the present study, we first examined whether HS activates mitophagy in intestinal epithelial cells. Our results showed that HS increased the expression of mitophagy-related proteins, including PINK1, Parkin, LC3-I/II, and Beclin1, suggesting activation of a compensatory mitophagic response aimed at eliminating damaged mitochondria. Nevertheless, this endogenous response appeared insufficient to effectively remove dysfunctional mitochondria, as mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress remained elevated under HS conditions. Notably, rutin supplementation further enhanced the expression of these mitophagy-related proteins.

To further investigate the role of mitophagy in rutin-mediated protection, Parkin, a key regulator of mitophagy, was depleted using siRNA. Parkin knockdown abolished the protective effects of rutin on mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and tight junction protein expression in CIECs exposed to HS, suggesting that Parkin-dependent mitophagy is involved in rutin-mediated protection. Consistent with the findings of Ma et al. (2025), our results indicate that rutin may facilitate the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria through modulation of mitophagy-related signaling pathways (Ma et al., 2025).

However, it should be noted that changes in the expression of mitophagy-related proteins alone provide only indirect evidence of altered mitophagic activity and cannot exclude the possibility of impaired autophagic flux. Therefore, the observed alterations in PINK1, Parkin, LC3, and Beclin1 should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies incorporating mitophagy flux assays are needed to dynamically monitor the entire mitophagic process and more accurately elucidate the role of mitophagy in HS-induced intestinal injury and rutin-mediated protection.

AMPK is a key cellular energy sensor that plays an important role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and responding to cellular energy stress, and has been widely implicated in the regulation of mitophagy across multiple cell types (Xia et al., 2025; Zuo et al., 2024). Previous studies have shown that AMPK can regulate PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy by enhancing PINK1 stability and promoting its accumulation on the outer mitochondrial membrane, thereby facilitating Parkin recruitment and activation (Longo et al., 2024). Activated Parkin subsequently ubiquitinates damaged mitochondrial proteins, promoting mitochondrial clearance through autophagic degradation. Together, the AMPK–PINK1/Parkin axis constitutes a central regulatory pathway involved in mitochondrial quality control.

In the present study, inhibition of AMPK markedly reduced the expression of PINK1, Parkin, LC3-I/II, and Beclin1, suggesting that rutin-mediated regulation of mitophagy-related signaling is associated with AMPK activation. Furthermore, inhibition of either AMPK or Parkin attenuated the protective effects of rutin on mitochondrial membrane potential, oxidative stress, and intestinal epithelial barrier integrity. These findings indicate that AMPK/PINK1/Parkin signaling is involved in the protective effects of rutin against HS-induced intestinal injury. However, because mitophagy was evaluated primarily through static protein markers rather than flux assays, the precise contribution of enhanced mitophagic activity requires further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, rutin alleviated HS-induced oxidative stress, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and mitochondrial impairment in broilers and CIECs. The protective effects of rutin were associated with activation of AMPK/PINK1-Parkin signaling and modulation of mitophagy-related pathways. Inhibition of AMPK or depletion of Parkin attenuated the beneficial effects of rutin on antioxidant capacity, mitochondrial function, and epithelial barrier integrity, suggesting an important role for AMPK/PINK1-Parkin signaling in rutin-mediated protection. These findings provide mechanistic insights into the protective effects of rutin against HS-induced intestinal injury and highlight its potential as a nutritional strategy for improving intestinal resilience under HS conditions in poultry.
