Metabolic disruption associated with e-cigarette vaping and secondhand exposure: Urinary metabolomics evidence from a Thai population
Authors: Preechaya Tajai, Suparada Sawatdikun, Tapakon Srisopa, Giatgong Konguthaithip, Pantira Parinyarux, Panida Ditsawanon, Piyapan Charoensook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing use of electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) has raised concerns regarding potential health effects associated with both direct use and secondhand exposure to e-cigarette aerosols. However, human evidence describing systemic metabolic alterations related to these exposures remains limited. In this study, metabolomics was applied to compare urinary metabolic profiles among active e-cigarette users, individuals exposed to secondhand e-cigarette aerosols, and unexposed control participants. Multivariate analyses were performed to identify exposure-related metabolic signatures, followed by pathway enrichment and evaluation of candidate biomarkers. Distinct metabolic differences were observed across exposure groups. Direct e-cigarette use was associated with alterations in amino acid metabolism, mitochondrial energy pathways, and redox-related processes, with glutathione metabolism identified as a key associated pathway and oxidized glutathione highlighted as a candidate urinary biomarker of redox-related metabolic alterations. Secondhand exposure was primarily associated with alterations in alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, with L-glutamic acid demonstrating discriminatory performance as a candidate metabolite associated with metabolic differences related to secondhand e-cigarette aerosol exposure. Comparative pathway analyses suggested that direct e-cigarette use was associated with more extensive metabolic alterations than secondhand exposure, particularly in pathways linking glutamine-glutamate metabolism, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and glutathione metabolism, suggesting a possible graded biological response to aerosol exposure. In addition, nicotine metabolism-related metabolites and e-liquid constituents, including propylene glycol, glycerol, and cotinine N-oxide, were identified as candidate urinary exposure biomarkers of e-cigarette exposure. Overall, these findings suggest that e-cigarette aerosol exposure may be associated with measurable metabolic responses, even at indirect exposure levels, and highlight the potential utility of metabolomics as an approach for identifying both exposure biomarkers and biomarkers of biological effect.

Introduction

The global prevalence of electronic cigarettes or e-cigarette use among younger individuals is approximately 16.8%, and the popularity of these products continues to increase worldwide [1], [2], [3], [4]. However, e-cigarettes remain prohibited in more than 25 countries, including Thailand [3], [4]. National data indicate that e-cigarette use in Thailand has risen consistently each year, with the number of users increasing by around fivefold since 2015 [3]. Additionally, passive e-cigarette exposure, or secondhand vaping, has raised concerns regarding potential health risks [5], [6], [7]. However, the prevalence of secondhand vaping remains unclear [6]. Further studies are required to understand better its global health impact and the associated health effects [5], [6], [7].

E-cigarettes are devices that heat a liquid such as propylene glycol and glycerol, to produce an inhalable aerosol that may contain nicotine [2], [3], [8]. These electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) were introduced as alternatives to conventional cigarettes and provide a method for vaporizing nicotine-containing solutions and flavoring agents instead of burning tobacco [2], [4]. However, e-cigarettes are not risk-free because their emissions can contain harmful substances such as diacetyl, a flavoring agent associated with pulmonary toxicity at high concentrations, as well as tobacco-specific nitrosamines and various volatile organic compounds [9], [10], [11], [12]. Many of these chemicals have uncertain long-term health effects, raising concerns about potential long-term health risks of respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, including potential associations with lung cancer [9], [10], [11], [12].

Health risks of secondhand e-cigarette aerosol remain uncertain, and many people continue to underestimate its potential harm, such as believing it causes only minimal harm or that it is just water vapor [5], [6], [7]. However, several studies have reported that indoor vaping exposes bystanders to airborne nicotine at levels lower than tobacco smoke, but still potentially meaningful [5], [7]. Secondhand e-cigarette exposure is also associated with respiratory symptoms, immunological effects, cardiovascular risks, as well as increased cotinine concentrations, suggesting that secondhand exposure may not be biologically inert and could be associated with adverse health effects [7].

Recent metabolomics studies have suggested that exposure to e-cigarette aerosols is associated with alterations in several metabolic pathways, indicating potential systemic biological effects of vaping [13]. Several human metabolomics studies have reported that vaping is associated with distinct metabolic profiles characterized by biomarkers related to oxidative stress and inflammation, alterations in energy metabolism, and changes in amino acid and lipid metabolic pathways [13], [14], [15]. These metabolic differences distinguish e-cigarette exposure from conventional smoking and non-exposure and suggest measurable biological responses [13], [14], [15]. In addition, in vivo and in vitro studies provide mechanistic evidence supporting the biological effects of e-cigarette aerosols and suggest that aerosol exposure may be associated with tissue injury, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and alterations in metabolic pathways involving amino acids, lipids, and cellular energy production [16], [17], [18]. Human metabolomic evidence on secondhand vaping is limited, with only two studies to date demonstrating measurable biomarkers of secondhand exposure in vulnerable populations, including pregnant women and children [19], [20]. Therefore, this metabolomics study aims to investigate urinary metabolite profiles in individuals who vape, those with secondhand exposure, and unexposed control participants. Metabolomics may provide a useful approach for identifying candidate exposure biomarkers and exploring molecular pathways associated with both e-cigarette use and secondhand exposure [21], [22].

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and informed consent

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (Human Ethics Committee) of the Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University, Thailand (Ethics Approval Reference: 282/2024 and 085/2025, Study Code: FOR−2567–0336 and FOR−2567–0760). Eligible participants included individuals who had actively used e-cigarettes for at least three months. Participants in the secondhand exposure group did not use tobacco products and had regular household exposure to a primary e-cigarette user for at least three months. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment, and the study results were reported anonymously.

Study design, setting, and population

This cross-sectional study investigated urinary metabolic profiles among individuals who actively used e-cigarettes, those exposed to secondhand e-cigarette aerosol, and unexposed control participants. Participants were Thai adults aged 20 years or older who resided in northern Thailand. Three groups were assigned as follows: (1) the e-cigarette user group included individuals who actively used e-cigarettes for at least three continuous months, with no concurrent use of conventional cigarettes or other tobacco products; (2) the secondhand exposure group consisted of individuals who did not use tobacco products and had regular household exposure to a primary e-cigarette user for at least three months; and (3) the control group comprised unexposed adults who neither used e-cigarettes nor lived in proximity to e-cigarette users and were frequency matched to the exposed groups according to age and sex distributions. Tobacco use status was determined by participant self-report, and no biochemical assessment of combustible tobacco exposure was performed. Only participants meeting the inclusion criteria were eligible for the study. First-morning urine samples were collected from all participants to minimize the effects of diurnal variation on urinary metabolite profiles.

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR)-based metabolomics method

Reagents, internal standard (I.S.), quality control (Q.C.), and sample preparation

Methanol and chloroform were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and deuterium oxide (D2O) was obtained from Merck-Sigma Group (St. Louis, MO, USA). Trimethylsilyl propanoic acid (TSP) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was selected as the internal reference compound for chemical shift calibration. Q.C. samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots from all samples and analyzed together with the experimental samples to monitor analytical stability and minimize analytical variation.

Urine samples were lyophilized and reconstituted in 0.6 mL of 0.1 M TSP in D2O. Metabolite measurements were performed on a 500 MHz NMR spectrometer using an optimized protocol to minimize interference from residual water signals [21], [22].

Acquisition parameters, peak assignment, and data analysis

1H NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker AVANCE 500 MHz spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) using a Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG, RD–90°, (t–180°), n-acquire) pulse sequence at 27 °C, with water suppression achieved through pre-saturation. The acquisition settings consisted of 16 scans, a relaxation delay of 1 s, an acquisition time of 3.95 s, a spectral width of 8278.146 Hz, and a 90° pulse with 16 signal averages. Baseline and phase adjustments were performed using TopSpin software (version 4.0.7, Bruker, Bremen, Germany).

Spectra within the 0–12 ppm range were processed, and metabolites were annotated using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB version 5.0, accessed 1 April 2025). TSP was used as the internal chemical shift reference [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]. Peak acquisition, chemical shifts, and J-coupling were analyzed using TopSpin software (version 4.0.7, Bruker, Bremen, Germany). MestRenova software (version 12.0.0, MestreLab Research, Santiago de Compostela, Spain) was used for spectral visualization.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

MetaboAnalyst (version 6.0, http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst, accessed on 1 April 2025) was used to evaluate metabolic differences in urine samples from e-cigarette users, individuals with secondhand exposure, and unexposed control participants. Prior to statistical analyses, data were median-normalized, log10-transformed, and auto-scaled using the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 workflow. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) model performance was evaluated using cross-validation and permutation testing implemented in MetaboAnalyst, and the resulting score plots were interpreted as exploratory visualization of overall metabolic differences among the study groups. The identified urinary metabolites were used as the input metabolite set for pathway mapping and subsequent enrichment analysis. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Homo sapiens pathway library from the Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB) implemented in MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Pathway enrichment was based on over-representation analysis, and pathway topology was evaluated using relative-betweenness centrality. Raw p-values generated by MetaboAnalyst were used for exploratory pathway interpretation, and pathway impact values were calculated according to the pathway topology algorithm implemented in MetaboAnalyst. Heatmap visualization and Pearson correlation analyses were performed to explore metabolic differences among the study groups. Candidate metabolites associated with e-cigarette exposure or biological responses were further evaluated using the Classical Univariate Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Analysis module implemented in MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Individual metabolites were assessed according to their discriminatory performance using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), while 95% confidence intervals and t-test p-values generated by MetaboAnalyst were used as complementary statistical measures for feature evaluation.

Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize the demographic characteristics of the 66 study participants. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%), and continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD). All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.4; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; www.graphpad.com, accessed on 1 April 2025).

Analysis of related pathways for e-cigarette use

Pathway enrichment and impact analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst (version 6.0) to investigate metabolic pathway alterations in urine samples from e-cigarette users compared with unexposed control participants. The analysis identified glutathione metabolism as the most prominently enriched pathway associated with e-cigarette use (Fig. 2). Glutathione metabolism plays a central role in maintaining cellular redox balance, antioxidant defense, and the regulation of cellular processes [26], [27]. Glutathione deficiency is known to contribute to oxidative stress, which is implicated in aging and the pathogenesis of various diseases [26], [27]. The observed enrichment of this pathway suggests that e-cigarette use is associated with alterations in glutathione-related metabolic processes, which may reflect changes in systemic redox regulation. In addition to glutathione metabolism, propanoate metabolism showed modest enrichment, suggesting potential alterations in energy-related metabolic pathways. The prominent enrichment of glutathione metabolism observed in the present study is consistent with findings from previous in vitro metabolomic investigations examining the biological effects of e-cigarette exposure [16], [28]. Studies using human lung epithelial A549 cells have demonstrated that exposure to increasing concentrations of e-cigarette vapor extract induces oxidative stress in a concentration-dependent manner, accompanied by significant alterations in glutathione metabolism identified through untargeted LC-MS/MS-based metabolomics [16]. Similarly, metabolomic analyses of human bronchial epithelial (BEAS−2B) cells exposed to e-cigarette aerosols, including vanillin-flavored formulations, have identified alterations in glutathione metabolism as well as energy, amino acid, antioxidant, and sphingolipid metabolic pathways associated with oxidative stress and several human diseases [28], [29]. In addition, in vivo studies have reported elevated oxidative damage markers, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), in mice exposed to e-cigarettes [18]. Metabolomic analyses further demonstrated that e-cigarette exposure altered 24 metabolic pathways, including amino acid metabolism and perturbations of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [18]. Together, these experimental findings align with our metabolomic results, suggesting that alterations in glutathione-related pathways observed at the cellular level may be reflected systemically in human e-cigarette users [26], [27]. Collectively, evidence from in vitro, in vivo, and human studies suggests that oxidative stress-related metabolic dysregulation may represent an important biological response associated with e-cigarette aerosol exposure [16], [18], [28], [30], [31], [32], [33].

Glutathione is maintained through both de novo synthesis from its constituent amino acids, including glutamate, cysteine, and glycine, and through the recycling of oxidized glutathione (GSSG) to its reduced form (GSH) [27], [34]. The dynamic balance between GSH and GSSG is essential for cellular redox homeostasis and plays a central role in protecting cells against oxidative stress [27], [34]. Oxidized glutathione is regenerated by glutathione reductase (GSR), which utilizes NADPH primarily supplied by the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) [27], [34]. Dysregulation of glutathione homeostasis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases [27], [34]. In this study, a decrease in oxidized glutathione was observed in e-cigarette users, consistent with pathway analysis suggesting alterations in glutathione metabolism and possible disruption of redox regulation associated with e-cigarette use. These findings are consistent with previous in vitro metabolomic studies in human lung epithelial (A549) and human bronchial epithelial (BEAS−2B) cells, which identified oxidized glutathione as a candidate biomarker and reported alterations in its levels following e-cigarette exposure [16], [28], [29].

Analysis of related pathways for secondhand exposure to e-cigarettes

Pathway enrichment and impact analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst (version 6.0) to investigate metabolic pathway alterations in urine samples from participants with secondhand exposure to e-cigarette aerosols compared with unexposed control participants. The analysis identified alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism as the most prominently enriched pathway associated with secondhand exposure (Fig. 5). Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism play an important role in amino acid turnover and intermediary metabolism, linking nitrogen metabolism with energy-related pathways [35], [36], [37]. Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate can enter central metabolic pathways, including the TCA cycle, to support energy production and biosynthetic processes [35], [36], [37]. In the present study, glutamate levels were decreased in the secondhand exposure group compared with unexposed control participants, suggesting alterations in amino acid metabolism and its integration with energy metabolism. Glutamine serves as a key metabolic intermediate within this network, contributing to glutamate availability and linking amino acid metabolism to both mitochondrial energy metabolism and cellular redox regulation [35], [37]. Consistent with this role, enrichment of the TCA cycle further suggests alterations in mitochondrial energy metabolism associated with secondhand exposure, as supported by the pathway analysis results. Evidence from metabolomic studies on secondhand e-cigarette exposure remains limited [19], [20]. One untargeted LC-MS/MS metabolomic study in pregnant women reported associations between secondhand exposure and metabolites involved in the pentose phosphate pathway, which is linked to cellular energy production and redox balance, consistent with our study [19]. In addition, an untargeted plasma metabolomic study in infants and children with secondhand e-cigarette exposure identified menthol glucuronide as a flavorant-related metabolite associated with secondhand exposure [20]. Pathway analysis revealed alterations in several metabolic pathways related to antioxidant systems and mitochondrial energy metabolism [20]. Furthermore, menthol glucuronide was correlated with oxidized lipids, suggesting that secondhand e-cigarette flavorant exposure may be associated with disruptions in redox-related metabolic processes that have been implicated in human lung disease [20].

An in vitro metabolomic study using BEAS−2B human bronchial epithelial cells exposed to maltol-flavored electronic nicotine delivery system (ENDS) vapors demonstrated that both firsthand and secondhand exposures disrupted amino acid metabolism, with alterations in key metabolites including glutamine and asparagine, consistent with our findings [38]. Glutamine is an important substrate for the TCA cycle through its conversion to glutamate and α-ketoglutarate, thereby linking amino acid metabolism directly to mitochondrial energy production [38]. These metabolite changes occurred in a dose-dependent manner, with secondhand exposure producing intermediate effects relative to firsthand exposure, suggesting that low-level aerosol exposure may be associated with measurable metabolic changes [38]. Additionally, secondhand exposure-associated alterations in antioxidant-related pathways and oxidized thiol metabolism suggest subtle changes in redox regulation, supporting the biological relevance of interconnected amino acid, energy, and redox metabolic pathways that may influence cellular homeostasis and could be associated with respiratory health outcomes [38].

Analysis of related pathways for e-cigarette users compared with secondhand exposure

Pathway enrichment and impact analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst (version 6.0) to investigate metabolic pathway alterations in urine samples from e-cigarette users compared with individuals with secondhand exposure to e-cigarette aerosols. The analysis identified alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism as the most prominently enriched pathway differentiating direct from indirect exposure (Fig. 8). This pathway plays a central role in amino acid turnover and intermediary metabolism, linking nitrogen metabolism with energy production through its integration with the TCA cycle [35], [36], [37]. Consistent with this finding, enrichment and pathway impact of the citrate cycle (TCA cycle) were also observed, suggesting differences in mitochondrial energy metabolism between e-cigarette users and individuals with secondhand exposure. Alterations in TCA-related intermediates suggest altered oxidative energy metabolism, with greater metabolic differences associated with direct e-cigarette use. In addition, glutathione metabolism was identified among the enriched pathways, suggesting differences in redox-related metabolic processes between groups and coordinated alterations in amino acid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and redox balance. Several previous studies have investigated metabolite alterations associated with e-cigarette exposure, with one untargeted metabolomic study reporting significant changes in TCA cycle-related metabolites, particularly in the plasma of e-cigarette users (p = 0.0086) [15]. Most TCA cycle-related metabolites were downregulated in e-cigarette users, consistent with our findings [15]. E-cigarette exposure may induce mitochondrial oxidative stress, dysregulation of mitochondrial complexes, and mitochondrial DNA damage, suggesting alterations in mitochondrial bioenergetic metabolism [15]. Moreover, salivary metabolomic profiling identified valine as a potential biomarker in e-cigarette users [33]. Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, provide carbon sources for the synthesis of TCA cycle intermediates; thus, alterations in their levels may lead to imbalances in TCA cycle reactions [33].

An in vivo study of acute e-cigarette exposure in mice demonstrated pronounced metabolic disturbances, characterized by downregulation of amino acid-related metabolites and disruption of TCA cycle-associated pathways [18]. Alterations in glutamine-glutamate, alanine-aspartate-glutamate, and glycine-serine-threonine metabolism, together with alterations in glutathione and taurine pathways, were consistent with impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism, redox imbalance, and elevated oxidative stress markers [18]. These metabolomic changes were further linked to metabolic disorders and lung disease, suggesting that amino acid- and TCA-related metabolites may serve as candidate biomarkers associated with the systemic biological effects of e-cigarette exposure [18]. Furthermore, exposure of BEAS−2B human bronchial epithelial cells to e-cigarette aerosols containing vanillin as a flavorant was associated with alterations in several amino acids, including glutamine, proline, isoleucine, leucine, and hydroxyproline, suggesting alterations in nitrogen metabolism, protein turnover, and mitochondrial energy pathways [29]. Consistent with these findings, other in vitro studies using human lung epithelial A549 cells demonstrated that exposure to e-cigarette vapor extract resulted in disruption of multiple metabolic pathways, including amino acid metabolism (glutamine-glutamate, alanine-aspartate-glutamate, and branched-chain amino acids) and glutathione metabolism, accompanied by increased oxidative stress biomarkers, suggesting coordinated changes in mitochondrial energy production and redox homeostasis [16].

Taken together, several human, in vivo, and in vitro metabolomic studies are consistent with our pathway analysis, suggesting that direct e-cigarette use may be associated with greater metabolic differences than secondhand exposure, particularly in pathways linking glutamine-glutamate metabolism, alanine-aspartate-glutamate metabolism, mitochondrial energy production, and glutathione metabolism, which may reflect systemic metabolic responses to e-cigarette aerosol exposure.

Exposure biomarkers for e-cigarette exposure

Urinary metabolomic profiling was used to identify candidate exposure biomarkers capable of distinguishing active e-cigarette users from unexposed control participants and individuals with secondhand e-cigarette aerosol exposure. Comparisons between individuals with secondhand exposure and unexposed control participants were not further evaluated in this exposure biomarker analysis because nicotine metabolism-related metabolites and e-liquid constituents did not demonstrate significant discriminatory performance between these two groups.

Active e-cigarette users were characterized by higher relative levels of metabolites associated with e-liquid composition and nicotine metabolism. Compared with unexposed control participants, propylene glycol and cotinine N-oxide were significantly elevated in the urine of e-cigarette users (Fig. 10). Propylene glycol findings were consistent with its role as a primary solvent in e-cigarette liquids, while cotinine N-oxide reflected increased systemic nicotine uptake among e-cigarette users. Further comparisons between active e-cigarette users and individuals with secondhand e-cigarette aerosol exposure identified nicotine, cotinine N-oxide, and glycerol as metabolites that differentiated the two groups. Nicotine from e-cigarette aerosols undergoes hepatic metabolism primarily via CYP2A6-mediated oxidation, resulting in the formation of cotinine as the major metabolite, followed by secondary metabolites including trans−3-hydroxycotinine, cotinine N-oxide, nornicotine, and 6-hydroxynicotine [15], [39]. Human metabolomic analyses in e-cigarette users demonstrated significant increases in cotinine, cotinine N-oxide, L-nornicotine, (S)-nicotine, trans−3-hydroxycotinine, and (R)−6-hydroxynicotine compared with controls, indicating robust activation of the nicotine degradation pathway following vaping exposure [15], [39]. These increases reflect enhanced nicotine biotransformation rather than downstream metabolic toxicity and suggest the utility of nicotine metabolism-related metabolites as candidate exposure biomarkers for e-cigarette use, consistent with our findings [15], [39].

Taken together, these findings suggest that e-liquid-related compounds, including propylene glycol, glycerol, nicotine, and cotinine N-oxide, may serve as candidate urinary exposure biomarkers for e-cigarette use.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to apply 1H NMR-based metabolomics to comprehensively compare e-cigarette users with unexposed control participants, individuals exposed to secondhand aerosol with unexposed control participants, and active e-cigarette users with those exposed to secondhand aerosol in a Thai population. Distinct exposure-related metabolic profiles were identified, characterized by alterations in amino acid metabolism, mitochondrial energy pathways, and redox-related processes. Glutathione metabolism emerged as a prominent pathway associated with direct e-cigarette use, with oxidized glutathione identified as a candidate urinary biomarker of redox-related metabolic changes, whereas alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism and L-glutamic acid were associated with secondhand e-cigarette aerosol exposure. Comparative pathway analyses further suggested that direct e-cigarette use may be associated with greater metabolic differences than secondhand exposure, particularly in pathways linking glutamine-glutamate metabolism, the TCA cycle, and antioxidant defense, suggesting graded metabolic responses across exposure groups supported by pathway-level alterations and candidate biomarkers of biological effects (Fig. 12). Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the relatively small sample size, particularly for the secondhand exposure group, may have limited statistical power and contributed to uncertainty in metabolomic discrimination. In addition, detailed characterization of e-cigarette aerosol exposure, including vaping frequency, duration of exposure, indoor ventilation, device type, nicotine concentration, flavoring agents, e-liquid composition, and household exposure intensity, was not systematically available and may have contributed to exposure variability and possible misclassification. Lifestyle and environmental factors that may influence urinary metabolomic profiles were not comprehensively assessed, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Furthermore, external validation of the identified candidate metabolites was not performed. Future studies with larger, well-characterized cohorts, detailed exposure assessment, and longitudinal designs are warranted to validate these findings and to further clarify the metabolic effects of direct and secondhand e-cigarette aerosol exposure.

Taken together, these findings suggest that e-cigarette aerosol exposure, including indirect exposure, is associated with measurable metabolic differences in humans and support the use of metabolomics as an approach for identifying candidate exposure biomarkers and metabolites associated with biological responses. This study provides novel human evidence on secondhand e-cigarette exposure and identifies metabolic pathways that may be associated with the biological responses to e-cigarette aerosol exposure. Future longitudinal studies with larger populations are warranted to validate these candidate biomarkers and to further investigate the health implications of chronic e-cigarette use and secondhand aerosol exposure.
