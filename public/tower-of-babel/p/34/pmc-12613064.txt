Mycobacterium lentiflavum cutaneous pseudotumor in an immunosuppressed host
Authors: Lo Tamburro, Uzoamaka Eke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mycobacterium lentiflavum infections have mostly been described as cervical lymphadenitis in children, or occasionally as pulmonary infection in immunocompromised hosts. Here, we present Mycobacterium lentiflavum as an isolated cutaneous pseudotumor in an immunosuppressed patient. The lesion was treated with surgical excision, which resolved without any recurrence after a year of follow up. This case shows the various presentations of this pathogen and the option of surgical excision instead of antimicrobial systemic therapy to achieve cure.

Introduction

Mycobacterium lentiflavum is a slow growing, pigmented, non-tuberculous mycobacterium with a distinctive mycolic acid pattern on high performance liquid chromatography, most closely resembling M. simiae and M. genavense by its unique 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequence, first identified in 1976 [1,2]. Like other nontuberculous mycobacteria, M. lentiflavum has been isolated from soil and drinking water sources and some human infections have been linked to water sources [3,4]. Its clinical importance has been primarily limited to reports of cervical lymphadenitis in children aged 2–6 years, a few cases of pulmonary disease, and isolated reports of bone and joint infections in immunocompromised and, rarely, immunocompetent adults aged 45–85 years [5]. Most of the cases are reported in Europe, with newer reports of nodular bronchiectactic pulmonary disease in Japan, predominantly in women, similar to Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) [6].

Cutaneous infection by M. lentiflavum is very rare, limited to only 2 case reports in the literature; one case from Brazil in relation to lipolytic injections in an immunocompetent patient, and the other from Spain, in a patient with AIDS who actively injected drugs [7,8]. In this report, we present a case of M. lentiflavium cutaneous infection in the U.S, which presented as a localized spindle cell pseudotumor in an immunosuppressed patient with no known inoculation source.

Case report

49-year-old African American female with multiple medical comorbidities including myasthenia gravis, on chronic prednisone therapy at 30 mg daily, mycophenolate mofetil at 1000 mg twice daily and monthly intravenous immunoglobulin injections, steroid induced diabetes and bilateral hip fracture, s/p intramedullary nail fixation bilaterally in May 2023. She had multiple admissions for recurrent infections including abdominal wall abscesses and bilateral hip intramedullary nail-associated infected hematomas and prolonged courses of antibiotics. She was incidentally found to have a left upper extremity lesion of 6 months duration in May 2023. The lesion, located on the medial part of the middle of the patient’s left arm, started as a fleshy sub centimeter lesion that remained relatively unchanged in size until 4 months prior to admission, when it started to increase in size and bleed intermittently. The patient was a non-smoker and had no pets. The patient did not engage in any occupation or outdoor activities that exposed her to soil or water in the environment. She was not aware of any local trauma, injury, bites, or other form of inoculation into the site. Physical exam revealed a 2 cm, firm, non-mobile, non-tender, and non-fluctuant fleshy lesion on an erythematous base with raised edges and bleeding surface that was not warm to palpation. (Fig. 1). A biopsy of the lesion revealed a monotonous proliferation of spindled to epithelioid histocytes arranged in a vaguely storiform pattern, mixed with clusters of neutrophils and sparse lymphocytes. Acid fast bacteria (AFB) and Fite stains showed numerous intracellular mycobacterial organisms in the histiocytes, consistent with a mycobacterial spindle cell pseudotumor (Fig. 2). The patient’s laboratory findings were WBC = 8.5 K/mcL, hematocrit = 29.4 %, platelet = 178 K/mcL, serum creatinine = 0.92 mg/dl, aspartate transaminase = 66 units/L, alanine transaminase = 88 units/L, alkaline phosphatase 244 units/L and total bilirubin = 0.9 mg/dl. AFB blood culture had no growth. HIV was non-reactive.

Due to the patient’s immunosuppressed status, there was concern for dissemination of the mycobacterial infection. CAT scan of the chest, abdomen and pelvis did not reveal any other lesions or lymphadenopathy. PET CT scan was performed in May and September 2023, which revealed focal increased metabolic activity in the region of the left upper arm soft tissues, corresponding to the area of the lesion, with no other hypermetabolic lesion to suggest metastatic infection.

The challenge for medical treatment for this patient was that she was receiving a prolonged course of systemic antibiotics for her recurrent infections. The patient was also allergic to topical lidocaine negating the use of local anesthetic. Eventually, surgical resection of the lesion was performed 6 months later under general anesthesia. Culture from the resected lesion also grew M. lentiflavum. Discussion

In this report, we present the first case report of M. lentiflavum in the U.S, and a rare, yet to be reported presentation as a cutaneous pseudotumor without any known inoculating source or predisposition. The organism was identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry, an FDA approved, rapid, cost-effective diagnostic method used in the microbiology laboratory to identify atypical mycobacteria [9,10].

Two other cutaneous cases of M. lentiflavum are reported in the literature, and both cases were most likely related to direct inoculation, one from a lipolytic formula injection, and the other from injection drug use in a patient with AIDS. Both cases presented as ulcerations with induration and evidence of skin and soft tissue infection [7,8]. On the contrary, our case presented as a pseudotumor, which progressively increased in size, without any evidence or history of direct inoculation into the affected site. Nonetheless, it is possible that the patient acquired the infection from an unknown inoculation from a soil or water source in the environment or health care system.

M. lentiflavum infection in adults has been seen predominantly in immunocompromised hosts, like our patient, with most cases involving the lungs [5,6]. This predilection for immunosuppressed hosts suggests that it is an opportunistic pathogen with low virulence. Nonetheless, even in immunosuppressed hosts, M. lentiflavum has been isolated in mixed cultures with other atypical mycobacteria or other organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus, and patients have been reported to recover without targeted treatment for M. lentiflavum [4]. Hence, a biopsy may need to be performed as deemed necessary to determine if it is an actual pathogen when it is isolated. The histopathology of our patient’s lesion showed a proliferation of spindled to epithelioid histiocytes containing numerous mycobacterial organisms, consistent with Mycobacterium associated inflammatory pseudotumor, with no well-formed granulomas or caseating necrosis (Fig. 2). Mycobacteria spindle cell tumors are rare entities that have been described in HIV/AIDS and other immunocompromised hosts, usually due to MAC, occurring mostly in lymph nodes, and rarely as cutaneous lesions [[11], [12], [13]]. Although this pseudotumor presentation appears to be benign, our patient’s lesion was progressively increasing in size, bled intermittently and caused the patient discomfort, both physically and aesthetically. It is unclear what the natural history and potential risk of mycobacterial dissemination would have been, had it not been excised. It was important to rule out disseminated disease in our patient given her immunocompromised status because disseminated infection has been reported in a patient with M. lentiflavum and the delayed diagnosis of dissemination in that case was associated with clinical deterioration [4].

Molecular diagnostics like 16S ribosomal RNA show that M. lentiflavum is most similar to M. simiae and M. genavense genetically. This is because there is a lot of overlap in the 16S gene that results in the cross identification of M. lentiflavum with M simiae and M genavense; hence, mass spectrometry is the preferred identification method. Other genes such as rpoB and hsp65 are often used for resolution to the species level of AFB, not 16S. [1,2,14,15]. While M. simiae is mostly associated with pulmonary infections in over 90 % of cases, M. genavense is responsible for disseminated infection in over 80 % of cases in immunosuppressed patients spanning HIV, solid organ transplant and patients with autoimmune conditions [16,17]. Phenotypically, M. lentiflavium is said to behave like MAC, with pulmonary disease disproportionately affecting women (∼70 %) where it presents as a nodular bronchiectasis similar to MAC [4,6].

The demography of published reports of M.lentiflavum infections is limited mainly to Europe, with scant reports in Asia and Australia [1,2,[4], [5], [6]]. Nonetheless, sampling of treated water in the U. S grew M. lentiflavum among other atypical mycobacteria [18]. Hence, it is only a matter of time before more clinical infections will be reported given that the prevalence of immunosuppressing conditions in U.S adults is estimated to be 6 %, which is more than double what it was in 2013 (2.7 %) [19].

There is heterogeneity in the treatment of M. lentiflavum infections. The majority of the cervical lymphadenitis cases in children were treated with surgical excision with resolution [4,5]. Pulmonary and disseminated disease have been treated similarly to MAC with a macrolide-based regimen, plus a rifampicin and ethambutol, with some cases also including isoniazid and pyrazinamide, which are classically used for M. tuberculosis [4,6]. In our patient, the dilemma we faced between choosing a surgical or pharmaceutical approach was the lack of data on how localized cutaneous cases should be treated. Some patients with localized nontuberculous mycobacterial infections have received systemic treatment alone while others received surgical and systemic treatment [20]. Of the cutaneous cases reported that were treated with systemic treatment, one of the cases improved and one was lost to follow up [7,8]. Based on the susceptibilities of our isolate, it appears that a macrolide-based regimen with a rifampicin ± linezolid or a newer quinolone, like moxifloxacin would have been an adequate regimen if systemic therapy was needed. We considered systemic treatment to be less desirable for our patient because she was already receiving several antimicrobials for other infections including trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for Pneumocystis jiroveci primary prophylaxis. This increased pill burden and the potential drug-drug interactions with the mycophenolate, rifampicin, and quinolones, if these medications were to be used, made us opt for surgical resection using the guidance from the successful treatment of localized lymphadenitis in children. So far, our patient has not experienced any recurrence of the lesion after a year of follow up.

Conclusion

M. lentiflavum is an emerging nontuberculous mycobacteria pathogen in immunosuppressed adults. The clinical presentation can vary from chronic pulmonary disease to an inoculation-related cutaneous infection as well as to a localized inflammatory pseudotumor as was found in our patient. It is important to ascertain the pathogenicity of the organism by performing a biopsy in some cases and efforts should be made to rule out disseminated disease, which may help determine the best approach to treatment. For localized cutaneous lesions where dissemination has been ruled out, surgical resection without systemic antimicrobial therapy may suffice for treatment.
