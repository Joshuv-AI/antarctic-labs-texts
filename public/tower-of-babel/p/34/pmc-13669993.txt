Transoral Ultrasound in the Diagnostic Workup of Peritonsillar Abscess—A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Tobias Todsen, Mads G Stage, Jacob Melchiors, Mathias Mejdahl, Amalie P Posselt, Anders R Madsen, Christoffer H Hahn, Christian Godballe, Sanne H Michaelsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeritonsillar abscess (PTA) is a common deep head and neck infection in younger adults. Because the condition can be difficult to distinguish from peritonsillar cellulitis on clinical examination, many patients undergo unnecessary diagnostic blind needle aspiration attempts. For this reason, transoral ultrasound has been suggested as a point‐of‐care imaging modality that may improve the diagnostic workup of PTA. This randomized clinical trial explores whether transoral ultrasound improves the management of patients with peritonsillar infection.MethodsA multicenter randomized clinical trial was conducted at the departments of Otorhinolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery at Copenhagen University Hospital—Rigshospitalet and Odense University Hospital in Denmark. Patients suspected of having a peritonsillar abscess were randomized to either standard clinical examination (control group) or standard clinical examination combined with transoral ultrasound (intervention group).ResultsNinety‐five patients (58 at Rigshospitalet and 37 at Odense University Hospital) were included in the study by 28 different physicians. Forty‐seven patients were allocated to the intervention group and 48 to the control group. The diagnostic accuracy was 80.9% in the intervention group versus 72.9% in the control group (p = 0.50). In the control group, 18 (37.5%) underwent unsuccessful needle aspirations compared to 12 (26.0%) in the intervention group. The intervention group underwent 31.9% fewer needle aspiration attempts compared with the control group (p < 0.01).ConclusionTransoral ultrasound can significantly decrease the number of needle aspiration attempts in patients with a suspected peritonsillar abscess. Still, we could not find any significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy or improved patient outcomes.Level of Evidence2.Keywords: infection, minimally invasive therapy, oropharynx, peritonsillar abscess, ultrasound/imaging

Introduction

Oropharyngeal infection, including peritonsillar cellulitis or abscess formation, is a common otolaryngology emergency, particularly among young adults [1]. Because of their similar clinical presentations, distinguishing peritonsillar cellulitis from peritonsillar abscess (PTA) on clinical examination alone can be challenging [2]. The correct diagnosis is crucial, as PTA requires early surgical treatment in the form of either needle aspiration, incision and drainage, or Quinsy tonsillectomy to avoid a potentially dangerous spread of pus to the surrounding tissues [2]. In contrast, peritonsillar cellulitis can often be managed with antibiotics alone. To achieve an accurate diagnosis, clinicians typically perform needle aspirations to determine whether pus is present or request a contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan [3, 4]. The recently developed radiation‐free, point‐of‐care diagnostic imaging modality, transoral ultrasound, has emerged to improve the diagnosis of patients with suspected PTA [5, 6]. Transoral ultrasound has shown great promise in oropharyngeal cancer diagnostics, with greater diagnostic accuracy than MR and clinical examination [7, 8] and can also be used to visualize parapharyngeal abscesses [9]. A meta‐analysis found that transoral ultrasound has high sensitivity (91%) and moderate specificity (75%) for diagnosing PTA; however, larger randomized trials are needed to determine the effect of transoral ultrasound use on patient outcomes. Only two studies (14 and 28 patients) have compared clinical evaluation to transoral ultrasound [8, 10]. The diagnostic accuracy in both studies were 64% in the non‐ultrasound group. This was mainly due to low specificity (aspiration attempts in patients without a PTA). Diagnostic accuracy based solely on clinical examination also depends on the clinician's level of experience [8, 10]. Previous studies have used transoral ultrasound as a diagnostic tool rather than directly guiding needle aspirations, which were still performed “blind”, that is, not guided by ultrasound [6, 10, 11, 12].

As a PTA is the most common deep head and neck infection, there is a substantial number of patients being affected by unnecessary aspiration attempts and possible prolonged disease due to insufficient diagnostics. This entails an economic burden that could be reduced with improved diagnostic accuracy. Blind aspirations of a suspected PTA can result in rare complications, such as hemorrhage [13, 14, 15]. To improve the aspiration technique and avoid rare complications, we developed a new transoral US technique using a small‐footprint transducer that enables real‐time guidance of transoral needle aspirations [16, 17]. Subsequently, we conducted a multicenter randomized clinical trial to evaluate whether transoral ultrasound improves the diagnostic workup of patients suspected of PTA compared with standard care [18].

Methods

We conducted a randomized controlled multicenter trial at the Departments of Otorhinolaryngology, Head & Neck Surgery and Audiology at Odense University Hospital and Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen University Hospital, Denmark. The protocol was registered at the ClinicalTrials.gov database (trial number: NCT03824288) and has been published [18]. Ethical approval was granted via an exemption letter from the Committee on Biomedical Research Ethics of the Capital Region of Denmark (protocol number: H‐18037012), and all participants provided informed consent. Participant data were stored in a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database [19, 20], with permission from the Capital Region of Denmark Data Protection Agency (protocol number: VD‐2018‐361).

Training Session for Residents

Danish otolaryngologists use head and neck ultrasound as an extension of the clinical examination daily, and formal neck ultrasound training is integrated into residency training [21]. However, because transoral ultrasound is not currently integrated into formal residency training, all residents on call received a one‐hour training session before they could include patients in the study. The training session consisted of a combination of didactic lectures and hands‐on simulation training of transoral ultrasound‐guided PTA aspiration on phantoms [18].

Enrolment of Study Participants

Patients suspected of having a PTA were referred from an emergency department or a general practitioner to the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, Head & Neck Surgery at either Odense University Hospital or Rigshospitalet. Patients presenting with either unilateral peritonsillar swelling and/or unilateral tonsillar protrusion and/or trismus were invited to participate in the study. However, patients could only participate if the ENT resident on call had completed training in transoral US. Exclusion criteria included: age under 18 years, inability to understand the verbal or written participant information, suspicion of a compromised airway or parapharyngeal abscess, or prior needle aspiration during their current course of disease. Patients who volunteered and met the inclusion criteria were included for randomization. The randomization was performed with a random permuted blocks technique available at https://www.sealedenvelope.com/ [22].

Clinical Examination and Treatment: All Patients

All participants underwent a clinical examination by the on‐call otolaryngology resident, including flexible trans nasal laryngoscopy. The degree of trismus was measured using a TheraBite (Atos Medical, Malmö, Sweden) range of motion scale. Needle aspiration was recommended if the suspicion of PTA was sustained after clinical inspection (control group) or transoral ultrasound (intervention group). The aspiration procedure is described separately for each group. Before needle aspiration, a spray of topical anesthetic (lidocaine 10 mg/dose) was applied, and 2% lidocaine with five micrograms of epinephrine was injected into the peritonsillar mucosa. Any aspirated pus was sent for microbiological examination. If no pus was aspirated or no needle aspiration was performed, a swab of the inflamed tonsil was sent for microbiological examination instead. After the needle aspiration, mosquito forceps were used to open the insertion site for additional drainage from the abscess cavity. After successful needle aspiration, patients were sent home with a follow‐up appointment in the outpatient clinic 24–36 h after their first consultation. If their general condition was affected, however, patients were admitted to the hospital for IV antibiotic treatment (suspected cellulitis) or Quincy tonsillectomy (suspected PTA). One month after their last visit, all patients received a phone call from one of the co‐authors, who asked them to report their total number of sick days since treatment.

Control Group

Since suspicion of PTA was an inclusion criterion, all patients in the control group underwent at least one needle aspiration attempt. A maximum of three needle aspiration attempts were undertaken, using a 14G needle attached to a 10 mL syringe on a Fine Needle Aspiration Handle. A needle aspiration was performed at the point of maximal protrusion; otherwise, one aspiration was attempted at each of the following positions: the upper, middle, and lower poles of the tonsil. In the case of pus, no further aspiration attempts were performed. If no pus had been aspirated after three attempts, the patient would either be discharged with Penicillin V and a planned follow‐up visit in the outpatient clinic or be admitted for either Quincy tonsillectomy or treatment with IV antibiotics.

Intervention Group

In the intervention group, the decision to perform a needle aspiration was preceded by a transoral US examination on a BK5000 US machine with a Burr‐Hole N11C5s and/or a larger Hockey Stick X18L5 transducer from BK Medical (BK Medical, Herlev, Denmark) (Figure 1). We recommended that the residents first use the Burr‐Hole transducer due to its smaller size. If they remained uncertain about the diagnosis after this, they could do a scan with the higher frequency Hockey Stick transducer. Before the scan, a topical anesthetic (lidocaine spray 10 mg/dose) was applied to the posterior pharynx to decrease the gag reflex. The transducer was protected with a medical glove filled with gel and placed on the area of peritonsillar swelling. If an abscess cavity was not immediately visible, a sweep from the cranial to the caudal end of the tonsil was performed (see technique described in previous publications [16, 17, 18, 23]). The residents were allowed to repeat the US examination in cases of uncertainty. If the transoral ultrasound examination did not confirm a PTA, no needle aspiration was attempted, even if an abscess was suspected on clinical examination. If a PTA was visible on US, an US‐guided aspiration was attempted using a custom‐made aspiration kit consisting of a 15 cm long 14G needle (Mediplast AB, Malmö, Sweden) attached to a 10 mL syringe via an IV extension tube (Figure 2A). The needle was attached to the Burr‐Hole transducer, and needle guide software (UA1346, BK Medical) was applied. If it was not possible to insert the needle guide in the mouth due to severe trismus, a free‐hand US‐guided technique with a 14G needle was performed instead (Figure 2B). If this approach was also not feasible, a blind needle aspiration was performed, guided by where the abscess had been visualized on the preceding transoral US examination. If the initial aspiration was without pus, up to two additional attempts were made.

After the procedure, the resident completed a subjective questionnaire assessing whether transoral ultrasound improved the diagnostic workup. If transoral ultrasound could not be performed due to a severe gag reflex or trismus, the data were still analyzed on an “intention to treat” basis.

Outcome Measures

The main objectives were to compare the total number of needle aspirations, the proportion of needle aspirations yielding pus in patients with suspected PTA, and diagnostic accuracy between the groups. A diagnosis of PTA was defined as the confirmation of pus (from needle aspiration or Quincy tonsillectomy) at the first or follow‐up outpatient clinic visit. Peritonsillar cellulitis was diagnosed if no pus was aspirated and the participant was successfully treated with antibiotics. The final diagnosis was established at the end of the study by reviewing the patient's chart.

Secondary outcomes were: the number of Quinsy tonsillectomies, days in hospital, visits in the outpatient clinic, patient‐reported sick days, and patient‐reported procedure‐related discomfort measured on an 11‐point numeric rating scale [24].

Exploratory measures were clinician's evaluation of the diagnostic benefit of transoral ultrasound in the intervention group and a calculation of the proportion of aspirations in the intervention group that were ultrasound‐guided.

Statistical Analysis

All enrolled patients were included in the analysis on an intention‐to‐treat basis. According to the power analysis, 88 participants were needed to detect an expected 28% (from 64.0% to 92.0%) difference in diagnostic accuracy between transoral ultrasound and standard care [18]. For each participant, diagnostic accuracy and the number of needle aspirations yielding pus were analyzed as binary outcomes. Diagnostic accuracy between the intervention and control groups was compared using binary logistic regression and a generalized estimating equation (GEE) to adjust for clustering within each physician and hospital. A negative binomial distribution was used to examine differences in sick days and needle aspirations between the groups. A 5% significance level was applied. Statistical Analysis Software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, North Carolina, U.S.) and R‐studio version 12.1 was used for the statistical analyses.

Results

From December 2018 to August 2019, a total of 95 participants were enrolled and randomized at Rigshospitalet (n = 59) and Odense University Hospital (n = 37). One participant was considered lost to follow‐up due to the unexplained presence of a broken randomization envelope without patient data at one center, see Figure 3 for CONSORT flowchart. The mean age of participants was 32.6 years (SD 13), and 48.4% (n = 46) were female (see demographics and clinical characteristics in Table 1). The median symptom duration before referral was 5.0 days (IQR 3.0–7.0). Sixty percent of participants (n = 57) were referred by their general practitioner, 23.6% (n = 22) from the emergency department, 11.6% (n = 11) from a private‐practice otolaryngologist, and 5.3% (n = 5) from other sources.

Main Objectives

At their first visit to the otolaryngology department, 31 (65.9%) of the participants in the intervention group and 47 (97.9%) of the participants in the control group received ≥ 1 needle aspiration (p < 0.01) (see Table 2). The mean number of needle aspirations was 2.3 (SD 1.2) in the intervention group and 3.0 (SD 0.9) in the control group (p < 0.01) (Figure 4). The proportion of needle aspirations with pus was 47.6% for the intervention group and 43.6% for the control group (p = 0.97). The diagnostic accuracy was 80.9% in the intervention group compared with 72.9% in the control group (p = 0.5). The sensitivity and specificity were 89.3% and 68.4% for the intervention group and 100% and 7.1% for the control group, respectively (Table 3).

Secondary Objectives

In total, 40.4% (n = 19/47) of the patients in the intervention group and 27.1% (n = 13/48) in the control group required additional surgical treatment after their first visit: Of the patients in the intervention group, 14 (29.8%) required a needle aspiration during a follow‐up visit, and 5 (10.6%) had a Quinsy tonsillectomy performed. Among patients in the control group, 12 (25.0%) required needle aspiration at a follow‐up visit, and 1 (2.1%) underwent Quinsy tonsillectomy. There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in the median number of outpatient clinic visits, the number of patient‐reported sick days after the first visit, or the number of days in hospital (Table 2). Fourteen patients (29.8%) in the intervention group were admitted to the hospital, compared with 8 (16.7%) in the control group (p = 0.07).

Finally, the patient‐reported discomfort related to the diagnostic intervention was not significantly different between the groups (score 6.93 for the intervention group and 6.32 for the control group out of a maximum score of 11).

Exploratory Objectives

The 95 participants were treated by 28 different otolaryngology residents at Rigshospitalet (N = 16) and Odense University Hospital (N = 12) with varying levels of experience in transoral ultrasound. In the clinician questionnaire for the intervention group, residents reported that transoral ultrasound had improved the diagnostic workup in 33 cases (73%), had not improved it in 10 cases (22%), and was undecided in 2 cases (4%).

Forty‐six (97.9%) participants in the intervention group underwent transoral ultrasound, whereas it was not feasible in one case (2.1%). The Burr‐Hole transducer was employed in 42 cases (91.3%), the Hockey‐stick transducer in one case (2.2%), and both transducers in eight cases (17.4%). In the control group, one participant was examined with transoral ultrasound, although it was not allowed according to randomization. Of the 31 needle aspirations in the intervention group, 14 (45.2%) were performed under ultrasound guidance: 7 (22.6%) with a needle guide and 7 (22%) with a freehand technique. The patients who received ultrasound‐guided aspirations had a sensitivity of 85.7% (n = 12/14).

Discussion

In this randomized study, we found that transoral ultrasound significantly reduced the number of needle aspiration attempts in patients with suspected PTA, while maintaining a high sensitivity of 89.3%. Approximately half of needle aspirations could be performed under ultrasound guidance rather than blindly, and residents reported that transoral ultrasound improved their diagnostic workup in 73% of cases. Consequently, transoral ultrasound can be a valuable diagnostic adjunct for patients with suspected peritonsillar abscess. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to explore a technique for ultrasound‐guided aspiration of peritonsillar abscesses. Because the study imitates how the transoral ultrasound would be used in clinical practice, we believe that our results are generalizable to emergency and otolaryngology departments.

The admission rate in the intervention group (14/47) was much higher than that of the control group. This can largely be explained by the greater number of tonsillectomies (five compared to one) in the intervention group.

A prior randomized clinical trial found that transoral US improved the diagnostic sensitivity from 50% to 100% compared to needle aspiration, but the study was limited by a small sample size and a low external validity seeing as experienced attending emergency physicians were available to supervise all scans [10]. In comparison, our trial reflects the clinical reality in which many different ENT residents on call will treat patients with a peritonsillar abscess without being supervised by an experienced consultant. This factor likely explains the lower sensitivity of 89.3% for the intervention group in our study.

Interestingly, we found that the number of Quincy tonsillectomies was higher in the intervention group than in the control group. PTA was confirmed during surgery in all patients undergoing Quincy tonsillectomy. In the intervention group, an abscess was identified by ultrasound in all five patients, but no pus was retrieved during needle aspiration. Seeing as the presence of an abscess was confirmed during surgery in all cases, we believe that the use of transoral ultrasound likely shortened their wait for definitive treatment. However, we cannot know whether these patients could have been successfully treated with intravenous antibiotics. In theory, transoral ultrasound could result in overtreatment if the ultrasound diagnosis makes the clinician more reluctant to settle for conservative treatment.

A strength of our study is its multicenter randomized design. We ensured standardized reporting of clinical findings during patient enrollment and provided a standardized hands‐on training program for all participating residents to ensure a common baseline level of competence [18]. However, our study also has several limitations. Firstly, many otolaryngology residents were on call during the study period, and nearly half performed their first‐ever transoral ultrasound‐guided aspiration on an enrolled participant. Furthermore, few residents were supervised during the procedure, as many patients were treated late in the evening or at night when no consultant was present. Both factors may have negatively affected the diagnostic accuracy. Assessing how clinicians' experience with transoral ultrasound and their learning curve influences the diagnostic accuracy of PTA would strengthen the study's conclusions. The lack of training experience might also explain why diagnostic accuracy was lower than anticipated, compared to findings from previous studies. Given the high turnover of junior doctors on call in most departments, our results may have high external validity (generalizability). But it is essential that clinicians feel comfortable using and trust the result of their transoral ultrasound scan. Therefore, training on patients with a PTA is essential for clinicians to apply the method and operate the equipment appropriately and accurately.

Another limitation is that more participants in the intervention group underwent Quinsy tonsillectomy (tons à chaud) than those in the control group. This makes it more challenging to compare patient outcomes, as the number of sick and hospital days increases among patients who undergo Quinsy tonsillectomy. Finally, the protocol instructed residents in the control group to make up to three needle aspiration attempts if no pus was retrieved. Outside a protocolized study, residents may likely have been satisfied with a single failed needle aspiration attempt if their clinical suspicion of a PTA is low. This limitation could therefore have led to more needle aspirations in the control group than in clinical reality, and it may reduce external validity.

Patient‐reported discomfort scores were high in both groups (6.93 vs. 6.32 out of 11), with no significant difference observed. This finding likely reflects the requirement for transducer contact with the palatine tonsil during transoral ultrasound, which commonly elicits a gag reflex. Consequently, the technique may not reduce procedural discomfort, although it may still offer clinical value by decreasing the need for invasive procedures such as needle aspiration.

Transcervical US is another method for diagnosing a PTA, with diagnostic accuracy comparable to transoral ultrasound but without the ability to guide needle aspiration [25]. It can be performed with a more commonly available linear or curved ultrasound transducer and can be performed on children and patients with trismus [7, 26]. Transoral US implementation requires significant resources, including investment in equipment, clinician training, and dedicated time for skill acquisition. Performing ultrasound examinations also increases consultation time, potentially impacting clinical workflow. Therefore, clear clinical benefits are necessary to justify its routine use.

Our study found that both transoral ultrasound and US‐guided needle aspiration are feasible tools to integrate into the diagnostic workup of patients with a suspected PTA. This accessible point‐of‐care adjunct may decrease the number of invasive needle aspirations and avoid unnecessary CT scans. Nevertheless, our study also demonstrates limitations in implementing a new diagnostic tool for a procedure often performed by unsupervised residents with limited experience. Larger studies in which physicians gain more experience with transoral ultrasound are needed to further explore its potential clinical benefits and cost‐effectiveness as a standard component of the diagnostic workup for PTA.

Transoral ultrasound represents a useful tool for diagnosing PTAs. The evidence presented in this study does not clearly justify adopting the technique as a standard approach. We also recommend adequate training and sufficient clinical experience when employing this method to enhance diagnostic accuracy. However, further studies are needed to clarify the importance of clinical experience in diagnosing a potential PTA using transoral ultrasound.

In conclusion, we found that transoral ultrasound significantly decreased the number of needle aspiration attempts in patients with a suspected PTA. However, we found a non‐significant increase in diagnostic accuracy in the ultrasound group and observed no difference in patient outcomes between the groups.
