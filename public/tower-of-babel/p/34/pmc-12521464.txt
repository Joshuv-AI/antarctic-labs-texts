Hypoxia-driven circ-TBC1D1/miR-520h/EPHB4 impairs trophoblast function in unexplained recurrent spontaneous abortion
Authors: Rong Hua, Qinshan Xie, Jiaxu Li, Yi Mo, Xiu Lin, Chun Huang, Min He, Fengqiong Liu, Yujie Huang, Jiangfan Wan, Yan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hypoxia during early pregnancy critically regulates trophoblast function, and its dysregulation has been implicated in unexplained recurrent spontaneous abortion (URSA). This study investigated the circ-TBC1D1/miR-520 h/EPHB4 regulatory axis in HTR-8/SVneo trophoblast function under hypoxic conditions (2% O2) and its association with URSA. Clinical analysis of chorionic tissues from 14 URSA patients and 14 controls revealed upregulated EPHB4 expression in URSA samples. EPHB4 suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion while increasing apoptosis and altering angiogenesis markers (VEGFA, CD31). Mechanistically, circ-TBC1D1 acted as a competitive endogenous RNA by sequestering miR-520 h, thereby elevating EPHB4 expression and modulating HTR-8/SVneo trophoblast function under hypoxia. Additionally, downregulated miR-520 h and a non-significant trend toward elevated circ-TBC1D1 were observed in chorionic tissues from URSA patients. These findings demonstrate that the circ-TBC1D1/miR-520 h/EPHB4 axis may impair extravillous trophoblast cell functionality, and dysregulation of this pathway in URSA patients may correlate with compromised uterine spiral artery remodeling. This study highlights potential molecular targets involved in URSA pathogenesis associated with hypoxic stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19797-8.

Introduction

Following fertilization, the zygote undergoes mitotic divisions to develop into a blastocyst, typically around day four postfertilization. Trophoblast cells, which constitute the outer layer of the blastocyst, play a key role in the formation of the placenta. These cells are essential for the implantation of the blastocyst, the establishment of maternal–fetal circulation, and the remodeling of the uterine spiral arteries1–3. Among them, abnormal function of extravillous trophoblast cells(EVT) can affect uterine spiral artery remodeling and may lead to pregnancy-related complications, including preeclampsia, spontaneous abortion, and fetal growth restriction4–6.

During the initial stages of pregnancy, the development of the placenta and the establishment of maternal–fetal circulation remains incomplete. This period is crucial for organogenesis in the fetus, which has a limited capacity for antioxidant defense. Both the fetus and the uterus undergo a state of hypoxia during this time7–10. Hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1α) and 2 alpha (HIF2α) are essential for adaptation to hypoxic conditions and play significant roles in various processes, including blastocyst implantation, trophoblast development and differentiation, maternal–fetal immune tolerance, uterine angiogenesis, and the transport of substances across the placenta11–14. However, the specific effects of hypoxia on extravillous trophoblast cells and the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. The literature on this subject is limited and often inconclusive, highlighting the necessity for further research.

Recurrent spontaneous abortion (RSA) is characterized by the occurrence of two or more consecutive spontaneous abortions15,16. It is estimated that RSA affects approximately 2.6% of women of reproductive age17, with an increasing incidence18,19. The etiology of RSA is multifaceted, encompassing a variety of factors, including genetic, anatomical, coagulation, immunologic, endocrine, metabolic, infectious, and male factors20,21. However, the underlying cause remains unidentified in approximately 50% of cases22, which are categorized as unexplained recurrent spontaneous abortion (URSA). Our previous research found that EPHB4 is overexpressed in RSA mouse embryonic tissue and may affect trophoblast function23. Other studies have been suggested that individuals with URSA may demonstrate trophoblast dysfunction24–27.

Hypoxia is a crucial mechanism that regulates trophoblast cell function. Furthermore, research into the function and mechanism of EPHB4 under hypoxia conditions may provide new insights into the etiology and pathogenesis of URSA. Therefore, this study proposes to first assess the expression levels of EPHB4 in individuals with URSA. Subsequently, the HTR-8/SVneo cell model will be utilized to further investigate the functions of EPHB4 and its potential upstream regulatory mechanisms under hypoxia conditions.

Results

EPHB4 is highly expressed in URSA chorionic villi

To further elucidate the relationship between EPHB4 and URSA, we collected villus tissue from patients diagnosed with URSA and examined EPHB4 expression. RT-qPCR revealed that EPHB4 mRNA expression was significantly increased, while Western blot and immunohistochemistry (IHC) demonstrated an increase in EPHB4 protein expression (Fig. 1). These findings suggest that EPHB4 is highly expressed in the chorionic villi of patients with URSA and may play a role in the pathogenesis of this condition.

EPHB4 regulates the function of extravillous trophoblast cells functions under hypoxia

To further investigate the potential mechanisms by which EPHB4 is involved in URSA, this study cultured HTR-8/SVneo cells under 2% O2 to simulate a physiological hypoxic environment and examine EPHB4’s function. First, the functional changes of these cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions were compared. The results showed that, compared to the normoxia group, HTR-8/SVneo cells exposed to physiological hypoxia exhibited reduced induced angiogenic capacity, decreased proliferation, migration, and invasion, and increased apoptosis (Fig. 2). Subsequently, EPHB4 expression was assessed in this model, revealing elevated mRNA and protein levels under hypoxia (Fig. 3). Therefore, EPHB4 expression was downregulated in the physiological hypoxia model to explore its role. The results demonstrated that EPHB4 downregulation significantly increased the tube formation length of HUVECs induced by extravillous trophoblast cells under hypoxia, along with increased expression of VEGFA and CD31, enhanced proliferation, reduced apoptosis, and improved invasion and migration abilities (Fig. 4). These findings indicate that under physiological hypoxia, EPHB4 regulates extravillous trophoblast cell functions by modulating their induction of surrounding angiogenesis, proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion.

MiR-520 h targets EPHB4 to regulate the function of extravillous trophoblast cells under hypoxia

To further investigate the potential upstream regulatory mechanisms of EPHB4, this study predicted microRNAs (miRNAs) that may regulate EPHB4 and identified miR-520 h as a potential upstream regulator (Fig. 5A-B). In HTR-8/SVneo cells, compared to normoxic conditions, miR-520 h expression decreased while EPHB4 expression increased under physiological hypoxia, demonstrating an inverse relationship (Fig. 5C), consistent with our hypothesis. A dual-luciferase reporter assay further confirmed the presence of a binding site between miR-520 h and EPHB4 (Fig. 5D). Moreover, overexpression of miR-520 h under physiological hypoxia reduced EPHB4 expression (Fig. 5E-F), further supporting the regulatory role of miR-520 h on EPHB4.

In addition, this study evaluated the effect of miR-520 h overexpression on the function of extravillous trophoblast cells under physiological hypoxia. The results demonstrated that miR-520 h overexpression enhanced peripheral angiogenesis, proliferation, invasion, and migration capacities of extravillous trophoblast cells under physiological hypoxia, while reducing the apoptosis rate. These effects were consistent with the downregulation of EPHB4 (Fig. 6A–F). This suggests that miR-520 h may regulate the function of extravillous trophoblast cells under physiological hypoxia by targeting EPHB4. Finally, reduced miR-520 h expression was detected in the chorionic villi tissue of URSA patients (Fig. 6G), indicating that miR-520 h may contribute to the occurrence and development of URSA by regulating EPHB4.

Circ-TBC1D1 targets miR-520 h/EPHB4 to regulate the function of extravillous trophoblast cells under hypoxia

To further investigate the potential upstream regulatory mechanisms of EPHB4, this study predicted circRNAs that may regulate miR-520 h and identified circ-TBC1D1 (hsa_circ_0069414) as a potential upstream regulator of miR-520 h (Fig. 7A-B). In HTR-8/SVneo cells, compared to normoxic conditions, circ-TBC1D1 expression increased under physiological hypoxia, exhibiting an inverse pattern relative to miR-520 h expression but a pattern consistent with EPHB4 expression (Fig. 7C), supporting our hypothesis. Dual-luciferase reporter assays further confirmed a binding site between circ-TBC1D1 and miR-520 h (Fig. 7D). Under physiological hypoxia, downregulation of circ-TBC1D1 led to increased miR-520 h expression and decreased EPHB4 expression (Fig. 7E–G). Additionally, rescue experiments demonstrated that the regulatory effect of circ-TBC1D1 on EPHB4 is mediated by miR-520 h (Fig. 7H-I). These findings further confirm the regulatory role of circ-TBC1D1 in the miR-520 h/EPHB4 pathway.

In addition, this study evaluated the effect of downregulating circ-TBC1D1 on the function of extravillous trophoblast cells under physiological hypoxia. The results demonstrated that downregulation of circ-TBC1D1 enhanced peripheral angiogenesis, proliferation, invasion, and migration capacities of extravillous trophoblast cells under physiological hypoxia, while reducing the apoptosis rate. These effects were consistent with those observed following miR-520 h overexpression and EPHB4 downregulation (Fig. 8A–F). This suggests that circ-TBC1D1 may regulate extravillous trophoblast cell function under physiological hypoxia by targeting the miR-520 h/EPHB4 axis. Additionally, the study observed a trend toward lower circ-TBC1D1 expression in the chorionic villi tissue of URSA patients, although this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 8G).

Discussion

The probability of recurrent spontaneous abortion increases with the number of previous miscarriages, often imposing significant physical and psychological burdens on patients17. Despite this, approximately half of RSA cases remain unexplained, posing considerable challenges for diagnosis and treatment. EPHB4, a member of the EPH receptor (erythropoietin-producing hepatocarcinoma receptor) family, is widely expressed in placental tissue and plays a crucial role in the development and maturation of villous blood vessels, as well as in the remodeling of uterine spiral arteries by extravillous trophoblast cells (EVTs)28. Studies have shown that in patients with preeclampsia, elevated expression of EPHB4 in trophoblasts leads to reduced proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of EVTs, increased apoptosis rates, and inhibition of EVT-mediated replacement of uterine spiral artery endothelial cells. This disruption of the normal remodeling process contributes to the onset of preeclampsia29–31. Our previous studies also found that EPHB4 expression is increased in embryonic tissues of RSA mice and regulates EVT proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and migration23. Therefore, to further investigate the role of EPHB4 in unexplained recurrent spontaneous abortion (URSA), this study collected chorionic tissues from healthy controls and URSA patients. Through RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses, we found that EPHB4 expression was elevated in the chorionic villi of URSA patients, suggesting its potential involvement in the onset and progression of URSA.

On the fourth day after fertilization, the blastocyst forms, and its outer layer of cells differentiates into cytotrophoblast cells (CTB) and syncytiotrophoblast cells (STB), which subsequently develop into the embryonic villi1–3,5–7,32. Approximately two weeks postimplantation, CTBs located at the end of the chorionic villous stem penetrate the STB layer to form columns of trophoblasts, which subsequently differentiate into extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) through epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT). These EVTs invade the endometrium and remodel the spiral arteries1,3,5,33, This process involves essential components of the placenta and is closely associated with several pregnancy complications, including preeclampsia, spontaneous abortion, and fetal growth restriction5,34. During the early stages of gestation, specifically prior to approximately ten weeks, the distal ends of the spiral uterine arteries are occluded by EVTs plugs, allowing only plasma to flow into the chorionic hiatus7. At this juncture, The intrauterine partial pressure of oxygen is maintained at 2% to 3%, resulting in a state of physiological hypoxia. Between the 10- and 12-week gestational periods, the gradual dissolution of EVTs plugs culminates in the completion of spiral artery remodeling, which facilitates the influx of maternal blood into the chorionic interstitium and the embryonic tissue is released from its physiological hypoxic state10,13,14. The physiological hypoxia experienced during the early stages of pregnancy induces the expression of the hypoxia-inducible factors HIF-1α and HIF-2α, which regulate several critical processes, including EVTs differentiation and uterine angiogenesis. These mechanisms are vital for sustaining the early stages of pregnancy8,10–14. Numerous studies have established a significant correlation between hypoxia and spontaneous abortion, particularly in cases of RSA. Differentially expressed genes in EVT of patients with recurrent spontaneous abortion are predominantly associated with hypoxia and glycolysis35. Furthermore, elevated expression levels of HIF-1α have been observed in both the blood and placental tissues of these patients, which is concomitant with oxidative stress36–39. Therefore, this study exposed HTR-8/SVneo cells to 2% O2 to establish an EVT model of early pregnancy hypoxia and to further investigate the potential role of EPHB4 in URSA. The results demonstrated that EPHB4 inhibited EVT functions under hypoxic conditions, including peripheral vascular formation, proliferation, invasion, and migration, while promoting apoptosis. Consistent with previous research, we observed increased EPHB4 expression in the villous tissue of URSA patients. Based on these findings, we infer that EPHB4 may contribute to the pathogenesis of URSA by impairing EVT function and disrupting EVT-mediated remodeling of uterine arteries. This discovery provides a novel perspective for a deeper understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying URSA.

Research has indicated that ncRNAs can regulate the proliferation and invasion of embryonic trophoblast cells by modulating processes such as the cell cycle, apoptosis, and EMT. Furthermore, ncRNAs have been associated with various pathological conditions, including preeclampsia and RSA40–43. CircRNAs play a critical role in embryonic development44,45. According to the competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) hypothesis, circRNAs can increase the expression of target genes by competitively binding to miRNAs, thereby alleviating the inhibitory effects of miRNAs on the mRNAs of target genes46–48. CircRNAs, recognized for their stability and high expression levels, often function as ceRNAs for miRNAs, thus establishing a circRNA‒miRNA‒mRNA regulatory network. Therefore, this study explored and validated potential upstream circRNA/miRNA regulatory factors of EPHB4, revealing that circ-TBC1D1 (hsa_circ-0069414) can upregulate EPHB4 expression by targeting miR-520 h, thereby modulating EVT function under hypoxic conditions. Additionally, in villous tissue from URSA patients, we observed low miR-520 h expression and high EPHB4 expression. Previous studies have shown that miR-520 h is highly expressed in the decidua49 and serum41 and inhibits angiogenesis by targeting the EPHB4/EphrinB2 signaling pathway50. This finding is consistent with our results; therefore, We hypothesize that the miR-520 h/EPHB4 pathway contributes to the pathogenesis of URSA by affecting EVT function, thereby opening new avenues for further investigation.

Additionally, this study found that, compared to the normoxic group, EVT under physiological hypoxia exhibited activation of the circ-TBC1D1/miR-520 h/EPHB4 pathway, which may represent a key mechanism for adapting to hypoxia. Similarly, activation of the miR-520 h/EPHB4 pathway was also observed in the villous tissue of patients with URSA. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that an imbalance in hypoxic adaptation may exist in the villous tissue of URSA patients, potentially representing another important mechanism underlying recurrent spontaneous abortion. However, further in-depth research is required to clarify this mechanism.

The development of the placenta is contingent upon intricate interactions between maternal and trophoblast cells. This study preliminarily explored the effects of early pregnancy hypoxia on EVT, but did not consider the roles of other maternal cell types. To accurately elucidate these effects and the underlying mechanisms, it is necessary to establish a multicellular model incorporating both maternal and trophoblast cells or to conduct animal experiments. Additionally, when assessing the impact of the circ-TBC1D1/miR-520 h/EPHB4 axis on EVT function under physiological hypoxia, this study included only hypoxia-negative control vector groups and hypoxia-targeted vector groups. While this approach can reveal the role of specific molecules under hypoxic conditions, the absence of normoxic untreated groups and normoxic negative control groups as baseline controls makes it difficult to directly compare the functional states of cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Consequently, it is not possible to precisely distinguish the independent effects of hypoxia and circ-TBC1D1/miR-520 h/EPHB4 regulation on EVT function.

Conclusion

This study found that under physiological hypoxic conditions, circ-TBC1D1 targets miR-520 h to upregulate EPHB4 expression, thereby impairing induced peripheral vascular angiogenesis as well as the proliferation, migration, and invasion of EVT, while increasing apoptosis. Additionally, downregulation of miR-520 h and upregulation of EPHB4 were observed in villous tissue from patients with URSA, suggesting that the miR-520 h/EPHB4 pathway may disrupt EVT function. This disruption likely affects the remodeling of uterine spiral arteries, contributing to the pathogenesis of URSA. Finally, it is crucial to acknowledge that we are currently unable to predict whether seemingly ‘healthy’ first-trimester samples will indeed result in a ‘healthy’ pregnancy outcome. This limitation poses a significant challenge in the study of first-trimester tissues, despite our stringent inclusion criteria aimed at selecting samples from pregnancies most likely to be “healthy.”

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

This study was conducted at Guangxi Reproductive Hospital from August 2023 to January 2024 to recruit 28 participants. The participants were categorized into two groups: the URSA group, which included 14 individuals, and the healthy induced abortion (normal control) group, which also included 14 individuals. The URSA group was defined on the basis of specific criteria: participants were required to have experienced at least two consecutive early spontaneous abortions, including biochemical pregnancies, occurring prior to 12 weeks of gestational age, and ultrasonographic examinations indicated the absence of embryonic development or fetal heart activity. Individuals with identifiable causes of abortion, such as genetic, anatomical, coagulation, immune, endocrine, or metabolic abnormalities; infections; or male factors, were excluded from this group. The normal control group was defined on the basis of specific criteria: individuals were required to undergo an induced abortion for nonmedical reasons and did not exhibit symptoms associated with spontaneous abortion, and all subjects with a history of spontaneous abortions (including biochemical pregnancies) or other potential risk factors for abortion were excluded. Following the abortion procedure, placental villus samples were divided into two portions: one portion was treated with RNA Later™ stabilizing solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), while the other was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, China). The portion treated with RNA Later™ was stored at -8 °C, whereas the fixed portion was stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Reproduction Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national/international guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Cell culture and transfection

The human chorionic extrachorionic trophoblast cell line HTR-8/SVneo, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), and the human embryonic kidney 293T cell line employed in this study were procured from the American Type Culture Collection. HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Solarbio, China); HUVECs were maintained in F-12 K medium (ATCC, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Solarbio, China); and 293T cells were grown in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Solarbio, China). All the cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and air (approximately 21% oxygen). For the induction of hypoxic conditions, the cells were incubated at 37 °C in an environment comprising 2% O2, 5% CO2, and 93% N2.

HTR-8/SVneo cells were transfected with enhanced infection solution (Genechem, Shanghai, China) and polybrene (Sigma‒Aldrich, Germany) in accordance with the established protocols for sh-circ-TBC1D1, miR-520 h mimic, sh-EPHB4, and KO-DICER (VectorBuilder, Guangzhou, China), as well as for oe-circ-TBC1D1 (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China) lentiviral vectors and their corresponding controls. Furthermore, 293T cells were transfected with polybrene (Sigma‒Aldrich, Germany) for the circ-TBC1D1-WT, circ-TBC1D1-MT, EPHB4-WT, and EPHB4-MT constructs of the pmirGLO luciferase vector plasmid (Promega, Beijing, China), in addition to the miR-520 h mimic and the negative control mimic (Thermo Fisher, USA).

CCK-8 assay

The proliferation of HTR-8/SVneo cells was assessed via the CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. Following initial seeding, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, after which the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance of each well was subsequently measured at 450 nm via a SpectraMax iD5 multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Shanghai, China).

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis in HTR-8/SVneo cells was assessed via an Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The treated cells were collected and resuspended in 100 µL of Annexin V binding buffer. The samples were subsequently incubated with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC solution for 10 min in a dark environment. Following this incubation, 10 µL of propidium iodide (PI) solution was added, and the cells were incubated in the dark for an additional 5 min. The apoptosis rate was then determined immediately via a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Transwell invasion assay

The invasion of HTR-8/SVneo cells was assessed via the use of Ceturegel™ Matrix LDEV-Free Matrix Gel (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) in combination with an HTS Transwell® 96-well Receiver Plate (Corning, USA). In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, the matrix gel was applied to the upper chamber of the Transwell and incubated at 37 °C for three hours. Following this incubation, the treated cells were resuspended in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium, and 200 µL of this suspension was added to the upper chamber. Concurrently, 600 µL of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. The cells were subsequently cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 48 h. After the incubation period, the cells adhering to the surface of the filter membrane in the upper chamber were removed, and the noninvaded cells on the filter membrane were discarded. The invading cells in the lower chamber were then fixed with 95% ethanol for 30 min. Following fixation, the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 30 min. Finally, the number of invaded cells was quantified via a CX43 light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Wound healing assay

HTR-8/SVneo cells that had been treated were plated in 6-well plates. Upon reaching a cell density of 90%, a 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a scratch in the center of the cell monolayer. The cells were subsequently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to eliminate any detached cells from the scratched area and were then supplemented with serum-free RPMI 1640 medium. The culture was maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cell migration was observed and documented via an IXplore Pro inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan) at 0 and 24 h postscratching.

Tube formation assay

HTR-8/SVneo cells were treated, subsequently plated in 6-well plates, and then cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h. The culture medium was then collected and centrifuged, and the resulting supernatant was used as a conditioned medium for HUVECs. Next, 60 µL of matrix gel was added to a 96-well plate and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 1 h. The HUVECs were resuspended in the conditioned medium to achieve a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL, and 100 µL of this suspension was inoculated into preconditioned 96-well plates. The plates were subsequently incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 6 h. Finally, angiogenesis in each well was assessed via an IXplore Pro inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan).

RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from treated HTR-8/SVneo cells and chorionic tissues via TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed via the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). Real-time fluorescent quantitative PCRs were executed with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara, Japan). All polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays were conducted on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The expression levels of each target gene were assessed via the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH or U6 serving as internal controls. The sequences of primers used were as follows: circ-TBC1D1: forward primer: 5′-CTGTCCACCGGCAGAGTATC-3′, reverse primer: 5′-GGTCTGGGAACTCAGCAAAA-3′; miR-520 h: forward primer: 5′-AAGTGCTTCCCTTTAGAG-3′, reverse primer: 5′-GAACATGTCTGCGTATCTC-3′; EPHB4: forward primer: 5′-ACATCACAGCCAGACCCAACTG-3′, reverse primer: 5′-AGGCAGAGAACTGCGACCACAA-3′; VEGFA: forward primer: 5′-TTGCCTTGCTGCTCTACCTCCA-3′, reverse primer: 5′-GATGGCAGTAGCTGCGCTGATA-3′; CD31: forward primer: 5′-AAGTGGAGTCCAGCCGCATATC-3′, reverse primer: 5′-ATGGAGCAGGACAGGTTCAGTC-3′; and GAPDH: forward primer: 5′-GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG-3′, reverse primer: 5′-ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA-3′.

Western blot

The treated HTR-8/SVneo cells and chorionic tissues were mixed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at a ratio of 100:1 for total protein extraction. The protein concentrations were determined via a Pierce™ BCA protein quantification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Following quantification, the protein samples were separated via 10% SDS‒PAGE and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). These membranes were initially blocked with 5% skim milk and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-EPHB4 (Abcam, UK), anti-VEGFA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and anti-GAPDH (Abcam, UK). The following day, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for one hour. Finally, the protein bands were developed via an Ultra Sensitive ECL Chemiluminescence Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and visualized with a Gel Doc 2000 Gel Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). The resulting data were analyzed via ImageJ software (v2.14.0) (https://imagej.net/ij/). The complete scan image of the original Western Blot image can be found in Supplementary Figure S1.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

293T cells were cultured in 24-well plates, and upon reaching a density of 80–90%, they were transfected with the pmirGLO luciferase reporter vector plasmid containing the circ-TBC1D1-WT, circ-TBC1D1-MT, EPHB4-WT, and EPHB4-MT promoter sequences (Promega, Beijing, China) in conjunction with the miR-520 h mimic and negative control (NC) mimic (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Following a 48-hour transfection period, the activities of firefly and sea kidney luciferases were assessed via the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, Beijing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunohistochemistry

Placental chorionic tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin (Sigma‒Aldrich, Germany). Tissue sections were prepared with a microtome (YIDI, Zhejiang, China) and deparaffinized. Antigen retrieval was performed by microwaving in citrate buffer (Wellbio, Shanghai, China). The sections were treated with a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity, and nonspecific binding sites were blocked with a 3% bovine serum albumin solution. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-EPHB4 (Afantibody, Hunan, China) and anti-VEGFA antibodies (huabio, Hangzhou, China). The next day, the sections were washed with PBS and incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. Color development was achieved via the use of a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine staining kit (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (Wellbio, Shanghai, China). After dehydration, the sections were mounted with a neutral dendrimer (Sigma‒Aldrich, Germany). Finally, the sections were scanned and analyzed via a PANNORAMIC 1000 scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary) and CaseViewer software (v2.4.0.119028, 3DHISTECH, Hungary) (https://www.3dhistech.com/), and the mean density was calculated via Image-Pro Plus 6.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1. All in vitro experiments were statistically analyzed based on the results of three independent replicate experiments. For comparisons between two groups with single-factor effects, independent samples t tests were used when the data met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance; otherwise, nonparametric tests were used. In cases involving multifactorial effects or comparisons among multiple groups, ANOVA or nonparametric tests were applied according to the same criteria. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05.
