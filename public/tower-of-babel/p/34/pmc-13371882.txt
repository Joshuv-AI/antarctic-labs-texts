Repeat offenders: Satellite DNA sequence variation drives heterochromatin instability
Authors: Madhav Jagannathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fertilization experiments using different mouse species reveal that variation in rapidly evolving repetitive sequences affects how chromosomes are packaged and distributed during cell division.

A large fraction of eukaryotic genomes, the instruction manuals for life, are made up of repetitive sequences. One prominent class of repeat sequences is satellite DNA, tandem repeats that are rich in the nucleotides adenine (A) and thymine (T). These AT-rich satellite DNAs can constitute 5 to 50% of genome content (1). Although these repeats are widespread across eukaryotes, they lack protein-coding potential and display poor conservation across species. Such features have led many to dismiss satellite DNA as “junk DNA” or “selfish parasites” (1). Across species, satellite DNA repeats are enriched at centromeres and the flanking pericentromeres on eukaryotic chromosomes. Such satellite DNA repeats are increasingly recognized as important contributors to genome organization and stability. For instance, satellite DNA repeats are thought to stabilize the chromosomal position and propagation of centromeres, epigenetically determined loci that direct accurate chromosome segregation during cell division (2). At the flanking pericentromeres, satellite DNA clustering contributes to genome organization during interphase, while heterochromatin formation at these repeats promotes accurate chromosome segregation during mitosis (3).

Despite these important, and often conserved, functions, satellite DNA evolves rapidly, displaying variation in sequence, position, and abundance, even between closely related species (1). One way to reconcile these paradoxical observations is that, in each species, satellite DNA repeats co-evolve with their associated binding proteins to maintain function (4). This model of satellite DNA-protein co-evolution predicts that sequence variation in satellite DNA repeats will have functional consequences. However, testing the model has proven challenging because of the inability to experimentally manipulate the repeats and monitor changes at the poorly assembled genomic regions in which they reside.

An alternative to directly manipulating satellite DNA repeats involves using inter-species hybrids, in which chromosomes containing species-specific satellite DNA repeats are brought together in the same organism. This approach has revealed chromosome organization and segregation defects in hybrids. Notably, in Drosophila, Xenopus, and mouse and plant hybrids, the observed defects predominantly affect the chromosomes of one species and cause chromosome lagging, stretching and breakage at satellite DNA or satellite DNA-proximal loci (5–9). However, hybrids also misexpress genes from their parental genomes, resulting in incompatibilities that can cause lethality or sterility (10). Therefore, this experimental approach does not easily distinguish the effects of satellite DNA variation from other defects afflicting hybrids. Consequently, the impact of satellite DNA sequence variation on cellular function remains poorly understood.

Lamelza and colleagues (11) use the one-cell mouse zygote as an experimental system to circumvent confounding factors associated with hybrid incompatibility and directly assess the impact of changes in satellite DNA sequence. They fertilize a Mus musculus oocyte with sperm from M. musculus or two closely related mouse species, M. caroli and M. pahari, that possess distinct satellite DNA repeats. Upon fertilization, sperm DNA from each species enters an identical cellular context, i.e., the M. musculus oocyte. The one-cell zygote is then arrested at metaphase, allowing analysis of satellite DNA-containing regions on the paternal chromosomes. Crucially, this analysis occurs prior to zygotic genome activation at the two-cell stage when potential hybrid incompatibilities occur.

Using this system, Lamelza et al. (11) show that variation in satellite DNA sequence influences heterochromatin stability (Fig. 1). Specifically, they observe that M. pahari chromosomes, but not M. musculus and M. caroli chromosomes, lack pericentromeric H2AK119Ub1, a heterochromatin mark deposited by polycomb repressive complex 1 (PRC1). They demonstrate that the lack of PRC1 pericentromeric heterochromatin on M. pahari chromosomes is likely due to diminished recruitment of CBX2, a PRC1 subunit, through its AT-hook DNA binding motif. Based on AlphaFold3 modeling, they suggest that the CBX2 AT-hook preferentially binds adenine nucleotide tracts that terminate in a thymine (AnT tracts), which are underrepresented in the M. pahari satellite repeats. Importantly, these findings directly link satellite DNA sequence variation to a cellular phenotype: differential levels of PRC1 heterochromatin.

The authors next tested the functional significance of differential heterochromatin at species-specific satellite DNA repeats. They found that PRC1 heterochromatin excludes the recruitment of the chromosome passenger complex (CPC), which normally functions to ensure accurate chromosome segregation during mitosis. Consequently, CPC localization and function are diminished on paternal chromosomes from M. musculus and M. caroli. This reduction in CPC recruitment is associated with higher rates of chromosome missegregation for the paternal M. musculus chromosomes. Unexpectedly, the lack of PRC1 heterochromatin and improved CPC recruitment observed at the M. pahari satellite repeats suggests that these paternal chromosomes may segregate more accurately than their M. musculus counterparts. Nevertheless, the findings highlight that altered heterochromatin stability, arising from satellite DNA sequence variation, affects chromosome segregation.

Overall, the Lamelza study offers compelling evidence that variation in satellite DNA sequences shapes heterochromatin stability and function. The data support the notion that satellite DNA repeats are functional genetic elements and that the rapid sequence evolution of these repeats necessitates compensatory co-evolution of their associated proteins to preserve function. One of the most intriguing findings of the study is that not all AT-rich satellite DNA sequences are equal, at least from the perspective of an AT-hook. Rather, subtle changes in the arrangement of adenines and thymines are sufficient to modify binding by AT-hooks, which are widespread across eukaryotic DNA binding proteins (12). If these subtle sequence changes can have outsized consequences in a newly fertilized egg, the effect of satellite DNA variation in other cells and tissues of an organism remains an exciting and open question for future research.
