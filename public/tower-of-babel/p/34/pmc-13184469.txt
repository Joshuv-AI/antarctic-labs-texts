Piezo1 mediates electrotaxis of alveolar epithelial cells via calcium-dependent PI3K/Akt signaling
Authors: Huanyu Luo, Yang Dai, Conghua Lu, Chengjun Shi, Wenyi Liu, Wanda Bi, Juan Du, Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAlveolar type II (AT2) epithelial cells play a vital role in lung injury repair, where their directed migration toward damaged regions is essential for epithelial regeneration. However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aimed to determine whether direct-current electric fields (EFs) act as directional cues for AT2 cell migration and to identify the key molecular mediators and intracellular signaling pathways involved.MethodsRNA-seq data from acute respiratory distress syndrome patient–derived AT2 cells were integrated with charged membrane protein signatures to identify candidate genes associated with electrotaxis. Cell migration and calcium dynamics were assessed using time-lapse imaging and live-cell calcium imaging, while pharmacological inhibition, quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and Western blot analyses were employed to investigate molecular mechanisms.ResultsIntegrative transcriptomic analysis identified Piezo1 as a candidate regulator associated with AT2 cell electrotaxis. Functional assays in A549 cells demonstrated that exposure to a direct-current EF (100 mV/mm) significantly promoted cathode-directed migration and increased Piezo1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels. Inhibition of Piezo1 using the specific antagonist GSMTx4, or chelation of intracellular calcium with BAPTA-AM, markedly disrupted EF-induced directional migration, as evidenced by reduced migration velocity and loss of directionality. Live-cell calcium imaging confirmed that Piezo1 is essential for EF-induced calcium influx. Moreover, Western blot analysis revealed that EF stimulation elevated the phospho-phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase and protein kinase B, which was significantly attenuated upon Piezo1 inhibition.ConclusionPiezo1-mediated calcium influx and subsequent phospho-phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B activation drive the electrotaxis of AT2 cells. These findings identify Piezo1 as a key bioelectrical sensor linking EFs to intracellular calcium signaling and directional migration, and suggest its potential as a therapeutic target for promoting epithelial regeneration in acute lung injury.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and other severe respiratory diseases remain major global health challenges, characterized by high morbidity and mortality.1,2 Although supportive therapies have improved patient outcomes, the underlying mechanisms governing lung injury repair remain incompletely understood, thereby limiting the development of effective strategies to promote alveolar regeneration and restore pulmonary function. Accumulating evidence indicates that dysfunction of alveolar type II (AT2) epithelial cells is closely associated with poor prognosis in ARDS and pulmonary fibrosis, underscoring their critical role in alveolar repair.3,4

As the resident progenitor cells of the alveolar epithelium, AT2 cells maintain alveolar homeostasis by self-renewing, differentiating into alveolar type I cells, and producing pulmonary surfactant to reduce surface tension.5, 6, 7 Therefore, efficient regulation of AT2 cell function is essential for successful epithelial regeneration following lung injury.

Efficient and directed migration of AT2 cells toward injured sites is a prerequisite for re-epithelialization and restoration of alveolar barrier integrity.8 This process is governed by a variety of environmental cues, yet the mechanisms coordinating AT2 cell migration remain incompletely defined. Among these cues, epithelial injury is known to disrupt ionic homeostasis and generate endogenous electric fields (EFs) at wound margins.9 These EFs function as bioelectric signals that guide directional migration—a phenomenon termed electrotaxis. Electrotaxis has been widely observed in epithelial, fibroblast, and immune cells.10, 11, 12, 13, 14 However, whether similar bioelectric cues emerge during ARDS, and how such signals could be harnessed or reproduced experimentally to guide AT2 cell migration, remain open questions.

Recent evidence highlights the role of charged membrane proteins (CMPs), including ion channels and membrane receptors, in sensing electrical stimuli and mediating electrotactic responses through mechanisms like electrophoresis and electro-osmosis.15 These CMPs redistribute along the membrane under EFs, initiating intracellular signaling cascades that modulate cytoskeletal dynamics and motility. Among them, Piezo1—a large trimeric mechanosensitive ion channel—has emerged as a critical transducer of mechanical and electrical cues into intracellular calcium signals.16,17 Piezo1 is widely expressed in epithelial cells, where it regulates diverse processes such as barrier integrity, cell migration, and mechanotransduction.18, 19, 20 In this study, we integrated transcriptomic data from ARDS patient-derived AT2 cells with CMP-related gene signatures to identify candidate electrotaxis regulators. We further performed functional validation using EF-stimulated A549 cells to investigate the role of Piezo1, a mechanosensitive ion channel, in transducing bioelectric cues and orchestrating directional migration in alveolar epithelial cells.

Methods

Cell culture and reagents

A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection and cultured in DMEM (Gibco 11995065) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco A5670701) and 1% Pen-Strep (Gibco 15140122) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The Piezo1-specific inhibitor GSMTx4 (MedChemExpress HY-P1410) was used at a final concentration of 5 μM as indicated. For calcium chelation experiments, cells were pre-treated with 2.5 μM BAPTA-AM (MedChemExpress HY-100545) for 30 min at 37 °C before EF stimulation. BAPTA-AM was maintained in the culture medium during EF stimulation and imaging.

EF stimulation of cells

EF stimulation was applied using a custom-built chamber as previously described.21 A direct current EF of 100 mV/mm was applied for 2 h at 37 °C. Time-lapse imaging was performed using an Operetta Confocal Light Sheet High-Content Analysis System (Molecular Devices) equipped with a 20 × objective. Images were acquired every 3 min for a total duration of 2 h. Cell migration trajectories were tracked and analyzed using ImageJ and Python.

Bioinformatic analysis

The bioinformatic workflow consisted of differential gene expression identification, CMP gene overlap analysis, and subsequent enrichment analyses. RNA sequencing data of AT2 cells from ARDS patients and healthy controls were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GSE190732). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using Seurat with a threshold of log2 fold change > 0.25 and adjusted p < 0.05. CMP gene sets were extracted from published electrotaxis-related proteomic datasets.15 Overlapping genes were identified using the VennDiagram R package. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package.

Calcium imaging

Intracellular calcium levels were measured using Fluo-4 AM dye (Beyotime S1061S). Cells were loaded with Fluo-4 AM (5 μM) for 30 min at 37 °C in serum-free medium, followed by washing and imaging using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI8). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against Piezo1 (Proteintech 15939-1-AP), Phospho-phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) (CST 4228s), PI3K (CST 4292s), Phospho-protein kinase B (Akt) (CST 9271s), Akt (CST 9272s), and GAPDH (CST 2118s), followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified using Image Lab software.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen 15596026CN). Complementary DNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Takara 639505). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Promega A6001) on a Bio-Rad CFX connect system. GAPDH was used as the internal control, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are as followed: piezo1-F: TCCTCCTGCTGCTGTGCTACG; piezo1-R: TTGCCGCCTCCTTCCTCTGG; GAPDH-F: CTGGGCTACACTGAGCACC; GAPDH-R: AAGTGGTCGTTGAGGGCAATG.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. A p values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

Results

Integrative analysis of ARDS patient-derived AT2 cell transcriptomic data and CMP signatures identifies Piezo1 as a regulator of AT2 cell electrotaxis

To explore key molecular factors potentially involved in alveolar epithelial cell electrotaxis, we performed integrated bioinformatics analysis combining RNA sequencing data from AT2 cells of ARDS patients and CMP signatures previously reported in electrotaxis-related proteomic datasets (Fig. 1A). By intersecting the DEGs of AT2 cells with the CMP gene set, we identified 326 overlapping candidate genes (Fig. 1B). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis revealed that these overlapping genes were significantly associated with biological processes such as calcium ion transport (Fig. 1C). Further heatmap analysis showed that representative genes in the "calcium ion transport" pathway displayed an overall upregulation trend in AT2 cells compared with healthy controls (Fig. 1D). Notably, Piezo1, a mechanosensitive calcium channel-encoding gene within this pathway, was among the most significantly upregulated DEGs in AT2 cells, as shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 1E), supporting its potential role in mediating electrotactic responses to bioelectric cues. Piezo1 is required for electrotaxis of A549 cells

To validate these bioinformatics predictions at the functional level, we next investigated whether Piezo1 plays a direct role in mediating electrotaxis using the alveolar epithelial-like cell line A549. Under control conditions (no EF), A549 cells exhibited random migration without directional preference. In contrast, exposure to a 100 mV/mm direct current EF markedly induced cathode-directed electrotaxis, as indicated by time-lapse imaging and migration trajectory plots (Fig. 2A and Appendix A, Appendix A). Quantitative analysis confirmed that both migration velocity and directionality were significantly increased under EF stimulation (Fig. 2A). We next assessed whether Piezo1 expression is regulated by EF stimulation. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that Piezo1 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated by approximately 2.5-fold in EF-treated cells compared with the control group (Fig. 2B). To further determine the functional relevance of Piezo1 in electrotaxis, A549 cells were treated with the selective Piezo1 inhibitor GSMTx4 (5 μM) prior to EF exposure. Time-lapse imaging showed that GSMTx4 treatment markedly suppressed EF-induced directional migration, leading to significantly reduced cell velocity and loss of directionality (Fig. 2D and Appendix Figs. 3&4). These results demonstrate that Piezo1 is essential for mediating electrotaxis in A549 cells and that its expression is strongly upregulated upon EF stimulation at both the mRNA and protein levels.

Calcium influx is required for EF-induced electrotaxis in A549 cells

Based on the above functional evidence supporting a critical role of Piezo1 in A549 cell electrotaxis, we next investigated the downstream signaling pathways potentially involved in Piezo1-mediated regulation, we performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome pathway enrichment analysis based on DEGs from the AT2 transcriptomic dataset. The results showed significant enrichment in calcium signaling and other related pathways. We further analyzed the expression level of Piezo1 within these pathways and its correlation with pathway-related genes (Fig. 3A), suggesting that calcium influx may play a critical role in Piezo1-dependent electrotaxis. To explore this possibility, we first monitored intracellular calcium levels using live-cell calcium imaging. EF stimulation (100 mV/mm) rapidly elevated cytosolic calcium in A549 cells compared to unstimulated controls (Fig. 3B and C). To further evaluate whether intracellular calcium is functionally required for electrotaxis, cells were pretreated with the calcium chelator BAPTA-AM (2.5 μM) to buffer cytosolic calcium before EF application. This intervention markedly disrupted directional migration, as evidenced by scattered trajectories and significantly reduced migration velocity and directionality (Fig. 3D and Appendix Figs. 5&6). Piezo1-mediated calcium influx regulates A549 cell electrotaxis via PI3K/Akt signaling activation

Following our observation that calcium influx is essential for EF-induced electrotaxis, we first investigated whether Piezo1 is responsible for mediating EF-triggered intracellular calcium signaling. Live-cell calcium imaging demonstrated that exposure to a direct-current EF (100 mV/mm) induced a rapid and sustained increase in cytosolic Ca2+ levels in A549 cells. Given that the PI3K/Akt pathway is a well-established downstream effector of calcium-dependent migratory signaling,10,14 we next examined whether Piezo1 regulates EF-induced PI3K/Akt activation. Western blot analysis revealed that EF stimulation significantly increased the phosphorylation of PI3K and Akt without affecting total protein levels. Importantly, inhibition of Piezo1 with GSMTx4 markedly attenuated EF-induced phosphorylation of both PI3K and Akt (Fig. 4C), suggesting that Piezo1 activity is required for activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway in response to EF stimulation.

To further determine whether intracellular calcium influx acts upstream of PI3K/Akt signaling, cells were pretreated with the calcium chelator BAPTA-AM prior to EF exposure. Chelation of intracellular Ca2+ significantly reduced EF-induced phosphorylation of PI3K and Akt, while total PI3K and Akt levels remained unchanged (Fig. 4D). Together, these findings demonstrate that EF stimulation activates Piezo1-mediated calcium influx, which in turn functions as an essential upstream regulator of PI3K/Akt signaling, thereby promoting directional migration of A549 cells.

Discussion

In this study, we identified Piezo1 as a potential regulator of electrotaxis in alveolar epithelial cells through integrated bioinformatic analysis and experimental validation. By comparing transcriptomic profiles of AT2 cells from ARDS patients with CMP signatures, Piezo1 emerged as a significantly upregulated gene associated with calcium signaling pathways. Functional experiments using A549 cells confirmed that EF stimulation promotes directional migration and increases Piezo1 expression. Furthermore, both pharmacological inhibition of Piezo1 and chelation of intracellular calcium abolished EF-induced electrotactic behavior and suppressed PI3K/Akt pathway activation, suggesting that Piezo1 mediates electrotaxis via calcium-dependent intracellular signaling. Previous studies have established the essential role of AT2 cells in lung injury repair, functioning as progenitor cells capable of regenerating the alveolar epithelium.6 However, the mechanisms guiding AT2 cell migration toward injury sites remain poorly defined. Our findings highlight the importance of bioelectric cues, particularly EFs, in directing AT2 cell migration, consistent with prior observations in other epithelial and immune cell types.

Importantly, our study suggests that Piezo1 acts as a CMP capable of sensing EF stimuli and converting them into intracellular calcium signals in alveolar epithelial cells. While previous studies have reported Piezo1-dependent electrosensation in other cell types, such as human umbilical vein endothelial cells,16 our work expands this knowledge by validating its functional relevance in lung epithelial cells. This reinforces the broader role of Piezo1 as a conserved electrosensor and provides new insight into how it regulates cytoskeletal dynamics and directional cell migration across different cellular contexts.

Piezo1 is widely recognized as a mechanosensitive ion channel that responds to membrane tension and stretch.17,22 Here, we extend its functional repertoire by implicating it in electrosensation, thereby advancing our understanding of the convergence between mechanical and electrical signals in epithelial cell biology. Notably, Piezo1-mediated calcium influx was shown to activate the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, in line with previous reports linking Piezo1 to proliferative and migratory signaling cascades.20,23,24

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, we primarily used A549 cells as an AT2-like model. Although A549 cells share some phenotypic features with AT2 cells, in vivo validation using primary AT2 cells or animal models will be essential to strengthen our conclusions. Second, while our in vitro EF experiments provide mechanistic insight, they do not fully recapitulate the complexity of ARDS, which is characterized by diffuse and heterogeneous lung injury. Direct measurements of endogenous EFs in ARDS lungs and the development of clinically feasible strategies for exogenous direct current field application remain important future directions. Third, the precise molecular mechanisms by which EF modulates Piezo1 activity require further study. It is unclear whether EF directly alters membrane charge distribution to activate Piezo1, or indirectly regulates its localization or gating through cytoskeletal rearrangements.

Taken together, our findings provide new mechanistic insight into AT2 cell migration and suggest that Piezo1 functions as a bioelectrical sensor linking EFs to intracellular signaling during lung epithelial repair. These observations may offer conceptual insight into how bioelectric cues could be leveraged to support epithelial regeneration following lung injury. Future studies will be required to characterize endogenous EF dynamics after lung injury and to determine whether controlled exogenous direct current fields can modulate epithelial repair in vivo.
