Proteomic maps of synapses and synaptic vesicles across the central and enteric nervous systems
Authors: Shiqi Liu, Qiwang Huang, Zhiwei Tu, Hao Shen, Guibin Wang, Huanjiao Jing, Yaoyao Bian, Xulian Cui, Linshuai Cui, Jian Wang, Chenxi Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile the synaptic proteome of the central nervous system (CNS) has been extensively characterized, its composition in the peripheral nervous system (PNS) remains unknown. There is an urgent need to delineate the precise proteomic landscape of the enteric nervous system (ENS), often referred to as the “second brain,” and to systematically compare its molecular heterogeneity with that of the CNS. To address this, we developed a streamlined workflow integrating subcellular organelle isolation with PulseDIA proteomics, enabling the identification of 13,534 synaptic proteins across the CNS and ENS (including 8807 for ENS). We defined 776 synaptic vesicle-resident proteins in the CNS and 1148 in the ENS. Comparative proteomic analysis revealed a fundamental divergence-convergence relationship: while the two nervous systems exhibit distinct molecular signatures, they maintain conserved functional organization. Furthermore, energy intake restriction in mice induced extensive ENS proteome remodeling. Pharmacological inhibition of FGFR1 kinase activity suppressed thermogenesis independently of energy substrate utilization, suggesting FGFR1’s potential role in ENS-mediated energy metabolism regulation. Collectively, our study provides a resource for neurobiological and neurological research and elucidates proteomic divergences and convergences between the CNS and ENS.Subject terms: Proteomics, Synaptic transmission, Proteome, Proteomic analysis

Introduction

The nervous system of mammals comprises an elaborate network of highly interconnected neurons. Interneuronal signal transmission, essential for coordinating diverse physiological processes, occurs primarily through specialized asymmetric junctions called synapses1. A typical chemical synapse consists of a presynaptic terminal and a postsynaptic terminal, separated by a ~20 nm synaptic cleft. The presynaptic terminal is specialized for neurotransmitter release via exocytosis: synaptic vesicles loaded with neurotransmitters are regulated by voltage-dependent calcium channels, which trigger their fusion and subsequent neurotransmitter release into the synaptic cleft. These neurotransmitters then bind to specific receptors on the postsynaptic terminal, initiating signal reception. Notably, synapses can modulate interneuronal communication, underscoring their intrinsic computational properties. These highly specialized subcellular structures, which integrate both signal transmission and information processing, are supported by a sophisticated complement of synaptic protein machinery2.

Over the past two decades, significant efforts have been devoted to elucidating the precise proteomic composition of synaptic subcellular structures. For instance, Takamori et al. combined SDS-PAGE separation with LC-MS analysis to identify 410 proteins in synaptic vesicles isolated from rat brains3. Boyken et al. developed a method to isolate highly enriched fractions of docked synaptic vesicles, identifying hundreds of proteins in presynaptic active zones4. To enable biochemical analysis of specific synapse subpopulations, a fluorescence-activated synaptosome sorting (FASS) approach was introduced, utilizing knock-in mice with fluorescently labeled glutamatergic synapses5. Subsequent FASS applications identified ~1800 synapse-type-enriched proteins across five brain regions and 18 distinct synapse types6. Notably, Taoufiq et al. established an ultra-definition workflow, significantly expanding the synaptic vesicle proteome inventory to 1466 proteins in rats7. While these and other pivotal studies8–13 have substantially advanced our understanding of the synaptic proteome, persistent technical limitations in subcellular proteomic methodologies have left certain aspects of the synaptic proteome unresolved, particularly the high starting material requirements and limited sensitivity for low-abundance, hidden proteins. To address this, we introduced a workflow that integrates organelle isolation with PulseDIA proteomics, an approach that eliminates the need for traditional off-line LC fractionation and its demanding large sample input. Moreover, existing research has predominantly focused on the central nervous system (CNS), while the proteomic landscape of mammalian peripheral nervous system (PNS) synapses remaining unexplored.

The enteric nervous system (ENS), often referred to as the “second brain,” represents the most extensive division of the PNS and regulates gastrointestinal function14,15. It comprises complex networks of efferent neurons, afferent neurons, and interneurons, forming an intrinsic neural circuitry. Growing evidence highlights the roles of the gut-brain axis in bidirectional communication linking the ENS and CNS, with implications for understanding and treating neurodegenerative, psychiatric, and metabolic disorders16. This cross-talk occurs through multiple pathways, including immune, endocrine, humoral, and direct neural connections. Clinically, the ENS has emerged as a promising target for not only common gastrointestinal disorders but also various neurological, neurodegenerative and metabolic diseases17–19. Despite these advances, the synaptic proteome composition of the ENS remains unknown. Key questions remain unanswered: how similar or distinct are the synaptic protein profiles between the ENS and CNS?

To address this question, we developed an integrated workflow combining subcellular organelle isolation with the PulseDIA proteomics method20 to systematically map the synaptic proteome in murine CNS (brain) and ENS (colon). We identified 13,534 synaptic proteins, tripling previous inventories7, including numerous previously uncharacterized components. Comparative analysis revealed a characteristic duality: while the ENS and CNS synaptic proteomes show molecular divergence, they maintain certain functional conservation, with many proteins linked to nervous system disorders. Using a mouse fasting model, we observed significant remodeling of colonic synaptic proteome architecture, and further fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) kinase inhibition suppressed thermogenesis without altering respiratory exchange ratio (RER). Collectively, our study establishes the first molecular atlas of synaptic proteins in the rodent colon, provides a systematic CNS-ENS comparative proteomic analysis, and creates a valuable resource for neurobiology and neurological research.

RESULTS

Workflow for profiling of the synaptic proteome and quality control

A streamlined workflow was developed to profile the synaptic proteome in both the CNS and ENS of mice (Fig. 1A). Following the dissection of whole brain and colon tissues, samples enriched with synaptic vesicles and synaptosomes were isolated using density gradient centrifugation. To mitigate sample loss, the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol was employed to enzymatically digest the protein mixtures into tryptic peptides that were subsequently prepared for LC-MS/MS analysis. Unlike the two-dimensional LC fractionation utilized in prior studies7, we implemented a multi-injection, gas-phase fractionation-enabled DIA (PulseDIA) strategy20 for proteomic analysis. This approach yielded high sequence coverage of synaptic proteins, facilitating the compilation of a spectral library encompassing 176,727 and 94,696 precursors for brain and colon fractions, respectively. For each synaptic fraction, three biological replicates (each pooled by four animals) were subjected to analysis using the regular DIA method, with the acquired data cross-referenced against the spectral library. This methodology ensures the acquisition of both quantitative and qualitative data for proteins of interest.

In this workflow, stringent quality control measures for the isolation of synaptic vesicles are crucial to ensure the accuracy of subcellular proteomic identification. These measures were implemented by examining the samples using electron microscopy and SDS-PAGE prior to proteomic analysis. The synaptosomes (SN) isolated from the mouse brain and colon tissues exhibited the characteristic suborganelle structures, while the synaptic vesicles (SV) displayed uniform vesicle structures with a diameter of ~50 nm (Fig. 1B). The SDS-PAGE analysis of synaptic vesicles and synaptosomes from both brain and colon revealed abundant protein profiles (Fig. 1C). The molecular identity of these subcellular organelles was further validated by the detection of specific protein markers (Fig. 1D). Gephyrin21, a post-synaptic membrane protein, was exclusively detected in the synaptosome samples from both brain and colon. Synaptophysin22, predominantly localized to synaptic vesicles, was identified in both SN and SV fractions. The presynaptic protein SNAP-2523 was also detected in both SN and SV fractions. As a negative control, the nuclear protein histone H3 was absent from all fractions. Collectively, the quality control data, including the characterization of subcellular organelle structures, proteome distribution, and region-specific protein markers, confirmed the purity of the isolated fractions, thereby ensuring the reliability of downstream proteomic analysis.

Proteomic profiling in CNS and ENS significantly expands the synaptic protein inventory

Using the streamlined workflow developed above, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the synaptic proteome from mouse brain and colon tissues. This resulted in the identification of 9239 proteins in brain SN fractions, 7994 in brain SV fractions, 8649 in colon SN fractions, and 7573 in colon SV fractions (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Data 1, one protein identified in at least two biological replicates was considered as a confident identification). After removing redundant identifications, a total of 13,534 proteins were identified in our dataset (Fig. 2B). Notably, the number of proteins identified in the brain SV and SN fractions was ~three-fold higher than that reported in previous studies using rat models7. By further combining the identifications from the colon, to the best of our knowledge, this represents the largest dataset of synaptic proteins in mammals to date. Subsequently, the identified synaptic proteins were analyzed using the SynGO database24, revealing that 12.0–16.3% of them in the four fractions matched entries in the database (Fig. 2A). Importantly, 206 proteins (2.5%, 206/7994) from the brain SV fractions were annotated as postsynaptic proteins. In comparison, previous studies7 reported a similar percentage (7%, 97/1466) of postsynaptic proteins in SV fractions when referenced against the SynGO database, further confirming the minimal contamination of postsynaptic proteins in our dataset. Furthermore, the 135 and 157 proteins detected only in SV fractions (Fig. 2C) are distributed across the low-abundance range of the total proteome (Fig S1A–D). This suggests they are likely masked by more abundant proteins in the SN fractions and only reach detectable levels following SV enrichment.

Our study substantially advances the proteomic profiling of synaptic proteins in the rodent brain. Specifically, 87% of SN and 91% of SV proteins identified in prior studies were also detected in our results (Supplementary Fig. 2). For example, in the synaptotagmin (Syt) family, most known members were detected (14 out of 17, Fig. 2D), and our data further expanded the reported identifications7 to include Syt4, Syt10, Syt16, Esyt2, and Syt-l-2, which play critical roles in nervous system functions. For instance, Syt4 modulates exocytosis and endocytosis in posterior pituitary nerve terminals by regulating vesicle fusion efficiency and pore conductance, thereby influencing neuropeptide release under different calcium conditions25. Syt10 was recently identified as a vesicular Ca²⁺ sensor upregulated by NPAS4 in response to excitotoxic stress26. Regarding V-ATPase complexes, we identified 33 out of 35 subunits reported by Taoufiq et al.7, with our study extending the total coverage to 60 subunits (Supplementary Table 1). This multi-subunit proton pump plays an essential role in energizing synaptic vesicle neurotransmitter loading through ATP-dependent proton translocation27.

Comparative analysis of the proteins across different synaptic fractions revealed that 7859 proteins were jointly identified in brain SV and SN fractions, while 7416 proteins were jointly identified in colon SV and SN fractions (Fig. 2E). Additionally, 7468 proteins were common to both brain and colon synaptic proteomes, accounting for 41.7% of the total identified proteins. Although synapses and synaptic vesicles in the brain and colon share structural and functional similarities and play crucial roles in the nervous system, they also exhibit significant heterogeneity stemming from differences in their locations and specific physiological functions.

We performed an intensity-based ranking analysis of all identified proteins across the four sample types, which revealed a broad dynamic range in protein abundance (Fig. 2F). Notably, RAB11a showed a higher intensity ranking in synaptic vesicles and synaptosomes isolated from the brain than from the colon. As a small GTPase, Rab11a orchestrates synaptic vesicle recycling and membrane trafficking in neurons, thereby regulating endosomal sorting and plasma membrane delivery of synaptic receptors and other cargoes critical for synaptic plasticity and neurotransmission28. In contrast, VGLUT1 was among the top-ranked proteins in all four fractions. This transporter primarily mediates glutamate uptake into synaptic vesicles and maintains vesicle tonicity, thereby stabilizing vesicle pools at presynaptic terminals through the regulation of vesicle mobility and clustering29.

In the volcano plot analysis of the brain and colon synaptic proteomes, we defined the “SV-resident” proteome using the criterion of an SV/SN ratio greater than 2 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Data 2). Based on this threshold, we identified 776 SV-resident proteins in brain synapses and 1148 in the colon. Several canonical synaptic vesicle proteins were co-identified in both brain and colon SV-resident fractions, such as synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2B (SV2B), synaptojanin 1 (Synj1), as well as subunits of vacuolar-type ATPase (V-ATPase), ATP6v1f, and ATP6v1g2. Importantly, SV2B, a 12-transmembrane synaptic vesicle protein, regulates neurotransmitter release through calcium sensing and vesicle pool maintenance, and is increasingly implicated in neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases30. Synj1 is a phosphoinositide phosphatase crucial for synaptic vesicle recycling and endosomal trafficking, whose dysfunction is linked to Parkinson’s disease and other neurological disorders through impaired membrane trafficking mechanisms31. For functional characterization, we performed ontology analysis of all identified brain and colon SV proteins using the SynGO database. The results demonstrated significant enrichment in synaptic processes, including synapse organization, presynaptic processes, metabolism, transport, etc. (Fig. 2H and Supplementary Data 3). These findings underscore conserved molecular mechanisms governing synaptic vesicle dynamics across both the CNS and ENS.

Synaptic proteome reveals both molecular heterogeneity and conservation between CNS and ENS

To explore whether molecular heterogeneity exists between the synaptic proteome of the CNS and ENS, we conducted a comparative analysis by examining both quantitative and qualitative features. From the quantitative perspective, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that all samples formed four distinct clusters according to their subcellular organelles (SV and SN) and tissue origins (brain and colon) (Fig. 3A). Pearson correlation analysis further demonstrated high reproducibility within each sample group, with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.88. Additionally, moderate correlation was observed between the synaptic proteins from different organs, with average Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.52 for SN and 0.58 for SV between brain and colon (Fig. 3B). These quantitative results demonstrate the heterogeneity of the CNS-ENS synaptic proteome, resulting from dynamic protein expression patterns. For instance, certain hormonal proteins, such as peptide YY (PYY) and pro-glucagon, were found to be exclusively distributed within colon fractions (Supplementary Data 1).

Qualitative analysis showed that brain SN and colon SN shared 71.7% (7468 of 10,420) common proteins, while brain SV and colon SV shared 65.4% (6157 of 9411) (the upper Venn diagrams in Fig. 3C), reinforcing the molecular similarities between the CNS and ENS. Notably, 28.3% of SN proteins and 34.6% of SV proteins showed organ-specific distributions. GO analysis of these organ-specific proteins revealed that 11 of the 15 top-enriched molecular function terms were shared between SN proteins from brain and colon (Fig. S3A–C), and 8 of the 15 top-enriched terms were shared between SV proteins (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C), indicating that despite their presence in different organs, these proteins perform similar functions in both the CNS and ENS. Together, these qualitative data demonstrate molecular and functional conservation between the CNS and ENS synaptic proteomes.

To further assess the synaptic proteome consistency between the CNS and ENS, we analyzed the shared proteins across brain SN/colon SN, brain SV/colon SV, and brain SV-resident/colon SV-resident fractions. Proteins were categorized based on fold-change (SV/SN) similarity between brain and colon samples. Those with variability < two-fold (regions I and II in Fig. 3C and Supplementary Data 4) between the two organs were considered consistent, revealing that 37% of SN proteins and 44% of SV proteins exhibited inter-organ consistency. Notably, the proportion of region I proteins progressively increased from SN (24%) to SV (28%) to SV-resident proteins (77%). Molecular function analysis using the SynGO database showed that region I was highly enriched for proteins involved in synaptic processes, synapse organization, and synaptic vesicle cycling (Fig. 3D). These results demonstrate that SV-resident proteins maintain high sequence and functional conservation between the CNS and ENS.

Many synaptic proteins are associated with nervous system diseases

We further investigated the pathological implications of SV proteins by querying the UniProt database. In the brain SV fraction, 493 proteins were associated with various brain diseases, including 189 linked to nervous system diseases (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Data 5). These disease-associated proteins spanned the entire abundance range and were derived from both SV-resident and SV-visitor repertoires, suggesting that disruptions in SV lifecycle processes are closely tied to disease mechanisms. Similarly, the colon SV fraction contained a comparable percentage of disease-associated proteins, with 155 linked to nervous system diseases, highlighting the pathological relevance of enteric synaptic vesicles. Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis of the 6157 proteins shared between brain and colon SV fractions revealed that the top-ranked pathways are heavily involved in neurodegenerative conditions, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Alzheimer’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and prion diseases (Supplementary Fig. 5). These findings underscore the functional and pathological parallels between synaptic proteomes in the brain and colon, while also revealing their organ-specific differences.

New SV-resident proteins are partially colocalized with synaptic vesicle markers

Primary neurons, which retain the original characteristics of neurons in vivo, provide an excellent model for investigating molecular colocalization in synaptic vesicles. To determine whether the newly identified SV-resident proteins are localized to synaptic vesicles, we prepared primary neurons from the brains of newborn P1 mice and performed immunofluorescence staining to assess colocalization with synaptophysin, a well-established marker for synaptic vesicles7. The results revealed that the SV-resident proteins COPZ2 and MAP3K6 colocalized with synaptophysin in specific regions of neurons (Fig. 4A, B), indicating their partial localization within synaptic vesicles. COPZ2 is a subunit of the coatomer protein complex I (COPI), which is essential for maintaining Golgi structure and mediating intracellular vesicle trafficking and protein sorting32. MAP3K6 is a serine/threonine protein kinase that regulates vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression and stress-induced signaling cascades, with critical roles in angiogenesis, tumor suppression, and neurovascular disease development33. The identification of these two proteins in synaptic vesicles of the brain suggests they may play potential functional roles in SV biology. Additionally, we extracted primary neurons from the mouse colon tissue and performed immunofluorescence screening, which demonstrated partial colocalization of REG4 with synaptophysin in specific areas of intestinal neurons (Fig. 4C). Reg4, an enteroendocrine hormone, inhibits high-fat diet-induced liver steatosis by suppressing intestinal fat absorption, possibly through modulation of the CaMKK2-AMPK signaling pathway34. These immunofluorescence colocalization results not only validate the presence of these proteins in synaptic vesicles but also reinforce the robustness of our proteomic approach. Furthermore, they underscore the importance of SV-resident proteins in synaptic function and regulation, highlighting their potential roles in both the ENS and CNS.

Synaptic proteins in ENS are involved in energy homeostasis regulation

The ENS plays a pivotal role in regulating energy metabolism in mice, with enteric synapses serving as critical units for neurochemical signal transmission. To investigate how the synaptic proteome in the ENS is associated with energy metabolism, we conducted a quantitative proteomic analysis of synaptosomes extracted from colons of mice subjected to a 2-day fasting period (Fig. 5A). PCA of the proteomic data revealed distinct clustering between the control group and the fasting group, indicating that 2-day fasting significantly altered the architecture of the synaptic proteome in the colon (Fig. 5B). A volcano plot further highlighted extensive changes in protein expression, with 158 proteins upregulated and 45 downregulated in response to fasting (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Data 6). GO analysis demonstrated that many differentially expressed synaptic proteins were enriched in metabolic processes, including phospholipid, hormone, glycerolipid, fatty acid, and alcohol metabolism (Fig. 5D). Interestingly, “defense response to bacterium” was also a top-ranked category, potentially reflecting fasting-induced alterations in the gut microbiome35. Similarly, KEGG pathway analysis identified metabolic pathways as a top-ranked, significantly enriched category (Fig. 5E). These findings collectively underscore the association between the enteric synaptic proteome and energy metabolism in mice. To ensure these metabolic shifts were ENS-specific and not confounded by non-neuronal contamination, we cross-referenced our fasting-responsive proteins with previously reported whole-intestine proteomic data36. Notably, 84% (37 of 44) of the proteins identified as differentially expressed at the whole-tissue level remained stable in our synaptic fractions (Supplementary Fig. 6). This significant divergence underscores the efficacy of our enrichment protocol and suggests that our findings primarily capture localized synaptic remodeling rather than global metabolic fluctuations.

Notably, FGFR1 kinase was significantly upregulated in the enteric synapses of fasting mice (Fig. 5C), suggesting its potential role in regulating energy metabolism during fasting. To validate this hypothesis, we conducted animal experiments using metabolic cages. Mice fasted for 1 day were administered the FGFR1 kinase inhibitors SU4984 via intraperitoneal injection, and their physiological indicators were monitored. A proper dose (50 mg/kg) was used to minimize the possible off-target effect of SU4984. Inhibition of FGFR1 kinase resulted in suppressed heat production in fasting mice, but no significant changes were observed in the RER (Fig. 5F, animal movement data shown in Supplementary Fig. 7). RER, which reflects the ratio of carbon dioxide (CO2) production to oxygen (O2) consumption, indicates the relative contribution of carbohydrates and lipids to overall energy expenditure. Since the experiments were performed under fasting conditions without food-derived energy intake, these results suggest that FGFR1 kinase is potentially involved in promoting thermogenesis but does not influence the source of energy expenditure. Collectively, our synaptic proteomic data, combined with the findings on FGFR1 kinase, provide a molecular foundation for further in-depth studies on metabolic regulation mechanisms and the biological functions of FGFR1 kinase in the context of energy homeostasis.

Discussion

Most previous studies have focused on elucidating the proteomic profiles of neural synapses in the CNS, while those in the PNS have often been neglected. To deepen our understanding of the precise proteome composition in the ENS—often referred to as the “second brain” due to its highly elaborate neural network14,15—we established a streamlined workflow combining subcellular organelle isolation with PulseDIA20 proteomic approach. Using this strategy, we performed comparative proteomic mapping between the CNS and ENS. The resulting inventory, comprising 13,534 synaptic proteins, represents the largest dataset to date. Notably, it not only confirms the presence of canonical synaptic proteins but also reveals previously overlooked hidden proteins with potential functional significance, such as Syt and V-ATPase families.

Our results demonstrate that the synaptic proteomes of the CNS and ENS exhibit a characteristic duality, i.e., molecular divergence coexists with functional convergence. This pattern may suggest that while the core machinery for synaptic vesicle cycling is fundamentally conserved to maintain basic neurotransmission (convergence), the ENS has undergone significant molecular diversification to meet the specialized physiological demands of the gastrointestinal tract (divergence). Synaptic function and structure are shaped by molecular diversity, with synapses classified by neurotransmitter identity, neuronal cell type, neuroanatomical region, morphology, plasticity, and other features1,2. Unsurprisingly, neurons and synapses in the CNS and ENS exhibit distinct populations, primarily reflecting their proteomic heterogeneity. Indeed, the moderate proteomic correlations (r = 0.52 for SN, 0.58 for SV) and expression consistency (37% for SN, 44% for SV proteins) in our data reflect system-specific adaptations likely shaped by their different physiological demands. Notably, this heterogeneity coexists with substantial molecular conservation, as evidenced by the high proportion of shared proteins (71.7% for SN, 65.4% for SV) and particularly the striking conservation of SV-resident proteins, with 77% showing consistent expression and function. This pattern suggests that core synaptic machinery is conserved to maintain basic function between the CNS and ENS of mammals, while allowing for molecular diversification that may underlie the specialized properties of each nervous system division. Moreover, the ENS synaptic proteome exhibits a unique molecular signature compared to the CNS. For instance, PYY preprohormone and pro-glucagon were detected exclusively in the colon SN and SV fractions. PYY is a 36-amino-acid gut hormone that plays a crucial role in appetite regulation, energy homeostasis, and gastrointestinal functions37. Similarly, pro-glucagon is processed into glucagon-like peptide-1, an essential incretin hormone that modulates glucose homeostasis and promotes satiety through both peripheral and central pathways38. The sequestration of these peptide precursors within ENS synaptic fractions may suggest a specialized mechanism for local neuro-endocrine coordination that is not mirrored in the CNS.

Our data demonstrate that a two-day fasting treatment in mice enhanced FGFR1 expression in enteric synapses. Subsequent administration of an FGFR1 kinase inhibitor to fasting mice suppressed heat production without affecting RER. FGFR1 is a cell surface receptor with tyrosine kinase activity, known to activate key signaling pathways, such as ERK/MAPK and PI3K/AKT39. Previous studies have implicated FGFR1 in cellular metabolic reprogramming, where its activation enhances metabolic processes in both rodents and humans39, while its inhibition in murine β-cells has been linked to diabetes40. FGF21-FGFR1/β-KL signaling modulates multi-organ crosstalk under pathophysiological metabolic stress41. However, the precise functional role of FGFR1-mediated signaling in enteric neurons and synapses remains unexplored. Our phenotypic data in mice suggest a potential involvement of FGFR1 signaling pathways in enteric neural circuit-mediated regulation of energy homeostasis. While the enrichment of FGFR1 in enteric synaptosomes and its upregulation upon fasting suggests a direct role in local synaptic signaling, further study is required to fully determine whether its effect on thermogenesis is purely ENS-driven or mediated via broader systemic feedback mechanisms. Future investigations will focus on clarifying the downstream FGFR1 signaling pathways by examining markers of FGFR1 activity during inhibition. Ultimately, conditional knockout mouse models and studies under various nutritional states will help elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this thermogenic regulation.

In conclusion, our integrated workflow demonstrates its utility and efficacy for synaptic proteome characterization, having enabled the identification of 13,534 synaptic proteins from the mouse CNS and ENS, including 8807 proteins detected in the ENS. Comparative analysis revealed a fundamental duality in synaptic proteomes: while exhibiting distinct molecular profiles between the two nervous systems, they maintain functional conservation, supporting the paradigm of functional convergence despite molecular divergence. Notably, energy intake inhibition by fasting remodeled the ENS proteomic architecture, with FGFR1 kinase emerging as a potential regulator of thermogenesis that operates independently of energy substrate utilization. Collectively, this study not only provides a valuable data resource for neurobiological and neurological research but also establishes FGFR1-mediated energy homeostasis regulation as a promising direction for future investigation. Finally, acknowledging potential sex-based differences in these molecular maps, we intend to include female cohorts in future studies to further expand this proteomic resource. Additionally, we will continue to optimize sub-cellular fraction separation protocols to further enhance the purity of isolated synaptic granules.

Methods

Animal experiment

Eight-week-old, male, specific pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6J mice (GempharmaTech Ltd.) were housed in a controlled environment (12-h light/dark cycle, 50–60% humidity) with two mice per cage. Mice had ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the National Center for Protein Sciences-Beijing (Protocol NCPSB-20240614-59MB). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Isolation of synaptosomes from the brain and colon of mice

The synaptosome isolation was performed according to previous reports42 with optimization. Mouse whole brain or colon tissues were homogenized in ice-cold sucrose buffer (0.25 M sucrose, 0.5 mM EGTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.1, supplemented with 1 µg/mL Roche protease inhibitor cocktail) using grinding beads. The homogenate was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was discarded. The supernatant was transferred to an ultracentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 30,000 × g (SW41 rotor, Beckman) for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in sucrose buffer and layered onto a discontinuous sucrose gradient (5–10.3%). After centrifugation at 45,000 × g (SW41 rotor) for 20 min at 4 °C, the synaptosome-enriched fraction at the 5–10.3% interface was collected and centrifuged again at 30,000 × g (SW41 rotor) for 10 min at 4 °C. The final pellet, containing purified synaptosomes, was obtained after supernatant removal. The isolation of enteric synaptosomes leverages the distinct sedimentation properties of 0.5–1.5 µm nerve terminals, effectively resolving them from smaller epithelial-derived extracellular vesicles to ensure high synaptic purity.

Isolation of synaptic vesicles from mouse brain and colon tissues

The synaptic vesicle isolation was performed according to previous reports7 with optimization. Grinding tubes and reagents were pre-cooled on ice, and all subsequent steps were performed on ice. Tissue samples (one brain or colon sample pooled from four animals) and grinding beads were placed into the chilled grinding tubes, followed by the addition of sucrose buffer (0.32 M sucrose, 4 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.4, supplemented with 1 µg/mL protease inhibitors) for homogenization. The homogenate was centrifuged at 1300 × g (SW41 rotor, Beckman) for 10 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was discarded. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 17,100 × g (SW41 rotor) for 15 min at 4 °C. After supernatant removal, the pellet was resuspended in sucrose buffer and centrifuged at 18,850 × g (SW41 rotor) for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in sucrose buffer and transferred to a glass homogenizer for osmotic lysis by adding water. Following homogenization, the synaptosome fraction was supplemented with 1 M HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.4) and centrifuged at 46,700 × g (SW41 rotor, Beckman) for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 257,300 × g (70Ti rotor, Beckman) for 2 h at 4 °C. The pellet was then layered onto a discontinuous sucrose gradient (2, 7, 13, and 22%) and centrifuged at 64,300 × g (70Ti rotor) for 4 h at 4 °C. The fraction between the 7 and 13% sucrose layers was collected, filtered through a 100-nm membrane, and centrifuged at 257,300 × g (70Ti rotor) for 1 h at 4 °C to isolate synaptic vesicles.

FASP digestion

For lysis of synaptic vesicles or synaptosomes, 8 M urea and 2 M thiourea 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer was added. The protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, followed by the addition of 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and incubation at 56 °C for 1 h to reduce disulfide bonds. Subsequently, 55 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min to alkylate free sulfhydryl groups. The reaction mixture was then transferred to a 10-kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) filter, along with 400 μL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer. Prior to use, the MWCO filter was pre-activated by washing three times with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer. The protein mixture was loaded onto the pre-activated ultrafiltration unit and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min. Buffer exchange was performed four times by adding fresh 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and centrifuging at 14,000 × g for 10 min each. Sequencing-grade modified trypsin (1:50 enzyme-to-substrate ratio, Promega) was then added, and the sample was digested overnight at 37 °C. After digestion, the tryptic peptides were transferred to a new microcentrifuge tube and dried under vacuum. The samples were stored at −80 °C until LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

The tryptic peptides were analyzed using on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Scientific) with an integrated field-asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry (FAIMS) technology, coupled to an EASY-nano-LC 1000 system. Lyophilized peptides were re-dissolved in solvent A (solvent A, 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water) and automatically loaded onto a homemade trap column (100 μm × 2 cm; particle size, 3 μm; pore size, 120 Å; SunChrom) at a flow rate of 10 μL/min. The sample was subsequently separated with a homemade analytical column at a flow rate of 600 nL/min (75 μm × 20 cm; particle size, 1.9 μm; pore size, 120 Å; SunChrom) with a 120-min gradient: 6% solvent B for 2 min, 6 to 7% for 1 min, 7 to 28% for 85 min, 28 to 45% for 22 min, 45 to 95% for 5 min, 95% for 5 min (solvent B, 95% acetonitrile/0.1% FA). The electrospray voltage of 2.3 kV was used.

For both regular DIA and PulseDIA acquisitions, the MS1 full scan (200–1500 m/z) was conducted at 120,000 resolving power, 300% normalized AGC target, and auto maximum injection time. The isolation windows for the PulseDIA method were complementary and discontinuous isolation windows for the same set of pulse injections, with three injections corresponding to three complementary isolation window (7 m/z each)20. All the isolation windows for both PulseDIA and regular DIA included a 0.5-m/z overlap between adjacent windows. Precursor mass range was set as m/z 400–550, m/z 550–704, and m/z 700–902. Each MS2 scan was conducted at 30,000 resolving power, 3000% normalized AGC target, and normalized HCD collision energy in stepped mode (25, 27.5, and 30%). The default charge was 3+, and the RF lens was set to 40%.

Data analysis of regular DIA and PulseDIA

The PulseDIA raw files were analyzed using Spectronaut 19.1 (Biognosys) against the UniProt Mus Musculus database (17,207 entries) and iRT peptide sequences, employing default BGS factory settings. The false discovery rate (FDR) thresholds were set at 1% for peptides, PSMs, and proteins, with selection of 3–6 fragments per peptide. Protein quantification was performed using the QUANT 2.0 (SN Standard) method with MS1-level quantification. Abundance correction for ontology proteins was implemented based on iRT proteins exhibiting consistent theoretical abundance. The constructed spectral library comprised 176,727 precursors for brain fractions and 94,696 for colon fractions, generated using default Spectronaut parameters. Subsequent analysis of regular DIA raw files against this spectral library yielded the final protein identifications.

For protein filtering and imputation procedures, different criteria were applied according to experimental design. In the synaptic protein profiling, proteins were retained when demonstrating <30% missing values within any experimental group, thereby preserving proteins uniquely present in either SV or SN fractions. For the fasting mouse model experiments exclusively for SN proteome analysis, proteins were retained when exhibiting <30% missing values across all samples. Missing value imputation was subsequently performed utilizing the minimum observed value within each proteome dataset.

Bioinformatics, statistics, and reproducibility

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID)43. Data processing, statistical testing, and visualization were implemented in R v4.4.3 using the limma (v3.62.2), ggplot2 (v4.0.4), and tidyr (v1.3.1) packages. Detailed statistical methods for each analysis are provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Electron microscopy imaging of the purified synaptosome

Purified synaptosomes were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde. After three washes (6 min each) with 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, the samples were fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer at 4 °C for 2 h. Following fixation, the samples were rinsed three times (6 min each) with distilled water. Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30, 50, 70, 80, 90, and 100%, 6 min per step), followed by a 6-min acetone wash. For resin infiltration, the samples were incubated sequentially in the four sequential buffers: 3:1 (v/v) acetone:TAAB 812 (1 h, RT); 1:1 (v/v) acetone:TAAB 812 (2 h, RT); 1:3 (v/v) acetone:TAAB 812 (3 h, RT); Pure TAAB 812 resin (17 h, RT). After infiltration, the samples were embedded and polymerized at 60 °C for 42 h. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were cut using a Leica UC6 ultramicrotome, stained with 2% uranyl acetate (15 min), rinsed six times with distilled water, counterstained with Reynolds’ lead citrate (5 min), and rinsed again six times. Finally, the sections were examined under a Thermo Fisher Tecnai Spirit 120-kV transmission electron microscope.

The sample preparation for negative-staining

For negative staining, a 3 μL aliquot of the synaptic vesicle sample was applied to a hydrophilized carbon-coated grid and incubated for 60 s. The excess solution was blotted with filter paper, followed by immediate addition of 3 μL staining buffer (2% uranyl acetate, 2% uranyl formate, and 3% phosphotungstic acid). After 60-s incubation, the staining solution was removed by blotting. This staining procedure was repeated once. After air-drying at room temperature, grids were stored in the dark for electron microscopy.

Western blotting

The protein samples were mixed with SDS-PAGE sample buffer, separated using 10% SDS-PAGE gel, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using the Bio-Rad Mini Transblot apparatus. The membrane was blocked with PBST containing 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. The blot was then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized by chemiluminescence. All antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Primary neuron culture from the cerebral cortex and the colon of mice

Cerebral cortex tissue was dissected from E16 to E18 fetal mice and immediately placed in ice-cold DMEM. The tissue was digested in 2 mL of digestion solution (Neurobasal culture medium, 2 mg/mL papain, 0.1 mg/mL DNase I) at 37 °C for 30 min. Digestion was terminated by adding 2 mL of fetal bovine serum. After removing the supernatant, the remaining tissue was further digested in 2 mL of fresh digestion solution for 2 min, then passed through a 200-µm mesh filter. The filtrate was collected in a 50 mL centrifuge tube, while undigested tissue residues were discarded. This process was repeated three times to maximize cell yield. The isolated primary neurons were resuspended in culture medium and plated onto poly-D-lysine/laminin-coated dishes. Cells were maintained in culture for 7 days to achieve 80% confluency for subsequent immunofluorescence experiments.

For primary neuronal culture from mouse colon, E16–E18 fetal colon tissues were dissected and sequentially digested with collagenase (2 h, 37 °C) and trypsin/EDTA (0.125 mg/mL; 15 min, 37 °C). Subsequent steps followed the standard protocol for cerebral cortex primary neuron culture as described above.

Immunofluorescence

After washing three times with PBS (3 min per wash), cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min) and rinsed again with PBS (3 × 3 min). Permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (20 min, room temperature), followed by PBS washes (3 × 5 min). Cells were blocked with goat serum (20 min, room temperature) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. The next day, samples were washed with PBST (3 × 3 min), incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody (1 h, room temperature), and rinsed again with PBST (3 × 3 min). Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst (5 min, dark), followed by extensive PBST washes (4 × 5 min) to remove excess dye. Images were acquired using a ZEISS LSM880 confocal microscope.

Mouse fasting experiment

Following a 2-week acclimation period, the mice were randomly allocated into two groups: (i) a control group with ad libitum access to food and (ii) a fasting group subjected to a 2-day fasting period. After the fasting intervention, all animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and colon tissues were immediately collected for synaptic proteome analysis.

Metabolic monitoring and kinase inhibitor administration

Mice were randomly assigned to either a vehicle control group (10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, 45% saline) or an FGFR1 inhibitor group (SU4984 from MedChemExpress, 50 mg/kg in vehicle), with both treatments administered via intraperitoneal injection (i.p.). Following 1 week of single-housing acclimation, mice were transferred to metabolic cages within a Comprehensive Lab Animal Monitoring System (CLAMS; Columbus Instruments) under standardized environmental parameters: 20% O₂, 0.05% CO₂, 0.9 LPM flow rate, and 800 mmHg pressure. Individual metabolic data were collected per channel per animal. After a 2-day adaptation period in the metabolic cages, mice underwent a 24-h fasting protocol with ad libitum water access. After that, the vehicle or FGFR1 inhibitor was administered via intraperitoneal injection (i.p.), during which CLAMS continuously monitored metabolic and physiological parameters.
