Serum GPX4, ALOX15, and FPN1 are associated with severity of carotid atherosclerosis: a cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Wei Wei, Jianwei Xiang, Wei Min, Xiaping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarotid atherosclerosis (CAS) is a major cause of ischemic stroke. Ferroptosis-related proteins glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), arachidonate 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15), and ferroportin-1 (FPN1) may be involved in atherosclerotic processes, but their clinical relevance in CAS remains unclear.ObjectiveTo examine the associations of serum GPX4, ALOX15, and FPN1 with CAS severity and evaluate their discriminatory performance for severe carotid stenosis.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study included 280 CAS patients classified into non-severe (n = 207) and severe (n = 73) stenosis groups. Serum GPX4, ALOX15, and FPN1 were measured by ELISA. Group differences were analyzed, ROC analysis assessed discriminatory performance, and logistic regression identified factors associated with severe CAS.ResultsSevere CAS patients were older and had higher proportions of diabetes, multiple plaques, and prior stroke. They also showed higher TC, LDL-C, hs-CRP, and IL-6, lower HDL-C, lower serum GPX4 and FPN1, and higher ALOX15. The AUCs were 0.721 for GPX4, 0.698 for FPN1, and 0.869 for ALOX15. The biomarker model yielded an AUC of 0.914. The combined model including clinical variables and the three biomarkers yielded an AUC of 0.991 and outperformed the clinical model alone. In multivariable analysis, age, HDL-C, Z-ln(hs-CRP), Z-ln(IL-6), GPX4, and ALOX15 remained independently associated with severe CAS.ConclusionSerum GPX4, ALOX15, and FPN1 were associated with CAS severity and provided additional discriminatory information for severe carotid stenosis in this cohort. Introduction

Carotid atherosclerosis (CAS) is an important manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis and a major cause of ischemic stroke [1]. Progressive plaque formation in the carotid artery can lead to luminal stenosis, plaque instability, artery-to-artery embolism, and cerebral ischemic events. Severe carotid stenosis, commonly defined as NASCET ≥ 70%, is clinically important because it reflects greater vascular burden and is closely related to clinical decision-making [2, 3]. Therefore, identifying circulating biomarkers associated with severe CAS may provide complementary information beyond conventional anatomic assessment.

Duplex ultrasonography, computed tomography angiography (CTA) [4], and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) [5] remain the main tools for diagnosing CAS and grading stenosis severity. These imaging modalities provide essential structural information, including plaque presence and luminal narrowing, but they do not directly reflect systemic inflammatory, oxidative, or ferroptosis-related changes associated with severe CAS. Previous reviews of serum biomarkers in carotid atherosclerosis have mainly focused on inflammatory, lipid-related, metabolic, and vascular remodeling markers [6, 7]. However, clinical evidence regarding circulating ferroptosis-related proteins in CAS remains limited.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxides, has emerged as a key mechanism in various cardiovascular pathologies, including AS [8]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that ferroptosis contributes to endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory activation, and lipid metabolism disturbances within atherosclerotic lesions [9]. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a central antioxidant enzyme that protects cell membranes from lipid peroxidation; its downregulation has been shown to exacerbate endothelial ferroptosis and accelerate AS progression in animal models [10]. Arachidonate 12/15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15) catalyzes the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, generating pro-inflammatory lipid mediators; endothelial overexpression of ALOX15 under disturbed flow promotes inflammation and lesion development, while its inhibition attenuates atherosclerosis in murine models [11]. Ferroportin 1 (FPN1), the sole known cellular iron exporter, regulates intracellular iron homeostasis; macrophage-specific deficiency of FPN1 leads to iron overload, enhanced oxidative stress, and increased plaque burden in experimental atherosclerosis [12]. In human carotid plaque tissue, altered ferroptosis-related indicators, including decreased GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression and increased Fe2+ and lipid peroxidation, have also been associated with plaque instability [13]. These findings support the potential relevance of ferroptosis-related pathways in CAS, but their reflection in circulating serum biomarkers remains unclear.

This study aimed to investigate the associations of serum GPX4, ALOX15, and FPN1 levels with disease severity in patients with CAS using a cross-sectional observational design. By integrating serum biomarker measurements with clinical and imaging data, we sought to clarify whether these circulating ferroptosis-related proteins are associated with severe carotid stenosis and whether they provide additional discriminatory information beyond conventional clinical and inflammatory variables.

Methods

Patients and study design

This cross-sectional observational study prospectively and consecutively enrolled 280 patients with CAS who visited the Department of Vascular Surgery, The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, between January 2022 and June 2024. Of the 312 patients initially screened, 32 did not meet the inclusion criteria and were excluded for various reasons, leaving 280 eligible participants included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of patient recruitment and grouping

The diagnosis of CAS was confirmed by duplex ultrasonography or computed tomography angiography (CTA) based on the presence of carotid plaques or an intima-media thickness (IMT) ≥ 1.0 mm in the common carotid artery. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of CAS; and (3) availability of complete clinical and laboratory data at baseline. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with recent (< 3 months) coronary revascularization or hospitalization for heart failure; (2) active systemic infection or inflammatory disease; (3) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; (4) malignancy or autoimmune disease; (5) pregnancy or lactation; and (6) inability or refusal to provide informed consent. Patients with ischemic symptoms, including ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), were identified based on clinical diagnosis and neuroimaging. “Multiple plaques” were defined as the presence of two or more distinct atherosclerotic plaques detected in one or both carotid arteries on duplex ultrasonography. All participants received standard medical management for carotid atherosclerosis and its risk factors according to current clinical guidelines at the time of admission. The study was not designed to evaluate treatment effects or prognosis.

Patients were grouped by stenosis severity using the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial (NASCET) percentage: severe (≥ 70%) vs. non-severe (< 70%) for the main analysis [2, 3], which is a widely accepted threshold for defining severe carotid stenosis. Stenosis was assessed primarily by duplex ultrasound and, when available, corroborated by CTA/MRA. Velocity criteria (e.g., ICA PSV > 230 cm/s or ICA/CCA PSV ratio ≥ 4.0 for ≥ 70%) were applied when direct diameter reduction could not be measured. Measurements were independently reviewed by two experienced vascular sonographers, and discrepancies > 10% were adjudicated by a third reviewer.

All participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (Approval No. LYEC2024-0160).

The minimum required sample size was estimated using the single-proportion formula n = Z1−α/22×p×(1 − p)/d2, where Z1−α/2 is the standard normal deviate corresponding to a two-tailed α of 0.05 (1.96), p is the anticipated proportion, and d is the desired precision. Based on our previous clinical experience, we expected approximately 24% of patients to have severe carotid stenosis (NASCET ≥ 70%), with a precision of 0.05. Under these assumptions, the calculated minimum sample size was about 280 patients. This calculation was used to ensure an adequate representation of patients with severe stenosis for group comparison and association analyses, rather than for developing a prognostic prediction model.

Measurement of GPX4, ALOX15, and FPN1

Serum GPX4 levels were measured using a sandwich ELISA kit (EH8916, FineTest, Wuhan, China; range 78.125–5000 pg/mL; sensitivity 46.875 pg/mL). Serum ALOX15 was determined with a chemiluminescent sandwich ELISA kit (LS-F29234, LSBio, Seattle, WA, USA; range 15.625–1000 pg/mL; sensitivity 9.375 pg/mL), and serum FPN1 with a sandwich ELISA kit (LS-F33705, LSBio, Seattle, WA, USA; range 0.156-10 ng/mL; sensitivity 0.094 ng/mL).

Fasting venous blood was collected within 24 h after diagnosis and enrollment, allowed to clot, centrifuged at 1500 g for 15 min, and stored at -80 °C until analysis. According to the manufacturers’ specifications, all three ELISA kits were applicable to human serum samples. All assays were performed in duplicate following the manufacturers’ instructions, and quality controls were included on each plate. The measured values were interpreted as circulating serum protein levels and were not assumed to directly represent plaque tissue expression or local ferroptosis activity.

Data collection

Demographic characteristics, clinical history, and laboratory data were collected at baseline, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, presence of multiple carotid plaques, history of stroke, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus. Laboratory parameters included total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). Serum lipid profiles, hs-CRP, and IL-6 were measured in the hospital’s clinical laboratory using standard automated biochemical and immunoassay methods. Detailed information on concurrent medications, including statins, antiplatelet agents, antihypertensive drugs, and glucose-lowering therapies, as well as anthropometric indices such as waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio, were not uniformly available in the dataset and therefore were not included in the present analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software, and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (Table S1 in supplementary file). Continuous variables were non-normally distributed and are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR, range), with comparisons performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess correlations among biomarkers and between baseline continuous variables and biomarkers. Sex-stratified comparisons of serum GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15 levels were performed as supplementary analyses.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the discriminatory performance of individual biomarkers and logistic regression-derived models for severe carotid stenosis. Predicted probabilities from logistic regression were used to generate model-based ROC curves, and AUCs were compared using the DeLong test. Internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples, and optimism-corrected AUCs were reported. Category-free net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to assess the incremental discriminatory information of the combined model compared with the clinical model.

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to examine factors associated with severe CAS. Univariate logistic regression was first performed for candidate variables. To reduce overfitting, the final multivariable model was restricted to seven clinically relevant variables: age, HDL-C, Z-ln(hs-CRP), Z-ln(IL-6), GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15. hs-CRP and IL-6 were natural-log transformed and standardized before model entry. HDL-C was retained as a binary variable to improve model stability and interpretability. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of all patients

A total of 280 patients with CAS were included in this cross-sectional observational study, including 207 in the non-severe stenosis group and 73 in the severe stenosis group according to NASCET criteria. Severe cases were significantly older (p < 0.001) and had higher proportions of multiple plaques, prior stroke, and diabetes (all p < 0.05, Table 1). The severe group also showed a modest but significant increase in TC (p = 0.032), lower HDL-C (p < 0.001), and higher LDL-C (p < 0.001). Inflammatory markers hs-CRP and IL-6 were markedly elevated in the severe group (both p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in sex distribution, BMI, TG, smoking status, alcohol consumption, or hypertension prevalence between groups.

Serum levels of GPX4, ALOX15, and FPN1 in different CAS patients

Building on the baseline characteristics, we next compared circulating ferroptosis‑related markers between groups and examined their inter‑relationships. As shown in Fig. 2A, GPX4 and FPN1 were significantly lower in the severe group compared with the non‑severe group (228.27 (124.88) pg/mL vs. 289.53 (121.46) pg/mL, Z = -5.629; 1.14 (0.74) ng/mL vs. 1.43 (0.63) ng/mL, Z = -5.039, respectively; both p < 0.001). In contrast, ALOX15 was markedly higher in the severe group (200.52 (96.04) pg/mL vs. 114.50 (56.86) pg/mL, Z = -9.391; p < 0.001).

B Correlation analysis between serum GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15 levels using Spearman’s rank correlation test

In correlation analyses (Fig. 2B), FPN1 showed a weak positive correlation with GPX4 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.171, p = 0.004), whereas ALOX15 showed weak inverse correlations with GPX4 (ρ = -0.158, p = 0.008) and FPN1 (ρ = -0.331, p < 0.001). These findings indicate a circulating biomarker pattern characterized by higher ALOX15 and lower GPX4 and FPN1 in patients with severe CAS, although the inter-marker correlations were modest. In sex-stratified analyses, ALOX15 remained significantly higher in the severe stenosis group in both men and women. GPX4 and FPN1 were significantly lower in women with severe stenosis, whereas these differences were not statistically significant in men, likely due to the limited number of severe cases among male participants (Table S3). Additional Spearman correlation analyses showed that age, HDL-C, hs-CRP, and IL-6 were correlated with at least one ferroptosis-related biomarker, whereas BMI, TC, TG, and LDL-C showed no consistent correlations with GPX4, FPN1, or ALOX15 (Table S4).

Discriminatory performance of biomarkers and clinical models for severe carotid stenosis

Given the significant differences in serum GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15 between groups, ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate their ability to distinguish severe from non-severe carotid stenosis. As shown in Fig. 3A-C, the AUCs for GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15 were 0.721, 0.698, and 0.869, respectively, with ALOX15 showing the highest discriminatory performance among the three individual biomarkers. A biomarker model including GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15 yielded an AUC of 0.914 (Fig. 3D).

D ROC curve of the biomarker model including GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15. E ROC curve of the clinical model including age, HDL-C, Z-ln(hs-CRP), and Z-ln(IL-6). F ROC curve of the combined model including clinical variables and the three ferroptosis-related biomarkers

To further assess whether these biomarkers provided additional discriminatory information beyond conventional clinical variables, we constructed a clinical model including age, HDL-C, Z-ln(hs-CRP), and Z-ln(IL-6). The clinical model yielded an AUC of 0.953 (Fig. 3E). After adding GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15 to the clinical model, the combined model showed an AUC of 0.991 (Fig. 3F). DeLong test showed that the combined model had a significantly higher AUC than both the biomarker model alone (0.991 vs. 0.914, p < 0.001) and the clinical model alone (0.991 vs. 0.953, p = 0.002), whereas the difference between the biomarker model and clinical model was not statistically significant (p = 0.097). Internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples showed limited optimism in the ROC models. The optimism-corrected AUCs were 0.911 for the biomarker model, 0.949 for the clinical model, and 0.987 for the combined model (Table S5). Reclassification analysis further showed that adding GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15 to the clinical model improved classification performance, with a category-free NRI of 1.681 (95% CI: 1.324–1.972, p < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.198 (95% CI: 0.117–0.318, p < 0.001). These results suggest that serum GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15 may provide additional discriminatory information for severe carotid stenosis in this cohort when combined with conventional clinical and inflammatory variables.

Logistic regression for severe carotid stenosis

Univariate logistic regression was first performed to examine factors associated with severe CAS. Age, TC, HDL-C, multiple plaques, stroke history, diabetes, Z-ln(hs-CRP), Z-ln(IL-6), GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15 were significantly associated with severe carotid stenosis. To reduce the risk of overfitting, the multivariable model was restricted to seven clinically relevant variables representing age, lipid status, inflammation, and ferroptosis-related biomarkers, including age, HDL-C, Z-ln(hs-CRP), Z-ln(IL-6), GPX4, FPN1, and ALOX15. Because hs-CRP and IL-6 showed skewed distributions, they were natural-log transformed and standardized before entry into the model. HDL-C was retained as a binary variable to improve model stability and interpretability. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis, and all VIF values were < 2.0, indicating no significant multicollinearity (Table S2 in supplementary file).

As shown in Table 2, age, HDL-C, Z-ln(hs-CRP), Z-ln(IL-6), GPX4, and ALOX15 remained independently associated with severe CAS in the revised multivariable model. Higher age, Z-ln(hs-CRP), Z-ln(IL-6), and ALOX15 were associated with increased odds of severe stenosis, whereas higher HDL-C and GPX4 were associated with decreased odds. FPN1 was significantly associated with severe CAS in univariate analysis but was not independently associated after multivariable adjustment. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed no evidence of poor model fit (p = 0.901).

Discussion

The clinical assessment of CAS severity is primarily based on imaging, whereas circulating biomarkers that reflect molecular features of vascular injury remain insufficiently characterized. Existing evidence on ferroptosis-related proteins in atherosclerosis has been largely derived from preclinical models [14]. In this cross-sectional observational study, severe CAS was associated with a circulating biomarker pattern characterized by higher ALOX15 and lower GPX4 and FPN1 levels. The biomarker model showed higher discriminatory performance than individual biomarkers, and the addition of these biomarkers to clinical and inflammatory variables further improved discrimination of severe stenosis in this cohort. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary association data rather than evidence of prognostic prediction.

GPX4 is a key antioxidant enzyme that prevents lipid peroxidation and inhibits ferroptosis as previously established in oxidative stress models [15]. Early transgenic studies also demonstrated that GPX4 overexpression protects against oxidative lipid damage in vivo [16]. FPN1 is the only known cellular iron exporter, maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis and protecting against iron overload-induced oxidative injury [17, 18]. Previous studies have demonstrated the critical role of FPN1 in AS and cardiovascular injury. In experimental AS, cathepsin B (CTSB) was shown to promote FPN1 degradation, disrupt iron homeostasis in macrophages, and induce ferroptosis, thereby accelerating plaque progression, while CTSB inhibition restored FPN1 levels and attenuated lesion development [19]. In human plaques, elevated FPN1 expression accompanied by reduced ferroxidase activity has been associated with local iron retention and oxidative stress [20]. In a diabetic myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury model, activation of the NRF2/FPN1 pathway improved cardiac function, reduced ferroptosis, and alleviated oxidative stress, highlighting the cardioprotective value of FPN1-mediated iron regulation [21].

With respect to GPX4, macrophage-specific GPX4 deletion was recently shown to promote foam-cell formation by increasing lipid uptake and reducing cholesterol efflux, underscoring its role in limiting macrophage ferroptosis during plaque initiation [22]. Qing-Xin-Jie-Yu Granule treatment upregulated the GPX4/xCT pathway, inhibited macrophage ferroptosis, reduced inflammatory cytokines, and stabilized plaques in ApoE-/- mice [23]. Ferroptosis was also found to exacerbate lipid metabolism disorders and vascular pathology via dysregulation of the TFR1/SLC11A2/GPX4 axis in atherosclerotic mice [24]. Pharmacologic inhibition of ferroptosis likewise alleviated atherosclerotic lesions in ApoE⁻/⁻ mice [25]. Furthermore, clinical data from acute ischemic stroke patients showed that serum GPX4 levels decreased with increasing disease severity and were inversely correlated with inflammatory markers of hs-CRP and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), suggesting a link between GPX4 deficiency and vascular injury severity [26]. Consistently, vulnerable carotid plaques exhibit decreased GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression together with elevated Fe2+ and lipid peroxidation, linking ferroptosis activation to plaque instability [13]. Recent reviews have summarized serum biomarkers related to carotid atherosclerosis, mainly involving inflammatory and lipid pathways [6, 7]. However, GPX4 overexpression alone did not significantly alter plaque size or composition despite lowering lipid peroxidation [27]. At the molecular level, miR-214-3p has been shown to promote macrophage ferroptosis and inflammation by repressing GPX4 transcription [28]. Recent evidence also indicates that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, contributes to cerebrovascular injury through oxidative and ferroptosis-associated mechanisms, showing potential mechanistic overlap with GPX4-mediated redox regulation [29]. However, clinical data on ferroptosis-related proteins such as GPX4 and FPN1 remain scarce. In our cohort, both GPX4 and FPN1 levels were significantly reduced in patients with severe CAS and showed a positive correlation, consistent with their coordinated protective roles against ferroptosis observed in preclinical studies. Unlike previous work mainly based on animal models or other cardiovascular conditions, our findings provide clinical evidence that lower serum GPX4 and FPN1 levels are associated with greater CAS severity. However, because FPN1 was not independently associated with severe stenosis after multivariable adjustment, its clinical relevance should be interpreted cautiously and requires further validation.

ALOX15 catalyzes the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids to generate pro-inflammatory lipid mediators. It has been implicated in endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation, and ferroptosis, processes that are central to the pathogenesis of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Although its role in AS has been less extensively characterized than other ferroptosis-related proteins, accumulating evidence points to ALOX15 as a pivotal upstream driver of ferroptotic injury in vascular tissues. In regions of disturbed flow prone to AS, endothelial ALOX15 expression is markedly upregulated, promoting the generation of the pro-inflammatory metabolite 15-S-HETE via SREBP2-dependent transcription; inhibition or knockdown of ALOX15 attenuates endothelial dysfunction and reduces disturbed flow-induced plaque formation [11]. Beyond AS, ALOX15 has been shown to exacerbate cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury by suppressing GPX4, enhancing ferroptosis, and amplifying inflammatory responses through PHD2-mediated destabilization of HIF-2α, whereas ALOX15 silencing alleviates these effects [30]. In myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury, ALOX15 mediates phospholipid peroxidation of PUFA-enriched phosphatidylethanolamines during ischemia, initiating robust oxidative damage during reperfusion [31]. Moreover, prolonged reperfusion is characterized by predominant cardiomyocyte ferroptosis driven by ALOX15-derived 15-HpETE; myocardial-specific ALOX15 knockout or pharmacological inhibition preserves mitochondrial function, suppresses ferroptosis, and improves cardiac recovery [32]. In our cohort, serum ALOX15 levels were significantly elevated in severe CAS and inversely correlated with GPX4 and FPN1. These findings suggest that elevated serum ALOX15 is associated with severe carotid stenosis in humans and are broadly consistent with experimental evidence linking ALOX15 to oxidative stress and vascular inflammation. Further mechanistic and longitudinal studies are required to determine whether circulating ALOX15 reflects plaque-level ferroptosis or vascular inflammatory activity.

An important consideration is that GPX4 and ALOX15 are mainly intracellular enzymes, whereas FPN1 is a membrane iron exporter. Therefore, serum levels of these proteins should not be interpreted as direct measures of their expression or activity within carotid plaques. Circulating levels may reflect a combination of systemic oxidative stress, inflammatory injury, cellular damage, extracellular vesicle release, or protein shedding. In the absence of paired plaque tissue analysis, our findings support their potential value as circulating biomarkers associated with CAS severity, but they do not establish that serum levels directly reflect local plaque ferroptosis.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although patients were prospectively enrolled, the present analysis was cross-sectional; therefore, temporal relationships, causal inference, and prognostic value for future stenosis progression or vascular events could not be assessed. Second, this was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size. Although internal bootstrap validation showed limited optimism in model performance, external validation in an independent cohort is still required. Third, detailed information on concurrent medications, including statins, antiplatelet agents, antihypertensive drugs, and glucose-lowering therapies, was not uniformly available. These treatments may influence lipid levels, inflammatory markers, oxidative stress, and potentially ferroptosis-related biomarkers, and medication-related confounding could not be fully excluded. Fourth, waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio were not available, and BMI alone may not fully reflect fat distribution. Fifth, GPX4, ALOX15, and FPN1 were measured in serum, and paired carotid plaque tissue was not available. Therefore, serum levels cannot be assumed to directly reflect plaque expression, plaque ferroptosis activity, or local vascular mechanisms. Finally, only three ferroptosis-related biomarkers were evaluated, and other ferroptosis-related mediators and longitudinal clinical outcomes were not included.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional observational study, severe carotid atherosclerosis was associated with higher serum ALOX15 levels and lower GPX4 and FPN1 levels. ALOX15 showed the highest individual discriminatory performance, and adding GPX4, ALOX15, and FPN1 to clinical and inflammatory variables improved discrimination of severe carotid stenosis in this cohort. Multivariable analysis showed that age, HDL-C, Z-ln(hs-CRP), Z-ln(IL-6), GPX4, and ALOX15 were independently associated with severe CAS, whereas FPN1 was not independently associated after adjustment. These findings suggest that ferroptosis-related circulating proteins may provide additional information on CAS severity, but their biological relevance and clinical utility require confirmation in longitudinal and externally validated studies.
