Computational identification of ECT2 as a potential pan-cancer biomarker and therapeutic target through integrated genomic data analysis
Authors: Minghao Chen, Li Wang, Guodong Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The ECT2 gene, encoding a guanine nucleotide exchange factor, plays crucial roles in cell cycle progression and cytoskeletal dynamics, implicating its involvement in various cancers. However, a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis integrating genomic data is still lacking. This study employed an integrated approach using data from multiple cancer genomics databases to assess ECT2 across various malignancies. Expression profiles of ECT2 were analyzed for differential expression across tumor stages and its association with clinical outcomes. Correlation analyses examined the relationship between ECT2 expression and immune cell infiltration levels. Pathway enrichment analysis identified biological processes influenced by ECT2 dysregulation in cancer progression. These methods facilitated a comprehensive exploration of ECT2’s role in cancer biology, revealing potential implications for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy. Analysis across 33 tumor types consistently shows elevated ECT2 expression. ECT2 correlates with tumor staging in 8 cancers and molecular subtypes in 13 cancers, and shows associations with immune subtypes in 22 cancers, suggesting its role in cancer progression and immune modulation. ECT2 demonstrates strong diagnostic potential (the area under the ROC curve > 0.9) in 16 cancers and correlates with poorer overall survival in 11 cancers. ECT2 expression correlates positively with microsatellite instability in STAD, MESO, UCEC, and READ, and negatively in DLBC; it correlates positively with tumor mutational burden in STAD, PAAD, ACC, LGG, and LUAD, and negatively in THYM. ECT2 also exhibits diverse correlations with immune checkpoint genes and specific immune cell types identified through CIBERSORT analysis. ECT2 interacts with proteins like RACGAP1, KIF23, enriched in pathways involving cell polarity, Ras signaling, and tight junctions, impacting cancer progression and stemness in various cancer types. This study offers comprehensive insights into ECT2’s role in cancer biology through integrative bioinformatics analyses. The results advocate for ECT2 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in diverse malignancies, suggesting avenues for personalized oncology strategies.

1. Introduction

Cancer continues to pose a formidable challenge in global public health, posing a serious threat to human well-being.[1] Advances in molecular biology and bioinformatics technologies have markedly enhanced our understanding of cancer mechanisms and progression. These investigations have revealed the diverse nature and intricate biological characteristics shared among various cancer types.[2] Hence, identifying and comprehensively understanding key genes and signaling pathways implicated in cancer development are critical for advancing novel therapeutic strategies and precision medicine.[3]

Epithelial cell transforming sequence 2 (ECT2) encodes a guanine nucleotide exchange factor for Rho GTPases,[4] overexpressed in multiple cancers and strongly linked to tumor invasion and metastasis.[5,6] ECT2 protein significantly regulates essential biological processes including cell proliferation, migration, and polarity.[7] Its aberrant expression and activation in tumors have led to its scrutiny as a potential oncogenic target.[8,9] Therefore, comprehensive analysis of the ECT2 gene and its protein products can significantly enhance our understanding of its mechanistic roles across various cancer types and its potential clinical applications.

This study aims to comprehensively analyze the expression patterns and potential biological functions of the ECT2 gene across different cancer types using bioinformatics tools and database resources. By integrating data from diverse sources, we aim to explore the roles of ECT2 in cancer development and elucidate its potential implications in cancer occurrence, progression, and treatment. Through this research, we aim to provide significant support for the development of novel cancer treatment strategies and precision medicine, thereby advancing the field of cancer biology and offering new insights for clinical practice.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Expression of ECT2 across pan-cancer

RNA expression analysis of ECT2 was performed using 2 primary datasets. Firstly, ECT2 expression across normal tissues and tumor cell lines was assessed using the Human Protein Atlas database (https://www.proteinatlas.org).[10] Secondly, RNA-seq data and clinical information from 10,534 samples across 33 cancer types and matched adjacent normal tissues were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database via UCSC XENA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/).[11] Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.2. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was employed to compare ECT2 expression levels between cancerous and adjacent non-cancerous tissues, with statistical significance defined as P < .05 (*), P < .01 (**), and P < .001 (***). Data visualization was performed using the ggplot2 package.

2.2. ECT2 expression in tumor stage, molecular subtypes, and immune subtypes of cancers

ECT2 expression across different cancer stages was obtained from the GEPIA2 database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/),[12] which utilizes RNA sequencing data from TCGA and GTEx projects. Molecular and immune subtypes were characterized using the TISIDB database (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/),[13] integrating diverse data to evaluate tumor-immune interactions. Immune subtypes are defined by integrating immune cell infiltration, immune gene expression, and immune-related markers to categorize tumor-immune interactions.

2.3. Prognostic and diagnostic value of ECT2

We evaluated the prognostic and diagnostic implications of ECT2 expression across multiple cancer types using RNA sequencing data obtained from the TCGA database. The diagnostic value of ECT2 expression was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the ROC curve (AUC). ROC analysis was performed using the Xiantao tool (https://www.xiantao.love/) to ascertain the accuracy of ECT2 in distinguishing pan-cancer tissues. Diagnostic accuracy thresholds were defined as follows: low accuracy (AUC: 0.5–0.7), moderate accuracy (AUC: 0.7–0.9), and high accuracy (AUC > 0.9).

Survival analyses, including overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI), were conducted using the log-rank test implemented in the Xiantao tool, with significance set at P < .05. Hazard ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals and P-values were calculated. Forest plots were generated using the Xiantao tool for visualization.

2.4. Genomic mutation of ECT2

Mutation analysis of the ECT2 gene across various tumors was conducted using the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (https://www.cbioportal.org/).[14] This platform integrates multidimensional cancer genomics data to visualize and analyze genetic alterations. Copy number variations and single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) of ECT2 were further analyzed using the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) web tool (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/GSCA/).[15] GSCA provides comprehensive analysis capabilities for exploring genetic alterations in cancer datasets.

2.5. Immune infiltration analysis

Previous studies have highlighted tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) as prognostic markers affecting cancer patient responses to immunotherapy.[16,17] To explore the immunological implications of ECT2 across diverse cancers, we employed a systematic analytical approach. Initially, using Spearman correlation analysis, we investigated the relationship between ECT2 expression levels and TMB/MSI scores across multiple cancer types based on TCGA gene expression profiles. Subsequently, we examined the association between ECT2 and crucial immune checkpoint genes (ITPRIPL1, SIGLEC15, TIGIT, CD274, HAVCR2, PDCD1, CTLA4, LAG3, and PDCD1LG2) using transcriptomic data from TCGA. Statistical significance was determined using appropriate tests adjusted for multiple comparisons.[18] Finally, to explain ECT2’s influence on immune cell infiltration, we utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm to estimate the proportions of distinct immune cell types in tumor samples based on TCGA gene expression profiles.[19]

2.6. Functional analysis of ECT2

To explore the functional interactions of ECT2, we employed GeneMANIA (http://www.genemania.org), a bioinformatics tool that integrates various datasets to predict protein–protein interactions.[20] This analysis offers insights into the network of proteins associated with ECT2, identifying potential functional partners and pathways. Based on the identified proteins, we performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses using the Xiantao Academic platform. Additionally, we utilized the GSCA platform to investigate ECT2’s involvement in 10 common cancer pathways.[15] GSCA integrates cancer genomic data to assess the enrichment and significance of ECT2 within these pathways, providing insights into its potential roles in cancer biology.

2.7. Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using curated gene sets sourced from the GSEA Molecular Signatures Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/downloads.jsp).[21] We assessed differences in ECT2 expression between high- and low-expression groups across 11 cancer types. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the “clusterProfiler” package in R, with significance thresholds set at FDR < 0.25 and adjusted P < .05. Each analysis involved 1000 gene set permutations for robustness. Visualizations of the results were generated using Xiantao Academic.

2.8. Cancer stemness analysis

RNA-sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for 11 cancers were obtained from TCGA. The mRNA stemness index (mRNAsi), calculated using the OCLR algorithm developed by Malta et al, utilized expression profiles of 11,774 genes.[22] Stemness index is a measure used to assess the stem cell-like characteristics of tumor cells, reflecting their self-renewal ability, differentiation potential, and resistance to treatment. Spearman correlation coefficients were computed on RNA expression data normalized to a scale of 0 to 1.

3. Results

3.1. ECT2 expression patterns

Analysis of the Human Protein Atlas database revealed diverse expression patterns of ECT2 in both normal tissues and cancer cell lines. In normal tissues, ECT2 demonstrates elevated expression levels in the thymus, bone marrow, testis, rectum, and retina, while displaying reduced expression in the choroid plexus, pituitary gland, epididymis, skeletal muscle, and ovary (Fig. 1A). Conversely, in cancer cell lines, ECT2 shows heightened expression in bile duct carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, adrenocortical carcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, and ovarian cancer, but diminished expression in multiple myeloma, leukemia, lymphoma, neuroblastoma, and testicular cancer (Fig. 1B). Comparative analysis of TCGA data across 33 tumor types and their adjacent tissues reveals heightened ECT2 expression in 17 cancers, such as BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, and COAD, with no instances of decreased expression observed (Fig. 1C). Pairwise comparisons further indicate markedly increased expression in tumor tissues across the majority of cancers, except for KICH and THCA (Fig. 1D).

3.2. Relationship between ECT2 expression and tumor staging, grading, and immune subtypes

Using the GEPIA2 database, we analyzed the association between ECT2 expression and tumor staging across various cancer types. Our findings reveal significant correlations between ECT2 expression and staging in 8 types of cancer: ACC, BRCA, KICH, KIRC, LIHC, LUAD, PAAD, and SKCM (Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q575). Conversely, no correlation was observed in other cancer types (Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q575).

Additionally, we employed the TISIB database to explore the relationship between ECT2 expression and molecular subtypes as well as immune subtypes. ECT2 expression levels were found to correlate with molecular subtypes in 13 cancers, specifically ACC, BRCA, COAD, HNSC, KIRP, LGG, LIHC, LUSC, OV, PRAD, READ, STAD, and UCEC (Fig. 2 and Figure S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q575). However, no correlation was observed in ESCA, GBM, PCPG, and SKCM. Furthermore, ECT2 expression demonstrated associations with immune subtypes in 22 cancers, while no associations were found in 8 other cancers (Fig. 3 and Figure S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q575).

3.3. Diagnostic and prognostic value

We evaluated the diagnostic value of ECT2 across various tumors using ROC curve analysis. The results indicate that ECT2 demonstrates strong diagnostic potential, with an area under the curve (AUC) >0.9 in 16 cancers: BRCA (AUC = 0.949), CESC (AUC = 0.996), CHOL (AUC = 1.000), COAD (AUC = 0.999), ESCA (AUC = 0.990), GBM (AUC = 0.984), HNSC (AUC = 0.928), LAML (AUC = 0.980), LUSC (AUC = 0.983), OV (AUC = 0.996), PAAD (AUC = 0.984), READ (AUC = 0.992), SARC (AUC = 0.973), STAD (AUC = 0.983), UCEC (AUC = 0.971), and UCS (AUC = 0.997) (Fig. 4). In 9 cancers, the AUC ranged between 0.7 and 0.9, while it was <0.7 in 6 cancers (Figure S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q575).

To assess the prognostic value of ECT2 across tumors, we examined OS, DSS, and PFI using TCGA data. High expression of ECT2 was associated with poorer OS in 11 cancers, indicating a negative correlation between high ECT2 expression and survival (Fig. 5A and B). Conversely, a positive correlation was observed in THYM. ECT2 expression was also negatively correlated with DSS in 9 cancers (Fig. 5C) and with PFI in 9 cancers (Fig. 5D).

3.4. Gene mutation analysis

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of gene mutations across pan-cancer tumors using data from the cBioPortal database, encompassing 10,953 patients. Among these cases, mutations were detected in 752 patients (7%). Notably, lung squamous cell carcinoma, ovarian cancer, and esophageal cancer exhibited significant mutation frequencies of 37.58%, 12.12%, and 19.23%, respectively (Fig. 6A). The predominant mutation type observed was amplification. Specifically, missense mutations were the most common mutation type at specific genomic sites (Fig. 6B).

The genetic alteration of ECT2. (A) Alteration frequency of ECT2 in pan-cancer. (B) The frequency and types of ECT2 somatic mutations in pan-cancer. (C) CNV pie charts of ECT2 in each cancer. Different colored sections represent different CNV types. (D) Heatmap of SNV percentage for ECT2. CNV = copy number variations, SNV = single-nucleotide variant.

Further exploration into copy number variation across different tumor types revealed widespread instances of copy number alterations, predominantly featuring heterozygous amplifications and deletions (Fig. 6C). Analysis of SNVs highlighted UCEC, SKCM, and COAD as having the highest frequencies, with 24%, 17%, and 13% of cases exhibiting SNVs, respectively (Fig. 6D).

3.5. Immune correlations

MSI and TMB are crucial indicators for predicting responses to cancer immunotherapy. We examined the relationship between ECT2 expression and MSI across various tumors, revealing a positive correlation in STAD, MESO, UCEC, and READ, and a negative correlation in DLBC (Fig. 7A). ECT2 expression showed positive correlations with TMB in STAD, PAAD, ACC, LGG, and LUAD, while demonstrating a negative correlation in THYM (Fig. 7B).

Further investigation into the correlation between ECT2 and immune checkpoint genes showed predominantly positive associations across tumors, with exceptions noted in ACC, CHOL, ESCA, HNSC, and UCS. Negative correlations were observed in CESC and TGCT (Fig. 7C).

Using CIBERSORT analysis to explore ECT2 expression’s relationship with immune cells, we identified significant correlations across multiple tumor types. Specifically, ECT2 expression was associated with T cell regulatory cells in 18 tumor types, B cell memory in 17 tumor types, and various T cell subsets (CD4+ memory resting, CD4+ memory activated), Macrophage M2, and Macrophage M1 in 14 tumor types (Fig. 7D).

3.6. Protein–protein interactions and function

To delve deeper into the role of ECT2 in tumors, we conducted an analysis of its protein interactome, performed GO and KEGG pathway analyses, and explored relevant tumor-related pathways. GeneMANIA-based protein interaction network analysis identified the top 20 proteins closely associated with ECT2: RACGAP1, KIF23, GGN, RHOA, PRKCQ, TIAM1, RPGRIP1L, TLE5, R3HDM1, MRPL27, PARD6A, UBE3A, ARIH1, GKAP1, PARD3, PARD6B, CDH1, PLK4, PLEK, and PKP4 (Fig. 8A). These interactions primarily involve physical associations.

PPI network and Pathway analysis of ECT2. (A) PPI network for ECT2 was constructed via GeneMANIA. (B) Bubble diagram for GO and KEGG analysis of ECT2-related genes. (C) Effect of upregulation of ECT2 on oncogenic pathway activity in pan-cancer. PPI = protein–protein interactions.

Functional enrichment analysis involved ECT2 and its top 50 associated proteins, revealing significant GO enrichments. In the biological process category, prominent pathways included establishment or maintenance of cell polarity, cortical cytoskeleton organization, Ras protein signal transduction, cell–cell junction organization, and regulation of cell shape. For cellular component (CC), enriched pathways were cell cortex, cell–cell junction, apical junction complex, bicellular tight junction, and cleavage furrow. Molecular function analysis highlighted pathways such as guanyl ribonucleotide binding, guanyl nucleotide binding, GTP binding, GTPase activity, and GTPase regulator activity. KEGG pathway analysis identified the top pathways as the Rap1 signaling pathway, Axon guidance, Tight junction, Hippo signaling pathway, and Adherens junction (Fig. 8B). Additionally, analysis using GSCALite revealed that ECT2 primarily activates apoptosis and cell cycle pathways while inhibiting Ras/MAPK and hormone ER pathways in common cancers (Fig. 8C).

3.7. GSEA analysis

We conducted GSEA across 11 types of cancers, revealing significant pathways including retinoblastoma gene in cancer, cell cycle checkpoints, mitotic spindle checkpoint, scavenging of heme from plasma, Cd22-mediated BCR regulation, and initial triggering of complement. These pathways are crucial in regulating cell cycle progression; their dysregulation, through mutation or inactivation, can lead to uncontrolled cell proliferation, promoting cancer formation and growth (Fig. 9).

3.8. Tumor stemness analysis

The tumor stemness index evaluates the similarity of tumor cells to stem cells and correlates with a higher degree of dedifferentiation in tumors, often associated with poorer prognosis. Across 11 types of cancers, we conducted tumor stemness scoring and found that except for KICH and KIRP, ECT2 was significantly associated with the other 9 types of cancers. Within the high ECT2 expression group, tumors exhibited higher tumor stemness scores (Fig. 10).

4. Discussion

This study employs comprehensive bioinformatics analysis to systematically investigate ECT2 expression patterns across diverse tumor types, its prognostic implications, and cellular functions. Our findings reveal that ECT2 exhibits high expression levels in various tumors and correlates significantly with tumor staging, grading, and adverse prognosis. Notably, ECT2 plays a pivotal role in cell cycle regulation, underscoring its potential as a critical driver of tumorigenesis. These results emphasize ECT2’s candidacy as a promising therapeutic target.

Rho GTPases are crucial cellular signaling proteins that regulate fundamental processes like cytoskeletal remodeling, cell migration, adhesion, polarization, and gene transcription.[23,24] ECT2 functions as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor for Rho GTPases, critically influencing cell proliferation, division, and apoptosis.[7] Previous studies have consistently reported dysregulated ECT2 expression across various cancers. For instance, breast cancer research highlights elevated ECT2 levels associated with advanced stage, poor differentiation, hormone receptor loss, and adverse prognosis.[25] Similarly, studies in pancreatic cancer suggest ECT2’s potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker.[26] Bioinformatics analyses and clinical studies in cervical cancer confirm significantly heightened ECT2 expression.[27] In gastric cancer, ECT2 expression markedly exceeds normal tissue levels, correlating with deeper invasion, ulceration, cancer emboli, neural invasion, and lymph node metastasis.[28] Lung cancer studies also identify elevated ECT2 in adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma.[29] These findings corroborate our analysis, underscoring ECT2’s widespread overexpression across cancers and its correlation with disease severity, immune classification, diagnosis, and prognosis. Notably, ECT2 holds promise as a diagnostic biomarker warranting further comprehensive investigation across cancer types.

Gene expression is intricately regulated by transcription factors,[30] DNA methylation,[31] histone modifications,[32] and gene mutations.[33] In tumorigenesis, tumor suppressor genes are often silenced by hypermethylation, while oncogenes are typically hypomethylated. Notably, studies have documented ECT2’s hypomethylated status in various human cancers, characterized by low methylation levels at its promoter and CpG islands.[34] Moreover, phosphorylation analysis reveals extensive ECT2 phosphorylation at multiple sites in tumor tissues, suggesting a role in promoting tumor progression.[34] Importantly, our findings highlight genetic mutations and copy number variations affecting ECT2 expression across multiple cancers.

The immune system plays a pivotal role in tumor development and treatment. Recent studies indicate that ECT2 regulates immune responses within the tumor microenvironment and influences the efficacy of immunotherapy. Elevated ECT2 expression in pancreatic cancer correlates with heightened sensitivity to PD-1 therapy and chemotherapy drugs.[26] Studies demonstrate that ECT2 expression positively correlates with immune-suppressive cell infiltration like MDSCs and negatively correlates with anti-tumor cell infiltration such as NKT cells across various human tumors.[34] ECT2 promotes M2-type macrophage polarization in hepatocellular carcinoma, impairing the function of critical immune cells like NK cells and T cells essential for suppressing tumor progression.[6] Our findings suggest that ECT2 expression levels modulate immune checkpoint molecule expression. Elevated ECT2 expression upregulates immune checkpoint molecules, potentially impeding the immune system’s ability to target tumors. The interaction between ECT2 and immune cells is intricate; notably, elevated ECT2 expression diminishes T cell regulatory and B cell memory, while enhancing T cell CD4+ memory resting, T cell CD4+ memory activated, and Macrophage M1 cells. These findings suggest that ECT2 may contribute to tumor immune evasion mechanisms, thereby compromising the immune system’s efficacy against tumors.

Recently, multiple studies have investigated ECT2’s role in cancer progression. Husser MC elucidated ECT2’s localization and its involvement in cell division processes.[35] Studies on lung adenocarcinoma demonstrate that ECT2 activates adhesion molecule components, and its inhibition reduces cancer cell proliferation and adhesion capabilities.[5] In colorectal cancer, nuclear ECT2 promotes ribosomal DNA transcription and ribosome biogenesis.[9] Research in cervical cancer indicates that ECT2 regulates the AKT/mTOR pathway, influencing cell proliferation, metastasis, and drug sensitivity; ECT2 silencing inhibits the AKT/mTOR pathway and induces apoptosis.[27] In gastric cancer, miR-223-3p modulates tumor behavior by regulating ECT2 expression, possibly through activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.[28] ECT2 is involved in KRAS-mediated transformation processes in lung adenocarcinoma by regulating rRNA synthesis.[29] Our analysis of ECT2’s molecular interactions identified RACGAP1 and KIF23 as closely associated proteins, both implicated in multiple human cancer types. Additional enrichment and GSEA analyses indicate that ECT2 primarily controls cell polarity maintenance, morphological changes, cell division, migration, and invasion capabilities.

A deep understanding of molecular functions enables the identification of potential therapeutic targets for personalized medicine, elucidating individual variations in patient responses to drug treatments and their underlying mechanisms. Research has investigated ECT2 as a promising therapeutic target in cancer, evaluating multiple ECT2 inhibitors and refining ligand designs targeting ECT2 via molecular dynamics, structural analysis, and free energy calculations,[34] thus bolstering its potential for future applications. A study in lung cancer highlighted the significance of the CircSETD3/FXR1/ECT2 axis in acquired resistance to gefitinib in NSCLC.[36] Another study demonstrated a correlation between ECT2 expression levels and radiosensitivity in lung and nasopharyngeal cancers, showing synergistic effects of PARP inhibitors and radiation that enhance sensitivity in radioresistant tumors with low ECT2 expression.[37] These findings collectively highlight ECT2’s potential as a therapeutic target in cancer treatment.

While this study comprehensively analyzed ECT2 expression patterns in various cancers and their prognostic implications using integrative bioinformatics approaches, it has limitations. Firstly, it relied primarily on data from public databases without experimental validation, potentially limiting the generalizability of some conclusions. Secondly, the heterogeneity across different cancer types may lead to varied mechanisms of action for ECT2, a facet not extensively explored in this study.

In summary, this study identified a significant correlation between elevated ECT2 expression and poor prognosis across multiple cancers through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis. ECT2, a guanine nucleotide exchange factor for Rho GTPases, plays a crucial role in cell division and cell cycle regulation. These findings not only enhance our understanding of ECT2’s role in cancer initiation and progression but also lay a theoretical foundation for the development of targeted therapies against ECT2. Future research should validate the precise mechanisms of ECT2 in cancer through experimental studies and explore its therapeutic potential.
