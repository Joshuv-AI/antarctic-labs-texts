Improving prediction of ypT0–1N0 response in rectal cancer: the added value of gross tumor type to magnetic resonance tumor regression grade after chemoradiotherapy in a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kyong-Min Kang, Mi-Jeong Choi, Hong-min Ahn, Heung-Kwon Oh, Duck-Woo Kim, Jungheum Cho, Won Chang, Young Hoon Kim, Kyoung Ho Lee, Yu Kyung Jun, Yonghoon Choi, Sung-Bum Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWhile MRI-based tumor regression grade (mrTRG) has shown promise in evaluating pathologic response to concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) in rectal cancer, its ability to predict pathologic complete response remains limited. This study aimed to enhance mrTRG’s diagnostic performance in predicting ypT0–1N0 status, a key factor in considering non-radical management after CCRT for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC).MethodsThis retrospective study included 430 patients with LARC who underwent radical resection following CCRT at a single referral hospital between April 2018 and September 2024. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify predictive factors associated with achieving ypT0–1N0 status. The diagnostic performances of mrTRG1–2 alone and in combination with other factors were assessed by comparing sensitivity, specificity, positive-predictive value (PPV), negative-predictive value, and area under the curve (AUC).ResultsNinety-three patients (21.6%) achieved ypT0–1N0. In the multivariable analysis, fungating type, cT1–2, and mrTRG1–2 were independent predictors for ypT0–1N0. Integrating mrTRG with gross tumor type yielded the highest AUC of 0.689 among the combined models. For predicting ypT0–1N0, the combination of mrTRG and gross tumor type improved PPV (79.2% vs. 41.5% for mrTRG alone) while also demonstrating enhanced sensitivity compared with ycT0–1N0, the conventional MRI-based predictor (40.9% vs. 22.6%).ConclusionThis study demonstrated that combining mrTRG and gross tumor type improved the PPV of mrTRG in predicting ypT0–1N0 after CCRT in LARC. Further studies are warranted to validate the role of gross tumor type in refining predictive systems for selecting candidates for non-radical treatment.

INTRODUCTION

The standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) typically involves concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) followed by total mesorectal excision (TME) [1]. Although the standardization of TME has contributed to improved outcomes for LARC in recent years by lowering local recurrence rates and increasing cancer-related survival rates [23], concerns persist regarding post-surgical complications, including low anterior resection syndrome and the potential need for a permanent colostomy after sphincter-preserving surgery [45]. In up to 30% of patients with rectal cancer, no viable tumor cells are detected after preoperative CCRT [67]. This corresponds to pathologic complete response (pCR), a well-established prognostic factor associated with excellent long-term outcomes [789] that potentially enables non-radical management, including local excision or the ‘watch and wait’ approach [710]. Therefore, accurate assessment of tumor response to CCRT is fundamental for identifying the most suitable candidates for this treatment strategy [1911]. MRI is regarded as the gold standard modality for staging rectal cancer, as it provides detailed visualization of the rectal wall, mesorectum, and adjacent pelvic structures [1213]. Although pre-CCRT MRI-based clinical T stage is recognized as a prognostic factor for pCR [714], the sensitivity of ycT0N0—the conventional post-CCRT MRI-based assessment for pCR—remains low [1315].

The magnetic resonance tumor regression grade (mrTRG) system, designed by the MERCURY study group, has been gaining attention as a diagnostic tool for assessing tumor response to CCRT [161718]. This system, derived from T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) on MRI, is analogous to the pathologic tumor regression grade (pTRG) [17]. It employs a 1–5 scale based on the ratio of residual tumor (indicated by intermediate signal intensity on T2WI) to fibrosis (characterized by significantly low signal intensity on T2WI) [19]. However, despite its association with long-term oncologic outcomes [17], a meta-analysis [20] has shown that the accuracy of mrTRG in detecting pCR and ypT0–1 remains constrained. While the integration of additional parameters, such as tumor markers, radiomics, or alternative imaging protocols with mrTRG [1919], has been proposed as a means to improve its diagnostic performance, research on this issue is currently insufficient. In particular, gross tumor type, a pre-CCRT parameter, has been reported to correlate with survival outcomes in colorectal cancer [2122]. However, it has not yet been considered a component of the combination model for response prediction.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association of gross tumor type with achieving ypT0–1N0 status—a surrogate of good response to CCRT and a suitable criterion for non-radical treatment including local excision [23]—and to analyze how this parameter may enhance the diagnostic accuracy of mrTRG for identifying good responders.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital approved this study and waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective design and the minimal risk of patient identification (No. B-2505-971-102).

Study population

This retrospective, comparative study enrolled 430 patients with LARC from a single tertiary referral hospital. Following CCRT, patients underwent radical resection between April 2018 and September 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) stage II (T3–4, N-negative) or stage III (any T, N-positive) disease as determined by MRI before CCRT, (2) tumor located ≤15 cm from the anal verge, and (3) patients who underwent anorectal MRI both pre- and post-CCRT for restaging, including yc staging and mrTRG evaluation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who had newly developed distant metastasis during CCRT, and (2) patients who underwent short-course radiotherapy.

Treatment

CCRT involved delivering a total dose of 45 Gy to the pelvis in 25 fractions, followed by a 5.4-Gy boost to the primary tumor in 3 additional fractions over a period of 5.5 weeks, consistent with the protocol used in our previous study [13]. One of the following four chemotherapeutic regimens was administered for CCRT: (1) capecitabine, (2) 5-fluorouracil, (3) tegafur-uracil, and (4) 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin. Surgery was performed 6–14 weeks after completion of CCRT, and all patients underwent TME.

Assessment

Tumor distance from the anal verge was evaluated before CCRT using digital rectal examination (DRE) and endoscopy. Gross tumor type was assessed through DRE, while endoscopic findings were applied for tumors located beyond the reach of DRE (upper rectal cancers). Gross tumor type was classified according to the Borrmann classification [24]: types 1 and 2 were defined as fungating morphology, while types 3 and 4 were considered infiltrative morphology. MRI scans of all patients were acquired both before CCRT and 6–7 weeks after its completion. The term ycT refers to T stage determined by MRI after CCRT, and ypT denotes T stage determined by histopathological examination of the resected specimen [25]. The classification of mrTRG is presented in Table 1 [17]. Pathological staging followed the guidelines outlined in the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC). The pTRG was evaluated based on the AJCC tumor regression grading system and categorized as follows: grade 0 (no residual tumor cells), grade 1 (single cells or small clusters of cells), grade 2 (residual cancer accompanied by a desmoplastic response), and grade 3 (minimal signs of tumor response) [26].

Statistical analysis

The Fisher exact test or Pearson chi-square test was used to assess categorical variables. Factors with missing values were excluded from the logistic regression analysis. A multivariable logistic regression model was developed to identify factors related to ypT0–1N0 using variables that were significantly associated in a univariable analysis and clinically relevant. A sensitivity analysis for predictive factors was performed, including clinical variables with a P-value of ≤0.2 in the univariable analysis. The areas under the curve (AUCs) based on receiver operating characteristic curve analyses of mrTRG1–2 alone and in combination with other factors for predicting ypT0–1N0 were compared using the DeLong’s method. Additionally, the sensitivity, specificity, positive-predictive value (PPV), and negative-predictive value (NPV) of mrTRG1–2 alone and in combination with related factors, as well as ycT0–1N0, the conventional MRI-based predictor, were analyzed. All statistical tests were 2-sided, with P-values of <0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, ver. 27 (IBM Corp.) and R software ver. 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

The clinicopathologic characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 2. Ninety-three patients (21.6%) achieved ypT0–1N0 post-CCRT. The univariable analysis revealed that the ypT0–1N0 rate was significantly higher for the fungating tumor type than for the infiltrating tumor type (65.9% vs. 9.7%, P < 0.001). Patients with cT1–2 exhibited a higher ypT0–1N0 rate than those with cT3–4 (57.4% vs. 17.2%, P < 0.001). Post-CCRT tumor size ≤2 cm was associated with a higher ypT0–1N0 rate than post-CCRT tumor size >2 cm (36.2% vs. 15.4%, P < 0.001). ycT0–1N0 was linked to a higher ypT0–1N0 rate than non-ycT0–1N0 (65.6% vs. 18.1%, P < 0.001). Patients classified as mrTRG1–2 had a higher ypT0–1N0 rate than those with mrTRG3–5 (41.5% vs. 13.0%, P < 0.001).

Predictive factors

Fungating type (odds ratio [OR], 0.073; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.040–0.132; P < 0.001), cT1–2 (OR, 0.315; 95% CI, 0.136–0.729; P = 0.007), and mrTRG1–2 (OR, 0.386; 95% CI, 0.207–0.720; P = 0.003) were identified as independent factors for ypT0–1N0 in the multivariable analysis (Table 3). The sensitivity analysis for predictive factors demonstrated consistent results (Table 4).

Diagnostic performance

Table 5 presents the diagnostic performance of each predictive model for ypT0–1N0. mrTRG alone had sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 58.1% (95% CI, 47.9–67.6), 77.4% (95% CI, 72.7–81.6), 41.5% (95% CI, 33.4–50.1), and 87.0% (95% CI, 82.7–90.3), respectively. The combination of mrTRG with gross tumor type showed sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 40.9% (95% CI, 30.4–49.9), 97.0% (95% CI, 94.3–98.2), 79.2% (95% CI, 63.5–86.7), and 85.3% (95% CI, 81.4–88.5), respectively. The combination of mrTRG with pre-CCRT clinical T stage had sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 22.0% (95% CI, 15.3–32.1), 97.3% (95% CI, 94.6–98.4), 69.0% (95% CI, 50.1–81.4), and 82.0% (95% CI, 77.9–85.4), respectively. The combination of mrTRG with gross tumor type and pre-CCRT clinical T stage showed sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 15.4% (95% CI, 9.2–23.7), 99.4% (95% CI, 97.9–99.8), 93.3% (95% CI, 64.0–96.5), and 80.9% (95% CI, 76.9–84.4), respectively. ycT0–1N0 alone had sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 22.6% (95% CI, 15.3–32.1), 97.7% (95% CI, 94.3–98.2), 65.6% (95% CI, 48.3–79.6), and 81.9% (95% CI, 77.8–85.4), respectively.

Receiver operating characteristic curves illustrating the diagnostic performances of different predictive models for identifying ypT0–1N0 status are presented in Fig. 1. The model integrating mrTRG with gross tumor type showed the highest AUC value (0.689; 95% CI, 0.620–0.759) among the following combinations of related factors: fungating type alone (AUC, 0.223; 95% CI, 0.162–0.285), cT1–2 alone (AUC, 0.385; 95% CI, 0.314–0.455), mrTRG alone (AUC, 0.678; 95% CI, 0.613–0.742), fungating type with cT1–2 (AUC, 0.584; 95% CI, 0.514–0.654), mrTRG1–2 with cT1–2 (AUC, 0.594; 95% CI, 0.524–0.665), mrTRG1–2 with both fungating type and cT1–2 (AUC, 0.574; 95% CI, 0.504–0.644), and ycT0–1N0 alone (AUC, 0.597; 95% CI, 0.526–0.667). DeLong’s test showed that the AUC of mrTRG combined with gross tumor type differed significantly from those of all other predictive models, except mrTRG alone (P = 0.823).

DISCUSSION

The present study developed a novel diagnostic model that integrates gross tumor type with mrTRG, based on the predictive factors for good responders to preoperative CCRT in LARC. We revealed that incorporating gross tumor type into mrTRG improved the PPV for detecting ypT0–1N0, increasing it from 41.5% with mrTRG alone to 79.2%, while increasing the sensitivity compared with ycT0–1N0 from 22.6% to 40.9%.

The reported diagnostic performance of MRI in assessing tumor response after CCRT has varied widely across studies, with sensitivities ranging from 16.7% to 84.0%, largely attributable to heterogeneity in study design and patient populations [152728]. In our previous study [13], we demonstrated that mrTRG1–2 exhibits a higher sensitivity (58.8% vs. 16.2%) but a lower PPV (44.0% vs. 57.9%) than ycT0–1N0. Given that a low PPV, reflecting a high false-positive rate, can lead to poor oncologic outcomes in non-radical treatment [29], a favorable mrTRG response alone is insufficient as an independent criterion for selecting patients for minimally invasive treatment following CCRT. Notably, combining predictive parameters with mrTRG presents a practical strategy to enhance its diagnostic accuracy for pathologic response [1], as its predictive performance for tumor response has been improved in previous studies by integrating various factors [1123031]. The infiltrating gross tumor type is associated with a higher risk of recurrence and mortality than the fungating type [2122]. However, few studies have established the relationship between gross tumor type and pathologic response to CCRT. An early study reported a positive association between fungating tumor morphology and achievement of ypT0–1N0, a finding that is consistent with the results of our study [10]. Although the model incorporating gross tumor type into mrTRG did not show a statistically significant difference in AUC compared with mrTRG alone, our findings demonstrated that the mrTRG system was refined in a way that improved not only sensitivity but also PPV compared with conventional MRI-based ycT0–1N0, which has been widely used in clinical practice.

Although pre-CCRT clinical T stage was also identified as an independent predictor for good responders in this study and its association with pathologic response has been reported in numerous studies [714], evidence on its diagnostic performance remains limited. When combined with mrTRG, the model exhibited notably low sensitivity (22.0%) in our analysis, carrying the potential risk of excluding patients who might otherwise benefit from non-radical treatment.

While studies on mrTRG have focused primarily on identifying ypT0–1, no research has specifically evaluated the ability of mrTRG to predict ypT0–1N0 [20]. Although the presence of residual nodal disease is associated with decreased disease-free survival and overall survival in rectal cancer, local excision does not address residual nodal disease, which may increase the likelihood of disease persistence [2332]. However, when selectively performed in patients with negative lymph node findings following CCRT, local excision can serve as a safe alternative to transabdominal resection [33]. Therefore, incorporating nodal negativity into response assessment might be essential in determining the suitability of non-radical treatment in LARC.

Both ycT0–1N0 and post-CCRT tumor size have been reported as independent predictors of pathologic response [3435]; however, this correlation was not significant in the present study. The observed association between ycT0–1N0 and post-CCRT tumor size with pathologic response may have been overemphasized, as cT stage, mrTRG, and gross tumor type were not included in multivariable models of previous studies.

This study had some limitations. First, selection bias was inherent owing to the retrospective single-center design. Although variability in mrTRG assessment among radiologists may have been present [1731], inter-observer agreement could not be evaluated in this study as the data were obtained from electronic medical records. In addition, gross tumor type assessed by DRE or endoscopy may also be subject to inter-observer variability, highlighting the need for standardized assessment criteria in future studies. Moreover, the variation in the interval between CCRT completion and surgery, ranging from 6 to 14 weeks in this study, may have influenced the evaluation of pathologic response. Second, the predictive models did not include either molecular biomarkers, such as cell-free or circulating tumor DNA and microRNA, or conventional clinical assessment modalities such as endoscopy and DRE. The clinical role of molecular markers in assessing minimal residual disease remains under investigation in ongoing trials in Korea and has not yet received regulatory approval from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. While post-CCRT endoscopy and DRE were routinely performed, assessment through these modalities was excluded because our institution had not prospectively collected endoscopic and DRE-based evaluations using a uniform 3-tier system for post-CCRT response assessment—this process has recently been initiated. Third, our study could not incorporate patients treated with total neoadjuvant therapy, as this approach was not reimbursed by the Korean National Health Insurance until September 2024, which restricted its clinical application during the study period. Finally, while the combination proposed in this study enhances tumor response prediction, its true value requires validation, as its diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.689 is insufficient to reliably select patients suitable for non-radical treatment.

Nonetheless, this study demonstrated that integrating gross tumor type with mrTRG markedly enhances the PPV in predicting ypT0–1N0 compared with mrTRG alone, while also improving the sensitivity of ycT0–1N0. Future prospective studies are warranted to clarify the potential role of gross tumor type in refining predictive systems for identifying appropriate candidates for non-radical treatment after CCRT.
