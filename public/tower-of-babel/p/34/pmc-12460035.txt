Detectability of Automated Tumor-feeder Detection Software Using : Angio-computed Tomography in Transarterial Chemoembolization for : Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Masatomo Ueda, Hidenori Mitani, Yu Nakaki, Hisayuki Kodama, Toshihiro Tachikake, Masumi Sumida, Takashi Moriya, Kei Morio, Wataru Fukumoto, Keigo Chosa, Kazuo Awai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose: To evaluate the performance of automated tumor-feeder detection software in transarterial chemoembolization for hepatocellular carcinoma using angio-computed tomography.Material and Methods: This was a retrospective study of 107 hepatocellular carcinomas in 74 patients who underwent selective transarterial chemoembolization between June 2021 and December 2022. Identification of tumor-feeding arteries on computed tomography during hepatic angiography images acquired prior to chemoembolization with angio-computed tomography was evaluated in two independent methods: analysis by automated tumor-feeder detection software and interpretation by radiologists. The sensitivity and positive predictive value of both were calculated, and the sensitivity was compared with the McNemar test. Differences with p <0.05 were considered statistically significant.Results: Transarterial chemoembolization was applied to 107 hepatocellular carcinoma tumors fed by 114 arteries. No significant difference was observed in sensitivity between the software and the interpretation of radiologists (90.4% vs. 95.6%, p = 0.15). The positive predictive value for the software was 90.4%; that for the interpretation of radiologists was 86.8%.Conclusions: The accuracy of automated tumor-feeder detection software applied to angio-computed tomography was comparable to that of radiologists.Keywords: automated tumor-feeder detection, transarterial chemoembolization, hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

Transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) is widely recognized as a standard treatment for patients with intermediate-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1]. The success of TACE largely depends on the accurate identification of tumor feeders, as incomplete embolization may result in tumor recurrence, and non-target embolization can lead to damage to healthy liver tissue [2].

The identification of tumor-feeding arteries has relied on the interpretation of digital subtraction angiography (DSA) or CT images by radiologists. Automated tumor-feeder detection (AFD) software using cone-beam CT during hepatic angiography (CTHA) has been developed as a tool to assist radiologists in accurately identifying tumor feeders [3]. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of AFD software, with its sensitivity ranging from 86% to 97% and positive predictive value (PPV) ranging from 83% to 99% [4-12]. Although AFD software with cone-beam CT has demonstrated these promising results, the utility of AFD software in angio-CT settings has not been fully investigated.

This study retrospectively evaluated the performance of AFD software during TACE using angio-CT by comparing its sensitivity and PPV with those of radiologists. The results of this study are expected to determine the clinical utility of AFD software in selective TACE with angio-CT.

Material and Methods

The author's institutional review board approved of this study. Written informed consent for TACE procedures was obtained from all patients. Individual informed consent for this study was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study; an opt-out method was alternatively employed.

Patients

This was a retrospective single-center study of HCC patients who underwent conventional TACE using AFD software (Embolization Plan, Canon Medical Systems [CMS], Tochigi, Japan) between June 2021 and December 2022. A total of 83 patients underwent conventional TACE during the period. Selective TACE (defined as TACE from distal to the segmental branch of the hepatic artery) was performed for 78 patients. Another four patients were excluded: two did not undergo CTHA, and two were treated via extrahepatic collateral arteries. Thus, 74 HCC patients who underwent selective TACE with CTHA were included. The Clinical Practice Guidelines for Hepatocellular Carcinoma (2017), published by the Japanese Society of Hepatology (https://bit.ly/3JVRka5), guided tumor diagnosis and surveillance in this study. Tumors were diagnosed based on high-attenuation dynamic contrast-enhanced CT or gadolinium ethoxybenzyl-diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid-enhanced MRI. Atypical cases on imaging were diagnosed by needle biopsy. The indication for TACE was determined by a consensus of radiologists and hepatologists, and it was performed for either Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging (1) B (intermediate) or BCLC 0/A (very early/early) cases in which other local therapies were unsuitable. Angiography and CTHA protocol

Written prior informed consent for TACE was obtained from all 74 patients. All procedures were performed by two board-certified interventional radiologists (IRists) with 12- or 25-years of experience using an angio-CT system (Alphenix sky-Aquilion Prime, CMS). Under local anesthesia, a 4-Fr sheath was inserted via the right femoral artery. A 4-Fr catheter was inserted into the common hepatic artery, and DSA was performed; 10 mL of contrast medium (iopamiron 370, Bayer Pharma, Osaka, Japan) was injected at a rate of 2 mL/s. Then, CTHA was performed 5 seconds after injecting 20 mL of the contrast medium diluted to 33% at 2 mL/s using a power injector (PRESSDUO elite, Nemoto Kyorindo, Tokyo, Japan). The flow rate and total volume of the contrast medium were occasionally adjusted by operators according to the hepatic arterial flow identified in the test injection. If replaced hepatic branches were present, or if the path to the common hepatic artery was tortuous, DSA and CTHA were performed with a 2.6-Fr microcatheter (Masters HF, ASAHI INTECC, Aichi, Japan) via a 4-Fr catheter placed at the origin of the celiac artery. DSA was acquired with a 12 × 16-inch flat-panel detector, using the following settings: tube voltage, 92 kV; tube current, 320 mA; and frame rate, three images/sec. For CTHA on an 80-row-detector CT scanner, images were reconstructed using a deep learning algorithm (Advanced intelligent Clear-IQ Engine: AiCE, CMS), with a matrix of 512 × 512 pixels, slice-thicknesses of 1 mm and 5 mm, and a 350-400 mm field-of-view adapted to the patient's physique.

Chemoembolization

A 1.8-Fr or 1.5-Fr microcatheter (BreakthroughTM AI 18, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA; Veloute Ultra, Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) was coaxially inserted through a 4-Fr catheter placed in the common hepatic artery to select the peripheral branch; when a 2.6-Fr microcatheter was used for CTHA, another 1.6-Fr microcatheter (MARVEL S, Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was employed as a tri-axial system. After confirming tumor enhancement with selective CTHA through the microcatheter that reached the feeding artery, an appropriate dose of chemotherapeutic agents mixed with ethiodized oil (Lipiodol, Guerbet, Villepinte, France) was infused, followed by gelatin particles (Gelpart, 1 mm diameter, Nippon Kayaku, Tokyo, Japan) until the tumor artery was filled. The chemotherapeutic agents were either epirubicin (Viatris Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan) or cisplatin (Nippon Kayaku, Tokyo, Japan), and the choice of drug was determined by the hepatologists based on the patient's condition. Epirubicin was dissolved in 1 mL of saline per 10 mg and emulsified with 1 mL of lipiodol. The mixture, containing 3-30 mg of epirubicin, was injected during TACE. Cisplatin powder (10 mg) was directly mixed with 1 mL of lipiodol, and a mixture of 10-50 mg cisplatin was injected. The quantity of lipiodol was determined based on the tumor size. Non-contrast CT was performed to confirm lipiodol uptake in the tumor; when it was insufficient, other feeders were addressed. Technical success was defined as lipiodol uptake in the entire tumor on non-contrast CT at the end of the procedure, which was evaluated for each tumor nodule.

Detection of tumor-feeding arteries by AFD software

After the CTHA scanning, radiological technologists used AFD software to analyze CTHA images on a workstation (Alphenix workstation, CMS). The user interface of the software is shown in Fig. 1. A circular region of interest (ROI) was placed on the tumor in the axial image under the IRist's instruction. The ROI was automatically changed to a volume of interest (VOI). When the area deviated from the VOI because the tumor was not spherical, another ROI was added to cover the entire lesion. In the presence of multiple tumors, the same method was applied to each tumor. Then the catheter tip was marked on the axial image, and probable tumor feeders were automatically identified. They were displayed as colored lines from the catheter tip to the tumor(s) on a three-dimensional (3D) image. The process from CTHA to completion of the analysis usually requires <5 minutes. Detection of tumor-feeding arteries on CTHA by radiologists

Diagnostic radiologists (18 or 34 years of experience) interpreted the CTHA images to identify the tumor feeders concurrently and independently of the AFD software analysis. They used the multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) view on a workstation (AWserver, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) to evaluate the feeding arteries on axial, coronal, and sagittal images (slice thickness 1 mm). They indicated all potential feeding arteries to the IRist performing TACE on the 3D image of CTHA.

Statistical analysis

True feeding arteries were defined as the vessels that produced tumor enhancement on selective CTHA. For arteries identified as feeding-positive by AFD and radiologists on CTHA, the sensitivity and PPV of each identification were calculated as percentages with the 95% CI. The McNemar test by R (version 3.2.3) was then performed to compare the sensitivity. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The 74 patients who underwent TACE had 107 HCC tumors fed by 114 arteries. The tumor characteristics are shown in Table 2. In 37 branches, TACE was performed at the subsegmental artery level, in 56 at the sub-subsegmental artery level, and in 21 at a site peripheral to the sub-sub-subsegmental artery level. Technical success was achieved in 101 tumors (94.3%). In six tumors, lipiodol uptake was not fully in the target tumor because the feeders were not identified (n = 2), hepatic reserve limited the treatment range (n = 3), and Gelpart particles leaked from another feeding artery (n = 1).

Tumor-feeder detection

Table 3 shows the detection performance of the AFD software and the radiologists interpreting CTHA. No significant difference was observed in sensitivity between the AFD software and radiologists (90.4% vs. 95.6%, p = 0.15). The software predicted 114 feeders; they included 11 false positives. The radiologists predicted 124 feeders, and 15 were false-positive. Consequently, the PPV of the AFD software was 90.4%, whereas that of the radiologists was 86.8%. The AFD software failed to detect 11 true feeders (9.6%) in 10 patients, while radiologists failed to detect five true feeders (4.4%) in five patients. Only two true feeders (1.8%) were missed by both the AFD software and radiologists. Analysis of the 10 cases containing 11 true feeders missed by the AFD software showed the following patterns of failure: In four cases, the software incorrectly identified adjacent non-feeding arteries as feeders, thereby failing to display the true feeders (a represented case is shown in Fig. 3). In another four cases, the software misinterpreted adjacent high-attenuation areas as vascular pathways; these areas included arterio-portal shunts (n = 2), lipiodol deposition from previous TACE (n = 1), and another enhanced tumor (n = 1). In the remaining two cases, the tumors were fed by multiple arteries, and the software failed to detect all of them. The median number of previous TACE sessions for the 10 patients whose feeders were missed by the AFD software was 3.5 (interquartile range, 1-6).

Discussion

This study showed that the AFD software applied to angio-CT provided sensitivity and PPV comparable to radiologists interpreting CTHA. The AFD software was found to identify approximately 90% of the true feeding arteries required for selective TACE.

Accurate identification of tumor-feeding arteries is crucial for successful selective TACE, ensuring complete embolization of the tumor and minimizing the risk of damage to healthy liver parenchyma [13, 14]. Miyayama et al. [3] reported that AFD software helped reduce procedural time and radiation exposure. The AFD software applied to angio-CT may also be useful for selective TACE.

The performance of the AFD software in this study was consistent with that reported in earlier studies. Previous reports of AFD software have shown that the sensitivity for detecting the feeders ranged from 86% to 97%, and the PPV ranged from 83% to 99% [4-12]. These studies utilized cone-beam CT, which is known for its higher spatial resolution and superior delineation of peripheral vessels compared to multi-detector CT [15]. Although angio-CT was used in this study, the performance of the AFD software was comparable to that of cone-beam CT reported previously. This finding indicates that the AFD software with angio-CT would work reliably, supporting its value in clinical practice.

On the other hand, it is important to recognize that AFD software cannot always achieve perfect identification of tumor-feeding arteries. Previous studies have reported common errors of AFD software: identifying adjacent incorrect vessels as feeders or misinterpreting high-attenuation areas, such as lipiodol accumulation, as vascular pathways [2-4]. Similar detection errors were observed in this study. The cases in which the AFD software failed to detect a feeder had a median of 3.5 previous TACE procedures. Repeated TACE leads to narrowing of the artery, formation of collateral vessels, and lipiodol deposition [16], which may have prevented detection by AFD software. Meanwhile, radiologists were also incomplete, missing five true feeders. Three of them were detectable by AFD, and only two (1.8%) were undetected by both the AFD software and radiologists in this study. Therefore, AFD should be used with caution, particularly in repeated TACE, whereas the combination of experienced radiologists and AFD software may improve the accuracy of feeder detection.

This retrospective study has several limitations. First, as a single-center investigation, the results may not be fully applicable to other institutions with different patient demographics, imaging equipment, or operator expertise. Second, the study did not evaluate procedural time, including the time to detect tumor-feeding arteries, or radiation exposure when using the AFD software compared to conventional methods. Moreover, factors such as tumor size, vascular complexity, and prior treatments, including lipiodol deposition, which may affect the software's performance, were not analyzed. Addressing these limitations in future multi-center and prospective studies will be essential to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the software's clinical utility.

In conclusion, the AFD software applied to angio-CT demonstrated accuracy comparable to that of radiologists in identifying tumor-feeding arteries in HCC cases. The performance of the AFD software can assist operators during selective TACE for HCC.
