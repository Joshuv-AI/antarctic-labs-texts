Diagnostic delay as health system navigation: a mixed-methods study of childhood cancer in Burkina Faso
Authors: Akiko Ida, Sonia Douamba Kaboret, Gabrielle Chantal Bouda, Fla Kouéta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiagnostic delay is a major contributor to childhood cancer mortality in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Existing studies identify determinants of delay but provide limited insight into how delays accumulate across care pathways. This study examined diagnostic delay among children with cancer in Burkina Faso using a health system navigation perspective and an explanatory mixed-methods design.MethodsWe conducted a sequential explanatory mixed-methods study at the two national paediatric oncology units in Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso. Quantitative analyses of children with a first cancer diagnosis (n = 30) examined associations between total diagnostic delay, number of provider encounters, and entry-point provider type, using minimal multivariable regression models. Findings were integrated through a health system navigation framework.ResultsThe median total diagnostic delay was 103.5 days (IQR 58–196), with referral delay representing the longest component. Background

Late diagnosis is a major contributor to childhood cancer mortality in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1–4]. While survival rates for childhood cancer exceed 80% in high-income countries, they remain below 30% in many LMICs [2, 5]. Early diagnosis is widely recognised as a key strategy for improving childhood cancer outcomes. However, children in low-income countries often experience substantially longer diagnostic intervals than those in high-income settings [1, 6]. In sub-Saharan Africa, children frequently present with advanced disease [1, 3, 4, 7, 8] despite growing efforts to promote early detection through initiatives such as the WHO Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer [5, 9]. Furthermore, many children remain undiagnosed and untreated. Recent estimates suggest that the proportion of undiagnosed childhood cancer cases is highest in West Africa (57%), compared with 3% in Western Europe and North America, indicating persistent global inequalities in this area [3].

Prolonged diagnostic delay has been documented across LMICs, including in African settings [1, 3, 8, 10, 11]. Diagnostic delay has been commonly conceptualised as a sequence of barriers occurring at different stages of care-seeking [11, 12]. Frameworks such as the Three-Delay Model and analyses based on patient and health-system delays have provided important insights into where delays occur and which factors contribute to them [12–16]. Recent reviews have identified a wide range of factors associated with delayed diagnosis, including socioeconomic vulnerability, limited health literacy, geographical barriers, use of traditional medicine, weaknesses in referral systems, and limited diagnostic capacity [6, 11, 12]. While previous studies have identified determinants of delayed diagnosis and described diagnostic pathways [17–20], less attention has been paid to the mechanisms through which delays accumulate during health system navigation [21].

This gap is particularly important in LMIC settings where patients move between public and private providers, and between biomedical and non-biomedical sources of care, in response to perceived effectiveness, accessibility, affordability, and referral experiences [19, 22]. Families may therefore navigate multiple providers and levels of care before reaching specialised services [11, 12, 23, 24]. However, in many LMICs, cancer is a relatively new policy subject and diagnostic capacity is unevenly distributed across healthcare providers; patients/caregivers are often confronted by socioeconomic challenges; and referral pathways are often weakly coordinated [4, 7, 8, 10, 11, 23, 25]. In this context, understanding how delays accumulate through navigation in Burkina Faso may have broader relevance beyond a single national context.

Recent work in global health and health services research has increasingly conceptualised care-seeking as a process of health system navigation [26]. In this study, health system navigation refers to the processes by which patients and caregivers seek timely diagnosis and treatment while overcoming barriers across different providers and levels of care, drawing on the concept of patient navigation proposed by Freeman and Rodriguez [21]. Referral pathways to diagnosis are strong predictors of cancer outcomes, although existing analyses have largely focused on high-income countries [27, 28]. Applying this perspective to childhood cancer diagnosis in LMIC settings may help explain not only where delays occur, but also how delays accumulate over time through interactions between diagnostic capacity, referral processes, care-seeking practices, and broader structural and social conditions.

In Burkina Faso, as in other LMICs, childhood cancer represents an important but under-documented public health concern [14, 29–32]. Burkina Faso is a low-income country in francophone West Africa, ranked 186th out of 193 countries on the Human Development Index [33]. Paediatric oncology services are centralised in two university hospitals in the capital, Ouagadougou, reflecting a typical health system structure in the region. In 2019, 227 patients under the age of 15 were treated, representing 20% of expected cases [34]. In francophone Africa, 77.5% of Burkitt’s lymphoma cases, one of the most common paediatric cancers in the region, were diagnosed at stage III or higher between 2016 and 2018 [32].

Existing studies have highlighted prolonged delays among children with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia [31], while identifying financial constraints and limited diagnostic resources as obstacles to diagnostic work-up [31], and socioeconomic vulnerability and limited diagnostic capacity as contributing factors to diagnostic delays [14, 35, 36]. Our previous study likewise found that pre-hospital delays were associated with household socioeconomic conditions [14]. However, the ways by which delays emerge and accumulate across care trajectories within the health system have not been sufficiently examined. This study therefore aimed to explore how and why diagnostic delays are prolonged among children with cancer in Burkina Faso, using the concept of health system navigation and an exploratory mixed-methods design.

Methods

Study design and mixed-methods approach

We conducted a sequential explanatory mixed-methods study with integrated interpretation [37] at two major national paediatric oncology units (POUs) in Burkina Faso to examine diagnostic delays among children with cancer. The quantitative analysis assessed predictors of total diagnostic delay (log-transformed), focusing on entry-point provider type and the number of provider encounters before the first consultation with the POU. Provider encounters referred to distinct episodes of consultation with biomedical or non-biomedical providers during care-seeking. Qualitative findings were used to interpret the quantitative patterns and explain how diagnostic delays accumulated through system navigation.

Quantitative analyses could measure the duration of delay and identify associations between diagnostic delay and patterns of provider encounters, including the number and types of providers consulted. However, they could not adequately explain how and why delays accumulated across care trajectories. Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings enabled examination of the dynamic navigation processes through which multiple provider encounters, referral discontinuities, and non-linear movement across providers contributed to prolonged diagnostic trajectories.

Study setting and participants

The public health system in Burkina Faso is organised into three main levels. Primary health care is provided through Centres de santé et de promotion sociale (CSPS), which constitute the first point of contact for most patients. In rural areas, community health workers (agents de santé communautaires, CHWs) and postes de santé may also serve as early points of contact and facilitate orientation and referral to CSPS. CSPS refer patients to district hospitals (Centres médicaux avec antenne chirurgicale, CMA) and regional hospitals (Centres hospitaliers régionaux, CHR) when more specialised care is required. Tertiary care is provided at university hospitals (Centres hospitaliers universitaires, CHU) at the national level, where specialised diagnostic services are available.

The public health system formally operates through a hierarchical referral structure using referral slips or letters to facilitate access to higher-level facilities. In our data, referral papers and referral letters were observed in several trajectories, particularly for transfers between public facilities and emergency transfers to tertiary POUs. However, care-seeking trajectories frequently extended beyond the formal referral hierarchy. Caregivers often described direct consultation at CSPS or private clinics without prior contact with CHWs or postes de santé, and movement between public, private, pharmacy-based, and non-biomedical providers was common during the diagnostic process.

For childhood cancer, two CHUs in the capital, Ouagadougou — the University Hospital of Yalgado Ouédraogo (CHU-YO) and the University Paediatric Hospital of Charles de Gaulle (CHUP-CDG) — are equipped with POUs and provide care for children with cancer nationwide.

Data were collected through hospital registries, medical records, interviewer-administered structured questionnaires, and semi-structured interviews with family members accompanying the child. The structured questionnaires and semi-structured interviews were conducted by the same research team.

Eligible participants were accompanying family members of children under 15 years of age with an initial episode of cancer who were admitted to the POU of CHU-YO or CHUP-CDG between December 2023 and January 2024. Of 52 eligible cases, 12 families declined participation, commonly citing distress related to the child’s condition, reluctance to engage during a period of suffering, and practical constraints. The remaining 40 caregivers consented to participate and completed an interviewer-administered structured questionnaire. During data cleaning, 10 cases were excluded (3 without confirmed diagnosis, 1 due to age criteria, and 6 follow-up cases), resulting in a final quantitative analytic sample of 30 cases.

From the questionnaire sample, 23 caregivers were purposively selected for in-depth interviews to ensure variation across children’s sex, age, diagnosis, socio-economic conditions, place of origin, displacement status, and caregiver relationship to the child. Of these, 20 corresponded to cases retained in the final quantitative dataset and were therefore included in the qualitative analyses and mixed-methods integration.

To ensure consistency in data collection, the first author provided training to the local research team on interview procedures and the use of the questionnaire and interview guide. Initial data collection was conducted jointly to align approaches across the research team, followed by ongoing supervision and regular review within the research team.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted by the first author, supported by a locally based anthropologist and trained research assistant, in French or local languages depending on participant preference. Interviews were audio-recorded with consent and later transcribed and translated into French when necessary.

To strengthen interpretation and contextualisation of findings, preliminary analyses were discussed within the local research team during data collection and later through workshops and follow-up discussions involving clinicians, patient representatives, policymakers, other health system stakeholders, and international partners. These discussions informed the refinement of interpretations and consideration of variation across care trajectories and policy implications.

Children generally entered tertiary hospitals through emergency services or speciality services before referral to the POU. Because dates of initial tertiary hospital entry (CHU entry dates) were not consistently retrievable across cases, delays occurring within tertiary hospitals prior to the first consultation at the POU may have been incorporated into the referral interval. In the sensitivity analyses, hospital delay was alternatively defined using CHU entry dates. Dates for symptom onset and first biomedical consultation were reconstructed through interviewer-administered questionnaires and semi-structured interviews using a trajectory-based approach. Caregivers were asked open-ended questions regarding the onset of illness and subsequent care trajectories (e.g., when symptoms were first noticed and how care-seeking unfolded from symptom onset to arrival at the current hospital), including consultations, referrals, hospital transfers, and use of non-biomedical care. Interviewers used successive healthcare provider encounters as temporal anchors to clarify chronology when exact dates were uncertain. Clinical records were prioritised when available, and caregiver-reported timeline information was used to supplement and cross-check missing or uncertain dates.

When discrepancies occurred, dates documented in medical records were prioritised for care episodes involving healthcare providers, whereas caregiver narratives were retained for symptom onset and early care-seeking events, which were not systematically recorded in clinical files. When exact dates were unavailable, approximate dates were assigned using standardised midpoint assumptions based on the best available information. Data completeness was high for all key dates and covariates used in the main analysis. In a separate sensitivity analysis, three observations were excluded because of missing information on CHU entry date.

Statistical analysis

Given the small sample size (n = 30) and the skewed distribution of delays, continuous outcomes were summarised using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Total diagnostic delay was log-transformed using log (1 + delay) to account for right skewness. Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate, and Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess associations with count variables. Minimal multivariable models were fitted to the log1p-transformed total delay to explore effect directions while limiting the number of covariates. All p-values were reported for reference only and were not given a causal interpretation.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of findings. First, hospital delay was alternatively defined using CHU entry dates, and the minimal regression model was re-estimated. Second, extreme referral-delay cases were identified using the conventional 1.5 × IQR rule and excluded to assess whether observed associations were driven by a small number of exceptionally long referral trajectories.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Main exposure variables

Analyses were conducted using two datasets: the full cohort (n = 30) and the interview subset (n = 20).

The primary exposures were (i) the first point of care (“entry-point provider”), operationalised as a three-level categorical variable (non-biomedical provider; primary care facility; higher-level biomedical facility), and (ii) the cumulative number of provider encounters before the first POU consultation, analysed as a continuous count and summarised using a three-level categorisation (1, 2–3, ≥ 4) for descriptive and exploratory comparisons. For visualisation in Fig. 2, provider encounters were displayed in five categories (1, 2, 3, 4, and ≥5). Provider encounters included both biomedical and non-biomedical providers (e.g., traditional healers and pharmacies), reflecting their importance in this setting. For the purpose of this categorisation, higher-level biomedical facilities included district hospitals, regional hospitals, a military hospital, non-oncology tertiary hospitals, and physician-led private biomedical facilities. The count excluded the specialised paediatric oncology unit itself, which represented the endpoint of the referral trajectory rather than an intermediate provider encounter.

Provider encounters were defined as distinct care-seeking episodes across biomedical and non-biomedical providers before diagnosis. Consecutive consultations related to the same episode of care with the same provider were counted as a single provider encounter. A return to a previously consulted provider after consultation elsewhere was counted as an additional provider encounter because it represented a distinct episode within the care pathway. Internal movements between services within CHU-YO or CHUP-CDG prior to consultation at the POU were not counted as separate provider encounters. This variable therefore reflected cumulative navigation complexity rather than the number of unique providers consulted. Capital residence was included as a covariate as a proxy for geographical proximity to specialised oncology services.

Private biomedical providers encompassed a heterogeneous range of facilities, from small clinics functioning similarly to primary public facilities to larger hospitals offering more advanced services. All private-sector facilities reported in the present dataset provided physician-led biomedical care and were therefore grouped within higher-level biomedical facilities in the quantitative analysis. In contrast, the qualitative trajectory analysis focused less on formal provider categories than on the dynamic roles providers played within care-seeking trajectories. Accordingly, the terms “private clinic” and “private hospital” were used descriptively to distinguish district-hospital-level providers from tertiary-level providers in the capital.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative findings were used to interpret quantitative patterns and elucidate mechanisms underlying the accumulation of provider encounters and cumulative diagnostic delay. For mixed-methods integration, analyses focused on the subset of 20 caregivers who participated in in-depth interviews.

Trajectory reconstruction was used to operationalise the navigation framework by examining movements across providers and referral pathways over time. This approach enabled identification of mechanisms through which multiple provider encounters, referral discontinuities, delayed escalation, and non-linear movement across providers contributed to prolonged diagnostic trajectories.

The analysis adopted an iterative, case-based approach focusing on care-seeking trajectories. Rather than organising findings solely around thematic barriers, interviews were reconstructed chronologically to trace pathways from symptom recognition to arrival at the POU.

Trajectories were analysed across four phases: (i) symptom recognition; (ii) first care decision; (iii) navigation across providers and referral pathways; and (iv) post-diagnosis care mobility. Within these phases, key milestones included the first provider encounter, the first POU consultation, and diagnosis confirmation. Prolonged and uncertain movement across multiple providers during the navigation phase was conceptualised as diagnostic wandering. Delay-generating events were identified, and cases were grouped according to dominant trajectory configurations. Patterns were then compared across shorter- and longer-delay trajectories and integrated with quantitative findings.

The questionnaire and interview guide were developed by the first author, refined through consultation with clinicians, health system stakeholders, and members of the local research team, and tested prior to data collection. Preliminary coding and trajectory reconstruction were conducted by the first author, with regular discussions within the research team and with co-authors to enhance interpretive consistency.

Data collection and preliminary analysis continued until no substantially new trajectory patterns or explanatory mechanisms were identified, at which point thematic saturation was considered to be reached. Given the focused analytical aim of examining care-seeking trajectories, the sample size was considered sufficient to capture variation across diagnostic pathways.

Integration strategy

The quantitative findings guided qualitative interpretation and analytic model development [37]. Integration focused on explaining quantitative patterns in the accumulation of provider encounters through different trajectory configurations, linking statistical associations to underlying mechanisms identified in the qualitative data (Fig. 1).

The cohort was evenly distributed by sex and cancer type. More than half of the children were under 5 years of age. Entry into the health system occurred through a range of providers, including non-biomedical providers (37%) and primary care facilities (43%). Indicators of structural and social vulnerability were common, including low parental educational attainment, limited French proficiency, household displacement, low electricity access, and fewer rooms in the household.

Distribution and structure of diagnostic delay

Among the 30 children who presented with an initial episode of cancer, the median total diagnostic delay was 103.5 days (IQR 58–196; range 6–947). The distribution was strongly right-skewed, with a mean of 161.2 days, reflecting a small number of extreme delays. The median prehospital delay (symptom onset to first biomedical consultation) was 9 days (IQR 0–91), whereas the median hospital delay from the first POU consultation to diagnosis was 7 days (IQR 3–19). Delays occurring within tertiary hospitals between initial admission (e.g., through emergency or other specialised services) and the first consultation at the POU were therefore incorporated within the referral interval. In contrast, the median referral delay between the first consultation at a biomedical facility and arrival at the POU was 33 days (IQR 9–97), indicating that delay accumulated predominantly during health system navigation rather than during initial care-seeking or the diagnostic confirmation stage.

A small number of cases accounted for exceptionally long delays, largely driven by prolonged referral intervals (e.g., referral delays exceeding 500 days in some cases), underscoring substantial heterogeneity in care pathways.

Type of entry-point provider and total diagnostic delay

Total diagnostic delay varied by entry-point provider. Children whose first contact was with non-biomedical providers (primarily traditional healers, but also pharmacy-based self-medication) had the longest median total delay, at 196 days (IQR 108–304; n = 11), followed by those whose first contact was with primary care facilities, at 117 days (IQR 65–135; n = 13). Children entering higher-level biomedical facilities experienced substantially shorter delays, at 39 days (IQR 36–54; n = 6) (Kruskal–Wallis χ² = 10.3, df = 2, p = 0.006). This pattern suggests a hierarchical gradient in delay according to the initial entry point into the health system.

Number of provider encounters and cumulative delay

Before diagnosis, 4 children (13%) consulted only one care provider, whereas 16 (53%) consulted 2–3 providers and 10 (33%) consulted ≥ 4 providers.

The number of provider encounters was positively associated with total diagnostic delay (Spearman’s ρ = 0.54, p = 0.002). When analysed categorically, the median total diagnostic delay increased in a dose‒dependent manner (Fig. 2).

Minimal linear regression model

In the minimal linear regression model including entry-point provider type and the number of provider encounters, the number of provider encounters remained positively associated with longer total diagnostic delay (Table 2). Each additional provider encounter was associated with a 24.3% increase in total diagnostic delay (95% CI 2.3%–51.1%; β = 0.22, p = 0.03). Estimates are presented as percentage changes derived from log1p-transformed coefficients. Compared with children who first presented to primary care facilities (reference category), those who initially sought non-biomedical care experienced a 38.1% longer delay (95% CI − 40.3% to 219.0%; p = 0.44), whereas those who entered higher-level biomedical facilities had a 50.1% shorter delay (95% CI − 81.6% to 35.0%; p = 0.16). Although the associations for entry-point categories were not statistically significant, the direction of the estimates was consistent with the hierarchical delay gradient. These findings suggest that part of the association between entry-point provider and diagnostic delay may operate through cumulative system navigation, although the estimates were imprecise because of the small sample size.

Qualitative findings: trajectory configurations and mechanisms of delay accumulation

Qualitative trajectory analysis explored the mechanisms underlying the quantitative association between provider encounters and prolonged diagnostic delay by identifying recurring patterns of fragmented navigation and delayed escalation across the health system. We identified two major trajectory configurations through which delays accumulated during health system navigation: “fragmented looping before escalation” and “system-induced bottlenecks” (Fig. 3).

Fragmented looping before escalation

In prolonged-delay cases, trajectories were characterised by repeated horizontal care-seeking across non-biomedical and primary care providers, as well as local private clinics, before escalation to higher levels of care.

Caregivers frequently interpreted initial symptoms as common childhood illnesses and engaged in self-medication, traditional medicine, or consultations at CSPS facilities and nearby private clinics. Referral to higher-level facilities was often delayed.

“We initially thought it was malaria and bought medicine at the pharmacy. When her condition did not improve, we took her to a private clinic and then to a health centre. But she still did not improve, and we began to think it was witchcraft until her condition further worsened.” (caregiver, child with haematological malignancy).

Local biomedical facilities often lacked diagnostic capacity, while opportunities for timely triage and referral were missed. Caregivers frequently took the initiative to seek alternative sources of care, often without achieving diagnostic resolution.

We visited five different places but did not get any solution.” (caregiver, child with solid tumour).

When I suggested taking him to a health centre, his father refused, and we often argued about it. When we brought him to a centre, the doctors were furious…by then his condition had worsened.” (caregiver, child with haematological malignancy).

Caregivers commonly described traditional healers as accessible, available, and attentive. Their interventions were often perceived as producing immediate and visible effects, even when such effects did not persist over time.

Escalation typically occurred only after marked deterioration, including visible tumour swelling, bleeding, or severe pain, leading to consultation at a CMA and subsequent referral to a CHR or CHU.

These factors did not necessarily generate prolonged delays at any single point in time. Rather, they often contributed to repeated movement between providers and delayed escalation, resulting in cumulative delay across the care pathway.

Fragmented looping trajectories before the first biomedical consultation did not always extend over long periods and therefore were not necessarily reflected in prolonged prehospital delays in the quantitative analysis. However, alternative care-seeking practices and intra-household decision-making dynamics often continued to shape navigation after entry into biomedical care, contributing to delayed or disrupted referrals before specialised oncology evaluation was achieved.

In several trajectories, fragmented navigation continued even after entry into biomedical care. Children were repeatedly managed within lower-level facilities, referred between providers of similar level, or moved non-linearly across levels of care before escalation. In some cases, caregivers returned from higher-level biomedical facilities to lower-level or non-biomedical providers when symptoms persisted without diagnostic resolution or when care was perceived as inaccessible or ineffective. These processes often prolonged the referral interval substantially beyond the initial biomedical consultation. Such looping trajectories contributed to the accumulation of provider encounters observed in the quantitative analysis. The longest-delay trajectories represented amplified forms of these looping processes, characterised by repeated consultations, delayed escalation, and prolonged referral intervals extending over many months.

In many cases, movement between providers did not simply reflect uncertainty or poor adherence to referral pathways. Caregivers often described actively seeking alternative providers when previous consultations failed to produce improvement, explanations, or referral progression. Such self-referral strategies frequently relied on extended family networks, particularly relatives who were familiar with navigating specialised services or who were able to facilitate access to providers in urban areas. These actions represented attempts to overcome perceived stagnation within existing care pathways rather than simple deviation from formal referral processes.

System-induced diagnostic bottlenecks

The second trajectory pattern involved early contact with higher-level facilities but prolonged diagnostic resolution because of bottlenecks within referral and diagnostic processes. Although some children reached higher-level biomedical facilities, including tertiary hospitals, relatively early, referral within the hospital, typically from the emergency unit to the POU, and subsequent diagnostic resolution were still substantially delayed. In several trajectories, prolonged waiting occurred before evaluation at the POU.

One caregiver reported:

“At the CHU, the nurse told me to make a doctor’s appointment at the POU. But the administrator said that there were too many patients and that I could only return in 2 months and 6 days. I went home with my child, but he soon became very sick. After 2 weeks, I brought him to a private hospital.” (caregiver, child with solid tumour).

In other cases, diagnostic procedures were outsourced without sufficient coordination:

“The doctor at the CHU told us to do a biopsy at a private clinic. The next day, my husband went to a recommended clinic, but they said that they could not do a biopsy on a child. He visited more than five private clinics before one finally agreed to do it, but we had to wait for another 5 weeks for our turn.” (caregiver, child with solid tumour).

In some trajectories, prolonged diagnostic uncertainty and repeated delays led caregivers to seek alternatives outside the biomedical system:

“Doctors could not find the cause of his liver dysfunction for months. They told us to do another medical test, but the technician was on leave and we could not proceed. The hospital told us to go home until the technician would come back. I visited several private clinics so that our son could have the test, but it was not available. Finally, we went home and restarted the traditional care. Then, the child got better.” (caregiver, child with solid tumour).

Movement back toward non-biomedical providers after biomedical consultation did not necessarily reflect rejection of hospital care. Rather, caregivers often described such decisions as pragmatic responses to prolonged diagnostic uncertainty, delayed appointments, lack of diagnostic resolution, or perceived stagnation within biomedical services. Some caregivers reported temporary symptomatic improvement after traditional treatment, while in other cases, traditional healers themselves encouraged families to seek hospital care.

These patterns help explain why prolonged navigation pathways occurred even among children who entered biomedical or tertiary-level care relatively early. In several trajectories, delays continued to accumulate after arrival at tertiary hospitals in the capital through diagnostic uncertainty, repeated consultations without diagnostic clarification despite ongoing contact with biomedical providers, prolonged waiting times for appointments, imaging and outsourced diagnostic procedures, followed by delayed referral from emergency units to POUs. These bottlenecks contributed to diagnostic delay without necessarily increasing the number of provider encounters recorded in the quantitative analysis, highlighting dimensions of navigation complexity that were only visible through qualitative trajectory reconstruction. In some of the longest-delay trajectories, these bottlenecks persisted over extended periods and interacted with repeated provider switching, further amplifying cumulative delay.

Short-delay trajectories: direct escalation and efficient referral

In contrast, shorter-delay cases were characterised by early recognition of severity and timely upward referral. In these cases, escalation to higher-level facilities occurred rapidly, limiting cumulative provider encounters and reducing opportunities for delay accumulation.

“We transported him to a private hospital nearby, and then he started to suffocate in the vehicle. Medical staff placed an oxygen mask (on him) and conducted medical tests rapidly. They said they did not have a specialist and called an ambulance to take him to the CHUP-CDG.” (caregiver, child with solid tumour).

Entry into private or specialised facilities sometimes facilitated direct transfer to tertiary care, reducing the need for intermediate navigation. Some children who presented with severe and visible symptoms were also referred directly from primary care facilities to a CHU in the capital.

Short-delay trajectories were often characterised not only by efficient referral processes but also by the availability of practical and social support. Healthcare professionals occasionally facilitated rapid referral through active coordination and practical support, including arranging ambulance transfers, mobilising professional networks, accompanying patients during referral, and, in some cases, providing financial assistance. Relatives and other social contacts also helped families navigate the health system through accompaniment, caregiving support, information sharing, and financial assistance. Sometimes, relatives familiar with specialised services or urban healthcare systems played a key role in accelerating access to tertiary care. Crowdfunding through social networking services also helped some families overcome financial barriers to referral and diagnostic procedures.

Together, these trajectories illustrate how timely escalation, effective referral coordination, and supportive social networks could interrupt the accumulation of delay observed in more fragmented pathways.

Cross-cutting structural and social conditions

Structural and social conditions shaped families’ ability to sustain navigation across both trajectory patterns. These included poverty, internal displacement, incidental livelihood loss, geographic distance from higher-level health facilities, gendered intra-household decision-making dynamics, and security-related mobility constraints.

One caregiver described financial exhaustion that interrupted care-seeking:

“My husband is a peasant. He lost most of his oxen to animal epidemics and had to sell the last one to keep us alive. At the regional hospital in Kaya, doctors told us to go to Ouagadougou in August, but we had nothing left to sell and could not go. Someone gave us 25,000 FCFA (about 42 USD), but it was finished immediately. We finally arrived in Ouagadougou in October.” (caregiver, child with solid tumour).

Peasants and herders who had been displaced from peripheral villages to towns often lost access to land, livestock, and other livelihood resources and were unable to secure sufficient alternative income for years. Despite state-supported free healthcare for children under five years of age and support from relatives, many families struggled to cover the costs of diagnostic tests, transportation, accommodation, and other expenses associated with prolonged care-seeking.

These structural and social conditions did not necessarily generate additional provider encounters directly. Rather, they influenced how families navigated referral pathways, responded to uncertainty, and sustained progression toward specialised care, thereby amplifying instability and discontinuity across multiple phases of the trajectory. In several cases, intra-household disagreement delayed referral follow-through and interrupted progression toward a definitive diagnosis. In some cases, language barriers compounded other forms of vulnerability. Caregivers with limited proficiency in French and major local languages sometimes reported difficulty understanding hospital instructions and navigating referral processes.

Family networks played an important role in navigation, as described in the subsection ‘Short-delay trajectories: direct escalation and efficient referral’. These networks could mitigate some of the barriers associated with poverty, displacement, and geographic distance, enabling more effective progression through the health system. However, they could also redirect trajectories toward alternative pathways when previous consultations were perceived as unsuccessful.

Integrated explanatory model of delay accumulation

Quantitative findings demonstrated that diagnostic delay accumulated primarily during health system navigation: referral delay was the longest component, and a greater number of provider encounters was associated with longer total diagnostic delay. Qualitative findings showed that this association reflected cumulative navigation processes, including movement across multiple providers and levels of care, delayed referral escalation, and repeated consultations before diagnostic resolution. Additional provider encounters frequently reflected continued navigation in the absence of definitive diagnostic anchoring (Table 3).

Integration of quantitative patterns and qualitative trajectory mechanisms

At the same time, qualitative analyses revealed additional forms of delay accumulation that were not fully captured by provider encounter counts. Delays also occurred within higher-level biomedical facilities through diagnostic uncertainty, prolonged waiting times, outsourced investigations, and repeated consultations before specialised oncology evaluation. Such delays often prolonged the navigation period within the same provider. These findings indicate that provider encounters captured important aspects of cumulative navigation but did not encompass all delay-generating processes.

Qualitative findings also provided context for the observed gradient according to the entry-point provider. Children who first entered care through non-biomedical providers or lower-level facilities often experienced “fragmented looping” before escalation, whereas early entry into higher-level biomedical facilities was more often associated with direct progression toward specialised oncology care.

Together, the integrated findings suggest that diagnostic delay accumulated through two dominant trajectory configurations: fragmented looping across pluralistic providers and system-induced bottlenecks within biomedical referral pathways (Fig. 4). Structural and social conditions shaped families’ progression through these pathways, influencing the continuity and pace of navigation toward specialised oncology care.

Discussion

Explaining cumulative diagnostic delay

This mixed-methods study examined diagnostic delay among children with cancer in Burkina Faso through the lens of health system navigation. Taken together, findings suggest that diagnostic delay is best understood as a cumulative process arising through navigation across a pluralistic, hierarchically stratified, and fragmented health system [21, 25, 26]. Total diagnostic delay was substantial, with most delay accumulating during the referral interval rather than during initial care-seeking or diagnostic confirmation. A greater number of provider encounters before reaching specialised oncology care was associated with longer total diagnostic delay. Two dominant processes of delay accumulation were identified: fragmented looping, characterised by repeated and horizontal care-seeking across lower-level providers before referral escalation, and system-induced diagnostic bottlenecks within higher-level biomedical facilities. Accumulated encounters reflected repeated attempts to obtain diagnostic clarification, referral progression, or symptom relief across multiple providers and levels of care. Provider encounters, therefore, functioned as an indicator of cumulative navigation complexity rather than merely reflecting repeated consultations.

The median total diagnostic delay observed in this study (103.5 days) was substantial yet comparable to estimates reported from Kenya (102 days) and Nigeria (109.9 days), longer than those in Senegal (90 days for hospitalisation), Tanzania (42.5 days for LAL patients) and South India (41 days) [13, 16, 17, 20, 38]. Variations in those studies may reflect differences in health system characteristics as well as in caregivers’ capacity and available resources that affect their navigation, notably socioeconomic conditions of families, geographic and security environment, health-seeking behaviour, accessibility and capacity of biomedical facilities and referral coordination [11, 12]. Studies from Egypt, Kenya, Tanzania and South Africa also reported substantial referral delays [16, 18, 39–41], suggesting that prolonged delays during health system navigation may be a common phenomenon in those settings.

Similar associations between multiple provider encounters and prolonged diagnostic delay have been documented by several childhood cancer studies from sub-Saharan Africa [16, 18, 20, 40]. In one study from Tanzania, most patients initially sought care at primary care facilities, where most delays seemed to have occurred [20]. In another study from northern Tanzania, patients visited a median of 2 facilities before arriving at a regional cancer centre, and the majority of visits to health centres and district hospitals did not yield a referral to higher-level facilities [18]. Makene et al. [40] described extensive “churning” through the health system, characterised by repeated movement between providers and self-directed transfers before diagnosis was established. Slow recognition of symptoms and delays in diagnostic testing contributed to such “churning”. Recent qualitative evidence from childhood cancer care in sub-Saharan Africa has likewise identified limited diagnostic and therapeutic capacity, shortages of trained personnel, and referral challenges as important barriers to timely diagnosis and treatment [11]. Our study extends this literature by suggesting that such provider accumulation may be more usefully understood as a marker of unresolved navigation across fragmented diagnostic pathways.

In contrast, children who entered the health system through higher-level biomedical facilities tended to experience shorter delays, whereas those who first consulted non-biomedical providers experienced longer delays, although estimates for entry-point categories were imprecise. Often, these biomedical facilities were equipped with qualified health personnel, the capacity to detect symptoms, and established referral/triage procedures, which facilitated timely referral escalation. Other studies confirm this observation. In the studies from South India and Tanzania [17, 20], which reported relatively short diagnostic intervals (41 days and 42.5 days), the first contact was usually a paediatrician. In the Tanzanian study, more than 45% of patients went directly to a secondary-level hospital, saving time for referral [20].

The first contact with non-biomedical providers was a predictor for longer diagnostic delays, even when patients/caregivers did not spend much time with them before moving to the first biomedical facility. This is because those caregivers often returned to traditional healers whenever they felt obstructed during the navigation. A similar case is reported by a study from Tanzania: caregivers sought alternative solutions with traditional healers when confronted by health system barriers [40]. Collectively, this phenomenon may be interpreted as an expression of caregivers' efforts to overcome health system-related challenges that prevent them from obtaining a diagnosis and accessing care.

Arriving at higher-level biomedical facilities did not always guarantee timely diagnosis, and sometimes even prolonged delays within those facilities. Qualitative findings showed a pattern of prolonged diagnostic intervals within higher-level biomedical facilities caused by various bottlenecks during the navigation process: diagnostic uncertainty, waiting times for appointments, and delays related to outsourced investigations led to caregivers’ new searches for alternative providers, which further extended their diagnostic delay. These findings highlight an important distinction between access to higher-level care and access to diagnostic resolution.

Studies from Egypt, Kenya, and South Africa similarly reported substantial delays within the health system after initial presentation, particularly when diagnostic pathways require movement across multiple services before specialised care is reached [16, 39, 42]. A study from Tanzania documented self-directed downward “churning” in the facility hierarchy among 40% of respondents [40]. In many LMICs, health resources for cancer care are limited and are often concentrated in urban areas. Limited diagnostic and therapeutic capacity, shortages of trained personnel, waiting times, and referral fragmentation are also recurrent barriers to timely childhood cancer diagnosis and treatment across sub-Saharan Africa [11, 12]. The concept of health system navigation can provide a framework to understand such a phenomenon in a coherent manner.

Our study found that caregivers heavily relied on non-biomedical providers, as well as on private providers. Although many studies focus on public biomedical facilities, such diverse sources of care may also need to be considered to understand health system navigation. A recent scoping review demonstrated that traditional healers remain actively involved across the cancer care continuum in many African settings, sometimes facilitating referral but also contributing to prolonged diagnostic pathways [43]. Our findings are consistent with a broader literature on African medical pluralism, which emphasises that movement between biomedical and non-biomedical therapeutic systems often forms part of ordinary care-seeking rather than representing a simple departure from biomedical care [44]. Rather than functioning solely as barriers to biomedical services, traditional healers formed part of broader therapeutic and diagnostic itineraries through which caregivers actively sought explanations, symptom relief, and access to more effective care.

Beyond provider encounters and referral processes, qualitative findings highlighted the importance of broader structural and social conditions in shaping diagnostic trajectories. Poverty, internal displacement, livelihood disruption, and intra-household decision-making dynamics influenced families’ ability to sustain progression through referral pathways. These factors did not independently generate additional provider encounters but often constrained families’ capacity to act on referral advice, complete investigations, or travel to specialised services in urban areas. Conversely, support from relatives, social networks, healthcare professionals, and public fundraising initiatives sometimes facilitated referral completion, access to specialised care, and timely escalation. Diagnostic trajectories therefore reflected not only characteristics of the health system itself but also unequal capacities of caregivers to navigate that system.

This interpretation is consistent with broader literature on access to care, which emphasises that successful engagement with health services depends not only on service availability but also on patients’ ability to seek, reach, and navigate care across different stages of the pathway [21, 25]. A recent review of childhood cancer diagnosis in LMICs likewise identified household income and social support as important influences on access to diagnosis and specialised care [11, 12]. Similar challenges have been reported for other chronic and complex conditions in LMICs, where sustained navigation across referral pathways depends on both health-system capacity and households' ability to mobilise socioeconomic resources [45, 46].

The mixed-methods design enabled us to examine how and why delays emerge and accumulate over the course of complex patient navigation processes. The concept of diagnostic delay as health system navigation provides a useful lens for understanding the ways disruptions in these processes contribute to delayed diagnosis and treatment over time. For successful management of non-communicable diseases in LMIC settings, continuity of care and timely movement across multiple providers and levels of the health system have become increasingly important. The perspective proposed by this article may therefore have broader relevance for other chronic and complex conditions in LMICs.

Several of these priorities align with ongoing efforts in Burkina Faso to strengthen childhood cancer referral systems, improve early recognition, and facilitate more rapid access to specialised oncology care.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, diagnostic delay was conceptualised as a dynamic and cumulative process unfolding through health system navigation across multiple providers, including non-biomedical sources of care and private facilities, enabling examination of delay accumulation mechanisms rather than isolated time points. Second, the explanatory mixed-methods design enabled quantitative associations to be interpreted through detailed trajectory narratives, providing insight into the mechanisms underlying observed patterns. Third, the analysis advances a structural health-system perspective that moves beyond binary patient-versus-system explanations by examining interactions between diagnostic capacity, referral processes, social vulnerability, and care-seeking practices. Fourth, to our knowledge, this is among the first studies in Burkina Faso to systematically examine diagnostic delay in childhood cancer using both quantitative and qualitative data, providing evidence from a setting where empirical research on childhood cancer diagnostic pathways remains limited.

This study also has several limitations. First, the sample was modest, participants were recruited through POUs, and the study was conducted in a single national context, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Second, the limited sample size precluded meaningful analyses by cancer type, and information on stage at diagnosis was not consistently available across tumour types and facilities. Some delays occurring within tertiary hospitals may also have been incorporated into the referral interval because dates of initial tertiary hospital entry were not consistently retrievable across cases. Third, quantitative analyses were exploratory and descriptive, and residual confounding from unmeasured clinical and contextual factors cannot be excluded. Recall bias may also have affected reports of symptom onset and early care-seeking events, although triangulation across interviews, questionnaires, and medical records likely reduced major inconsistencies in pathway reconstruction.

Despite these limitations, convergence between the quantitative and qualitative findings strengthens the plausibility of the proposed explanation that diagnostic delay accumulates through health system navigation.

Conclusions

Diagnostic delay among children with cancer in Burkina Faso emerged as a cumulative process of health system navigation across a pluralistic, hierarchically stratified, and fragmented healthcare system. Delays accumulated through fragmented looping across public, private, and non-biomedical providers, as well as through bottlenecks within higher-level biomedical facilities. Provider encounters functioned as an indicator of cumulative navigation complexity rather than merely reflecting the number of consultations. Structural and social vulnerabilities—including poverty, displacement, insecurity, and gendered decision-making—further shaped families’ capacity to progress through referral pathways and access specialised care.

Efforts to reduce diagnostic delay should prioritise strengthening referral coordination, patient navigation, and diagnostic capacity across multiple levels of the health system, while also addressing the structural and social constraints that influence access to care. Beyond childhood cancer, a health system navigation perspective may complement existing determinant-based approaches by explaining how barriers encountered at different stages become linked through patients’ movements across providers and levels of care. This perspective may therefore provide a useful framework for understanding delayed diagnosis in childhood cancer and other chronic and complex conditions in resource-constrained settings.
