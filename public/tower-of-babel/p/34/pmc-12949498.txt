Unveiling the heterogeneity of T2DM: a narrative review of clustering algorithms in stratifying comorbidity and complication risk
Authors: Namra Aziz, Neha Kamran, Aysha Almas, Gerald S Bloomfield, Asma Ahmed, Zainab Samad, Tariq Mahmood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes is a growing global health concern, affecting 537 million adults worldwide, significantly contributing to mortality and disability. Cluster analysis, a branch of Unsupervised machine learning (ML), has been increasingly used to identify distinct subgroups of patients with diabetes, enabling better disease stratification and personalized treatment strategies. Despite the growing burden of diabetes, clinical management often relies on generalized treatment approaches that may not account for patient heterogeneity. There is a need to better understand the underlying subtypes of diabetes to enable more personalized care. This narrative review evaluates the application of clustering in stratifying diabetes patients across diverse populations based on clinical, biomarker, and molecular characteristics and more. A literature search was conducted using PubMed, and Google Scholar following the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) guidelines. A total of 22 articles were finalized to be included in the analysis where different clustering approaches on diabetes patients have been used. This review further classifies the studies into diabetes subgroups focusing specifically on type 2 diabetes patients where Severe Autoimmune Diabetes (SAID), Severe Insulin Deficient Diabetes (SIDD), Mild Obesity-Related Diabetes (MOD), and Mild Age-Related Diabetes (MARD) clusters were most commonly found, and biomarker-based classification such as inflammatory and molecular including only type 2 diabetes patients, where additional two clusters: Severe Insulin Resistant Diabetes with Relative Insulin Insufficiency (SIRD-RII), and Mild Age-Related Diabetes with Relative Insulin Insufficiency (MARD-RII), were found. This review underscores the potential of clustering in diabetes research and identifies gaps in standardization across studies. Introduction

Diabetes is a chronic metabolic disorder of significant global health concern, affecting 537 million adults (20–79 years) worldwide. This means that every 1 in 10 individuals worldwide is living with diabetes, with a higher prevalence in Asian countries. The prevalence of diabetes is expected to increase to 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045 [1]. In Pakistan, the overall weighted prevalence reported in a 2017 national diabetes survey was 26.3% [2], and the age-standardised prevalence in the age group 29–79 is the highest in Pakistan as estimated by International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [3].

Diabetes is associated with major complications such as myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke and is linked to a significant impact on disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). The average life expectancy of patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM), decreases by about 6 years after the age of 50 [4]. In 2019, the global burden of DALYs due to T2DM was substantial, totaling 66.3 million [5]. The spectrum of chronic complications associated with diabetes is extensive including neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, foot ulcers, delayed wound healing, kidney dysfunction, amputation, organ dysfunction, susceptibility to infections, sepsis, skin disorders, hearing impairment, and cardiovascular disorders. Preventing and controlling diabetes through collaborative efforts and effective interventions is crucial. Early screening, increased awareness, and timely healthcare interventions are vital to reducing the global burden of diabetes [6].

South Asian populations, including Pakistanis, have a known genetic predisposition, a lower BMI threshold for risk, and unique metabolic profiles (e.g., lower adiponectin), suggesting the globally identified diabetes subtypes (SAID, SIDD, MARD, etc.) may present and cluster differently, or have different proportions, in the Pakistani population. Pakistan as a lower-middle-income country (LMIC), faces a significant health challenge with T2DM, ranking as the 3rd country globally in diabetes prevalence after China and India. The prevalence of diabetes in Pakistan is alarmingly high at 26.7%, with this number steadily increasing each year [1], and 31.4% in adults as per IDF 2025 atlas. Factors contributing to the high prevalence of diabetes in Pakistan include genetics, lifestyle changes, obesity, suboptimal physical activity, unhealthy food practices, low literacy rates, lack of awareness, and poverty.

In Pakistan, the main focus is on the treatment of glucose and keep it in control using old standards of care such as noting glucose levels at fasting, random, and 2-hours after meals, however, new standards of care need to be introduced to practice which would involve managing glucose levels through fat reduction, or improving renal outcomes, etc., and machine learning clustering can enable us to find latent patterns in data and identify distinct subgroups or phenotypes within population with diabetes. These subgroups often differ in their clinical characteristics, complication risks, and treatment responses. Recognizing these patterns is crucial for clinicians, as it allows for more personalized care, early risk stratification, and targeted interventions. For example, patients with severe insulin resistance may benefit from different therapeutic strategies than those with insulin deficiency. Without such stratification, the healthcare community risks applying generalized treatment protocols that may not be effective for all patients, leading to suboptimal outcomes.

In a country like Pakistan where 37.2% earn less than $3.65 per day [7] paying for diabetes management becomes challenging which amplifies the urgency for robust data driven solution for diabetes management aiding automated diagnosis, prediction, and transforming public health strategies [8–10]. Moreover, a large proportion of cases remain undiagnosed, and there are major challenges in healthcare access, particularly in rural areas. This means personalized, subtype-based care is needed to allocate scarce resources effectively and target interventions, and this can be achieved by leveraging machine learning systems specifically clustering analysis to identify theses population specific subtypes for Pakistani population.

Machine learning (ML) is a multidisciplinary field that utilizes artificial intelligence, probability and statistics, computational complexity theory, etc., to focus on how to get computers to program themselves (from experience plus some initial structure) [11]. In unsupervised ML, the algorithms are trained on unlabeled data to identify hidden patterns and relationships within the data without any explicit guidance or predefined labels, and cluster analysis then calculates heterogeneity between data points and assigns them to similar clusters based on similarity or distance metrics, etc. This branch of ML, cluster analysis, can be a valuable tool in addressing the increasing prevalence of diabetes in Pakistan by identifying those at increased risk and provide early detection, prediction, and management of the disease.

As clustering analysis continues to evolve, it can potentially reveal previously unknown insights that may not have been discernible to the human eye. The different standard methods of clustering include distance-based, density-based, grid-based, model-based and ensemble clustering [12]. Past studies in the healthcare sector have shown significant usage of ML to predict and analyze disease risk or treatment options in several conditions including diabetes. For example, a review discusses how ML can be applied to automate diabetes screening, detect complications, and develop predictive models for personalized care [13]. Besides predicting complication and mortality risk in diabetes, an important area is the identification of specific phenotypes within diabetes patients that may behave differently in terms of complication rates, disease progression and overall risk of death. Identifying such phenotypes might be useful for clinicians for delineating management plans and structuring decision-making conversations with patients. This review is motivated by the need to synthesize existing evidence on clustering applications in diabetes care and to highlight its potential in transforming clinical decision-making and public health strategies.

Clustering algorithm mostly used in papers on type 2 diabetes

Understanding the methodological differences between these approaches is essential to interpret the findings across studies. The following section briefly outlines the most commonly used clustering techniques in diabetes research, highlighting their relevance, strengths, and limitations. Below are the descriptions of the clustering algorithms that were used in the articles included in this review.

KMeans is one of the most widely used clustering algorithms in diabetes research due to its simplicity, scalability, and ease of interpretation. It has been effectively applied to identify subgroups based on metabolic profiles, treatment patterns, and complication risks, making it a practical choice for large-scale clinical datasets [14]. KMeans uses the concept of minimizing distances between data points and cluster centroids. It initially randomly selects a predefined number of data points as cluster centroids and assigns each data point to the cluster corresponding to the nearest centroid determined by a distance metric (Euclidean distance by default). Each iteration updates the cluster centroids calculated as mean of the cluster data points. The iterative assignment process continues until the cluster membership stabilizes or a convergence criterion is met.

Two-step clustering [15] is particularly useful in diabetes research for handling mixed data types; both categorical and continuous. Its ability to scale to large datasets and automatically determine the optimal number of clusters makes it suitable for clinical applications involving diverse patient characteristics. Statistical measures such as Bayesian Information criterion (BIC) or Akaike’s information criterion (AIC) determine the optimal number of clusters. As the first step, a single pass is made through the data to compress the raw input data into a manageable set of subclusters. The subclusters are formed using a distance measure based in likelihood. The second step uses hierarchical clustering to progressively merge the subclusters into larger clusters (agglomerative). Two-step clustering is known to be scalable and handle large datasets efficiently and is less sensitive to the order of variables than K-Means.

Hierarchical clustering is valuable in diabetes studies for its ability to reveal nested relationships among patient subgroups. It allows researchers to explore how closely related different phenotypes are and is often used when the number of clusters is not known in advance. Hierarchical clustering consists of agglomerative or divisive clustering techniques where data points are organized into nested hierarchy of clusters based on pairwise distances. In agglomerative approach, the data points are considered as individual clusters and are merged with the closest clusters based on a prespecified linkage criterion for e.g., single, complete, average, or Ward’s method. This iterative process continues until all observations are grouped into a single cluster.

Latent Class Analysis (LCA) is a probabilistic finite mixture model clustering technique that has been applied to uncover hidden subgroups within people with diabetes based on categorical variables. It is particularly useful for identifying phenotypes that may not be apparent through traditional clustering methods. LCA assumes that the associations in the observed categorical variables are explained by membership in an unobserved latent variable (class). It maximizes the likelihood of the observed data and assigns individuals to latent classes based on posterior probabilities. Each observation is classified into the class with the highest posterior probability. diabetix.

Motivation for research questions

Despite the growing use of machine learning in healthcare, the lack of standardized clustering approaches in diabetes research has limited our ability to uncover hidden disease patterns, stratify risk, and personalize treatment. Without these insights, clinicians often rely on generalized protocols that may not address the unique needs of diverse patient subgroups. This gap results in missed opportunities for early intervention, inefficient resource allocation, and suboptimal outcomes. The following research questions aim to explore how clustering can bridge this gap and enhance our understanding of disease heterogeneity in T2DM.

RQ1: How have clustering algorithms been applied in healthcare to understand patterns, complications, comorbidities, and biological heterogeneity in patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM)?

RQ2: To what extent does clustering improve our understanding of disease progression, symptom patterns, and comorbid conditions in T2DM patients?

RQ3: How is clustering used to uncover interactions between T2DM and other health conditions in multimorbid populations (e.g., cancer, cardiovascular disease, mental health disorders)?

Methodology

This review was structured according to the SANRA (Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles) guidelines to enhance methodological rigor and transparency [16]. For this narrative literature review, PubMed, and Google Scholar were searched from inception to 2025. The keyword search strategy was last run on 25th July 2025. The search terms included (“cluster analysis” OR “unsupervised machine learning” OR “clustering” OR “unsupervised ML”), (“type 2 diabetes” OR “T2 diabetes” OR T2DM), (“complication*”), and (“comorbid*” OR “multimorbid*”). To complement the PubMed search, a refined query using the ‘intitle’ operator was applied in Google Scholar to retrieve studies explicitly focused on clustering approaches in type 2 diabetes. This helped ensure relevance while maintaining breadth through manual screening. The google scholar search query included keywords such as “clustering”, (“type 2 diabetes” OR T2DM), comorbidity, and “complication”. Studies were included if they involved patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) and applied clustering techniques either from machine learning (such as KMeans, hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN, KModes) or statistical approaches (such as Latent Class Analysis or finite mixture models) to identify patterns, subgroups, comorbidity profiles, or complications in T2DM. Articles were required to demonstrate how clustering contributed to the understanding of disease progression, symptom trajectories, multimorbidity, or healthcare utilization. Only peer-reviewed studies conducted on human subjects and published in English were considered.

Moreover, exclusion criteria reflected excluding studies mentioned other types of diabetes or did not clearly isolate T2DM populations. Papers that employed only association analysis, regression models, or statistical correlations without implementing any clustering method were also excluded. Additionally, studies using clustering solely for data preprocessing, such as outlier detection or feature grouping, rather than for the purpose of subgroup or pattern discovery, were not included. Reviews, editorials, case reports, and conference abstracts lacking original data analysis were also excluded.

The articles were screened based on title, abstract, and full-text relevancy. PubMed retrieved 47 results out of which 18 were selected after title/abstract and full-text screening, while Google Scholar database retrieved 114 results out of which 5 highly relevant studies were selected based on snowballing articles retrieved using the search strategy. One duplicate was found bringing the total to 22 studies to be included in the review (Fig. 1). After screening and selecting the final 22 studies, a manual data extraction process was conducted to capture key variables including clustering method, number and nature of clusters, comorbidities, and outcomes. The extracted data were then thematically analyzed to identify recurring patterns and clinical implications. These themes were synthesized to structure the Results section and guide interpretation. This narrative review was exempt from the ethical review as no human participants were involved.

Early risk stratification in T2DM

Metabolic markers

The successful replication of at least 4 clusters from Ahlqvist Swedish clusters [39] (Severe Autoimmune Diabetes (SAID), Severe Insulin-Deficient Diabetes (SIDD), Severe Insulin-Resistant Diabetes (SIRD), Mild Obesity-Related Diabetes (MOD), and Mild Age-Related Diabetes (MARD) were found in Chinese and European populations [32]. They stratified newly diagnosed patients with T2DM (< 12 months) revealing distinct patterns at diagnosis such as metabolic and clinical characteristics. The comorbidity and complication risk varied significantly among subtypes with the highest risk of developing diabetic kidney disease, and dyslipidemia in SIRD cluster. The risk of CVD and hypertension in MOD and SIRD clusters was reduced to none in Chinese cohort after adjusting for modifiable risk factors such as BMI, HbA1c, smoking, and dyslipidemia, suggesting that management of these factors can reduce risk of CVD and hypertension [17]. However, the CVD risk remained in SIRD for German cohort even after adjustments [28]. The medication pattern also varied as metformin was mostly used in SIRD (with obesity) and MOD clusters, while had its lowest usage in SIDD cluster. SIRD cluster also had the highest use of SGLT-2i and GLP-1RA. The SIDD cluster patients showed the highest use of insulin and alpha-glucosidase inhibitors (AGI). The SIRD group had the lowest physical fitness in comparison to SAID, and MARD in German population. The overall physical activity levels among all diabetes subgroups were similar, explaining that physical fitness is not only dependent on physical activity levels, but also on metabolic abnormalities such as metabolic dysfunction. Elevated fibrosis – 4 (FIB-4) levels were found in Chinese population to be strongly associated with myocardial infarction (MI) in elderly adults (age ≥ 65 years), females with high BMI (≥ 23.9), individuals with comorbidities; hypertension, congenital heart disease (CHD), chronic heart failure, and arteriosclerosis, and also significant in patients without dyslipidemia, stroke, diabetic retinopathy, fatty liver disease, and other chronic liver diseases, suggesting that higher FIB-4 in these groups might more accurately reflect CVD risk [19].

Clustering beyond traditional methods

A study in USA on Black adults used agglomerative hierarchical clustering to generate 3 clusters which were differentiated by echocardiographic indices and cardiac biomarkers [26]. The “hypertrophic” cluster had the highest LV mass, altered LV structure-function, and elevated cardiac biomarkers (BNP and hs-cTnl), and a significantly increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) compared to the other two clusters indicating the significant prognostic value of subclinical myocardial infarction and elevated cardiac biomarkers in predicting future cardiovascular events. Moreover, it also highlights the potential of clustering in revealing underlying cardiac phenotypes that better predict cardiovascular prognosis than traditional clinical groupings based solely on these comorbidities.

Inflammatory markers and reproducibility of clusters

A study conducted on African American women used hierarchical clustering and found 6 inflammatory signatures from 16 plasma cytokines/chemokines that significantly correlate with obesity, T2DM, and hypertension status in these women [36]. BMI alone was insufficient to provide metabolic risk; inflammatory profiles (signatures) provide additional discriminatory power. Some metabolically healthy obese individuals shared inflammation patterns with lean healthy individuals. Moreover, medication effectiveness was observed in these patients as metformin significantly reduced plasma inflammatory cytokines. The second cluster having highest fraction of T2DM/hypertension, and unmedicated, had a relatively low inflammation score, suggesting an immune exhaustion rate in long-duration T2DM. The 4th cluster with obese individuals, no T2DM, and highest inflammation score revealed that individuals were at a higher risk of developing T2DM. The clusters were internally validated for robustness and reproducibility by testing it on subjects initially excluded from clustering and externally validated in two different cohorts of African American women.

Translational potential of polygenic risk scores

To assess the extent to which genetic risk for T2DM is shared between British Pakistani and Bangladeshi (BPB), and European populations, Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was conducted resulting in 5 clusters in two models: Clinical, and Integrated [25]. Replication of European-ascertained T2DM loci in the BPB was weak, with only 33% of European loci replicating in the BPB population and sharing causal variants. The Integrated Risk Tool (IRS) performed best in reclassification of individuals aged less than 40 years deemed at low risk by QDiabetes (an established clinical risk tool) alone. These individuals tended to be free from comorbidities. The PRS distribution differed significantly between clinically derived T2DM subgroups. Integrating PRS into clustering analysis delineated new subgroups including a Probable Severe Insulin Deficient (pSIDD); subgroup characterized by high PRS, young age of onset, low BMI, and low triglycerides, consistent with beta-cell dysfunction. The diabetes subgroups identified using IRS model were associated with differential rates of progression to complications, which were not apparent in the clusters found by the Clinical model. The use of QDiabetes for integrating with PRS makes the findings highly relevant for potential clinical application within the UK NHS context. Moreover, improved risk prediction in younger individuals (under 40 years) who are often slim and free from comorbidities addresses a critical gap in current practice.

Precision medicine

The following subthemes illustrate how clustering approaches contribute to precision medicine by identifying distinct patient profiles, biomarker patterns, and treatment responses. These insights enable more personalized care strategies, risk stratification, and targeted interventions for individuals with T2DM.

Inflammatory biomarkers

The inflammatory biomarkers in Croatia indicated high heterogeneity of T2DM patients, demonstrating that distinct patterns of inflammation, comorbidities, and clinical progression exist [30]. Chronic inflammation indicated by neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A), interleukin-37 (IL-37), c-reactive protein (CRP), and hemoglobin (Hb) proved to be a central driver in the development and progression of T2DM, its associated comorbidities, and end-organ damage [30]. Inflammatory markers show different phases of chronic inflammation and correlate with specific clinical context, for e.g., higher NLR/IL-17 A in new-onset obesity and hypertension in women, and high IL-37 in older males with long standing disease. Sex dimorphism revealed significant gender-dependent differences in T2DM progression and comorbidity patterns, particularly for women in peri- and post-menopausal phase. These clusters provide a practical framework for personalized risk stratification and management of T2DM patients in primary care by deconstructing patient complexity and guiding clinical reasoning.

Reproducibility of clusters

The combination of two-step and KMeans clustering replicated the 5 Ahlqvist et al., clusters namely: SAID, SIRD, MARD, SIDD, MOD on the same Swedish population using ORIGIN trial participants revealing the reproducibility of clustering methods and strengthening the external validity of the Ahlqvist model [29]). A large proportion (28%) of Latin American individuals were assigned to the SIDD group compared to Europeans (18%). The SIRD and SIDD subgroup had a higher incidence of renal outcomes compared to MARD with SIRD having chronic kidney disease stage 3 A: HR 1.49; stage 3B: HR 2.25; microalbuminuria: HR 1.56, and SIDD having microalbuminuria (HR 1.36). Diabetes subtypes differed in their glycaemic response to insulin glargine. All subgroups were tested against both standard care and the MARD cluster. SIDD showed a 13% decreased occurrence of hyperglycaemia, SAID showed a 19% decrease, and MOD showed a 9% decrease with glargine. However, no significant difference was found for SIRD with glargine on hyperglycaemia. This differential glycemic response to insulin glargine implies that certain subgroups might benefit from specific therapies, paving the way for personalized pharmacotherapy in diabetes. These reproducible clusters support precision medicine by enabling consistent identification of patient subtypes that respond differently to therapies.

Optimized CGM utilization

A heterogeneity analysis using hierarchical clustering on continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data conducted on 6533 T2DM individuals identified 4 distinct clusters of CGM features, highlighting the inherent heterogeneity and redundancy within the broad range of metrics extracted from CGM data [18]). These four clusters were based on magnitude (time-in-range, min and max glucose); frequency; and low blood sugar. The cluster features had heterogenic association with diabetic complications, and lab values (HDL, creatinine, C-peptide), showing stronger links than mean glucose. The features related to glycemic variability emerged as particularly informative, showing stronger statistical associations with diabetic complications and certain lab markers compared to mean glucose, suggesting that glycemic variability provides insights beyond average glucose levels. The strength and direction of associations between CGM features and clinical covariates can vary significantly depending on whether individuals are on insulin or sulfonylurea therapy, highlighting the need to account for treatment regimens. This advanced CGM analysis could contribute to a better understanding of disease heterogeneity in T2DM and has the potential to aid in risk stratification and guiding individualized treatment decisions.

Machine learning for phenomapping

In US and Canada, phenomapping strategies were used to identify subgroups of patients with T2DM having distinct clinical characteristics and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk trajectories [31]). Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), LCA, Finite Mixture Model (FMM), and PCA techniques were used. FMM clustering with 3 phenogroups was the optimal strategy to identify replicable subgroups of T2DM patients with distinct cardiometabolic and risk factor profiles. These phenogroups had distinct clinical characteristics and downstream risk of major CHD events, all-cause mortality, hypoglycaemic events, and medication non-adherence across both derivation and validation cohorts. Phenogroup 1 was the riskiest as it had the highest burden of comorbidities and diabetes complications, highest risk of major CHD events and all-cause mortality. This group also had the highest prevalence of traditional CVD risk factors and T2DM complications. Phenogroup 2 had the lowest burden of microvascular complications, and phenogroup 3 had the fewest comorbidities and lowest risk of mortality. This phenomapping strategy can inform the development of personalized treatment approaches for T2DM patients, guided by their baseline risk and clinical characteristics.

Treatment optimization

Disease trajectory analysis

KMeans has also shown to make 17 distinct clusters on Russian population with a good visual separation based on t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding) and a 0.34 silhouette coefficient [33]). These clusters revealed a heterogeneous disease course for T2DM patients using a transition-between-states model, i.e., Markov chains to create probabilistic schemes of transitions between different diabetic states, allowing for specific patient groups. Patterns of complications such as polyneuropathy, retinopathy, nephropathy, and diabetic foot were found to have varied frequencies across clusters. The markov analysis for specific clusters demonstrated the effectiveness of certain therapies (e.g., lipid-lowering drugs, hypoglycemics) in leading to metabolic compensation, even in severe cases.

T2DM profiles using medication intake patterns were created using natural language processing (NLP- text mining) of physician notes extracting longitudinal EMR data (medication records, and lab test results) in a Slovenian population [23]. Patients exhibited significant heterogeneity in their medication usage patterns and associated clinical profiles particularly evident in middle-age groups. A transition from healthier (fewer medications) to less healthier groups (more complications and medications) was found in patient flow analysis as patients with diabetes aged. The frequency and diversity of prescribed medications (ATC LV2 drugs) generally increase with age, reflecting growing complexity and concomitant complications. After antidiabetic medications, the next most common types of prescribed drugs were for the cardiovascular system (ATC class C), and for blood-forming organs (ATC class B). SGLT-2 inhibitors were noted for their dual benefit in diabetes and CVD health. Moreover, CKD in clusters was implied through consistent findings of lower eGFR values alongside initially high medication burden, suggesting worsening kidney function as a major complication. As kidney function declines, certain medications, particularly those contraindicated in renal impairment, may be discontinued, reflecting a shift in treatment strategy rather than reduced disease burden.

Treatment invariance/gap

To study drug treatment variation in T2DM patients in relation to their comorbid status, two-step clustering and non-linear canonical correlation analysis was carried out on Korean National Health Insurance Service - National Sample Cohort (NHIS-NSC) databases [35]). 7 comorbidity baseline clusters, 20 treatment baseline clusters, and 12 comorbidity follow-up clusters were found in terms of similarity among baseline comorbidity, drug treatment, and follow-up comorbidity clustering. A substantial proportion of T2DM patients had comorbidities at baseline (64.9% with ≥ 1, 22.6% with ≥ 2). This burden significantly increased over 4 years (84.1% with ≥ 1, 49.5% with ≥ 2). As the number of comorbidities increased, and when both diabetes-related and unrelated comorbidities were present, common drug treatment patterns became less identifiable or completely absent, suggesting a rise in treatment variance in more complex patient groups. Common treatment patterns, such as Metformin (MET) and Sulfonylurea (SU) as monotherapy or in combination (MET + SU), were found in patients with fewer comorbidities. Combinations like MET + DPP4i or SU + TZD were observed in patients with ≥ 2 diabetes-related comorbidities. Within 4 years, 15.8% of patients developed microvascular complications and 6.5% developed macrovascular complications. A direct, clear relationship between specific baseline comorbidity or drug treatment patterns and the occurrence of micro or macrovascular complications was not identified using clustering suggesting that all glucose-lowering drugs aim to control glucose, which is key to preventing complications. Moreover, this study highlights that clinical guidelines may not adequately cover treatment for complex comorbid patients, leading to observable treatment variations in practice.

Novel therapeutic targets

Metabolic pathways and protein interactions associated with “diabesotension” (overlapping triad of T2DM, hypertension, and obesity) were delineated and 10 clusters were found out of which 2 were selected based on ROC-AUC score [21]). 14 specific proteins (STX3, VAMP2, DLAT, PDHX, OGDH, and STAT5A) were identified as potential diabesotension related proteins. The pathways associated with tripartite interplay of T2DM, hypertension, and obesity included amino acid metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, SNARE-mediated vesicle transport, insulin and salivary secretion, and the glucagon and HIF-1 signaling pathways, thus identifying novel candidates for diabesotension biomarkers and therapeutic targets. The study elucidated how dysregulation in core metabolic processes and secretion mechanisms contributes to complex interplay of three conditions : SNARE deficiencies are linked to T2DM and obesity, Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex components are crucial in diabetes onset and progression, STAT5A’s role in adipocyte metabolism and its link to glucocorticoid signaling connects to obesity, insulin sensitivity, and hypertension, AIFM1 mutations are linked to pulmonary hypertension, and its role in mitochondrial function connects to obesity and diabetes susceptibility.

Impact of different clustering approaches

Soft clustering – overlapping clusters

To draw out a clinical picture of the underlying causes of long-standing T2DM with comorbidities and complications in Arab (UAE) population, the two-step clustering method was used to find out optimal number of clusters followed by the Kohonen Self Organizing Map (SOM) algorithm – a neural network used to map high-dimensional data to a lower-dimension, and 5 clusters were found out of which 4 matched that of Ahlqvist’s (SIRD, SIDD, MARC, MOD), and Mild Early Onset Diabetes (MEOD) a newly found cluster in this population in mostly lean/overweight patients [22]. This cluster described individuals with early disease development in life (mean age 36.5 years), characterized by insulin secretory dysfunction and mild insulin resistance with mild abnormal glucose homeostasis. This group also had retinopathy, peripheral neuropathy, and ischemic heart disease. This subtype is mostly seen in no-white/non-Caucasian populations. Although 4 clusters were replicated from Ahlqvist, there was significant overlap (57%) between these clusters (MARD, MOD, MEOD). Only SIRD showed distinctiveness.

Kernelized autoencoder and classical clustering

A longitudinal analysis of electronic health records (EHR) was conducted via deep learning which mapped 5 years of multivariate time series (MTS) data into a latent space embedding similarities and differences between patients based on disease evolution [24]). The encoder stacked LSTM layers to map MTS input into the latent space and then decoder stacked another LSTM layer to map latent space vectors back to original MTS for reconstruction. Seven distinct longitudinal clusters were obtained for men and women with a bijective relation between the sexes namely: neuropathic cluster; hypercholesteremic cluster multiple complication cluster, vascular disease cluster, hypertensive cluster, retinopathy cluster, and metabolic cluster. BMI decreased over time in all clusters except for in multiple complications cluster, and retinopathy cluster, where it increased. HbA1c showed an upward trend in all clusters except for multiple complications cluster where stable levels were shown, and metabolic cluster where it depicted a downward trend. Each cluster represented a unique phenotype trajectory with distinct mean ages, diabetes duration, glycemic control, obesity status, lipid profiles, renal function, drug usage patterns and comorbidity prevalence.

Spatiotemporal clustering and T2DM mortality

A study done in China identifies high-risk spatiotemporal clusters of T2DM mortality and explored associations between economic factors, air pollutants, and T2DM mortality risk [20]). The clustering was done using discrete Poisson space-time scan statistic and 6 spatiotemporal high-risk clusters were identified. T2DM mortality was highest in areas where economy was progressing from lower-middle to upper-middle income levels, suggesting association between economic development and increased mortality. Pilot assessments indicated a significant association between carbon monoxide (CO) and T2DM mortality. Moreover, higher mortality rates were observed in people with lower educational attainment and specific occupations (farmers, retired, unemployed). The proportion of death occurring at home (65.23%) in high-risk areas, despite economic development suggests that the cost of T2DM management and access to timely medical care for complications remain significant barriers leading to higher mortality rates.

Discussion

This review aimed to explore how clustering algorithms have been applied to identify subtypes of T2DM with a focus on patterns, comorbidities, and disease progression. Across studies, KMeans clustering emerged as the most common method, yet reproducibility of identified subtypes varied depending on population heterogeneity, underscoring the need for broader validation [17, 19, 27, 28, 30, 33].

Overall clustering proved to be instrumental in uncovering hidden subgroups within T2DM populations, offering insights into disease progression, symptom trajectories, and comorbidity patterns. Longitudinal approaches such as autoencoders and Markov chains revealed how clinical parameters like BMI and HbA1c evolve across phenotypes, while CGM-based clustering highlights the prognostic value of glycemic variability over average glucose levels. These temporal and dynamic analyses support more nuanced risk stratification and personalized care. In multimorbid populations, clustering has uncovered complex interactions between T2DM and other conditions such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, hypertension, and mental health disorders. Building on earlier findings, phenomapping, graph-based clustering, and inflammatory biomarker profiling have demonstrated how overlapping disease mechanisms and comorbidity burdens influence treatment responses and outcomes. These findings support the use of clustering as a powerful tool to uncover hidden phenotypes and guide personalized care in diverse patient populations.

Diabetes is not a single disease; it comprises distinct subgroups with different underlying pathophysiology [39]. Clustering has revealed distinct cardiovascular risk profiles among T2DM subgroups, particularly in SIRD and SAID clusters. Demonstrating specific risks such as CVD, low physical fitness is apparent very early in the disease course allowing for early risk stratification [17, 28, 32]. The integration of polygenic risk scores (PRS) and imaging biomarkers enhanced prediction, especially in younger individuals. These insights, along with phenomapping approaches, support the development of personalized cardiovascular therapies such as SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists. Individuals with subclinical myocardial infarction dysfunctions who are at a higher cardiovascular risk could also benefit from the potential utility of cluster analysis, such as subclinical CVD detection early on in diabetes based on imaging markers [40]. These studies are crucial for understanding disease patterns and heterogeneity in specific racial or ethnic groups, especially when disparities exist [41]. Moreover, biomarkers can be examined for their utility as a prognostic tool and for the development of new drug targets to address obesity-driven inflammation [42].

The stratification of multimorbidity and inflammatory patterns with T2DM enables healthcare systems to factor in their impact on health care costs, resource utilization, and patient burden by identifying hidden phenotypes related to disease onset, duration, and comorbidity development linked to inflammatory and multimorbidity patterns [43], and indirectly uncover interactions between these profiles and T2DM and hence, support the implementation of precision medicine, for e.g., T2DM with obesity would require aggressive management, while T2DM with cardiovascular profile might call for an intensive multidisciplinary care beyond glycemic control [30]. The grouping of patients into clusters with similar disease courses can help optimize treatment processes within endocrinology units, leading to more tailored counselling and medication prescriptions. The finding of differential glycemic response to insulin glargine subtypes suggests potential for optimizing glucose-lowering treatment strategies based on subtype assignment [29, 44].

Treatment and drug usage data is widely available in healthcare facilities worldwide. Insights into medication usage patterns across age groups can help optimize treatment plans. Higher use of medication implies higher healthcare resource utilization, hence, identifying profiles with different needs can inform resource allocation [33]. The treatment invariance reveals an increasing ambiguity in understanding treatment patterns as comorbidity complexity increases, indicating areas where current guidelines may be insufficient or where treatment decisions are highly individualized, potentially leading to varied care quality [35, 45]. There is, thus, a need for developing specific, evidence-based guidelines for T2DM patients with complex comorbidities, rather than “one-size-fits-all” approach. The application of bioinformatics tools (graph clustering, PPI networks, pathway enrichment) to understand complex diseases can propose new molecular targets for drug development and intervention strategies [21]). Moreover, the molecular pathways (e.g., SNARE-mediated vesicle transport, pyruvate dehydrogenase components, STAT5A) present potential targets for small-molecule modulators or other specific inhibitors to restore metabolic balance and mitigate the impact of insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction.

Moreover, the question of what exactly causes diabetes remains unsolved. In Pakistan, major focus is on macro complications, and they are broadly understood, however, more emphasis needs to be placed on micro-vascular dysfunction as well. ML clustering could help in identifying population-specific endophenotypes and precisely link patient characteristics to associated poor outcomes. For example, Non-Arteritic Anterior Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (NAION) which is a specific, severe example of micro-vascular dysfunction (neuropathy), is likely under-reported or poorly managed in Pakistan. The novelty suggests that clustering research could identify the specific subtype that is highly susceptible to this rare but devastating complication, enabling preventative screening.

SGLT-2 inhibitors are globally known for their renal and cardioprotective effects than just simple glucose lowering. In Pakistan, where kidney disease is a major complication, this class of drug represents a novel paradigm shift in diabetes management that is not yet fully integrated or accessible to all patients. Identifying subtypes such as SIRD-RII (including patients with specific renal-cardio risk) could benefit from this drug type.

Ozempic (Semaglutide) and other GLP-1 RAs are novel for promoting fat reduction, overall weight loss and better managed glycemic control. This drug class represents a potential tool in aiding the management of the MOD cluster in Pakistan where there are high prevalence of obesity and its strong association with diabetes.

The application of clustering algorithms holds promise for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) especially Pakistan, where the burden of type 2 diabetes is highest for adults [3] and healthcare resources are limited. By identifying distinct patient subgroups based on clinical and biomarker profiles, clustering can support more efficient resource allocation, early risk stratification, and personalized treatment strategies ensuring cost-effectiveness for the government as well as the public. There is also a need for validating clusters in Pakistan to ensure their relevance and effectiveness in LMIC contexts. Incorporating region-specific data and comorbidity patterns could enhance the utility of clustering in guiding public health interventions and clinical decision-making in Pakistan.

The review is novel and highly relevant to global health, specifically through the lens of Pakistan, which faces the world’s highest comparative diabetes prevalence. The novelty lies not in the mere existence of clustering or the newest drugs, but in their critical need and poorly understood implementation within this resource-constrained, yet genetically and culturally distinct, high-burden population.

Limitations and future directions

While clustering can provide valuable insight into T2DM management, there are limitations associated with it too that cannot be overlooked. First, cluster drift, which is the tendency of patient assignments to vary across clusters over time, raising concerns regarding the stability of subtypes and their long-term use in clinical decision making. To address this, the clustering methods could be paired with advanced temporal models such as recurrent neural networks or memory-based architectures, to track patients who could transition over time.

Secondly, many studies lacked detailed data on microvascular complications, limiting the ability to fully characterize risk across all major diabetes outcomes. Moreover, most analyses were conducted in homogenous cohorts, restricting generalizability to more diverse populations. A need for validation studies across multi-ethnic datasets exists to assess the reproducibility of clusters and validate findings which could then support precision medicine at a larger scale. From a methodological perspective, the challenge of extracting information from unstructured data remains significant. The use of NLP to extract these rich insights has become vital as many of the datasets are not part of tabular data but they can add much more value to the analyses and understanding of T2DM.

Looking forward, innovative approaches provide new opportunities. The use of soft clustering methods such as Kohonen self-organizing maps highlights the inherent overlap and continuous nature of T2DM characteristics, challenging the rigid assumptions of hard clustering and opening avenues for better treatment options.
