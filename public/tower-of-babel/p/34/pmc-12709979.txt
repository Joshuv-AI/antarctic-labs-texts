Targeted drug conjugates in cancer therapy: Challenges and opportunities
Authors: Geng Jia, Yuqi Jiang, Xiaoyang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Traditional chemotherapy is often accompanied by off-target toxicity, resulting in adverse side effects and driving the development of targeted therapies. Targeted drug conjugates (TDCs) typically comprise targeting ligands, such as specific antibodies, peptides, or small molecules, attached to a cytotoxic agent via a chemical linker. In this study, we briefly discussed the molecular aspects of the key components of TDCs and the mechanisms by which these key factors exert their activity. Moreover, we reviewed FDA-approved TDCs and promising candidates in clinical trials and discussed current challenges and future directions for TDC development, providing insights for the research and development of novel cancer therapeutics using TDCs. TDCs combine the advantages of highly specific targeting and a potent killing effect, enabling accurate and efficient cancer cell elimination. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have shown good efficacy in treating various cancers; however, they still present limitations such as immunogenicity, hematotoxicity, and complex pharmacokinetics. Smaller peptide-drug conjugates (PDCs) and small molecule-drug conjugates (SMDCs) may combine the advantages of ADCs while overcoming some of their limitations, thereby presenting more efficacious and safer alternatives. TDCs enhance the therapeutic effects of cytotoxic agents and reduce their adverse effects. However, tumor heterogeneity, limited transmembrane permeability, and drug resistance pose significant challenges for TDCs, potentially affecting their therapeutic efficacy. Nevertheless, TDCs are a promising therapeutic approach for cancer treatment, achieving precise drug delivery while minimizing toxicity and side effects on normal cells.

Introduction

Traditional cancer chemotherapy involves small-molecule cytotoxic agents that achieve therapeutic benefits by inhibiting the rapid proliferation of cancer cells or inducing apoptosis of tumor cells. Combinations of chemotherapeutic agents are gradually changing the paradigm of chemotherapy. Nevertheless, the therapeutic effects of chemotherapy may not always be apparent, particularly in metastatic solid tumors. Such lack of response can result in drug resistance, resulting in a high mortality rate in patients with metastatic solid tumors [1]. The primary limitation of traditional small-molecule chemotherapeutic drugs is their lack of target specificity, inevitably resulting in off-target side effects [2]. Chemotherapeutic drugs can accumulate in certain healthy tissues and fail to reach pathological sites to exert their effects [3]. Targeted drug conjugates (TDCs) are a promising approach in cancer therapy and can achieve precise killing of cancer cells. TDCs are developed through conjugating cytotoxic agents with highly specific targeting molecules, enhancing the specificity of cancer treatment and achieving lower systemic toxicity than traditional chemotherapy [[4], [5], [6]]. This enhanced specificity and reduced toxicity allow for a broader therapeutic window, potentially increasing the safety and efficacy of cancer treatment.

Antibodies, as natural immune molecules, have high specificity and affinity, which allows targeting of specific disease markers. Antibody-based drugs have become increasingly prevalent in the pharmaceutical field with advancements in monoclonal antibody (mAb) technology [4]. Antibodies are viable delivery vehicles for cytotoxic agents owing to their tumor-targeting ability, which allows cytotoxic drug accumulation at tumor sites, reduces off-target side effects, and improves the cancer therapeutic index. Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have emerged as a new modality in cancer therapy, serving to bridge the gap between monoclonal antibodies (mAb) and cytotoxic drugs and provide patients with novel therapeutic options [7]. Paul Ehrlich introduced the concept of “magic bullets” in the early 20th century, a vision for a targeted drug delivery system that could selectively target and destroy harmful cells without harming the rest of the body. This innovative idea laid the groundwork for the development of targeted therapies, including ADCs, which reached a significant milestone when Pfizer approved the first ADC, Mylotarg, in 2000 for the treatment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [8]. Since then, technological iterations have broadened the scope and effectiveness of ADCs, expanding the range of targets from CD33 to include human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP-2), and Nectin-4, among others [9]. Approximately half of Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved ADCs have been approved within the last five years [9]. The clinical success of ADCs is evident in their enhanced therapeutic window and significant therapeutic effects; nevertheless, challenges including drug resistance, intricate pharmacokinetic profiles, and production costs persist.

Other delivery systems can theoretically replace antibodies considering the design concept of ADCs, effectively circumventing limitations such as poor permeability and immunogenicity associated with antibodies. For example, tumor-targeting peptides and small-molecule ligands specific to tumor-associated antigens may serve as alternatives to antibodies. This has resulted in the development of peptide-drug conjugates (PDCs) and small molecule-drug conjugates (SMDCs), which have become increasingly popular amid the rapid advancement of medical technology [10,11]. This conjugation trend typically follows a trinary structure targeting ligand-linker-effector molecules. PDCs and SMDCs may be potential next-generation therapies. Their smaller molecular size may contribute to enhanced penetration into tumor tissues, simplify synthesis processes, and potentially reduce adverse effects compared to those of ADCs [5]. Furthermore, the reduced dimensions of PDCs and SMDCs frequently present more predictable pharmacokinetic profiles than the intricate mechanisms of ADCs [12]. ADCs remain clinically successful; nevertheless, PDCs and SMDCs may emerge as important adjuvants or alternatives for targeted therapy in the future.

ADCs, PDCs, and SMDCs involve different target ligands and conjugation technologies in the field of targeted therapies, and their research and development reflect technological advances and innovations in targeted drug design and delivery. ADCs utilize the advantage of the high targeting ability of antibodies to achieve precise killing of cancer cells. Several ADC drugs have received FDA approval for cancer therapy, highlighting their precise targeting capabilities and potent cytotoxic effects for clinical applications [9]. PDCs combine the specificity of peptides with cytotoxicity, lower immunogenicity, and better pharmacokinetic properties than those of ADCs. PDCs’ reduced molecular size facilitates more efficient penetration into tumor tissues compared to ADCs, and their diminished immunogenicity mitigates potential adverse effects in patients [13]. In addition, SMDCs employ small molecules as targeting ligands and are characterized by simple molecular structures, relatively easy synthesis, and cost-effectiveness. Their small molecular size enhances tissue penetration and improves drug distribution, contributing to their therapeutic efficacy [14].

Several intrinsic limitations in TDC development necessitate innovative solutions, including potential immunogenic responses that may hinder therapy tolerability and increase costs due to complex production processes. Moreover, the evolution of drug resistance in tumor cells poses a significant threat to the long-term efficacy of TDCs, whereas pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic complexities present analytical challenges. Clinical trial designs should be critically refined to accurately assess the safety and efficacy profiles of TDCs. Drug design optimization aims to enhance the penetration and targeting of tumor tissues, potentially improving therapeutic outcomes. Technological advancements, particularly in nanotechnology, have refined TDC pharmacokinetics, potentially reducing production costs and increasing therapeutic indices. A comprehensive overview of TDCs may offer insight into their therapeutic efficacy and limitations, thereby guiding future research and clinical practice. In this study, we explored the structural components of TDCs, including the selection of targeting ligands, the design of linkers, and commonly used payloads. Next, we provided an overview of clinical advancements in this field. Finally, we discussed the significant challenges encountered with these drugs and proposed future research directions that may offer valuable insights into the development of innovative precision cancer therapies.

Antibody-drug conjugates

ADCs are an emerging class of potent therapeutics that integrate chemotherapy and immunotherapy. ADCs constitute mAbs covalently attached to cytotoxic agents using synthetic linkers. These conjugates combine the potent antitumor efficacy of small cytotoxic molecules with the selectivity, stability, and pharmacokinetic properties of mAbs. ADCs offer a novel approach to targeted cancer therapy and are anticipated to become alternatives to traditional chemotherapeutic agents in the future.

Mechanism of action of ADCs

ADCs are intravenously administered to prevent mAb degradation by gastric acids or proteases [15]. The general mechanism of action of internalized ADCs can be summarized in six steps (Fig. 1): (1) binding of ADCs to target antigens on tumor cells, (2) internalization of ADC-antigen complexes into tumor cells, (3) intracellular transport and degradation of ADCs, (4) release of free payload and action, (5) destruction of microtubule proteins or DNA, and (6) induction of apoptosis in tumor cells. The specific expression of target antigens on the tumor cell surface is a prerequisite for ADC binding and efficacy [16]. The expression of an ideal target antigen in tumor cells should exceed three times that in normal cells [17]. The ADC-antigen complex enters the cell via one of the following internalization pathways, resulting in the formation of early endosomes: (1) grid protein-mediated endocytosis, (2) fossa-mediated endocytosis, and (3) non-receptor-mediated endocytosis [18,19].Fig. 1Traditional mechanism of action of ADCs. ADCs are generally designed to be internalized by cancer cells and subsequently processed through the endocytic pathway, resulting in the release of cytotoxic payloads and exerting their therapeutic effect. Nonetheless, not all cell surface antigens initiate endocytosis, and the rate of internalization can significantly vary [20]. Internalization causes the cell membrane to invaginate, resulting in an influx of hydrogen ions that acidify the endosome. This acidic environment promotes interactions between the Fc fragment of mAbs and the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) [21]. Following binding to FcRns, certain ADCs are typically excluded from cells and re-enter the circulation or peritumor environment under physiological conditions (pH ​= ​7.4) [22]. Endothelial cells express a significant quantity of FcRns [23]; therefore, this circulation mechanism serves to effectively remove ADCs from cells to prolong their half-life [24] while also preventing injury to normal endothelial cells [25]. ADC-containing vesicles typically progress to the late endosome stage [26], where they fuse with lysosomes, resulting in acidification. This acidic environment and lysosomal proteases, such as cathepsin B and plasmin, result in the degradation of ADC linkers and mAbs, thereby releasing free cytotoxic payloads into the cytoplasm. These payloads disrupt cellular mechanisms either by binding to DNA or microtubule proteins, ultimately inducing cell death [21,[27], [28], [29], [30]].

Non-internalized ADCs also deliver their payload to targeted tumor tissue. Cytotoxic payloads are released by proteolytic enzymes or reducing factors in the extracellular tumor environment and diffuse into tumor cells to exert their cytotoxic effects. Furthermore, proteases or reductants released from apoptotic tumor cells can facilitate payload release [31]. Payloads from non-internalized ADCs can also diffuse into neighboring tumor cells, even those that do not express the target antigen, resulting in a bystander effect [32].

Monoclonal antibodies in ADCs

The optimal target antigen should exhibit differential expression on normal and tumor cell surfaces, with an abundance in tumor cells (>105/cell) [33]. Antigens should possess a satisfactory internalization pathway and efficiency [24,34], although some ADCs may function effectively without internalization [31]. The target antigen should exhibit sufficient affinity to prevent off-target ADC internalization and ensure the efficient aggregation and sustained residence of ADCs at the tumor site [35,36]. Furthermore, the target antigen on tumor cells must be sufficiently expressed to allow ADCs to deliver a cytotoxic payload at a lethal concentration [34]. Among the targets of ADCs approved by the FDA, five are indicated for hematological cancers, including CD22, CD30, CD33, CD79b, and BCMA, whereas four are indicated for solid tumors, namely HER2, nectin-4, Trop-2, and EGFR [9]. More than 50 targets have been implicated in ADC development, with rapid advancements in ADCs targeting CD19, Mesothelin, prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), EGFR, Nectin-4, CD56, CD138, and CD74.

mAbs that act as carriers for cytotoxic payloads and form the core of ADCs must exhibit high targeting selectivity and affinity, allowing precise binding to tumor cell surface antigens [21]. Additionally, these mAbs should exhibit low cross-reactivity with normal cells and possess a long plasma half-life to minimize off-target effects and maximize therapeutic efficacy, respectively. The Fc fragment of mAbs should have suitable attachment sites for cytotoxic payloads, ensuring no interference with antigen binding [37,38].

Current mAbs used in ADC drugs are predominantly immunoglobulin G (IgG) molecules, which offer numerous advantages, including high affinity for target antigens, long circulation half-life, abundant natural coupling sites, and modifiable active sites. Therefore, IgG molecules are highly favorable for ADC development [[39], [40], [41]]. IgG molecules are typically classified into four categories: IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4. IgG1, commonly used in ADCs, is the most abundant IgG subtype in serum and can induce robust effector functions, such as antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody-dependent cell-mediated phagocytosis (ADCP), and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), owing to its high binding affinity for Fc receptors. IgG2 provides additional coupling sites owing to its four disulfide linkages [42]; however, it is rarely used owing to its unique isomeric disulfide structure and complex hinge regions. IgG2 also tends to form dimers and aggregates, reducing ADC concentration [43]. Similar to IgG1, IgG3 exhibits robust secondary immune responses, including ADCC and CDC. However, IgG3 is limited by its relatively short circulating half-life and rapid clearance rate, affecting its therapeutic utility [44]. Human IgG4 induces ADCP [45]; however, a propensity for Fab fragment exchange has been observed in IgG4, which may lead to the formation of new hybrid IgG4 molecules [46].

Previous studies have demonstrated that the Fc region of ADCs interacts with immune cells, inducing ADCC, ADCP, and CDC and enhancing antitumor immunity [47] Emerging evidence indicates that certain ADCs can induce immunogenic cell death in cancer cells, causing the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate innate and adaptive tumor-specific immune responses [48,49]. ADCs bearing pyrrolobenzodiazepine (PBD) and tubulysin payloads elicited immunogenic cell death and exhibited greater antitumor activity in immunocompetent mice than in immunodeficient mice [49]. These findings highlight the contribution of the immune system to ADC activity. ADC payloads, such as microtubule-destabilizing agents, directly influence dendritic cell maturation and activation, thereby stimulating T-cell priming and expansion [50]. ADCs employing such payloads may contribute to more potent and specific anti-tumor immunity. This immune response activation contributes to the long-term effectiveness of cancer treatment by directly killing cancer cells and stimulating a memory response.

Moreover, mAbs should demonstrate low immunogenicity. First-generation ADCs mostly employ mouse-derived mAbs, which have been observed to trigger serious immunogenicity-related side effects in humans [4]. Mouse-derived antibodies were subsequently replaced with chimeric antibodies with the emergence of recombinant technology. However, anti-chimeric antibody responses were observed in humans, reducing the therapeutic efficacy of ADCs [51]. Currently, most mAbs are humanized antibodies that include only the complementary determining regions (CDRs) of the mouse variable region [51]. mAbs can exert direct antitumor effects by inhibiting the signaling pathways used in promoting tumor progression. Furthermore, unmodified mAbs, or ‘naked’ mAbs, have been effectively used as single-agent therapies, as demonstrated by trastuzumab for HER2-positive tumors and cetuximab for colorectal cancer [52,53].

Bispecific antibodies equipped with two antigen-binding sites can enhance binding specificity and therapeutic efficacy. Certain antigens, such as prolactin receptor (PRLR), may not be highly expressed on the surface of breast cancer cells but rather demonstrate a sustained low expression level. ADCs designed with bispecific antibodies targeting both HER2 and PRLR can enhance the binding capacity of antibodies to antigens and effectively eradicate breast cancer cells compared to those targeting a single antigen [54]. However, research on ADCs that employ bispecific antibodies remains limited, and many candidates are still in the preclinical phase [55].

Linkers in ADCs

The linker is a crucial component of ADCs used to effectively connect mAbs to their cytotoxic payload. Linkers must possess high stability in circulation, ensuring that the payload is not released until the target is reached, thereby minimizing off-target effects [56]. ADC linkers should exhibit high water solubility to facilitate conjugation with mAbs and prevent the formation of inactive ADC aggregates [56]. They must be nontoxic and designed to avoid provoking an immune response. Furthermore, the linker should enable the effective release of cytotoxic payloads or their metabolites at the tumor site [57]. The choice of an appropriate linker is typically guided by the target antigen and cytotoxic agent, considering ADC-antigen internalization dynamics, intracellular degradation pathways, and intrinsic characteristics of the payload [41]. This selection was further refined based on the mechanism of drug release, classifying the linkers into cleavable and non-cleavable classes (Fig. 2). The drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR), a critical quality attribute of ADCs, indicates the average number of cytotoxic drug molecules attached to the antibody. Utilizing this index is essential to ensure the homogeneity of the ADCs and balance their potency and safety profiles. A lower DAR can reduce the potency of ADCs, whereas a higher DAR can affect their pharmacodynamics and toxicity [51]. Determination of the DAR primarily depends on the number of antibody binding sites, toxicity level of the payload, and conjugation method of the ADC [51].

Non-cleavable linkers in ADCs

Non-cleavable linkers in ADCs require the internalization of ADCs and subsequent degradation of mAbs within lysosomes, resulting in the release of a linker-payload complex. Therefore, these ADCs require targeted cell internalization and subsequent intracellular degradation to exert their effects [59]. The presence of charged amino acid attachments can limit cellular permeability to released payloads, potentially limiting the diffusion of the cytotoxic agent into neighboring cells and compromising the therapeutic efficacy of ADCs. Furthermore, the introduction of charged or hydrophilic linkers can enhance the charge or polarity of the ADCs, which may restrict their efflux via drug efflux pumps, thereby decreasing the likelihood of drug resistance in cancer cells [60].

Commonly used non-cleavable linkers in ADC design include thioether and maleimide linkers. These linkers exhibit enhanced plasma stability and are less prone to degradation by proteases than cleavable linkers, contributing to improved tolerability and allowing for more predictable off-target toxicity profiles [61]. The released payload derivatives must effectively kill tumor cells and offer a broader therapeutic window, causing them to be particularly suitable for treating hematological malignancies or solid tumors with high expression levels of target antigens [33,61].

Cleavable linkers in ADCs

Cleavable linkers can exploit specific physiological environments to facilitate the release of cytotoxic drugs. Cleavable linkers have broader applications than their non-cleavable counterparts; however, they tend to exhibit lower circulation stability. Hence, the key characteristic of a successful cleavable linker is its capacity to harness the discrepancy between the bloodstream and tumor microenvironment or intracellular conditions, thereby enabling efficient drug delivery (Fig. 2). For example, internalization enables ADCs to access lysosomes with a low pH and high levels of hydrolases, whereas tumor cells have higher glutathione concentrations in the cytoplasm than in the extracellular environment. The plasma stability of cleavable ADCs depends on the attachment site of the linker to the antibody because certain binding sites can protect against hydrolase-mediated degradation [62]. The development of reliable cleavable linkers is crucial for optimizing the delivery of cytotoxic payloads to target cells, considering the limited tumor permeability of mAbs and the challenges associated with internalization, intracellular transport, and drug release.

Acid Cleavable Linkers: Acid-cleavable linkers capitalize on the acidic conditions within endosomes (pH 5.5–6.2) and lysosomes (pH 4.5–5.0) to hydrolyze acid-labile moieties, such as hydrazones, which remain stable at the neutral pH of blood (pH 7.4). However, hydrazone-based linkers may still be degraded in the extracellular environment owing to slow hydrolysis at physiological pH [63]. A notable success is Gemtuzumab with a hydrazone-based linker, also known as ozogamicin (mylotarg), which has undergone two separate approval processes. Reports have suggested that a carbonate-containing acid-cleavable linker conjugated to SN-38 exhibits enhanced stability in isolated human serum [64]. Specifically, the incorporation of a para-aminobenzyl (PAB) moiety into a carbonate linker significantly improves the plasma stability of ADCs [57]. This enhancement is crucial for the controlled release of therapeutic agents and the mitigation of adverse effects associated with premature drug release.

Reducible Disulfide Linkers: Reducible disulfide linkers selectively release cytotoxic drugs by harnessing the significant difference in reduction potential between the plasma and cytoplasm. Disulfides are stable in the blood but vulnerable to nucleophilic attack by thiols. Despite the presence of high concentrations of human serum albumin, which serves as a major source of thiols in plasma, residue (Cys34)-containing free thiols exhibits limited reducing power for disulfide bonds owing to their deep location within the protein structure [65]. In contrast, the cytoplasm contains significantly higher levels of thiol-containing glutathione, typically ranging from 1 to 10 ​mmol/L. These levels are considerably higher than that in plasma, which is approximately 5 ​μmol/L [65,66]. The concentration of reduced glutathione in the cytoplasm of tumor cells is typically approximately 1000 times that in normal cells. Tumor cell proliferation and oxidative stress often lead to a significant release of glutathione [67,68]. Additionally, protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) within tumor cells may contribute to the reduction of disulfide-containing ADCs. The more common collocation involves engineered cysteine residues on mAbs, designed to form direct disulfide bonds with the thiol groups in maytansinoids [65]. For example, HuC242, a humanized mAb-targeting cancer-associated antigen (canAg), is conjugated to maytansinoid via a reducible disulfide linker, resulting in huc242-SPDB-DM4, a novel ADC capable of transporting maytansinoid to pancreatic cancer cells [69].

Cathepsin B-sensitive Linkers: Cathepsin B is a cysteine protease predominantly located in advanced endosomes and lysosomes of healthy mammalian cells and is frequently overexpressed in various types of tumor cells [70]. ADCs that incorporate cathepsin B-sensitive peptide linkers require selective internalization into cancer cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis to exert their therapeutic effects. Payloads are attached to the C-terminus of the dipeptide linker, which is cleaved by the carboxy-dipeptidase activity of cathepsin B. These dipeptide linkers demonstrate stability in the bloodstream and ensure exclusive release of the drug within target cells [70]. Furthermore, cathepsin B preferentially recognizes sequences, such as Val-Cit and Phe-Lys, where the protease breaks the peptide bonds at the C-terminus of Val-Cit, Val-Ala, or Phe-Lys. Payloads with large steric volumes should be coupled with a stable spacer unit that does not alter the drug characteristics, allowing the active site of cathepsin B to fully access the cleavage site. The para-aminobenzyl carbamate (PABC) bond is one of the most commonly used spacers because it spontaneously undergoes 1,6-elimination following proteolysis, thereby releasing the unmodified payloads [57]. Phe-Lys-PABC and Val-Cit-PABC linkers have been effectively developed for ADCs using monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) as a payload. The Phe-Lys-PABC linker has a plasma half-life of 12 ​h, whereas the Val-Cit-PABC linker exhibits a significantly longer half-life of 80 ​h. The shorter half-life of Phe-Lys-PABC suggests potential non-specificity, possibly resulting in off-target toxicity [71].

Glycosidase-Sensitive Linkers: β-Glucuronidase, a hydrolytic enzyme in the glycosidase family, is located within lysosomes and plays a key role in catalyzing the degradation of β-glucuronic acid residues in polysaccharides. This enzyme is secreted into the necrotic regions of certain tumors, where it retains extracellular enzymatic activity [72]. The glycosidic bond of the glucuronic acid-containing linker is selectively cleaved by glucuronidase, facilitating the targeted release of cytotoxic payloads. The development of hydrophilic β-glucuronic acid allowed the synthesis of heavily loaded ADCs with a DAR of 8, without inducing aggregation or compromising hydrophobicity. This advancement has significantly improved plasma clearance and in vivo efficacy [73].

The β-galactoside-based linker represents an alternative glycosidase-sensitive linker hydrolyzed by β-galactosidase, an enzyme similar to glucuronidase and often over-expressed in certain tumor cells [74]. ADCs with trastuzumab conjugated with a β-galactoside linker and MMAE showed greater potency than those with trastuzumab conjugated with the Val-Cit-PABC linker and MMAE. Furthermore, trastuzumab conjugated with a β-galactoside linker and the drug DM1 demonstrated superior efficacy compared to the approved trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1), particularly for the treatment of HER2+ breast tumors [57,74].

Payloads in ADCs

The cytotoxic payload is a critical component of ADCs and is responsible for their therapeutic efficacy by determining the lethality of ADCs against target tumor cells. Several criteria are essential for an effective cytotoxic payload. (1) Cytotoxic molecules must exhibit high lethality because only a small quantity of cytotoxic molecules can enter the cell owing to the low expression levels and limited internalization efficiency of tumor cell surface target antigens [75]. Therefore, payloads are usually required to induce tumor cell death at subnanomolar and picomolar concentrations. Previous evidence indicated that achieving an IC50 value of 1 ​μM for a therapeutic molecule requires the expression of over 100 million receptors on the cell surface [76]. (2) Cytotoxic molecules must possess appropriate water solubility to prevent antibody aggregation and ensure prolonged ADC circulation. Additionally, they should remain stable in the bloodstream to prevent premature release or degradation. (3) Moreover, cytotoxic molecules should have modifiable sites to enable the introduction of functional groups, thereby facilitating their interaction with mAbs. (4) Finally, cytotoxic molecules should not elicit immune responses. Clinically used chemotherapeutic drugs are initially selected as payloads in the early stages; however, their therapeutic efficacy when conjugated with mAbs is often insufficient [77].

Approved and investigational ADCs predominantly use microtubule-targeting and DNA-damaging agents as payloads, specifically designed to engage distinct intracellular targets (Table 1) [78]. Microtubule-Targeting Agents: Microtubules are attractive targets for therapeutic intervention owing to their polymerization and depolymerization kinetics. They play crucial roles in various cellular processes, including cell morphology, division, signal transduction, and intracellular material transport. Microtubule-targeting agents interact with microtubule proteins by promoting or inhibiting microtubule assembly. These agents disrupt the normal structure and function of spindle filaments, which are composed of microtubules, thereby impeding sister chromatid separation during mitosis. This disruption ultimately leads to cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase, followed by apoptosis. These agents exhibit pronounced anti-tumor effects while reducing toxicity to normal cells because cancer cells divide much faster than most healthy cells [80]. Microtubule-targeting agents are classified into two principal categories based on their mechanisms of action: (a) tubulin polymerization promoters, exemplified by Auristatins, which bind to the β-tubulin subunit of α-β tubulin dimers and induce unregulated microtubule growth, and (b) microtubule assembly inhibitors, such as maytansinoids, which impede the polymerization of α-β tubulin dimers, thereby preventing the formation of stable microtubule structures [81]. Microtubules feature six established binding sites for various compounds, including vinca alkaloids, colchicine, taxanes, maytansinoids, laulimalides, and pironetin. Auristatin and maytansinoids are potential inhibitors of microtubule assembly because they bind near the vinblastine-binding site on tubulin.

Auristatin, a synthetic anticancer agent, evolved from the natural compound dolastatin 10 (DLS 10) [82]. However, clinical trials for DLS 10 were halted owing to concerns about nonspecific toxicity, significant systemic side effects, and potent adverse reactions [71,83,84]. In response, auristatin was developed as a synthetic analog of dolastatin, addressing these issues while improving water solubility relative to the parent compound [85].

Two innovative synthetic derivatives of auristatin, monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) and monomethyl auristatin F (MMAF), have been widely employed as payloads for ADC drugs to improve the stability of payloads within lysosomes and their therapeutic efficacy. MMAF includes a charged phenylalanine at its C-terminus and demonstrates reduced activity and limited cell membrane penetration compared to MMAE. The enhanced permeability of MMAE enables it to affect adjacent cells, thereby inducing a bystander effect that amplifies its therapeutic efficacy [81]. Brentuximab vedotin, commercially known as Acetris, is an FDA-approved ADC that employs MMAE as the payload. It features an antibody (brentuximab or cAC10) targeting the CD30 receptor conjugated to warhead MMAE using a cathepsin-cleavable linker [86].

Maytansinoids originate from maytansines, which are natural toxins known for their macrolide structure [85]. They have a similar mechanism of action to that of vinblastine and other vina alkaloids but possess significantly higher levels of toxicity [87]. Despite their limited tumor cell specificity and risk of severe systemic side effects, maytansinoids are utilized in the development of targeted therapeutic agents in oncology owing to their potent cytotoxic properties. However, certain ADCs containing maytansinoids are susceptible to substrate recognition by multidrug resistance protein 1 (MDR1), a vital membrane protein that actively pumps various exogenous substances outside the cell. In addition, many payloads in ADCs are hydrophobic, contributing to antibody aggregation [88]. However, the introduction of a series of hydrophilic linkers into the structural design of ADCs can prevent aggregation. These hydrophobic linkers increase the drug content (DAR) of mAbs and polarity of ADCs, facilitating greater delivery of cytotoxic drugs to target cells and allowing the formation of maytansinoid metabolites that are less susceptible to MDR1 efflux, respectively [1]. Maytansines undergo a series of derivatizations to incorporate sulfhydryl groups owing to the absence of a suitable functional group directly conjugated to the linker, resulting in compounds such as DM1 and DM4. The maytansine derivatives DM1 and DM4 have been successfully employed in approved ADCs [89].

DNA-Damaging Agents: DNA-damaging agents exert potent cytotoxic effects by binding to the minor groove of the DNA double helix. This interaction results in DNA alkylation, double-strand breakage, intercalation, or cross-linking, ultimately triggering apoptotic mechanisms and inducing cell death [41]. Representative compounds include PBD, duocarmycin, doxorubicin, and calicheamicin, all of which exhibit potent cytotoxicity. ADCs constructed with DNA-damaging agents are particularly advantageous for treating tumors with relatively low expression levels of target antigens [90]. DNA-damaging agents display excellent activity across all phases of the cell cycle and have shown remarkable efficacy against both dividing and non-dividing cells, including non-dividing cancer stem cells (CSCs). Additionally, they can be effectively combined with drugs that inhibit DNA repair mechanisms to enhance their therapeutic potential [91,92].

PBD, identified from Streptomyces sp. As an antitumor and antimicrobial agent, can intercalate into the minor groove of the DNA double helix and covalently bind to the C2-amino groups of guanine residues, forming a PBD-DNA complex. This complex disrupts the binding of transcription factors to DNA and the normal function of various enzymes, thereby interfering with cellular processes [93]. PBD dimers are formed by connecting two PBD monomers at the C8 position, each of which can then covalently bind to the C2-amino groups of guanine residues, causing DNA interstrand cross-linking damage and inhibiting DNA replication [94].

Duocarmycins, initially discovered in Streptomyces spp., have recently been identified as potential antimicrobial agents [95]. They exhibit significant cycle-independent cytotoxicity in vitro against various rapidly dividing cancer cells, with IC50 values in the picomolar range [96]. Duocarmycins exert their effects by alkylating the minor groove of DNA, which is abundant in AT-base pairs. This alkylation results in DNA strand breaks, ultimately inducing apoptosis [97].

Calicheamicins are potent antitumor agents derived from Micromonospora echinospora and are characterized by their ability to induce DNA double-strand breaks by interacting with a specific DNA sequence in the minor groove, typically TCCTAGGA. Their cytotoxicity is evidenced by IC50 values in the picomolar range, indicating their high potency. However, calicheamicins lack specificity for cancer cells and can potentially cause substantial DNA damage in normal cells. This lack of specificity limits their use as a standard cancer treatment. Researchers are currently exploring strategies to harness their cytotoxic properties while minimizing off-target effects, which can be achieved by combining them with other therapies or employing targeted delivery systems. Calicheamicins tend to be favorable payload candidates for ADCs owing to their unique characteristics, including small molecular weight, potent cytotoxicity, and a well-defined mechanism of action [85].

FDA-approved ADCs

Recent innovations in ADC technology have significantly enhanced their therapeutic efficacy, addressed previous constraints, and expanded their application in oncology. The evolution of ADCs spans three generations and is characterized by advancements in antibodies, payloads, and linkers. Numerous ADCs are currently being evaluated in different clinical trial phases, reflecting the ongoing momentum of this targeted therapeutic approach for cancer treatment [98,99].

The FDA has approved 13 ADCs targeting 11 different antigens that are prominently expressed on the surface of cancer cells (Table 2, Fig. 3) [8]. These approaches underscore the progress in targeted cancer therapies. The development of ADCs represents a significant advancement in personalized medicine, offering more precise and less toxic treatment options for patients with specific cancer types. Continued research and clinical trials are required to expand the range of targets and indications for ADCs.

Gemtuzumab ozogamicin (mylotarg), developed by Pfizer, was the first ADC drug to be approved by the FDA. Gemtuzumab ozogamicin is a first-generation ADC developed using a mouse monoclonal anti-CD33 antibody linked to calicheamicin via a cleavable linker (Fig. 3). The payload, calicheamicin, is released using the pH gradient within the target cancer cells, causing DNA damage and ultimately leading to cell death. This drug was initially approved in May 2000 for the treatment of CD33-positive acute myeloid leukemia (AML), representing a novel strategy in cancer therapy [100,101]. However, after approval, phase IV trials failed to validate the initial benefits, prompting concerns regarding its safety profile owing to the increased incidence of mortality and liver toxicity [102]. Consequently, gemtuzumab ozogamicin was voluntarily withdrawn from the market in September 2010 owing to safety concerns. Subsequent research has continued to explore the potential of gemtuzumab ozogamicin, leading to a re-evaluation of its role in AML treatment. New clinical data showing its efficacy and safety with appropriate use resulted in the FDA reapproval of gemtuzumab ozogamicin in September 2017. It is now approved for treating adults with newly diagnosed CD33-positive AML and for children aged ≥ 2 years with relapsed or refractory CD33-positive AML [8,103,104].

First-generation ADC drugs encounter several challenges. The limited number of target antigens expressed on the cell surface restricts the permeability of ADC drugs [58]. The hydrazone linker used in gemtuzumab ozogamicin is designed to be cleaved by acids in endosomes or lysosomes, utilizing the pH gradient between these organelles and the systemic circulation (pH 7.4). However, the hydrazone bond can also hydrolyze in the systemic circulation, resulting in off-target effects and reduced drug delivery to target cells [105,106]. Additionally, the IgG4 antibody used in gemtuzumab ozogamicin treatment was ineffective in mediating ADCC and CDC. The conjugation technology presents several challenges. Low coupling efficiency results in the lack of attachment of certain mAbs to the cytotoxic payload. Furthermore, attaching the payload to the mAb via lysine residues results in a highly heterogeneous DAR. Variability in the DAR can affect the pharmacokinetics, stability, and efficacy of ADC [8].

The challenges associated with first-generation ADC drugs provide valuable insights for the development of subsequent ADC generations. Researchers have addressed these limitations by improving linker stability, selecting antibodies capable of mediating effector functions, optimizing the DAR, and enhancing conjugation methods to improve the overall therapeutic index of ADCs. These advancements have enabled second-generation ADCs to overcome the shortcomings of their predecessors and offer improved cancer treatment outcomes.

Brentuximab vedotin (Fig. 3), a second-generation ADC developed by Seagen and marketed as Adcetris, was granted FDA approval in August 2011 for the treatment of Hodgkin's lymphoma (HL) and systemic anaplastic large cell lymphoma (sALCL) [107]. Its structure integrates three components: anti-CD30 brentuximab (cAC10), the microtubule inhibitor MMAE, and a dipeptide (Val-Cit) cleavable linker. Each brentuximab molecule carries an average of four MMAE [108]. This drug has received FDA approval for the treatment of high-risk pediatric diseases and is increasingly being used in combination therapies.

Brentuximab vedotin represents a significant advancement over earlier ADCs, addressing several issues to improve efficacy and safety. It employs a humanized antibody with a high affinity for the CD30 target antigen, thereby reducing cross-reactivity with normal tissues and minimizing immunogenicity. Furthermore, it employs an IgG1 isotype, improving its capability to mediate ADCC, CDC, and ADCP effects [81,109]. The payload, monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE), is attached via cysteine residues, resulting in a more homogenous distribution of DARs at positions 2, 4, and 6. This approach is preferred over lysine attachment, which results in a wider range of DARs, potentially affecting stability and efficacy. The selection of MMAE as a cytotoxic agent is critical because it is more potent than the payloads used in first-generation ADCs and can induce bystander effects, thereby expanding the therapeutic window [[110], [111], [112], [113]].

Trastuzumab emtansine, also known as Kadcyla, was developed by Roche. It is composed of trastuzumab (IgG1), which targets HER2, conjugated to DM1, a highly potent cytotoxic agent, through a stable thioether linker (MCC). Each trastuzumab molecule typically carries approximately 3–4 DM1 molecules (Fig. 3) [89,114]. Trastuzumab emtansine was first approved by the FDA in 2013 for the treatment of patients with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer who had previously received trastuzumab and taxane chemotherapy [8]. Notably, trastuzumab emtansine was the first approved ADC drug featuring a non-cleavable linker, ensuring that the cytotoxic payloads remain covalently attached to the antibody until they reach the tumor cell. This design enhances the stability of ADC drugs in circulation and reduces systemic exposure to cytotoxic agents. Additionally, trastuzumab emtansine was the first ADC approved for the treatment of solid tumors, signifying a new era of targeted therapy against HER2-positive breast cancer and offering an alternative or subsequent treatment option with proven benefits regarding efficacy and safety [8].

Inotuzumab ozogamicin (Besponsa), developed by Pfizer, uses a humanized mAb specific to CD22, a protein found on the surface of B cells, conjugated to the potent anticancer drug calicheamicin. Inotuzumab ozogamicin was granted accelerated approval by the FDA in August 2017 for the treatment of adults with relapsed or refractory B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [115]. Second-generation inotuzumab ozogamicin contains the same linker-payload complex as the first-generation gemtuzumab ozogamicin; however, the DAR value of inotuzumab ozogamicin is significantly higher than that of gemtuzumab ozogamicin (Fig. 3) [8]. The difference in the DARs between these ADCs can be attributed to factors related to the choice of antibodies and technological innovations applied in their design and development.

Polatuzumab vedotin (Polivy) and enfortumab vedotin (Padcev), which are similar to brentuximab vedotin, are designed using a linker-payload system featuring a dipeptide linker and the microtubule inhibitor MMAE (Fig. 3) [8]. Polatuzumab vedotin employs a humanized IgG1 mAb targeting CD79b, whereas enfortumab vedotin employs a recombinant humanized IgG1 mAb targeting Nectin-4, with an average DAR of approximately 3.5. Polatuzumab vedotin was approved by the FDA in June 2019 for relapsed or refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), whereas enfortumab vedotin was approved in December 2019 for advanced urothelial carcinoma.

Trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu) is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting HER2 conjugated to a potent topoisomerase I inhibitor, the exatecan derivative (DXd), via a cleavable tetrapeptide (Gly Gly Phe-Gly) linker (Fig. 3) [116]. It was approved by the FDA in December 2019 and has been indicated for HER2-positive, unresectable, or metastatic breast cancer after anti-HER2 therapy [116]. Its rapid approval underscores its significant clinical efficacy and manageable safety profile. Trastuzumab deruxtecan and trastuzumab emtansine are designed to target HER2; however, they exhibit distinct structural differences that reflect advancements in ADC technology. Trastuzumab deruxtecan employs a cleavable linker, which allows for the precise release of the payload within cancer cells, enhancing efficacy while reducing systemic exposure to cytotoxins. Additionally, it uses site-specific conjugation to ensure uniform payload distribution while maintaining a high DAR of 8 [117]. Conversely, Trastuzumab emtansine utilizes a non-cleavable linker and traditional random conjugation methods, potentially resulting in a less homogenous payload distribution (DAR ​= ​3.5) and reduced therapeutic effect. These improvements highlight ongoing developments in ADC technology aimed at increasing the specificity, potency, and safety of cancer treatments [118].

Sacituzumab govitecan (Trodelvy) is a Trop-2-targeting ADC with a CL2A cleavable linker and the topoisomerase I inhibitor SN-38. It was approved by the FDA in 2019 for the treatment of metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC) after at least two prior therapies (Fig. 3) [119]. Belantamab mafodotin (Blenrep) employs a humanized IgG1 mAb targeting BCMA conjugated to the microtubule inhibitor MMAF through a non-cleavable linker. It was granted accelerated FDA approval in 2020 for the treatment of relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (MM) in patients who had received at least four prior regimens, including proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory agents, and anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies [8]. Loncastuximab tesirine-ipyle (Zynlonta) constitutes an anti-CD19 human IgG1 mAb conjugated to the PBD dimer, SG3199, through a protease-cleavable linker, with a DAR of approximately 2.3. It was approved by the FDA in 2021 for the treatment of relapsed or refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) after at least two prior regimens of systemic therapy [8]. Lastly, tisotumab vedotin is an anti-TF ADC with a valine-alanine dipeptide cleavable linker and an MMAE payload. It was approved by the FDA in 2021 for the treatment of recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer after at least one prior therapy, representing a significant scientific milestone and offering a novel treatment option for patients with this aggressive disease [119].

Mirvetuximab soravtansin employs an FRα-targeted antibody linked to a microtubule inhibitor via a cleavable linker. FRα is typically expressed at low levels in normal tissues, whereas it is overexpressed in certain types of epithelial ovarian cancer, endometrial adenocarcinoma, triple-negative breast cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer. Clinical trials have demonstrated the potential of mirvetuximab soravtansin for treating patients with FRα-positive, platinum-resistant ovarian cancer. A phase I study (NCT01609556) reported an objective response rate (ORR) of 26 ​%, with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 4.8 months and a median duration of response (DOR) of 19.1 weeks [120]. Common treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) include diarrhea, blurred vision, nausea, and fatigue. The SORAYA study, a phase II trial, reported an ORR of 32.4 ​% and a median DOR of 6.9 months in patients with platinum-resistant ovarian cancer with high FRα expression [121]. A phase III study compared mirvetuximab sorafenib to chemotherapy, showing a comparable safety profile with fewer TRAEs, without meeting the primary endpoint of improved PFS [122]. Mirvetuximab soravtansin represents a significant advancement in the treatment of FRα-positive cancers, offering a targeted therapy with the potential to improve patient outcomes.

The heterogeneity observed in first- and second-generation ADCs, characterized by variable payload attachment locations and the number of mAbs (Table 2), presents considerable challenges in predicting pharmacokinetics and efficacy [118]. Significant advancements have been undergone in the development of third-generation ADCs that employ site-specific coupling technologies. These advancements enhance the predictability and performance of ADC drugs by employing techniques such as the introduction of specific amino acids into the amino acid sequence of mAbs by antibody engineering, the use of enzyme-mediated site-specific coupling techniques, and the acquisition of site-specific mAbs from natural antibodies [118]. The increasing sophistication of ADC design is driven by the aim to improve therapeutic effects. Selecting superior target antigens is crucial to allow specific binding of ADCs to tumor cells while sparing normal cells. Antibody optimization involves enhancing binding affinity, improving internalization rates, and reducing potential immunogenicity. Exploring different linkers enables the customization of ADC properties, such as stability during circulation and controlled release of the payload upon reaching the tumor microenvironment. Cytotoxic payloads must be carefully selected based on their ability to effectively induce cell death while minimizing systemic toxicity. Finally, the development of site-specific coupling techniques ensures homogeneity of payload attachment, resulting in more predictable pharmacokinetics, decreased off-target effects, and improved efficacy. Collectively, these advancements in ADC technology hold promise in delivering more effective and safer treatments for various cancers.

Peptide-drug conjugates

PDCs are an emerging class of therapeutic agents inspired by ADC development that use a peptide as the targeting moiety [10]. This strategic shift offers several potential advantages, including enhanced tissue penetration, reduced immunogenicity, and simplified manufacturing processes [[123], [124], [125]]. Despite the high specificity and potency of ADCs, challenges such as their large sizes, high immunogenicity, complex manufacturing processes, and limited tissue penetration have driven the exploration of alternative delivery systems such as PDCs [126]. PDCs share structural similarities with ADCs, comprising three essential components: the targeting peptide, linker, and cytotoxic payload [10]. PDCs have emerged as a promising therapeutic approach, particularly in oncology, aiming to overcome the limitations of ADCs while maintaining the benefits of targeted drug delivery.

Targeting peptides in PDCs

Ideal peptides that serve as carriers in PDCs should have strong target-binding affinity, high stability, low immunogenicity, efficient internalization, and a prolonged plasma half-life [127,128]. PDC peptides are generally categorized into cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), such as Pep-1, Pentratin, PepFact14, and Transportan, and cell-targeting peptides (CTPs), including PEGA, somatostatin analogs, bombesin analogs, and RGD peptides. These peptides enhance the local concentration of cytotoxic agents at the tumor site while reducing systemic toxicity, thereby mitigating adverse reactions and ultimately achieving therapeutic effects (Fig. 4). However, the selection and design of these peptides are critical for the efficacy and safety of PDCs. In recent years, rapid advancements in phage display combinatorial libraries, One-bead One-compound (OBOC) combinatorial libraries, and Positional Scanning-synthetic Peptide Combinatorial Libraries (PSSPCLs) have led to the discovery and rational design of numerous novel peptides suitable as carriers for PDCs [[129], [130], [131]]. The CPPs used in PDCs are short peptides with specific biological properties that enable them to traverse cell membranes and enter the intracellular space. These peptides typically comprise 5–30 amino acids and possess defined sequences and structures that facilitate cell membrane interactions and internalization [132]. CPPs should have good cell membrane permeability, stability, biocompatibility, and low toxicity. However, the mechanism of CPP cellular entry remains unclear. Certain CPPs are hypothesized to penetrate cells through energy-dependent endocytosis or receptor-mediated pathways, whereas others employ energy-independent nonendocytic mechanisms [[133], [134], [135]]. Additionally, reports have indicated that certain CPPs can transport cytotoxic molecules directly across the phospholipid bilayer into cells [134]. CPPs encompass various types, including those derived from natural sources, such as Tat peptide (from HIV-1 virus), penetratin (from Antennapedia protein), and pVEC (from frog skin), and synthetically designed peptides or lipopeptides [136]. CPPs possess a distinctive capability to penetrate cell membranes; however, their limited target specificity causes them to be less favorable candidates for targeted drug delivery vehicles [136]. Various strategies have been developed to overcome these limitations, such as sequence engineering, fusion with targeting peptides, and the use of cleavable linkers to generate activatable CPPs (aCPPs) that combine targeting and penetration functionalities [137].

CTPs are vital for PDC success in cancer therapy, providing a means of delivering drugs with high precision to cancer cells, which is essential for improving treatment outcomes and patient prognosis. These peptides must meet several requirements to effectively target cells, including high affinity and specificity for the target receptor, stability under physiological conditions, minimal immunogenicity, and efficient cellular internalization. Mechanistically, cell-targeting peptides recognize and bind to specific cell surface receptors, triggering downstream signaling pathways that lead to the internalization of the peptide-drug complex (Fig. 4). This process is often mediated by clathrin-coated pits or caveolae, resulting in either endosomal trapping or lysosomal degradation [138,139]. One advantage of CTPs over mAbs used in ADCs is their smaller sizes, allowing for better tissue penetration and potentially higher tumor uptake. Moreover, peptides can be synthesized at a lower cost and with greater scalability than mAbs when designing TDCs. However, mAbs provide higher target specificity and a longer circulating half-life, resulting in improved tumor targeting and reduced off-target effects.

Several methods have been developed to improve the stability and cell permeability of peptides [140,141]. Cyclization modifications are often used to determine the secondary structure of peptides to improve their binding affinity to the target and increase their absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) [141]. Amino acid modification involves using d-amino acids instead of l-amino acids or introducing unnatural amino acids. This strategy aims to reduce the risk of peptide degradation by proteases, ultimately enhancing their chemical stability and biological activity [141]. Peptides are typically conjugated to chemical macromolecules such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), polysialic acid (PSA), or fatty chains, enhancing their lipophilicity [140]. This modification decreases the renal clearance rate of peptides, prolongs their blood circulation time, and improves their bioavailability. Dosage modifications such as penetration enhancers, acid-stable coatings, and nanocarriers have been shown to enhance the oral bioavailability of peptides [140]. These strategies protect peptides from gastric acid and intestinal enzymes, facilitate crossing into epithelial cells, and improve intracellular delivery efficiency. Therefore, enhancing the overall chemical and enzymatic stability of peptides using these approaches may improve the stability and ADME properties of PDCs [140].

Linkers in PDCs

Linkers are important components of PDCs that connect the peptide carrier and drug payload, affecting the stability, specificity, and efficacy of PDCs. Linkers can be classified into cleavable and non-cleavable linkers (Fig. 5) [142]. Cleavable linkers can be cleaved by chemical or enzymatic triggers, such as pH, glutathione, or proteases. Many chemical bonds are pH sensitive, including acetals, hemiacetals, imines, salicylaldehydes, hydroxyacetals, and hydroxybenzaldehydes. These linkers can be cleaved in the acidic environment of tumor cells to release drugs. Glutathione can cleave a variety of chemical bonds, including disulfides, thioethers/selenium/tellurium, diesel/disulfides, ferrocenes, and metal thiols [140]. Dipeptides, including Val-Ala and Val-Cit, serve as substrates for upregulated enzymes in cancer cells and exhibit good stability in circulation [142]. Recent advancements in cleavable linkers include the development of aryl sulfate linkers, which are cleaved by lysosomal sulfatases. After cleavage, the aryl sulfate linker undergoes 1,6-elimination to release the unmodified drug [143]. Non-cleavable linkers use chemical components such as succinimidyl thioethers, oximes, and triazoles to bind chemotherapeutic drugs to target peptides (Fig. 5). Noncleavable linkers do not respond to external stimuli and release drugs solely through peptide metabolism. PDCs and ADCs share commonalities in their release mechanisms; nevertheless, notable differences exist in the design and composition of their respective linkers. PDC linkers tend to be shorter and more flexible than those used for ADCs [142]. This may be attributed to the smaller size of the peptides, which do not require the same level of structural support as antibodies. Conversely, ADC linkers are typically longer and more intricate to accommodate the larger antibody dimensions. Moreover, ADC linkers may incorporate spacer units to mitigate steric hindrance and maintain the mAb binding capability. The distinctive features of PDCs and ADCs dictate their design and functionality in the context of their respective drug delivery systems.

Payloads in PDCs

Cytotoxic agents are the key components that enable ADCs and PDCs to exert their therapeutic effects. However, significant differences exist in the specific cytotoxic molecules used between these technologies. Antibodies demonstrate impressive specificity and affinity; however, their tissue penetration and internalization efficiency are limited [144,145]. Conversely, peptides may exhibit a comparatively lower affinity than antibodies, but their smaller molecular size facilitates improved tissue and cellular penetration. Therefore, ADCs typically carry highly cytotoxic agents, such as N-acetyl-calicheamicin (calicheamicin derivatives), MMAE/MMAF, and DM-1/DM-4. These agents may cause severe systemic toxicity when used in isolation [146]. ADC design requires a delicate balance between efficacy and safety. Conversely, smaller cytotoxic molecules, such as doxorubicin, paclitaxel, camptothecin, and their derivatives, are commonly used in PDCs for clinical monotherapy or combination chemotherapy (Fig. 6), without the need for pharmacological re-evaluation, thereby significantly shortening the development cycle and cost of PDCs [140]. In addition to the aforementioned chemotherapeutic agents, certain protein-based drugs with poor targeting ability and low bioavailability, such as interferons and tumor necrosis factors, have demonstrated anti-tumor effects by inhibiting protein synthesis and inducing apoptosis. Additionally, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and antisense oligonucleotides (AONs) are emerging in tumor therapy [147,148]. Peptide-protein conjugates, formed by combining targeting peptides with protein-based drugs, offer a viable approach to address the limitations of protein-based therapies [128,149,150].

Advances of PDCs in clinical applications

PDCs are a promising class of TDC therapeutics. Several PDCs have been approved by the FDA, including Lutathera and Pepaxto, and these innovative compounds have revolutionized the landscape of cancer treatment (Table 3). Melflufen, marketed as Pepaxto, is a pioneer PDC that received accelerated FDA approval in February 2021. It is indicated for the treatment of adult patients with relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (MM) who have received at least four prior therapy regimens and whose disease is refractory to at least one proteasome inhibitor, one immunomodulatory agent, and one monoclonal antibody targeting CD38 [151]. Melflufen is a lipophilic dipeptide composed of melphalan and para-fluoro-l-phenylalanine. Melphalan, a potent DNA alkylating agent, is a prototype drug for the effective treatment of MM. Its mechanism involves the alkylation of DNA guanine bases, leading to abnormal linkages within or between DNA strands, which results in DNA breaks and ultimately induces cell death. However, its high hydrophilicity limits its therapeutic efficacy [152]. Aminopeptidases are present in all human cells and are overexpressed in a variety of tumors, including MM [153]. Melflufen is rapidly absorbed by myeloma cells owing to its high lipophilicity. Once inside the cell, the peptide conjugated to melflufen is immediately cleaved by aminopeptidases and esterases, releasing the hydrophilic alkylating agent payload, melphalan (Fig. 7) [[153], [154], [155]]. Small molecule-drug conjugates

Small organic ligands can be used as alternatives to antibodies and peptides for tumor-targeting applications. Small molecule-drug conjugates (SMDCs) are attracting increasing interest as an alternative approach to antibodies for drug delivery and tumor-targeting applications owing to their advanced tumor penetration, contrasting with high-molecular-weight ADCs [11]. An SMDC typically constitutes a targeting ligand, spacer, cleavable linker, and therapeutic payload. The ratio of payload to small-molecule-targeting ligands in the preparation of SMDCs is more precisely controlled than that of ADCs. Moreover, SMDCs present strengths such as manageable synthesis and a non-immunogenic nature. The molecular weights of SMDCs are typically low, potentially enhancing cell permeability in solid tumors and improving in vitro and in vivo stability [165,166]. However, the small size of SMDCs is the primary reason for their extremely short half-life in circulation, which in turn limits their gradual uptake by tumors and compromises their therapeutic efficacy [167,168].

Mechanistically, endosomes arise from receptor-mediated endocytosis and fuse with lysosomes to release cytotoxic payloads into the cytoplasm (Fig. 9) [12]. In particular, certain SMDCs release cytotoxic molecules directly onto the surface of tumor cells. They employ disulfide or peptide bonds to attach cytotoxic payloads to targeting ligands, leveraging the elevated levels of glutathione or proteases in the extracellular tumor environment compared to those in normal tissue. This design allows for selective cleavage of the linker and subsequent payload release, as demonstrated by SMDCs targeting carbonic anhydrase IX [169,170].Fig. 9Cellular Processing of SMDCs. The parallels between ADCs and SMDCs highlight the potential of SMDCs as a promising therapeutic strategy and suggest that research on ADCs could accelerate the development and optimization of SMDCs. This field has promising potential for future research and development in the field of TDCs.

Targeting ligands in SMDCs

Several factors must be considered in SMDC development, including the selectivity, binding affinity, penetration, and retention of targeted ligands. The size of SMDCs is a critical aspect, as evidenced by the limitations in cell penetration observed in ADCs with larger molecular sizes. The permeability, retention effects, mechanism of cellular entry, and excretion of SMDCs are influenced by their size. Identification of ligands with extremely high affinities remains challenging. Target ligands with a dissociation constant (KD) of at least 10 ​nM are often required to ensure adequate selectivity and affinity, which can aid in minimizing the drug dose [167]. Identifying suitable targets for SMDCs is challenging owing to the limited availability of targets. An ideal target allows recycling and rapid regeneration [167]. Current targets, such as folate receptor (FR), prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), somatostatin receptor, carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX) [11], phosphatidylserine [171], aminopeptidase N [14], low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 [172], and glucose transporter 1 [173], have shown promise.

The initial targeting ligands, which are analogs of natural receptor ligands, show promise in SMDCs. These include folate derivatives for FR, glutamic acid urea derivatives for PSMA, somatostatin analogs for somatostatin receptor, and aromatic sulfonamides for CAIX (Fig. 10) [169,[174], [175], [176]]. The emergence of DNA-encoded chemical library technologies has simplified the development of tailored ligand lead compounds with higher affinities compared to traditional high-throughput screening methods. This technology could potentially accelerate the discovery and development of novel SMDCs [177].Fig. 10Structures of the most promising small molecule targeting ligands.Fig. 10

Linkers in SMDCs

The linker design in both SMDCs and ADCs is pivotal for their structural integrity and functionality. Linker design plays a crucial role in preserving the function of targeting ligands and payloads, optimizing the therapeutic agent release at the target site and regulating the pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic properties of drug conjugation [167]. The distinction is that the SMDC linker generally comprises a spacer and a cleavable bridge (Table 4). The spacer distances the drug from the targeting ligand, reducing potential spatial interference between the two components [178]. Adding a water-soluble spacer such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), peptidoglycan, or hydrophilic amino acids can greatly increase the water solubility of the final SMDCs, considering that targeted ligands or cytotoxic molecules are generally extremely lipophilic, which benefits receptor binding and membrane permeability [167]. The characteristics of spacers have not yet been fully elucidated; nevertheless, rigid spacers perform better than flexible spacers in certain cases [14,167,179]. The evolution of spacer technology in SMDCs shifted from the early use of carbohydrates, acidic residues, and saccharoamino acids to the introduction of glutamic acid and glutamine [180]. On the other hand, cleavable linkers are frequently utilized in the design of SMDCs. SMDCs can rapidly bind to receptors and are cleared by the kidneys in vivo; therefore, they do not require linkers with the same stringent criteria as those required for ADCs [182]. Two types of cleavable linkers are commonly used in SMDCs. Disulfide-containing cleavable linkers are triggered to break by the high levels of oxidants present in tumor cells. They undergo 1,2-elimination and 1,6-elimination under physiological conditions (37 ​°C and pH 7.4), thereby releasing their cytotoxic payload [183]. Acid-sensitive cleavable linkers contain acid-sensitive functional groups such as acetals, hydrazones, and esters, which are sensitive to environmental pH changes [184,185]. The fundamental linker requirements are similar between SMDCs and ADCs; however, the precise design and composition of the linker can vary depending on the delivery system.

Payloads in SMDCs

The success of an SMDC depends on the ability of the cytotoxic payload to deliver a potent attack on target cells. These FDA-approved payloads, including vincristine, paclitaxel, mitomycin C, microtubulin inhibitors, and the irinotecan metabolite SN-38, have been meticulously selected for their proven therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles (Fig. 11). They primarily function by inhibiting crucial cellular processes, including DNA replication, apoptosis resistance, cell division, protein synthesis, and key metabolic pathways such as glycolysis [167,208]. SMDC potency depends on various payload-related factors, including their inherent cytotoxicity, efficient release from the ligand-linker complex, evasion of drug efflux pumps, intracellular metabolism, and efficiency in reaching specific intracellular targets [167]. Payloads with high therapeutic potential typically exhibit strong binding affinities, reducing the risk of multidrug resistance and minimizing intracellular metabolism [167]. The efficacy of SMDCs can be improved by attaching multiple therapeutic agents to a single homing ligand; however, this presents significant challenges [209]. Moreover, the therapeutic payload should be adaptable to modification by featuring functional groups, such as carboxyl, amine, sulfhydryl, and aldehyde groups, to facilitate conjugation. To achieve optimal therapeutic efficacy, the payload must have sufficient membrane permeability and hydrophobicity to escape the endosomal compartment and prevent SMDC aggregation and precipitation [73].Fig. 11Representative cytotoxic agents in SMDC design. Challenges

TDCs represent a class of precision medicine that aims to more effectively treat diseases such as cancer by specifically targeting cancer cells while sparing normal cells [210]. The fundamental elements of TDCs depend on three critical components: the targeting ligand, linker, and payload. Regarding targeting ligands, ongoing research is being conducted to identify and develop new molecules with greater functions and potency that can selectively bind to tumor cell markers. These include innovative forms such as bispecific antibodies, which can target two different antigens; single-domain antibodies (also known as nanobodies), which offer stability and can penetrate tissues deeply; and peptides, which can be engineered for high specificity. Traditional TDCs often carry cytotoxic agents that kill cancer cells. However, a trend towards exploring non-cytotoxic molecules with novel mechanisms of action exists. These include radioelements for targeted radiation therapy and oligonucleotides that modulate gene expression. The linker connects the targeting ligand to the payload and must be carefully designed to maintain the stability of the conjugate in the bloodstream and release the payload only at the tumor site. Innovation in linker technology involves creating linkers that can be cleaved under specific enzymatic conditions prevalent in tumor cells or the tumor microenvironment, ensuring controlled and efficient payload release. Regarding conjugation methods, the evolution from random to site-specific conjugation represents a significant advancement. Site-specific conjugation ensures that each TDC molecule carries a consistent and predetermined number of payloads, enhancing pharmacokinetic predictability, stability, and safety profiles. Furthermore, this controlled strategy aids in achieving consistent therapeutic effects.

ADCs combine the specificity of mAbs with the potency of cytotoxic agents to effectively target cancer cells. However, several challenges are encountered in ADC development and application, despite their advantages regarding selectivity, low off-target toxicity, and potency. ADCs can inadvertently bind to non-tumor cells when target antigens are not uniquely expressed by cancer cells, resulting in reduced therapeutic specificity and an increased risk of off-target cytotoxicity. This limitation narrows the therapeutic window, causing challenges in administering effective doses without causing unacceptable levels of toxicity in healthy tissues [211]. Instability in the linker connecting the antibody to the cytotoxic agent can result in premature release of the payload into the bloodstream. Such non-specific release contributes to systemic toxicity and diminishes the therapeutic benefits of ADCs [212]. Moreover, ADCs can cause adverse events such as thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, leukopenia, anemia, or peripheral neuropathy. Proactively anticipating and managing these side effects, often through dose adjustments, is crucial to minimize patient risks [213]. ADCs exhibit more complex pharmacokinetic properties than traditional cytotoxic agents, primarily owing to their prolonged half-life and dynamic interactions within the circulation [8], causing difficulty in characterizing the clinical profile of ADCs and guiding the design of new ADCs.

The rapid advancements in ADCs in recent years have introduced mixed therapeutic effects in cancer treatments. Despite their success, the emergence of tumor resistance is a significant challenge associated with ADC development and application, substantially affecting their efficacy and clinical application. Resistance mechanisms to ADCs are multifaceted and can be influenced by various factors arising from each component of the ADC or by pathways involved in the mechanism of action of ADCs [214]. Cancer cells may downregulate or mutate the target antigens recognized by ADCs, thereby reducing the binding affinity of the antibody component and compromising the efficacy of ADC therapy. Lysosomal degradation is a crucial step in the intracellular processing of ADCs, in which the linker is cleaved to release the active drugs. Impaired lysosomal protein hydrolysis or acidification can hinder this process, resulting in reduced drug release and cytotoxicity [214]. Tumor cells may upregulate the expression of multidrug resistance transporters, such as ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, which actively pump out the cytotoxic payload delivered by ADCs. This efflux mechanism prevents intracellular accumulation of drugs and confers resistance to ADC therapy [214]. Resistance to brentuximab vedotin has been associated with loss of CD30 antigen expression in patients with anaplastic large-cell lymphoma [215]. Resistance to gemtuzumab ozogamicin, brentuximab vedotin, and T-DM1 has been correlated to drug efflux pumps [216]. Furthermore, the presence of antigenic ligands and defects in the internalization pathway may lead to ADC resistance.

Additionally, the use of mutant proteins with higher levels of ubiquitination than wild-type proteins as targets [217], bispecific antibodies [218] and in ADCs carrying two distinct payloads offers opportunities for advancing ADC development [219]. Nonetheless, smaller peptide fragments and single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) are being explored as alternatives to full-length mAbs, particularly in PDC technology.

PDCs are promising therapeutic agents with advantages over ADCs, including smaller sizes and potentially greater tissue penetration [220]. However, their small size presents challenges, particularly related to rapid renal clearance, which limits their ability to effectively accumulate at pharmacological sites [221]. To overcome these limitations, several strategies have been developed to extend the half-lives of PDCs in circulation. PEGylation involves attaching PEG molecules to the peptide component of the PDC. PEG is hydrophilic and increases the hydrodynamic volume of the molecule, reducing its renal clearance rate and consequently prolonging its half-life in the circulation [222]. Another strategy is fusing PDC with human serum albumin (HSA) or the Fc region of antibodies. These proteins naturally circulate in the bloodstream for long periods and can utilize the FcRn-mediated recycling pathway to avoid degradation and extend the half-life of the conjugate [223,224]. Both strategies increase the molecular size of the PDC, which can mitigate the advantage of high tissue permeability from the smaller size of the peptides. Moreover, the specificity and targeting of peptides are marginally inferior to those of ADCs.

As a part of targeted therapy, SMDC research faces significant challenges owing to the lack of active products in the development pipeline. Only a limited number of targeting ligands have been systematically assessed, with a focus on folate receptors [208]. Certain SMDCs exhibit renal uptake to some extent, which may remain residual during renal clearance [225]. Additionally, the limited oral bioavailability of SMDCs restricts their flexibility and convenience of administration. This can be attributed to their poor solubility, necessitating further research to overcome this limitation.

No single delivery format or fixed development paradigm in targeted drug therapy suits most indications. Continuous innovation in the fields of targeting systems, linkers, and payloads and advancements in conjugation techniques hold promise in improving the therapeutic index of TDCs and providing more effective treatment options for patients with cancer.

Conclusion

TDCs have emerged as a promising therapeutic approach to cancer treatment [146]. TDCs can achieve precise delivery by coupling payloads with specific targeting ligands, thereby improving the selectivity and efficacy of cytotoxic payloads while minimizing toxicity and side effects on normal cells. TDCs typically comprise three components, a targeting ligand, a linker, and an effector, all of which influence their stability, selectivity, and toxicity. In this article, we compared three different types of TDCs, namely ADCs, PDCs, and SMDCs, and discussed their designs, mechanisms of action, and current research status.

The targeting capabilities of TDCs vary significantly depending on the nature of their targeting moieties. ADCs utilize the high affinity of mAbs for antigens, providing precise targeting, albeit with limitations in tissue penetration owing to the relatively large size of antibodies. Conversely, PDCs employ peptides for targeting, which are smaller than antibodies and can achieve better tissue penetration [13]. Peptides can target a wide range of epitopes, including those inaccessible to antibodies. SMDCs rely on small molecules for target binding and can more effectively penetrate cancer tissues [6]. These molecules may have lower specificities than antibodies and peptides. Identifying additional effective small-molecule ligands as targets is necessary. Variations in the targeting capabilities significantly influence TDC pharmacokinetics. ADCs typically have a long circulation time owing to the long half-life of antibodies [4], which can be advantageous for sustained therapeutic effects but may also lead to prolonged exposure and potential toxicity. In contrast, PDCs are smaller than ADCs, resulting in faster tissue penetration and shorter circulation times [10]. This can be advantageous for targeting tumors with dense stroma or poor vascularization. PDCs are generally metabolized and cleared from the body faster than ADCs, which reduces their systemic toxicity. SMDCs are characterized by their very small size and may exhibit rapid distribution and elimination kinetics [6]. This can be beneficial for rapid targeting and release of the payload but may also result in a shorter duration of action. The clinical applicability of these conjugates is influenced by their targeting capabilities and pharmacokinetic profiles. ADCs are the most clinically advanced drugs, with several FDA-approved drugs for various cancers. ADCs have demonstrated significant efficacy; however, challenges such as potential immunogenic responses, hematotoxicity, and low cell permeability can still be encountered owing to the antibody component [9]. PDCs are emerging as alternatives to ADCs, with the potential for improved targeting and reduced immunogenicity compared to ADCs. Two PDCs have already been approved by the FDA; nevertheless, their development remains in its early stages. SMDCs have the potential for high permeability and low immunogenicity but face challenges in development, such as identifying stable and effective small-molecule ligands and optimizing linker chemistry for drug release. However, the low specificity of small molecules increases the risk of off-target effects, which should be carefully and preemptively managed in clinical applications.

The design and development of TDCs require a comprehensive consideration of various factors, including the expression level and internalization capability of the target antigen; affinity and immunogenicity of the targeting ligand; stability and cleavability of the linker; and toxicity and permeability of the effector molecule to achieve optimal efficacy and safety. The classification and innovation of TDCs primarily depend on their targeting systems, such as antibodies, peptides, and small molecules, which possess their own characteristics and are indicated for different types of tumors and treatment options.

The field of targeted therapy is undergoing rapid evolution. In addition to the well-established ADCs, PDCs, and SMDCs, a plethora of innovative conjugation technologies are being developed to treat various diseases, particularly cancer. These include radionuclide drugs (RDCs), immune-stimulating antibodies conjugates (ISACs), antibody degraders conjugates (ADeCs), antibody fragment conjugates (FDCs), virus-like drugs conjugates (VDCs), antibody-oligonucleotides conjugates (AOCs), antibody-cells conjugates (ACCs), and antibody-biopolymer conjugates (ABCs), among others. Technological progress continues to advance the field of TDCs, and diverse choices of targeting systems, linkers, and effectors have been utilized, resulting in field segmentation. The current over-segmentation in the field of TDCs has resulted in a crossover between different concept drugs; nevertheless, each technology presents unique opportunities and challenges.
