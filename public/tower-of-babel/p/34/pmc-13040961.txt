Development, validation and visualization of a nomogram for the prediction of in-hospital cardiac arrest in patients after successful primary PCI for STEMI
Authors: Wei Luo, Zixu Zhao, Minghui Zhang, Can Zhou, Bingbing Ke, Chen Li, Zhili Jiang, Fei Liu, Lin Wang, Wangyang Yang, Yunxiao Yang, Keyang Zheng, Yu Liu, Chengqian Yin, Xinyong Zhang, Hai Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) is an infrequent but lethal complication in patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Data on the characteristics and predictors of IHCA in STEMI patients after successful primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) are limited. This study aimed to identify the risk factors of IHCA and construct a predictive nomogram.MethodA total of 5121 STEMI patients treated with successful PPCI from January 2018 to July 2023 at Beijing Anzhen Hospital were retrospectively enrolled in our study. Subjects were randomly divided into a development group and a validation group in a 7:3 ratio. Optimal predictive variables were selected using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) and logistic regression models. A nomogram based on these predictors was then created to estimate IHCA probability.ResultsAmong them, 94 patients (1.8% [95% CI: 1.4%-2.1%]) experienced IHCA after PPCI, and the in-hospital mortality rate was 35.1% in these patients. Lasso regression was implemented to identify predictors of IHCA, including age, Killip III-IV, systolic blood pressure, and moderate to severe calcification, with non-zero coefficients. A nomogram model for predicting the risk of IHCA after PPCI in STEMI patients was constructed with the above independent predictors, with a C-index of 0.823 (95%CI 0.764–0.881). The model’s calibration curve closely aligned with the ideal reference, indicating strong agreement between predicted and actual outcomes, and the internal validation showed a C-index of 0.828 (95%CI 0.751–0.905). Clinical decision analysis further confirmed that the nomogram model demonstrated good clinical effectiveness.ConclusionOur study identified several independent risk factors for IHCA in STEMI patients after successful PPCI based on a Lasso-logistic regression model, offering individualized and reliable cardiac arrest risk predictions during hospitalization.Trial registrationNot applicable.Graphical Abstract Graphical Summary. Introduction

Cardiac Arrest is a not rare but serious complication in acute myocardial infarction, especially for those with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), as 5%-10% of patients could develop cardiac arrest after STEMI [1, 2]. In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) was an independent predictor of poor prognosis in STEMI patients, as it was associated with 10-fold higher mortality compared to those without cardiac arrest [3]. In the era of early revascularization, especially primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI), there has been a continuous reduction in the incidence of cardiac arrest of STEMI adverse events over the years, while the evidence of the characteristics and risk factors of IHCA after successful early revascularization is scarce [4]. IHCA progresses rapidly and often results in poor outcomes due to the limited window for effective intervention, making early identification of high-risk patients essential for prevention efforts. Developing a convenient and reliable prediction model is therefore crucial, as it enables healthcare providers to assess and manage the risk of IHCA proactively, potentially improving survival rates and reducing complications.

Nomograms, graphical tools derived from statistical predictive models like logistic regression, integrate various predictors to provide a visual and intuitive probability estimation of individual disease risks or clinical outcomes. Its application has been reported in predicting the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with acute myocardial infarction after PPCI [5–7]. In this study, we investigated the incidence and risk factors for IHCA and constructed a nomogram model to predict the risk of IHCA in STEMI patients undergoing successful PPCI, which would be helpful in providing timely management of IHCA.

Method

Study population

The study was a retrospective analysis enrolling patients diagnosed with STEMI undergoing PPCI from January 2018 to July 2023 at Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing, China. Patients presenting with STEMI treated with successful PPCI were included. Successful PPCI was defined as the procedure being performed within 24 h from onset and Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) 3 flow was achieved after the procedure. We excluded patients with intravenous thrombolysis, pre-PCI cardiac arrest/cardiogenic shock/cardiac resuscitation, or elective PCI. The Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University approved this study (Ethical approval number: 2025153x). The reporting of this study followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) checklist (Supplemental Table S1).

Definition of variables

IHCA was defined as the sudden pause of cardiac mechanic activity during hospitalization after successful PPCI requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation. IHCA was reported by physicians and documented in the medical records. The patient’s demographic data, previous medical history, and angiographic characteristics were obtained via the original electronic medical records. Immediate treatments were defined as the initiation of therapies within 24 h after first medical contact. The drugs used immediately after first medical contact, including dual anti-platelet treatment, statins, β-blocker, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ACEI/ARB), and spironolactone were collected. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was obtained from the first transthoracic echocardiography performed after hospital admission and before the primary PCI procedure. Moderate calcification is defined as radiopaque densities noted only during the cardiac cycle and typically involving one side of the vascular wall. Severe calcification is defined as radiopaque densities visible without cardiac motion prior to contrast injection and generally involving both sides of the arterial wall. Median imputation was applied for variables with less than 5% missing values. Variables with over 30% missing data were excluded from the analysis. The extent of missingness for all variables included in the study is summarized in Supplemental Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and compared using a 2-sample t-test or rank-sum test based on whether the data were distributed normally. Categorical variables were described as counts and percentages and compared between groups using χ2 tests. The dataset of enrolled patients was randomly divided into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. The prediction model including optimal features selected by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) regression was constructed based on logistic regression and was further validated in the testing sets. A concordance index (C-index) and calibration curves were calculated to measure the predictive performance of the nomogram. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the clinical significance of the model. Internal bootstrap validation with 1,000 resamples was performed to obtain optimism-corrected estimates of model performance, including the C-index, calibration slope, calibration intercept, and Brier score. We also conducted a temporal validation by dividing the dataset into pre- and post-COVID-19 eras. To assess the added clinical value of our model, we compared it with a model based on the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) risk score, employing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, the C-index, continuous net reclassification improvement (cNRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA software (version 18).

Results

Incidence, causes and mortality of IHCA

From January 2018 to July 2023, 5121 STEMI patients who underwent successful PPCI were enrolled in our study (Fig. 1). Among them, 94 patients (1.8% [95% CI: 1.4%-2.1%]) experienced cardiac arrest during hospitalization. About 66% of the IHCA occurred within 24 h after PCI as shown in Figure S1A in the Supplemental material. Direct causes of IHCA were evaluated in Figure S1B in the Supplemental material. Among the 94 IHCA cases, 34 (36.2%) occurred with cardiogenic shock, 28 (29.8%) occurred with ventricular tachycardia, 18 (19.2%) occurred with mechanical complications (e.g. rupture of the ventricular free wall or ventricular septal defect), causes of 14 (14.8%) were unable to identify. Of all patients enrolled in our study, 65 patients died during hospitalization, among which the in-hospital mortality rate was 35.1% in patients with IHCA, and 0.6% in those without as shown in Figure S2 in the Supplemental material. The Kaplan-Meier curve for survival from in-hospital mortality was shown in Figure S1, as IHCA was associated with significantly higher in-hospital mortality (p < 0.001).

Feature selection

In the training cohort, the 25 potential prognosis-related variables were screened and dimensionally reduced by Lasso regression to avoid variables overfitting as shown in Fig. 2. The regularization parameter (λ) was set to 0.009, and variables with non-zero coefficients were retained for subsequent logistic regression model construction. Finally, four significant factors (age, SBP, Killip classification, and moderate to severe calcification), selected by Lasso regression, were assessed as independent predictors for IHCA in multivariate logistic regression models. The corresponding odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are presented in Table 2, and the coefficients are presented in Supplemental Table S3. These factors were further integrated to construct a prognostic nomogram, and the model was validated in the testing cohort as shown in Fig. 3.

Nomogram model validation and clinical application

The nomogram exhibited good accuracy for predicting IHCA in STEMI patients undergoing successful PPCI. The nomogram showed a C-index of 0.823 (95%CI 0.764–0.881) for predicting IHCA in the training set and a C-index of 0.828 (95%CI 0.751–0.905) in the testing set (Fig. 4A and B). The calibration curves of the nomogram for the prediction of IHCA demonstrated a good consistency between predicted and observed outcomes in the training and testing sets, as shown in Fig. 4C and D. Optimism-corrected estimates also indicated good performance of our model (Supplemental Table S4). Additionally, the decision curve analysis demonstrated that our prediction model provided a positive net clinical benefit, supporting its potential utility in guiding clinical management (Fig. 5A and B). The model maintained good discrimination in temporal validation (AUCs of 0.803 for the pre-COVID-19 era and 0.853 for the post-COVID-19 era) (Supplemental Figure S3-S4). Our model demonstrated better predictive accuracy and clinical applicability for identifying IHCA risk in STEMI patients after successful PPCI compared with the GRACE-based model (Supplemental Table S5 and Supplemental Figure S5-S6).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a nomogram model for predicting IHCA following PPCI in STEMI patients, utilizing variables that were selected by the Lasso regression model and statistically significant in multivariate logistic regression analysis. Our findings demonstrated that the C-index of the model was 0.828 in the testing set, indicating robust predictive accuracy and highlighting the significant predictive value of this nomogram model for predicting IHCA in patients after successful PPCI.

Cardiac arrest is a dreadful complication after STEMI and is associated with an increased risk of mortality [8]. IHCA, often viewed as a condition with a poor prognosis, could occur in around 2.2% of patients after STEMI, among which more than half of these patients would die during hospitalization [9]. Despite this poor prognosis, the evidence on IHCA is limited [10]. Our study demonstrated that IHCA in STEMI patients after successful PPCI was not common but remains a high risk of mortality. A retrospective cohort study showed that nearly 55% of cardiac arrests were caused by cardiogenic shock in STEMI patients after PCI, and 86% of cardiac arrest patients also experienced ventricular tachycardia [11]. Our results demonstrated that IHCA in more than a third of patients was caused by cardiogenic shock, whereas IHCA in nearly 30% of patients was caused by ventricular tachycardia. These results were also in line with the data from a multicenter study that showed that 37.7% of STEMI patients with IHCA had also experienced cardiogenic shock [12].

Currently, there is limited data on the predictor of IHCA in the PPCI era. It has been widely accepted that aging is one of the most predominant predictors of cardiac arrest, and our study identified and validated that aging was associated with a higher risk for IHCA [8]. A previous study showed that out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, cardiogenic shock, and left main coronary artery disease were associated with an increased risk of cardiac arrest in STEMI patients after PCI [11]. A cohort identified that moderate to severe chronic kidney disease was an independent predictor of adverse events during hospitalization in these patients [13]. Reduced LVEF has been recognized to be associated with an increased risk of cardiac arrest after myocardial infarction [14]. All relevant studies in this field primarily focused on out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. We also found that impaired cardiac function (Killip class) was an independent factor associated with a higher risk of IHCA.

Lesions responsible for STEMI are typically considered to be soft, friable, and not calcified [15]. However, data from previous studies showed the presence of moderate to severe calcified coronary lesions was associated with an increasing risk of mortality and stent thrombosis, posing big challenges during PCI in patients with acute coronary syndrome [16]. At 10 years after PCI with a new-generation drug-eluting stent, the adverse events after procedures increased by the degree of coronary calcification, and the presence of heavily calcified lesions was an independent risk factor of mortality [17]. Our study identified that moderate to severe coronary calcification was independently associated with IHCA. This might be explained by stent under-expansion and more prone to plaque instability, in-stent thrombosis, or delayed healing after stent implantation, exacerbating the severity of the STEMI. It has been widely recognized that intravascular imaging-guided PCIs including optical coherence tomography or intravascular ultrasound in calcified lesions achieve better procedural and clinical prognosis compared to angiography-guided PCI [18]. PCI strategies such as the use of cutting balloons, high-pressure post-dilatation balloons, and dedicated devices like rotational atherectomy have been refined to improve successful rate, but mostly applied in non-STEMI cases. The safety and efficacy of the application of these methods in STEMI patients require further studies [19].

Existing prediction models for adverse events in hospitalized patients primarily focus on predicting mortality or other comorbidities [5–7, 20, 21]. He et al. and Fang et al. constructed models for major adverse cardiovascular events exhibiting high predictive efficacy and feasibility [5, 7]. By integrating multiple clinical indicators into a multivariate regression model, the nomogram displays line segments of varying lengths, each representing the influence of individual risk factors, to estimate a patient’s probability of experiencing a cardiac arrest event. However, no model specifically targets in-hospital cardiac arrest. Previous studies have primarily identified individual risk factors associated with IHCA but have not developed comprehensive predictive models. Furthermore, many of these studies included redundant risk factors, making it challenging to construct a prediction model that is both practical and convenient for clinical application. This study addresses these gaps by refining relevant predictors, aiming to establish a streamlined and user-friendly model to effectively predict IHCA risk. To better individualize patient outcomes, we used Lasso regression to screen four risk factors most associated with IHCA: age, SBP, Killip classification, and moderate to severe calcification as significant predictors with coefficients that were not zero. The results demonstrate that our nomogram provides accurate prediction of IHCA in STEMI patients after successful PPCI. Specifically, this model may facilitate early risk stratification, enabling clinicians to identify high-risk patients who may benefit from intensive monitoring and management, including continuous telemetry, intensive care unit admission, early electrophysiology consultation, and closer echocardiographic surveillance for mechanical complications. The model may further support individualized decision-making and optimize resource allocation in clinical practice.

Limitation

Our study has several limitations. First, this study was observational and might be affected by uncalculated confounders. Several important data related to IHCA and patient prognosis were not recorded. In particular, stent thrombosis was not systematically recorded in our dataset, which may have led to an underestimation of the incidence of in-hospital cardiac arrest and could have influenced risk prediction. In addition, other variables GP IIb/IIIa inhibitor use, aspiration thrombectomy, symptom-to-FMC time, and mechanical circulatory support—were not recorded. Second, our study relied on admission data to adjust baseline characteristics and outcomes, which might be influenced by time-dependent variables, such as drug use. What’s more, due to the retrospective nature of our study, we were unable to further specify the cause of IHCA, therefore, the specific etiology of IHCA was unable to be identified. Third, the relatively small number of IHCA events may limit model robustness and external generalizability, despite the use of internal bootstrap correction. Our study performed only internal validation and lacked an independent external validation cohort. Further research using large-sample, multicenter data is needed to enhance the generalizability and clinical applicability of the model.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated age, SBP, Killip classification, and moderate to severe classification were independent factors for IHCA in STEMI patients with successful PPCI based on the Lasso-logistic regression model, and a nomogram model for the prediction of IHCA was constructed based on these factors. The validation showed the accurate and stable predictive performance of the nomogram, indicating a potential for early prediction of IHCA after successful PPCI in STEMI patients.
