Age‐Related Differences in Efficacy and Safety of Subcutaneous Immunotherapy in Allergic Rhinitis: A Real‐World Study
Authors: Jiaxin Jia, Xuan Yuan, Liyuan Liu, Shaobing Xie, Lai Meng, Wei Zhong, Hua Zhang, Weihong Jiang, Can Liao, Zhihai Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate age‐related differences in efficacy and safety of subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT) among patients with allergic rhinitis (AR).Study designRetrospective cohort study.SettingTertiary referral center.MethodsAR patients who completed a 3‐year course of dust mite SCIT with a 2‐year post‐SCIT follow‐up were categorized into pediatric and adult groups. Baseline characteristics, SCIT efficacy, and adverse reactions were compared between groups. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of SCIT efficacy and adverse reaction.Results889 patients were included, comprising 544 children and 345 adults. Adults exhibited higher baseline symptom burden, higher rates of former or current smoking and alcohol consumption, longer AR duration, more frequent dose adjustments during SCIT, and greater prevalence of comorbid asthma and urticaria. In contrast, children had higher frequencies of family history of allergy, monosensitization, food allergy, and secondary immunotherapy. Multivariable logistic regression confirmed that older age, particularly adult status, was an independent risk factor for reduced SCIT efficacy at both 1 and 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation, after adjusting for clinical confounders. Adverse reactions, including both local and systemic events, occurred more frequently in children, though the majority were mild and occurred during the maintenance phase. Notably, older or adult age was independently associated with a lower risk of SCIT‐related adverse reactions.ConclusionPediatric patients demonstrated superior short‐ and long‐term SCIT efficacy compared to adults, along with a higher incidence of adverse reactions. These results support age‐specific strategies to maximize clinical benefits and minimize risks in SCIT for AR.Keywords: adult, adverse reactions, allergic rhinitis, children, efficacy, subcutaneous immunotherapy

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a common chronic inflammatory disorder of the nasal mucosa, mediated by immunoglobulin E and triggered by exposure to airborne allergens such as house dust mites (HDM). 1 Affecting individuals across all age groups, AR is characterized by persistent symptoms, including nasal obstruction, rhinorrhea, sneezing, and nasal itching, that substantially impair quality of life and daily functioning. 2 With its global prevalence continuing to rise, there is an urgent clinical need for long‐term disease‐modifying strategies that go beyond symptomatic relief. 3

Allergen immunotherapy, encompassing both subcutaneous (SCIT) and sublingual routes, remains the only established intervention capable of altering the natural course of allergic diseases by inducing sustained immunologic tolerance. 4 Among these, SCIT has demonstrated superior efficacy in improving symptoms, reducing pharmacologic reliance, and mitigating disease progression, particularly in HDM‐sensitized patients. 5 , 6 However, its use is also associated with a higher frequency of adverse reactions, both local and systemic, necessitating individualized risk‐benefit evaluation. 7 Furthermore, considerable inter‐individual variability exists in treatment response, highlighting the need to identify patient‐related factors that predict clinical outcomes. 7 , 8 , 9

Age is an essential clinical and biological variable that may significantly influence the therapeutic outcomes and safety profile of allergen immunotherapy. Pediatric and adult patients differ not only in immunologic development and reactivity but also in disease history, allergen exposure, and lifestyle‐related cofactors such as smoking and alcohol use. 10 , 11 These distinctions can modulate immune responsiveness, trajectories of symptom resolution, and the risk profile for treatment‐related adverse events. 12 , 13 Despite these age‐related contrasts, current clinical guidelines offer limited age‐specific recommendations, and direct comparative data on SCIT performance in children versus adults remain scarce. 14 Most existing studies are constrained by small sample sizes, narrow age ranges, or short follow‐up durations, thereby limiting their generalizability to real‐world practice. 12 , 15 As SCIT continues to expand in clinical use, there is a pressing need to clarify whether and how age modifies both its clinical benefits and potential risks. 16 Robust, large‐scale investigations are essential to determine whether pediatric and adult patients require differentiated approaches to optimize outcomes and ensure safety during and after immunotherapy.

On this basis, we hypothesized that pediatric patients would demonstrate greater clinical efficacy but a higher incidence of adverse reactions to HDM‐specific SCIT compared with adults. To test this hypothesis, the present study undertakes a comprehensive investigation using a large, well‐characterized real‐world cohort. We systematically evaluated short‐term and long‐term treatment efficacy, sustained outcomes following SCIT discontinuation, and the full spectrum of local and systemic adverse reactions. Through detailed subgroup analyses and multivariate modeling, we aimed to clarify how age influences clinical response and safety, and to identify key predictors of SCIT success and risk. These insights may inform the development of age‐adapted immunotherapy strategies and enhance precision in the management of allergic airway disease.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This real‐world retrospective cohort study included AR patients who completed a full 3‐year course of HDM‐specific SCIT at Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, from January 2012 to January 2019, with a subsequent 2‐year follow‐up following SCIT. Participants were classified into pediatric and adult groups based on age at SCIT initiation, with pediatric patients defined as those aged ≥5 and <18 years, and adult patients as those aged ≥18 years. 15 Demographic and clinical information was extracted from electronic medical records. Smoking status was categorized as never, former (quit at least 12 months before SCIT without relapse), or current (daily smoking within 1 month prior to SCIT and continued during treatment) 5 . Secondary immunotherapy was defined as a repeated immunotherapy course following a previous incomplete HDM‐specific SCIT or SLIT regimen. 7 Sensitization patterns were categorized as monosensitization, defined as sensitization exclusively to Dermatophagoides farinae and/or Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, and polysensitization, defined as sensitization to HDM along with at least one other aeroallergen. 6 The study received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, and all participants or guardians provided written informed consent.

Eligibility criteria included: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of AR based on characteristic clinical presentation and a positive skin prick test (SPT) to Dermatophagoides farinae and/or Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus; (2) age of at least 5 years; (3) accept the standard HDM‐specific SCIT protocol; and (4) provision of written informed consent by the patient or a legal guardian. Patients were excluded if they had: (1) incomplete clinical records; (2) malignancy, psychiatric illness, or pregnancy; (3) severe asthma or significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities; (4) SCIT discontinuation or missing treatment documentation; (5) poor adherence to follow‐up; or (6) severe chronic rhinosinusitis or a history of nasal surgery.

SPT

SPT was performed following a standardized protocol. 9 All participants were instructed to discontinue antihistamines for at least 72 hours prior to testing. The panel of tested aeroallergens included HDM (Dermatophagoides farinae and Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus), cockroach, mixed grass, tree pollen, ragweed, mugwort, cotton wool, feather, mould mix I, and animal dander. The skin index (SI) was calculated as the ratio of the allergen‐induced wheal diameter to the histamine‐induced wheal diameter. SPT responses were graded as follows: SI ≤ 25% (−), >25% to <50% (+), ≥50% to <100% (++), ≥100% to ≤200% (+++), and >200% (++++). 9 Only patients demonstrating a positive SPT reaction of grade ++ or higher to Dermatophagoides farinae and Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus were deemed eligible for HDM‐specific SCIT initiation.

SCIT Protocol

Participants underwent SCIT using a standardized HDM extract (Novo‐Helisen Depot; Allergopharma), containing Dermatophagoides farinae and Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus in equal proportions; no other allergen extracts were administered. 5 Treatment included an initial treatment phase and a maintenance treatment phase. The initial phase involved gradual dose escalation at 7 to 14 days intervals, starting with vial No. 1 and progressing to the highest tolerated dose from vial No. 3. During the maintenance phase, the target dose from vial No. 3 was administered at intervals of 4 to 6 weeks. SCIT was administered on an outpatient basis under supervision by trained otolaryngology physicians. Injections were preceded by routine evaluation for adverse reactions or acute respiratory symptoms. Dose adjustments were made according to individual tolerability following institutional protocols. 17 Postinjection observation lasted at least 30 minutes to monitor for immediate reactions.

Follow‐Up and Efficacy Assessment

All included patients underwent structured follow‐up over a total period of 5 years, including monthly evaluations throughout the SCIT phase and annual evaluations during the post‐SCIT phase. Symptom burden and medication use were assessed using 4 validated instruments: the Total Nasal Symptom Score (TNSS), 18 Symptom and Medication Score (SMS), 16 Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RQLQ), 19 and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). 20 To comprehensively capture both short‐term efficacy and long‐term therapeutic durability, SCIT efficacy were systematically analyzed at 5 predefined timepoints: baseline prior to SCIT initiation (T0), after 1 year of SCIT (T1), after 2 years of SCIT (T2), at the end of the full 3‐year SCIT course (T3), and 2 years following SCIT discontinuation (T5). These timepoints were chosen to represent key stages of SCIT progression and to facilitate a longitudinal comparison of SCIT response dynamics across age groups.

AR severity was classified based on TNSS score, with >0 to ≤4 indicating mild and >4 to ≤12 indicating moderate‐to‐severe. 21 SCIT was deemed effective if the SMS demonstrated a reduction of more than 30% from baseline following SCIT; otherwise, the outcome was classified as ineffective. 22

Adverse Events Monitoring and Classification

All patients were monitored for at least 30 minutes after each injection. Adverse reactions were classified using the World Allergy Organization grading system. Local reactions (LRs) were defined as erythema, swelling, or itching at the injection site and graded based on wheal diameter. Systemic reactions (SRs) were defined as symptoms occurring beyond the injection site and were graded from 0 to 5. Each reaction was recorded in detail regarding timing, severity, and management.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the chi‐square test. Continuous data were reported as means ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables or as medians with interquartile ranges for skewed distributions. Group comparisons were conducted using Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Changes in symptom scores over time were assessed using linear mixed‐effects models. Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify independent predictors of SCIT efficacy and adverse events, with unadjusted and adjusted models. Covariates included in the multivariable models were selected based on clinical relevance and previous literature. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided P < .05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Baseline Clinical Characteristics of Children and Adult AR Patients

A total of 889 AR patients were enrolled in this study after eligibility screening, comprising 544 pediatric patients with a mean age of 10.0 years and 345 adult patients with a mean age of 30.5 years. Adult patients showed a greater prevalence of former and current smoking, along with higher alcohol consumption, longer AR duration, and increased comorbidities such as asthma and urticaria (P < .05, Table 1). In contrast, children demonstrated a higher prevalence of family history of allergy, monosensitization, secondary immunotherapy, and food allergy (P < .05, Table 1). Baseline symptom assessments revealed that adults had significantly higher TNSS, SMS, VAS, and RQLQ scores than children, indicating a greater disease burden at treatment initiation (P < .05, Table 1). Moreover, adults underwent more frequent dose modifications during SCIT, reflecting increased complexity in clinical management (P < .05, Table 1). No significant differences were observed in other demographic or clinical variables between the 2 groups (P > .05, Table 1).

Demographic Data and Clinical Characteristics of Pediatric and Adult Patients

Comparison of SCIT Efficacy Between Pediatric and Adult Patients

As shown in Figure 1A, adult patients consistently exhibited significantly higher TNSS, SMS, VAS, and RQLQ scores than children at year 1, year 2, year 3, and 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation (P < .05), indicating a persistently heavier symptom burden. In contrast, children demonstrated significantly greater symptom improvements from baseline at year 1, year 3, and 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation across all symptom scales (P < 0.05; Figure 1B‐D), suggesting a more robust and durable clinical response. According to the predefined criteria for SCIT efficacy, 387 (71.1%) children and 215 (62.3%) adults achieved effective SCIT at year 1 (Figure 1E). By year 3, the number of effective cases increased to 459 (84.4%) in the pediatric group and 258 (74.8%) in the adult group (Figure 1F). Importantly, 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation, a substantial proportion of patients continued to meet the efficacy threshold, with 443 (81.4%) children and 244 (70.7%) adults maintaining effective treatment (Figure 1G). Children exhibited significantly higher effective rates than adults at year 1, year 3, and 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation (P < .05; Figure 1E‐G), reflecting a more favorable trajectory and sustained treatment success.

Adult Age as an Independent Predictor of Reduced Short‐ and Long‐Term SCIT Efficacy

To identify determinants of short‐term and long‐term SCIT efficacy, patients were stratified into effective and ineffective groups based on treatment response at 1 year and at 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation. As shown in Figure 2A, multivariate logistic regression analyses revealed that older age, former or current smoking, overweight or obesity, longer AR duration, polysensitization, comorbid asthma, and more frequent dose modifications were significantly associated with reduced SCIT efficacy at year 1 (P < .05). In contrast, female sex and higher baseline TNSS scores were identified as protective predictors of favorable treatment response (P < .05; Figure 2A). Similarly, at 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation, older age, current smoking, obesity, longer AR duration, polysensitization, comorbid asthma, and more frequent dose modifications remained significant negative predictors of long‐term SCIT efficacy (P < .05; Figure 2B).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identifying independent predictors of SCIT efficacy. (A) 1 year of SCIT; (B) 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation. SCIT, subcutaneous immunotherapy.

To further explore the independent role of age in SCIT efficacy, we dichotomized patients into children and adults and performed both unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression analyses. As shown in Table 2, even after adjusting for potential confounders including sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, AR duration, family history of allergy, secondary immunotherapy, monosensitization, baseline TNSS, no. of dose modification, and comorbid conditions, adult patients consistently exhibited significantly lower odds of SCIT efficacy at both 1 year and 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation compared to children (P < .05). These findings establish adult age as a robust and independent risk factor for reduced SCIT efficacy.

Increased Incidence of LRs and SRs Among Pediatric Patients

We next compared the incidence and characteristics of adverse reactions during SCIT between pediatric and adult patients. As shown in Table 3, over the 3‐year SCIT period, children received 31,994 injections and adults received 20,598 injections. A total of 903 (2.8%) LRs, and 362 (1.1%) SRs were observed in the pediatric group, while adults experienced 431 (2.1%) LRs and 61 (0.3%) SRs. Both LRs and SRs occurred at significantly higher rates in children than in adults (P < .05; Table 3). Most LRs and SRs in both groups occurred during the maintenance phase and within 30 minutes of injection. Notably, the proportion of LRs occurring within 30 minutes was significantly higher in children (93.8% vs 85.6%, P < .05), suggesting greater early‐phase reactivity. The majority of LRs measured 5 to 20 mm in diameter, and most SRs were classified as grade 1 in grade. No significant differences were found between the 2 groups regarding the phase or grade distribution of LRs, or the phase, timing, or grade of SRs (P > .05).

Independent Protective Role of Adult Age Against SCIT‐Related Adverse Reactions

To further explore the determinants of adverse events during SCIT, multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted by stratifying patients based on the occurrence of LRs and SRs. As shown in Figure 3A, female sex, overweight or obesity, polysensitization, comorbid asthma, and more frequent dose modifications were all significantly associated with an increased risk of LRs (P < .05). In contrast, older age was found to be a protective factor against LRs (P < .05). Similarly, Figure 3B illustrates that obesity, asthma, and more frequent dose modifications were significantly associated with a greater risk of SRs, while older age remained a protective predictor (P < .05).

To further evaluate the independent contribution of age to SCIT‐related adverse events, patients were dichotomized into children and adults, and unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models were applied (Table 4). After controlling for potential confounders including sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, AR duration, family history of allergy, secondary immunotherapy, monosensitization, baseline TNSS, number of dose modification, and comorbid conditions, adult age remained significantly and independently associated with a lower likelihood of both LRs and SRs (P < .05). These findings suggest that adults are inherently less susceptible to SCIT‐induced immunological reactivity compared to children, further supporting age‐based risk stratification and tailored safety monitoring during SCIT.

Discussion

This study represents one of the most comprehensive real‐world analyses comparing the efficacy and safety of standardized HDM‐specific SCIT between pediatric and adult AR patients. Leveraging a large, well‐characterized cohort with extended post‐SCIT follow‐up, we systematically assessed both short‐ and long‐term therapeutic responses alongside the complete spectrum of LRs and SRs. By integrating multidimensional outcome assessments at predefined intervals, this study provides important evidence on how age‐related clinical and immunologic factors shape treatment trajectories. Notably, our work extends existing literature by incorporating an unusually long follow‐up window (3 years of SCIT plus 2 years postdiscontinuation), conducting injection‐level and phase‐specific safety analyses across more than 50,000 injections, and applying rigorous adjustment for major confounders within a uniform HDM‐only protocol. These methodological strengths enable a more definitive characterization of age as an independent determinant of both efficacy and safety, thereby addressing a critical evidence gap that previous studies have left unresolved.

Our findings demonstrate that pediatric patients experienced significantly greater clinical improvement and higher SCIT efficacy throughout treatment and at 2 years post‐SCIT discontinuation compared to their adult counterparts. This age‐dependent disparity remained significant after adjustment for baseline confounders, underscoring the independent role of age in determining SCIT efficacy. These observations align with previous studies suggesting that younger patients may benefit from greater immunologic plasticity and earlier disease intervention, thereby facilitating more effective tolerance induction. 15 , 23 , 24 Mechanistically, pediatric patients have been shown to exhibit enhanced antigen‐specific T‐cell responses, higher regulatory T cell ratios, and more favorable dendritic cell profiles during allergen immunotherapy, contributing to efficient immune deviation and suppression of Th2‐driven inflammation. 25 , 26

In contrast, adults demonstrated attenuated symptom improvement and lower SCIT efficacy, even after long‐term treatment. Several factors may contribute to this disparity. Age‐related immune remodeling, characterized by immunosenescence and chronic low‐grade inflammation, can impair immune tolerance development. 27 , 28 In addition, chronic allergen exposure and irreversible airway remodeling in adults may alter the immunologic milieu and reduce responsiveness to therapy. 29 Notably, adults in our cohort exhibited longer AR duration, greater exposure to smoking and alcohol, and higher rates of comorbidities such as asthma and urticaria, all of which have been independently associated with suboptimal SCIT efficacy. Importantly, adult age remained a robust independent negative predictor of SCIT efficacy even after multivariate adjustment, suggesting that age exerts a distinct biological influence beyond clinical covariates.

From a safety perspective, children experienced a significantly higher incidence of both LRs and SRs. Although the majority of these events were mild and occurred within 30 minutes postinjection, the frequency of adverse reactions was consistently higher in the pediatric group. This pattern may reflect heightened immune reactivity in children, potentially driven by increased mast cell density, vascular permeability, or lower activation thresholds. 30 , 31 These findings are consistent with our prior work and other studies indicating that younger age is a risk factor for hypersensitivity reactions during SCIT, possibly due to more pronounced Th2 polarization. 7 , 8 , 32 , 33 Additionally, higher rates of monosensitization and limited exposure to modulating factors such as tobacco or occupational allergens may render children both more immunologically reactive and more responsive to therapy. 5 , 34 , 35 Conversely, older age was independently associated with reduced risk of adverse events, suggesting that immunosenescence may attenuate hypersensitivity while concurrently limiting therapeutic benefit.

In light of these findings, our study provides evidence that may help address the current gap in clinical guidelines, which offer limited age‐specific recommendations for allergen immunotherapy. 1 , 3 , 36 The observation that children tended to achieve greater efficacy but also experienced more frequent adverse reactions, whereas adults showed comparatively attenuated efficacy but fewer reactions, suggests that age could be an important factor in balancing therapeutic benefit and safety. These results support the consideration of age‐adapted approaches in clinical practice‐for example, enhanced monitoring and safety measures in children, and early intervention with careful comorbidity management in adults. While not definitive, such real‐world data may contribute to the evidence base needed for future updates of practice guidelines and more individualized decision‐making in allergen immunotherapy.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the retrospective, single‐center design may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of results. Second, although comprehensive multivariable analyses were performed, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded, and the substantial baseline differences between children and adults mean that age‐related effects should be interpreted with caution. Third, as the study focused exclusively on HDM‐specific SCIT, findings may not be directly applicable to patients sensitized to other aeroallergens. Moreover, despite efficacy was defined using the SMS, which integrates rescue medication use, the influence of concomitant pharmacotherapy cannot be entirely excluded. In addition, because patients who did not complete the 5‐year follow‐up were excluded, the possibility of attrition bias cannot be ruled out. Finally, the absence of immune biomarker data further limits mechanistic interpretation of age‐related differences. Future prospective, multicenter studies incorporating diverse allergen targets and integrated immunophenotyping are warranted to validate these observations and refine age‐adapted immunotherapy protocols.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling real‐world evidence that patient age plays a pivotal role in shaping both the clinical efficacy and safety profile of HDM‐specific SCIT for AR. Pediatric patients exhibited more pronounced and durable symptom improvement compared to adults, albeit with a higher incidence of mostly mild adverse reactions. This may reflect age‐related immune remodeling and a more complex comorbidity landscape. These findings underscore the necessity of incorporating age‐related considerations into immunotherapy decision‐making. Tailoring SCIT strategies based on age may enhance therapeutic effectiveness, mitigate risk, and support more personalized, precision‐guided allergy care.
