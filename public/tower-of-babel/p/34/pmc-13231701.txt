Adjunctive effect of gaseous ozone on inflammatory biomarkers and microbial profile in grade C molar-incisor pattern periodontitis: a randomized split-mouth placebo-controlled clinical trial
Authors: Deniz Ozbay Cetiner, Burcu Karaduman, Ayse Ege Selman, Sevim Gonen, Ahu Uraz Corekci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis randomized, split-mouth study aimed to evaluate the effects of adjunctive gaseous ozone therapy versus nonsurgical periodontal treatment (NSPT) alone on inflammatory cytokine levels in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF), periodontal pathogens, and clinical periodontal parameters in grade C periodontitis patients with a molar-incisor pattern (C/MIP).MethodsSeventeen (8 males and 9 females), aged 23–42 years (mean ± SD = 31.80 ± 5.26 years) systemically healthy C/MIP patients were included in the study. After NSPT was performed on all patients, topical gaseous ozone was applied to the test quadrant. This randomized split-mouth clinical trial allocated contralateral sites to test or control groups by coin toss. Clinical parameters (Plaque index, gingival index, bleeding on probing, probing depth), total GCF levels of tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 (analyzed via ELISA), and the presence of major periodontal pathogens including Porphyromonas gingivalis, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Prevotella intermedia and Tannerella forsythia (quantified by qPCR) were evaluated at baseline, 1 month, and 3 months post-treatment. Statistical analyses included both parametric (Student’s t-test, Paired Samples t-test) and non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Spearman’s rank correlation test), depending on data distribution.ResultsBoth groups showed significant clinical improvement at 3 months, with no inter-group differences. However, the total amount of IL-8 in GCF levels significantly decreased after adjunctive ozone therapy (p = 0.008). A transient reduction in Prevotella intermedia was observed only in the control group. No significant microbial differences were found between groups at any time point.ConclusionsAdjunctive gaseous ozone therapy showed a significant reduction only in IL-8 levels, without additional clinical or microbiological benefits over NSPT alone in C/MIP patients.Trial registrationThe study has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with the registration number-first posted date: NCT05971706-2023–08-02.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08213-w.

Background

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease initiated by bacterial biofilm and exacerbated by the host’s immune response [1]. This interaction leads to the overproduction of inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines and chemokines, in the periodontal tissues and gingival crevicular fluid (GCF), ultimately causing the destruction of periodontal tissues and alveolar bone loss [2].

Grade C (high-risk) periodontitis with a molar-incisor pattern (C/MIP)-formerly known as localized aggressive periodontitis (LAgP)-represents a severe, rapidly progressing phenotype [3]. Distinguished by its early onset, familial aggregation, and occurrence in systemically healthy individuals, LAgP is further characterized by a hyperreactive host immune phenotype [4–6]. Despite minimal biofilm accumulation, this exaggerated immunoinflammatory response exacerbates tissue destruction, posing significant challenges for management [7, 8].

Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, and IL-8 are key pro-inflammatory cytokines involved in the pathogenesis of periodontal disease. TNF-α and IL-1β play crucial roles in the initiation and amplification of the inflammatory response by promoting the recruitment of immune cells and stimulating the expression of matrix-degrading enzymes [9–11]. IL-6 contributes to both local inflammation and systemic immune responses and has been associated with disease severity and progression [12]. IL-8 primarily acts as a chemoattractant for neutrophils, guiding their migration to sites of infection and inflammation [13]. Elevated levels of these cytokines in GCF have been strongly correlated with periodontal tissue destruction and alveolar bone loss [9, 14, 15].

Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (A. actinomycetemcomitans), Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis), Prevotella intermedia (P. intermedia), and Tannerella forsythia (T. forsythia) are key periodontopathogenic bacteria implicated in the initiation and progression of periodontal disease. These species possess various virulence factors—such as leukotoxins, proteolytic enzymes, and lipopolysaccharides—that enable them to evade host defenses and induce strong inflammatory responses [16] ommunity shifts favoring their overrepresentation, together with dysregulated host responses, are linked to the clinical manifestations of periodontitis [17].

The main objective of periodontal therapy is to control or reduce pathogenic bacteria and inflammation while improving clinical outcomes such as probing depth (PD) and promoting overall periodontal health. Nonsurgical periodontal treatment (NSPT) remains the cornerstone of periodontal therapy. While NSPT is effective in reducing microbial load and clinical inflammation, its efficacy is often limited in anatomically challenging areas, such as furcation regions and deep infrabony defects [18]. Moreover, in patients with C/MIP, an exaggerated sensitivity to bacterial stimuli limits the effectiveness of both pathogen elimination and modulation of the immune response. It is widely accepted that mechanical debridement serves as the primary therapeutic approach for reducing the subgingival bacterial load. However, to address its limitations, adjunctive therapies, including topical antiseptics, local or systemic antibiotics, laser applications, and host response modulation therapies, have been investigated in previous studies [18]. Nevertheless, inconsistent findings and the absence of a clear consensus regarding the most effective adjunctive strategies highlight the necessity for further research into novel therapeutic approaches especially in high-risk patients [18, 19].

Among adjunctive therapies, ozone therapy (OT) has emerged as a promising adjunctive modality in NSPT due to its potent antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties [20, 21]. It has been demonstrated that gaseous ozone improves periodontal repair by promoting the proliferation of immunocompetent cells and boosting immunoglobulin synthesis [22]. Additionally, it promotes the production of biologically active compounds that are essential for reducing inflammation and promoting wound healing, including prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and interleukins [23].

The effectiveness of OT in NSPT is not consistently proven by literature. Some studies have demonstrated statistically and clinically significant improvements with ozone application [24, 25], while others have failed to show any additional benefit over NSPT alone [23]. Despite its potential therapeutic benefits, there is currently no biochemical or microbiological evidence to support the effectiveness of adjunctive OT especially in patients with C/MIP. Therefore, the current study aimed to evaluate the impact of gaseous ozone application, as an adjuvant to NSPT, on the levels of inflammatory cytokines in GCF, periodontal pathogens, and clinical outcomes in patients with C/MIP. By comprehensively evaluating these outcomes, the study sought to elucidate the potential of OT to enhance the effectiveness of NSPT in this challenging condition. The null hypothesis of this study was that OT would have no significant effect on periodontal parameters, microbial load and biomarker levels in C/MIP patients.

Methods

This randomized, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind, split‐mouth study was approved by the Biruni University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (protocol no: 2015-KAEK-79–23-01, approval date: 27 September 2023) and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration [26]. This trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05971706, August 2023), prior to obtaining ethical approval (27 September 2023) and before patient recruitment commenced in October 2023. All eligible participants received complete details about the nature, potential risks and advantages of taking part in the study during the recruitment phase, which was held from October 2023 to June 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the GCF levels of IL-8 at 3rd month after NSPT, as reported in a previous study [23]. Sixteen participants in each group were required to detect a difference with a power of 90% and an α error of 0.05. Seventeen patients were included for any possible dropout during the study.

Study population

Seventeen individuals (8 males and 9 females), aged 23–42 years (mean ± SD = 31.80 ± 5.26 years) clinically and radiographically diagnosed with C/MIP were enrolled in the study [27]. Participants were recruited from the Department of Periodontology at Biruni University, Faculty of Dentistry, in Istanbul, Turkiye.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) individuals who self-reported as systemically healthy; 2) individuals who had not received NSPT within six months of the enrollment date; 3) those who had not used antibiotic or anti-inflammatory medications within the six months prior to treatment; 4) no current smokers or vapers (former smokers with a cessation period of ≥ 12 months were eligible); 5) those who were neither lactating or pregnant; 6) patients with the diagnosis of C/MIP, which is characterized by the involvement of at least two sites (incisor and/or first molar) and no more than two additional teeth beyond the incisors and first molars, with PD ≥ 5 mm, attachment loss, and a minimum of 2 mm bone loss observed on radiographs; and 7) patients with PD ≥ 5 mm and at least 2 mm bone loss in first molars or incisors in both contralateral quadrants 8) those who did not have any contraindications for NSPT or ozone application. Participants who had fewer than 20 teeth, wore partial dentures, or had fixed prosthodontics were ineligible for the study.

Study design

The study employed a split-mouth design, in which the contralateral quadrants of each patient with similar periodontal status were randomly allocated to one of the two treatment modalities by an experienced investigator (D.O.C.) who neither collected data nor performed the procedures, using a coin toss method for randomization. The control quadrants (n = 17) received NSPT alone, whereas the test quadrant (n = 17) received NSPT followed by adjunctive OT.

The study schedule is shown in Fig. 1. The sampling sites were selected following the initial screening process and the completion of clinical periodontal measurements. One day later, samples of subgingival plaque and GCF were obtained. Periodontal clinical measurements

A number of measurements were made, including plaque index (PI) [28], gingival index (GI) [29], bleeding on probing (BoP), and PD. All clinical parameters were measured at six sites per tooth (mesio-, mid-, disto-buccal, mesio-, mid-, and disto-palatal), with the exception of the third molars, using a Williams periodontal probe (Nordent Manufacturing Inc., Elk Grove Vilage, IL, USA). One calibrated and experienced examiner (B.K.), who was blind to the treatment assignments, performed the periodontal clinical examinations. The inter-examiner reproducibility was ensured by repeating the PD measurements at six sites per tooth in five periodontitis patients not included in the study, with a 48-h interval between assessments. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated as 0.997 (0.990–0.999), indicating excellent intra-examiner reproducibility.

Sampling processes

GCF and subgingival plaque were collected from three periodontal pockets in molar or incisor teeth with PD ≥ 5 mm and BoP, with radiographic evidence of bone loss in both the test and control quadrants.

GCF sampling

The sample sites were prepared by isolating the sites with cotton rolls to prevent contamination from saliva, carefully removing the supragingival plaque with curettes, and gently air-drying the sites. To collect GCF, standardized sterile paper strips (Periopaper, ProFlow Inc., Amityville, NY, USA) were inserted into the crevice with minimum resistance and left in place for 30 s. The strips with blood or saliva contamination were discarded. The amount of fluid in each strip was measured using an electronic device (Periotron 6000, Proflow Inc., Amityville, NY, USA). A standard curve was used to convert measurements from electronic devices into actual volumes (µL). The samples from each quadrant, comprising six paper strips, were then transferred into sterile microtubes and stored at −80 °C until subsequent analysis.

Subgingival plaque sampling

Subgingival plaque samples were collected from the same sites as the GCF samples, immediately following GCF sampling. For each site, two standard sterile paper points (No. 30) were used, one parallel to the tooth axis and the other at a 45° angle. Both paper points were left in place for 30 s before being transferred to screw-capped sterile vials. Prior to microbiological analysis, the samples were stored at −80 °C.

Nonsurgical Periodontal Treatment (NSPT)

NSPT was administered in two sessions. The first session involved full mouth supragingival professional mechanical plaque removal (PMPR), followed by full mouth subgingival PMPR one week later. The initial treatment also included an oral health evaluation and patient education on proper toothbrushing and interdental cleaning techniques. PMPR was performed on all patients by a single blinded researcher (A.E.S.), who was unaware of the test and control quadrants, using both hand instruments (Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA) and ultrasonic devices (EMS Minimaster, Nyon, Switzerland) for supragingival and subgingival instrumentation. NSPT was not accompanied by any medications such as antibiotics or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. The patients were instructed to maintain their self-performed oral hygiene throughout the study.

Ozone Therapy (OT)

Following subgingival PMPR, OT was immediately administered on the test quadrant and was repeated 3 times within a one-week period, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. The procedure was performed using an ozone-generating device (Ozone DTA Ozone Generator, Denta Tec Dental AS, Norway) equipped with a periodontal tip (PA Probe, Ozone Generator, Denta Tec Dental AS, Norway). An experienced investigator (A.U.C.) carried out the OT by positioning the periodontal tip 1 mm apical to the pocket depth, targeting only periodontal pockets with a depth of at least 5 mm. Ozone was applied for 30 s at a fixed concentration of 2.100 ppm (80% oxygen) via a connected handpiece. On the control side, the probe was similarly positioned for placebo ozone applications; however, the ozone generator remained inactive to ensure patient’s blinding.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) analysis

Each tube containing the sample strips was eluted with 250 μl Hank’s buffer containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin (Sigma, St Louis, MO, USA.) by centrifuging (2000 × g, 4 °C) for 5 min. To identify each cytokine, the supernatants were separated into four aliquots after the strips were removed. Following the manufacturer's instructions, the levels of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) -α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 were measured using ELISA (RayBiotech Inc., Norcross, GA, USA). The optical density was measured at 450 nm after color development was completed. Concentrations of cytokines were measured in pg/μL, and the total amounts (pg) of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 in each sample was calculated by multiplying the concentration of each cytokine by the sample volume. Detection limits for the ELISA kits (RayBiotech, Norcross, GA, USA) were as follows: IL-1β: 0.3–100 pg/mL; IL-6: 3–1000 pg/mL; IL-8: 1–600 pg/mL; TNF-α: 30–6000 pg/mL. Values below these detection thresholds were recorded as zero for statistical analyses.

Microbiological analysis

Quantitative-Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) was employed in microbiological analysis focused on A. actinomycetemcomitans ATCC 43718, P. gingivalis ATCC 33277, P. intermedia ATCC25611, and T. forsythia ATCC 43037 and total bacterial amount. The syber green based assay (SYBR) technique was used to evaluate each sample. DNA extraction was performed with Insta-Gene Matrix Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA.) and quantified using a DNA high-sensitivity kit on a Qubit Fluorometer 3 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Detection was carried out using the MESA Blue PCR Master Mix (Eurogentec, Belgium) for SYBR assay on a Bio-Rad CFX96 instrument (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Bacterial loads were quantified by qPCR using a plasmid-based standard curve of the 16S rRNA gene, and results were expressed as log10 copies per sample.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were carried out using the IBM Statistical Product and Service Solutions (IBM SPSS Statistics) software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA.). The normality of the distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Mann–Whitney U test was used for inter-group comparisons. Intra-group differences across time points were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The relationships between variables were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation. A significance level of 0.05 was established for all statistical tests. To mitigate the risk of Type I errors, Bonferroni correction was applied to all potential multiple comparisons.

Results

All patients successfully completed the study, with no reported adverse effects attributable to OT. Furthermore, no postoperative complications such as infections, abscesses, or suppuration were observed in any of the subjects.

No significant differences were observed between the groups in GCF cytokine concentrations at baseline (p > 0.05). Although cytokine concentrations decreased in both groups following treatment, this reduction was not statistically significant at any time point (p > 0.05) (Table S1).

The data revealed no significant differences in the total amounts of all cytokines at baseline (p > 0.05). The total amount of GCF IL-8 significantly decreased at the third month post-treatment compared to baseline only in the test group (p = 0.01). However, no significant changes were observed in the total amounts of TNF-α, IL-1β, or IL-6 over time in either the control or test group (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Microbiologic findings

Although baseline P. intermedia levels did not differ significantly between the two groups (p > 0.05), a significant reduction was observed only in the control group at 1- and 3-months following treatment (p = 0.05, p = 0.04, respectively). No significant differences were observed between or within groups in P. gingivalis, A. actinomycetemcomitans, or T. forsythia levels over time (p > 0.05) (Table 3). Discussion

Periodontitis is a multifactorial inflammatory disease characterized by microbial-host response interactions. The primary goal of NSPT is to eliminate the pathogenic bacteria responsible for the initiation of the disease and to manage the resulting inflammation.

Numerous studies have investigated the application of OT in periodontal diseases [23, 24, 30, 31]. However, evidence remains limited regarding its clinical application as an adjunctive treatment option for C/MIP patients. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first split-mouth randomized controlled study to evaluate the adjunctive effect of OT with NSPT in C/MIP patients.

The results showed that periodontal clinical parameters improved in both groups after treatment, with no significant difference between the groups during the follow-up periods. Moreover, both NSPT with or without OT resulted in a decrease in PD at 3 months post-treatment. Effective plaque and calculus removal, along with healing, were achieved through conventional NSPT in both groups, consistent with previous studies and well-documented findings [25, 32–34]. Decreases in PI, GI and BoP scores in both quadrants are not surprising, likely to reflect the improvements in oral hygiene and inflammation control. However, no statistically significances were noted between groups over period of time that failed to show any additional benefit of OT, aligns with the findings of previous studies [23].

C/MIP is characterized by rapid progression and early periodontal destruction, indicating that host susceptibility, in addition to microbial factors, contributes significantly to disease severity [27, 34–36]. While clinical parameters describe current periodontal status, they provide limited insight into host–microbe interactions. For this reason, analyzing host-derived biomarkers in GCF is valuable, particularly when total amounts are considered, as they more comprehensively reflect overall changes in biomarker production compared to concentrations [37–39]. Accordingly, in the present study, comparisons were based on total amount values.

In the present study, no significant differences were observed in the total GCF amounts of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in both groups compared to baseline. However, a decrease in the total GCF amount of IL-8 was noted in the test group 3 months after treatment. The fact that no significant change was observed in the total GCF amounts of TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 may indicate that these mediators are more resistant to treatment or that chronic inflammation may persist. This suggests that the role of these mediators may change in the long term after treatment and that more time may be needed for a significant decline. Despite improvements in clinical indicators and microbiological alterations, Rosalem et al. [40] discovered that IL-1β and IL-8 from shallow/deep sites in AgP patients remained unaltered 3 months after treatment. In a study by Radvar et al. [41] it is observed that although NSPT was clinically successful, it did not result in a change of unstimulated or LPS-stimulated IL-6 production by monocytes in AgP. Similarly, the lack of change in IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β levels in our study may suggest that the role of these cytokines in the pathogenesis of periodontal disease is influenced not only by clinical manifestations but also by the more complex interactions of immune responses. According to the results of the present study, adjunctive OT did not affect these cytokines. Although this finding aligns with existing literature, it raises questions about the effectiveness of devices that generate ozone at varying intensities [25]. The decrease in IL-8 and its continued significant positive correlation with T. forsythia observed only in the test group may suggest a transient immunomodulatory effect of OT. While this could indicate a potential advantage over conventional treatment, such an interpretation should be made with caution, as the clinical relevance of this finding remains uncertain. Moreover, it should be emphasized that the 3-month follow-up may be insufficient to determine whether these immunological changes translate into long-term clinical improvement. Branco-de-Almeida et al. [42] reported that cytokines in the healthy and affected regions of LAgP patients affect each other, suggesting a cross-regulation mechanism. Following treatment, inflammatory mediators engage in dynamic mutual regulation, with the relationships between biomarkers undergoing continuous changes. This indicates even when the disease appears clinically stable, an active process of reconfiguration continues at the biochemical level. The lack of significant change in TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels after treatment may be attributed to their potential maintenance at a balanced level within the cross-regulation mechanism. C/MIP periodontitis, when diagnosed in advanced stages with significant bone destruction, requires a complex treatment approach. Furthermore, to ensure pocket healing, regeneration procedures might be required. NSPT, with or without OT, may not have been effective enough in reducing the proinflammatory response in C/MIP periodontitis, which is characterized by deep and narrow pockets. On the other hand, a study that assessed the efficacy of topical OT following surgical regenerative treatment reported no benefits of additional OT [30]. This finding suggests that the effectiveness of OT is limited.

Moreover, the observed reduction in IL-8 levels in GCF at the third month, in correlation with microbiological data, highlights the potential anti-inflammatory effects of adjunctive OT. IL-8 plays a pivotal role in the recruitment and activation of neutrophils at sites of inflammation, and its elevated levels have been associated with the progression of periodontal disease [43]. Therefore, a decrease in IL-8 concentration in the test group may reflect a reduction in inflammatory burden following adjunctive OT. Nevertheless, cytokine responses are highly dynamic and may fluctuate significantly in the early stages of periodontal healing and it is essential to consider the temporal dynamics of cytokine responses during healing. Sampling IL-8 and other pro-inflammatory cytokines at earlier time intervals—such as days 7, 14, and 21—might provide deeper insight into the biological mechanisms underlying ozone mediated therapies [44, 45]. This could uncover transient changes that might not be apparent at later time points.

In addition, the frequency and duration of ozone application may substantially influence its clinical efficacy. The present study employed a single application; however, it remains to be elucidated whether repeated administration—such as weekly applications within the first month or monthly maintenance doses—could enhance clinical and immunological outcomes. Future studies should explore different dosing regimens and delivery systems (e.g., gaseous ozone vs. ozonated water) to establish the most effective and sustainable therapeutic approach [46, 47].

Although there is still much to learn about the microbiota of grade C periodontitis, a growing amount of research is emphasizing the part that microorganisms play in disease initiation, progression, and treatment outcomes. An increased inflammatory response to lipopolysaccharide is a major immunological feature of young patients with grade C periodontitis; localized grade C patients exhibit greater levels of inflammatory markers than healthy individuals [6]. Moreover, the treatment responses in these patients may be shaped by their unique inflammatory profile [48]. Remarkably, compared to adults with periodontitis, people with generalized grade C periodontitis have a hyperresponsive monocyte phenotype, which is characterized by higher levels of lipopolysaccharide-stimulated TNF-α and IL-1β [49]. These findings indicate that NSPT can attenuate inflammatory responses to bacteria; however, patient-specific immune responses may be crucial in influencing the success of treatment [50].

Although there is a scarcity of studies addressing these aspects concurrently, it is difficult to examine host responses without considering the microbial modifications that occur after treatment in grade C periodontitis. Specific bacterial species, including Aggregatibacter and Tannerella, are typically found in increased densities in diseased areas, particularly those with generalized or localized grade C periodontitis. When young people with grade C periodontitis receive treatment, these bacterial species decrease and the taxa linked to periodontal health become more prevalent [51, 52].

Numerous studies have investigated the microbiology of C/MIP. However, advancements in microbiological techniques, changes in disease terminology, and evolving diagnostic criteria have resulted in a continuously expanding body of literature, making it challenging to draw definitive conclusions. In this study, although all microorganism levels decreased in both groups during the follow-up periods, a statistically significant difference was observed only in P. intermedia levels in the control group at months 1 and 3 compared to baseline. Conventional NSPT is widely regarded as an effective method for reducing microbial counts [53]. However, NSPT does not completely eliminate microorganisms from anatomically challenging areas, such as furcation regions and infrabony defects.

The high levels of A. actinomycetemcomitans in C/MIP diseased sites align with numerous earlier reports highlighting the strong association of this species with LAgP [54–56]. The persistence of A. actinomycetemcomitans, T. forsythia abundance after treatment is unexpected and may be attributed to their ability to invade epithelial cells and fibroblasts [57–59] Huth et al. [59], reported that gaseous ozone is more effective against bacteria growing in the planktonic state compared to biofilms and in the case of ozone, they concluded that the lower effectiveness against biofilm-associated bacteria may partly derive from the decomposition of ozone after contact with organic substances, like the extracellular matrix. In our study, it can be thought that microorganisms were organized in biofilm and this structure prevented the penetration of ozone. According to the present results, both NSPT alone and NSPT with OT were associated with reductions in P. intermedia at 1 and 3 months after treatment. Median values reached zero in both groups; however, these decrease did not reach statistical significance in test group, and no differences were observed between groups. This suggests that the reductions are primarily attributable to NSPT rather than adjunctive OT. While ecological shifts in the subgingival microbiota cannot be ruled out, the available data do not allow firm conclusions regarding mechanisms of recolonization, and such interpretations should be made with caution. In a study involving patients with chronic periodontitis, P. intermedia was the only species found to be sensitive to OT at the third-month follow-up [23]. highlighting possible differences due to methodology, patient population, or treatment protocol. Overall, the decreases observed in both groups underscore the efficacy of NSPT; however, the lack of statistically significant microbiological differences indicates that adjunctive OT did not confer additional benefit. Thus, while both groups demonstrated comparable clinical improvements, these did not translate into additional microbiological benefits with OT. Moreover, given that microbial dynamics are time-dependent, a longer follow-up period may be required to evaluate sustained ecological or immunological effects.

Although split-mouth studies offer several advantages, the previously discussed drawbacks—such as potential crossover or spill-over effects between test and control quadrants within the same patient—represent a significant limitation of this study design. Furthermore, the fact that the sampled teeth were not separately evaluated as single-rooted or multi-rooted is also a limitation of our study. A notable limitation of the present study is that a considerable proportion of cytokine measurements fell below the limit of detection, particularly for TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. Median values of 0.00 at several time points suggest a high frequency of non-detectable measurements, which may have reduced the sensitivity of the analyses to detect subtle biological differences between groups. Therefore, biomarker-related findings should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, the relatively small sample size (n = 17) inherently limits the generalizability of the results. In addition, while it is well-established that a three-month period is typically adequate for healing following NSPT, earlier follow-up could provide valuable insights into the changes in the analyzed biochemical and microbiological parameters over time.

Conclusion

This study indicates a selective effect of adjunctive OT, with a significant reduction observed only for IL-8 among the inflammatory biomarkers analyzed. Given the pivotal role of IL-8 in neutrophil recruitment and inflammatory cascades, this finding may indicate a transient immunomodulatory effect of OT, although its long-term clinical relevance remains uncertain. Importantly, NSPT alone remains the gold standard therapy, demonstrating consistent clinical and microbiological improvements irrespective of adjunctive measures. While adjunctive OT did not provide an additional antimicrobial advantage—particularly against P. intermedia—its selective impact on IL-8 suggests that specific biochemical pathways may be modulated. Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and optimized ozone delivery protocols are needed to clarify these effects. Briefly, our findings reinforce the effectiveness of NSPT as the cornerstone of periodontal therapy and contribute to a more detailed understanding of the limited but potentially targeted role of adjunctive OT in periodontal treatment.
