Optimizing antibiotic use for cesarean section in anticipation of the diagnosis-related groups payment system: a retrospective cohort study on the impact of clinical pharmacist intervention
Authors: Ting Chen, Ranran Wang, Fenfen Gu, Jinlian Zhang, Chao Li, Lei Jin, Lixia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn anticipation of the nationwide implementation of the Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRG) 2.0 payment system in China, scheduled to take effect in 2025, hospitals are proactively seeking strategies to enhance care quality while optimizing resource utilization. Perioperative antibiotic stewardship for cesarean sections represents a key focus for these preparatory initiatives.ObjectivesTo evaluate whether a structured clinical pharmacist-led intervention pathway, implemented in anticipation of the DRG payment reform, improves antibiotic appropriateness and reduces adverse outcomes and costs.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study involving 640 women who underwent cesarean section at a tertiary hospital. The control group (n = 314) received standard care (January–June 2024); the intervention group (n = 326) received care guided by a pharmacist-led pathway (July–December 2024), encompassing preoperative assessment, intraoperative support, and postoperative monitoring.ResultsThe intervention significantly increased the rate of rational antibiotic use (94.8% vs. 89.2%, P = 0.009) and reduced postoperative infection rates (22.7% vs. 31.8%, P < 0.01). Multivariate analysis identified pharmacist intervention as an independent protective factor against infection (OR = 0.642, 95% CI: 0.449–0.918, P = 0.015). Additionally, the intervention group exhibited significantly lower total hospitalization costs, drug costs, and antibiotic costs (all P < 0.01).ConclusionIn this real-world, before-and-after study, the implementation of a standardized clinical pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship pathway—anticipating China’s forthcoming DRG payment system— was associated with more rational antibiotic use, reduced postoperative infection rates, shorter hospital stays, and lower total costs among women undergoing cesarean sections. These findings underscore the potential value of integrating clinical pharmacists into perioperative care teams as a proactive, real-world strategy to enhance quality and efficiency in high-risk obstetric populations prior to the nationwide implementation of DRG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13756-026-01789-6.Keywords: Clinical pharmacist, Cesarean section, Antibiotic, Diagnosis-related groups, Pharmacist intervention

Introduction

The rational application of perioperative antibiotics in cesarean section is crucial for preventing postoperative infections, improving patient prognosis, and reducing medical costs. The risk of postoperative infection following cesarean section is influenced by multiple factors. Patient-related factors include age, obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, and other underlying conditions [1]. Obstetric factors involve prolonged premature rupture of membranes (PROM > 12 h), prolonged labor (> 24 h), and chorioamnionitis [2]. Additionally, surgery-related factors such as vertical skin incisions and prolonged operation duration, significantly increase the risk of surgical site infections (SSIs). Currently, there are numerous issues regarding the perioperative use of antibiotics in cesarean section, particularly concerning the inappropriate timing of prophylactic medication, excessively long treatment courses, and inappropriate selection of agents [3–5]. These medication practices not only increase the economic burden on patients but also may lead to serious consequences, such as a higher incidence of adverse drug reactions and exacerbated bacterial resistance.

Current authoritative guidelines establish clear standards for perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis. For cesarean delivery, it is recommended to administer a single preoperative dose of antibiotics, such as cefazolin (a first-generation cephalosporin), within 60 min prior to the skin incision, without routine continuation postoperatively in the absence of infection [6]. In cases of preterm prelabor rupture of membranes (PPROM), antenatal antibiotic therapy is indicated to prolong the latency period (i.e., the time from membrane rupture to delivery); however, this prophylactic course should not extend into the postpartum period unless clinical signs of infection are present [6–7]. The widespread deviations from these evidence-based recommendations underscore the urgent need for effective intervention strategies.

Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRGs) represent a case-mix-based payment and management model that classifies cases with similar clinical processes and comparable resource consumption into the same group, thereby achieving standardized management of medical services and cost control [8]. In recent years, China has been actively promoting the nationwide implementation of the DRG payment system, which is scheduled to take full effect in 2025. Healthcare institutions are proactively seeking strategies to demonstrate significant advantages in standardizing diagnosis and treatment behaviors, optimizing resource allocation, and improving medical quality [9]. Clinical pharmacists possess unique professional advantages in promoting the rational use of antibiotics [10–11]. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a structured intervention pathway led by clinical pharmacists—implemented in preparation for the upcoming DRG payment system—to improve the rational use of perioperative antibiotics during cesarean section. It systematically evaluates the effectiveness of this model in enhancing medication rationality and conducts an in-depth analysis of the implementation outcomes of pharmacist interventions. The goal is to provide theoretical foundations and practical guidance for integrating clinical pharmacists into institutional readiness efforts for DRG-based healthcare reforms. Importantly, this intervention was implemented prior to the activation of the DRG payment system at our institution, serving as a proactive measure to align clinical practice with the anticipated financial incentives for cost-effective, high-quality care.

Methods

Study design and setting

This single-center, retrospective cohort study employed a before-and-after design at Xinhua Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. All consecutive patients undergoing cesarean delivery between January and December 2024 were eligible. The control group (January–June 2024; n = 314) received standard antimicrobial management by obstetric and anesthesia teams, whereas the pharmacist intervention group (July–December 2024; n = 326) received care via a pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship pathway. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (XHEC-C-2025-005-1), adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Participants

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) elective or emergency cesarean delivery; (3) complete perioperative medical records.

Exclusion criteria: (1) concomitant non-obstetric surgery; (2) postoperative transfer to intensive care or voluntary discharge; (3) missing data on essential study variables.

Allocation was determined solely by surgical date relative to pathway implementation, with no patient-level selection. Flowchart of participant enrollment is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of participant enrollment. A total of 803 cesarean section cases were identified from January 1 to December 31, 2024. After excluding 27 cases with combined surgery, 12 transferred postoperatively, and 124 with incomplete data, 640 cases were enrolled. Patients were assigned to either the pre-intervention group (January–June 2024, n = 314) or the pharmacist intervention group (July–December 2024, n = 326). Emergency cesarean section was defined as an unplanned procedure performed within 24 h of admission; elective CS was scheduled prior to admission. Among the 640 participants, 79 (12.3%) underwent emergency CS and 561 (87.7%) had elective procedures. CS: cesarean section

Intervention

The pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship pathway (Fig. 2), developed in accordance with national guidelines [12–15], was implemented prospectively from July to December 2024. The participating pharmacists were full-time, licensed clinicians with a minimum of three years of direct patient care experience and formal training in either antimicrobial stewardship or obstetric pharmacy. Prior to the rollout, they completed a standardized two-hour training program that covered protocol application and case-based simulations to ensure adherence to the established protocols. While the final prescribing authority remained with physicians, pharmacists actively participated in multidisciplinary rounds and conducted daily reviews of prescriptions.

Pharmacist intervention pathway for antibiotic use in cesarean section. This supervision protocol delineates the pharmacist-led antimicrobial management pathway for patients undergoing cesarean sections. It encompasses a comprehensive preoperative risk assessment, meticulous intraoperative adherence verification, and a dynamic postoperative evaluation of both efficacy and safety. The protocol employs green boxes to signify points of pharmacist intervention, while diamonds indicate critical decision nodes within the process

The pathway consisted of three phases:

Preoperative

Assessment of infection risk, allergy history, renal/hepatic function, and verification of guideline-concordant prophylaxis (e.g., cefazolin within 60 min before incision).

Intraoperative

Monitoring of antibiotic timing, redosing if surgery > 3 h or blood loss > 1500 mL, and adverse reaction surveillance.

Postoperative

Daily evaluation of infection markers, assessment of antibiotic continuation beyond 24 h, and individualized recommendations (e.g., de-escalation, dose adjustment, or discontinuation). Beyond adherence to protocols, pharmacists actively participated as partners in infection surveillance. Through daily involvement in ward rounds and real-time reviews of vital signs, inflammatory markers, and culture results, they effectively differentiated true infectious complications from benign postoperative fever or inflammation.

For detailed components and decision criteria of the pharmacist-led intervention pathway for antimicrobial stewardship in cesarean delivery, please refer to Appendix S1. The monitoring process for antibiotic use across different infectious sites following cesarean section is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. All interventions were documented electronically, with a clinician acceptance rate of 97%.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

Rate of any clinically diagnosed postoperative infection during the index hospital stay (from cesarean delivery to discharge), as documented in electronic medical records by attending clinicians. All infection types were included (e.g., endometritis, urinary tract infection, surgical site infection, respiratory or gastrointestinal infections).

Secondary outcomes

(1) Rational antibiotic use; (2) total hospitalization cost (RMB); (3) length of hospital stay (days); (4) antibiotic-specific and total drug costs.

Costs reflect the total inpatient expenditure for the index admission, consistent with China’s DRG system, which consolidates payment for all conditions treated during that hospitalization.

Rational antibiotic use was defined using pre-specified, objective criteria based on national guidelines and institutional protocols [6–7, 12–13], assessed separately for two mutually exclusive scenarios:

(i)Perioperative prophylaxis: Rational only if all three criteria were met: (a) first- or second-generation cephalosporin; (b) administration within 60 min before incision; and (c) duration ≤ 24 h;

Perioperative prophylaxis: Rational only if all three criteria were met: (a) first- or second-generation cephalosporin; (b) administration within 60 min before incision; and (c) duration ≤ 24 h;

(ii)Therapeutic use: Rational only if all four criteria were fulfilled: (1) documented clinical indication (e.g., fever ≥ 38 °C, purulent discharge, uterine tenderness, or elevated CRP/WBC); (2) appropriate antibiotic selection based on likely pathogens and local resistance patterns; (3) correct dosing adjusted for maternal weight and renal function; and (4) duration aligned with infection severity (e.g., 5–7 days for endometritis; longer courses required specific justification). Any antibiotic use beyond 24 h without a documented indication was classified as irrational.

Therapeutic use: Rational only if all four criteria were fulfilled: (1) documented clinical indication (e.g., fever ≥ 38 °C, purulent discharge, uterine tenderness, or elevated CRP/WBC); (2) appropriate antibiotic selection based on likely pathogens and local resistance patterns; (3) correct dosing adjusted for maternal weight and renal function; and (4) duration aligned with infection severity (e.g., 5–7 days for endometritis; longer courses required specific justification). Any antibiotic use beyond 24 h without a documented indication was classified as irrational.

The rational use rate was calculated by dividing the number of patients whose antibiotic use (either prophylactic or therapeutic) met all pre-specified criteria by the total number of patients in each group.

The pathogen detection rate is defined as the proportion of patients with clinically suspected infections who have a positive microbiological culture result from any relevant clinical specimen, including wound swabs, blood cultures, urine cultures, vaginal swabs, or sputum samples.

Outcome definitions and assessment procedures

Infection diagnoses were made prospectively by attending clinicians using standardized institutional criteria. To ensure reproducibility, explicit diagnostic thresholds were applied.

Endometritis was defined as the presence of fever (temperature ≥ 38.0 °C after the first 24 postoperative hours) in conjunction with at least two of the following criteria: (1) uterine tenderness; (2) purulent or foul-smelling lochia; (3) elevated inflammatory markers (WBC > 12 × 10⁹/L or CRP elevation).

A Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) required the presence of compatible symptoms along with either: (i) a positive urine culture (≥ 10⁵ CFU/mL), or (ii) pyuria on urinalysis accompanied by clinical signs that justify antimicrobial treatment.

Respiratory, gastrointestinal, and other infections were diagnosed based on new-onset, compatible clinical presentations. Supportive testing (imaging and cultures) was obtained when indicated, although it was not mandatory, reflecting pragmatic clinical practice.

Microbiological confirmation was not required, which aligns with standard guidelines for the clinical diagnosis of postpartum endometritis and other acute infections where empirical therapy is the norm.

All outcome data—including infection diagnoses and antibiotic use assessments—were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records by two trained research coordinators blinded to group assignment. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or adjudication by a senior clinician, also blinded. The clinicians making the original diagnoses and treatment decisions were unaware of the study’s purpose or timeline.

Data collection

Data were extracted using a standardized case report form that included demographics (age, weight, parity), perioperative indicators (surgical duration, incision type, emergency status, and rupture of membranes), antibiotic details (agent name, dose, timing of the first dose, total duration, and indication for therapy), laboratory results (white blood cell count, C-reactive protein, and neutrophil count), medication costs (total drug cost and antibiotic cost), and clinical outcomes (postoperative infection, length of stay, and neonatal outcomes). As our institution serves as a regional tertiary referral center for high-risk obstetric cases, the study population may not represent general obstetric populations.

Sample size considerations

This retrospective cohort included all eligible consecutive patients. Although no formal a priori sample size calculation was conducted due to the observational design, our final sample of 640 patients provides sufficient precision to detect an absolute risk reduction of 10% points (from 32% to 22%) with approximately 85% power (α = 0.05, two-sided)—a magnitude widely regarded as clinically meaningful in surgical antimicrobial stewardship.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 29.0 was used for statistical analysis. All continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range [M (Q1, Q3)] and compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as cases (percentage) [n (%)], and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test (when theoretical frequency < 5) was utilized for inter-group comparison. Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR) is defined as the difference in infection rates between the control and intervention groups. Relative Risk Reduction (RRR) is calculated using the formula: (infection rate in the control group – infection rate in the intervention group) / infection rate in the control group.

Multivariable logistic regression models estimated the independent association between pharmacist intervention and key outcomes (postoperative infection, hospitalization cost > 20,000 RMB, length of stay ≥ 6 days), adjusting a priori for clinically relevant confounders: maternal age, diabetes, hypertension, emergency cesarean delivery, and rupture of membranes. Subgroup analyses were prespecified for the urgency of surgery (elective vs. emergency cesarean delivery) to evaluate the consistency of the intervention effect across these clinically distinct populations.The homogeneity of the intervention effect across prespecified subgroups, such as the urgency of cesarean delivery, was evaluated by incorporating an interaction term (intervention group × subgroup variable) into a generalized linear model (with a binomial distribution and logit link). The significance of the interaction terms was then tested within the regression models. Missing data were minimal (< 5% for all variables), and a complete-case analysis was performed. Model fit was assessed via Hosmer–Lemeshow test; discrimination via area under the ROC curve (AUC). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) confirmed absence of multicollinearity (all VIFs < 2.5). Unadjusted p-values are reported for pre-specified confirmatory analyses (primary and key secondary outcomes). All other results are exploratory and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. All tests were two-tailed; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparisons of baseline characteristics between the two groups showed no significant differences (Table 1). Although baseline characteristics were generally balanced, the intervention group showed a trend toward lower prevalence of certain comorbidities. The subsequent analyses were adjusted for these potential confounders.

Analysis of differences in preoperative laboratory indicators and medication use between the two groups

For preoperative laboratory indicators, significant differences were observed in neutrophil counts and total bilirubin levels between the control group and the pharmacist intervention group (P < 0.01), although all values remained within the normal range. These variables were well balanced between the two groups, including emergency cesarean delivery (10.5% vs. 14.1%, P = 0.166), transverse incision (96.5% vs. 94.5%, P = 0.220), premature rupture of membranes (12.4% vs. 15.0%, P = 0.338), and primiparity (76.8% vs. 77.6%, P = 0.796) (see Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1 for complete comparisons). A statistically significant difference was found in the history of previous anti-infective treatment (6 cases in the pharmacist intervention group vs. 0 in the control group, P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding the infection rate during the current pregnancy or in the results of pathogen detection. In terms of antibiotic selection, the control group exhibited a higher proportion of cefuroxime exposure during pregnancy compared to the pharmacist intervention group (6.7% vs. 2.1%, P < 0.01). The control group also had a higher rate of respiratory tract infections than the pharmacist intervention group (P < 0.01). The pharmacist intervention group showed a higher proportion of preoperative cefuroxime use (P < 0.05), while the control group preferred azithromycin and ceftriaxone (P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 1).

Analysis of differences in postoperative outcomes between the two groups

The length of hospital stay in the pharmacist intervention group was shorter than that in the control group [5 (5,7) vs. 6 (5,8), P < 0.05]. The pharmacist intervention group also showed lower postoperative medication duration [2 (2,2) vs. 2 (2,3)], white blood cell counts [10.8 (9.0,13.0) vs. 12.4 (10.2,14.6)], and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels [66 (40,96) vs. 74 (48,106)] compared with the control group (all P < 0.01). Although the median postoperative antibiotic duration was 2 days in both groups, the intervention significantly increased the proportion of patients who discontinued antibiotics by 48 h (76.4% vs. 63.4%; P < 0.001). There was no significant difference in neonatal weight between the two groups (normal birth weight 2500–4000 g: 87.3% vs. 87.4%, P = 0.951; see Table 2 for complete data). Neonatal jaundice was more prevalent in the control group (26.8% vs. 16.9%, P = 0.002), whereas neonatal hypoxemia was solely noted in the pharmacist intervention group (3.7% vs. 0%, P < 0.001).

Analysis of antibiotic use in postoperative infection patients between the two groups

The pharmacist intervention group had a significantly lower incidence of composite postoperative infections than the control group (22.7% [74/326] vs. 31.8% [100/314]; P < 0.01). This corresponds to an ARR of 9.1% and an RRR of 28.6% (relative risk [RR] = 0.71, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.55–0.92). The pharmacist intervention group demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of first- and second-generation cephalosporin use (82.2% vs. 67.2%; P < 0.001), whereas the control group exhibited a greater proportion of cefmetazole use (38.2% vs. 20.9%; P < 0.001). Among the various types of postoperative infections, genital tract infections emerged as the most prevalent category. The incidence of genital tract infections was significantly lower in the pharmacist intervention group compared to the control group (19.9% [65/326] vs. 28.0% [88/314], P < 0.05), accounting for 87.8% (65/74) and 88.0% (88/100) of all infectious complications in each group, respectively. There was no significant difference in the incidence of urinary tract infections between the two groups (1.6% vs. 2.1%, P = 0.606). Pathogen detection rates were low in both groups, with 3.8% (12/314) in the control group and 4.0% (13/326) in the intervention group (P = 0.965). The primary pathogens identified were Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus faecalis, with no significant differences between the groups. The rational use rate of postoperative antibiotics was higher in the Pharmacist intervention group than in the control group (94.8% vs. 89.2%, P = 0.009). Among patients with irrational antibiotic use, the control group exhibited a greater number of cases with excessively prolonged postoperative medication duration (34 vs. 17, P < 0.01) and a higher incidence of irrational combination therapy (5 vs. 0, P < 0.05).(Supplementary Tables 2−1). Of the 25 infection episodes, 23 (92.0%) were confirmed bacteriologically, while 2 (8.0%) were attributed to viral pathogens. Susceptibility testing was conducted for 19 out of the 23 bacterial cases (82.6%), and the results were actively utilized to guide therapy in 12 cases. The bacteriological distribution and available antimicrobial susceptibility data from post-cesarean infections are presented in Supplementary Tables 2–2.

Subgroup analysis by urgency of surgery

Given the distinctive distribution of surgical urgency within our cohort, we conducted a prespecified subgroup analysis to examine the consistency of the intervention’s effect. Stratification by the urgency of surgery revealed that among the 640 patients, 561 (87.7%) underwent elective cesarean delivery, while 79 (12.3%) underwent emergency cesarean delivery. The distribution of surgical urgency was well-balanced between the two study groups: among elective surgery patients, the proportions in the intervention and control groups were 49.9% (280/561) and 50.1% (281/561), respectively. Among emergency surgery patients, the proportions were 58.2% (46/79) and 41.8% (33/79), respectively (P = 0.166). The intervention demonstrated benefits for patients irrespective of surgical urgency. In the elective surgery subgroup, the rate of genital tract infection decreased from 28.1% (79/281) in the control group to 21.1% (59/280) in the intervention group. In the emergency surgery subgroup, the infection rate decreased from 27.3% (9/33) to 13.0% (6/46). A generalized linear model interaction analysis confirmed that the effect of the intervention did not differ significantly by surgical urgency (interaction P = 0.388) (Supplementary Tables 2−1), indicating a consistent and universal benefit for both elective and emergency cesarean delivery patients.

Comparison of hospitalization costs, drug costs, and antibiotic consumption between the two groups

Total hospitalization costs were significantly lower in the pharmacist intervention group compared to the control group (median 19,424.5 RMB vs. 20,621.8 RMB, P = 0.002; see Table 3). To assess whether the cost reduction associated with pharmacist intervention was independent of infection status, we stratified total hospitalization costs by the presence or absence of postoperative infection. Among non-infected patients, total hospitalization costs were lower in the pharmacist intervention group than in the control group. Total drug costs were significantly lower in the pharmacist intervention group (median 3426 RMB vs. 4050 RMB in the control group, P < 0.001). Additionally, antibiotic costs were also significantly reduced (median 120 RMB vs. 262 RMB, P < 0.001). Pharmacist intervention cost estimation: Based on an average monthly salary of 15,000 RMB for clinical pharmacists in Shanghai and an estimated time commitment of 15–30 min per patient (comprising chart review: 5–10 min; real-time intervention/communication: 5–15 min; documentation: 5 min), the direct labor cost was calculated at approximately 50 RMB per patient (Table 4).

Analysis of influencing factors for hospitalization duration ≥ 6 days

Among the 640 patients, 329 (51.4%) experienced a hospital stay of six days or longer, while 311 (48.6%) had stays of less than six days, resulting in an overall median length of stay of six days. A multivariable logistic regression model, which adjusted for factors such as pharmacist intervention, neonatal birth weight, preoperative diastolic blood pressure, duration of the operation, multiple gestation, maternal diabetes, total duration of inpatient antibiotic therapy, postoperative infection, type of surgical incision, PROM, and emergency cesarean delivery, identified eight variables that were independently associated with prolonged hospitalization (P < 0.05). Specifically, lower preoperative diastolic blood pressure, multiple pregnancies, extended use of inpatient antibiotics, vertical surgical incision (as opposed to transverse), newborn weight <2500g, diabetes history and surgical duration each correlated with increased odds of prolonged hospitalization. In contrast, PROM was significantly associated with decreased odds of prolonged hospitalization (OR < 1), suggesting it was not a risk factor for an extended stay in this cohort. Although the duration of the operation did not significantly differ between study periods (Supplementary Table 1), it remained an independent predictor of longer hospital stays, consistent with its established impact on postoperative recovery. The model exhibited excellent calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.405) and acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.721). Full results can be found in Supplementary Table 3.

Association between pharmacist-led intervention and neonatal jaundice

After adjusting for emergency cesarean delivery, prolonged PROM (≥ 12 h), ART, preoperative infection, and birth weight < 2500 g, infants in the pharmacist intervention group exhibited significantly lower odds of developing neonatal jaundice compared to those in the control group (OR = 0.56; 95% CI 0.38–0.82; P = 0.003). This association remained robust even after considering key perinatal risk factors, indicating an independent protective effect of the intervention (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

This study was conducted not under an active DRG payment system, but in anticipation of its imminent nationwide rollout in China. Our objective was to evaluate whether pharmacist-led stewardship could simultaneously enhance the appropriateness of antibiotic use and reduce resource utilization within the complex environment of a high-acuity tertiary obstetric center, a setting characterized by an inherently elevated baseline infection risk.

Principal findings and alignment with the triple aim of healthcare

Beyond technical interventions—such as timing optimization, regimen selection, and duration control—the clinical pharmacist functions as a coordinator, educator, and steward within the perioperative ecosystem. By participating in daily ward rounds, contributing to multidisciplinary consultations, and co-developing institutional protocols, pharmacists effectively bridge the gaps between guidelines and practice, cost containment and quality assurance, as well as individual patient care and system-level efficiency. In anticipation of China’s nationwide implementation of the DRG payment system, scheduled for 2025, the proactive integration of clinical pharmacists into the perioperative care team for cesarean deliveries exemplifies how institutions can align clinical practice with forthcoming value-based incentives. This real-world, before-and-after study demonstrates that integrating clinical pharmacists into the perioperative care team for cesarean deliveries under China’s DRG payment system simultaneously advances the “triple aim” of healthcare: improved clinical outcomes (reduced postoperative infections), enhanced process quality (increased rational antibiotic use), and greater economic efficiency (lower total and antibiotic-related costs). These findings position pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship not merely as an ancillary service, but as an essential component of proactive, value-oriented obstetric care in the transition toward DRG-based reimbursement.

Clinical impact: the role of clinical pharmacists in infection prevention and qntimicrobial surveillance

This study reported a composite postoperative infection rate, with 31.8% in the control group compared to 22.7% in the pharmacist intervention group, substantially exceeding the documented range of 9.67% to 17.3% in general obstetric populations [16] and markedly exceeding the 3% to 15% typically cited for surgical site infections (SSIs) alone in global literature. However, this comparison is misleading if the endpoints are conflated: our primary outcome encompassed all clinically diagnosed infectious complications, not SSI alone. Crucially, 88 of 100 infections (88%) in the control group were classified as genital tract infections, predominantly endometritis—a pattern consistent with high-risk obstetric cohorts where endometritis, rather than wound infection, constitutes the dominant post-cesarean morbidity. The same proportion (87.8%, 65/74) was observed in the intervention group. The plausibility of this elevated burden is supported by robust epidemiological evidence. In a landmark cohort of over 10,000 cesarean deliveries, Tita et al. [17] reported a 19.9% incidence of endometritis under prospective active surveillance—and 23.0% via database query—when only standard narrow-spectrum cephalosporin prophylaxis was used, despite structured infection control monitoring. These figures reflect the substantial residual risk of genital tract infection even in settings with routine antibiotic prophylaxis and structured monitoring.

Importantly, the elevated baseline infection rate observed in our cohort accurately reflects the clinical burden of a high-acuity tertiary referral population, which is characterized by a high prevalence of diabetes, previous cesarean deliveries, and various comorbidities, rather than a diagnostic overcall. Rather than undermining the validity of our findings, this real-world complexity enhances their relevance: if pharmacist-led stewardship can effectively reduce infections and costs in such a high-risk setting, the benefits are likely generalizable to lower-risk populations, where the absolute risk reduction may be smaller, yet the relative impact remains significant.

Although measured risk factors such as emergency cesarean delivery, prolonged rupture of membranes, or vertical incision were present only in a minority of patients, undiagnosed or subclinical chorioamnionitis, frequent intrapartum vaginal examinations, delays between decision-to-incision, or other unmeasured procedural exposures common in high-acuity obstetric care may have contributed to the high rate of genital tract infection. Notably, endometritis—often triggered by ascending polymicrobial flora in the setting of labor or membrane disruption—is particularly sensitive to such contextual factors, even when classic risk markers are absent. This suggests that a uniform preventive approach may not be suitable for all cases. Instead, it is essential to stratify patients based on critical risk factors, including the mode of delivery (e.g., emergency cesarean section versus elective cesarean section), labor status, duration of membrane rupture, and others. Enhanced preventive measures, such as the administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, should be provided specifically for high-risk populations [17–18]. The elevated baseline likely reflects the confluence of patient acuity and a clinically pragmatic case definition, rather than systemic lapses in aseptic practice or surveillance artifact. Although this limits the generalizability of the absolute risk reduction to lower-risk populations, it underscores the particular relevance of pharmacist-led stewardship in high-burden, resource-constrained tertiary settings, where optimizing antibiotic use can meaningfully reduce the most prevalent form of postoperative infection. While the baseline infection rate was high, the intervention achieved an absolute risk reduction of 9.1%, which corresponds to a number needed to treat (NNT) of approximately 11; this indicates that for every 11 patients managed with pharmacist-led stewardship, one postoperative infection would be prevented.

In light of this high-risk context, our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence indicating that clinical pharmacists play a crucial role in optimizing perioperative antimicrobial use and reducing the incidence of SSIs in obstetrics. Consistent with studies conducted by researchers [19–20], pharmacist-led interventions significantly improved the appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing, as demonstrated in our cohort, which exhibited a rational use rate of 94.8% compared to 89.2% in the control group. Notably, adherence to guidelines for perioperative prophylaxis reached 100% in the intervention group, in alignment with best-practice benchmarks that emphasize the timely, single-dose administration of narrow-spectrum agents, such as cefazolin [21] This high level of compliance directly addresses key modifiable risk factors for post-cesarean infection. The literature documents that Gram-negative bacilli—particularly Escherichia coli (31%) and Klebsiella spp. (34%)—account for the majority of intra-abdominal infections following cesarean delivery, with Gram-negative organisms implicated in up to 76% of peritonitis cases [22]. Despite a relatively low pathogen detection rate in our setting, which is a common challenge in resource-constrained environments, pharmacists ensured that empirical regimens effectively covered these prevalent pathogens while avoiding unnecessary broadening of the spectrum. Critically, they also curtailed prolonged postoperative antibiotic courses, reducing the average duration by 1.2 days and eliminating irrational combination therapies that offer no added benefit but increase the risk of collateral damage, including Clostridioides difficile infection and antimicrobial resistance. These changes reflect a more judicious use of antibiotics and a reduction in unnecessary prolonged therapy, aligning with core antimicrobial stewardship principles.

This dynamic monitoring facilitated earlier de-escalation or discontinuation of antibiotics in non-infected patients—a practice that has been shown to reduce antimicrobial exposure without compromising safety [19–20]. In this capacity, pharmacists not only enhanced individual patient outcomes but also contributed to institutional antimicrobial stewardship goals in preparation for the upcoming DRG payment system, under which unnecessary antibiotic use is expected to negatively impact hospital financial performance. Collectively, these actions illustrate that clinical pharmacists serve as more than medication experts—they are integral to a proactive, data-informed infection prevention ecosystem, bridging guideline evidence, microbiological insights, and frontline clinical decision-making.

Economic value in anticipation of the DRG framework: a dominant strategy for high-value obstetric care

Our study demonstrates that pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship for cesarean delivery is a dominant strategy in health economic terms, delivering better outcomes at a lower cost, as defined in standard cost-effectiveness frameworks [23]. The pharmacist-led intervention reduced total hospitalization expenses by 1,197 RMB per patient and lowered antibiotic costs by a median of 142.6 RMB (from 262.3 RMB to 119.7 RMB; P < 0.001), resulting in a net saving of 92.6 RMB per patient after accounting for intervention costs, while simultaneously reducing postoperative infection rates from 31.8% to 22.7%.The intervention was associated with a significant reduction in post-cesarean genital tract infections, predominantly endometritis, decreasing from 28.0% in the control group to 19.9% in the intervention group (P = 0.016). This reduction likely contributed to more judicious antibiotic use, which in turn influenced key metrics of healthcare resource utilization. Specifically, longer postoperative antibiotic use was independently linked to higher odds of hospitalization costs exceeding 20,000 RMB (OR = 1.23), while greater total in-hospital antibiotic exposure strongly predicted prolonged stays (> 6 days; OR = 1.44). Both antibiotic-related variables also demonstrated statistically significant associations with their respective outcomes in univariate analyses. Although genital tract infection did not remain statistically significant in the final multivariable model for prolonged length of stay, possibly due to collinearity with antibiotic duration, its strong univariate association and well-established clinical impact support its role as a key modifiable target for cost containment under the upcoming DRG payment framework. With an estimated pharmacist labor cost of only 50 RMB per patient (approximately 20 min), the ROI reached 185.2%. Even if pharmacist time doubled, net savings remained positive (42.6 RMB per patient; ROI > 40%), confirming the robustness of our findings under conservative assumptions.

In the context of China’s forthcoming DRG payment system, where hospitals receive fixed reimbursement per case irrespective of actual resource utilization, internal cost reductions directly enhance operational margins without compromising quality. This perspective reframes clinical pharmacy services from being perceived as a cost center to being recognized as a high-return strategic investment that aligns with future DRG incentives. Our findings resonate with broader health economic principles articulated in [20], which underscore the importance of prioritizing interventions that enhance both clinical and economic outcomes, particularly in surgical settings where antimicrobial misuse is common and is expected to be financially penalized under bundled payments. While our analysis conservatively excludes broader benefits, such as the reduction of antimicrobial resistance, the immediate financial and clinical advantages strongly advocate for scaling this model within obstetric care. Under fee-for-service models, reduced antibiotic use might inadvertently decrease hospital revenue; under the upcoming DRG system, however, the same reduction directly improves margins—creating a rare alignment between clinical stewardship and financial sustainability in the transition toward value-based payment.

Interpretation of exploratory findings

Our multivariable analyses reveal that post-cesarean outcomes—ranging from infection to hospital length of stay—are shaped by a confluence of surgical, obstetric, and therapeutic factors, rather than any single determinant. Notably, the direction and magnitude of certain risk factors differed meaningfully between outcomes, underscoring the context-dependence of clinical indicators.

Regarding postoperative infection, two non-modifiable factors emerged as independent predictors: ART and preoperative infection. The association with ART likely reflects its strong linkage to placental abnormalities (e.g., previa, accreta) and preterm birth—conditions that increase surgical complexity and tissue trauma—rather than a direct biological effect of ART itself. Given the absence of consistent evidence in current guidelines and the lack of a plausible mechanistic pathway, this finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating, warranting validation in studies with detailed placental and intrapartum data. In contrast, pharmacist-led intervention was independently associated with reduced infection risk, reinforcing its role as a system-level quality lever. Surprisingly, vertical incision—typically considered a risk factor for wound complications—was linked to lower odds of infection in our cohort (OR < 1). This counterintuitive result may stem from the selective use of vertical incisions in high-risk scenarios (e.g., extreme obesity, dense adhesions, or anticipated massive hemorrhage), where surgeons concurrently implement enhanced aseptic precautions, meticulous hemostasis, and intensified postoperative monitoring, thereby offsetting the inherent wound-healing disadvantage.

With respect to prolonged hospitalization (≥ 6 days), a distinct set of predictors emerged. Multiple gestations—despite their low prevalence—conferred nearly ten-fold higher odds of an extended stay, underscoring the persistent physiological burden of multifetal pregnancy even under standardized care. Vertical incision, lower preoperative diastolic blood pressure (possibly reflecting hemodynamic instability or volume depletion), and extended inpatient antibiotic use were all independently associated with longer stays. Critically, the latter reinforces that prolonged antimicrobial therapy is not merely a marker but a potential driver of delayed discharge—a modifiable target for stewardship programs.

Intriguingly, PROM—a well-established risk factor for maternal infection in many settings [24–26]—exhibited a divergent risk profile in our cohort. Contrary to conventional expectations, PROM was not associated with increased postoperative infection, likely due to consistent adherence to evidence-based protocols: all patients with PROM exceeding 12 h received guideline-concordant prophylactic antibiotics [27], and those with prolonged rupture (greater than 24 h) underwent timely delivery (typically within 48 h), thereby minimizing the window for ascending infection. Moreover, PROM was strongly protective against prolonged hospitalization (OR = 0.21), suggesting that these same streamlined pathways—prioritizing rapid delivery and early discharge in predominantly term, non-infected cases—effectively transformed a traditional obstetric risk indicator into a marker of efficient care coordination. Together, these findings illustrate how standardized clinical processes can not only neutralize expected harms but also generate unexpected benefits, reshaping the prognostic meaning of established risk factors.

The analysis also revealed nuanced findings regarding neonatal outcomes. The intervention group demonstrated a lower incidence of neonatal jaundice, an effect unlikely attributable to the pharmacology of cephalosporins (as cefazolin and cefuroxime do not impair bilirubin metabolism). This may reflect indirect benefits of optimized maternal care, such as reduced inflammatory responses or enhanced perioperative coordination. Conversely, neonatal hypoxemia was observed exclusively in the intervention group (3.7% vs. 0%, P < 0.001). While this difference is statistically significant, no established biological mechanism links cephalosporin administration to impaired neonatal oxygenation. Baseline maternal and obstetric characteristics were well balanced between groups, and most cases occurred during scheduled cesarean deliveries under controlled conditions. However, given the temporal sequence of our study (control period first, followed by intervention), we cannot rule out unmeasured changes in clinical practice over time as a potential explanation for the observed signal. Such changes may include the adoption of more sensitive neonatal monitoring protocols (e.g., routine or extended pulse oximetry), increased clinical vigilance for respiratory distress, or modifications in immediate postnatal care pathways during the later phase of the study. Taken together, the absence of a biologically plausible link, coupled with the potential for temporal confounding or heightened surveillance, strongly suggests that this statistical association is not causally related to the antibiotic intervention itself, but rather reflects non-intervention-related changes in detection or care patterns throughout the study period.

Collectively, these findings indicate that while clinical risk factors establish the context, therapeutic decisions influence the trajectory of outcomes. Future pharmacy-led models should therefore transition from uniform protocols to risk-adapted bundles, including intensified monitoring for ART pregnancies with placental abnormalities, integration of glycemic control and wound healing in diabetic patients, and real-time de-escalation guided by biomarkers in high-inflammatory states. Prospective studies with larger samples, comprehensive intrapartum data, and long-term follow-up are essential to validate these approaches and clarify rare neonatal outcomes.

Policy and implementation implications

Our findings support proactively integrating clinical pharmacists into institutional structures in preparation for effective DRG implementation. Practical steps include: (1) defining clear pharmacist roles in perioperative pathways; (2) incorporating metrics such as “pharmacist intervention coverage” and “antibiotic rationality rate” into hospital quality dashboards; (3) establishing multidisciplinary DRG medication committees (pharmacists, physicians, infection control, insurance); and (4) developing intelligent monitoring systems to flag irrational prescribing in real time. For high-risk subgroups (e.g., ART pregnancies, diabetes, obesity), targeted joint management involving pharmacists, nurses, and obstetricians may further amplify benefits and enhance readiness for value-based payment models such as DRG.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations inherent to its design. As a single-center, non-randomized before-and-after comparison, it is susceptible to unmeasured confounding and has limited generalizability, particularly to healthcare systems operating under different DRG payment models or alternative reimbursement mechanisms. Although we rigorously adjusted for known clinical and demographic predictors in multivariate models, and the baseline characteristics were well balanced between cohorts, the possibility of residual confounding from unobserved factors (e.g., subtle shifts in surgical technique or nursing practices) cannot be ruled out.

We acknowledge that our institutional diagnostic criteria for postoperative infection require the presence of fever in conjunction with clinical signs, such as uterine tenderness or purulent lochia, or the initiation of antibiotics by a physician for suspected infection. These criteria are intentionally sensitive, reflecting real-world decision-making in environments where rapid microbiological confirmation is often unavailable. Consequently, this approach may result in higher reported infection rates compared to studies that rely solely on culture-proven surgical site infections.

Notably, neonatal hypoxemia was exclusively observed in the intervention group. However, as this outcome was not pre-specified, it may have been influenced by increased surveillance or changes in monitoring practices over time. Therefore, this signal should be interpreted with caution and considered as hypothesis-generating rather than indicative of a genuine adverse effect. Additionally, this study lacks long-term follow-up data; therefore, outcomes such as late-onset surgical site infections, readmissions, and delayed antibiotic-related adverse events were not captured.

Nonetheless, definitive evidence requires higher-quality designs. Future work should prioritize prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trials to confirm efficacy and cost-effectiveness across diverse settings. Additionally, factorial or stepped-wedge designs could isolate the marginal impact of specific stewardship components (e.g., preoperative timing vs. postoperative de-escalation), while interrupted time series analyses on larger datasets would better account for underlying temporal trends. Such studies are essential to inform policy and practice before the widespread adoption of pharmacist-led stewardship in healthcare systems transitioning to DRG-based payment models.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that the structured involvement of clinical pharmacists in in perioperative care for cesarean sections—implemented in anticipation of DRG payment system—is associated with more standardized perioperative antibiotic use. During the same period, favorable trends were observed in key clinical outcomes—including reduced postoperative infection rates and shorter hospital stays—as well as in economic efficiency, as indicated by lower total costs. Given the non-randomized, before-and-after design and the potential for unmeasured confounding, these concurrent improvements cannot be definitively attributed to the pharmacist intervention alone. Nevertheless, the consistency of these findings across multiple domains supports the potential value of pharmacist-led antimicrobial stewardship as a preparatory strategy for value-based payment systems. The integration of clinical pharmacists as a high-value, system-level intervention in obstetric care transitioning toward DRG-based reimbursement is supported by emerging evidence. A prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trial is warranted to establish causality and guide national scale-up efforts.
