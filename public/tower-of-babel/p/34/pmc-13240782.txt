An injectable tough hydrogel sealant enabling rapid hemostasis and promoting oral tissue regeneration
Authors: Jinghan Wang, Gang He, Zheng Pan, Yiwen Xian, Luyuan Chen, Wen Sui, Chong Zhang, Decheng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Repair of oral soft-tissue injuries remains challenging due to the moist, bacteria-rich, and mechanically active oral environment, as well as the limitations of current sealants in wet adhesion, mechanical strength, biocompatibility, bioactivity, and sealing durability. Here, we report an injectable hydrogel sealant (PAG) composed of tetra-armed poly(ethylene glycol) succinimidyl succinate and amine-functionalized gelatin, which rapidly forms in situ via NHS-amine coupling without external triggers. The optimized formulation undergoes gelation within seconds and exhibits robust mechanical properties, as well as superior adhesive strength and burst pressure compared with commercial fibrin glue. PAG demonstrates excellent cytocompatibility, hemocompatibility, and biodegradability, while promoting fibroblast proliferation in vitro. In vivo, it enables rapid hemostasis within 3 s and effective tissue repair in an acute rat tongue perforation model, markedly outperforming suture, gelatin sponge, and fibrin glue controls. Moreover, PAG effectively protects early-stage wounds and accelerates repair in both rat and porcine oral mucosal defect models. Mechanistic studies indicate that PAG establishes a pro-regenerative microenvironment by attenuating excessive inflammation, enhancing angiogenesis, and promoting M2-dominant macrophage polarization. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the engineered PAG hydrogel enables rapid, sutureless sealing and repair of oral soft-tissue wounds, highlighting its translational potential.

Introduction

Oral soft-tissue defects, ranging from superficial mucosal lesions to full-thickness perforations, commonly arise from trauma, surgical resection, or iatrogenic injuries [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Such injuries can impair essential oral functions, including speech, mastication, and swallowing, while increasing the risk of infection, hemorrhage, and delayed healing. Rapid hemostasis and effective wound sealing are critical for restoring tissue integrity and preventing secondary complications [[6], [7], [8]]. Superficial mucosal wounds are typically managed with protective or bioactive formulations, such as hyaluronic acid gels (GENGIGEL®), lipid sprays (Episil®), and mucoadhesive films or rinses (MuGard™), which primarily relieve pain and promote re-epithelialization [[9], [10], [11]]. In parallel, topical hemostatic agents, such as gelatin- or chitosan-based sponges and powders, are employed to control oral bleeding [12,13]. However, their therapeutic efficacy is often limited by the moist, microbe-rich, and mechanically dynamic oral environment, which compromises material retention and durability [6,14]. In contrast, deep or full-thickness oral soft-tissue defects usually require surgical interventions, including grafting or flap reconstruction, resulting in increased procedural complexity and postoperative morbidity [15,16]. Suturing alone is technically demanding in the confined oral cavity, prolongs operative time, and may cause secondary tissue trauma or localized scarring [[17], [18], [19]]. These challenges highlight the urgent need for innovative material designs capable of achieving rapid hemostasis and stable wound closure for oral soft-tissue defects.

Tissue sealants and bioadhesives can facilitate hemostasis and wound closure and have been adopted in clinical practice as convenient adjuncts or alternatives to suturing, providing structural and functional support in soft-tissue repair [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. However, their clinical performance in the oral cavity remains limited by insufficient mechanical strength, inadequate adhesion under wet and dynamically mobile conditions, and biosafety concerns [26,27]. For example, cyanoacrylates provide strong initial adhesion but are brittle and inflexible, rendering them susceptible to failure under tissue motion and associated with heat release and toxic degradation products [28,29]. Fibrin sealants (e.g., Tisseel and Evicel) exhibit good biocompatibility but display weak adhesion in high-shear oral environments, whereas synthetic aldehyde-protein adhesives, such as BioGlue, achieve strong bonding yet may pose risks associated with high stiffness and cytotoxicity [25,30,31]. Consequently, most commercial sealants are used primarily as adjuncts to sutures rather than as standalone solutions, and their ability to provide durable hemostatic sealing and support tissue regeneration in the oral cavity remains inconsistent.

Several bioadhesives and tissue sealants have recently been developed for oral soft-tissue repair [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. Natural biopolymers generally exhibit excellent biocompatibility but are limited by insufficient mechanical strength and weak tissue adhesion. In contrast, synthetic polymers can be engineered to achieve tailored mechanical and functional properties but often lack intrinsic bioactivity and may not effectively support tissue regeneration or biointegration [38]. In addition, reliance on external activation methods, such as UV irradiation, or the incorporation of components with potential biosafety risks may further limit their clinical effectiveness [7,[39], [40], [41]]. While extensive research has focused on superficial mucosal repair, deeper and structurally complex injuries, such as full-thickness tongue defects, remain largely underexplored. Unlike mucosal wounds, these defects involve highly vascularized muscular tissue, significant hemorrhage, and continuous mechanical stress, posing substantially greater challenges for rapid hemostasis and stable wound closure [42].

In this study, we developed a multifunctional injectable hydrogel to overcome the limitations of current oral soft-tissue sealants and evaluated its hemostatic and regenerative performance in rat and porcine oral soft-tissue defect models (Scheme 1). Our previous work established a covalently crosslinked tough hydrogel based on tetra-armed poly(ethylene glycol) succinimidyl succinate (tetra-PEG-SS) and amine-functionalized gelatin (AGel) for achieving rapid hemostasis in arterial injuries [43]. However, the pre-mixing of high-concentration polymer solutions through multiple back-and-forth transfers to prepare the super-tough hydrogel may limit its ease of use and reproducibility, and its application to highly dynamic soft tissues remains largely unexplored. Herein, we optimized this platform for oral soft tissues using a double-tube injection strategy, combining bioactive gelatin to support tissue remodeling and hemostasis with tetra-PEG to provide mechanical strength and robust tissue adhesion. Using a double-barrel syringe equipped with a helical static mixer, the tetra-PEG-SS/AGel (PAG) hydrogel rapidly forms in situ through NHS-amine coupling without external triggers. We systematically characterized its physical properties, including gelation kinetics, tissue adhesion, mechanical strength, swelling behavior, and burst pressure. Cytocompatibility, cell migration, and hemocompatibility were evaluated in vitro. In vivo biodegradability was assessed using a subcutaneous implantation model, and hemostatic performance was evaluated in an acute rat tongue perforation model against commercial controls. Furthermore, we evaluated suture-free oral soft-tissue repair and regeneration in rat tongue perforation and mucosal defect models, as well as in porcine buccal and labial wounds, and investigated the underlying mechanisms of hydrogel-mediated healing. Materials and methods

Materials

Hydroxyl-terminated four-arm poly(ethylene glycol) (tetra-PEG-OH, Mw = 10 kDa) was obtained from SINOPEG Biotech Co., Ltd. (Xiamen, China). Gelatin (Mw ≈ 60 kDa) derived from cold-water fish skin was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Artificial saliva (AS, pH 7.4) was supplied by Vokai Biotechnology (Beijing, China). LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit was purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Succinic anhydride, ethylenediamine, 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), and anhydrous dichloromethane (DCM) were obtained from Energy Chemical (Shanghai, China). Gelatin sponge (Gel-S) and fibrin glue were obtained from Fukangsen Medical Devices Co., Ltd. (Guilin, China) and Bioseal Biotech Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China), respectively. Cy5.5 NHS ester was purchased from Shaanxi New Research Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Xi'an, China). SweScript All-in-One RT SuperMix for quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) and Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix were obtained from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Fresh porcine tissues (skin, tongue, and buccal mucosa) were purchased from a local market.

Instrumentation

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra were acquired using a Bruker AVIII HD 400 MHz spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained at room temperature on a Nicolet Magna IR-750 spectrometer (Thermo Nicolet, USA). Rheological properties of the hydrogels were characterized using a HAAKE MARS III rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mechanical properties and adhesive performance were evaluated with a universal testing machine (INSTRON 68TM-5). Microstructural was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Quanta 450, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. qPCR analyses were performed using a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Synthesis of tetra-PEG-SS

Tetra-PEG-SS was synthesized via a two-step esterification and activation process following previously reported procedures [44]. Briefly, tetra-PEG-OH (10 g, 1 mmol), succinic anhydride (1 g, 10 mmol), and DMAP (611 mg, 5 mmol) were dissolved in anhydrous DCM (300 mL) and stirred under a nitrogen atmosphere for 24 h. The reaction mixture was washed with saturated brine, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated. The intermediate tetra-PEG-COOH was obtained by precipitation in diethyl ether and vacuum drying. Subsequently, the tetra-PEG-COOH (10 g, 1 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous DCM (600 mL), followed by the addition of EDC (1.9 g, 10 mmol) and NHS (1.2 g, 10 mmol). The reaction was then carried out under nitrogen for an additional 24 h. After purification by washing with saturated brine, the organic phase was dried and concentrated, followed by precipitation in diethyl ether to afford tetra-PEG-SS as a white powder. The chemical structure was confirmed by 1H NMR and FT-IR spectroscopy.

Preparation of amino-gelatin (AGel)

Following previously reported procedures [43,45], gelatin (5 g) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 100 mL) at 40 °C. Ethylenediamine (15 mL) was added, and the pH was adjusted to approximately 5.0 using 12 M HCl. Amidation was carried out by adding EDC (2.3 g), and the reaction was stirred overnight at 25 °C. The resulting solution was dialyzed against deionized water (MWCO 4 kDa) and lyophilized to obtain AGel. The amino group content of AGel was quantified by the trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS) assay using a β-alanine calibration curve.

Preparation of PAG hydrogel

Tetra-PEG-SS and AGel were separately dissolved in borate buffer solution (BBS, pH 8.0) at concentrations of 1, 3, 5, 10, and 15% (w/v). Equal volumes of the two precursor solutions were mixed using a dual-barrel syringe to form tetra-PEG-SS/AGel (PAG) hydrogels in situ.

Gelation time test

Gelation time was determined using the vial-tilting method [46,47]. Precursor solutions at different concentrations were transferred into 5 mL glass vials and inverted at regular intervals. The gelation time was defined as the point at which no observable flow occurred upon inversion. Each condition was tested in triplicate.

Swelling behavior test

Cylindrical PAG hydrogels (20 mm in diameter and 3 mm in height) were immersed in artificial saliva at 37 °C until swelling equilibrium was reached. At selected time points, samples were gently blotted to remove surface liquid, and their wet mass (Mt) and corresponding volume (Vt) were measured. The degree of swelling was quantified relative to the initial mass (M0) and volume (V0), with the mass-based swelling ratio and the volumetric swelling ratio calculated as (Mt - M0)/M0 × 100% and (Vt - V0)/V0 × 100%, respectively. Three samples were tested for each group.

Mechanical characterization

Oscillatory rheology was performed at 25 °C using a 20-mm parallel plate with a 2-mm gap. Frequency sweeps (0.1-10 Hz, 1% strain) provided storage (G′) and loss moduli (G″), while strain sweeps (γ = 1-150% at 1 Hz) determined the critical strain for network collapse. Compression tests were performed on both freshly prepared and equilibrium-swollen hydrogels using a universal testing machine at a rate of 10 mm/min until either 90% strain was reached or the samples ruptured.

Cyclic compression tests were conducted at a fixed compressive strain of 50% using a universal testing machine operated at a constant crosshead speed of 10 mm/min. After reaching the target strain, the load was released immediately, allowing the sample to return to its original height at the same rate. This loading-unloading cycle was repeated 50 consecutive times. Before testing, the dimensions of each hydrogel specimen were measured using a digital caliper. Three independent samples were evaluated for each experimental group.

Torsion test and adhesive interfaces

A torsion test was first performed to assess the adhesive performance of the PAG hydrogel on wet porcine tongue and buccal mucosa tissues. Circular defects (5-mm diameter) were created on pre-wetted tissues and immediately sealed by in situ application of the hydrogel. After 30 s to allow gelation and interfacial adhesion, the hydrogel-adhered tissues were subjected to stretching, twisting, bending, and continuous water flushing to evaluate adhesive robustness under dynamic conditions.

The adhesive interfaces between the hydrogel and the tissues were examined using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and cryo-scanning electron microscopy (cryo-SEM, FEI Quanta 450, USA). Briefly, tissue-hydrogel samples were mounted on conductive stubs, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for 30 s, and transferred under vacuum to a low-temperature preparation system (Quorum PP3000T, UK). Samples were sublimated at −90 °C for 10 min, sputter-coated with gold (10 mA, 60 s), and imaged on a FEI Quanta 450 SEM at −140 °C and 10 kV to visualize the hydrogel-tissue interface.

Adhesion property evaluation

Quantitative adhesion was further evaluated using lap shear, 180° peel, and tensile adhesion tests [48,49]. Porcine tongue and buccal mucosa tissues were rinsed, wetted, and trimmed into rectangular specimens (50 mm × 10 mm). For each test, 100 μL of hydrogel precursor was applied to one specimen, overlaid with a second specimen of identical size to form a uniform bonded interface (effective adhesion area: 10 mm × 10 mm), and compressed with a 200 g weight for 20 s. Fibrin glue (100 μL) was used as a control under identical conditions.

Lap shear tests were performed at a rate of 10 mm/min until failure, with adhesive strength calculated as the maximum force divided by the bonded area. For 180° peel tests, samples were peeled at a rate of 30 mm/min, and interfacial toughness was expressed as 2F/d, where F is the steady-state peeling force, and d is the interface width. Tensile adhesion was measured by fixing the non-bonded ends in a vertical setup and loading at a rate of 10 mm/min until interfacial failure. Tensile adhesive strength was calculated as the maximum force divided by the bonded area. All experiments were repeated three times.

In vitro sealing stability

Circular defects (5 mm in diameter) were created on freshly excised porcine buccal mucosa tissues, which were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water before testing. The defects were sealed in situ with 100 μL of PAG hydrogel, while GENGIGEL® and fibrin glue were used as control materials, and untreated defects served as negative controls. After allowing 1 min for gelation and adhesion, the sealed tissues were immersed in artificial saliva containing crushed rodent chow particles. Continuous stirring (1000 rpm) was applied to simulate the dynamic shear forces in the oral cavity. The sealing performance was monitored over time by photographing the tissues and assessing the integrity and adhesion of the hydrogels.

Burst pressure test

Similar to previously reported methods, porcine tongue or buccal mucosa tissues with a 3-mm diameter defect were connected to a syringe pump and a pressure monitor and then filled with PBS [50,51]. The defects were sealed in situ with 500 μL of PAG hydrogel or an equivalent volume of fibrin glue. After 1 min to allow complete gelation, PBS was infused at a constant rate of 4 mL/s to apply pressure to the sealed defect. The burst pressure was defined as the maximum pressure sustained before seal failure. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

In vitro biocompatibility

Hemocompatibility of PAG hydrogels was assessed using a standard hemolysis test [52,53]. Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated from fresh whole blood by mixing it with PBS at a 1:10 ratio and then centrifuging at 2000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the RBC pellet was washed three times with PBS until the supernatant was clear. A 5% (v/v) RBC suspension was prepared in PBS. Hydrogel samples were finely cut and dispersed in the RBC suspension to achieve final concentrations of 20, 50, and 100 mg/mL. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h and then centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 15 min. Supernatants were collected for visual inspection and absorbance measurement at 540 nm. PBS and 0.1% Triton X-100 served as negative (A0) and positive (Ap) controls, respectively. Hemolysis (%) was calculated as [(As - A0)/(Ap - A0)]× 100%.

For the cell compatibility test, sterilized PAG hydrogels (0.2, 0.5, and 1.0 g) were incubated in 10 mL of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C for 24 h to obtain hydrogel extracts. Human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs, Procell, China) were seeded at a density of 2000 cells per well in 96-well plates and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with hydrogel extracts, and cells were incubated for 1, 2, or 3 days, with cells cultured in standard DMEM serving as a control. At each time point, cell viability was determined using a CCK-8 assay (Abbkine, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Additionally, cell viability and morphology were evaluated using the LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit. HGFs were seeded at 2000 cells per 100 μL per well in 96-well plates and co-cultured with the hydrogel extracts for 1, 2, and 3 days. Following staining, samples were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) to visualize live and dead cells.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration was evaluated using a 12-well Culture-Insert system (Ibidi). HGFs were seeded at a density of 2000 cells per 100 μL in each well and allowed to attach and grow for 24 h at 37 °C. After removal of the insert to generate a defined cell-free gap, the culture medium was replaced with hydrogel extract. Cell migration into the gap was monitored at specified time points, and the area covered by migrating cells was measured using ImageJ software (version 1.54 g). The migration ratio was quantified as the percentage of gap closure based on the initial cell-free area (S0) and the remaining unclosed area (St) at each time point, using the following equation: migration ratio (%) = [(S0 - St)/S0] × 100.

In vitro degradability

Freeze-dried cylindrical PAG hydrogels were weighed to obtain the initial mass (W0), and then immersed in artificial saliva at 37 °C under gentle shaking (n = 3). At predetermined time points, the hydrogels were removed, freeze-dried, and weighed to determine the remaining mass (Wt). The degradation profile of the hydrogels was expressed as the percentage of mass remaining, calculated as (Wt/W0) × 100%.

In vivo degradation behavior

To investigate the in vivo degradation behavior of the PAG hydrogel, Cy5.5 NHS ester (10 mg) was first covalently conjugated to AGel (1.5 g) in DMSO under stirring overnight. The resulting mixture was purified by extensive dialysis against deionized water using a dialysis membrane (MWCO 4000 Da) for 2 days to remove residual unreacted Cy5.5 NHS ester. The fluorescently labeled PAG hydrogel was obtained via in situ gelation by mixing equal volumes of Cy5.5-labeled AGel (10%, w/v) and tetra-PEG-SS (10%, w/v) solutions.

For in vivo assessment, a 3-mm incision was created on the dorsal region of male C57BL/6 mice (20-25 g), followed by subcutaneous implantation of 50 mg of the Cy5.5-labeled hydrogel (n = 5). Hydrogel degradation was monitored using an in vivo imaging system at predetermined time points by monitoring Cy5.5 fluorescence (λex = 690 nm, λem = 700 nm). Additionally, the major organs and gastrointestinal tissues were harvested at selected time points and subjected to ex vivo fluorescence imaging to evaluate the biodistribution and clearance pathways of the degradation products.

In vivo oral mucosal repair in a rat model

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (8-9 weeks old, 250-300 g) were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane via inhalation, and anesthesia was maintained at 1.5% isoflurane during surgery. Circular mucosal defects (3-mm diameter) were created on the buccal mucosa using a biopsy punch, and the epithelial layer was removed with scissors. Defects were treated with GENGIGEL® or PAG hydrogel, with untreated defects serving as controls. Wound healing was monitored by photographing the defects daily from day 0 to day 5, and wound areas (S) were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54 g). On postoperative day 5, 3 mL of blood was collected from each rat via cardiac puncture for complete blood count and serum biochemical analysis. Rats were then euthanized, and the buccal mucosa at the wound site was harvested for paraffin embedding. Wound healing ratio was calculated as the percentage reduction in wound area: [(S0 – St)/S0] × 100%, where S0 is the wound area immediately after surgery and St is the wound area at the indicated time point (n = 8 per group).

In vivo tongue wound hemostasis and repair in a rat model

Male SD rats (8-9 weeks old, 250-300 g) were anesthetized via inhalation of 2.5% isoflurane, with anesthesia maintained at 1.5% isoflurane throughout the procedure. The tongue was exposed using a circular clamp, and a full-thickness defect (4-mm diameter) was created with a biopsy punch. The resulting wounds were treated by suture closure, gelatin sponge filling, or in situ application of fibrin glue or PAG hydrogel. Excess blood on the wound surface was carefully absorbed using pre-weighed gauze to quantify blood loss. Hemostatic performance was evaluated by recording bleeding time with a stopwatch and calculating blood loss from the weight difference of gauze before and after the procedure. On days 4 and 9 after surgery, rats were sacrificed, and the repaired tongue tissues were excised and processed for paraffin embedding and subsequent histological examination.

In vivo oral mucosal repair in porcine models

Female Bama pigs (≈30 kg) were anesthetized via intramuscular injection of Zoletil-50 (1.0 mL) and maintained with 1.5% isoflurane during surgery. Pigs were placed in a lateral position, and circular mucosal defects (5-mm diameter) were created on the buccal and labial mucosa. After cleaning the wound site, the defects were treated with PAG hydrogel, with commercial GENGIGEL® as a positive control and untreated defects as a negative control. Wound healing was documented on days 2, 4, and 6 post-surgery. Defect areas were quantified using ImageJ (version 1.54g) to calculate wound healing ratios (n = 4) as [(S0 - St)/S0] × 100%, where S0 is the wound area immediately after surgery and St is the wound area at the predetermined postoperative day.

Bacterial colonization analysis

Bacterial colonization on the wound surface was assessed in various treatment groups. Samples from mucosal defects and tongue wounds were collected at multiple postoperative time points using sterile medical swabs. The swabs were immersed in LB broth and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Bacterial growth was quantified by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600). For colony analysis, cultures were serially diluted and plated on LB agar, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 12 h to assess bacterial colony formation.

Histology and immunohistochemical analysis

Tissue specimens were collected from the wound sites, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and embedded in paraffin. Serial 4-μm sections were prepared for analysis. Histological evaluation was performed using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome staining to assess tissue morphology and collagen deposition.

For immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2, and nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 3% BSA in PBS. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature. IHC staining was visualized with DAB, nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, and sections were mounted for imaging. Images were captured using a slide scanner (NanoZoomer S60, Japan), and positive staining areas were quantified using ImageJ. IHC staining was performed to evaluate inflammation, immune response, and nerve tissue integrity using myeloperoxidase (MPO, 1:1000, Servicebio, China), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, 1:500, Servicebio, China), neurofilament 200 (NF200, 1:1000, Servicebio, China), and protein gene product 9.5 (PGP9.5, 1:500, Servicebio, China). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining was conducted to further assess tissue regeneration, including angiogenesis and vascular maturation (CD31/α-SMA, 1:1200, Servicebio, China), peripheral nerve regeneration (growth-associated protein 43, GAP43, 1:500, Servicebio, China), and macrophage polarization using inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, 1:1000, Servicebio, China) and CD206 (1:1000, Servicebio, China).

Transcriptome analysis

On postoperative day 5, approximately 50 mg of buccal mucosal wound tissue was harvested from rats and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen before homogenization. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA purity, concentration, and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, Qubit RNA BR Assay Kit, and ScreenTape system.

High-quality RNA samples were subjected to mRNA enrichment using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module. Subsequently, mRNA libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. Library concentration was determined using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit, fragment size distribution was analyzed with D1000 ScreenTape, and the molar concentration of each library was accurately quantified using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Illumina) with universal qPCR master mix. Finally, the qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the NovaSeq S4 reagent kit to generate paired-end sequencing data.

For RT-qPCR analysis, first-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using SweScript All-in-One RT SuperMix for qPCR. qPCR was performed using Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio™ real-time PCR system. Gene expression levels were quantified based on SYBR Green fluorescence detection. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1 of the Supporting Information.

Animal experiments and statistical analysis

The animal experiments were conducted following the guidelines of the Animal Research Committee of Shenzhen TOP Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Protocol No. TOPGM-IACUC-2024-0371). All animals used in this study, including mice, SD rats, and Bama pigs, were purchased from Beijing Fuhao Experimental Animal Breeding Center (Beijing, China).

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.5.0 and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Statistical significance among multiple groups was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), while comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-sided t-test (ns = not significant, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

Results and discussion

Fabrication of the PAG hydrogel

A two-component injectable hydrogel sealant composed of tetra-armed poly(ethylene glycol) succinimidyl succinate (tetra-PEG-SS) and amine-functionalized gelatin (AGel) was developed for rapid hemostatic sealing and repair of oral soft-tissue defects. Cold-water fish gelatin was selected to improve precursor fluidity at room temperature and was subsequently functionalized with ethylenediamine to increase the primary amine density and crosslinking efficiency (Fig. S1A). Successful amine functionalization was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy, which showed characteristic methylene proton signals at 3.40 and 3.04 ppm corresponding to ethylenediamine (Fig. S1B and C). Quantitative analysis further revealed that the amine content increased from 0.52 ± 0.003 mmol/g in pristine gelatin to 0.93 ± 0.023 mmol/g after modification (Fig. S2). Tetra-PEG-SS was synthesized via NHS activation of tetra-PEG-COOH (Fig. S3), serving as a multifunctional crosslinker to enhance network integrity and mechanical strength. 1H NMR confirmed successful NHS functionalization, with characteristic NHS proton signals and integration ratios (Ia:Ib:Id) consistent with theoretical values (2:1:1) (Fig. S3B).

To prepare the PAG hydrogel, equal volumes of tetra-PEG-SS and AGel solutions at identical concentrations were mixed using a double-barrel syringe equipped with a helical static mixer. Upon mixing, the NHS-amine coupling reaction proceeded rapidly, yielding a uniform light-yellow hydrogel without the need for external initiators, heat, catalysts, or UV irradiation. Gelation kinetics were tuned by varying precursor concentration. Increasing polymer concentration from 1.0 to 10.0% (w/v) shortened the gelation time from 33.0 ± 1.2 s to 5.4 ± 0.4 s due to the increased reactive group density (Fig. 1A). However, further increasing the concentration to 15% slightly prolonged gelation (8.1 ± 0.8 s), likely because the elevated viscosity hindered the diffusion of reactive groups. Notably, no gelation occurred when tetra-PEG-SS was mixed with unmodified gelatin (Fig. S4), underscoring the critical role of amine functionalization in network formation. The chemical structure of the PAG gel was verified by ATR-FTIR spectroscopy, which exhibited characteristic peaks at around 1726 cm−1 (ester C=O), 1640 cm−1 (amide I), 1540 cm−1 (amide Ⅱ), and 1102 cm−1 (ether repeat unit) (Fig. S5). Swelling properties of PAG hydrogel

Controlled swelling is critical for effective tissue sealing and nutrient transport, while excessive swelling can compromise both adhesion and mechanical stability, thereby limiting hydrogel performance in dynamic oral environments [21,54]. We then evaluated the swelling behavior of the engineered hydrogel in artificial saliva by monitoring volume and mass swelling ratios (Fig. S7A). After 24 h of incubation, all hydrogels reached swelling equilibrium. Hydrogels prepared at 3%, 5%, 10%, and 15% (w/v) exhibited maximum volume-swelling ratios of 7.8 ± 0.5%, 3.8 ± 0.4%, 12.1 ± 4.0%, and 50.0 ± 1.9% (Fig. S7B), respectively, with corresponding mass-swelling ratios of 18.6 ± 0.9%, 5.0 ± 1.2%, 23.6 ± 2.9%, and 57.6 ± 1.5% (Fig. 1B). Hydrogels at lower concentrations generally exhibited relatively low swelling, which could be attributed to the combined effects of covalent crosslinks, hydrophilic-hydrophobic balance, and gelatin-mediated non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic attractions. In particular, the reduced swelling observed from 3% to 5% (w/v) was associated with the formation of a denser and more effective covalent crosslinked network at higher polymer content. In contrast, the pronounced swelling of the 15% hydrogel was likely due to the substantially increased precursor viscosity at high concentration, which restricted polymer chain diffusion and mixing during gelation, leading to a less uniform network with heterogeneous crosslinking regions that permit greater water uptake.

Mechanical properties of PAG hydrogel

Hydrogel-based sealants for oral wound repair must withstand repetitive mechanical loading caused by mastication, swallowing, and speaking while maintaining structural and functional integrity. The mechanical properties of the hydrogel were evaluated using rheological and compressive tests. Rheological analyses of hydrogels prepared at 3%, 5%, 10%, and 15% revealed solid-like viscoelastic behavior, with storage moduli (G′) consistently exceeding loss moduli (G″) across the tested frequency range (Fig. 1C). Increasing polymer concentration progressively enhanced G′ from 0.4 ± 0.009 kPa at 3% to 5.7 ± 0.2 kPa at 10% and 8.7 ± 0.3 kPa at 15% (Fig. S8), indicating increased network stiffness. Further amplitude sweep measurements revealed the intersection of G′ and G″ at higher strain, indicative of network yielding. The crossover strain increased from 45% at 3% (w/v) to a maximum of 70% at 5% (w/v), followed by a gradual decrease to 65% and 58% for the 10% and 15% (w/v) formulations, respectively (Fig. 1D and Fig. S9). This trend suggests that increasing polymer concentration enhances network stiffness, whereas an excessive concentration leads to a reduced yield strain and an earlier onset of network yielding under deformation.

Compression testing further supported the rheological results (Fig. 1E). The ultimate compressive strength of PAG hydrogels increased significantly with polymer concentration, from 18.1 ± 3.3 kPa (3%) to 192.0 ± 6.5 kPa (5%) and 352.7 ± 27.2 kPa (10%), consistent with enhanced crosslinking density (Fig. 1F). The 10% hydrogel outperformed commercial fibrin glue in compressive strength, while no further improvement was observed at 15%. Notably, the 10% formulation exhibited a higher fracture strain than the 15% hydrogel, indicating a favorable balance between stiffness and deformability. The compressive strength of hydrogels at swelling equilibrium in artificial saliva was also evaluated (Fig. S10). Both the 10% and 15% hydrogels retained substantial mechanical strength (≈250 kPa), markedly higher than 3% (16.0 ± 2.0 kPa), 5% (175.0 ± 8.7 kPa) hydrogels, and fibrin glue (126.7 ± 6.7 kPa), demonstrating robust stability under hydrated conditions (Fig. 1G).

The fatigue resistance of PAG was evaluated via cyclic compression testing at 50% strain using the 10% hydrogel as a representative. Over 50 loading-unloading cycles, both the as-prepared (Fig. 1H) and swollen hydrogels (Fig. S11A and B) in artificial saliva maintained structural integrity without visible failure. The stress-strain profiles showed minimal changes after cyclic loading, indicating that the hydrogel maintained its mechanical performance under repeated compression. Meanwhile, a modest increase in compressive strength and modulus was detected after cycling (Fig. 1I and Fig. S11C), which may be attributed to stress-induced network rearrangement and slight structural densification during by cyclic deformation. These results demonstrate that PAG hydrogels, particularly at 10% concentration, possess strong mechanical robustness, extensibility, and fatigue resistance under repetitive mechanical loading.

Tissue adhesive performance

Beyond mechanical strength, effective oral sealants must exhibit strong and reliable tissue adhesion to maintain defect coverage under dynamic oral conditions. To evaluate the adhesive performance of PAG hydrogels, lap shear, 180° peeling, and tensile adhesion tests were conducted on freshly isolated porcine tongue and buccal mucosa, with commercial fibrin glue serving as a control (Fig. 2A–C; Fig. S12 and S13). On porcine tongue tissue, the adhesive performance of PAG was enhanced with precursor concentration. Specifically, shear strength, interfacial toughness, and tensile adhesion strength increased from 4.1 ± 0.4 kPa, 10.6 ± 0.6 J/m2, and 6.0 ± 0.4 kPa at 3% to maximum values of 30.3 ± 3.8 kPa, 52.2 ± 5.5 J/m2, and 16.9 ± 0.7 kPa at 10%, respectively. No statistically significant difference was observed between the 10% and 15% formulations, both of which markedly outperformed fibrin glue (6.8 ± 0.4 kPa shear strength, 5.9 ± 0.6 J/m2 toughness, and 3.6 ± 0.3 kPa tensile strength). A similar concentration-dependent trend was observed on buccal mucosa, where the 10% PAG hydrogel exhibited the highest adhesion, ranging from 2.0- to 4.7-fold higher than that of fibrin glue across the tested modes. Tissue adhesive performance is governed by both interfacial bonding strength and the intrinsic toughness of the matrix [55,56]. Upon contact with moist oral tissues, the NHS ester groups in tetra-PEG-SS rapidly react with surface-exposed primary amines to form covalent amide bonds, providing strong and durable interfacial anchorage. The enhanced adhesion observed at higher polymer concentrations can be attributed to increased matrix stiffness and a higher effective availability of NHS ester groups for covalent coupling. Additionally, gelatin-mediated electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and ionic interactions with tissue surfaces further contribute to the overall adhesive performance (Fig. 2D). The relatively lower adhesive strength on buccal mucosa compared to tongue tissue is likely due to its smoother and more compliant epithelial surface, reduced keratinization, and higher intrinsic hydration, which collectively decrease the effective contact area and limit the formation of stable interfacial interactions.

The microstructure of the adhesive interface was further examined using H&E staining and SEM. H&E staining revealed a distinct and continuous interfacial layer between the hydrogel and porcine tongue tissue, confirming robust adhesion (Fig. 2E). SEM images highlighted the porous architecture of PAG and intimate contact between the hydrogel and tissue surfaces (Fig. 2F). The hydrogel closely conformed to the tissue topography, forming a continuous interpenetrating layer that enabled stable adhesion even on complex surfaces. These findings underscore the ability of PAG hydrogels to achieve strong and adaptable tissue attachment, a critical requirement for effectively sealing irregular oral defects.

In vitro defect sealing performance

The adhesive sealing capability of PAG was evaluated using 5-mm diameter defects created on freshly isolated porcine tongue and buccal mucosa. The defects were sealed in situ with the hydrogel, and burst pressure measurements were performed using a custom-built setup in which internal pressure was gradually increased by pumping PBS until failure occurred. As shown in Fig. 2G and S14, the burst pressures on tongue tissue increased with precursor concentration, reaching 67.9 ± 15.1 mmHg, 243.6 ± 16.0 mmHg, 468.8 ± 28.2 mmHg, and 520.6 ± 22.0 mmHg for hydrogels prepared at concentrations of 3%, 5%, 10%, and 15% (w/v), respectively. Notably, the 10% and 15% hydrogels exhibited burst pressures significantly exceeding normal human arterial blood pressure (≈120 mmHg), indicating their potential to withstand high-pressure hemorrhage. Moreover, these values were 7-fold higher than those of commercial fibrin glue (67.2 ± 6.7 mmHg). A similar concentration-dependent trend was observed in buccal mucosa, with burst pressures reaching 387.1 ± 29.9 mmHg and 439.9 ± 17.0 mmHg for the 10% and 15% hydrogels, respectively, both significantly outperforming fibrin glue. Since the 10% and 15% formulations exhibited comparable overall performance, the 10% hydrogel was selected for subsequent studies to balance optimal network homogeneity and processability while reducing precursor consumption.

To further demonstrate sealing capacity, 5-mm defects on porcine tongue and buccal mucosa were sealed with PAG hydrogel and subjected to torsion tests (Fig. 2H and S15). The hydrogel maintained strong adhesion and structural integrity under stretching, twisting, bending, and water flushing, without detachment or visible morphological damage. Ex vivo sealing stability was further assessed by immersing the tissues in artificial saliva containing crushed rat chow particles under continuous mechanical agitation to simulate the dynamic shear forces of the oral environment (Fig. 2I and J). Commercial GENGIGEL® and fibrin glue were included as controls. Within 5 min, food particles penetrated untreated defects, and GENGIGEL® detached from the tissue, indicating failure of sealing. After 6 h of continuous agitation, fibrin glue also detached, leaving defects filled with food debris, similar to the untreated controls. In contrast, PAG effectively resisted external stresses, maintained structural integrity, and prevented infiltration of food particles over the entire 48 h observation period, demonstrating robust and durable sealing performance under dynamic oral conditions.

Biocompatibility and degradability

Biocompatibility is a critical requirement for hydrogel sealants intended for clinical applications to avoid adverse outcomes such as inflammation, immune reactions, or tissue toxicity. We first assessed the hemocompatibility of PAG via a hemolysis assay by incubating hydrogels with erythrocyte suspensions. Representative images showed a light-red supernatant comparable to the PBS negative control, in contrast to the bright-red coloration observed for the Triton X-100 positive control (Fig. 3A). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the hemolysis ratio of PAG remained below 1.0%, well within the acceptable threshold of 5%, even at concentrations up to 100 mg/mL, demonstrating excellent blood compatibility [57,58]. The cytocompatibility of PAG was further assessed by culturing HGFs with hydrogel extracts and measuring cell viability using the CCK-8 assay. Over 3 days, cell viability remained above 90% and exceeded that of the control on days 2 and 3, indicating the promotion of cell growth (Fig. 3B). Cell migration, a key process in wound healing, was assessed using an in vitro scratch assay. HGFs exposed to PAG extracts exhibited migration ratios of 54.8 ± 3.0%, 56.8 ± 8.0%, and 65.6 ± 0.5% at 12 h for extract concentrations of 1, 5, and 10 mg/mL, respectively, compared with 35.6 ± 2.4% in the control group. This enhanced motility is likely attributable to the intrinsic bioactivity of gelatin, which facilitates cell adhesion and proliferation through integrin-mediated interactions. These results indicate that PAG effectively supports fibroblast migration, highlighting its potential as a bioactive dressing to accelerate tissue repair.

Hydrogel dressings for oral mucosa should provide effective protection during the early stage of wound healing while exhibiting controlled degradability to minimize the risks associated with prolonged foreign material retention and facilitate tissue regeneration. In vitro degradation studies in artificial saliva at 37 °C showed moderate mass loss (24.8 ± 3.5%) within the first 24 h, followed by accelerated breakdown, with complete degradation occurring by 48 h (Fig. 3F). This rapid degradation is attributed to the intrinsic cyclization-induced cleavage of succinyl ester linkages, as reported in our previous work [43,44]. The in vivo biodegradation behavior of PAG was further evaluated via subcutaneous implantation of Cy5.5-labeled hydrogel in the dorsal region of mice. In vivo fluorescence imaging revealed a time-dependent decrease in fluorescence intensity, which became nearly undetectable by day 11, indicating progressive degradation of the PAG hydrogel. Ex vivo biodistribution analysis of major organs further demonstrated that the degradation products were primarily cleared through hepatic, renal, and gastrointestinal pathways (Fig. S16). The slower degradation observed in vivo compared with that observed in vitro was likely due to reduced interstitial fluid exchange and limited mass transport within the subcutaneous environment. These findings demonstrated the excellent biocompatibility, pro-migratory bioactivity, and controllable biodegradability of the PAG hydrogel.

Histological analysis of oral mucosal tissues on day 5 further highlighted differences in tissue organization. H&E staining revealed complete epithelial coverage in all groups. To further assess inflammatory responses and tissue repair, immunohistochemical staining was performed for key inflammatory and angiogenic markers. MPO, a marker of neutrophil activation, and TNF-α, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, were first evaluated [59,60]. On day 5, the untreated and GENGIGEL® groups exhibited high MPO and TNF-α expression, indicating persistent inflammation (Fig. 4H and K). In contrast, PAG treatment markedly reduced the expression of both markers, demonstrating effective attenuation of neutrophil infiltration and local inflammatory responses. Macrophage polarization plays a pivotal role in wound healing, with pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages mediating pathogen clearance, whereas anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages support tissue repair and angiogenesis through growth factor secretion. Macrophage phenotype was assessed using iNOS and CD206 as markers for M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively [61,62]. PAG treatment significantly decreased the proportion of iNOS-positive M1 macrophages while increasing CD206-positive M2 macrophages relative to the untreated and GENGIGEL® groups, suggesting that the hydrogel promotes a pro-regenerative immune microenvironment (Fig. 4I and L). By contrast, the higher prevalence of M1 macrophages in the untreated and GENGIGEL® groups likely reflects sustained inflammatory stimuli due to the absence of an effective physical barrier, which may impede resolution and tissue regeneration.

Angiogenesis and vascular maturation are essential for supplying oxygen and nutrients to regenerating tissues. We therefore performed CD31 and α-SMA immunostaining to evaluate endothelial cell-mediated neovascularization and myofibroblast-driven vascular maturation, respectively [63,64]. Wounds treated by PAG exhibited substantially higher expression of both markers (Fig. 4J and M), indicating enhanced formation of functional blood vessels that likely support granulation tissue development and accelerate mucosal repair. These findings reveal that PAG not only suppresses excessive inflammation by limiting neutrophil infiltration and promoting M2 macrophage polarization but also facilitates angiogenesis and tissue remodeling, thereby establishing a pro-regenerative microenvironment for rapid and effective mucosal healing.

Throughout the follow-up period, PAG-treated tongue wounds exhibited rapid surface closure and nearly complete contour restoration with minimal tissue contraction. In contrast, the untreated and suture groups exhibited delayed healing characterized by pronounced concave deformities, while the gelatin sponge and fibrin glue groups displayed irregular wound surfaces, indicating impaired wound regeneration. Histological analysis at the early stage (day 4) revealed incomplete epithelial coverage and substantial connective tissue defects in the untreated and suture groups (Fig. S22A). In the gelatin sponge and fibrin glue groups, residual material persisted within the wound, markedly impeding epithelial continuity and cellular infiltration. By comparison, the PAG-treated group exhibited substantial regeneration of the underlying connective tissue accompanied by material degradation. Consistently, Masson's trichrome staining confirmed early collagen deposition in the PAG group, with fibers displaying a relatively ordered arrangement (Fig. S22B). By day 9, the untreated and sutured groups had achieved epithelial closure but still exhibited pronounced concave deformities, accompanied by disorganized muscle fibers and irregular collagen distribution (Fig. 6E and Fig. S23). The gelatin sponge group showed the poorest repair outcome, with persistent epithelial disruption, extensive connective tissue damage, and pronounced inflammatory infiltration. Fibrin glue treatment also resulted in tissue depression and excessive collagen accumulation, indicative of fibrotic remodeling. In sharp contrast, PAG-treated wounds demonstrated near-complete restoration of normal tongue architecture, featuring a continuous epithelium, well-aligned muscle fibers, and uniformly organized collagen without excessive deposition.

Immunohistochemical analyses revealed that MPO and TNF-α expression were markedly lower in the PAG group compared with all control groups at days 4 and 9 (Fig. 6F,G and Fig. S24), indicating effective suppression of excessive inflammation. Immunostaining for iNOS and CD206 further demonstrated that the PAG-treated group maintained the lowest iNOS expression and the highest CD206 expression at both time points (Fig. 6H,I and Fig. S25A and B), indicating a sustained M2-dominant macrophage phenotype that supports an anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative microenvironment. Given the tongue's rich vascular and neural composition, angiogenesis and nerve regeneration are critical for functional repair. Analyses of CD31 and α-SMA expression revealed that PAG consistently induced the highest levels among all groups (Fig. 6J and Fig. S25C), reflecting robust angiogenic activity and organized vessel formation, thereby ensuring adequate oxygen and nutrient supply to the healing tissue. To further evaluate neural regeneration during tongue tissue repair, immunostaining for GAP43 (a marker of axonal regeneration), PGP9.5 (a pan-neuronal axonal marker), and NF200 (a marker of mature nerve fibers) was performed on regenerated tongue tissues (Fig. 6H and J; Fig. S26 and S27) [[65], [66], [67], [68]]. Quantitative analysis revealed significantly higher expression levels of GAP43, PGP9.5, and NF200 in the PAG-treated group than in the other groups, indicating enhanced neural regeneration, including axonal sprouting, nerve fiber growth, and maturation of regenerated axons. These findings demonstrated that PAG hydrogel achieved rapid hemostasis in full-thickness tongue defects and, by establishing an anti-inflammatory, M2 macrophage-dominant microenvironment, promoted vascular and neural repair, thereby supporting tongue tissue restoration.

In vivo oral mucosal repair in porcine models

Building on the promising results from rat studies, we evaluated the sealing and healing performance of the PAG hydrogel in a clinically relevant porcine oral mucosal defect model, including both buccal and labial sites, with GENGIGEL® and untreated defects serving as controls (Fig. 7A). Circular 5-mm defects were created on the mucosa with a biopsy punch (Fig. S28). Upon in situ application, PAG rapidly conformed to the moist tissue surface and formed a robust, fluid-tight seal within 10 s, effectively preventing bleeding and protecting the wound from mechanical irritation. No abnormal health conditions, such as fever, lethargy, or infection, were observed during daily monitoring. For buccal mucosal defects, macroscopic analysis revealed markedly accelerated wound closure in the PAG group, achieving 67.3 ± 3.2% and 97.0 ± 2.5% closure on days 2 and 6, respectively, which were higher than those of GENGIGEL® (35.3 ± 6.2% and 86.2 ± 4.1%) and untreated wounds (31.9 ± 8.2% and 80.6 ± 3.2%) (Fig. 7B and C). In contrast, the control and GENGIGEL® groups exhibited incomplete epithelialization, inflamed granulation tissue, and pseudomembrane formation (Fig. 7D). A similar healing pattern was observed in the labial mucosa after 6 days of repair. PAG treatment resulted in higher wound closure rates and more complete epithelial restoration compared with the untreated and GENGIGEL® groups (Fig. S30 and S31). Consistent with the buccal mucosa findings, immunostaining revealed markedly reduced inflammation (lower MPO, TNF-α, and iNOS expression), enhanced myofibroblast activity and neovascularization (higher α-SMA and CD31 expression), and a predominance of M2 macrophages (elevated CD206 expression) (Figs. These results validate the therapeutic potential of the PAG hydrogel for oral mucosal repair, demonstrating rapid wound sealing and organized tissue regeneration through inflammation suppression, enhanced angiogenesis, and the establishment of a pro-regenerative immune microenvironment.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed an injectable hydrogel-based sealant (PAG) composed of PEG and gelatin for rapid hemostatic sealing and repair of oral soft-tissue injuries. Upon mixing NHS-terminated tetra-PEG-SS with amine-rich AGel, the hydrogel spontaneously forms via amide bond formation, enabling facile application in real-time clinical settings. The optimized PAG formulation undergoes in situ gelation within seconds and exhibits low swelling and robust mechanical properties, while demonstrating superior adhesive strength and burst pressure compared with commercial fibrin glue, allowing rapid and effective sealing of soft-tissue wounds. PAG demonstrates excellent cytocompatibility, hemocompatibility, and biodegradability while promoting fibroblast proliferation. In an acute rat tongue perforation model, PAG achieved rapid hemostasis within 3 s and accelerated tissue and neural regeneration, outperforming sutures, gelatin sponges, and fibrin glue. Moreover, PAG effectively protected early-stage mucosal wounds and accelerated repair in both rat and porcine models. Mechanistic studies indicate that PAG establishes a pro-regenerative microenvironment by attenuating excessive inflammation, enhancing angiogenesis, and promoting M2-dominant macrophage polarization. Overall, our results highlight the strong clinical potential of PAG hydrogel for sutureless sealing and repair of oral soft-tissue injuries.

Despite these promising outcomes, several aspects warrant further investigation to validate the clinical translation potential of the engineered hydrogel. Additional optimization of precursor composition, gelation kinetics, adhesive and mechanical performance, and handling characteristics is required to improve its adaptability in complex clinical scenarios. Evaluation in larger or more anatomically complex defect models is also essential to better simulate clinical conditions. While the current degradation profile is suitable for rapidly healing oral injuries, it may require further tuning for chronic, large-scale, or slow-regenerating wounds that demand prolonged tissue support. Long-term in vivo studies are also required to assess potential chronic inflammatory responses or delayed adverse effects associated with degradation products. Moreover, considering the bacteria-rich environment of the oral cavity, incorporating intrinsic antimicrobial functionality into the hydrogel may further reduce infection risk and enhance regenerative outcomes.
