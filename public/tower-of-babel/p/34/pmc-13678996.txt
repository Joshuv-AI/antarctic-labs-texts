Association of creatinine to body weight ratio with glycemic progression and remission in Chinese adults with prediabetes: a large-scale retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hengchang Xu, Yueqin Gou, Wenhong Zhai, Bo Lu, Zhihui Ye, Dongni Sun, Weijia Xiao, Changchun Cao, Haohan Zhai, Haochuan Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe creatinine-to-body weight ratio (Cre/BW) has been proposed as a practical surrogate marker of skeletal muscle mass. Emerging evidence highlights a notable correlation connecting Cre/BW to diabetes alongside various metabolic dysfunctions; however, its role in glycemic worsening and the restoration of normoglycemia in individuals with prediabetes has not been well defined. This study, therefore, examined the effect of Cre/BW on glycemic remission and progression among Chinese adults with prediabetes.MethodsA total of 24,506 adults with prediabetes from 11 cities in China were included in this retrospective cohort study based on health examination data. Cox proportional hazards models and subgroup analyses were used to estimate associations of Cre/BW with incident diabetes and remission to normoglycemia. Potential nonlinearity in these relationships was assessed by RCS analyses.ResultsDuring an observation period spanning 2.9 years (median), 11,170 participants (45.58%) achieved remission to normoglycemia, and 2,641 (10.78%) progressed to diabetes. In multivariable-adjusted Cox models, higher Cre/BW was linked to a greater likelihood of remission (HR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.28–1.55, p < 0.0001) and lower hazard of progression to diabetes (HR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.35–0.52, p < 0.0001). RCS analyses indicated a nonlinear association between Cre/BW and diabetes progression: the HR was 0.29 (95% CI: 0.20–0.40) when Cre/BW was < 1.07 μmol/L/kg and increased to 0.66 (95% CI: 0.46–0.95) when Cre/BW was ≥ 1.07 μmol/L/kg. Subgroup analyses suggested that the effect of Cre/BW on glycemic remission was more pronounced in men and in participants aged 40–50 years; a stronger effect of Cre/BW on glycemic progression was also observed among those aged < 30 years (all interaction p < 0.05).ConclusionIn adults with prediabetes, Cre/BW was linked to bidirectional changes in glycemic status: higher Cre/BW was linked to a lower hazard of progression to diabetes and a greater likelihood of remission to normoglycemia.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02406-8.Keywords: Prediabetes, Creatinine-to-body weight ratio, Diabetes, Remission to normoglycemia, Cohort study, Muscle mass

Introduction

Prediabetes—commonly characterized by impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) and/or impaired fasting glucose (IFG)—is an intermediate dysglycemic condition that lies on the continuum from normoglycemia to overt diabetes [1]. Because symptoms are often subtle or absent, this condition is frequently overlooked in routine practice and may not prompt timely clinical attention [2–4]. Growing data show that prediabetes is not only a harbinger of subsequent diabetes development [2–4], but is also linked to multiple chronic metabolic disorders and unfavorable health outcomes [5–10]. Notably, recent studies indicate that achieving and maintaining appropriate glycemic control during the prediabetic stage can lower the risk of diabetes-related complications [11–13]. Accordingly, identifying actionable determinants that influence shifts in glycemic status among individuals with prediabetes is essential for improving long-term health and quality of life.

Over the past decade, mounting interest has focused on how skeletal muscle quantity relates to metabolic disease risk [14, 15]. Given that skeletal muscle is the predominant insulin-sensitive tissue in the human body, it plays a pivotal role in glucose uptake and overall glycemic regulation [16]. Consistent epidemiologic and clinical evidence demonstrates that diminished muscle mass is linked to an increased burden of insulin resistance, abnormal glucose metabolism, and diabetes [17, 18]. Nevertheless, direct quantification of muscle mass with bioelectrical impedance analysis or dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry remains challenging in large population-based settings, as these approaches depend on specialized equipment, time, and financial resources. As an alternative, creatinine-to–body weight ratio (Cre/BW) offers a highly economical and easily obtainable surrogate marker reflecting skeletal mass [19]. Because circulating creatinine is derived largely from skeletal muscle metabolism, its level generally tracks with muscle quantity [20]. Normalizing creatinine to body weight may further improve interpretability by incorporating body size, thereby providing a metric that may better reflect muscle-related metabolic capacity than creatinine alone [21]. However, serum creatinine is also widely used as an indirect marker of kidney function, and its interpretation in epidemiological studies is inherently limited [22–24]. Circulating creatinine concentrations are influenced not only by creatinine generation from skeletal muscle but also by renal clearance, age, sex, dietary intake, and body composition [22–24]. Thus, creatinine-based indices such as Cre/BW should be interpreted as pragmatic composite markers reflecting both muscle-related creatinine production and renal handling of creatinine, rather than as direct measures of skeletal muscle mass or kidney function [25]. Several investigations have evaluated Cre/BW in relation to metabolic outcomes. Hashimoto and colleagues reported an effect of Cre/BW on subsequent T2DM risk [19], and other evidence suggests that decreased Cre/BW is linked to a greater likelihood of fatty liver [26]. Nevertheless, the extent to which Cre/BW relates to glycemic transitions—specifically progression to diabetes or reversion to normoglycemia—among individuals with prediabetes remains insufficiently characterized, warranting further systematic investigation. Accordingly, the present study used a retrospective cohort to examine the effect of Cre/BW on longitudinal glycemic transitions in prediabetic adults.

Methods

Data source

Anonymized data from the Dryad digital repository were used for this retrospective cohort analysis [27]. The dataset, initially compiled by Chen et al. [27], is publicly accessible under Dryad’s user terms, and the authors of the present work had full access to the complete data for secondary analyses; all procedures adhered to the repository’s usage requirements. In the source study, the protocol received approval from the ethics committee of the Rich Healthcare Group, which granted a waiver of written informed consent [27]. All procedures in this secondary evaluation complied fully with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

Participant-level clinical data originated within a preventive screening repository managed through the Rich Healthcare Group network. Individuals received routine check-ups at affiliated centers across 32 Chinese provinces during a seven-year timeframe (2010–2016). The source population included 685,277 adults (≥20 years), provided they attended multiple clinical visits throughout our evaluation timeframe. We excluded participants who met any of the following conditions: (1) non-physiological body mass index (BMI) values (n = 152); (2) absent baseline data on sex, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), body weight, or heigh (n = 135,317); (3) diabetes diagnosed before enrollment (n = 7,112); (4) follow-up duration < 2 years (n = 324,233); (5) indeterminate diabetes status at follow-up (n = 6,630); (6) failure to satisfy prediabetes definitions at baseline (n = 185,815); (7) absent data on Cre/BW (n = 1,342); or (8) outlying Cre/BW values exceeding ±3 standard deviations from the mean (n = 170). After these exclusions, 24,506 participants with prediabetes at baseline were retained for analysis (Fig. 1).

Study population

Variables

Assessment of Cre/BW and outcome

Cre/BW was derived by dividing Cre (μmol/L) by body weight (kg). Glycemic remission was recorded when a follow-up FPG measurement was < 5.6 mmol/L and the participant did not report having diabetes [28]. Prediabetes at baseline was operationalized as impaired fasting glucose, defined by an FPG range of 5.6–6.9 mmol/L [28]. Incident diabetes during follow-up was identified by self-reported diabetes and/or an FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L [28].

Covariates

Based on biological plausibility and established precedents in the literature, potential confounders were selected for analysis. These covariates were categorized as follows: (1) Continuous variables: These included demographic and anthropometric data (age, height), hemodynamic parameters (diastolic blood pressure [DBP] and systolic blood pressure [SBP]), and biochemical indices, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and FPG. The lipid panel included total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), while hepatic function was assessed via alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST); (2) Categorical variables: These encompassed sex, family history of diabetes and lifestyle behaviors (drinking and smoking).

Data collection and measurements

Baseline evaluations were completed by certified clinical staff using prespecified operating procedures. Participants wore lightweight clothing and removed their shoes for anthropometric assessment. Body mass was determined using calibrated digital scales with an accuracy of 0.1 kg, and standing height was read to the nearest 0.1 cm with a wall-mounted stadiometer. Resting blood pressure was obtained with a mercury sphygmomanometer after the participant had remained seated for at least 5 minutes. BMI was derived as weight divided by height squared. At enrollment, lifestyle exposures were captured with standardized, structured questionnaires [27]. Smoking status and alcohol intake were each classified as current, former, or never. The same questionnaires were used to record demographic characteristics and relevant medical history. For laboratory testing, venous blood was obtained from adults who had undergone an overnight fast of at least 10 hours and analyzed on a Beckman 5800 automated chemistry platform [27].

Missing data processing

In our investigation, an analysis of missing data revealed several variables with incomplete records as outlined below: blood pressure (n = 7), TC (n = 398), triglycerides (n = 398), alanine aminotransferase (ALT; n = 133), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN; n = 1,575). In contrast, larger amounts of incomplete data were noted for lipid fractions (LDL-C: n = 8,733; HDL-C: n = 9,363), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; n = 13,899), and lifestyle variables (smoking and alcohol use; n = 16,300 each; 72.02%). To reduce the risk of bias and preserve statistical efficiency, missing data were addressed through multiple imputation by chained equations [29]. The imputation procedure included ALT, SBP, FPG, age, BUN, TC, sex, TG, height, HDL-C, DBP, AST, LDL-C, family history of diabetes, and smoking and drinking status. Missingness was handled under the missing-at-random assumption [30].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in EmpowerStats. Participants were categorized into three groups according to blood glucose status. Continuous data with an approximately normal distribution was reported as mean ± SD, whereas non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median (range). Categorical data was presented as counts and proportions. Differences across blood glucose status groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA (normal continuous variables), chi-square test (categorical variables) or Kruskal–Wallis test (skewed continuous variables).

The relationships of Cre/BW with glycemic remission and progression were examined using Cox proportional hazards models, with effect estimates reported as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Three models were fitted: an unadjusted model (Model 1); a minimally adjusted model including age, sex, height, SBP, and DBP (Model 2); and a fully adjusted model that additionally incorporated FPG, blood urea nitrogen, ALT, AST, triglycerides, LDL-C, family history of diabetes, and drinking and smoking status (Model 3). We tested the proportional hazards assumption by including an interaction term between Cre/BW and the logarithm of follow-up time in the model. No significant violation of this assumption was discovered. TC was not entered in the multivariable models because of collinearity with other covariates (Table S1).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions were applied to test non-linear associations of Cre/BW with remission and progression. When nonlinearity was suggested, segmented (piecewise) Cox regression was applied to identify the threshold point. Because of the well-established associations of alcohol use, family history of diabetes, and smoking with the risk of glycemic transitions [31–34], we conducted sensitivity analyses that repeated the primary models after excluding participants with these characteristics. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to age, sex, and hypertension status, based on prior literature [35–37]. Given that multiple interaction tests were conducted, interaction p-values were additionally adjusted for multiple comparisons to reduce the likelihood of false-positive findings. Interaction terms were tested to assess effect modification. Two-sided hypothesis testing was applied throughout, and a p value of less than 0.05 was interpreted as evidence of statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of adults with prediabetes

In this cohort, 24,506 adults with prediabetes were retained in the analytic cohort. Of these, 16,232 were men, representing 66.24% of the study population, and the mean age was 49.29 years. Baseline profiles stratified by glycemic category are summarized in Table 1. Relative to the normal glucose regulation (NGR) group, participants classified as having prediabetes or diabetes were generally older and exhibited higher BMI, body weight, and blood pressure, along with increased levels of AST, ALT, triglycerides, creatinine (Cre), LDL-C, FPG, BUN, and TC, whereas the Cre/BW and HDL-C were lower (all trend tests, p < 0.001). Additionally, the diabetes group exhibited the highest proportions of men, current smokers, and current alcohol consumers (all p < 0.001).

The incidence rate of glycemic transitions

During an observation period spanning 2.9 years (median), 11,170 reversion events (prediabetes to NGR) and 2,641 incident diabetes cases were documented. As presented in Table 2, the cumulative incidence of reversion to NGR increased monotonically across Cre/BW quartiles, from 41.86% in the lowest quartile (Q1) to 48.70% in the highest quartile (Q4). Correspondingly, the incidence rate rose from 14.10 to 16.62 per 100 person-years. In contrast, Table 3 shows an inverse gradient for diabetes onset: cumulative diabetes incidence declined from 14.46% in Q1 to 7.79% in Q4, with incidence rates decreasing from 4.87 to 2.66 per 100 person-years. These opposing patterns were corroborated by Kaplan–Meier curves (Figs. 2A and 2B), which demonstrated clear separation across Cre/BW quartiles for both outcomes (log-rank p < 0.001).

The effect of Cre/BW on glycemic remission and progression

Using three stepwise Cox proportional hazards models (Table 4), we assessed the association of Cre/BW with both glycemic remission and disease progression. Across all models, higher Cre/BW was consistently related to a greater hazard of reverting to normoglycemia and a decreased hazard of progressing to diabetes among adults with prediabetes, and these associations remained after adjustment for potential confounders. After comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders in Model 3, every 1-unit increase in Cre/BW corresponded to a 41% higher hazard of remission to normoglycemia (HR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.28–1.55, p < 0.0001) and a 58% lower hazard of progression to diabetes (HR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.35–0.52, p < 0.0001). We further examined dose–response by categorizing Cre/BW, and the category-based analyses showed the same pattern: increasing Cre/BW was linked to higher remission rates and reduced risk of diabetes onset in individuals with prediabetes.

Sensitivity analysis

We examined the stability of these findings through multiple sensitivity analyses (Table 5). Restricting the sample to never smokers (Model 4), never alcohol users (Model 5), and individuals without a family history of diabetes (Model 6) yielded results consistent with the main analysis: Cre/BW remained significantly linked to a higher probability of returning to NGR and a lower risk of new-onset diabetes. In the never-smoking subgroup, a 1-unit increase in Cre/BW was linked to a 46% higher hazard of reversion to NGR (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.31–1.62, p < 0.0001) and a 49% lower hazard of incident diabetes (HR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.40–0.66, p < 0.0001). Comparable association patterns were also observed among never drinkers and among participants without a diabetic family history.

RCS analysis

To evaluate nonlinearity and possible cutoffs, we applied RCS modeling (Table 6 and Fig. 3). For incident diabetes, the curve indicated a statistically supported change-point at a Cre/BW level of 1.07 µmol/L/kg (log-likelihood ratio test p = 0.005) (Fig. 3B). When Cre/BW was below 1.07 µmol/L/kg, each 1-unit increment corresponded to a 71% lower hazard of diabetes (HR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.20–0.40, p < 0.0001). At values above this point, the inverse association weakened yet persisted (HR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.46–0.95, p = 0.0237). In contrast, for reversion to NGR, we found no evidence of a breakpoint (log-likelihood ratio test, p = 0.336), consistent with an approximately linear pattern (Fig. 3A).

The results of subgroup analyses

Additional effect-modification analyses by age category, sex, and baseline hypertension are summarized in Table 7. Age significantly influenced the relationship between Cre/BW and return to NGR (P for interaction = 0.0026): the effect of Cre/BW on return to NGR was strongest among participants aged 40–50 years (HR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.48–2.19, p < 0.0001) but was markedly weaker and not statistically evident in those aged ≥ 60 years (HR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.85–1.27, p = 0.7244). A similar age-dependent pattern was observed for incident diabetes, with the risk-reducing association more apparent in younger individuals and gradually diminishing with increasing age (P for interaction < 0.0001). Sex also modified the Cre/BW–NGR association (P for interaction = 0.0300), showing a larger effect in men (HR = 1.52; 95% CI: 1.35–1.71) than in women (HR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.08–1.44). In contrast, there was no evidence that sex altered the association with diabetes onset (P for interaction = 0.881). Results were comparable in participants with versus without hypertension, with no significant interactions in either analysis (both P for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

In our retrospective cohort study of Chinese adults with prediabetes, we investigated, for the first time, the effect of Cre/BW on glycemic transitions. The findings demonstrated a significant relationship: higher Cre/BW levels were linked to both a higher rate of regression to normoglycemia and a decreased risk of diabetes progression. Furthermore, the nonlinear effect of Cre/BW on diabetes progression was tested, with an inflection point at a Cre/BW value of 1.07 µmol/L/kg. Subgroup analyses further identified age and gender as effect modifiers for the association of Cre/BW with glycemic transitions.

Prediabetes is widely regarded as the last critical stage at which progression to diabetes can still be averted [2–4]. Therefore, accurately delineating the determinants of glycemic variability is essential for lowering subsequent diabetes risk. Population-based studies indicate that, in the absence of any intervention, about 31% of individuals with prediabetes return to normoglycemia within five years [38]. After lifestyle modification or pharmacotherapy, reported remission rates increase to 32.2%–81% [12, 13, 39–43]. These observations imply that targeting modifiable contributors to glucose regulation may meaningfully diminish the likelihood of future diabetes. Insulin resistance constitutes a central pathophysiologic mechanism underlying elevated baseline glucose and is also a major determinant of whether prediabetes progresses or remits [44, 45]. As the predominant site of insulin-stimulated glucose disposal, skeletal muscle is indispensable for glucose homeostasis, and its quantity is closely linked to glycemic fluctuations [39, 46–49]. Emerging evidence highlights evaluating Cre/BW as a convenient and inexpensive surrogate metric for estimating muscle mass. A Japanese cohort [19] found that an inverse effect of Cre/BW on diabetes. Consistently, using NHANES data, Srikanthan et al. [14] reported an inverse effect of relative muscle mass on both insulin resistance and prediabetes. A cross-sectional study [50] further indicated that an inverse effect of muscle mass on diabetes among Korean individuals. Building on this evidence, our study examined the effect of Cre/BW on longitudinal glycemic transitions and additionally identified effect modification by age, sex, and hypertension. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of our findings: the association persisted among participants who never smoked or consumed alcohol and among those without a family history of diabetes. Subgroup analyses suggested that the effect of Cre/BW on glycemic remission was more pronounced in men and in participants aged 40–50 years; a stronger effect of Cre/BW on glycemic progression was also observed among those aged < 30 years. Clinicians should incorporate Cre/BW into routine risk assessments to facilitate more individualized management strategies for patients with prediabetes.

Several plausible biological pathways may underlie the link between a reduced Cre/BW and a higher likelihood of prediabetes. As the principal site of insulin-mediated glucose disposal, skeletal muscle is central to maintaining systemic glycemic balance [50]. When muscle mass is relatively low—as approximated by a lower Cre/BW—the capacity for peripheral glucose uptake and utilization may be diminished, thereby promoting insulin resistance and facilitating the transition toward prediabetes [17, 51]. In addition, skeletal muscle functions as an endocrine tissue that releases myokines, which can modulate whole-body energy metabolism and insulin responsiveness [51–53]. Finally, reduced muscle mass may also serve as a marker of adverse behavioral or nutritional profiles, such as physical inactivity or suboptimal dietary intake, both of which are well-recognized contributors to worsening glycemic status over time [54, 55].

In our analysis, the effect of Cre/BW on diabetes was clearly non-linear, with a turning point at 1.07 µmol/L/kg. When Cre/BW was below this value, each 1-unit increase was linked to a 71% reduction in diabetes risk. Once Cre/BW exceeded 1.07 µmol/L/kg, the positive association persisted but was attenuated: a further 1-unit rise corresponded to a 34% lower risk. Lower Cre/BW likely reflects relatively lower skeletal muscle mass. Because skeletal muscle is the principal site for insulin-mediated peripheral glucose uptake and utilization, increases in muscle mass/functional capacity in this lower range would be expected to enhance glucose disposal [16, 56]. In parallel, greater muscle mass would likely increase the release of myokines, which can modulate whole-body energy metabolism and improve insulin responsiveness [52, 53]. Therefore, as Cre/BW increases up to this point, the likelihood of glycemic reversion (remission) would rise more appreciably. For Cre/BW > 1.07 μmol/L/kg: Beyond this inflection point, additional increases in Cre/BW may yield diminishing incremental returns. Biologically, this could occur because peripheral glucose uptake/utilization and myokine-mediated endocrine effects may approach a capacity limit (i.e., the system becomes less responsive to further increases in muscle-related signals) [51–53]. As a result, the protective effect may continue but becomes less pronounced, leading to the observed slowing/attenuation in the association after the threshold. This threshold-type pattern may indicate a multifaceted interaction among skeletal muscle quantity, overall body composition, kidney function, and broader metabolic status [57–59]. Comparable non-linear associations have also been reported in investigations linking muscle mass to other metabolic endpoints [60].

Although Cre/BW may serve as a convenient surrogate indicator related to skeletal muscle mass, its interpretation requires caution. Serum creatinine is not determined solely by muscle-derived creatinine generation, but is also influenced by age, sex, dietary intake, and renal clearance. Therefore, Cre/BW should be regarded as a composite marker reflecting both muscle-related creatinine production and renal handling of creatinine, rather than a direct measure of skeletal muscle mass. In individuals with impaired kidney function, elevated serum creatinine may result from reduced renal filtration rather than greater muscle mass. Such renal impairment could potentially lead to misclassification of Cre/BW and influence the observed associations between Cre/BW and glycemic transitions. In the present study, blood urea nitrogen was included as a covariate in the multivariable models to partially account for renal-related metabolic status. However, BUN alone cannot fully characterize kidney function, and detailed indicators such as estimated glomerular filtration rate, albuminuria, and formally defined chronic kidney disease status were not comprehensively assessed. Therefore, residual confounding related to renal dysfunction cannot be completely excluded. Future prospective studies incorporating direct measures of body composition, eGFR, albuminuria, and chronic kidney disease status are needed to validate our findings and to clarify whether the observed associations are primarily attributable to skeletal muscle mass, renal function, or their combined effects.

To our knowledge, this investigation is the first to link Cre/BW with subsequent glycemic trajectories among individuals with prediabetes. These findings suggest that Cre/BW may serve as a practical reference metric for monitoring glucose control in Chinese populations at elevated risk for future diabetes. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because this was a retrospective observational study based on secondary data, causal relationships between Cre/BW and subsequent glycemic outcomes cannot be inferred. Although we adjusted for multiple metabolic covariates and performed sensitivity analyses, residual confounding remains possible. Several important determinants of both Cre/BW and glycemic outcomes were unavailable in the original DATADRYAD dataset, including physical activity, resistance exercise, dietary intake, medication use, glucose-lowering therapies, and socioeconomic status. Therefore, the observed associations between Cre/BW and glycemic remission or progression should be interpreted with caution. Second, Cre/BW is an indirect surrogate marker related to skeletal muscle mass and is inherently less precise than direct body-composition assessments, such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, or bioelectrical impedance analysis. In addition, serum creatinine is influenced not only by muscle-derived creatinine generation but also by renal handling, dietary intake, age, sex, and body composition. Thus, Cre/BW should be interpreted as a pragmatic composite marker rather than a direct measure of skeletal muscle mass or kidney function. Third, glycemic status was defined using fasting plasma glucose alone, without oral glucose tolerance testing or glycated hemoglobin measurements. The dataset analyzed in the present study was derived from the DATADRYAD repository (http://www.datadryad.org), and the source dataset did not include oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results or HbA1c measurements for participants in either the overall cohort or identifiable subgroups. Consequently, the absence of OGTT and HbA1c data may have resulted in misclassification of both prediabetes and diabetes status. In particular, individuals with isolated postprandial hyperglycemia may not have been captured by FPG alone, and participants with discordant HbA1c and FPG values may have been inaccurately categorized. Fourth, Cre/BW was assessed only at baseline. Longitudinal changes in Cre/BW, which may reflect changes in muscle mass, renal function, nutritional status, or overall health, could influence subsequent glycemic outcomes but could not be evaluated in the present analysis. Fifth, the dataset included only participants with a follow-up duration of at least two years, as individuals with follow-up durations shorter than two years were excluded in the original study. Therefore, our estimates are conditional on survival and continued observation for at least two years. This restriction may have introduced selection bias, because individuals with rapid progression to diabetes, early glycemic deterioration, or early loss to follow-up may have been disproportionately excluded. As a result, the incidence or progression of diabetes may have been underestimated, whereas the probability of remission may have been overestimated among those remaining in the analytic cohort. This issue may also limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly to individuals with prediabetes who experience rapid glycemic worsening. Finally, because the cohort consisted exclusively of Chinese participants, the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic and geographic populations should be interpreted cautiously. Future prospective studies with comprehensive data collection are warranted to validate our findings. Such studies should incorporate detailed assessments of physical activity and resistance exercise, dietary intake, medication use, glucose-lowering therapies, socioeconomic status, renal function, direct body-composition measurements, and comprehensive glycemic assessments, including FPG, OGTT, and HbA1c. Interventional studies are also needed to determine whether targeted strategies that modify Cre/BW or its underlying determinants can directly influence glycemic progression or promote remission among individuals with prediabetes.

Conclusion

Cre/BW is independently associated with subsequent glycemic trajectories in individuals with prediabetes. Higher Cre/BW was linked to a greater likelihood of reverting to normoglycemia and a lower probability of progressing to diabetes. These findings suggest that Cre/BW may serve as a useful prognostic marker for glycemic transitions in prediabetes, and support its potential relevance in blood glucose risk stratification.
