Intraocular lens tilt and decentration and optical quality after primary intraocular lens implantation in pediatric cataract patients
Authors: Hongzhe Li, Jiaying Cen, Wentao Tong, Fuman Yang, Ruiwen Zhang, Dandan Wang, Pingjun Chang, Yune Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposesTo investigate the characteristics and associated factors of intraocular lens (IOL) tilt and decentration in pediatric cataract patients undergoing primary IOL implantation and to explore their impact on postoperative optical quality.MethodsChildren who underwent cataract extraction with posterior capsulotomy, primary anterior vitrectomy, and in-the-bag IOL implantation over 6 months were enrolled. Patient data and ocular biometric parameters were retrieved from electronic files. Retroillumination images were obtained to evaluate capsular outcomes. IOL tilt and decentration were assessed using the swept-source optical coherence tomography, and wavefront aberrations were measured with the OPD-Scan III.ResultsA total of 125 eyes from 125 patients with a mean age at surgery of 4.74 ​± ​1.49 years were included. The IOLs demonstrated a mean tilt of 6.02 ​± ​1.97° toward the temporal direction and a mean decentration of 0.28 ​± ​0.13 ​mm toward the superior-temporal direction. Multivariate analysis revealed that shorter axial length (AL; P ​= ​0.009) and significant cortical proliferation (P ​= ​0.021) were associated with greater IOL tilt. In addition, smaller corneal diameter (CD; P ​= ​0.008) and incomplete capsulorhexis-IOL overlap (P ​= ​0.022) were associated with larger IOL decentration. Patients with IOL tilt ≥ 7° and decentration ≥ 0.40 ​mm exhibited higher internal total higher-order aberrations (tHOA) and internal astigmatism, both of which were positively correlated with IOL misalignment (all P ​< ​0.05)ConclusionsShorter AL, smaller CD, significant cortical proliferation, and incomplete capsulorhexis-IOL overlap contributed to greater IOL misalignment in pediatric cataract patients with primary in-the-bag IOL implantation, which may compromise postoperative optical quality by increasing internal tHOA and astigmatism.Keywords: IOL tilt, IOL decentration, Optical quality, Pediatric cataract surgery, Primary IOL implantation

Introduction

Pediatric cataract represents one of the leading causes of childhood visual impairment worldwide.1 Timely cataract extraction is crucial for preventing deprivation amblyopia.2 Due to the exceptionally high proliferative potential of lens epithelial cells (LECs) in children, preservation of the posterior capsule almost invariably results in the development of posterior capsule opacification (PCO).3 Consequently, posterior capsulotomy and anterior vitrectomy are routinely performed in pediatric cataract surgery.4 In addition, correction of aphakia is essential for visual rehabilitation, with primary in-the-bag intraocular lens (IOL) implantation being the most common and increasingly adopted approach, particularly in children older than two years.5

Numerous studies have demonstrated that accurate IOL alignment is critical for achieving satisfactory visual outcomes and several determinants of IOL misalignment have been identified in adults.6,7 However, no study to date has quantitatively assessed IOL alignment in pediatric patients, and evidence regarding the associated factors remains scarce in this population, who possess unique ocular characteristics, including shorter axial length (AL), smaller corneal diameter (CD), shallower anterior chamber depth (ACD), steeper keratometry value, greater LECs proliferation, and ongoing ocular growth.8 Furthermore, previous studies have shown that an IOL tilt of ≥ 7° or a decentration of ≥ 0.4 ​mm, while not necessarily compromising surgical safety, can significantly impair optical quality by increasing higher-order aberrations and is thus regarded as clinically significant.9,10 Nevertheless, the relationship between IOL tilt or decentration and optical quality in pediatric patients remains unclear.

Purkinje and Scheimpflug imaging have previously been used to assess IOL alignment relative to the pupillary axis; however, their accuracy can be influenced by pupil morphology.11 With the advent of second-generation swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT; Casia2, Tomey, Nagoya, Japan), IOL tilt and decentration can now be measured with high precision using the corneal topographic axis as the reference12. Therefore, this study aims to measure IOL tilt and decentration in pediatric cataract patients with primary IOL implantation using the Casia2, investigate the characteristics and factors associated with IOL tilt and decentration, and explore their effect on postoperative visual quality.

Method

This cross-sectional study was conducted between January 2022 and August 2024, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical clearance was provided by the Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (ID: H2022-064-K-56-01), and written consent was obtained from each patient's guardian.

Patients

Children who underwent cataract extraction with posterior capsulotomy, anterior vitrectomy, and in-the-bag IOL implantation at the Hangzhou Branch of the Eye Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University over 6 months were eligible for this study. The inclusion criteria were: (1) congenital or developmental cataract; (2) age ≤ 8 years; and (3) good cooperation with examinations. The exclusion criteria included: (1) significant ocular comorbidities, defined as the presence of complex persistent fetal vasculature (PFV) requiring posterior vitrectomy, severe microphthalmos (AL ​< ​16 ​mm), severe microcornea (CD ​< ​9.0 ​mm), or other ocular structural anomalies13; (2) previous ocular trauma or surgery; (3) intraoperative or postoperative complications; and (4) synechiae or insufficient pupillary dilation. The first operated eye was selected in cases of bilateral cataracts.

Preoperative examinations

Patient data, including gender, age, and the presence of concurrent posterior capsule defect (PCD) or PFV, were collected. The IOLMaster 700 (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) was used to obtain ocular biometric parameters, including AL, ACD, CD, lens thickness (LT), and keratometry, with CD further verified preoperatively under general anesthesia.

Surgery

All surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon (YZ) under general anesthesia. The procedure involved a 2.2 ​mm scleral tunnel incision at 12 o'clock with two corneal incisions, anterior continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis, lens aspiration, and a 3.5–4.0 ​mm posterior capsulotomy with limited anterior vitrectomy. A single-piece hydrophobic acrylic IOL (A1-UV, ZCB00, or SN60WF) was subsequently implanted in the capsular bag. The SRK/T and Barrett Universal II formulas were used for IOL power calculation, and the intended postoperative refraction was individualized according to patient age based on our prior clinical experience.14

Postoperative examinations

At 6 months or later after surgery, all patients underwent comprehensive examinations, including assessment of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), handheld iCare tonometry or non-contact tonometry, and slit-lamp examination. Retroillumination images were obtained after mydriasis for capsular outcome assessment. Significant cortical proliferation was defined as visible proliferated cortex or Elschnig pearls,15 and further categorized as limited (within the capsule opening) or extensive (invasion into the anterior or posterior capsule opening) (Fig. 1).16 The capsulorhexis-IOL overlap was classified as complete (360° continuous) or incomplete. Two ophthalmologists (HL and FY) independently evaluated each image, and any discrepancies were adjudicated by a senior ophthalmologist (PC). Wavefront aberrations were measured with the OPD SCAN-III (Nidek Technologies, Gamagori, Japan), and root mean square values of internal higher-order aberrations, including total higher-order aberration (tHOA), coma, trefoil, spherical aberration (SA), and astigmatism, were recorded.

(A) Representative capsular outcome without significant cortical proliferation (None); (B) Significant cortical proliferation within the capsule opening (Limited); (C) Significant cortical proliferation invasion into the capsule opening (Extensive).

SS-OCT measurement

IOL tilt and decentration were assessed using the Casia2 following pupil dilation. The Casia2 captures eight cross-sectional images at different meridians, which are reconstructed into a three-dimensional model of the IOL. Based on these data, the built-in software automatically calculates IOL tilt and decentration using the corneal topographic axis as the reference. In this study, children were instructed to fixate on an internal target while eye position was monitored, and scanning was initiated once the cornea was centered. Although the software automatically delineates the IOL contour, all markings were verified and manually refined to ensure measurement accuracy (Fig. 2). Clinically significant IOL tilt and decentration were defined as ≥ 7° and ≥ 0.4 ​mm, respectively, according to previous studies.9,10

Examples of three-dimensional images of the IOL. (A) Cortical proliferation beneath the posterior IOL surface elevates the adjacent edge; (B) Cortical proliferation over the anterior IOL surface depresses the adjacent edge.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ​± ​standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts with percentages. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student's t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Correlations were evaluated with Pearson's or Spearman's coefficients. Factors associated with IOL tilt, decentration, and visual acuity were analyzed using linear regression, whereas logistic regression was applied to identify risk factors for IOL tilt ≥ 7° and decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm. The predictive performance of ocular parameters for clinically significant IOL misalignment was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). Statistical significance was defined as P ​< ​0.05. The sample size for the comparison of optical quality was calculated using PASS version 15.0 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA), based on pilot data from the first 20 patients, with a statistical power of 80% and a two-sided significance level of 0.05. Total higher-order aberration (tHOA) was designated as the primary endpoint, and the calculation indicated that a minimum of 52 eyes was required.

Result

Patient demographics and characteristics

A total of 125 eyes from 125 patients with a mean age at surgery of 4.74 ​± ​1.49 years (2.5–8.0 years) were included in this study. Patient demographics and characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Fifty-eight patients (46.4%) were male, and 59 patients (47.2%) presented with bilateral cataracts. Thirty-two eyes (25.6%) had concurrent PCD, and 14 eyes (11.2%) had PFV. The mean follow-up period was 34.8 ​± ​20.7 months. Incomplete capsulorhexis-IOL overlap was present in 39 eyes (31.2%). Significant cortical proliferation was observed in 59 eyes (47.2%), of which 36 eyes (28.8%) were classified as limited and 23 eyes (18.4%) as extensive.

IOL tilt and decentration

The distribution of IOL tilt and decentration is shown in Table 2 and Fig. 3. The mean IOL tilt was 6.02 ​± ​1.97°, with 37 eyes (29.6%) demonstrating a tilt ≥ 7° and 33 eyes (26.4%) a tilt < 5°. The mean IOL decentration was 0.28 ​± ​0.13 ​mm, with 20 eyes (16.0%) exhibiting a decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm and 34 eyes (27.2%) ​< ​0.2 ​mm. IOL tilt was predominantly directed temporally, whereas decentration was most frequently localized to the supero-temporal region.

Associated factors with IOL misalignment

Table 3 summarizes the results of the linear regression analyses for IOL misalignment. Greater IOL tilt was significantly associated with shorter AL (P ​= ​0.009) and the presence of significant cortical proliferation (P ​= ​0.021). In addition, larger IOL decentration was inversely associated with CD (P ​= ​0.008) and positively associated with incomplete capsulorhexis-IOL overlap (P ​= ​0.022). The risk factors for IOL tilt ≥ 7° and decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm were assessed using logistic regression analyses (Supplemental Table 1). Shorter AL (P ​= ​0.021) and extensive cortical proliferation (P ​= ​0.013) were identified as risk factors for IOL tilt ≥ 7°. Additionally, smaller CD (P ​= ​0.001) and incomplete capsulorhexis-IOL overlap (P ​= ​0.036) were identified as risk factors for IOL decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm.

Impact of IOL misalignment on optical quality

OPD SCAN III examinations were available for 57 patients. To evaluate the relationship between IOL misalignment and optical quality, cut-off values of 7° for tilt and 0.40 ​mm for decentration were applied for grouping and statistical analysis (Fig. 5). Patients with IOL tilt ≥ 7° exhibited significantly higher internal tHOA (P ​= ​0.021) and internal astigmatism (P ​= ​0.026). Similarly, patients with IOL decentration ≥ 0.40 ​mm showed significantly higher internal tHOA (P ​= ​0.008), coma (P ​= ​0.018), and internal astigmatism (P ​= ​0.015). Internal tHOA and astigmatism were both positively correlated with IOL tilt (P ​= ​0.011 and P ​= ​0.026, respectively) and IOL decentration (P ​= ​0.018 and P ​= ​0.010, respectively). No significant correlations were observed between IOL misalignment and internal coma, trefoil, or spherical aberration (all P ​> ​0.05) (Table 4).

Impact of IOL misalignment on visual acuity

The BCVA of patients with IOL tilt ≥ 7° was significantly worse than that of patients with IOL tilt < 7° (0.27 ​± ​0.30 logMAR vs. 0.15 ​± ​0.20 logMAR, P ​= ​0.049). While no significant difference in BCVA was observed between patients with and without IOL decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm (0.25 ​± ​0.27 logMAR vs. 0.17 ​± ​0.23 logMAR, P ​= ​0.226). Linear regression analyses revealed that BCVA was significantly associated with unilateral cataract and PFV (P ​= ​0.023 and P ​= ​0.010, respectively). Given that PFV was more prevalent in unilateral pediatric cataracts and may affect prognosis, a further analysis was conducted in bilateral cataract patients without PFV (n ​= ​59). After adjusting for sex, age, ocular parameters, and capsular outcomes, BCVA was not directly associated with IOL tilt ≥ 7° or decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm ​(P ​= ​0.288 and P ​= ​0.186, respectively) (Supplemental Table 2).

Discussion

Primary IOL implantation has been widely adopted for the management of pediatric aphakia.17,18 However, achieving optimal IOL positioning in these eyes remains challenging and may compromise the quality of foveal imaging.19 To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the characteristics and associated factors of IOL tilt and decentration in pediatric cataract patients undergoing primary IOL implantation. Our findings demonstrated that IOLs in this population exhibited a mean tilt of 6.02 ​± ​1.97° toward the temporal direction and a mean decentration of 0.28 ​± ​0.13 ​mm toward the superior-temporal direction. Shorter AL and significant cortical proliferation were associated with greater IOL tilt, whereas smaller CD and incomplete capsulorhexis-IOL overlap were associated with larger IOL decentration.

Previous studies have defined IOL tilt ≥ 7° and decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm as clinically significant because of their adverse impact on visual quality.9,10 In our cohort, 32 eyes (29.6%) demonstrated an IOL tilt ≥ 7° and 20 eyes (16.0%) an IOL decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm. These rates were substantially higher than those reported in adults, where the incidence of IOL tilt ≥ 7° ranges from 5.77% to 11.59% and IOL decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm from 6.73% to 10.72%.6,7,20,21 The comparatively higher incidence of IOL misalignment in children may be attributed to unique pediatric ocular characteristics, such as shorter AL and smaller CD, as well as capsular factors including stronger capsular contraction and increased LEC proliferation.

We demonstrated that shorter AL and significant cortical proliferation were significantly associated with greater IOL tilt. Chen et al. reported that IOLs were more prone to tilt with decreasing AL in adult cataract patients,21 and Wang et al. observed that a 1 ​mm elongation of AL corresponded to a 0.228° reduction in IOL tilt.22 Our study found similar results, showing a strong correlation between IOL tilt and AL (β ​= ​−0.368). The relatively smaller ALs (21.59 ​± ​1.45 ​mm) in our pediatric cohort, compared to the AL range of 23.48–24.80 ​mm in those adult studies,21,22 may help explain the higher IOL tilt observed in our study. Postoperatively, migration and metaplasia of residual LECs may lead to positional changes of the IOL.23 Because LEC proliferation is markedly higher in children than in adults, primary posterior capsulotomy and anterior vitrectomy, although effective in reducing the incidence of PCO, cannot eliminate LECs. Residual LECs may continue to proliferate and even obstruct the visual axis, particularly within the first six months after surgery.24,25 In the current study, all patients were followed up for more than 6 months, and our results highlighted that significant cortical proliferation could induce postoperative IOL tilt. We hypothesize that the proliferated cortex could exert an opposing force on the IOL by pushing the capsule away from the IOL surface. Specifically, cortical proliferation beneath the posterior IOL surface can elevate the adjacent edge, whereas proliferation over the anterior surface may depress the adjacent edge (Fig. 2).

In our study, incomplete capsulorhexis-IOL overlap and smaller CD were significantly associated with larger IOL decentration. The relationship between capsulorhexis features and IOL position has been investigated in adults, with an overlarge capsulorhexis contributing to greater IOL decentration.21 Nagata et al. further demonstrated that IOL decentration tended to occur opposite the area where the optic was not fully covered.26 Our findings also confirmed that capsulorhexis-IOL overlap was significantly correlated with IOL decentration in pediatric patients. Unlike adults, capsule contraction is more pronounced in pediatric patients,16 which may explain the relatively large IOL decentration observed in our study. Evidence from earlier studies shows that eyes with larger CD generally exhibit larger capsular bag dimensions, which may allow for easier IOL shifting.27,28 However, our study found an inconsistent result, with greater IOL decentration negatively associated with CD. Previous studies have also shown that IOL misalignment was more likely to occur in adult microphthalmos patients than in routine cataract cases.29,30 We speculate that this discrepancy may be due to the incompatibility between the size of the capsule bag and the implanted IOL. Currently available IOLs are manufactured with a fixed overall diameter, tailored to the anatomy of the normal adult eye, which typically has a larger capsular bag compared to pediatric eyes and adult microphthalmos cases.31 These fixed-size IOLs may be incompatible with the small intraocular space and may be squeezed and unable to fully stretch within the narrow capsular bag, resulting in increased tensional forces and a greater likelihood of IOL decentration. Therefore, child-sized or microphthalmos-compatible IOLs need to be designed.

Unlike adult cataract procedures, pediatric surgeries commonly incorporate a primary posterior capsulotomy combined with anterior vitrectomy.4 Previous adult studies have demonstrated comparable degrees of IOL tilt and decentration in cases with posterior continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis (PPCCC) and routine cataract procedures.32 Further investigations found that eyes in the PPCCC group exhibited faster IOL-capsular adhesion and minimal IOL positional fluctuation over time, suggesting that PPCCC may improve IOL axial stability.33,34 In addition, although combined vitrectomy with air tamponade has been reported to affect IOL stability.35 A recent comparative study found no evidence of IOL displacement following phacovitrectomy without gas injection for macular disease, compared with phacoemulsification alone.36 In our study, only limited anterior vitrectomy was performed in pediatric cases. We believe that pediatric-specific surgical techniques may be unlikely to increase the risk of IOL instability.

Our study is the first to evaluate the efficacy of ocular biometric parameters in predicting clinically significant IOL tilt and decentration in pediatric cataract patients. The results showed that AL had moderate discriminatory power for predicting IOL tilt ≥ 7°, with an AUC of 0.666 and an optimal cut-off value of 21.09 ​mm. CD demonstrated a stronger predictive ability for IOL decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm, with an AUC of 0.766 and a cut-off value of 10.65 ​mm.

Our study is the first to evaluate the efficacy of ocular biometric parameters in predicting clinically significant IOL tilt and decentration in pediatric cataract patients. The results showed that AL had moderate discriminatory power for predicting IOL tilt ≥ 7°, with an AUC of 0.666 and an optimal cut-off value of 21.09 ​mm. CD demonstrated stronger predictive ability for IOL decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm, with an AUC of 0.766 and a cut-off value of 10.65 ​mm. Overall, the model exhibited moderate predictive performance. Several factors may account for these suboptimal results. First, pediatric patients exhibit greater physiological variability in ocular biometric parameters compared with adults. Second, CD was used as a surrogate parameter for estimating capsular bag size, which may not linearly reflect the actual capsular bag dimensions and its match with the IOL. Third, postoperative capsular changes, such as cortical proliferation, may compromise the reliability of purely anatomical predictions. Future studies should incorporate more specific anatomical and surgical factors to improve predictive precision. Moreover, threshold-based models are inherently limited in capturing complex multivariate interactions beyond AL and CD. Employing more robust multivariate predictive models would therefore enhance clinical applicability. Despite these limitations, the predictive performance of our model was comparable to that reported in previous studies on adult patients using similar methods, where the AUC for predicting clinically significant IOL misalignment based on AL ranged from 0.666 to 0.802.6,37 Such predictive approaches may assist clinicians in evaluating postoperative IOL stability and in making optimal surgical decisions, such as performing cataract surgery with planned aphakia and implanting IOL when the eyes develop,38 or using the IOL optic capture technique.39

Our study observed that higher-order aberrations were more pronounced in patients with IOL tilt ≥ 7° and decentration ≥ 0.4 ​mm, showing a positive correlation with IOL misalignment, consistent with findings in adult populations.9,10 Furthermore, we identified an increasing trend in internal astigmatism with greater IOL tilt and decentration. Although IOL misalignment does not necessarily imply surgical safety risks, optical quality is critical for visual rehabilitation following pediatric cataract surgery. Therefore, caution is warranted when considering optically sophisticated IOLs in pediatric patients,40,41 especially those with risk factors for increased IOL tilt and decentration. Even with aspheric monofocal IOLs, predicting IOL tilt and decentration remains essential. Notably, the thresholds for clinically significant IOL misalignment in this study were derived from adult data, and pediatric-specific thresholds remain undefined. Future large-scale studies are warranted to establish such criteria.

The present study is strengthened by its substantial sample size and the application of CASIA2 for precise assessment of IOL alignment. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, to ensure reliable measurements, uncooperative young children were excluded, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to younger pediatric cohorts. Second, although all IOLs in this study were single-piece hydrophobic acrylic lenses, restricting the analysis to a single IOL model might have eliminated potential variability introduced by different IOL types. Third, the OPD SCAN III examination was not available for all patients, potentially reducing the statistical power of the analyses. Finally, future studies should consider incorporating additional ocular parameters to further improve the predictive performance of ROC models for IOL misalignment.

Conclusions

This study is the first to quantitatively measure IOL alignment and explore its impact on postoperative optical quality in pediatric cataract patients with primary IOL Implantation. Our results indicated that shorter AL and significant cortical proliferation were associated with greater IOL tilt, while smaller CD and incomplete capsulorhexis-IOL overlap were associated with larger IOL decentration. Furthermore, we found that IOL misalignment in pediatric cataract patients contributed to increased internal higher-order aberrations and internal astigmatism, potentially diminishing optical quality.
