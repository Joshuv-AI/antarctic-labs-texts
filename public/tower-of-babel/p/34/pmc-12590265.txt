Dental Anomalies: Prevalence, Patterns of Association and Relationship With Dental Age
Authors: Blanca Loscertales-Martín-de-Agar, Álvaro Cabezas-Corado, María Baus-Domínguez, Jesús Loscertales-Abril, Daniel Torres-Lagares
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental anomalies represent frequent findings in orthodontics and pediatric dentistry, often leading to functional and esthetic problems. Although their etiology is multifactorial, the role of genetic factors is increasingly recognised.ObjectiveTo determine the prevalence of dental anomalies and identify associative patterns that may suggest a shared genetic origin.Study DesignThis observational, cross-sectional retrospective study analysed panoramic radiographs from patients attending 2 orthodontic and one pediatric dentistry practices. Inclusion criteria excluded cases with prior tooth loss due to trauma, caries, or extractions, syndromes, systemic diseases, or craniofacial malformations. Descriptive statistics were combined with hierarchical cluster analysis and inferential tests to identify associations between anomalies with 95% confidence intervals (IC). A p value of<.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsRecords were obtained corresponding to 1,904 patients with a mean age of 10.69 ±5.06 años. Dental anomalies were found in 35.5% of patients, with hypodontia of upper lateral incisors (9.3%) and palatal impaction of upper canines (9.2%) being the most frequent. Multiple anomalies occurred in 10.4% of patients. Five associative patterns were identified, with the strongest link observed between upper lateral incisor hypodontia and palatal impaction of upper canines (p < .001).ConclusionsThis large-scale study provides new insights into the co-occurrence of dental anomalies, supporting the hypothesis of common genetic factors. The identified patterns may facilitate early detection and risk prediction in orthodontic planning.Clinical RelevanceIdentifying associative patterns of dental anomalies through cluster analysis enables early detection of related conditions, such as predicting canine impaction from lateral incisor hypodontia, improving orthodontic diagnosis, treatment planning, and preventive strategies in pediatric and orthodontic patients.

Introduction

The development of human dentition is a complex and highly coordinated process that involves multiple tissues and functions, yet its precise regulation is only partially understood.1 Given the complexity of primary eruption, deciduous tooth exfoliation, and permanent tooth eruption, several factors can disrupt the normal sequence or timing, leading to occlusal disturbances.2,3

Abnormalities in the number, morphology, size, position, or eruption pathway and timing, of teeth are frequently observed in orthodontic and pediatric dental patients, as they can significantly affect the dental arches and occlusion.4 Understanding the etiology of these anomalies is essential to enable early diagnosis and preventive or interceptive measures.5

Although the causes of certain developmental anomalies are known, most remain unexplained. The interplay between genetic and environmental factors is still debated, but the current consensus favors a predominantly genetic basis, as familial clustering of dental anomalies is often reported.4, 5, 6

Genetic alterations may produce a single dental anomaly or a combination of anomalies. Because the same genetic defect can manifest through different phenotypes, the identification of associative patterns of dental anomalies within a population could provide valuable clues to their genetic origins.7

The objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of dental anomalies in a large cohort of orthodontic and pediatric dental patients, and to identify patterns of association among these anomalies through hierarchical cluster analysis. These findings may enhance understanding of shared etiological factors, support early detection, and contribute to risk assessment in clinical orthodontic practice.

Materials and methods

An observational clinical study was designed (on panoramic radiographs), cross-sectional and retrospective; with a first descriptive part of prevalences, and a second analytical part, within which, the relationship of the presence of anomalies with a possible delay in dental age is a case-control study, using matched pairs, and the association between anomalies is a study of independent groups.

This study was undertaken with pretreatment records of 3 private practices (2 exclusively orthodontic and one exclusively pediatric dentistry) that were examined, searching for cases that met the following requirements: (1) At least one panoramic radiograph was available prior to the start of orthodontic treatment, with adequate quality conditions: correct exposure and contrast, sufficiently sharp structures, absence of appreciable magnification, symmetry, and without artifacts or blurred areas that would impede proper interpretation. All available panoramic radiographs were recorded for each patient. To ensure a single reading per patient, for those with more than one radiograph, the one from the most appropriate age was chosen to create age groups with a maximum number of elements, ensuring they were as homogeneous as possible, taking into account that there are ages at which a panoramic radiograph cannot be obtained with these quality criteria, (2) Absence of previous tooth loss associated with trauma, caries, periodontal disease or extractions (anamnesis data were used to avoid erroneous diagnoses of agenesis, as well as to determine whether the patient had previously had any supernumerary teeth extracted, and to see whether the dental history recorded the presence of any supernumerary teeth - or other anomalies - that were not seen on panoramic radiographs), (3) Absence of facial clefts and other craniofacial malformations, as well as syndromes and/or systemic diseases and (4) Belong to the Caucasian group.

Variables

Demographic variables

The demographic variables recorded were sex and chronological age (decimal age) at the time each panoramic radiograph was taken. Records were obtained from 1,904 patients (1123 females and 781 males). The mean age was 10.69 ± 5.06 years, with a range of 3.0 to 63.9 years and a median of 9.82 years. Regarding the age distribution in the sample, 83.36% of cases were between 6 and 13 years old. The age distribution by sex was homogeneous (Graph 1).

Radiographic variables

A series of radiographic variables were taken into account, using the corresponding criteria and measurement methods that justify them, such as dental age8,9 and a list of dental anomalies including Hyperodontia (mesiodens and other supernumeraries), Hypodontia (including the complete absence of second premolars, complete absence, microdontic and conoid upper lateral incisors10,11 and complete absence of others12), Impactions, we consider impacted teeth in an ectopic position, whose eruption was stopped, and with their eruptive trajectory altered (upper canines,13 lower canines, incisors14 and premolar15,16), Blocking of upper canines (unlike impaction, blockage of the upper canines is not an eruptive anomaly, since there is no alteration in the eruption path, but rather an anatomical situation in which the canines are not erupted into the arch because they are blocked by an obstacle or due to lack of space to emerge),17 Transpositions (Upper jaw: Canine with first premolar, lateral incisor, central incisor, second premolar or others; Mandible: Canine with lateral incisor), Transmigration (unerupted lower canine with crown crossing the midline), Ankylosis of primary molars (distance between tooth and occlusal plane greater than 1 mm), Ectopic eruption of upper permanent first molars, Impaction of second molars and Primary eruption failure of molars. Yes/no and unilateral (right or left)/bilateral values were used for all variables. Although the conoid shape or microdontic size in lateral incisors was considered as hypodontia, the other teeth studied were not evaluated according to their morphological anomalies (abnormal size and shapes). Third molars were excluded in all anomalies.

This retrospective study was conducted using anonymised clinical and radiographic data obtained from existing patient records. No personal identifiers were collected, and all data were handled in compliance with applicable data protection regulations. According to the guidelines of the local Institutional Review Board, studies that involve secondary analysis of fully anonymised data without any direct patient contact or intervention are exempt from ethical approval and informed consent requirements.

Radiographic analysis

The radiographs were analysed following the protocol established in An Atlas of Dentition in Childhood by Duterloo in 1991 and Pinho et al. (2005).18 First, a general observation and comparison of the right and left sides were made, followed by a systematic analysis of the erupted and unerupted teeth in each quadrant (counting and identifying them), and finally, the measurements corresponding to the variables described were made.

All analyses of the panoramic radiographs and measurements on the panoramic radiographs were made by the same person.

Some patients did not have complete data, such as the date of birth, the date on which the radiograph was taken, or significant difficulty assessing some variables. In these cases, the data was recorded as missing or absent.

Statistical method

Measurement of method error

A subsample of 30 patients was taken randomly, and the radiographs were re-examined by 1 operator, repeating the measurements of each of the parameters: dental age and presence or absence of anomalies (for this purpose the detailed measurements of the variables studied were again taken). The same systematic approach was followed the first time, aiming to estimate the measurement error and evaluate intra-observer reproducibility. The Dahlberg formula was used to estimate the error of the dental age measurement method (X) (Figure 1), particularised to the 33 double measurements of the current analysis. The relative error of the method was also calculated as the quotient between d and the mean of the variable involved and expressed as a percentage. For dental age, the difference between the first and second measurements was calculated. The mean difference was 0.073 ± 0.169 years, with a 95% confidence interval (0.016, 0.131). Since the interval does not contain zero (by a small margin), it can be concluded that the first measurement tended to measure a somewhat older age than the second. Dahlberg's d was put at 0.132 years, which is an estimate of the error of the age measurement method. Relativising the D (to the magnitude of what is being measured), a relative error of 1.22% was obtained, indicating reasonably good reproducibility.

With respect to the variables indicating the presence or absence of anomalies, the Kappa concordance index obtained a constant value of 1 for all of them. Consequently, it was possible to conclude an absolute reproducibility in the anomaly identification method.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS 15.0 statistical package.

First, the descriptive analysis contains the most relevant statistics for all the variables in the analysis: mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum and median for continuous variables; absolute and relative frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. The relative frequencies of the presence and type of the different anomalies represent the prevalence of these in the sample, so estimates of the true prevalence in the population were calculated using 95% confidence intervals (CI). Due to the large sample size (n = 1904), the normal approximation was valid for the calculation of these intervals, even for particularly small prevalences (p ≤ .002). To analyse the possible existence of sexual dimorphism, the χ2 test was applied (or Fisher's exact test when there was a cell with an expected value lower than 5).

Second, a hierarchical cluster analysis was developed to identify homogeneous subgroups of individuals based on their patterns of association with anomalies. Since the variables describing presence/absence were dichotomous, the squared Euclidean distance was used to measure binary similarity data between cases and/or clusters. The clustering method used was intergroup linkage.

Thirdly, to complement the information provided by the multivariate analysis (Cluster), a second line of work was developed in which the association between anomalies was analysed according to the method applied by Baccetti (1998).19 In a first analysis, a subsample of 600 patients was extracted to serve as a control group, and from the remaining cases, 4 independent subgroups of 44 individuals were established, each with one of the 4 most frequent primary anomalies (agenesis of the lower second premolars, hypodontia of the upper lateral incisors, palatal impaction of the upper canines and blockage of the upper canines). The control group and those with primary anomalies were randomly selected from the pool of 1904 subjects in the sample. The only condition was that the subgroups had empty intersection. The statistical test applied to contrast homogeneity of prevalences was the χ2 test, whose power was 0.78 to detect 2 prevalences of 5% and 20% at a confidence level of 95%.

In a second analysis, we worked with the 12 most frequent anomalies (agenesis of lower second premolars, agenesis of upper second premolars, hypodontia of upper lateral incisors, palatal impaction of upper canines, vestibular impaction of upper canines, impaction of incisors, impaction of premolars, blockage of upper canines, transposition, ectopic eruption of upper first molars, impaction of lower second molars and hyperodontia). The control group consisted of 200 cases and the 12 subgroups consisted of 20 individuals each. As in the previous analysis, the control group and the subgroups were obtained randomly from the sample of 1904 subjects, and the subgroups fulfilled the condition of not sharing subjects. The statistical test applied to contrast homogeneity of prevalences was Fisher's test, whose power was 0.31 to detect 2 prevalences of 5% and 20% at a confidence level of 95%.

Fourthly, inferential analysis comprises those tests and statistical methods used to contrast the research hypotheses. For the study of the relationship between dental age delay and the presence of anomalies, a matched pairs design was used, in which everyone in the sample with a given anomaly was paired with another homogeneous individual in terms of sex and chronological age, but without the anomaly. To check that the pair matching was correctly performed, a 2 × 2 contingency table of the sex of everyone in the pair was obtained and the hypothesis of equality of chronological age was tested using a related samples t-test. For each anomaly, a subset of pairs of individuals was extracted from the database, the size of which was established by the prevalence of the anomaly, although in almost all subgroups the number of pairs was slightly lower than the prevalence of the anomaly, since individuals with unknown chronological and/or dental age were discarded. The mean dental age of the related individuals was compared by paired samples t-test, after checking the normality of the variable by Kolmogorov test in large samples (>30 cases) and Shapiro-Wilks in small samples (≤30 cases). When normality was not met, the alternative nonparametric Wilcoxon test (Wilcoxon signed ranks test) was used. For a t-test, with a significance level of 5% and considering an effect size to be detected of 0.5 (mean), the minimum power achieved is 0.85 for those anomalies with a prevalence higher than 40 cases in the sample; 0.73 for prevalence higher than 30 cases; 0.54 for prevalence higher than 20 cases. The significance level used in the analyses was 5% (α = 0.05).

Hyperodontia

The prevalence of hyperodontia in the sample was 1.47% (28 cases). It can be estimated, with 95% confidence, that the true prevalence in the population is between the limits 0.93 and 2.01%. The prevalence of mesiodens and other supernumerary teeth was the same (0.74%), the upper lateral incisors and lower right premolars being the most affected by hyperodontia (0.11% respectively each). There were no significant differences between sexes. However, regarding mesiodens, applying the Wilcoxon test due to its low prevalence in the sample, significant differences were found (Z = 0.046) when comparing the relationship of dental age in individuals with anomaly versus individuals without anomaly with the same chronological age and sex, with the dental age being higher in the sample that presented mesiodens (7.69± 1.69).

Hypodontia

The prevalence of hypodontia in the sample was 14.97% (285 subjects), with an estimate of the actual prevalence in the population of between 13.37 and 16.57%. There was no sexual dimorphism.

The prevalence of agenesis (AG) of the lower second premolars (5-5) in the sample was 4.94%, one of the 4 most frequent anomalies in hypodontia, with no preference for bilateral or unilateral right and left). There was a certain tendency towards a delay in dental age in individuals with this anomaly, which was statistically significant (P = .001).

The upper second premolars (5+5) had a lower prevalence in the sample than the lower ones, 1.94%, and there was a preference for bilateral cases (1.16%).

Hypodontia of upper lateral incisors (2+2), agenesis and microdontic incisors, was the most frequent anomaly in the sample, being observed in 177 individuals (9.3%). This delay was recorded as significant concerning dental age (P = .017).

Other agenesis, different from the above, was found in 31 individuals (1.63%), including agenesis of the lower right incisor (0.21%) or the upper left first premolar (4+4) (0.16%).

Impactions

Within this group of anomalies, the most frequently observed, and second in frequency of all the anomalies, was palatal impaction of the upper canines (3+3), found in 175 patients (9.19% of the sample), with a prevalence of bilateral cases. There was no significance in terms of delay in dental age in the presence of this impaction (P = .157).

Vestibular impaction of the upper canines (3+3) had a much lower prevalence in the sample (1.84%), and bilateral cases were also the most numerous (0.79%). There was a certain tendency to a delay in dental age in these patients, although not significant (P < .1).

Only 10 individuals of the sample (0.53%) had lower canines (3-3) impacted, and there were no differences in dental age between them and individuals who did not present such impaction.

The prevalence of impacted incisors was 1.73%, of which the majority were upper incisors (1.68%), since only one subject had impacted lower incisors (0.05%), making this the anomaly with the lowest prevalence in the sample. Among the impacted upper incisors, the most numerous were the central incisors (1+1). Impacted premolars are the second most frequent type of impaction in the sample (2.21%), with the prevalence of impacted second premolars much higher (2.14%) than that of impacted first premolars (0.11%). The mean dental age in the cases presenting premolar impaction was 9.04 ±2.11 years, while in the control subgroup, it was 9.52 ±2.31. There was a statistically significant delay in dental age for this anomaly (P = .005).

Regarding the second molar, all the impactions were recorded in the mandible, with a prevalence of 2.89%. They presented with a similar frequency on the right and left sides and more frequently bilaterally (1.37%). There was no evidence of delayed dental age in the presence of this anomaly (P = .359).

Blockage of the upper canines

The upper canines (3+3) were found blocked in 111 subjects of the sample (5.83%), thus being the third most frequent anomaly. Most of the cases observed were bilateral (3.68%). No differences were found between sexes. Its relationship with dental age could not be considered significant (P = .653).

Transpositions

The sample prevalence of transpositions was 1.42%, most of which were upper transpositions (1.26% vs. 0.16% of lower transpositions). Regarding the type, the most frequent transposition was that of the upper canine with the first premolar, followed by the upper canine with the lateral incisor, and that of the lower canine with the lateral incisor. There were no significant differences between the sexes. Regarding the transposition of the upper canine with the first premolar, given that the sample of this anomaly was small, the analysis using the Wilcoxon test indicated that there were no differences in the distribution of dental age in the 2 subgroups (with and without transposition) (Z = -1.491). For the rest of the transpositions, the use of any type of statistical test was ruled out because they were anomalies with very low prevalence.

Transmigrations

No patient with transmigration was observed in the sample.

Ankylosis of primary molars

This anomaly was observed in 15 individuals of the sample (0.79%), with no differences between sexes. The second primary molars had the highest prevalence (0.67%), being very similar in both arches. In most cases (0.46%) there were 2 or more ankylosed primary molars. No differences were found in the distribution of the dental age of the group with ankylosed primary molars and those paired with the same.

Ectopic eruption of upper permanent first molar teeth

The prevalence of this anomaly in the sample was 2.10%, and the actual prevalence in the population can be estimated between 1.46 and 2.75%. More than half of the cases were bilateral (1.26%). The mean delay recorded in individuals with ectopic eruption of upper permanent first molars (6+6) was -0.33 ± 1.10 years, a difference that suggested a powerful trend, although it was not statistically significant (P = .062).

Primary failure of eruption of lower molars

This anomaly was observed only in the lower arch. It had a sample prevalence of 1.00%. It was most frequently observed in the second permanent molars (0.79%) and almost half of the cases were bilateral (0.47%). The difference in dental age with the group without anomalies indicated that it was advanced for the subgroup of cases, but this difference could not be accepted as significant (Z < 0.1).

Multivariate cluster analysis

In 672 subjects of the sample (35.3%) at least one anomaly was observed, and among them almost a quarter of the total sample (24.9%) presented only one, while in the remaining cases (10.4%) 2 or more anomalies were recorded. No individual presented more than 5 anomalies. On these 197 individuals presenting at least 2 dental anomalies, a hierarchical cluster analysis was performed, a procedure that classified 82.7% of them into 5 different segments. The first 3 segments (1 to 3) included 69% of the individuals in the subsample, and the next 2 (4 and 5) comprised 13.7%. Segment 1 (S1) consisted of 70 individuals and was characterized by the association between upper lateral incisor hypodontia (84.3%) and palatal impaction of upper canines (72.9%). Since approximately 75% of the subjects included in this block had one of these 2 anomalies, both were associated in a high percentage of individuals (at least 50%).

In segment 2 (S2), composed of 34 individuals, the presence of the different agenesis studied stood out. Lower second premolars dominated (88% in the segment, compared to 25.9% in the general group), which was associated with agenesis of upper second premolars (58.8%), hypodontia of upper lateral incisors (52.9%), and "other agenesis" (23.5%).

Segment 3 (S3), with 32 individuals, was mainly characterized by the association of upper canine blockage (68.8%) with lower permanent second molar impaction (56.3%).

In segment 4 (S4), formed by 14 individuals, the presence of upper canines impacted in the vestibular region predominated (92.9%), which in turn had a high association with hypodontia of the upper lateral incisors (50%). Notably, of the 3 subjects in the working sample in which "other maxillary transpositions" were recorded, 2 were grouped in this block.

In segment 5, composed of 13 individuals, 3 anomalies were mainly associated: palatal impaction of upper canines (61.5%), impaction of premolars (69.2%), and ectopic eruption of upper permanent first molars (61.5%).

Discussion

The development of human dentition is governed by a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors, which determine tooth number, morphology, size, and eruption sequence.20 Alterations in these developmental processes often result in dental anomalies that not only affect the esthetic and functional aspects of occlusion but also complicate orthodontic treatment planning.7 The present study, based on a large retrospective sample of 1,904 patients, aimed to evaluate the prevalence of various dental anomalies and to identify patterns of co-occurrence that may indicate a shared genetic etiology.

Prevalence of dental anomalies

The overall prevalence of dental anomalies in our study was 35.3%, with 10.4% of patients presenting 2 or more anomalies. These results are in line with the highest prevalence rates reported in orthodontic populations, which range widely from 1.73%21 to 61.3%.22 Such variability across studies can be explained by differences in the diagnostic criteria employed, the types of anomalies analysed, and the ethnic or population groups studied.

Hypodontia was the most frequent anomaly, particularly affecting upper lateral incisors (9.3%), followed by agenesis of lower second premolars (4.94%) and upper second premolars (1.94%). This distribution supports the concept of morphogenetic fields, which postulates that the most distal teeth of each series are the most prone to agenesis, particularly upper lateral incisors, second premolars, and third molars,12 as observed in the study of Yasa et al., where agenesis of the third molars occurred in 37.28 % in a Kazakh population.22, 23 Our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting upper lateral incisor hypodontia as the most common anomaly, although some studies report lower second premolar agenesis as the leading finding since it is one of the teeth with the greatest abnormalities, both in number and morphologically.24,25 The high prevalence observed in our study can also be attributed to the inclusion of microdontic and conoid lateral incisors within the hypodontia category.26

The second most common anomaly was palatal impaction of upper canines (9.19%), a prevalence similar to previous reports27. Vestibular impaction of upper canines was less frequent (1.84%), and bilateral cases predominated. Other anomalies, such as impaction of incisors (1.73%) and premolars (2.21%), were less frequent, with impacted upper incisors being significantly more common in males (P = .004). The prevalence of supernumerary teeth (1.47%), particularly mesiodens (0.74%), was within the expected range of 0.93–2.01%.28,29 Unlike other studies, we did not observe supernumerary molars, which have been reported in some populations.29

Sexual dimorphism and dental age

Although most anomalies were slightly more common in females, significant sexual dimorphism was found only in impacted upper incisors (P = .004). This distribution pattern, where hypodontia is more prevalent in females while supernumerary teeth are more frequent in males, is in line with Brook’s model.30

Our findings also highlight a relationship between dental anomalies and delayed dental development. Significant delays in dental age were associated with agenesis of lower second premolars (P = .001) and hypodontia of upper lateral incisors (P = .017), which corroborates previous studies.31,32 Palatal impaction of upper canines showed a trend toward delayed dental age, although it did not reach statistical significance.33 Conversely, mesiodens were associated with advanced dental age, supporting the hypothesis that supernumerary teeth may be linked to accelerated dental maturation.34

Associative patterns and genetic implications

A major contribution of this study is the identification of 5 distinct associative patterns of anomalies through hierarchical cluster analysis, which reinforces the hypothesis that certain dental anomalies share a common genetic basis, opening the possibility of future research in the identification of key genetic or familial data. The strongest association was observed between upper lateral incisor hypodontia and palatal impaction of upper canines, an association extensively documented in the literature.27,35 This relationship is often explained by 2 complementary theories: the eruptive guidance theory, which highlights the influence of lateral incisor anomalies on canine eruption36, and a genetic theory suggesting a shared hereditary origin for these conditions.37,38

Segment 2 of our cluster analysis was characterized by the co-occurrence of various types of agenesis, particularly involving lower and upper second premolars, as well as upper lateral incisors. This finding is consistent with the results of Baccetti19, who described similar associations, and supports the view that a single genetic alteration can affect multiple tooth groups.11,39 The association of ankylosed primary molars with this cluster further supports previous studies suggesting that infraocclusion can act as an early marker for hypodontia and other anomalies.33,40

Segment 3 revealed an association between upper canine blockage and lower second molar impaction. Given that second molar impaction is often attributed to space deficiency in the dental arch, this association likely reflects local mechanical factors rather than genetic ones.27,41,42 Likewise, the observed co-occurrence of hypodontia and incisor impaction may also result from anatomical and mechanical factors related to arch length and tooth position.43,44

Supernumerary teeth did not show significant associations with other anomalies, which is consistent with reports by Mercuri et al.45 and Francisco Cammarata-Scalisi.46 However, some trends were observed, suggesting that further studies with larger datasets may reveal subtler associations.

Unlike previous studies with smaller sample sizes (typically <800 patients),11,19,22 our analysis of 1,904 panoramic radiographs provides a highly robust dataset with sufficient statistical power to detect even low-prevalence anomalies. Moreover, the application of hierarchical cluster analysis, rarely employed in dental anomaly studies, allowed us to objectively classify individuals into subgroups with consistent patterns of co-occurrence. This methodological approach also reveals complex multi-anomaly profiles that can inform predictive diagnostics and future research.

Clinical implications

Understanding the prevalence and associative patterns of dental anomalies has practical implications for orthodontics and pediatric dentistry. The early identification of anomalies, particularly hypodontia or microdontia of upper lateral incisors, can serve as a predictive marker for conditions such as upper canine impaction or other complex eruption issues. This knowledge allows clinicians to implement preventive or interceptive measures at an early stage, potentially reducing the risk of complications such as root resorption of adjacent teeth.47 Furthermore, recognising the potential genetic basis of these anomalies may lead to improved counseling for patients and families, and could open the door to genetic screening and risk prediction in the future.

The patterns identified through cluster analysis have direct implications for clinical practice. Recognising these patterns during mixed dentition could allow orthodontists to plan interceptive interventions, potentially avoiding severe complications such as root resorption.47 This predictive approach represents a step toward more individualised and biologically informed orthodontic care.

Limitations of the study

The primary limitation of this study is the exclusive use of panoramic radiographs. While panoramic imaging is widely accessible and practical for epidemiological studies, it does not allow for detailed evaluation of structural or root anomalies because they are 2D images that suffer from structure superimposition, distortion, and magnification.22,48 Complementary imaging methods, such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), providing multiplanar, thin-sliced, 3D images without superimposition, offering superior resolution and accuracy for identifying fine anatomical changes, could have improved diagnostic precision,48 so their use in future research is recommended.

Another limitation of this study is the potential selection or referral bias, since the sample was drawn from orthodontic and pediatric dentistry practices, where patients may have a higher probability of presenting dental anomalies compared with the general population. This circumstance may lead to an overestimation of prevalence figures. Although strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to minimise diagnostic variability, the sample cannot be considered fully representative of the general pediatric or orthodontic population. Future studies using population-based samples or multicenter recruitment, including general dental practices, would help to mitigate this limitation and provide more generalizable estimates. Moreover, the presence of missing data for some variables could have influenced the robustness of the analyses.

Additionally, the retrospective nature of the study precludes the assessment of environmental factors and does not permit direct genetic confirmation of the associations observed. Despite these limitations, the large sample size (n = 1,904), strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the combination of descriptive, inferential, and multivariate statistical analyses provide robust evidence to support the findings.

While this study provides an extensive discussion of the potential genetic etiology underlying dental anomalies, it must be acknowledged that no direct genetic or familial data were collected or analysed. Therefore, any reference to hereditary mechanisms should be interpreted as a theoretical framework supported by existing literature, rather than as a conclusion derived from the present results. This represents an important limitation of the study. Future research incorporating genetic testing, family history, or molecular analyses would be essential to clarify the role of hereditary factors in the development of dental anomalies and to strengthen the evidence base in this area.

Conclusion

In the analysed population, 35.5% presented at least one dental anomaly, with hypodontia of upper lateral incisors and palatal impaction of upper canines being the most prevalent abnormalities. Upper incisor impaction was more frequently recorded in men than in women, a difference that reached statistical significance. A relationship was also identified between certain anomalies and dental age, with agenesis of lower second premolars, hypodontia of upper lateral incisors, and premolar impaction being associated with delayed dental maturation, while the presence of mesiodens was associated with advanced dental maturation. More than one anomaly was found in 10.4% of individuals, and the analysis of associative patterns allowed the establishment of 5 distinct segments, with the first 3 accounting for 69% of the cases with abnormalities. In particular, the first segment was characterised by the coexistence of hypodontia of upper lateral incisors and palatal impaction of canines. The second, due to the presence of multiple agenesis, predominantly in upper and lower second premolars and upper lateral incisors; and the third, due to the association of blocked upper canines with impacted lower second molars.

Overall, the findings of this study expand the available knowledge about the prevalence and association patterns of dental anomalies and constitute a starting point for future research aimed at clarifying their possible genetic origin.
