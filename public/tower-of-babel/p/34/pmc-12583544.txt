Stimulus-responsive Pickering emulsion droplet-based microreactors for green biphasic catalysis
Authors: Ansar Abbas, Xiaoqian Zhang, Sameer Hussain, Ruizhao Cai, Minghui Zhang, Tie Chong, Bernard P. Binks, Silong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pickering emulsions (PEs) are constantly emerging as promising platforms in biphasic catalysis. However, the existing nanoparticle-stabilized PE systems suffer from several drawbacks, such as reactant diffusion, particle leaching, incompatible reaction environments, tedious workups and poor catalyst recovery. The use of nanogel particles in PE catalysis could be highly advantageous in streamlining processes. Herein, we report a general approach to designing a PE droplet-based reactor by using a combination of egg white nanogel particles (EWNGs, 0.01 wt.%) and the newly synthesized switchable surfactant 3-(10-carboxydecyl)-1-methyl-1H-imidazolium bromide (ImDec-COOH) at a very low concentration of 0.50 wt.%. This versatile approach helps EWNGs in PEs overcome existing limitations, such as reactant diffusion and incompatible reaction environments, while stimulus-responsive surfactants increase the pH of PE systems. Thus, nanogel particles stabilize the PE system, and imidazolium-based surfactants effectively facilitate organic transformation through biphasic catalysis. As a proof-of-concept, the as-developed microreactor was employed to achieve a one-pot synthesis of benzothiazoles and benzoxazole in high yield, in situ product separation and excellent catalyst recovery.

Introduction

The discovery of active catalysis and product separation methods is critical because most chemical reactions in industry are catalytic. Pickering emulsions (PEs) are stabilized by colloidal particles of appropriate wettability1,2 and have advantages over conventional emulsions, such as improved stability, less flocculation and environmental friendliness. Recently, PEs have been effectively used in many interfacial catalytic processes because of their special interfacial properties3–5. With the constant growth of PE technology, solid catalysts are becoming attractive media and exhibit many superior characteristics to the corresponding bulk catalysts6–14. However, the existence of solid particles at the interface can hinder the diffusion of reactants to catalytic sites, which can affect the reaction rate and selectivity. Moreover, the solid particles used to stabilize these PEs can sometimes leach into the reaction mixture, leading to contamination and loss of catalytic activity (Scheme 1A)15. Unfortunately, the highly stable oil‒water interfaces of PEs are not favorable for demulsification, which is necessary for product separation and catalyst recovery after completion of the reaction. One of the major challenges in this area of catalysis has yet to be overcome17,18. Filtration and centrifugation are common approaches for demulsifying a stable PE19. Although effective in many cases, they are unfeasible when the droplet size is < 1 μm. Thus, detrimental factors such as high energy consumption, catalyst loss, filter blockage and longer processing times hinder PE catalysis20 in terms of product separation and catalyst recovery. Moreover, these methods are incompatible with the concept of a sustainable approach.

In this context, PEs combined with stimuli-responsive surfactants have recently attracted increased amounts of attention due to their ability to be used for controllable surface modification21. After reversible transformation from active to inactive states, surfactants can be recovered or used again from the product stream by applying environmental stimuli such as CO222, pH23, light24, redox processes25 and magnetic fields26. However, additional separation is still needed for oil-related formulations to remove such inactive surfactants from oil-soluble or oil products. Recently, Yu and colleagues27 described enzymatic hydrolysis processes in CO2/N2-responsive PEs, where the emulsions were broken by bubbling N2 and the nanoparticles as well as enzymes were recycled from the water phase. However, the oil-soluble inactive surfactant (N,N-dimethyl dodecyl amine) was distributed in the organic phase, contaminating the product and making it difficult to recycle or reuse (Scheme 1C).

To address these issues, developing innovative sustainable approaches to PE catalysis is essential for integrating chemical reactions, product separation, catalyst recovery and emulsion recycling. The use of nanogel particles instead of solid particles in PE catalysis could be an attractive alternative. Nanogel particles can potentially solve the issues of particle leaching, size and reactant diffusion by offering a robust interface between the particles and the continuous phase, which ensures good catalytic activity. The porosity, pore size tunability and high surface area of the nanogel particles facilitate their strong adsorption onto the surface of droplets, creating a more stable PE system for the loading of catalytic species, which can be confined within the particles to prevent leaching into the continuous phase. These multiple features of nanogel particles encouraged us to develop a more ideal nanogel particle-stabilized PE system to avoid particle leaching and reactant diffusion (Scheme 1D). Accordingly, a multifunctional pH-responsive PE system was developed by using egg white nanogel particles (EWNGs, 0.01 wt.%) and a bola pH-switchable surfactant (3-(10-carboxydecyl)-1-methylimidazolium bromide, ImDec-COOH). The surfactant can be converted from the cationic form to a hydrophilic zwitterionic form (ImDec-COO-) through alteration of the pH, which allows the PE system to be easily demulsified (Scheme 2A and 2B). To demonstrate feasibility, the synthesis of benzo-fused heterocyclic compounds such as benzoxazole and benzothiazole was achieved in the newly developed PE system. Importantly, after completion of the reaction and under alkaline pH, the surfactant and nanogel particles were transferred to the water phase, leaving the product in the organic phase, making separation easy and catalyst recovery possible (vide infra).

Results and discussion

The highly pure egg white nanogel (EWNG) particles exhibited an average diameter (dav) of ~181 nm (Fig. S1A) and possessed a zeta potential of −12 mV when dispersed in pure water (pH = 7, Fig. S1B) with a cube-like morphology (Fig. S1C and D). To check for the presence of any free protein in the EWNGs or the surrounding aqueous medium, we initially examined the surface tension of the EWNG particles in pure water. The results revealed a decrease in surface tension as the concentration of EWNG particles increased. On the other hand, when the EWNG particle dispersions were subjected to centrifugation for 5 min at 10,000 rpm, the surface tension of the supernatant was found to be nearly identical (71.8 ± 0.1 mN/m) to the surface tension of pure water (Fig. S2A and B). This strongly indicated that the highly pure EWNG particles did not contain free protein and thus acted as a stabilizer of the emulsions. Moreover, it has also been reported that egg whites can self-assemble into nanogel particles without leaving any free protein when exposed to heat or other denaturing conditions28. EWNGs were selected as stabilizers for Pickering emulsion catalysis due to their porous structure, high surface area, and excellent interfacial adsorption properties. Their use addresses common limitations of solid particles, such as leaching and limited reactant diffusion by providing a robust, pH-responsive interface that enhances emulsion stability and catalytic efficiency.

A stimulus-responsive PE was developed using EWNGs (0.01 wt.%) and 3-(10-carboxydecyl)-1-methyl-1H-imidazolium bromide (ImDec-COOH). ImDec-COOH was chosen due to its unique amphiphilic structure, catalytic nature, and pH sensitivity. ImDec-COOH consists of an imidazolium cation along with a carboxylic group on either side of its chain. Under alkaline conditions, the carboxylic acid group can be converted to a carboxylate ion, and ImDec-COOH can be transformed from cationic (critical micelle concentration, cmc = 1.0 wt.%, γcmc = 42.1 mN/m, pH = 4 ± 0.5) to zwitterionic (ImDec-COO-, cmc = 1.5 wt.%, γcmc = 44.2 mN/m, pH = 7.5 ± 0.5) (Figs. S3, S4A and Table S2). ImDec-COO- can be converted to its cationic form (ImDec-COOH) by decreasing the pH (4.0 ± 0.5). The surface activity at the oil-water interface also changes depending on the pH (Fig. S4B). The structure of ImDec-COOH was characterized by mass spectrometry (TOF-MS) and 1H-NMR (Figs. S5 and S6A). To prove the formation of a microreactor and prior to investigating the one-pot synthesis of heterocyclic compounds within the PE droplet-based microreactor, we investigated the morphology of the EWNG particles and dried droplets of PEs separately. As shown in Fig. 3(A), the EWNGs exhibited porous nanogel structures with large surface areas ideal for forming a dense particle layer at the oil–water interface in emulsions. Additionally, elemental mapping analysis revealed the homogenous distribution of C and O across the gel network. Fluorescence microscopy images of a PE (3.5 mL of water/3.5 mL of oil) stabilized with EWNGs (0.01 wt.%) and ImDec-COOH (0.50 wt.%) stained with Nile red dye (0.1%) were recorded using a Carl Zeiss observer3 (HAL 100) microscope under an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and emission wavelength of 565 nm, revealing green spherical droplets and confirming the presence of oil in the core (Figure S10). To achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the emulsion structure and the formation of the microreactor, cryo-TEM was performed on frozen emulsion droplets. Since freezing n-decane/water (1:1) was somewhat challenging during cryo-TEM analysis, only 2% n-decane was used in combination with water for analysis to ensure proper vitrification and image quality. Figure 3B illustrates the cube-like morphology of the EWNGs adsorbed at the oil-water interface, confirming their role as stabilizers in the PE system. Additionally, the microstructure of PEs was also confirmed through TEM analysis, which revealed that porous EWNGs were assembled at the oil–water interface of droplets (Fig. 3C), consistent with previous observations by Floury et al.29. The pores between particles create accessible pathways for the substrate, promoting efficient mass transfer during catalytic reactions. Consequently, this characteristic not only facilitates rapid mass transfer but also addresses the existing limitations in PE catalysis. The corresponding C, O and N elemental mapping analyses were also performed to locate the positions of the surfactant and EWNGs in the emulsion droplets. The control mapping experiment with surfactant only indicated that elemental C, O and N were homogeneously distributed in the water phase, where they formed micelles around the oil droplets (Fig. 3D). On the other hand, along with surfactants, the EWNGs were adsorbed onto the surface of the oil droplets, ultimately forming a layer, which suggested that the EWNGs were adsorbed onto the oil droplet through the balance of electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions with the surfactant and the oil droplets (Fig. 3E). CLSM images showed that the fluorescent EWNGs were essentially located at the oil–water interface. The developed PE system (particles + surfactant) demonstrated excellent pH-responsive behavior, as shown in Fig. 4. When the pH of the PE was adjusted to 7.5 ± 0.5 through the addition of NaOH (20 µL of 0.1 M) to an emulsion (3.5 mL water/3.5 mL oil) with gentle stirring, ImDec-COOH was deprotonated into the more hydrophilic zwitterionic form (ImDec-COO-), and the emulsion demulsified in 20 min. Interestingly, most of the surfactant molecules entered the aqueous phase after phase separation triggered by alkaline pH. The 1H NMR spectra showed a distinct shift, corresponding to the presence of the surfactant in the aqueous phase. More impressively, when the pH was adjusted back to acidic (4.0 ± 0.5) by the addition of concentrated HCl (0.1 M), the surfactant molecules were protonated again into a cationic form (ImDec-COOH), and a stable O/W PE with a similar droplet size to the original PE (1.5 µm) was obtained following sonification. Notably, after 6 cycles, the emulsion stability and droplet size were nearly identical to those initially observed, indicating that the PE emulsifier could be reused and recycled from the water phase.Fig. 4Investigation of pH-responsive behavior of PE system.Digital photos and optical micrographs of PEs stabilized by 0.01 wt.% EWNGs and 0.50 wt.% ImDec-COOH after several demulsification and restabilization processes using HCl/NaOH followed by sonification.

To gain deeper insight into the pH-responsive behavior of the PE, the interaction between the surfactant and EWNGs was then studied under alkaline and acidic conditions (Fig. 5A, B). Under acidic pH (4.0), the zeta potential of the EWNGs (0.01 wt.%) rapidly increased from −12 to 0 mV upon increasing the ImDec-COOH concentration from 0.005 to 0.5 wt.%. This can be attributed to charge neutralization of the EWNG particles by adsorption of the positively charged ImDec-COOH (Fig. 5C). It is believed that at a very low surfactant concentration, cationic ImDec-COOH is adsorbed on the negatively charged EWNGs via electrostatic interactions. After increasing the ImDec-COOH concentration to 2.0 wt.%, the zeta potential further increased to +19.6 mV, indicating hemimicelle or bilayer adsorption30. On the other hand, at alkaline pH (7.5), the zeta potential of the EWNGs changed only slightly from −12 to −9.5 mV upon increasing the ImDec-COOH concentration from 0.005 to 2.0 wt.% (Fig. 5D). This indicates that at high surfactant concentrations31, most bola-type zwitterionic ImDec-COO- molecules prefer to self-assemble through favorable electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions instead of binding with anionic EWNGs. Alternatively, as described earlier, the opposite charges of the two hydrophilic groups in zwitterionic surfactants are not very balanced, and the imidazolium cation (positive charge) could be slightly weaker than the negative charge of the carboxylate group32. Therefore, ImDec-COO- could behave as an anionic surfactant and be partially neutralized by the imidazolium cation ion via the formation of ion pairs. Therefore, ImDec-COO- desorbed from the EWNGs, rendering them hydrophilic again and resulting in demulsification.Fig. 5Investigation of interactions between surfactant and EWNGs.Schematic illustration of interactions between surfactant and EWNGs at (A) acidic and (B) alkaline pH values. The interactions between ImDec-COOH and the EWNGs were then analyzed via surface tension measurements33. After the addition of 0.01 wt.% EWNGs at acidic pH, the surface tension of the ImDec-COOH solutions increased slightly (Fig. 5E). This is because the adsorption of ImDec-COOH at the oil-water interface decreased due to the adsorption of the positively charged ImDec-COOH on the anionic EWNGs. The surface tension results confirmed that a monolayer of ImDec-COOH was formed on the surface of the EWNG particles, rendering the particles surface active34. However, ImDec-COOH can be adsorbed on particles via hemi-micelle or bilayer formation at higher surfactant concentrations. In contrast, the surface tension of the surfactant at alkaline pH was unaffected after the addition of EWNGs (Fig. 5F), confirming that there was no adsorption of ImDec-COO- on the EWNG particles35.

Furthermore, the interactions between ImDec-COOH and EWNGs can also be confirmed by monitoring shifts in the contact angle of n-decane oil droplets captured on glass slides immersed in aqueous solutions of different surfactant concentrations and EWNGs (0.01 wt.%) at 25 °C. Upon increasing the ImDec-COOH concentration from 0.005 to 0.10 wt.% under acidic conditions, the contact angle increased from 29.3° to 46.3° (Table 1), indicating an increase in hydrophobicity36. However, above 0.50 wt.% surfactant, the contact angle decreased, indicating that the transition from monolayer to bilayer adsorption rendered the system hydrophilic again37. The non-adsorption of surfactants on the EWNGs under alkaline conditions was also obvious, as the contact angle hardly changed with respect to the surfactant concentration (Table 1). Additionally, since EWNGs are rich in functional groups capable of hydrogen bonding, such as amide (-CONH-) and hydroxyl (-OH) groups, the formation of hydrogen bonds between the -COOH groups of ImDec-COOH and EWNGs is plausible. Recently, we described the formulation of an O/W emulsion of very small droplet size (< 1.0 µm) by using a combination of positively charged carbon dots (CDs) and the cationic surfactant CTAB38. The concentration of CDs in the water phase was reduced only slightly (0.45 wt.%) compared to the initial concentration (0.50 wt.%), indicating that almost no CD particles were adsorbed at the oil–water interface of the emulsion droplets. Thus, it is believed that the particles remained suspended in the water phase. The concentration of EWNGs in PEs after creaming was also measured, and the results are shown in Table S3. This finding provides evidence that the concentration of EWNG particles at the oil-water interface of PEs containing ImDec-COOH (0.01–1.0 wt.%) decreased considerably from 0.5 to 0.025 wt.%, showing that nearly all the EWNG particles were adsorbed at the oil-water interface of the emulsion droplets. In addition to n-decane, stable O/W Pickering emulsions were also obtained with toluene and vegetable oil using a combination of 0.01 wt.% EWNGs and 0.005–2.0 wt.% ImDec-COOH. The corresponding digital photos and optical micrographs are provided in Figs. S11-S14. These emulsions were stable for more than 6 months.

The as-synthesized surfactant (ImDec-COOH) possesses an imidazolium cationic group at the end of its molecular structure, which is assumed to be catalytically active via the production of superoxide radical (Fig. S15). Hence, the aforementioned pH-responsive PEs may be crucial in the development of effective, inexpensive and recyclable reaction systems. As a proof-of-concept, pH-responsive PEs were used as reaction media for the synthesis of benzo-fused heterocyclic compounds, as shown in Fig. 6a. The reaction demonstrated highly efficient conversion, easy separation, reusability of the catalyst and a shorter reaction time (20 min). In addition to its scientific significance, the synthesis of benzo-fused heterocyclic compounds such as benzimidazoles and benzothiazoles is highly beneficial due to their diverse biological and therapeutic activities, such as antiviral39, antitumor40 and anticancer41 activities. The most popular methods generally involve the condensation of an aldehyde with 2-aminothiophenol and 2-aminophenol in the presence of oxidative reagents and catalysts, such as the Fe(HSO4)342, cobalt(III)-salen complex supported on activated carbon43, H2O2/CAN18 and 4-methoxy-TEMPO44. Many of these reagents are expensive, corrosive or toxic and require long reaction times and tedious work-up. Keeping these drawbacks in mind, an as-fabricated PE droplet-based microreactor was employed. Since pH-responsive PEs are O/W type, water-soluble reactants such as 2-aminophenol can diffuse into the water phase during stirring and participate in the reaction. After completion of the reaction, the PE could be demulsified under gentle stirring by adjusting the pH to 7.5-8 (Fig. 6B). Notably, the products in the organic phase can be easily separated using a syringe and simple liquid transfer. A PE can be regenerated again for subsequent reaction cycles by adjusting the pH to acidic (4.0). The surfactant (catalyst) and EWNGs in the water phase could be reused directly in the next batch of reactions without requiring any further treatment.Fig. 6Investigation of substrate scope.A Synthesis of benzoxazoles in switchable PE reaction media. The recyclability of the biphasic catalysis system was thoroughly checked for 15 reaction cycles after separating the product each time by changing the emulsion pH to alkaline. Selected photographs of the reaction mixture (following completion of the reaction) after a particular cycle and the product in the organic layer after the respective separations are shown in Fig. 7(A, B). It is clear from the images that the green benzoxazole product was efficiently transferred to the organic phase after phase separation. Moreover, fluorescence images of the organic and water phases taken after separation induced by NaOH (pH 7.5) also confirmed the presence of the product exclusively in the organic phase (Fig. 7C (i, ii)). On the other hand, most surfactants (catalysts) remain in the water phase. In addition, the weight loss of the surfactant (catalyst) was ~30% after 15 reaction cycles (Fig. 7D, E). The yield of the product obtained using the recovered surfactant (catalyst) after each cycle was changed only marginally, as shown in Fig. 7F. Therefore, product-catalyst separation and surfactant (catalyst) recycling were conducted throughout 15 reaction cycles in a facile manner by simply altering the pH, circumventing the constraints of traditional separation methods. It is noteworthy to mention that the emulsion system remained mostly stable during the course of the reaction. However, since the reaction was carried out at elevated temperatures, slight phase separation was observed after the reaction process. Despite this minor instability, the system maintained sufficient emulsion integrity to facilitate effective biphasic catalysis. Complete phase separation was successfully achieved by triggering the pH to 7.5 at the end of the reaction. Overall, the highly efficient benzo-fused heterocyclic reactions performed using this microreactor exhibit the following advantages: (i) they greatly reduce the risk caused by corrosive/toxic oxidative reagents and catalysts; (ii) they can save considerable time/energy since conventional protocols require long reaction times and tedious workup; and (iii) the emulsion catalysis system is highly recyclable. Conclusion

In summary, a Pickering emulsion (PE) droplet-based microreactor was designed and developed for the one-pot green synthesis of benzo-fused heterocyclic products through biphasic catalysis with in situ separation and excellent catalyst recovery. A pH-switchable microreactor was fabricated using a combination of the newly synthesized surfactant ImDec-COOH (0.5 wt.%) and egg white nanogel particles (EWNGs, 0.01 wt.%) in n-decane-in-water PEs. A conceptually innovative method for in situ separation and reprocessing of surfactants (catalysts) is established by simply tuning the pH. The process of product-catalyst separation and catalyst recycling is found to be quite efficient, as evidenced by 15 reaction cycles. The overall efficacy of benzo-fused heterocyclic reactions is considerably improved by simplifying the workup procedure since consecutive catalyst separation and recycling can be performed in one pot. Therefore, the proposed protocol opens a new pathway for the development of new sustainable and green organic transformations and will likely facilitate a broad range of organic reactions. However, the use of nanogel particles in PE catalysis is still a new research area, and further studies are needed to optimize their properties and to investigate their performance in different catalytic systems.

Methods

Preparation of hen egg white nanogels

Hen egg white (EW) was heated in a water bath (110 °C, 1 h) until gelation. Later the acquired gels were cut into small cubes (1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm). Then 50 g of egg white gel was added into 100 mL ultrapure water followed by homogenization using a homogenizer (IKA-T25, IKA Instruments Ltd., Germany) for 10 min at a speed of 4000 rpm. The homogenized samples were further crushed by a high-pressure homogenizer (M-110y, high-shear fluid processor, USA). The final concentration of the EWNGs was 50% (w/v).

Core-shell preparation of egg white nanogel linked with ImDec-COOH

The EWNGs were further diluted to 0.005%, 0.01%, 0.05%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5% and 2.0% w/v from an original dispersion before Pickering emulsion preparation. Moreover, different concentrations of ImDec-COOH (0.005%, 0.01%, 0.05%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5% and 2.0% w/v) were also prepared and added to the above egg protein nanogel dispersions with 10 min of stirring to obtain the targeted core-shell (EW-ImDec-COOH) samples.

Preparation of O/W Pickering emulsion

2.5 mL of decane was dispersed into 2.5 mL of EW-ImDec-COOH dispersion or ultrapure water with sonification (ultrasonic bath sonicator, SKE-22S). The glass vial was hung securely in the ultrasonic bath filled with water and the power was set to 700 W. The mixture was sonicated for 10 min ensuring it was fully submerged. The prepared Pickering emulsion was stored at room temperature. Photographs of emulsions were recorded using a digital camera. Microscopy of emulsions was recorded using a VHX-1000 microscope (Keyence Co.). SEM images of dried Pickering emulsion droplets were recorded using a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, S-4800). After emulsion preparation, a drop of it was spread onto a clean dry silicon wafer and left to dry at 25 °C inside an incubator. Once dried, the silicon wafer was affixed to the specimen stage of the FE-SEM instrument (Hitachi S-4800) using conductive adhesive and the sample was coated with gold and analyzed at an acceleration voltage of 3 kV.

General procedure for preparation of benzoxazoles and benzothiazoles

A 20 mL vial was charged with a mixture of 2-aminophenol or 2-amino thiophenol (1 mmol), triethyl orthoformate (1.5 mmol), egg white nanogels (0.01 wt.%) and surfactant (catalyst, 0.50 mmol) into 1.5 mL of n-decane and 1.5 mL of water (1:1). The combined biphasic system was sonicated for 5 min eventually forming a green O/W Pickering emulsion. Later the mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 20 min. After completion of the reaction (monitored by thin-layer chromatography, TLC), the mixture was cooled to room temperature and NaOH (1 M) solution was added to adjust the pH (7.5 ± 0.5). After keeping the sample for 15 min at room temperature the upper organic phase was transferred by syringe and evaporated to afford the product.
