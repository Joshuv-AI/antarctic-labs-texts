Integrating AUROC and SSMD for quality control in high-throughput screening assays
Authors: Xiaohua Douglas Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-throughput screening (HTS) assays are pivotal in modern biomedical research, particularly in drug discovery and functional genomics. Ensuring the quality and reliability of HTS data is critical, especially when dealing with the small sample sizes that are typical in such assays. This study explores the integration of two powerful statistical metrics—Strictly Standardized Mean Difference (SSMD) and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC)—for quality control (QC) in HTS. SSMD offers a standardized, interpretable measure of effect size, while AUROC provides a threshold-independent assessment of discriminative power. By establishing the theoretical and empirical relationships between AUROC and SSMD, we demonstrate how these metrics complement each other and enhance QC practices. We provide parametric, semi-parametric, and non-parametric estimation methods, and demonstrate the utility of the integrated framework in real HTS datasets. Our findings support the joint application of SSMD and AUROC as a robust and interpretable approach to improving QC in HTS, particularly under constraints of limited sample sizes of positive and negative controls.Keywords: SSMD, AUROC, d⁺-probability, High-throughput screening, Quality control

High-throughput screening (HTS) assays have become a cornerstone of modern biomedical research, enabling the rapid evaluation of vast libraries of compounds or biological entities [1–3]. These assays are indispensable in fields such as drug discovery, functional genomics, and toxicology, where large-scale testing is required to identify potential hits for further investigation [4–6]. However, the sheer volume of data generated by HTS assays necessitates stringent quality control (QC) measures to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of results [7–9]. Without robust QC, technical variability—arising from factors such as plate-to-plate differences or reagent inconsistencies—can compromise the integrity of the data, leading to erroneous conclusions and wasted resources [4,5,8,9].

Central to effective QC in HTS assays are statistical metrics that assess the performance of the assay in distinguishing between positive and negative controls [7,8]. One such metric is SSMD which is a well-established metric for QC and hit selection in HTS [4,5,8,10–15]. Introduced by Zhang (2007) [8] as a robust alternative to traditional metrics such as the Z-factor [7], SSMD offers intuitive interpretation and a clear probabilistic foundation. It quantifies the standardized mean difference between control groups, accounting for their variability [5,8]. As a standardized effect size, SSMD includes established thresholds to classify assay quality [5,9].

The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), a widely recognized measure in binary classification, evaluates the assay’s ability to differentiate between positive and negative controls across all possible thresholds [16,17]. In practice, AUROC is typically estimated using non-parametric methods. The most common approach is based on the Mann–Whitney U statistic [18], which computes AUROC by comparing all possible pairs of positive and negative instances to estimate the probability that a randomly selected positive instance is ranked higher than a randomly selected negative one. It is simple to implement, robust across a wide range of data distributions, and supports confidence interval estimation—most notably via DeLong’s method [19] and bootstrap resampling techniques [20], both of which are widely implemented in statistical software (e.g., pROC in R, scikit-learn in Python). While ties in scores may slightly reduce precision, they are handled appropriately through averaged ranking. Nonetheless, non-parametric AUROC estimation needs additional attention to handle tied score and can be less efficient than parametric methods when parametric assumptions (e.g., normality) hold. In such cases, parametric approaches—leveraging AUROC’s relationship with d⁺-probability and SSMD—can offer gains in efficiency and interpretability, as explored in this article.

While SSMD is a well-established metric for QC in HTS studies—valued for its interpretability and standardized thresholds rooted in d⁺-probability and effect size—AUROC offers a complementary perspective by quantifying discriminative power, a standard in machine learning. In HTS, AUROC provides a global summary of ranking performance. This probabilistic view, while widely used in statistics and machine learning, differs from the clinical decision-making emphasis of researchers such as Margaret Pepe [21], where evaluation focuses on specific operating points and the trade-offs between false positives and false negatives. These considerations are also relevant for hit selection in HTS, as assay thresholds ultimately determine which compounds advance to later validation stages. AUROC’s global nature does not directly encode such threshold-specific trade-offs, but for QC in HTS, its value lies in identifying assays with strong overall discriminatory capacity, which supports prioritizing compounds with large phenotypic effects and reducing the likelihood of passing weak or spurious hits. SSMD is unaffected by tied scores and requires no special handling for them; its confidence intervals can be derived analytically using the non-central t-distribution, as described by Zhang [5,22]. Integrating SSMD and AUROC enables a more comprehensive evaluation of assay performance by capturing both effect size and classification accuracy.

In this manuscript, we explore the integration of AUROC and SSMD for QC in HTS assays. We examine their mathematical relationship and analyze real HTS data to evaluate the feasibility of this approach, under small and moderate sample size conditions. We also assess the potential advantages and limitations of combining these metrics, aiming to determine whether their joint use can support more meaningful QC practices. Ultimately, our goal is to enhance the reliability of HTS assays, ensuring that they continue to drive meaningful advancements in biomedical research.

Mathematical relationships among AUROC, d⁺-Probability And SSMD

The relationships among AUROC, d⁺-probability, and SSMD can be examined from both theoretical and practical perspectives, which roughly correspond to the population and sample levels in statistical terms.

AUROC is originally defined as the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, which assesses the ability to classify positive and negative controls based on measured values. In ROC analysis, a threshold is applied to these values to determine whether each sample is classified as positive or negative. Two key performance metrics at any given threshold are sensitivity—the proportion of positive controls correctly identified as positive—and specificity—the proportion of negative controls correctly identified as negative. The ROC curve plots sensitivity (True Positive Rate) against 1 — specificity (False Positive Rate). See Fig. 1 for examples of ROC curves and their corresponding AUROC’s. The ROC curve–based AUROC reflects how well the classifier distinguishes positive from negative controls across all possible thresholds, providing a summary measure of the model’s discriminative ability. Specifically, AUROC = 1: Perfect classification — all positive controls are ranked higher than all negative controls. AUROC = 0.5: No discriminative power — equivalent to random guessing; and AUROC < 0.5: Inverted classification — the model performs worse than random; flipping predictions would improve performance [23].

In mathematics, it can be shown that the ROC curve–based AUROC is equal to the probability that a randomly selected positive control receives a higher score than a randomly selected negative control given the mean of the positive control is greater than that of the negative control [25]. This probability is termed the probability-based AUROC and is identical to d⁺-probability. Zhang [5,24] derived the relationship between d⁺-probability and SSMD under three distributions: normal, symmetric unimodal and unimodal with finite variance, respectively. Estimation of AUROC, d⁺-Probability and sSMD

The mathematical relationships among AUROC, d⁺-probability, and SSMD are derived under the assumption of an infinite number of samples for both positive and negative controls—that is, they are defined at the population level in a statistical term. In practice, however, we typically work with a limited number of samples, usually between 2 and 16 per control group. That is, we have to work on the sample level in a statistical term. As a result, we must estimate AUROC, d⁺-probability, and SSMD from the available data and then explore the relationships among these estimates.

AUROC, d⁺-probability and SSMD can be estimated using both parametric and non-parametric methods, each offering distinct advantages depending on the context. Parametric estimation assumes a specific distribution for the underlying data—typically normality—which allows for more precise and potentially efficient estimation when the assumptions hold. They are likely to have close mathematical form and can thus offer analytical advantages, especially when the underlying assumptions are met.

In contrast, non-parametric estimation makes minimal assumptions about the underlying data distribution, relying instead on the empirical ranking of observed values. This approach enhances robustness to violations of distributional assumptions, making it suitable for data with outliers or skewed distributions. Since non-parametric methods do not require normality or homoscedasticity, they are broadly applicable across various types of measured values. However, these methods can be less efficient than parametric approaches when the parametric assumptions are valid. They often require larger sample sizes to achieve comparable precision and may perform suboptimally in small-sample settings (See Panels H1 and H2 of Fig. 1 for an example).

The use of both approaches is essential: parametric methods provide analytical convenience and power under ideal conditions, while non-parametric methods offer flexibility and reliability in real-world settings where assumptions may not hold. Together, they provide a comprehensive understanding of AUROC, d⁺-probability, and SSMD across diverse experimental and clinical scenarios. Rank-based estimation of AUROC

The rank-based estimation of AUROC, also termed as the empirical estimation, is the most common non-parametric method and is mathematically built on the Mann–Whitney U statistic. Hanley and McNeil (1982) [27] provided one of the earliest comprehensive explanations of the relationship between the AUROC, the Mann–Whitney U statistic, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, clarifying that AUROC can be interpreted as the probability that a randomly chosen positive case will have a higher test value than a randomly chosen negative case. Empirical ROC curve integration

This non-parametric method involves directly constructing the empirical ROC curve and computing the area under it using the trapezoidal rule. Examples of QC in HTS studies

To demonstrate the application of both SSMD and AUROC for QC, we utilized two HTS screens previously analyzed by Zhang et al. [11]. who accounted for sampling error in their QC assessment. The first was a hepatitis C virus (HCV) siRNA primary screen in a 384-well format, and the second was an arrayed CRISPR/Cas9 screen in a 96-well format aimed at identifying host factors involved in coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) infection. As in a typical HTS screen, these experiments incorporated small numbers of control wells per plate—16 positive and 16 negative controls for the HCV screen, and just 2 positive and 5 negative controls for the CRISPR/Cas9 screen—highlighting the challenges of sampling variability in typical HTS designs.

Given that the log-transformed data satisfied normality and homoscedasticity in both screens (Zhang et al [11].), SSMD was estimated using the UMVUE under these assumptions (Formula 1). In parallel, AUROC was estimated via the U-statistic and by integrating the empirical ROC curve for each plate. Because no tied values occurred, the U-statistic and empirical ROC integration produced identical AUROC estimates.

In the HCV siRNA screen, separation between positive and negative controls increased from Plate 05 to Plate 77 (Fig. 1, Panels A1–B1), with SSMD rising from 3.177 to 6.232 and SSMD-derived AUROC from 0.99925 to 1. The empirical AUROC, however, remained 1 for both plates due to non-overlapping values, masking the improvement in QC. A similar pattern occurred in the CVB3 CRISPR/Cas9 screen (Panels E1–G1), where SSMD increased from 1.917 to 15.416 and SSMD-derived AUROC from 0.97236 to 1, while the empirical AUROC stayed at 1 throughout.

To assess poor separation, two simulated plates (S1, S2) were generated under settings similar to the HCV and CVB3 screens. With small sample sizes (e.g., two positive control wells in the CVB3 setting), it was not uncommon for all positive control values to be smaller than all negative control values (Panel H1) when true separation was poor. Because it is rank-based, the empirical AUROC returned 1 in such cases, incorrectly suggesting perfect separation and excellent QC. In contrast, the SSMD-derived AUROC for Plate S2 was low, correctly indicating poor QC. When sample sizes were larger (e.g., 16 wells per control in the HCV setting), the two AUROC measures aligned more closely (Panels D1–D2). Across all scenarios, SSMD-derived ROC curves were smooth, whereas empirical ROC curves were stepwise.

Panel C1 further illustrates that SSMD-derived AUROC might be more robust to outliers under approximate normality. Here, two outliers reduced the empirical AUROC to 0.88672, while the SSMD-derived AUROC remained higher at 0.98195, closer to the ideal 1 expected without outliers. While empirical AUROC is theoretically more robust when normality is strongly violated, these results highlight the SSMD-derived AUROC’s advantage in normally or near-normally distributed data: a smooth, idealized measure of classification performance that may avoid misleading QC conclusions in small-sample or extreme-value scenarios.

Fig. 2 shows the results of both SSMD and AUROC in evaluating assay quality in all plates across the two real HTS screens. Despite the small number of control wells per plate, the observed SSMD values across most plates met or exceeded the QC thresholds when designated negative controls were used. In the HCV siRNA screen, the majority of plates achieved estimated SSMD values of ≥4.2, satisfying the sample-level QC criterion for 384-well plates (Panel A). Based on Table 3, the corresponding AUROC values derived from SSMD would be expected to exceed 0.999999 under normal distribution assumptions, 0.9874 under symmetric unimodal distributions, and 0.9748 under general unimodal distributions. Empirically, AUROC values estimated using U-statistics or empirical ROC curve integration were equal to 1 in 92 out of 97 plates (Panel B), confirming near-perfect discrimination. Notably, in both screens, the AUROC values estimated by U-statistics were identical to those derived from empirical ROC curve integration, underscoring the reliability and consistency of these estimation methods (Panels B and D).

These extremely high AUROC values demonstrate that even under conditions of limited control data—such as small sample sizes typical in HTS screens—the assays with high quality maintained strong discriminative power. This reinforces the value of integrating SSMD and AUROC in quality control evaluations, as AUROC complements SSMD by offering a probabilistic interpretation of group separation and robustness to distributional assumptions. Together, these results demonstrate the feasibility and utility of integrating SSMD and AUROC for HTS quality control, particularly in small sample settings common in biological screens.

Discussion and conclusion

This study offers a comprehensive analysis of the theoretical foundations and practical advantages of integrating SSMD and AUROC for QC in HTS assays. While SSMD is valued for its interpretability to mean differences and effect size [5,8,26], AUROC complements it by quantifying an assay’s discriminative capacity across all thresholds [19,23,27]. By unifying these metrics through their shared basis in d⁺-probability, we propose a probabilistically coherent framework that enhances QC decision-making.

The mathematical derivation demonstrates an intrinsic connection between AUROC and SSMD in both parametric and non-parametric settings. Parametric estimators, which rely on distributional assumptions, can achieve greater precision when normality and homoscedasticity hold, whereas non-parametric methods offer robustness across a broader range of distributions. Non-parametric approaches require additional steps to handle tied scores, whereas AUROC derived from SSMD does not. This duality enables practitioners to tailor QC strategies to the specific characteristics of their data.

The AUROC values corresponding to the SSMD-based QC criteria [5, 26], as presented in Tables 2 and 3, establish a probabilistic framework for interpreting SSMD thresholds in HTS studies. By linking SSMD cut-offs to AUROC values under various distributional assumptions—both at the population and sample levels—this framework offers a standardized and probabilistically meaningful interpretation of assay quality. At the population level, AUROC values approaching 1 under normal and unimodal distributions indicate that high SSMD values correspond to near-perfect discrimination between control groups. When incorporating sampling error [11], as reflected in Table 3, the framework remains robust and supports strong discriminatory power. Even under relaxed assumptions such as unimodality, AUROC values remain consistently high, reinforcing the reliability of SSMD thresholds across different plate formats and experimental conditions. This AUROC-based interpretation enhances the clarity, comparability, and generalizability of QC benchmarks across studies.

Real-world applications, including HCV siRNA screen in the 384-well format [28] and CRISPR/Cas9 HTS screen in the 96-well format [29], illustrate the practical feasibility and benefits of this integrated approach. Despite the frequent challenge of limited control replicates per plate, both SSMD and AUROC—used independently or in combination—consistently identified high assay quality. Notably, AUROC provided a meaningful probabilistic interpretation of SSMD thresholds, contextualizing QC decisions under varying distributional scenarios.

Findings from the HCV siRNA and CVB3 CRISPR/Cas9 screens underscore the practical value of combining SSMD and AUROC. As the separation between positive and negative controls increased, both SSMD and SSMD-based AUROC improved accordingly, indicating enhanced assay performance, whereas the empirical AUROC stayed 1 for those plates with non-overlapping values, masking the improvement in QC (Fig. 1). This illustrates the value of SSMD-derived AUROC as a smooth, interpretable, and more stable metric, especially in small-sample contexts where rank-based metrics may be less reliable.

The implications of this work are twofold. First, integrating AUROC with SSMD yields a more comprehensive evaluation of assay performance by jointly considering effect size and discriminative ability. Second, the probabilistic interpretation of QC thresholds enhances reproducibility and interpretability in HTS-based decision-making—key factors in early-stage drug discovery and other high-stakes biomedical applications.

While our examples are drawn from datasets with approximately normal and homoscedastic distributions—conditions often seen in well-controlled HTS screens, especially with certain data transformation such as log-transformation—we acknowledge that real-world data may deviate substantially from these assumptions. Skewed or heavy-tailed distributions, different effect size and limited sample sizes can all influence the accuracy and stability of AUROC estimation. Rank-based and empirical integration methods tend to be more robust to certain departures from normality whereas parametric approaches may yield biased estimates when the normality assumption is violated. A systematic simulation study varying distributional form, effect size, and noise level would provide a rigorous quantitative comparison of these methods. Although such an analysis is beyond the scope of the current work—and may be less accessible to a non-statistical audience—the simulated plate examples presented here (Panels D1, D2, H1, and H2) offer a controlled illustration of how the three AUROC estimation methods compare in a typical HTS scenario. Future work will extend these comparisons through more comprehensive simulations and applications across diverse assay formats to further assess method robustness.

In conclusion, the proposed SSMD-AUROC integration addresses the individual limitations of each metric and provides a more informative, interpretable, and reliable framework for QC in HTS. As experimental designs increasingly emphasize miniaturization and scale, such dual-metric strategies offer a rigorous foundation for effective assay evaluation. Future work may explore the integration of machine learning models to further enhance HTS quality assessment and support data-driven optimization in high-throughput experimentation.
