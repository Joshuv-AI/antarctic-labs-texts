Ganciclovir for severe neonatal varicella pneumonia when acyclovir is unavailable: a case report
Authors: Yuetong Shen, Shuangshuang Qu
Published: 2026

Introduction

Varicella, caused by the varicella-zoster virus (VZV), is a highly contagious disease. Although varicella during pregnancy is relatively uncommon, with an estimated incidence of 0.7 per 1,000 pregnancies [1], it poses significant risks to neonates when intrauterine infections occur. In adults, varicella typically manifests as mild illness. However, in unvaccinated neonates, particularly those born prematurely or exposed to the virus at the time of delivery, the infection can result in severe and potentially life-threatening complications, including pneumonia [2]. Varicella pneumonia, although uncommon in healthy children, is more prevalent in neonates because of their underdeveloped immune systems and compromised lung defenses. Before the advent of antiviral therapies, the mortality rate of varicella pneumonia was alarmingly high, reaching 30% [3]. Acyclovir is widely recognized as a first-line treatment for VZV infections [4]. Neonates presenting with signs of varicella, particularly those with neurological or ophthalmic complications, benefit from IV acyclovir therapy [5]. However, there is a significant gap in the literature regarding alternative treatments such as ganciclovir for cases where acyclovir is unavailable or contraindicated. In this report, we document the first known case of neonatal varicella pneumonia that was successfully treated with ganciclovir, highlighting its potential as an alternative therapeutic option in such critical scenarios.

Case presentation

A female neonate of Asian descent was admitted on the 9th day of life with symptoms of rash, fever, and respiratory distress. She was born after a well-monitored pregnancy that was unremarkable until the 39th week. At 39 weeks of gestation, four days before delivery, the mother, who was HIV-negative, had no history of varicella and had not received the varicella vaccine, developed pruritic exanthema characterized by lesions that progressed from the maculopapular to vesicular, pustular, and crusted stages, which were indicative of varicella. The delivery was uncomplicated, and the neonate’s condition was stable at birth. Following delivery, the infant was transferred to the neonatal observation unit for two hours, remained stable, and was then discharged home under the care of her grandmother, ensuring no contact with the mother. However, no specific varicella immunoglobulins were administered, as they were not stocked at the birthing hospital. She was fed formula milk and her temperature was monitored daily.

On the seventh day of life, she began to exhibit frothy sputum, an early sign of neonatal respiratory distress initially overlooked by her parents. The following day, a skin rash developed. Two days after the onset of the rash, she developed fever and experienced rapidly worsening respiratory distress, leading to medical consultation at the Neonatology Department of the General Hospital of the Northern Theater Command.

Upon arrival, the newborn had a temperature of 37.5 °C, a respiratory rate of 78 breaths/minute, and an oxygen saturation of 95% on room air. Lung auscultation revealed diffuse bilateral subcrepitant rales. Apart from tachycardia (164 bpm), the cardiac examination results were normal. The skin showed macules, papules, and vesicles on an erythematous background, and characteristic varicella lesions (Fig. 1a and b). The mucous membranes and integuments were slightly yellow, with jaundice. Other physical examination results were unremarkable. On laboratory examination, the white blood cell count was 5.8 × 109/L with 54.6% lymphocytes, and the hemoglobin level and platelet count were 178 g/L and 154 × 109/L, respectively, which were within the normal range for a neonate and did not indicate any specific abnormalities. Additionally, although the total bilirubin level was 268.5 µmol/L, the predominance of indirect bilirubin is a common finding in newborns due to the physiological jaundice that often occurs in the early days of life. C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, electrolytes, blood sugar, lactate dehydrogenase, myocardial enzymes, renal and liver function tests, routine urine and fecal tests, and cerebrospinal fluid analyses were normal. Chest radiography revealed diffuse interstitial syndrome in both lungs (Fig. 2). Given that bacterial infections are a common cause of neonatal respiratory distress, empiric antibiotic therapy with cefoperazone-sulbactam was initiated while awaiting confirmatory testing, despite a strong clinical suspicion of VZV infection. This was discontinued once VZV infection was confirmed. Acyclovir, the first-line treatment for VZV, was unavailable at our hospital, precluding its use. Considering that intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) provides passive immunity, we decided to proceed with the IVIG therapy.

Thirty-one hours after admission, the patient developed severe respiratory distress with unmaintained oxygen saturation at 5 L/min via a facemask. Chest radiography revealed bilateral patchy ground-glass opacities (Fig. 3). Mechanical ventilation was deemed necessary; however, all ventilators were in use, necessitating a transfer to another hospital for further treatment. Unfortunately, acyclovir was unavailable at the receiving hospital. Therefore, after a joint decision by the directors of both hospitals, ganciclovir was chosen as an alternative treatment.

Final diagnosis

The following day, metagenomic next-generation sequencing of the infant’s blood confirmed varicella-zoster virus (VZV) infection, detecting 109,491 sequences uniquely mapped to VZV (genus: Varicellovirus; type: dsDNA), representing 100% relative abundance among viral reads with a confidence level of 99%. No sequences corresponding to other herpesviruses or any significant bacterial, fungal, or parasitic pathogens were identified. Conventional serological testing for the TORCH panel (toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, herpes simplex virus) showed no IgM antibodies, and the Treponema pallidum particle agglutination (TPPA) assay was also negative; multiple bacterial and fungal cultures were likewise negative.

Treatment

The patient received ganciclovir (5 mg/kg every 12 h) for eight days and IVIG therapy (400 mg/kg once daily) for three days.

Outcome and follow-up

The infant was successfully weaned off mechanical ventilation on the 11th day post-transfer. Blood gas analysis demonstrated significant improvement, with PaCO₂ decreasing from 63.8 mmHg pre-intubation to 43.1 mmHg at extubation and a PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio of 346. Although chest X-ray at that time still showed bilateral haziness without complete resolution, this radiographic lag behind clinical improvement is a well-recognized phenomenon in resolving pneumonia. C-reactive protein peaked at 29.44 mg/L and normalized to 2.87 mg/L by day 13. The infant was discharged with stable breathing and crusted rash. Given the higher risk of hematologic complications and potential liver and kidney dysfunction associated with ganciclovir use in neonates, we closely monitored these parameters. No abnormalities were observed.

Although repeat chest X-ray and blood viral load testing at 2 weeks post-discharge were recommended, the family declined as the infant remained clinically well. Telephone follow-up at 2 and 4 weeks confirmed the infant was thriving with no respiratory symptoms or developmental concerns.

Discussion

Perinatal varicella, resulting from maternal viremia, can lead to fetal infection through transplacental transmission as well as via ascending infection during delivery or exposure to infectious respiratory droplets or lesions postnatally. Clinical observations indicate that varicella transmitted in utero typically manifests within the first 10–12 days of infant life [1]. In the case of our patient, the development of a skin rash in the mother four days before delivery, followed by a similar rash in the infant on the eighth day of life, strongly suggests that the infection was transmitted transplacentally from the mother to the fetus. When varicella is acquired in utero, the severity of infection in infants is largely determined by the timing of maternal infection relative to delivery. Fetuses exposed to VZV between 20 and 6 days before delivery may have a reduced risk of complications owing to the transfer of maternal antibodies. However, if the mother develops varicella rashes between 4 days before and 2 days after delivery, the risk of neonatal death from varicella can be as high as 20% because of insufficient time for the production and transmission of maternal protective antibodies to the fetus via the placenta [6].

Newborns without protective antibodies may benefit from varicella-zoster immunoglobulin (VZIG), which reduces the risk of severe disease if administered within 96 h [7] or up to 10 days [8] after exposure. When VZIG is unavailable, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) may be an alternative [9]. In this case, the infant did not receive VZIG or IVIG after birth and subsequently developed respiratory symptoms on the 7th day of life. IVIG was administered upon admission; however, it did not prevent the progression of severe pneumonia. IVIG primarily provides a broad spectrum of antibodies that can modulate the immune response and offer temporary protection against various infections. This case suggests that passive immunization alone may be insufficient to control severe neonatal infections, particularly when the disease has advanced and involves the respiratory system. Given that immunoglobulins do not specifically target viral mechanisms, and considering the complexity of the clinical situation, antiviral intervention is essential.

Acyclovir reduces the risk of developing complications associated with varicella [7]. One challenge in managing this newborn was the unavailability of injectable acyclovir in both hospitals, as neither institution routinely stocks this medication, typically reserving formularies for oral valacyclovir used in adult patients. Given the severity of the infant’s condition and the need for prompt antiviral therapy, ganciclovir was chosen as an alternative because its mechanism of action is similar to that of a nucleoside analog that inhibits viral DNA polymerase. Currently, nine human herpesviruses have been identified, classified into three subfamilies: α-Herpesvirinae, including Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 (HSV-1), HSV-2, and VZV; β-Herpesvirinae, which includes Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Human Herpesvirus 6 A (HHV-6 A), HHV-6B, HHV-7; and γ-Herpesvirinae, which includes Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) and HHV-8 [4]. In clinical practice, ganciclovir is typically preferred for the treatment of CMV infections, whereas acyclovir is the standard treatment for VZV infections. Both antiviral agents are nucleoside analogs that selectively inhibit the virus-encoded DNA polymerase. Their antiviral activity results from the inhibition of viral DNA synthesis through competitive incorporation of deoxyguanosine triphosphate into the DNA chain [4]. However, they differ in their activation pathways. Acyclovir requires phosphorylation by HSV- or VZV-encoded thymidine kinases (TK) to become active, whereas ganciclovir—though preferentially phosphorylated by viral protein kinases (UL97 for CMV, U69 for HHV-6) [10]—can also be activated in VZV-infected cells through alternative pathways. Koyano et al. demonstrated that VZV encodes both a thymidine kinase and a protein kinase (PK), and that ganciclovir can be phosphorylated by both enzymes, particularly the VZV PK which is analogous to the CMV UL97 kinase [11]. This dual activation pathway provides a biochemical rationale for ganciclovir’s activity against VZV. Direct in vitro evidence confirms the potency of ganciclovir against VZV; Andrei et al. reported a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of merely 1.67 µM in human embryonic lung (HEL) fibroblast cultures, establishing its intrinsic antiviral activity [12]. Notably, in TK-deficient VZV strains—a common mechanism of acyclovir resistance—ganciclovir retained partial activity, with the reduction in susceptibility being less pronounced than for acyclovir and other TK-dependent drugs, suggesting its activation is not entirely TK-dependent [13]. These findings provide a pharmacological rationale for considering ganciclovir as an alternative for VZV infections when first-line therapy is unavailable. This laboratory evidence is corroborated by clinical reports of successful ganciclovir use for VZV infections. Kishore et al. described PCR-confirmed VZV acute retinal necrosis treated with intravitreal ganciclovir, with lesion healing by day 9 and good visual recovery [14]. Cai et al. reported an AIDS patient with VZV meningoencephalitis who improved on ganciclovir-containing combination therapy [15], and Collet documented the first case of VZV meningoencephalitis successfully treated with systemic ganciclovir after acyclovir-induced renal failure [16]. These cumulative data support ganciclovir as a plausible alternative for VZV pneumonia when first-line therapy is unavailable. Although these findings are promising, further research is needed to establish the broader applicability of ganciclovir in VZV treatment.

Neutropenia and nephrotoxicity are common side effects of ganciclovir in neonates, necessitating careful consideration of its dosage and duration. The pharmacokinetic characteristics of ganciclovir in neonates are development-dependent, with elimination primarily dependent on glomerular filtration and tubular secretion that mature with postnatal age. In a recent Bayesian pharmacokinetic analysis of an extremely low birth weight preterm neonate, Kata et al. demonstrated a marked increase in clearance, which increased from 0.048 L/hr/kg at postnatal day 30 to 0.273 L/hr/kg at day 93, highlighting the dynamic nature of renal maturation in this population [17]. This highlights the importance of considering both body weight and postnatal age when individualizing dosing regimens. Regarding the evidence-based basis for recommended doses, the regimen of 5 mg/kg every 12 h—which we used in our patient—has been validated in neonatal populations. Acosta et al. conducted a prospective pharmacokinetic study in premature infants (mean gestational age 26.7 weeks) receiving ganciclovir for congenital CMV infection and found that this dose achieved a geometric mean 12-hour area-under-the-curve (AUC₁₂) of 52.7 mg·h/L, supporting its use as an appropriate starting point [18].Similar pharmacokinetic profiles have been observed at doses of 4 and 6 mg/kg, although individualized dosing based on serum creatinine clearance and body weight is recommended [19]. Sunada et al. reported that, in an extremely premature infant with perinatal CMV infection, an initial dose of 5 mg/kg every 12 h successfully reduced the viral load, whereas extending the dosing interval to 24 h worsened the viremia [20]. In our case, we administered ganciclovir at 5 mg/kg every 12 h, closely monitoring complete blood counts, renal function, and liver enzymes throughout treatment; no abnormalities were observed. Treatment was discontinued eight days after respiratory symptoms resolved, with no adverse effects or recurrence. Regarding renal safety, Mde et al. [21] showed that acyclovir increases renal resistance and decreases glomerular blood flow, leading to acute renal impairment, whereas ganciclovir shows no similar toxicity and improves glomerular filtration. These findings, which are also supported by those of Collet et al. [16], suggest a potentially safer renal profile for ganciclovir than for acyclovir. Further randomized controlled trials are required to establish optimal ganciclovir treatment protocols to ensure both efficacy and safety.

It is also important to consider the appropriate timing for initiating antiviral therapy. The Norwegian Pediatric Association’s Guide to General Pediatrics recommends the administration of antiviral prophylaxis after exposure in immunocompromised patients if VZIG has not been provided [7]. Blumenthal suggested administering intravenous acyclovir at a dose of 30 mg/kg/day, divided into three doses, starting on the seventh day after the onset of maternal rash and continuing for 10 days [6]. Additionally, Albrecht advised that if clinical signs of varicella are present, antiviral treatment should be initiated promptly, ideally within 24 h of rash onset, to minimize the risk of severe disease [7]. In our case, the diagnosis of congenital varicella was confirmed by the presence of maternal varicella during pregnancy, along with congenital skin lesions following a dermatomal distribution. Our initial plan was to wait for at least two days for a confirmed VZV DNA result before initiating antiviral therapy, a decision that could have delayed treatment.

Acyclovir was unavailable in these two hospitals because they prioritized stocking oral valacyclovir, which offers better bioavailability for the treatment of adult varicella. Given the extremely low incidence of perinatal varicella in neonates, intravenous acyclovir was not administered despite being the first-line treatment. However, data regarding valacyclovir use in children are limited. Kimberlin et al. [22] showed that a 20 mg/kg dose of valacyclovir oral suspension was effective and well tolerated in children aged 3 months to 11 years. However, no dosing recommendations are available for infants under 3 months of age because of reduced drug clearance in this age group. Therefore, both hospitals opted to administer IV ganciclovir as an alternative treatment. The successful management of severe VZV pneumonia with ganciclovir, particularly in the absence of acyclovir, highlights the potential of this drug as a viable alternative in resource-limited settings where standard treatments are not accessible.

This case highlights the clinical significance of ganciclovir as a potential life-saving alternative for critically ill neonates when standard therapy is unavailable, although its use was driven by necessity rather than established evidence of its superior efficacy. While this positive outcome suggests that ganciclovir may have broader applications, the inherent limitations of a single case report—which provides only low-level evidence—underscore the need for further research. Given the lack of direct comparative studies with acyclovir and limited data on its safety and efficacy in neonates, multicenter retrospective cohort studies are warranted to build a higher-level evidence base. Prospective studies exploring the pharmacokinetics, long-term effects, and comparative efficacy of ganciclovir and acyclovir are essential to guide their safe and effective use in neonatal populations.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the potential efficacy of ganciclovir as an alternative treatment for severe VZV pneumonia in neonates for whom acyclovir is unavailable. Although the successful outcome in this case is encouraging, the off-label use of ganciclovir should be approached with caution, and further research is needed to establish its safety and efficacy. This case underscores the importance of considering alternative antiviral therapies in situations where standard treatments are inaccessible and highlights the need for rigorous clinical studies to guide treatment decisions in neonatal VZV infections.
