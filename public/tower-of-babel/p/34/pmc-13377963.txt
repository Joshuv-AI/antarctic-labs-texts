The Urinary Iodine Level Based on Different Duration of Low-Iodine Diet for Radioactive Iodine Therapy

Clinical Relevance

A 1-week low-iodine diet may be sufficient before radioactive iodine ablation, potentially reducing delays and patient burden. Evidence on low-iodine diet benefit for ablation remains conflicting, underscoring the need for prospective randomized trials to define optimal duration and urinary iodine targets.

Introduction

Radioactive iodine (RAI) ablation with I-131 is often used as an adjuvant treatment in patients with differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC). Following a total or near-total thyroidectomy, RAI ablation helps eliminate residual normal thyroid tissue including microscopic cancer tissue.1, 2, 3 The combination of RAI ablation therapy and thyroid hormone suppression has been shown to prolong disease-free survival, lower long-term cancer recurrence rates, and improve overall mortality in patients with moderate- to high-risk DTC.2,4 Optimal RAI uptake is achieved by elevating thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels and adhering to a low-iodine diet (LID) prior to ablation.

One known method to enhance the uptake of RAI in thyroid remanent and metastatic lesions is by implementing an LID prior to ablation. LID is commonly recommended for 1 to 2 weeks prior to RAI therapy to reduce iodine stores and promote uptake of RAI by residual thyroid tissue or DTC cells.5 Excess iodine exposure may compete with RAI for transport through the sodium-iodide symporter, potentially reducing thyroidal uptake and diminishing the effectiveness of RAI therapy.5,6 Clinical studies demonstrate that iodine restriction before treatment lowers urinary iodine levels and may improve RAI uptake, whereas higher iodine levels at the time of therapy have been associated with less favorable treatment responses.5,7 For example, Goslings demonstrated that in patients with metastatic follicular thyroid cancer, a 4-day LID increased RAI tumor uptake by approximately 1.8-fold and extended the effective half-life of RAI by about 1.2-fold.8 Similarly, a 2003 study reported greater 24-hour neck uptake of RAI in patients who followed a 4-day LID.9 LID therefore focuses on limiting iodine-rich foods such as iodized salt, seafood and seaweed, dairy products, egg yolks, soy products, and processed foods that may contain iodine-based additives.6

Studies have shown that LID in preparation for RAI therapy has both a physical and psychological impact, often contributing to anxiety, depression, and insecurity.10,11 The duration and stringency of LID often differ depending on different guidelines. The American Thyroid Association recommends limiting dietary iodine intake to less than 50 μg/d for 1 to 2 weeks.6,9,12 The British Thyroid Association recommends a LID for 1 to 2 weeks,13 and the European Thyroid Cancer Taskforce recommends a LID for 3 weeks.14 Notably, the stringency of the LID is not specified by these 2 latter groups.

The optimal period of LID during preparation for RAI therapy is not well established. At the University of Washington, we conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent RAI for thyroid cancer and compared urine iodine concentration in patients who had either 1 or 2 weeks of LID prior to RAI.

Materials and Methods

We retrospectively reviewed all patients in a single academic center with a diagnosis of DTCs who underwent total thyroidectomy and RAI during the period 2022-2024. The study did not require approval by the institutional review board given retrospective study design and no patient identifiers were included. Patients were excluded if they were younger than age 18 years or if they did not have urine iodine level. All patients followed concomitant recombinant TSH-stimulated RAI therapy and a LID of less than 50 μg/d for 1 to 2 weeks. Patients were instructed to avoid major iodine sources, such as iodized salt, seafood, dairy, processed foods, commercial breads, and foods containing red dye #3. Patients were grouped retrospectively based on 1 versus 2-week iodine diet restriction prior to RAI therapy.

Results

There were 72 patients who met the criteria (Table). The median age of this cohort was 46 years (range: 19-82) and 63.9% were female. Discussion

In this retrospective review of 72 patients undergoing treatment with RAI for thyroid cancer at the University of Washington, urinary iodine measurements were similar after completing 1-week versus 2-week LID restriction. The importance of restricting iodine intake to increase uptake of RAI is known, but the minimum duration is not. A randomized controlled trial in 2010 studied whether 3 weeks is superior to 2 weeks for a LID as a means to increase thyroid uptake of RAI and reduce urinary iodine.15 Of their 46 patients, they found that both diets for a 2- and 3-week LID caused significant decrease in urinary iodine, without a significant difference in urinary iodine excretion between the 2 groups. Therefore, the authors concluded that 2 weeks was most likely sufficient to augment thyroid uptake of RAI.15 Another study conducted in 2014 evaluated 202 patients with papillary thyroid cancer who underwent total thyroidectomy and were preparing for RAI. All participants followed a 2-week LID prior to treatment, and morning spot urine samples were collected from each patient at both the first and second week of the diet. They found that median urinary iodine excretion values were not statistically significant between 1 and 2 weeks of LID; additionally, the study reported no difference in ablation success rates between urinary iodine excretion values. Therefore, the authors suggested that a 1-week LID was sufficient to achieve the target urinary iodine levels for RAI preparation.16

The impact of a LID on ablation success remains uncertain. A 2001 study reported no significant difference in ablation rates between patients maintained on a 2-week LID (68.2%) and those on a regular diet (62.0%).17 Similarly, a 2018 investigation of intermediate-risk DTC patients following the Japanese protocol of a 2-week LID found that urinary iodine excretion was not predictive of ablation outcomes, as assessed by their whole-body scan at 6 to 8 months post-RAI.18

In contrast, a 2003 study of 120 DTC patients demonstrated superior RAI ablation rate outcomes with a 4-day LID, with 65% of patients achieving successful ablation at 6 months compared with 48% of historical controls.9 In addition, a 2013 retrospective review of 295 papillary thyroid cancer patients who underwent total thyroidectomy and a 1 to 2 week LID showed that ablation rates were significantly lower in individuals with urinary iodine/creatinine ratios >250 μg/gCr at the time of RAI administration compared with those <250 μg/gCr. However, no differences were observed among subgroups with urinary iodine/creatinine ratios <50, 50 to 100, and 100 to 250 μg/gCr.19 Overall, evidence regarding the influence of LID on RAI ablation efficacy remains inconsistent.

The limitations of our study include its retrospective study design and potential issues with patient adherence. Specifically, adherence to dietary iodine intake and duration of dietary restriction may affect the measured urinary iodine outcome, introducing a confounding factor. However, potential patient nonadherence would likely mimic future clinical scenarios and similarly impact larger cohort studies; therefore, we expect the impact of this confounder to be minimal and the outcomes to remain applicable to other comparable studies.

Although the urine iodine level was lower in patients who followed a 2-week LID restriction, there was no statistically significant difference between those who completed a 1 versus 2-week low-iodine restriction prior to RAI therapy. This suggests that a 1-week LID may be adequate for RAI treatment. This would decrease time to treatment for patients and decrease the burden of LID. A shorter 1-week LID may improve patient adherence and quality of life by reducing the duration of restrictive dietary modifications, which often exclude common foods such as dairy, seafood, and iodized salt and can be difficult for patients to maintain. A prospective analysis with a larger cohort is needed to validate these findings further, as well as evaluating the RAI ablation success rates between the 1 versus 2-week LID restriction groups.

Conclusion

The urine iodine level was lower in patients who followed a 2-week LID restriction. However, there was no statistically significant difference between those who completed a 1 versus 2-week low-iodine restriction prior to RAI therapy, suggesting a 1-week LID may be adequate for RAI treatment. This would decrease time to treatment for patients and decrease the burden of LID. Overall, there is conflicting evidence whether LID benefits RAI ablation rates, and given most of the current literature are retrospective studies, future large-scale prospective randomized control trials are needed to identify the optimal duration of LID and define the target urinary iodine level for successful RAI ablation.
