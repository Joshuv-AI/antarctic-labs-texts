Parallel Sequencing of the Two Arms on a Bispecific Antibody by Electron Capture Dissociation on a timsOmni Platform Reveals Several By-products in the Controlled Fab-arm Exchange Process
Authors: Iuliia Stroganova, Simon Ollivier, Laura Radić, Athanasios Smyrnakis, Mariangela Kosmopoulou, Janke Schinkel, Kwinten Sliepen, Jean-François Greisch, Albert J.R. Heck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) can simultaneously target two distinct antigens, offering enhanced therapeutic efficacy compared to conventional antibodies. However, their complex manufacturing process, involving pairing of four distinct polypeptide chains (two heavy and two light chains), enhances the analytical challenges in quality control. Here, we applied top-down mass spectrometry using electron capture dissociation (ECD) on a novel timsOmni platform to characterize the bsAb LaC49 (AR4A/AR3C) and its monospecific precursors, IgG1-AR4A and IgG1-AR3C. This bsAb was developed to target different epitopes of the E1E2 envelope glycoprotein on the surface of hepatitis C virus (HCV). The bsAb was prepared via controlled Fab-arm exchange. Native mass spectrometry revealed that the sample contained the intended bsAb but also products that displayed substantial structural heterogeneity, including residual monospecific IgG1-AR4A and multiple other mass variants. ECD MS/MS analysis of the F­(ab’)2 of these products provided comprehensive sequence coverage of the CDR3 regions from all four polypeptide chains, generating clear sequence ladders potentially suitable for de novo sequencing. Important impurities could be identified that could not be characterized by MS1 (intact mass) analysis alone, including multiple (alternative) signal peptide cleavages in the AR4A antibody and partial reduction of a functionally important disulfide bridge in the CDR3 region of the AR3C heavy chain, likely induced during bsAb preparation. Our findings demonstrate that top-down MS/MS analysis is key for the molecular-level characterization of bsAb modifications, as standalone intact mass analysis proved insufficient to distinguish between closely related molecular variants (i.e., separated by only 0.04 Da). This study also emphasizes the need for comprehensive batch-to-batch quality control during bsAb production, as structural modifications can impact binding specificity, therapeutic efficacy, and safety. The timsOmni platform with ECD fragmentation provides a powerful novel analytical tool for ensuring consistent product quality in bispecific antibody manufacturing and for antibody sequencing applications.

Introduction

Bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) are engineered therapeutic proteins that can simultaneously bind to two distinct antigens or different epitopes on the same target molecule. , Unlike conventional monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that typically possess identical antigen-binding domains, bsAbs unite two functionally different fragment antigen-binding (Fab) arms, each designed to recognize a distinct molecular target. This dual-targeting capability enables diverse therapeutic mechanisms including T-cell redirection to tumor sites, simultaneous blocking of multiple signaling pathways, and increased binding specificity by interacting with two different cell-surface antigens instead of one. , The clinical success of this approach is reflected in the rapid increase in regulatory approvals, with at least 20 approved bispecific antibodies by 2025, particularly accelerating since 2020.

BsAb manufacturing presents challenges in achieving correct heavy and light chain pairing when co-expressing four distinct polypeptides from two different mAbs, while simultaneously promoting heavy chain heterodimerization in the fragment crystallizable (Fc) regions. Several alternative engineering strategies address these issues, including knobs-into-holes (KiH) technology, CrossMab approaches, and controlled Fab-arm exchange (cFAE), commercialized as the DuoBody platform. , Given these manufacturing challenges and their impact on product quality, comprehensive analytical characterization of the intended product and potential by-products is essential for ensuring therapeutic efficacy, safety, and regulatory compliance.

There are many analytical techniques that can be employed to characterize mAbs/bsAbs. For example, circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy provides global information on secondary structure and has been extensively applied for monitoring antibody aggregation, thermal stability assessment, and conformational changes during stress conditions. , Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) remains the gold standard for monitoring aggregation and stability of antibodies, achieving more than 99% purity assessment as required for therapeutic applications. , Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) provides a rapid assessment of protein integrity and molecular weight, as well as a control of protein aggregation and purity. While these methods provide valuable insights into overall antibody stability and conformation by accessible analytical workflows, they have limited capacity to detect minor structural variations or distinguish between closely related molecular species that may differ in other important quality attributes.

To overcome these limitations and achieve molecular-level resolution, mass spectrometry represents a powerful analytical tool that provides detailed structural information about individual molecules, enabling identification of impurities and modifications. , While intact mass analysis (MS1) can distinguish between modifications with different molecular weights and provide relative quantification of protein variants, it cannot distinguish between isobaric modifications or provide site-specific localization information. Such aspects require tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) for comprehensive characterization. Bottom-up proteomics, while more technologically mature and more widely used, requires digesting proteins into small peptides, which typically results in undesired loss of important connectivity and proteoform information. Therefore, top-down proteomics approaches offer significant advantages for antibody characterization, requiring less sample preparation and preserving interchain connectivity. , MS/MS fragmentation of intact antibodies or their large domains (Fab or F­(ab’)2 arms) provides valuable structural information by generating sequence-specific fragment ions that enable sequence identification and putative modification localization. In top-down proteomics, especially electron-based fragmentation techniques (ExD), including electron capture dissociation (ECD), electron transfer dissociation (ETD), and activated ion ETD (AI-ETD), have proven their value for therapeutic antibody characterization, − alongside the more classical use of collision induced dissociation (CID). ExD methods for top-down protein characterization can conserve labile post-translational modifications such as glycosylation, and produce abundant complementary c- and z-type fragment ions that provide extensive backbone cleavage for proteins up to ∼30 kDa. , In the context of antibody sequencing, the three hypervariable complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) located in the Fab region define the target binding specificity and affinity of antibody–antigen interactions, making them the most important sequences for antibody characterization and in de novo sequencing applications. These CDRs are found within the variable domains of the heavy (VH) and light (VL) chains (Figure A). However, CDR1 and CDR2 are constrained within disulfide bonds and are generally less accessible under non-reducing conditions. The CDR3 regions, in contrast, can be sequenced due to their location outside disulfide bonds. , Therefore, here we applied ECD fragmentation to characterize the CDR3 regions of a bsAb.

Recently, we described a prototype timsOmni instrument and evaluated the use of different ExD methods, to sequence mAbs. The here described Omnitrap technology enables ion accumulation for enhanced sensitivity, ExD with tunable electron energy, resonant CID with adjustable gas composition, and MSn fragmentation. We showed that in top-down sequencing of mAbs, ionizing under native conditions was beneficial, as the resulting fragment ions became more distinctively spread out, away from the precursor ions in the m/z domain. Here, we used this prototype timsOmni instrument for the comprehensive structural characterization of a bispecific antibody (bsAb LaC49). The bsAb LaC49 was produced by controlled Fab-arm exchange (cFAE) of two mAbs, IgG1-AR4A and IgG1-AR3C, which target two distinct epitopes on the hepatitis C virus (HCV) E1E2 envelope glycoprotein (Figure A). The cFAE method allows for the formation of bsAbs following incubation of two monospecific precursors, each containing a point mutation in their CH3 region (F405L or K409R), with a reducing agent (Figure A). We performed native top-down proteomics to characterize all (by)­products formed following cFAE of the monospecific precursors. IdeS digestion was used to generate F­(ab’)2 segments of the bsAb, which were the primary focus of our mass spectrometric analysis, as these contain information about chain pairing and also harbor all CDRs. The application of ECD preserved the antibody sequence integrity and allowed us to explore disulfide connectivity, map posttranslational modifications, and investigate potential batch-to-batch variations. The ECD-induced top-down proteomics data also provided a/c-type ion sequence ladders covering the CDR3 regions of all four different polypeptide chains in parallel, demonstrating the utility of this approach for de novo sequencing of even bispecific antibodies.

Methods

Intact Antibody Analysis

The bsAb LaC49 (originally labeled AR4A/AR3C IgG bsAb) and its mAb precursors IgG1-AR4A and IgG1-AR3C were produced as described previously , and stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at −20 °C prior to the analysis. The bispecific antibody and its mAb precursors were all derived from a single batch. Briefly, the bsAb was assembled from the two mAb precursors via controlled Fab-arm exchange (cFAE), in which the precursor antibodies were mixed at a 1:1 molar ratio and subjected to reducing conditions (2-mercaptoethylamine, 2-MEA) to drive efficient heterodimer formation through point CH3 domain mutations, followed by reoxidation.

All antibodies were then deglycosylated using PNGase F before mass analysis by incubating 25 μg of antibodies with 2.5 μL of PNGase F overnight at 37 °C (500 rpm) at a 10:1 antibody to enzyme ratio (mass:units). Antibodies were purified by buffer exchange into 150 mM ammonium acetate, pH 7.4 using Vivaspin 500 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off filters (8 cycles of 10 min, 4 °C, 10 000 rpm) to prepare them for native mass spectrometry analysis.

F­(ab’)2 Preparation

The LaC49 bsAb prepared by controlled Fab-arm exchange was digested by incubating 20 μg of the bsAb with an IgG-specific cysteine protease IdeS that digests antibodies below the hinge region to produce F­(ab’)2 segments (see Figure A). A 10:1 antibody to enzyme ratio (mass:mass) was used. IdeS digestion was performed overnight at 37 °C in PBS with 750 rpm agitation. F­(ab’)2 segments were purified by buffer exchange into native conditions (150 mM ammonium acetate, pH 7.5) using Amicon membrane with 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off (7 cycles of 10 min, 4 °C, 10 000 rpm).

Fab Preparation

Fab segments of AR3C and AR4A monospecific antibodies were prepared by incubating 100 μg of each antibody with the IgG1-specific protease (IgdE) that cleaves above the hinge, used at a 10:1 protein to enzyme ratio (mass:mass) in PBS overnight at 37 °C (750 rpm). AR4A Fab segments were purified by removing Fc segments through incubation with 25 μL of FcXL slurry for 1 h at room temperature (21 °C, 750 rpm agitation), followed by IgdE removal using 50 μL of Ni-NTA slurry for 30 min at room temperature (21 °C, 750 rpm agitation). AR4A Fab segments were subsequently reduced by incubating in 30 mM tris­(2-carboxyethyl)­phosphine (TCEP) in 0.1% formic acid (FA) for 1.5 h at 60 °C without agitation. TCEP was removed by buffer exchange into denaturing conditions (0.1% FA in water) using Vivaspin 500 5 kDa molecular weight cut-off filters (8 cycles for 30 min, 4 °C, 10 000 rpm). AR3C Fab was buffer exchanged directly after IgdE incubation into native conditions (150 mM ammonium acetate, pH 7.5) using Vivaspin 500 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off filters (8 cycles of 10 min, 4 °C, 10 000 rpm).

SDS-PAGE Analysis

For SDS-PAGE analyses 4 μg of antibody was mixed with XT sample buffer (Bio-Rad) with 50 mM DTT and PBS (total volume 20 μL), incubated at 95 °C on a thermo block for 10 min and loaded on a 12% Bis-Tris gel (Criterion XT). Gels were run in XT MOPS Running Buffer (Bio-Rad) for 15 min at 80 V, 30 min at 160 V, and finally 20 min at 180 V. Gel staining was performed using Precision Plus Protein Dual Color Standards (Bio-Rad) overnight, followed by destaining with Milli-Q water.

Mass Spectrometry

MS1 Measurements

Deglycosylated native antibodies were analyzed using an ultrahigh mass range (UHMR) Q-Exactive Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The instrument was calibrated using cesium iodide (CsI) cluster ions prior to measurements. Antibodies were measured at ∼4 μM, and ∼2 μL of each sample was loaded into in-house pulled gold-coated borosilicate emitters. In-source trapping was disabled, source fragmentation was set to 20 eV, HCD was set at 75 eV. Mass spectra were acquired at 6250 resolution at m/z 400.

Native Top-Down Measurements

Measurements of F­(ab’)2 of bsAb LaC49 and (reduced) Fab segments of AR4A and AR3C were performed on a prototype timsOmni (Bruker) which is a modified version of a timsTOF Ultra (Bruker) with an incorporated Omnitrap linear ion trap. The instrument was calibrated using CsI cluster ions prior to measurements. The main experimental parameters used on the timsOmni for MS/MS F­(ab’)2 and Fab measurements are provided in Table S1. Briefly, bsAb LaC49 F­(ab’)2 molecules, AR4A reduced Fab molecules, and AR3C native Fab molecules were measured at a concentration of 2.5, 20, and 30 μM, respectively. Protein concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop. Approximately 2 μL of each sample was loaded into in-house pulled gold-coated borosilicate emitters. Samples were electrosprayed using nanoelectrospray (nanoESI) NEOS-Source (Bruker). For native conditions, in-source CID was set at 90 eV, while 30–50 eV was used for reduced denaturing conditions. For MS/MS analysis, ions of interest were isolated in the timsTOF quadrupole (Q0) with an isolation width of 2–10 Th (see Table S2 for m/z of precursors and isolation width) and transferred to segment Q5 of the Omnitrap, where they were accumulated for 2 s. ECD fragmentation was then performed in Q5 by irradiating ions for 50 ms under native conditions and 15 ms under denaturing conditions. ECD was performed at an electron energy of ∼0 eV. Mass spectra were acquired over ∼1 h in the 300–7000 m/z mass range. The Omnitrap was operated at a rectangular waveform frequency of 800 kHz.

Data Analysis

The MS1 spectra of deglycosylated antibodies were averaged for a few minutes and exported as .raw data files for mass deconvolution using UniDec software version 8.0.1. ECD MS/MS spectra were averaged for approximately 1 h using Bruker Compass DataAnalysis version 6.1 software and exported as .xy data files for further processing in OmniScape 2025b version 1.10.11 with the Confirmation workflow. Fragment ion search was performed using the known sequences of the light and heavy chains (see Table S4 for these sequences). Fragment ions were identified by searching for a, c, and z-type fragment ions within 5 ppm mass tolerance, with a minimum intensity within mass tolerance of 60%, a correlation threshold of 20%, a score threshold of 3.0 au, and peak acceptance stringency set to “Medium”. For isotopically resolved species in the MS1 spectra, such as LC and Fab’ fragments, we determined masses by matching experimental and theoretical isotopic patterns, selecting the isotope peak pairing that yielded the closest agreement. For isotopically unresolved ions, notably F­(ab’)2, we compared deconvoluted experimental masses with the theoretical average masses. For MS/MS sequencing data analyzed using OmniScape software, fragment ion assignments incorporated the complete isotopic distribution of each fragment.

Results

Analysis of the Anticipated bsAb

Previously, we have used controlled Fab-arm exchange (cFAE) to produce the LaC49 bsAb. , Conventional biochemical analyses confirmed that LaC49 was bispecific, of high purity, bound to E1E2, and retained HCV neutralizing activity. Here, we wanted to analyze this bsAb in more detail using native top-down analysis by ECD on the timsOmni instrument to characterize the effect of the cFAE production method on the quality of antibody, examine sequence integrity and the CDR3 region coverage, and detect modifications potentially introduced during production or storage. To characterize the CDR3 regions of the bsAb LaC49 F­(ab’)2 segments, we performed exclusively electron capture dissociation (ECD) mass spectrometry analysis. The native top-down ECD MS/MS spectra revealed the presence of charge-reduced species alongside lower mass ECD fragment ions (Figure B). Charge state deconvolution of the MS/MS spectra provided easy-to-read ion sequence ladders (Figure A) that could be nearly fully explained, unambiguously confirming CDR3 assignments for the four distinct chains, i.e., the two heavy and two light chains. Detailed fragment assignments and sequence maps of the four distinct polypeptide chains are presented in Figure B–E, which demonstrate extensive coverage of all the CDR3 variable regions by consecutive a- and c-type fragment ion series. The details of the assigned modifications will be discussed below in more detail.

Analysis of Observed By-products

To assess the number of potentially different CDR3 assignments that need to be considered, of which one example is displayed in Figure , we set out to characterize molecular heterogeneity of the produced antibody samples from one specific batch in more detail. As a first step, the MS1 spectra of all three individual intact antibody samples (AR3C, AR4A, and LaC49) were acquired (see Figure A), following PNGase F treatment to reduce proteoform complexity arising from N-glycosylation.

Native MS1 mass spectra of AR3C, AR4A, and the bsAb, after PNGase F-treatment. (A) Overlayed MS1 spectra of AR3C (purple), AR4A (orange), and LaC49 bsAb (black) with charge states annotated for the bsAb. For AR3C just one molecular variant is observed (purple), for AR4A three major molecular variants can be observed, while for the bsAb at least 5 abundant variants are observed (B) Charge-deconvoluted native mass spectrum of the produced LaC49 bsAb revealing the approximate masses of the detected antibody variants. Peaks at ∼148 kDa correspond to the intended bsAb, while those at approximately 150–151 kDa correspond to residual monospecific AR4A antibodies, revealing that controlled Fab-arm exchange had likely been performed with excess of AR4A antibody.

AR3C displayed a single dominant species in different charge states, while AR4A had multiple peaks corresponding to various molecular variants. Most notably, the LaC49 bsAb sample contained two distinct populations with multiple variants each: a few lower-mass variants (Mw ∼ 148 kDa) corresponding to the intended bsAb formed through controlled Fab-arm exchange, and several higher-mass variants (Mw ∼ 151 kDa). The latter signals clearly resemble those we observed for the monospecific AR4A. This second population hints at the presence of unreacted and/or excess AR4A present during the bsAb production process. Although the cFAE reaction was planned with equimolar amounts of AR4A (K409R) and AR3C (F405L), the substantial presence of residual AR4A (∼41%) and minimal AR3C (∼1%) compared to the desired bsAb product (∼58%) suggests an initial excess of AR4A. The kinetic asymmetry of the cFAE process, where K409R-containing antibodies dissociate more slowly into half-molecules than F405L-containing antibodies, may contribute to incomplete exchange of the AR4A arm.

The charge-deconvoluted mass spectrum of all the products observed in the production of the bsAb LaC49 is depicted in Figure B. Quantitative analysis of peak intensities revealed that the bsAb population was approximately 1.4-fold more abundant than the residual AR4A. Radić et al. previously investigated the binding of a “dead arm” bsAb containing one HCV-specific AR4A Fab and one irrelevant Fab to E1E2 by mass photometry. As expected, the majority of bsAb molecules bound monovalently with their one functional Fab arm. However, a smaller proportion (∼12%) of observed E1E2:Ab complexes corresponded to bivalent binding of the bsAb. Presumably, this bivalent binding results from contamination with the original parental antibody, which is in line with our mass spectrometry findings of residual AR4A in the LaC49 bsAb preparation (Figure B). Although the cFAE methodology results in typically >95% conversion efficiency from parental homodimers to bispecific products, the excess amount of monospecific AR4A detected here, could be caused by variations in starting material ratios or reaction conditions, leading to batch-to-batch variations in the production of bsAb. This highlights the value of native MS in the batch control of cFAE-produced bsAbs.

To further confirm these findings, we performed SDS-PAGE analysis under reducing conditions (see Figure S1), which revealed indeed an approximately 2-fold higher light chain abundance from AR4A compared to AR3C in the bsAb sample used here. This indicates that AR4A is present in excess relative to AR3C during bsAb production. However, the SDS-PAGE data could not resolve the additional mass heterogeneity observed in the intact native mass spectra, specifically the presence of two major forms in bsAb and three major forms in AR4A (see Figure B). To further characterize these structural variants, as a second step, we performed native top-down ECD MS/MS analysis on the by hinge-directed proteolysis generated Fab molecules of AR3C and AR4A on the timsOmni.

Analysis of Production Variants and Modifications of bsAb

Modifications of the AR4A Antibody

Upon closer examination of the peaks depicted in Figure B, we observed that the mass difference between the different variants of bsAb or AR4A was approximately ∼360 Da. Analysis of the mass spectrum of bsAb after IdeS digestion (Figure S2) reveals that the mass difference between the F­(ab’)2 segments of bsAb and AR4A is more precisely 365 Da (see Table S3). In this spectrum, we also observed ion signals of the free light chains, specifically two variants of the AR4A light chain that were separated in mass even more precisely by 365.17 Da. This consistent mass shift observed in both the intact antibodies, the F­(ab’)2 segments, and the AR4A light chains suggests a mass variant on the light chain of AR4A, responsible for generating multiple variants of both AR4A and the LaC49 bsAb. Based on the observed mass shift, we initially hypothesized that this modification could correspond to a Hex(1)­HexNAc(1) O-glycan (theoretical monoisotopic mass: 365.13 Da). Although rare, O-glycosylation on Fab segments has been previously reported. To validate this hypothesis and determine the precise location of the modification on the light chain, reduced denatured Fab variants of AR4A was prepared with the light chain being mass selected for top-down ECD mass analysis on the timsOmni.

From the resulting MS/MS spectra the +365.17 Da modification could be localized to the N-terminus of the light chain, as this placement was required to explain the a- and c-type fragment ions observed in the F­(ab’)2 fragmentation pattern of bsAb (Figure D). Hex(1)­HexNAc(1) O-glycan modification was placed at the N-terminus (Figure A), but this yielded only ∼54% sequence coverage with no N-terminal fragments explained. Next, we explored whether the 365 Da mass difference could be the result from an unexpected alternative signal peptide cleavage site. Signal peptide miscleavage is sometimes reported to occur during antibody production in mammalian cell lines, and can result in truncation and extension of the N-terminus, which can be readily detected by mass spectrometry. , As shown in Figure B, signal peptide cleavage co-occurred at two sites, generating two light chain variants: LC-I, a truncated form missing the N-terminal glutamic acid (E), and LC-II, containing three additional amino acids (VHE; +365.17 Da). The LC-I cleavage could be predicted by a web version of SignalP 6.0, a machine learning-based tool for predicting signal peptide cleavage sites, and intact masses confirmed both LC-I and LC-II variants (Table S3). The ECD fragmentation data strongly supported the LC-II structure. Taking this modification into account, the sequence coverage improved substantially (from ∼54 to ∼71%) with multiple N-terminal fragment ions now being correctly identified (Figure B). Although such alternative signal peptide cleavages can occur, the nearly equal propensity (Figure B) of the two cleavages observed here is quite striking.

The mass difference between the two possible modification variants, namely an O-glycan or an alternatively cleaved signal peptide variant is only −0.04 Da. MS/MS measurements on the reduced light chain offered a unique opportunity to distinguish between these two modifications. They allowed us to access small N-terminal a- and c-type fragment ions (∼1000 Da) which translated in a high ppm error of ∼40 ppm for the lighter of the two modifications (the O-glycan). The light chain MS/MS data thereby unambiguously rules out the O-glycosylation as this would require extending the tolerance beyond 40 ppm to enable assignments for the reduced species.

For the AR4A heavy chain, one cleavage variant was observed with four missing N-terminal amino acids (EVQL) (Figure S3), similar to LC-I of AR4A which was missing only first E. Interestingly, while SignalP 6.0 predicted that the cleavage would result in a VHEVQL N-terminal sequence of the heavy chain, our data showed cleavage after EVQL, indicating a discrepancy between the predicted and observed cleavage site. Strikingly, while AR4A exhibited an alternative cleavage site, AR3C did not show such heterogeneity. This can possibly be explained by the different signal peptides used: AR4A antibodies were produced with the signal peptide sequence MGWSCIILFLVATATGVH, while AR3C was produced with the same signal peptide but with an additional C-terminal S (MGWSCIILFLVATATGVHS). According to the von Heijne rule, position −1 (last amino acid of the signal peptide, immediately before the cleavage site) and −3 (third amino acid before cleavage site) should be a small, uncharged amino acid for proper signal peptide cleavage. AR3C follows this rule with serine (S) at position −1, while AR4A has histidine (H), which is both charged and relatively large, possibly explaining the observed alternative cleavages for AR4A. Computational approaches using high-throughput mutagenesis and screening of signal peptide variants have been developed to further investigate how mutations in one or two amino acids at the C-terminus of signal peptide affect predicted cleavage site. They have confirmed the importance of small, uncharged amino acids (AGS) at the −1 position of signal peptides for proper signal peptide cleavage, consistent with the von Heijne rule. These alternative cleavage sites can contribute significantly to antibody molecular heterogeneity and underscore the importance of detailed characterization during bsAb production, ideally by protein-centric mass spectrometry.

Modifications of the AR3C Antibody

An intriguing modification was observed within the CDR3 region of the heavy chain of AR3C, albeit only in the bsAb sample. The heavy chain CDR3 region of AR3C contains two cysteines that can form an intramolecular disulfide bridge, which stabilizes a β-hairpin-like structure that has been shown to be essential for E2 epitope recognition , (see Figure D). Our top-down ECD mass spectrometry analysis revealed a subtle difference between the AR3C as present in the monoclonal antibody and the AR3C incorporated into the LaC49 bsAb. The ECD MS/MS spectrum of the AR3C Fab segment showed that the CDR3 disulfide bridge was intact when the AR3C mAb was analyzed under native conditions (see Figure C). In contrast, analyzing the F­(ab’)2 of the LaC49 bsAb, we found this disulfide bridge to be partially reduced, as evidenced by the detection of fragment ions in this region (see Figure C). This observation may have important functional implications. Although functional binding assays were not performed, it has been demonstrated that cysteine-to-alanine/serine mutations, which eliminate the disulfide bridge, may substantially reduce AR3C binding to the E2 protein and HCV neutralization potency. The partial reduction of the disulfide bridge likely occurs during the bsAb manufacturing by cFAE when 2-mercaptoethylamine (2-MEA) is used to reduce the interchain hinge region disulfides. 2-MEA can also non-selectively break exposed intrachain S–S bonds throughout the protein structure, affecting the CDR3 disulfide bridge in the AR3C. , Additionally, we observed partial reduction of the first intrachain disulfide bond in the AR4A heavy chain within the bsAb format (see Figure E). The earlier study by Radić et al. did show 2:1 E1E2:Ab binding for the LaC49 bsAb, suggesting that the bsAb can use both its arms, thus the AR3C arm in the bsAb format still retains sufficient binding capabilities. This could indicate batch-to-batch variation or only partial reduction of the disulfide bridge. Nevertheless, our study emphasizes the importance of monitoring such modifications as they can be crucial for the binding and neutralizing properties of bsAb.

Conclusions

This study highlights the importance of quality control during bispecific antibody production, as next to the intended product several by-products can be formed that are not detectable by conventional biochemical methods. The prototype timsOmni platform used here effectively revealed various modifications present in the samples, including residual unreacted mAb, heterogeneous signal peptide processing and cFAE-induced partial reduction of a functionally important disulfide bond, providing detailed structural information on these therapeutic biomolecules. Our data demonstrate that detailed, highly resolved, and accurate MS/MS analysis is essential to understand all modifications, as MS1 data alone is insufficient to distinguish between different modification sites, as shown for the AR4A light chain.

Our findings emphasize the need for detailed batch-to-batch control to monitor modifications that occur during production and storage of bispecific antibodies. Such modifications can affect important binding sites, as demonstrated by the disulfide bridge reduction observed in the AR3C heavy chain. Given these structural changes can impact therapeutic function, consistent analytical monitoring is essential for maintaining product quality.

The ECD MS/MS spectra also resulted in clear sequence ladders that could potentially be used for de novo sequencing applications, extending the analytical utility of this approach beyond routine quality control to applications such as sequence confirmation in bsAb development and validation of commercial antibodies for proof-of-concept studies.
