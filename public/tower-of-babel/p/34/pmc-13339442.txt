Opportunities, challenges, and sustainability of lay co-research teams in low resource community settings in Sri Lanka
Authors: Rukmi Anuradha Jayasinghe, Oshini Sri Jayasinghe, Sujani Preethika, Kusumalatha Manamperi, Ranjani Kandambi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity-led research (CLR) positions community members as active partners in generating knowledge about issues affecting their wellbeing. This approach is grounded in the principle that those closest to a problem are best placed to identify meaningful and sustainable solutions. In Sri Lanka’s plantation sector, families face persistent socio-economic and health inequities, including limited access to education, nutrition, and healthcare. The Women in Community Research (WinCR) group was established to address these challenges by enabling community members to lead research, document local realities, and translate evidence into advocacy to inform policy and practice.MethodsIn 2019, two community health and development practitioners established a voluntary lay co-researcher group, WinCR, consisting of 12 women from plantation communities with an interest in research. Participants were recruited through an open invitation. A structured capacity-building programme was implemented, covering qualitative research methods, advocacy, and skills such as communication and leadership. Learning was participatory and supported through peer mentoring and continuous feedback. The group applied these skills in a community-led study on maternal and child nutrition and later expanded their work to areas including school attendance, early literacy, and early childhood development. This paper outlines the establishment and sustainability of WinCR, as well as the key opportunities and challenges encountered from February 2019 to November 2025.ResultsCLR created several opportunities. The co-researchers’ lived experiences improved the cultural relevance of research tools, strengthened rapport with participants, and increased acceptance of findings. Capacity-building enhanced confidence, leadership, and recognition, leading to collaborations with government, non-governmental, and academic institutions. Key challenges included the intensive facilitation required for adult learning, low digital literacy, limited access to research materials in local languages, and initial skepticism from some professionals. Maintaining objectivity was occasionally difficult due to personal connections to the research topics. Research engagement remained focused on health, nutrition, education, and child development.ConclusionEstablishing and sustaining a lay co-researcher team in a deprived community is both feasible and valuable. CLR enhances research relevance, community ownership, and advocacy while empowering participants with new skills. Despite challenges related to training, recognition, and technological limitations, the WinCR initiative demonstrates the potential of community-led research and offers a practical model for inclusive research in underserved settings.Keywords: Community-led research (CLR), Community engagement & involvement in research (CEI), Lay co-researchers, Participatory approach, Health and social inequities, Community empowerment, Adult learning, Advocacy, Women in research

Background

Community Engagement & Involvement in Research (CEI) is comparatively a novel approach that involves the process of collaborating with and through groups of people connected by geographic location, shared interests, or common circumstances to address issues that influence their health and overall wellbeing [1]. Based on the long recognized the motto of the social justice ‘Those closest to the problem are closest to the solution’, the individuals and groups most affected by a problem are also best positioned to identify effective solutions, then it is essential to provide communities and their representative organizations with adequate resources to independently investigate and address these issues [2]. The community-led research model enables communities to define their own research priorities, collect and analyse relevant data, and design and implement appropriate solutions-either autonomously or in collaboration with external partners. Members of the community who work in the community-led research team could identify as co-researchers in such a context. A key advancement of this model is the inclusion of lay individuals as co-researchers, thereby enabling communities to contribute meaningfully across all stages of the research cycle.

Community-led and community-based participatory research (CBPR) has been widely recognized as a way to improve the relevance and impact of research by actively involving communities in all stages of the process [3, 4]. Studies show that such approaches can strengthen trust, improve data quality, and increase the likelihood that findings are used in real-world settings [4, 5]. For example, evidence from both high-income and low- and middle-income countries highlights how community participation leads to more context-specific interventions and better health and social outcomes [3, 5]. Recent work, including in global health literature, also emphasizes “localization” efforts-shifting leadership and decision-making power to local actors as essential for sustainable impact [6, 7].

In South Asia, participatory approaches have been mostly used in areas such as maternal and child health and violence prevention demonstrating improvements in health & wellbeing, service uptake and community awareness when local groups are actively involved [8–11]. However, literature specific to Sri Lanka remains limited, with only a few studies documenting structured, community-led research efforts [12]. While community engagement is valued, they are often project-based and not sustained overtime as independent community-led research initiatives. There is also limited documentation on building community members’ research capacity and enabling them to lead research and advocacy initiatives.

This initiative addresses these gaps by providing a practical example of establishing and sustaining a community-led research group in Sri Lanka, contributing context-specific insights to the broader evidence base on community-engaged research.

In 2019, two community health and development practitioners (RAJ, primary author and SP, one of the co-authors of this paper) established an independent, lay co-researcher group in Sri Lanka called Women in Community Research (WinCR), consisting of 12 women from low-country plantation communities who demonstrated both interest and capacity for research.

Sri Lanka’s low-country plantation sector, consisting of large- and mid-scale tea estates, is home to a population living on or near the estates, who are socially and geographically isolated and face persistent vulnerabilities. This historically neglected community continues to experience longstanding challenges. Families in the plantation sector particularly women and children experience persistent socio-economic and health inequities that limit access to quality education, nutrition, healthcare, and overall well-being. Children and women in these communities face high rates of malnutrition and restricted access to skilled healthcare, as many remote facilities are inadequately equipped, understaffed, and difficult to reach, impeding both preventive care and timely treatment [13, 14]. Schools within plantation estates also suffer from inadequate infrastructure, high dropout rates, insufficient staffing, and limited readiness for digital education [15]. Therefore, this initiative was established with local women who demonstrated strong enthusiasm to identify above context-specific challenges in community health, education and child development, lead research, and engage in advocacy to influence change within their own communities, rather than relying on external “helicopter researchers” whose interventions and policy recommendations may not reflect local realities.

In this paper, we aim to describe (a) the process of establishing and sustaining a lay co-researcher group within a marginalized community from January 2019 to November 2025 and (b) the challenges and opportunities encountered throughout this process. This paper presents a practice-informed longitudinal reflective and analytical account of the establishment, implementation, and sustainability of a community-led lay co-researcher initiative in a marginalized plantation community in Sri Lanka.

Development and operationalization of the WinCR initiative

Establishing ‘women in community research’ group

In 2019, twelve female community champions from parent support groups linked to a well-established, externally funded community health and nutrition project joined the WinCR initiative. During the implementation of this project, some of these women demonstrated strong observational and analytical skills, contributing to program design, planning, monitoring, and evaluation. They also showed a strong interest in creating positive change within their communities through their own actions and activities. Recognizing and valuing these skills and interests, the primary author, after one year, shared an open invitation message to assess interest in forming an independent community-led research group. Twelve women expressed their willingness to participate. Subsequently, the co-founders requested participants to submit a written expression of interest outlining their motivation, research interests, observational skills, community engagement, availability, and preferred areas of involvement. These responses provided a clear understanding of each participant’s abilities and potential for further development. Based on this assessment, all twelve women were selected and enrolled in the capacity-building programme.

All twelve female community researchers are permanent residents of low-country tea plantation estates in Sri Lanka, spanning from Morawaka to the Deniyaya area. By profession, two are early childhood care and development teachers, two work as assistants at the Estate Child Development Centre, and the remaining members engage in community well-being initiatives as volunteers. None of the participants had previously engaged in or completed formal university education. Their ages range from 30 to 52 years, and most are fluent in both local languages, Sinhala and Tamil.

Needs assessment and capacity building

To identify knowledge and skill gaps among the women in the research group, a qualitative needs assessment was conducted. A series of focus group discussions with selected participants was held to explore these gaps. The co-founders developed the discussion guide, facilitated the discussions, and summarized the findings. Based on the identified competency gaps, the co-founders designed activities to address these gaps (see Table 1), and a phased capacity-building program was subsequently implemented. In the first phase, which lasted approximately nine months, the group strengthened their understanding of fundamental qualitative research methodologies through a series of participatory learning sessions. All sessions were conducted in local languages and incorporated real-life examples, case studies, and case-based learning approaches. The facilitators, who were also the co-founders of the group, employed innovative participatory tools, including community mapping, problem tree analysis, ranking with dots, and role-plays, to enhance participants’ competencies and confidence in research. Also, best practices among researchers on how to maintain a research log or diary and write reflection notes were discussed, and their experiences were shared. The content of each session was presented in a simplified manner without compromising technical rigor. The learning series covered key topics such as an introduction to qualitative research, research ethics, data collection tools and methods, data validation, data analysis, interpretation, and dissemination.

This phase primarily emphasized foundational qualitative and participatory research methodologies. This approach was intentionally adopted to support women with limited formal education and digital access in developing essential competencies in community observation, interviewing, facilitation, reflective analysis, and interpretation of lived experiences. Qualitative methods were considered more appropriate during the early developmental stages due to their accessibility, flexibility, lower technological dependency, and alignment with community-led exploration of context-specific social issues. While quantitative methodologies were not conceptually excluded, they were not prioritized initially, as the group first required confidence-building, relational engagement skills, and contextual inquiry competencies before progressing toward more technically intensive research approaches.

In the second phase, which lasted approximately three months, a dedicated session was held on community-led advocacy, focusing on effectively communicating research findings to relevant stakeholders to influence decision-making and policy change.

The third phase, lasting around six months, included a series of participatory sessions on skills such as communication and presentation, leadership development, and preparing written, oral, and visual creative summaries. The learning modules were developed by the co-founders and delivered to the group through in-person and online, as pandemic-related restrictions affected in-person meetings. Additionally, self-evaluations were conducted after each session to identify areas for further improvement. Peer-to-peer mentoring, group learning, and continuous self-assessment were employed to sustain learning and promote ongoing skill development.

The entire process spanned approximately 18 months, with refresher training and follow-up sessions still being conducted to reinforce learning. Facilitators were intentional in ensuring that the sessions remained supportive, participatory, and engaging rather than evaluative or test-like, thereby minimizing power imbalances and avoiding hierarchical control within the group.

Structure and functioning of WinCR

WinCR comprises two co-founders who also act as co-technical facilitators rather than leaders, an additional technical expert in CBPR and a group of lay-researcher women. Lay researchers make decisions on identifying social gaps and determining which research projects to initiate and continue, while technical facilitators support the process by helping define and refine the scope of each study. Community researchers also receive continuous guidance, learning, and support from facilitators to move forward as an independent community-led research group.

Participation in WinCR was entirely voluntary. Lay co-researchers, particularly as women faced the ongoing challenge of balancing work and domestic responsibilities with their research roles. These challenges were openly acknowledged within the group; however, participants remained actively engaged because the research was closely connected to their everyday lives and local contexts. As a result, their participation was driven by a strong sense of purpose and relevance, and was not experienced as an additional burden. The incorporation of flexible scheduling further supported this engagement, enabling participants to exercise autonomy over their time management and planning. Additionally, working in pairs or small sub-groups also facilitated the sharing of responsibilities and mutual support. While no formal financial compensation was provided, some members benefited from paid opportunities through external collaborations. In addition, participants gained non-monetary benefits, including access to educational opportunities, skills development, and expanded professional networks.

Initial implementation research

Soon after the first phase of capacity building, potential research topics were discussed as a team. Lay co-researchers brought forward community issues and identified gaps relevant to their local context. Based on these discussions and prior experience in community nutrition, the topic was selected, and the co-founders refined the study scope. The research aimed to identify knowledge gaps related to food patterns, dietary diversity, and gender roles across different categories of tea estates within the district, and to examine how these factors influence maternal and child nutrition. It also sought to enhance understanding of local nutrition practices to optimize community interventions for underserved populations.

The research group collaboratively engaged in all stages of the study, from planning to dissemination. Researchers were initially divided into three sub-groups to develop the research plan, study design and qualitative data collection tools, including interview and focus group discussion guides, enhancing participation and flexibility. These were later reviewed, refined, and consolidated into a single framework with support from technical facilitators. The researchers mapped out the communities, assigned data collection roles based on individual competencies, and conducted participant recruitment. Data collection was scheduled flexibly, and the group contributed to obtaining administrative approvals and preparing ethical clearance applications. Data were collected using paper-based methods, and thematic analysis was conducted manually. Findings were documented in local languages and compiled into reports. Throughout the process, the group worked collaboratively. The findings were later shared by researchers at national and sub-national forums with stakeholders, including health officials, academics, and non-governmental organizations.

Continuation of research

Every two weeks, group meetings with learning tutorials, open forums and feedback sessions articulated their skills. The sustainability of WinCR was affected by the COVID-19 pandemic and the economic and political crisis in Sri Lanka during 2020–2023, which impacted planning and engagement. However, during this period, members participated in externally paid assignments in qualitative data collection, on rapid need assessments of the community during the COVID-19 pandemic. Also, some of the academic institutions involved this group and offered online learning opportunities for some individuals on community health promotion.

Sustaining of WinCR

Sustaining the group has been supported by a combination of intrinsic motivation, facilitative support, and external recognition. By engaging in research grounded in their own lived experiences, members are able to identify key issues within their communities, which strengthens their sense of purpose, ongoing motivation, and empowerment. Facilitators have played a vital role by providing consistent and approachable support; as community-based professionals, they possess a strong understanding of the local context, maintain close, trust-based relationships with participants, and minimize power imbalances, thereby strengthening social capital within the group.

In addition, external engagement with professional networks has contributed to sustaining the initiative. Over time, the group has gained recognition through community platforms, magazines, and national-level visibility, enhancing both credibility and acceptance. Opportunities for continuous learning and skill development have supported capacity building, while collaborative processes and shared decision-making have reinforced participants’ sense of ownership. Together, these factors have strengthened commitment and supported the long-term continuation of the group’s activities. Currently, the group is in the process of expanding to additional plantation areas and planning to invite more community researchers to join the initiative.

Opportunities

Community-led research appropriate and applicable within diverse and deprived community settings

In community-led research, community members participate as co-researchers throughout the project, engaging at different stages and to varying degrees. Because they have lived experiences and a deep understanding of the health issues being investigated, they make valuable contributions during the study design and data collection phases. For example, their insights are essential when developing context-appropriate data collection tools and planning for factors such as community politics, family power dynamics, gender, religion, and cultural norms. This involvement helps enhance the reliability and validity of the data collected.

Community-led research meaningfully strengthens community well-being and helps shape responsive policy change

To date, based on the research conducted, the findings of the community-led research were translated into a community-led advocacy initiative and were able to influence local authorities to further contextualize nutrition promotion interventions across the plantation community. They also helped address gaps in education and early childhood development in plantation areas. Moreover, some findings supported the streamlining of certain NGOs’ project interventions by helping to prioritize the direction of their projects.

Building capacity and empowerment of lay people lead to context specific, locally relevant research

The right amount of education on qualitative research methodologies based on real-world cases exposes community researchers to the ‘scientific world’. As community adults, they share common privileges associated with attending academic exploration and experimentation via short-term learning experiences, an opportunity distinct from the community-level experience. The group is not expected to demonstrate the same professional expertise as faculty or graduate students, or the same level of professional accountability as government or non-government partners. Free of preoccupied knowledge and conceptualized mindsets made it easy for them to capture the core- learnings. Simultaneously, capacity building on leadership qualities and other soft skills improved their competencies and confidence level to engage with the process with motivation. Smaller peer groups were expected and assumed a hallmark of personal growth and learning. Constructive feedback from professionals and decision-makers provided them with more credibility in the national and sub-national meetings.

Networking and collaborating with government, non-government and academic entities

Over the period, different thematic projects hired them for designing, data collection, and other stages of research as well as evaluations. The group has been collaborating with different government and non-government and academic entities. These networks have provided explicit opportunities to continuous learning from different experts and improve experiences and relevant skills. Local health and education authorities recognized this group as a facilitation group for their future initiatives. Ultimately, these collaborations and opportunities were fruitful and well-received by the group, enabling researchers to foster more effective partnerships.

Grounded in lived experiences enable engagement across different thematic areas

Drawing on their own lived experiences and identified community issues, the researchers have been engaged in a range of thematic areas, including community child health, education, early childhood care and development (ECCD), and emergency response. We also see opportunities to expand their involvement in the future, particularly in community maternal health and mental well-being.

Challenges and limitations

Start-up and continuation of this initiative required substantial facilitation and resources

While CBPR typically demands a high level of support, this initiative faced additional challenges due to the involvement of a lay co-researcher team from a deprived community. Technical facilitators not only trained the group but also had to adapt their approaches by learning adult education methods, tailoring content to participants’ needs, translating materials into local languages, and communicating in simple, accessible language while maintaining technical rigor. The team developed new knowledge and skills gradually, demonstrating strong enthusiasm throughout the process. However, learning was time-consuming, as adults often require time to adjust to structured learning environments. As a result, sustained and additional effort was needed - both initially and ongoing to deliver educational sessions and maintain momentum.

Gaps in e-literacy and poor accessibility to digital tools and language barriers of co-lay researchers

E-literacy among the community researchers was relatively low, and limited access to computers or other digital devices further restricted their ability to engage with essential research technologies. Today, many basic research activities rely on digital tools. Computers or mobile devices are needed to access training, join online sessions, collect and store data, communicate with the team, and prepare reports. In the early stages, especially before and during the pandemic, the group did not have smartphones or the e-skills to use platforms like Zoom or WhatsApp. Over time, they learned to use these platforms, but they still do not use computers. Data collection, storage, analysis, report preparation are all done manually. This reduced both the efficiency and confidence of the team in completing technology-based tasks.

Additionally, the availability of research materials in local languages is minimal, while most academic literature is published in English. This limited their accessibility for Literature. Also, many research guides, training materials, and online courses are mainly available in English, which makes it difficult for them to fully understand or use these resources. It also limited their ability to join wider learning opportunities such as webinars, presentations, discussions with external experts and communicating their findings to a broader audience. The group’s limited English proficiency therefore posed a considerable barrier to accessing external knowledge, slowing the overall learning process.

Poor Initial recognition, limited acknowledgment

As community-led research is a relatively novel concept in Sri Lanka, community participation has traditionally been limited to data collection, with research design, analysis, and leadership largely confined to academic settings. The concept of communities conducting their own research and engaging throughout the entire research process represents a significant shift, particularly in a context where the term “research” is predominantly associated with academia.

Initially, the group faced resistance from some academics and professionals, who questioned their capacity to conduct research, given that the group was not affiliated with a recognized institution and had limited prior research experience. However, over time, the group gradually gained visibility, recognition and appreciation for their work. The initiative has been consistently appreciated by the community, as members perceive the researchers as addressing issues grounded in their own lived experiences and working toward meaningful, locally driven change.

Retention of lay co-researchers in the group over the long term

Initially, twelve lay co-researchers were engaged during the start-up phase; however, by end of the reporting period, this number had decreased to eight. The attrition was primarily due to personal circumstances, including relocation, health issues, and voluntary withdrawal. We acknowledge that maintaining a voluntary group has been challenging, despite the members’ high level of enthusiasm for contributing to community change, gaining recognition, and engaging in learning opportunities.

Discussion

This process provides important insights into the establishment and sustainability of a community-led research model within a marginalized plantation setting in Sri Lanka. The findings reinforce the core principles of Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR), demonstrating that the meaningful involvement of community members strengthens trust, enhances cultural validity, and improves the contextual relevance of research processes and outcomes [3, 4]. By actively engaging lay co-researchers across the research cycle, this initiative contributes to a shift from extractive research practices toward more inclusive and locally grounded knowledge production.

A key contribution of this community initiative lies in demonstrating how community members without formal research background can progressively develop research capacity and confidence when supported through structured facilitation. This finding aligns with evidence that CBPR not only generates contextually relevant data but also builds community capacity and empowerment [5]. Importantly, this initiative extends existing literature by presenting a sustained, community-led model rather than a short-term, project-based engagement. In doing so, it highlights how long-term participation can strengthen ownership, credibility, and collective agency within marginalized communities. Moreover, while some participatory research scholars caution that formal training can risk reproducing academic power imbalances and undermining the authenticity of lay co‑researchers’ voices [16], the WinCR demonstrates context‑appropriate skill development in research is important when co‑researchers are leading their own community‑identified topics and driving the research process.

Despite these contributions, the findings highlight several structural and contextual challenges. The process of building research capacity among a voluntary group with limited prior exposure to research was time-intensive and required sustained facilitation. This reflects broader evidence that participatory research in low-resource settings often demands additional time, flexibility, and adaptation to local learning needs [17]. Initial scepticism from professionals also indicates persistent power hierarchies within research systems, where lay researchers may not be readily recognized as credible contributors [18]. However, consistent engagement and demonstrated outputs helped to gradually build trust and legitimacy, consistent with findings on partnership synergy and trust-building in CBPR [19]. Contextual factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic and economic and political instability in Sri Lanka also disrupted engagement and limited access to resources, highlighting the importance of resilience and adaptability in community-led research initiatives.

Motivation and retention of voluntary participants remain important considerations. While this initiative relied primarily on intrinsic motivation, existing evidence suggests that a combination of financial and non-financial incentives can strengthen engagement and sustainability in community-based programs [20, 21]. In this context, non-monetary benefits such as skill development, social recognition, and opportunities for external engagement appeared to play a significant role. Future models may benefit from more structured incentive mechanisms to support long-term participation.

This implementation process has several strengths, including its real-world application, longitudinal engagement, and focus on a marginalized and under-researched population. However, limitations should be considered. The findings are based on a single group, which may limit transferability. Challenges related to digital access, language, and participant retention may have influenced the process. These were partially mitigated through continuous mentoring, peer support, and flexible engagement approaches [22].

The findings have several implications. For practice, integrating community-led research approaches into health, nutrition, and education programs can improve the cultural relevance and effectiveness of interventions. For policy, there is a need to formally recognize and support community researchers within research and development frameworks, particularly in underserved settings. Strengthening institutional pathways for community engagement can help bridge the gap between evidence generation and policy action [23, 24].

This article also addresses a critical gap in the literature. While participatory approaches have been widely documented in global health, there is limited evidence on structured, community-led research models in South Asia, particularly in plantation-sector communities in Sri Lanka. Existing studies in the region have largely focused on community participation in data collection or intervention delivery, with limited emphasis on community ownership of the full research process. This initiative contributes to filling this gap by documenting the processes, challenges, and sustainability of a lay co-researcher model, offering context-specific evidence that can inform similar initiatives in low-resource settings.

Based on these findings, several key recommendations emerge. First, the longitudinal experience of WinCR suggests that the development of lay co-researchers in low-resource settings is not a linear training process but an iterative and evolving process of capacity building, practice, reflection, mentorship, and increasing autonomy. Participants initially contributed through their lived experiences and community knowledge, but progressively strengthened their research, leadership, and advocacy capabilities through repeated cycles of participatory learning, real-world research implementation, peer support, self-reflection, and facilitator feedback. Over time, responsibility, decision-making, and ownership increasingly shifted towards the community researchers themselves, enabling them to independently identify research priorities, design and conduct studies, facilitate community engagement activities, and participate in advocacy efforts. This finding extends existing participatory research literature by illustrating how sustained engagement over time can facilitate a gradual transition from research participation to autonomous community-led inquiry and action. While community-based participatory research frameworks emphasize collaboration, trust-building, and shared decision-making [3, 5, 25], less attention has been given to the mechanisms through which marginalized community members progressively develop the confidence, critical inquiry skills, ethical awareness, leadership capacity, and advocacy competencies required to assume increasingly independent research roles [4, 24, 26]. The WinCR experience suggests that this transition is supported not by sequential training alone, but by ongoing opportunities for experiential learning, reflection, mentorship, and shared decision-making embedded within the research process itself. These findings highlight the value of long-term investment in participatory capacity strengthening approaches that foster community ownership, local leadership, and the sustainability of community-led research initiatives, particularly among populations with limited formal educational opportunities.

Second, the process suggests that trained lay co-researchers can function as community knowledge intermediaries who bridge local lived realities with formal institutions such as health, education, and social service sectors. Formally recognizing community-led research groups as advisory bodies within local governance and service planning structures, particularly in underserved communities, can help ensure that decision-making is contextually relevant and responsive to local realities. This would move community participation beyond consultation and position communities as active producers, and translators of evidence for localized decision-making.

Third, the observation from this implementation indicates the inadequacy of conventional digitally dependent research systems for communities experiencing technological exclusion. Future research initiatives should intentionally design and validate low-literacy and low-technology participatory research methodologies, including visual coding systems, oral reflection techniques, community mapping tools, and paper-based thematic analysis approaches. Also, unlike many participatory research initiatives where dissemination remains academically driven, this process demonstrates the value of embedding advocacy training directly within the research cycle. Future community-led research models should incorporate structured advocacy preparation as a core methodological phase alongside data collection and analysis. This integration may strengthen the translation of local evidence into community action, improve responsiveness of local institutions, and enhance the practical impact of participatory research in socially marginalized settings.

Finally, the sustainability of WinCR suggests the importance of shifting participatory research away from short-term externally funded projects toward autonomous community research ecosystems that continue beyond individual studies. Future models should prioritize long-term social infrastructure development, including peer mentorship systems, intergenerational recruitment, collaborative leadership structures, and locally owned research agendas. Such an approach may support continuity, resilience during political and economic instability, and the emergence of independent community-generated evidence systems in low-resource contexts.

Conclusions

This initiative demonstrates that sustaining a lay co-researcher team in a socioeconomically deprived community setting is both feasible and valuable. Community-led research can enhance cultural relevance, strengthen local advocacy, and support community empowerment. Although challenges remain, particularly in relation to training, digital access, and recognition, our findings suggest that this approach can contribute to addressing complex social and health inequities.

The experience of WinCR provides practical insights into how marginalized communities can meaningfully contribute to knowledge production. With appropriate support, lay co-researchers may bring valuable lived experience and contextual knowledge that can strengthen research processes and support the co-creation of locally relevant evidence. As this study is based on a single community initiative, further research is needed to explore the transferability and long-term sustainability of this approach in other settings.
