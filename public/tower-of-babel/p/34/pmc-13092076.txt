Effect of heat stress and GlyNAC supplementation on jejunal morphology, hepatic inflammation infiltration and distribution of GSH content in chickens
Authors: Chen Wang, Liya Jin, Zhengshun Jia, Tengfei Ma, Quanwei Wei, Yansen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study evaluated the effects of cyclic heat stress (cHS) and diets supplemented with a combination of glycine and N-acetylcysteine (GlyNAC) on growth performance, serum antioxidant capacity and glutathione (GSH) content in serum, liver and pectoral muscles of Cyan-shank partridge chickens. Sixty-four male chickens (60 days old) were selected and randomly divided into four groups: CON + TN (basal diet; TN, thermoneutral condition with continuous 25 °C), GlyNAC + TN (basal diet + GlyNAC; TN), CON + cHS (basal diet; cHS, 32 °C for 4 h daily), and GlyNAC + cHS (basal diet + GlyNAC; cHS). The experimental period was 7 days. Results indicated that cHS significantly increased rectal temperature of chickens during heat treatment period (P < 0.05). Nevertheless, neither cHS nor GlyNAC supplementation affected growth performance or serum malondialdehyde levels. GlyNAC supplementation increased the GSH content in serum, liver and pectoral muscle (P < 0.05), but neither cHS nor GlyNAC altered jejunal GSH levels. The cHS decreased the ratio of villous height to crypt depth (V:C) and increased hepatic inflammatory infiltration score (IIS), while GlyNAC supplementation improved both the V:C ratio and hepatic IIS (P < 0.05). In liver, cHS downregulated the protein expression of caspase 3 and glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit (GCLM), as well as the mRNA expression of GCLM gene. In contrast, GlyNAC supplementation upregulated hepatic caspase 3 protein expression and the mRNA expression of GCLM genes (P < 0.05). In conclusion, dietary GlyNAC supplementation maintained the elevated GSH content in both serum and liver, while alleviating cHS-induced impairment of jejunal integrity and hepatic inflammatory infiltration.Keywords: GlyNAC, GSH, Caspase 3, Jejunum, Pectoral muscle

Introduction

Heat stress (HS) has emerged as a major challenge confronting the global poultry industry, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions (Gonzalez-Rivas et al., 2020; Ratriyanto and Mosenthin, 2018). Under heat stress conditions, chickens exhibited impaired physiological functions and reduced production performance (El-Kholy et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023). Chickens were highly susceptible to elevated ambient temperatures, which invariably induced the accumulation of excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress and damage in liver, muscle and intestinal tissues (Barrett et al., 2019; Carvalho Dos Santos et al., 2020; Lara and Rostagno, 2013; Wang et al., 2021). Consequently, identifying effective nutritional interventions to mitigate ROS-induced damage and enhance antioxidant defense has become a critical focus for poultry researchers.

Various antioxidants have been explored, among which N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and glycine (Gly) have shown significant promise due to their pivotal roles in the biosynthesis of glutathione (GSH). As a potent thiol donor and precursor of cysteine, NAC has been extensively studied for its ability to enhance antioxidant capacity, inhibit apoptosis, and exert anti-inflammatory effects (Dash et al., 2020; Dludla et al., 2020; Hou et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2021). As the essential substrate for the final ligation step catalyzed by GSH synthetase, Gly can reduce erythrocyte lipid peroxidation, enhance antioxidant enzyme activity, and scavenge excess ROS, thus inhibiting apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction (Bai et al., 2018; Deng et al., 2023; Won et al., 2023). The scientific rationale for a combination of Gly and NAC (GlyNAC) is rooted in the multi-step enzymatic biosynthesis of GSH. NAC provides the essential cysteine moiety for the formation of γ-glutamylcysteine, while glycine serves as the obligate substrate for the final step of GSH synthesis (Vašková et al., 2023). The dual-precursor GlyNAC strategy exerted synergistic effects and demonstrated superior efficacy over monotherapy in improving growth performance and overall health status in Nile tilapia (Xie et al., 2016). Recent studies reported GlyNAC can mitigate GSH deficiency, thereby attenuating systemic oxidative stress and chronic inflammation in mice (Kumar et al., 2023a, 2022; Sekhar, 2021). However, whether this synergistic GlyNAC intervention can protect against HS in poultry production remains to be elucidated.

The Cyan-shank partridge chicken, a prominent native breed in China, provides an ideal model for investigating these effects due to its advantages such as strong adaptability, high-quality meat, and excellent flavor (Lai et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2022). Despite their good heat tolerance, native chicken breeds are still affected by HS during hot summer months in south China. Therefore, evaluating the potential of GlyNAC to protect this valuable breed is of both scientific and economic importance. To simulate a more natural climatic pattern, this study employed a cyclic heat stress (cHS) model to evaluate the effects of dietary GlyNAC supplementation on growth performance, serum antioxidant capacity, hepatic inflammatory infiltration score (IIS), jejunal morphology and glutathione (GSH) content in serum, liver and pectoral muscles of Cyan-shank partridge chickens.

Materials and methods

Animals and dietary treatments

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (permit number SYXK (Su) 2021–0036). Sixty-four male Cyan-shank partridge chickens (60 days old) with similar body weight (BW) were randomly assigned to four treatment groups, with four replicates per group and four birds per replicate. Birds were housed in three-layer steel cages, with four birds per cage. Treatments were arranged in a 2 × 2 factorial design, including two levels of heat treatment, thermoneutral condition (TN) with continuous 25 °C; cHS, 32 °C for 4 h daily) and two levels of dietary GlyNAC supplementation (CON, basal diet; GlyNAC, basal diet supplemented with GlyNAC). The four treatment groups were as follows: CON + TN, CON + cHS, GlyNAC + TN and GlyNAC + cHS. For the cHS groups, the ambient temperature was cyclically increased from 25 to 32°C between 10:00 and 10:30, maintained at 32°C for 4 h (10:30 to 14:30), and subsequently returned to 25°C from 14:30 to 15:00 during each 24-h cycle. All groups were exposed to TN or cHS for 7 consecutive days and air relative humidity (RH) was maintained at 80%. In the GlyNAC groups, basal diet was supplemented with a combination of 0.5% Gly (S20159, Shanghai Yuanye, Shanghai, China) and 0.5% NAC (S20137, Shanghai Yuanye, Shanghai, China), according to previous studies (Deng et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021). The basal diet was formulated to meet the nutritional requirements recommended by the National Research Council (NRC, 1994) and its composition was detailed in Table 1. Birds were subjected to the experimental treatment for 7 consecutive days, from day 60 to day 67 of age.

Rectal temperature measurement

On days 1 and 5 of experimental treatment, rectal temperatures were measured at 08:30 (pre-cHS) and 13:00 (during cHS) using a rectal thermometer (TH‑5, Physitemp, Clifton, NJ, USA), respectively. At each time point, two birds per cage were randomly selected for rectal temperature measurement. To minimize handling-induced fluctuations in core temperature, rectal temperature was measured using the side-pinning method immediately following cage capture as described by previous study (Herborn et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2025a). Measurements were performed by a two-person team: one individual restrained the bird while the other inserted a specialized small-animal digital thermometer probe 2–3 cm into the rectum (Li et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025a). Stable readings were recorded approximately two seconds post-insertion. The thermometry system maintained an accuracy of ±0.1°C.

Growth performance

The BW of each bird was recorded at the ages of 60 and 67 days to calculate average daily gain (ADG). Final BW of each bird (67 days old) was recorded at 10:00 on the day after the 7-d 24-h cyclic treatment. The birds were subjected to a 12-hour fasting period before BW measurement. To control for variations in feed intake, each bird was provided 120 g of diet per day, based on the maximum daily intake observed among the four groups during the acclimation period. During the 7-day experimental period, daily feed intake for each group was recorded, and average daily feed intake (ADFI) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were subsequently calculated. The provision of clean water was available ad libitum throughout the whole trial period.

Sample collection

Following the final BW recording, two birds per cage (67 days old) were randomly selected for blood sampling via the wing vein. The isolated serum samples were stored at −80°C until analysis. After the blood collection, these birds were euthanized via carbon dioxide asphyxiation and exsanguination. The heart, spleen, and liver from two birds per cage were dissected and weighed. The organ index was calculated as (organ weight/BW) × 100%. Tissue specimens (liver, pectoral muscle, and jejunum) were excised for gene and protein expression assays, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and maintained at −80°C until analysis. Furthermore, a part of liver (1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm cube) and a 2-cm segment of jejunum were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histomorphology analysis.

Serum antioxidant parameters and tissue GSH content

Serum antioxidant parameters, including GSH, malondialdehyde (MDA), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), GSH peroxidase (GSH-Px), catalase (CAT), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), were analyzed. Serum activities of T-SOD (A001-1-2), GSH-Px (A005-1-2), and CAT (A007-1-1), along with the levels of GSH (A006-2-1), MDA (A003-1-2), and T-AOC (A015-3-1) were determined using commercial assay kits from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Supernatants from liver, jejunum, and pectoral muscle tissues were used to determine tissue GSH content.

Histopathological analysis

Following fixation, the liver and jejunum were subjected to gradient alcohol dehydration, paraffin embedding, sectioning at 5 μm with a Leica microtome (RM2235, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and observed using a microscope (BX51, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For each bird, the villus height and crypt depth of the jejunum were measured using Image J 1.7.0 software (NIH, Bethesda, USA). Hepatic IIS was assessed using nonalcoholic fatty liver disease activity score method, with scores ranging from 0 to 3 (Brunt et al., 2011; Choi et al., 2018; Nikeghbalian et al., 2021).

Western blot

Samples of liver and pectoral muscle were crushed on dry ice to obtain homogenous aliquots and lysed in RIPA lysate (P0013B, Biyuntian, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations of the extracted samples were measured using the BCA protein assay kit (PC0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Protein lysates were heated for 10 min at 95°C in 5 × SDS - PAGE loading buffer (P0015, Biyuntian, Shanghai, China) and then ran on a 12% SDS - PAGE gel (C651300, Sangon, Shanghai, China). Separated proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (88520, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), blocked for an hour using 5% milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), and stained with primary antibodies (Table S1). After washed with TBST, membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h and the antiobody-stained protein signal was visualized using an ECL luminescence kit (P0018S, Biyuntian, Shanghai, China) in an ultrasensitive chemiluminescence gel imaging system (1708370, Bio-Rad, CA, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA of each sample was extracted using FreeZol Reagent RNA kit (R711-01, Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) and reversed-transcribed to generate cDNA using 5 X All-In-One RT MasterMix (G592, Applied Biological Materials Inc., Richmond, Canada). Specific primers for glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC), glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit (GCLM) and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). and listed in Table S2. The qRT-PCR assays were carried out using SYBR Green master mix (RK21203, ABclonal, Wuhan, China) on the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (A28575, Thermo Scientific, Sunnyvale, USA). To minimize experimental bias, the relative mRNA expression of target genes was normalized against the geometric mean of two housekeeping genes (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase and β-actin) using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Vandesompele et al., 2002).

Statistical analysis

Two-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the effects of heat treatment (H) and dietary GlyNAC supplementation (D) and their interaction (H × D) using the GLM procedure in SPSS software (version 23.0, SPSS, Inc., Chicago, USA). When a significant interaction was observed (P < 0.05), simple main effect analysis was performed, and pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni adjustment to control for Type I error inflation associated with multiple comparisons. Data are expressed as "mean ± SEM." Probability values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Rectal temperature, growth performance and relative organ weight

No mortality was observed among the chickens throughout the 7-d experimental period. As shown in Fig. 1, cHS significantly increased rectal temperatures in the GlyNAC + cHS group and the CON + cHS group on days 1 and 5 of heat treatment, respectively. Meanwhile, there was no significant change in rectal temperature of birds in the GlyNAC + TN group and the CON + TN group as birds were still kept at TN condition. As shown in Table 2, there was no significant difference in initial BW, final BW, ADFI, ADG and FCR between CON groups and GlyNAC supplementation groups or between NT groups and cHS groups. Moreover, there was no significant difference of the H × D interaction in these parameters of growth performance after the 7-d cHS. In addition, there was no significant difference in relative organ weight of heart, liver and spleen among the four groups (Table 3).

Serum antioxidative parameters

The effect of cHS and dietary GlyNAC supplementation on redox status and serum antioxidant parameters was studied. Based on 2-way ANOVA analysis, GlyNAC supplementation increased serum GSH content (P = 0.007) and decreased serum GSH-Px activities (P = 0.012) (Table 4). cHS decreased serum CAT activities (P = 0.022) and serum GSH content increased from 50.87 to 91.36 μmol/L in the GlyNAC + cHS group compared with that in the CON + cHS group (Table 4). Moreover, there was no significant difference in serum MDA content and activities of T-AOC and T-SOD of birds among the four groups.

Distribution of GSH content in liver, jejunum and pectoral muscle

The effect of cHS and GlyNAC supplementation on the GSH content of liver, jejunum and pectoral muscle were presented in Table 5. Based on 2-way ANOVA analysis, no significant main effects were observed for heat treatment or dietary GlyNAC supplementation, respectively. There was a H × D interaction in the GSH content of liver (P = 0.003). In the liver, the GSH content was up-regulated in the GlyNAC + TN group compared to the CON + TN group (P < 0.05), and there was a lower GSH content in the GlyNAC + cHS group than that in the GlyNAC + TN group (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in the GSH content of jejunum between CON group and GlyNAC group or between TN group and cHS group. In addition, GlyNAC supplementation significantly increased the GSH content in pectoral muscles (P < 0.05).

Hepatic inflammation infiltration

In Fig. 2, it is difficult to observe clear lobular boundaries and separate adjacent hepatic lobules in Cyan-shank partridge chickens. With additional magnification of the tissue section, hepatocytes were well-arranged with clear boundaries and relatively homogeneous cytoplasm in the CON + TN group and the GlyNAC + NT group. Pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration were observed in the GlyNAC + cHS group and CON + cHS group (Fig. 2). In areas proximal to inflammatory cell infiltration, hepatocytes exhibited an increased tendency to undergo nuclear pyknosis and necrosis. Based on 2-way ANOVA analysis, cHS significantly increased IIS (P < 0.01), while GlyNAC supplementation decreased IIS (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference of the H × D interaction.

Jejunal morphology

Morphological analysis of the jejunum was conducted to assess whether cHS induced intestinal damage. Results were shown in Fig. 3. Bird jejunal structures of four groups was intact, with villi arranged in an orderly manner. The villus height ranged from 1000 to 1500 μm, and the crypt depth ranged from 200 to 300 μm. Based on 2-way ANOVA analysis, GlyNAC supplementation increased the villous height (P < 0.01), and decreased crypt depth of bird jejunum (P < 0.01). There was a significant increase in the ratio of villous height to crypt depth (V:C) as birds fed with GlyNAC, and cHS decreased the V:C ratio (P < 0.01). Birds had higher V:C ratio in the GlyNAC + TN group than that in the CON + TN group (P < 0.01). In addition, the V:C ratio was lower in the GlyNAC + cHS group than that in the GlyNAC + TN group.

Caspase-3 and GSH synthesis-related proteins in liver and pectoral muscles

Based on 2-way ANOVA analysis, both cHS and GlyNAC supplementation significantly decreased liver GCLM expression after the 7-d experimental period (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4c). There was a H × D interaction in the liver caspase-3 expression (P = 0.038). Caspase-3 was lower in the CON + cHS group than that in the CON + TN group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4d). cHS decreased caspase-3 level in the CON + cHS group as compared with the CON + TN group (P < 0.05). Dietary GlyNAC supplementation increased caspase-3 level in the GlyNAC + cHS group as compared with the CON + cHS group (P < 0.05). In the pectoral muscles, there was no significant difference in the protein expression of GCLC, GCLM and caspase-3 between CON group and GlyNAC supplementation group or between TN group and cHS group (Fig. 4e-h). Moreover, there was no significant difference of the H × D interaction in the three proteins of birds among the four groups.

GSH synthesis-related genes in liver and pectoral muscles

In the liver, a significant H × D interaction was observed for the mRNA expression of GCLC (P < 0.001) and GCLM (P = 0.001) (Table 6). Under the TN conditions, GlyNAC supplementation markedly up-regulated the expression of GCLC and GCLM compared to the CON+TN group (P < 0.05). However, this stimulatory effect was significantly suppressed under the cHS conditions (P < 0.05). For Nrf2, although the H × D interaction was not significant, the main effect of heat treatment was significant (P = 0.025), with cHS leading to a downregulation of Nrf2 expression. In the pectoral muscles, no significant H × D interactions were detected for the expression of GCLC, GCLM, or Nrf2. However, the main effects of both heat treatment and dietary GlyNAC supplementation were prominent. cHS significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of GCLC (P = 0.027) and Nrf2 (P = 0.039) compared to the TN group. Dietary GlyNAC supplementation significantly enhanced the mRNA expression of GCLC (P = 0.009) and GCLM (P = 0.034).

Discussion

The rectum is generally considered the primary site for measuring core body temperature in poultry. Rectal temperature increases significantly in broiler chickens and hens when environmental temperature rises (Farag and Alagawany, 2018). A marked elevation in rectal temperature is widely regarded as an indicator of HS, which negatively affects physiological responses, antioxidant status, intestinal absorption, and growth performance in broiler chickens (Awad et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2023). Cyan-shank partridge chicken is native broiler breed with strong thermoregulatory capacity and is mainly distributed in southern China (Zhao et al., 2019). Previous study reported rectal temperature of Cyan-shank partridge chickens increased when the ambient temperature exceeded 26.71 °C at the RH level of 80% (Wang et al., 2025a). In the present study, rectal temperature increased significantly at 32°C and 80% RH, indicating that the birds were unable to maintain normal body temperature and were experiencing HS as exposed to cHS. Previous study has demonstrated that cHS (32 °C for 8 h daily) had a lower effect on feed intake and weight gain and no effect on the FCR. In contrast, nutrient digestibility was only influenced by continuous HS (continuous 32 °C) in comparison to control broiler chickens from 21 to 42 days of age (de Souza et al., 2016). Notably, in the present study, cHS had no significant impact on final BW, ADFI, ADG, and FCR, nor did it affect organ index. This might be attributed to the limited daily exposure (32 °C for 4 h daily) and the short duration of the cHS, which was likely insufficient to induce measurable alterations in the growth performance of birds at 60 days of age.

In chickens, cHS (32-34 °C for 8 h daily) significantly altered serum MDA content, GSH concentration and activities of T-SOD, and CAT (Chen et al., 2020; Lan et al., 2023). Serum MDA and GSH levels in mice were unaffected by cHS (42 °C, 2 h/day) during the first two days, whereas a significant increase in MDA levels was observed, alongside a concomitant reduction in GSH content by days 8 and 12 (Li et al., 2013). In the present study, the absence of significant differences in serum MDA content, T-SOD activity, and T-AOC across treatment groups suggested that the birds experienced a mild HS (32 °C for 4 h daily), allowing sufficient time for them to re-establish antioxidant homeostasis. Mainly synthesized in the liver, serum CAT activities responded dynamically to HS intensity, exhibiting an initial upregulation under mild conditions followed by a marked decline as the stress progressed to a higher level in mice and lambs (Li et al., 2013; Shi et al., 2020). The significant reduction in CAT activity observed in this study implied that cHS altered the balanced relationship among various antioxidant parameters and the serum antioxidant system would likely undergo a gradual shift toward imbalance as exposure duration increased. Moreover, this study found GlyNAC supplementation significantly elevated serum GSH levels, indicating that GlyNAC promoted GSH synthesis and impacted the GSH distribution in chickens.

Glutathione is predominantly synthesized in the liver, which contributes a substantial proportion of the GSH present in the serum and serves as the major source of circulating GSH (Vašková et al., 2023). Under normal conditions, newly synthesized hepatic GSH is not extensively retained within hepatocytes but is actively transported into the circulation via GSH specific transporters, such as multidrug resistance associated proteins (MRPs), expressed on the cell membrane (Fig. 5) (Labarrere and Kassab, 2022; Vázquez-Meza et al., 2023). In the present study, GlyNAC significantly increased GSH content in serum and liver, indicating that GlyNAC can promote de novo GSH synthesis in the liver and further elevated serum GSH levels. Notably, hepatic GSH content was significantly reduced in chickens subjected to cHS, whereas serum GSH levels exhibited an opposite pattern, increasing from 71.98 to 91.36 μmol/L, suggesting a potential shift in GSH distribution between the liver and the systemic circulation. As the primary detoxification organ of the body, the liver upregulated its detoxification and used GSH as a conjugation substrate to generate non-toxic adducts in response to high levels of endogenous or exogenous toxins (Lu, 1999). Thus, this study hypothesized that with an adequate supply of GlyNAC, newly synthesized hepatic GSH might be rapidly exported into the bloodstream to counteract cHS-induced oxidative stress in peripheral organs, such as the intestine. Further research is warranted to investigate the specific regulatory effects of GlyNAC on hepatic GSH transport and the distribution of GSH content under HS conditions (Fig. 5).

Intestinal epithelial cells are also capable of de novo GSH synthesis and release small amounts into the bloodstream (Silvagno et al., 2020; Ziegler et al., 2003). Other tissues, including skeletal muscle, lungs, and brain, can synthesize GSH. However, this locally produced GSH is primarily utilized for tissue specific antioxidant defense and metabolic functions (Lu, 2013). In chickens, the small intestinal tract had relatively higher GSH concentration compared with liver (Tang et al., 2019). Previous study reported cHS (32-34 °C for 8 h daily) induced jejunal oxidative stress and decreased V:C ratio in chickens (Chen et al., 2020). In the present study, jejunal GSH level was higher than that observed in the liver and pectoral muscles, suggesting the jejunum might possess a more robust endogenous buffering system. In addition, jejunal GSH content remained unaffected by either cHS or GlyNAC supplementation, indicating jejunum might effectively neutralize cHS-induced oxidative stress and maintain GSH concentration via the endogenous buffering system. Previous study found GSH supplementation alleviated the impairment induced by cysteine deprivation in cultured intestinal epithelial cells of porcine (Jiao et al., 2022). Thus, high levels of serum GSH might play a key role in maintaining the concentrations of GSH in the jejunum of chicken. In this study, GlyNAC also significantly increased GSH content in pectoral muscle, indicating that GlyNAC can promote de novo GSH synthesis in the pectoral muscle.

The V:C ratio reflects morphological alterations of the intestinal mucosa (Kikusato and Toyomizu, 2023; Nanto-Hara et al., 2020). Villus height indicates the nutrient absorptive capacity of the intestinal mucosa, whereas crypt depth is associated with the differentiation and renewal of epithelial cells that are sloughed from the villus tips (Nelson, 2021; Wang et al., 2025b). Previous research has shown that HS significantly reduced the V:C ratio of duodenum and increased plasma endotoxin concentrations in broiler chickens (Alhenaky et al., 2017). HS damaged the intestinal mucosal integrity of chickens by disrupting the tight junctions of intestinal epithelial cells. Endotoxins can be translocated into the bloodstream via the impaired intestinal mucosa (Wang, 2021). The liver is the first organ exposed to these translocated endotoxins via the portal vein. Endotoxins stimulate hepatic secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, which recruit peripheral blood lymphocytes to the liver, leading to increased inflammatory cell infiltration (Hsu and Schnabl, 2023). In the present study, cHS decreased the V:C ratio and increased IIS, indicating that cHS impaired jejunal integrity and elevated the risk of hepatic exposure to intestinal toxins, thereby increasing hepatic inflammatory infiltration. Compared with the CON + cHS group, birds of the GlyNAC + cHS group showed an increased V:C ratio and reduced hepatic IIS, indicating GlyNAC supplementation mitigated cHS-induced jejunal damage and hepatic inflammation.

Glutathione is supplied by two core mechanisms: de novo synthesis and glutathione disulfide (GSSG) recycling, which collectively sustain the cellular antioxidant pool (Lu, 2013)(Fig. 5). De novo synthesis is regulated by the two subunits, GCLC and GCLM. GCLC serves as the rate-limiting component, directly determining the baseline efficiency of GSH production, while GCLM forms a heterodimer with GCLC, enhancing its substrate affinity (Vašková et al., 2023). Under normal conditions, GCLC and GCLM bind to form a stable heterodimer that resists ubiquitination (Yang et al., 2007). When intracellular GSH accumulates excessively, GCLM dissociates from GCLC via a negative feedback mechanism (Franklin et al., 2009) (Fig. 5). Free GCLC monomers were readily recognized by E3 ubiquitin-protein ligases and subsequently degraded by the proteasome (Chen et al., 2020). Similarly, free GCLM monomers are cleared by the ubiquitin-proteasome system due to conformational instability, which suppresses de novo GSH synthesis and maintains intracellular GSH homeostasis (Lu, 2013). In this study, hepatic GCLM protein levels decreased in the GlyNAC group compared with the control groups, suggesting that sustained high intracellular GSH may promote GCLM degradation by affecting the GCLC-GCLM heterodimer. Reduced cellular level of caspase-3 is generally considered a marker of increased apoptosis (Bai et al., 2018;Dash et al., 2020; Dludla et al., 2020). In the present study, the caspase-3 level exhibited the down-regulation trend in the CON + NT group and the up-regulation trend in the GlyNAC + cHS group, suggesting that GlyNAC supplementation might attenuate cHS-induced hepatocyte apoptosis in Cyan-shank partridge chickens.

As a key antioxidant transcription factor, Nrf2 directly binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) to regulate the transcriptional expression of GCLC and GCLM (Fig. 5) (Lee et al., 2003). Under normal conditions, Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) constitutively targets Nrf2 for Cul3-dependent ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, preventing excessive accumulation of free Nrf2 and restraining transcription of downstream targets, including GCLC and GCLM (Kobayashi et al., 2004). Previous studies have shown that GlyNAC supplementation promotes Nrf2 nuclear translocation, leading to upregulation of Nrf2, GCLC, and GCLM gene expression in the liver and brain of aged mice (Kumar et al., 2022, 2023b). In the present study, relative mRNA expression of GCLM and GCLC increased in the liver and pectoral muscle of birds fed GlyNAC, suggesting that dietary GlyNAC may enhance GCLM and GCLC gene expression via the Nrf2 signaling pathway. Notably, changes in GCLM protein expression displayed an opposite trend to its gene expression. Considering the restricted number of biological replicates, further experiments are needed to validate the functional role of the Nrf2-GCLC/GCLM signaling pathway in GSH synthesis (Fig. 5).

Conclusion

In conclusion, a 7-day cHS (32 °C for 4 h daily) induced mild physiological stress in chickens, characterized by the elevated hepatic IIS and a reduced jejunal V:C ratio, despite the maintenance of stable growth performance. Dietary GlyNAC supplementation effectively mitigated these adverse physiological effects by enhancing hepatic and systemic GSH synthesis. This protective effect was regulated by the transcriptional changes of GCLC and GCLM genes involved in GSH biosynthesis. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that dietary GlyNAC serves as a potent nutritional strategy to preserve redox homeostasis and maintain organ integrity in poultry exposed to HS.
