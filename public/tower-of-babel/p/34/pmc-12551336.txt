Production and quality evaluation of biscuit made from African yam beans, sweet potato composite flour enriched with African star apple
Authors: Otu Okogeri, Gloria I Gibson-Umeh, Amarachi C Uwandu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study investigated the production of biscuit from African yam beans/sweet potato composite flour fortified with African star apple. Biscuits, produced from African yam beans (AYB), sweet potato (STP) and African star apple (ASA) flour blends in the ratios (AYB: STP: ASA): 50:45:5; 45:45:10; and 40:40:20, were subjected to analysis of proximate composition, and physical and sensory properties; while the blends were analysed for functional properties. Biscuits made from AYB: STP (50:50) blend served as control. Moisture, ash, fat, fiber, protein and carbohydrate contents ranged from 5.90 to 7.30%; 2.17–2.26%; 8.30–9.10%; 6.75–7.47%; 12.50-12.95%, and 61.85–63.25% respectively. Fiber and fat contents increased with increasing levels of ASA. Spread ratio ranged from 19.39 to 19.86; thickness from 2.52 to 2.56 mm; diameter, 50.21 to 50.97 mm; and weight, 13.26 to 15.77 g. Thickness and diameter of biscuits also increased as the percentage of ASA increased. Based on sensory scores, biscuits made from AYB: STP: ASA blend in the ratio of 50:45:5 were preferred to the control and samples made from other blend ratios. The above findings suggest that, AYB: STP: ASA blend containing less that 10% ASA can be considered the ideal ratio for the production of nutritious and organoleptically acceptable biscuits.Keywords: African star apple, Proximate composition, Composite flour, Underutilized crop, Sensory properties, Biscuit

Introduction

Dietary habits in Nigerian urban areas are increasingly becoming more westernized especially in terms of consumption of wheat and wheat-based products [1]. Part of wheat flour used for production of various confectioneries can be substituted or supplemented with flours from other cereals, oil seeds, and lesser known legumes. These composite flours with low gluten content are still suitable for breads, cakes and biscuits where weaker flours are desirable [2]. The substitution and supplementation of flours help to make up for deficiencies and combat malnutrition. Many snacks are however poor in protein and as such contribute to poor nutritional quality. The use of legumes and oilseeds as means of enriching snacks with protein, minerals and vitamins has been proposed in the literature [1].

African yam bean (AYB) (Sphenostylis stenocarpa) is a hard-to-cook underexploited leguminous plant grown extensively in West African countries including Nigeria [3]. It has been reported to be rich in protein with values in the range of 19–30%; in essential and non-essential amino acids; and in essential fatty acids: alpha-linolenic acid and linoleic acid [4]. Carbohydrate, bioactive compounds such as flavonoids and phenolic acids; and minerals such as calcium, iron, zinc and magnesium have also been reported to be present in AYB [5]. This attractive nutritional profile makes AYB a desirable fortifying agent and hence has been used in fortification of varieties of food products including breakfast meals [6], cookies [7], and instant noodles [8] among others. Hence, the use of African yam bean in composite flour for biscuit production is expected to yield nutritionally enhanced biscuit.

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batata L.) is a tuber of tropical and subtropical environments that can be easily grown with low cost of production by both small and large scale farmers [9]. They contain more carbohydrates including dietary fibre and starch, than other root and tuber crops; in addition to a wide range of micronutrients including minerals and vitamins A, C and D [9]. Yellow and orange-fleshed tubers are characterized by high levels of phenolic acids particularly caffeic acid, chloregenic acid, and caffeoylquinic acid derivatives [10]. Sweet potato flour has been used in industrial production of biscuits, chips, crisps and other bakery products and can serve as a substitute for wheat flour [11]. In addition to its nutritional value, sweet potato has the potential to improve aesthetic value by adding color and flavor to food products.

African star apple (ASA) (Chrysophyllum albidum) on the other hand, is an underutilized fruit of important value in Africa, with potential medicinal, industrial and food uses [12]. It is distributed throughout the southern part of Nigeria and commonly known as Udara (Igbo dialect), Agbalumo (Yoruba dialect) and Agwaluma (Hausa dialect). The edible pulp has been reported as a good source of vitamins C, A, B1 and B2, in addition to fiber, carbohydrate and antioxidants [12]. Snacks, including biscuits are generally poor sources of protein and as a result contribute to poor nutritional quality. However, considering their popularity and ready to eat nature, there is need to enrich the nutritional composition of biscuits. A wide range of materials including mushroom [13], banana flour [14] and decaffeinated green tea leaves [15] have been used to improve the nutritional quality of biscuits. African yam bean is high in protein but poor in fat and carbohydrate, hence the need to fortify its nutrient composition with readily available local crops rich in the nutrients lacking in AYB. AYB blended with sweet potato and African star apple could potentially serve as raw material for the production of nutritionally enriched gluten-free biscuit. In addition, no literature was found on the enrichment of biscuit with African star apple, a neglected tropical fruit; hence, the present study also attempts to find new area of application for ASA, promote generation of income in local communities where this fruit abounds, and encourage more research necessary for identify other areas of application for ASA.

Materials and methods

Source of raw material

African yam bean, sweet potatoes and African star apples were purchased from open market in Abakaliki, Ebonyi State. Other ingredients such as sugar, margarine, egg and baking powder were purchased from a local supermarket.

Sample preparation

Preparation of African yam bean flour

African yam beans flour was prepared according to the method described by Enwere [16], with slight modification. Brown African yam bean seeds were sorted to remove foreign materials, soaked in water for 12 h for easy dehulling, and then blanched for 15 min to reduce the anti-nutritional factors and beany flavor that may have been present. In continuation, the seeds were oven-dried (Lab oven SLW 53, Harrow Scientific Ltd) at 55 °C until constant weight; milled using laboratory attrition mill, and sieved using 450 μm aperture sieves, to obtain African yam bean flour.

Preparation of sweet potato flour

Sweet potato flour was prepared using the method described by Effiong et al. [8]. Briefly, potato tubers were sorted, washed, manually peeled and sliced into thin pieces. The slices were blanched at 65 °C for 15 min, oven-dried (Lab oven SLW 53, Harrow Scientific Ltd) at 55 °C until constant weight; then milled using laboratory attrition mill and sieved using 450 μm aperture sieve, to obtain potato flour.

Preparation of African star apple flour

Fresh ripe fruits of African star apple were sorted, washed and manually opened to remove the seeds and the peel. The pulp was sliced into thin pieces and sundried in a tray until constant weight was achieved (4 days at average daytime temperature of 32 ± 2 °C). The dried pulp was then milled using laboratory attrition mill (Hosokawa Pin Mill), and sieved using 450 μm aperture sieve to obtain African star apple flour.

The flours produced (as described above) were further blended according to the ratios shown in Table 1, for biscuit production. The ratios were selected based on a preliminary test, where a total of 12 blends were processed into biscuits and assessed for their sensory attributes (which are the most obvious attributes evaluated by consumers of biscuit). Samples with low or undesirable acceptability scores were eliminated from further studies.

Production of biscuit

Biscuits were prepared according to the method described by Chauhan et al. [17]. with little modification. Precisely, shortening (margarine) (40 g) and sugar (40 g) were mixed, and added to the mixture of flour blend (100 g), water (15 ml), sodium bicarbonate (1 g), and skim milk powder (10 g); then mixed thoroughly to form dough. The dough was kneaded and sheeted to a uniform thickness of 0.25 cm, cut into circular shapes of 5 cm diameter, and transferred to a baking tray. Baking was accomplished in 15 min in an oven (Harrow Scientific Ltd) preheated to 170 °C. One set of the freshly produced biscuits was subjected to sensory evaluation, while the other set was cooled and subjected to the analyses outlined.

Proximate analysis

Moisture, Crude protein, Fat, Fiber and Ash contents were evaluated according to the AOAC [18], while Carbohydrate was determined by weight difference method, carried out by subtracting the sum of moisture, protein, fat, fiber, and ash contents from 100.

Functional properties of flour blends

Water Absorption Capacity, Oil Absorption Capacity, Bulk density and swelling index of flour blends were determined according to the methods described by Onwuka [19].

Physical characteristics

Diameter and thickness of biscuits were measured using a Vernier Caliper, the weight by digital weighing balance (G&G, JJ 600), while the spread ratio was calculated by dividing the diameter of biscuits by their height [20].

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was carried out using 15 panelists (staff and students) from the Department of Food Science and Technology, Ebonyi State University, Abakaliki. The panelists were screened using the triangle test [21] and selected based on their ability to consistently discriminate between biscuits with and without added African star apple. Biscuits were evaluated by rating on a 9-point hedonic scale where 1 = dislike extremely; 5 = neither like nor dislike; and 9 = like extremely, according to acceptability of appearance, taste, color, flavor, texture, crispness, and general acceptability. Evaluation of sensory attributes of samples and control was carried out based on the recommendations described by Malcolmson [22]. Each panelist received freshly baked biscuit (the control), evaluated it, and took a 2-minute break before evaluating another biscuit with added African star apple at varying levels. For evaluation of taste, panelists were asked to cleanse their palates with warm water after each tasting.

Statistical analysis

Analytical data were subjected to Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), using SPSS version 17.0, and differences were considered to be statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Proximate composition

The proximate composition of biscuits made from African yam beans, sweet potato composite flour, enriched with African star apple at varying levels, are presented in Table 2. Moisture content, an indicator of crispness and the time at which samples can be stored without the development of moulds, ranged from 5.9 to 7.3% but decreased with increase in ASA. The variation could be attributed to the particular class of carbohydrates (mono-, oligo, or poly-saccharides) dominating in each sample. Fruits (ASA), tubers (STP) and legumes (AYB) are rich in carbohydrates, which due to their multiple polar hydroxyl groups, confer hydrophilic nature on the blends. It is speculated that depending on the predominant class of carbohydrates, brought about by increasing amounts of ASA (and decreasing amounts of AYB and STP), the hydrophilic nature (and the moisture content) of AS05, AS10, and AS20 can either increase or decrease. Nevertheless, the moisture contents recorded in this study are within the acceptable limits for biscuit [23].

The fat contents of samples increased with increase in the amount of ASA, with AS20 recording the highest fat content (9.1%) and AS05 the lowest; suggesting that the addition of ASA incorporated fat into the biscuit. This is not surprising, as the fat content of the pulp of African star apple has been reported to be as high as 15% [24]. The fat contents of samples containing 5–20% ASA were significantly higher (p < 0.05) than that of the control with no added ASA (Table 2). In terms of protein content, values of samples ranged from 12.5 to 12.95% and were significantly lower (p < 0.05) than 14.2% recorded for the control (Table 2). This confirms that AYB is a good source of protein as reported in the literature for different varieties of AYB [4, 5] but also suggests that the inclusion of ASA brought about decrease in protein content. Fruits (ASA) are generally known to be poor in protein; hence, the decrease could be attributed to dilution effect. The protein contents of samples increased slightly with increase in added ASA, however, no statistical difference (p >0.05) existed between the protein contents of samples. The ash content of samples ranged from 2.17 to 2.26% and did not differ significantly (p < 0.05) from the control (2.3%), suggesting that the addition of ASA had no impact on the ash content. Samples with added ASA recorded significantly higher (p < 0.05) crude fiber content than the control (5.12%). Crude fiber content of samples ranged from 6.75 to 7.47% with no significant difference (p >0.05) among them. This suggests that that addition of up to 20% African star apple flour to African yam bean and sweet potato composite flour had no significant impact on the fiber content of biscuits. Carbohydrate content was slightly higher (64.5%) in the control biscuit compared to the samples which recorded values in the range of 61.85–63.25%. However, the carbohydrate contents shown in Table 2 can be considered acceptable for biscuits from composite flour.

Water and oil absorption capacity for instance can affect texture and appearance of food products. The bulk density of the flour blends ranged from 0.66 to 0.69 g/ml and differed significantly (p < 0.05) from the control (0.60 g/ml). This indicates that addition of African star apple flour led to increase in bulk density of blends. There was no significant difference (p >0.05) among the bulk density of samples. Water absorption capacity decreased with the addition of African star apple flour. Among the samples, the blend with 5% African star apple flour recorded the lowest WAC (1.45 ml/g) and the blend with 20%, the highest (1.75 ml/g). The increasing trend observed among the samples is in line with the trend observed in protein contents (Table 2), and suggests that higher protein content (brought about by increasing the amount of ASA) led to increasing water absorption capacity of blends. Water absorption capacity has been reported to indicate the volume of water required to form gruels with suitable consistency [25] and high WAC is important in improving yield and consistency of flour-based products. Hence, the WAC of samples (1.45–1.75%) suggests that the flour blends with 5–20% added African star apple flour, could result in products with improved yield and consistency. Higher oil absorption capacity (OAC) on the other hand was recorded for blends with added African star apple flour compared to the control (Table 3). OAC of samples ranged from 1.25 to 1.90 ml/g with the sample having 5% African star apple flour (AS05) recording the highest OAC and the sample having 20%, the lowest. The decreasing trend is in line with the increasing trend observed in fiber contents (Table 2). Fiber is known to have high water holding capacity and this would normally lead to low oil absorption. The decreasing trend observed among AS05, AS10 and AS20 could therefore be attributed to the increasing fibre contents brought about by increasing amounts of ASA. OAC has been reported to improve flavor and mouthfeel of food products [26]. There was significant difference (P < 0.05) between the OAC of AS05 and that of the other two samples. The swelling capacity of the samples ranged from 0.26 to 0.32 ml/g and were statistically higher (P < 0.05) (with the exception of AS05) than the swelling capacity of the control. The swelling capacity of samples increased with increase in the level of added African star apple flour. The proportion of individual flours in a flour blend is a major factor affecting swelling capacity, and individual flours having high starch content can increase swelling capacity of blends. Hence, the increasing swelling capacity of samples when the proportion of ASA increased suggests that ASA may be relatively rich in starch.

The spread ratio of samples ranged from 19.39 to 20.12 and was lower than that of the control. Spread ratio is a major parameter in achieving a good quality biscuit and high spread ratio has been associated with biscuit quality [27]. There was no statistical difference between the spread ratio of samples and control; or among the samples. Hence, the addition of African star apple flour at the levels studied, did not significantly impact spread ratio. Results in Table 3 also indicate that incorporation of African star apple flour into blends of African yam bean and sweet potato flours, brought about significant changes (P < 0.05) between the control and samples in terms of diameter of biscuits. The thickness of the biscuits ranged from 2.50 to 2.56 mm. The lowest thickness was observed in ASC while the highest was observed in AS10. The diameter of biscuits ranged from 50.12 to 50.97 mm. The lowest value was observed in the control biscuit (ASC) while the highest was observed in AS20. Significant differences (P < 0.05) existed among the biscuit samples and between the samples and control. Changes in the diameter size did not follow any clear trend. Also the weight of the biscuits ranged from 13.26 to 15.77 g, with no clear trend, however, significant differences in weight (P < 0.05) existed among the biscuit samples and between samples and control. Generally, addition of ASA did not significantly improve nor worsen the physical properties of the biscuits.

Sensory properties

Sensory evaluation scores of biscuit as shown in Table 4 indicate that biscuits prepared with 5–20% African star apple flour, recorded similar mean acceptability scores (p > 0.05) for appearance, texture and crispness, as the biscuit produced from blend of African yam bean and sweet potato flour (control). This suggests that the addition of ASA flour at the levels studied did not impact these sensory attributes. Mean scores for color acceptability increased with increasing percentage of African star apple flour, suggesting that the characteristic orange-red color of ASA improved the color of biscuits. The highest score for color acceptability was recorded for biscuit with 20% African star apple (AS20). The sensory score for taste decreased slightly (p > 0.05) with addition of 5% ASA flour, but decreased significantly (p < 0.05) at ASA levels of 10 and 20%, suggesting that the addition of African star apple at levels greater than 5% led to loss in taste. This may be attributed to the sour mouthfeel of African star apple. Similar trend was observed for flavour acceptability where biscuits with 5% ASA were preferred to those with 10 and 20% ASA.

Conclusion

The present work has shown that addition of African star apple (ASA), a neglected tropical fruit, to blends of African yam bean (an underexploited legume) and sweet potato flours, has potential for production of biscuit with acceptable nutritional, physical, and sensory properties. Addition of ASA generally improved the ash, protein, fat, and carbohydrate contents of biscuits. The preferred biscuits in terms of sensory attributes were those made from blend of 50% African yam beans, 45% sweet potatoes and 5% African star apple; and can be adopted as the ideal blend ratio for the production of ASA-fortified biscuit. The present work has also identified biscuit production as potential application for African star apple.
