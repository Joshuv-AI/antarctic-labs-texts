Undergraduate nursing students’ holistic perspectives on escape room teaching in nursing education: a qualitative meta-synthesis
Authors: Ying Xiao, Hongxiu Wang, Huilu Yao, Xiaorong Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNursing education aims to cultivate highly competent nursing professionals, yet traditional teaching methods are insufficient to meet evolving clinical demands. While escape room teaching can help improve nursing students’ comprehensive abilities, research systematically examining students’ holistic perspectives on this educational approach remains scarce. This study aims to explore the experiences, perceived competency transformation, and optimization strategies of undergraduate nursing students in escape room teaching, thereby supporting the optimization and advancement of this instructional model.MethodsA qualitative meta-synthesis was performed following PRISMA and ENTREQ guidelines. 11 databases were searched from inception to March 25, 2026, using keywords including “undergraduate nursing student”, “nursing education”, and “escape room”. From 3,236 initial records, 18 eligible studies were included after screening, and data were analyzed using thematic synthesis.ResultsThree core themes were identified: (1) contradictory experiences, combining immersive engagement and operational difficulties, exploratory motivation and performance anxiety, teamwork belonging and responsibility diffusion; (2) multidimensional competency transformation, including contextualized cognitive integration, task-driven skill internalization, and experiential development of professional literacy; (3) targeted optimization strategies, emphasizing authentic clinical scenarios, clear procedural guidance, personalized team allocation, and systematic assessment and feedback.ConclusionEscape room teaching represents an innovative, experience-rich approach in nursing education that cultivates students’ positive experiences and their perceived growth in cognition, practical skills and professional competence. At the same time, it also brings about mixed emotions and operational dilemmas. Enhancing scenario authenticity, procedural clarity, personalized support and systematic evaluation can better meet students’ subjective learning needs and further promote the innovation of nursing education.Review RegistrationThis study was registered on the PROSPERO platform on November 11, 2025 (Registration number: CRD420251186004).Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

The core goal of nursing education is to cultivate professional nurses who meet the demands of clinical practice and possess developmental potential [1]. In the contemporary era, the rapid advancement of healthcare services and the continuous improvement of clinical nursing quality have placed higher demands on the comprehensive competencies of nursing professionals. Most undergraduate nursing programs are three- to four-year full-time curricula with a structured integration of theoretical instruction and clinical practice. The curriculum is organized in progressive stages. Early phases focus on the delivery of foundational knowledge and hands-on skill training, while later phases include supervised clinical rotations across a variety of departments to support the ongoing translation of theoretical knowledge into clinical competence [2, 3].

Under the traditional model, undergraduate nursing students face dual dilemmas between theoretical learning and clinical practice. At the theoretical level, courses are mostly delivered in fragmented modules, and nursing students find it difficult to systematically integrate discrete knowledge points. Weaknesses in clinical reasoning and critical thinking further prevent them from developing coherent and mature clinical thinking. At the practical level, clinical scenarios are complex and variable with high uncertainty in acute condition changes. Nursing students receive insufficiently in-depth practical training and demonstrate limited early clinical experience, emergency management capacity, and team collaboration skills. These gaps result in poor integration between theory and practice, which fails to meet the demand for high-caliber, comprehensive nursing professionals under the new era [4–6]. Nursing education therefore urgently requires instructional innovation and the promotion of diverse teaching approaches such as participatory, inquiry-based, and immersive methods. Instructional reforms should be carried out to stimulate autonomous learning motivation, strengthen students’ practical competence and critical thinking, and ultimately cultivate professional nursing personnel with solid theoretical foundations who can respond flexibly to complex clinical situations. As an innovative simulation-based teaching method [7], escape room teaching aligns with the aforementioned needs of nursing education reform and provides a new approach for diversified teaching.

Escape room teaching is an instructional approach that takes core nursing knowledge as its guiding thread, directs learners to complete preset theoretical problems or practical operational tasks within a specified time, and enables them to escape the room by solving relevant puzzles [8]. Puzzle-based activities represent the core of this instructional model. Gamified elements must be deeply integrated with instructional objectives, and abstract nursing knowledge must be translated into puzzle tasks consistent with real clinical scenarios. Supporting scenarios and simulated props are used to construct an immersive learning environment. This model overcomes the constraints of traditional one-way didactic teaching. It encourages students to actively explore clues and collaborate with peers during immersive learning, which effectively promotes knowledge integration and the improvement of clinical reasoning ability. Through highly engaging and challenging scenario simulation, the systematic development of core nursing competencies is achieved [9–12]. To date, this emerging instructional model has been relatively widely applied in global nursing education. Its positive effects on knowledge internalization and the enhancement of practical competence have provided an important practical foundation for nursing education reform [13].

However, despite the merits of escape room teaching, its effectiveness is hampered by several challenges, including nursing students’ inefficient knowledge application under pressure [14] and low acceptance of this instructional method [15, 16]. While existing qualitative studies have investigated nursing students’ experiences, individual studies fail to systematically capture their experiences, perceived competency transformation, and needs for instructional improvement. Current systematic reviews focus mainly on quantitative outcomes [17, 18], and qualitative meta-syntheses integrating these dimensions remain scarce. As students’ experiences form the foundational basis, their perceived mechanisms of competency transformation represent the intrinsic outcome of these experiences, and demands for instructional optimization serve as a practical extension thereof, the systematic integration of these interrelated components is essential to refining the escape room teaching model and improving its effectiveness. This study therefore synthesizes qualitative evidence to answer the following research questions: (1) What experiences do undergraduate nursing students gain from escape room-based learning? (2) How do undergraduate nursing students perceive the impact of escape room teaching on their professional competency development? (3) What targeted strategies can optimize the design, implementation and evaluation of escape room teaching in nursing education?

Methods

To deeply explore undergraduate nursing students’ holistic perspectives on participating in escape room teaching, this study adopted a qualitative meta-synthesis approach [19] to systematically identify, appraise, and synthesize relevant qualitative research data. The systematic literature retrieval and screening processes were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines [20]. Two researchers independently extracted relevant information, and thematic synthesis was performed using the three-step approach proposed by Thomas and Harden [21]. For reporting transparency of the qualitative synthesis, this study adhered to the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) Statement [22]. This study was registered on the PROSPERO platform (Registration number: CRD420251186004).

Search methods

Two researchers (YX, HXW) independently conducted systematic searches of Chinese and English databases including PubMed, the Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, Medline, CNKI, the Wanfang Database, the VIP Database and Sinomed. The initial search was performed on October 17, 2025, and an updated search was conducted on March 25, 2026, to identify qualitative studies and mixed-methods studies with qualitative data published from database inception to the updated search date. Search strategies were developed following group discussions, combining free-text terms and subject headings adapted to each database. Terms for “escape room” were formulated based on prior literature and included synonymous expressions to ensure comprehensiveness, and all search terms were validated by a nursing education expert (XRM) with more than a decade of clinical nursing education experience for accuracy and relevance. To avoid missing eligible qualitative studies, no predefined qualitative study filters were used in the initial search and eligible qualitative and mixed-methods studies were identified manually during the screening process. The full search strategy was piloted and refined prior to formal implementation to enhance its sensitivity and precision. Additionally, the snowball method was employed to trace relevant literature, minimizing the risk of missing pertinent studies. Data extraction

Retrieved studies were imported into EndNote X9 for duplicate removal. Irrelevant studies were excluded through title and abstract screening, and full-text reviews were conducted for the remaining ones. Two researchers (YX, HLY) with specialized training in evidence-based methodologies independently extracted data from included studies using a standardized form, with cross-verification of all items after extraction. Disagreements were resolved via consultation with a third researcher (XRM). For mixed-methods studies, original quotations were extracted and synthesized, complemented by the thematic focus and relevant analytical content of the primary studies, to ensure the meta-synthesis was grounded solely in qualitative evidence. Extracted information included authors, country, publication year, research aim, instructional theme, study population, research methods, data collection and analysis approaches, and core qualitative insights.

Quality appraisal

Two professionally trained researchers (YX, HXW) independently used the JBI-QARI [23] to assess literature quality, using yes (Y), no (N), and unclear (U) as the evaluation criteria. If discrepancies existed in their opinions, consensus was reached by consulting a third researcher (XRM).

Data analysis and synthesis

Thomas and Harden’s [21] three-stage thematic synthesis approach was adopted to synthesize and integrate the included literature. This synthesis process was primarily inductive. First, two researchers (XY, HXW) read the included qualitative studies intensively and repeatedly to familiarize themselves with the content, extracting research findings and supporting textual data. Line-by-line coding of participants’ original quotations was conducted, with the consistency between results and data as well as the trustworthiness of research findings independently assessed, and coding consensus reached through discussion. Second, coded text was categorized and synthesized to develop descriptive themes, with sub-themes established where necessary. Finally, similarities across all categories were repeatedly evaluated to generate analytical themes, ensuring no under- or overrepresentation of themes and sub-themes and thus achieving a comprehensive synthesis of research findings. The coding and analysis processes were assisted by NVivo 15 software. The included studies were heterogeneous in teaching themes, settings, and student levels, which reflects the diverse application of escape room teaching in undergraduate nursing education. As qualitative meta-synthesis aims to identify common mechanisms and patterns across contexts [26], all included studies that employed escape rooms as the core instructional strategy in undergraduate nursing education provided a unified conceptual foundation for the integration. Accordingly, a single thematic synthesis approach was adopted to extract common characteristics and multidimensional core meanings across contexts. To validate the credibility of the meta-synthesis findings, the ConQual approach [27] was employed to assess confidence in synthesized themes, with evidence categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low. All themes were initially ranked as “high” and subsequently downgraded based on dependability, reflecting limitations identified in these methodological domains.

Characteristics of included studies

The 18 included qualitative studies involved 922 nursing students, encompassing 5 countries: the United States(n = 8), Spain(n = 5), China(n = 2), Turkey(n = 2), Brazil(n = 1). Published between 2017 and 2025, 13 studies (72.2%) were released in 2022 or later. The methodological approaches primarily included phenomenological studies(n = 6) [14, 15, 39–42] and qualitative descriptive studies(n = 12) [16, 28–38]. Semi-structured interviews were the primary data collection method (n = 11, 61.1%) [14, 15, 28–30, 32, 35, 36, 39–41], of which 7 used semi-structured focus group interviews [28–30, 32, 36, 40, 41]. Additionally, some studies collected data via open-ended survey questions [16, 31, 33–35, 37, 38, 42]. Among the 9 studies [15, 28–30, 32, 35, 39–41] that explicitly reported interview duration, the length ranged from 10 to 60 minutes. The most common duration was 30–40 minutes (n = 5, 55.6%) [15, 28, 29, 39, 41], while 9 studies did not report the duration of data collection. Among the instructional themes in the included studies, clinical nursing-related themes accounted for the largest proportion at 72.2% [15, 16, 28, 30–32, 35–41]. Nursing fundamentals and theoretical education followed at 22.2% [14, 32, 34, 42], whereas nursing core competency development constituted 5.6% [29]. Quality of the included studies

Quality appraisal results showed that 13 studies received 8 “Y” ratings [15, 16, 28–38] and 5 studies received 9 “Y”s [14, 39–42]. All included studies met the predefined criteria, with no exclusions during the appraisal stage. Detailed quality appraisal results are presented in Table 3. Although highly consistent in methodology, research questions/objectives, data collection, analysis and result interpretation, the studies had methodological limitations: some lacked explicit philosophical perspectives [16, 28–38] or ethical approval evidence [15], and none explored the interplay between researchers and the research process. Inter-rater reliability for quality appraisal was 90%-100% with an overall agreement rate of 97%, validating the process reliability. Based on the JBI ConQual framework [27], the three core themes were rated moderate evidence quality owing to a one-level downgrade in credibility. Results of the included studies

This qualitative meta-synthesis included 18 studies. Guided by credibility and dependability principles, three core themes were identified: contradictory experiences in escape room teaching, mechanisms of competency transformation in escape room teaching, and optimization pathways for escape room teaching, each supported by direct participant quotations. Theme 1: Contradictory Experiences in Escape Room Teaching

This theme analyzes students’ diverse experiences in escape room teaching, with the synthesized research findings encompassing four sub-themes, detailed as follows:

Immersive Experience vs. Operational Dilemmas

This sub-theme presents the experiential tension students encountered during escape room teaching, revealing a contradictory state where the advantages of immersive teaching coexist with operational barriers. Students indicated that the immersive design and innovative nature of escape room teaching, compared to traditional teaching methods, better stimulated their learning interest and active participation [15, 30, 34, 42], “Everyone was fully invested. This type of game is quite fun—it sparks a spirit of inquiry, feels less stressful, and I’d love to do it again (laughs)” [15]. Meanwhile, students clearly reported that vague explanations of the teaching process by instructors left them confused about operations, leading to negative emotions such as frustration and unease [16, 30, 40]. “I was very frustrated” [16],“Fear of the unknown” [30].

Exploratory Excitement vs. Performative Anxiety

This sub-theme focuses on two opposing emotional experiences of students in escape room teaching, triggered by the unknown nature of the teaching and the public setting, highlighting the contradiction between the joy of exploration and anxious feelings. Students noted that the unknown and challenging aspects of escape room teaching effectively sparked their interest in exploration and excitement, allowing them to gain a pleasant experience in learning [14, 15, 39], “I prefer escape room teaching because it is full of the unknown, and I enjoy challenging games” [15],“I’m really loving this format, it’s like playing an exciting puzzle game” [39]. However, some students noted the teaching activities took place in public, requiring learning and hands-on work in front of others, which easily triggered their performative anxiety. Particularly, students with social anxiety or low self-esteem felt even more embarrassed during public demonstrations and assessments [14, 32, 40], “I was so nervous that I couldn’t even do up my tunic (first test)” [40].

Collaborative Belonging vs. Responsibility Diffusion

This sub-theme reveals the dual experiences of students during team collaboration in escape room teaching, reflecting the contradictory dynamic between the development of a sense of belonging and the diffusion of responsibility. Students reported that through role-based coordination and mutual support, they formed a tight learning community and gained a strong sense of team belonging and collaborative enjoyment [15, 33, 38], “Everyone’s full engagement helped us become more familiar with each other and fostered greater cohesion” [15], “What I liked the most was the sense of camaraderie that develops when solving and overcoming each clue as a team” [33]. Meanwhile, students also indicated that the absence of leadership or imbalanced role allocation within the team resulted in obvious diffusion of responsibility, which triggered collaborative chaos and negatively affected their participation experience [14, 34, 35, 41], “In a large group, not all participants get equally involved” [14], “… It was really challenging to determine the leader within the team, but it was necessary” [41].

Challenge Motivation vs. Frustration Inhibition

This sub-theme focuses on two opposing experiences of students during escape room challenge completion, highlighting the contradiction between the motivational effect of challenges and the inhibitory effect triggered by frustration. Students reported that escape room challenges effectively boosted their learning motivation and brought them a sense of accomplishment when solving puzzles and completing clinical tasks, thereby improving their professional self-efficacy and confidence in nursing practice [28, 36, 41], “Yeah, I feel like it made us feel more confident in what we knew” [28], “… I believe I can definitely distinguish IAD from other wounds” [41]. However, some students pointed out that repeated unsuccessful attempts to pass challenges led to intense frustration, which suppressed learning initiative and resulted in negative experiences [31, 33, 37], “What I liked the least was that, despite all the competitiveness we went through, we lost” [33].

In summary, students’ experiences in escape room teaching exhibit contradictory characteristics, and four types of dual experiential tensions collectively constitute the core learning perceptions under this instructional model.

Theme 2: Mechanisms of Competency Transformation in Escape Room Teaching

This theme analyzes students’ perceived mechanisms of competency transformation in escape room teaching. The synthesized findings include three sub-themes, as detailed below:

Contextual Cognitive Transformation

This sub-theme focuses on the cognitive impact of contextual design, explaining the dual shifts in students’ cognitive patterns and learning attitudes. Students reported that escape room teaching connects theory and practice through contextual scenario design, shifting their cognitive mode from rote memorization to clinical contextual reasoning. They recognized that this transformation helps to address the cognitive limitations of traditional teaching [15, 30, 36], “We need to integrate theory with practice. For example, preparing skin test solutions requires thinking on the spot, and calculating oxygen flow rates too. Usually, I just memorize textbooks, but this time I learned to calculate based on actual conditions, which is also how it will be when I enter clinical practice later” [15], “You can read all day about it, but when you go in the actual room and see it … you don’t have to memorize everything that’s on pages of notes” [30]. Meanwhile, students believed that contextual design guided a meaningful shift, freeing them from the constraint of studying for exams and fostering a sense of clinical responsibility and cautiousness [15, 35, 40], “I didn’t carefully verify the medical orders in the final level (frustrated). The patient clearly had hypoglycemia, but I failed to detect the error in the orders and administered normal saline. If this happened in clinical practice, the consequences would be serious. From now on, I need to be more cautious and learn to handle situations flexibly based on patients’ conditions” [15].

Task-Driven Skill Internalization

This sub-theme focuses on the impact of contextualized practical tasks on students’ skill development, illustrating the process through which students perceive the internalization of professional nursing skills and the improvement of their application abilities during task engagement. Students reported that escape room teaching, supported by contextualized practical tasks, enabled them to experience the gradual transformation of nursing skills from external learning to internal application. First, students noted that the hands-on tasks in the escape room provided opportunities to practice and refine their skills, helping to consolidate their nursing technical proficiency [15, 32, 39], “The one that affected me the most was withdrawing medication from the ampoule because I had never broken an ampoule before” [32]. Second, students believed that clinical scenario design guided them to become familiar with and master standardized emergency protocols, and to develop corresponding response strategies in simulated high-stress environments, thereby enhancing their emergency response and specialized nursing capabilities [28, 35, 37], “I think being in that situation it was high stress, and of course, obviously this isn’t a real life patient, but I think we can imagine that when we are in that situation, that it’s only going to be more stressful and more time sensitive because this isn’t just an escape room, the patient’s hemorrhaging and you’re trying to save them …” [35].

Experiential Construction of Professionalism

This sub-theme focuses on the role of experiential practice in the development of students’ professional literacy, explaining the pathways through which students perceive the cultivation and internalization of professional qualities during immersive practice. First, students reported that participation in simulated tasks strengthened their teamwork abilities, and some noted the emergence of their leadership skills during task progression [15, 29, 35], “Teamwork skills were improved, because in clinical practice, no one works alone—we all need to collaborate as a team”[15], “When you begin to understand the role of nurses during undergraduate degree, leadership even appears as a necessary skill, but it is very difficult to learn this in theory. With the Escape, it seems like it made more sense” [29]. Second, students believed that the multidimensional clinical demands embedded in authentic scenarios fostered their awareness of interprofessional collaboration [30], “our team was full of nurses … I already know what they’re thinking … they’re probably thinking what I’m thinking”, “With social work and everyone … that way we get the team experience” [30]. Finally, through simulated nurse-patient interaction scenarios, students perceived the shaping and enhancement of their humanistic care capabilities and sense of responsibility [15, 31, 35], “I used to know about humanistic care because teachers always talked about it, but when patients were in discomfort, I would only say ‘Don’t worry, it’s fine.’ Looking back, that kind of comfort was so useless (laughs), but I learned better this time” [15], “In practice, I’m not going to do something wrong for the patient and they deserve the best care, so I’m going to figure out the best care for them” [35].

In summary, based on students’ experiences, escape room teaching promotes competency development among nursing students through multidimensional transformation pathways.

Theme 3: Optimization Pathways for Escape Room Teaching

This theme examines optimization pathways for escape room teaching in nursing education. Targeted improvement strategies are synthesized based on students’ experiences and feedback, with the research findings encompassing four sub-themes as detailed below:

Authentic Construction of Clinical Scenarios

This sub-theme focuses on the optimization of clinical scenario development, identifying the impact of scenario authenticity on learning experiences and key improvement priorities based on student feedback. Students regarded the authenticity and relevance of clinical scenarios as prerequisites for maintaining continuous engagement and active learning [30, 31, 42], “It gave that in-home type of feel even though it was on campus … it really looks like an actual home, so you could really get into the activity” [30]. Students also reported that insufficient scenario fidelity and a lack of functional props easily distracted their attention and diminished the effectiveness of immersive learning [15, 30, 39], “If there are similar evaluations in the future, I hope the scene setup can feel more realistic” [39]. In line with student feedback, instructional design can refine scenario details to match clinical practice, using contextually appropriate physical props and environmental arrangements to enhance realism and relevance. For emergency scenarios, role-playing, on-site object placement, and visual effects can be employed to create a tense atmosphere and further strengthen scenario authenticity [15, 32], “For the scenario where someone gets into a car accident while buying groceries, you could have a person lying on the ground, place some vegetables and other items nearby, and use paint to create a pool of blood to make it more authentic. That way, we would feel more nervous and urgent” [15].

Clear Guidance for Standardized Procedures

This sub-theme focuses on the optimization of teaching processes and guideline design. Drawing on student feedback, it clarifies the importance of clear, systematic process guidelines for improving instructional effectiveness and articulates students’ specific needs for such guidance. To address issues including ambiguous processes and inadequate guidance during teaching, students recommended that a complete task workflow be clearly defined before participation. Instructors should explain activity rules and learning objectives in detail and provide written guidance materials to prevent unclear instructions from undermining learning effectiveness [14, 16, 30], “We need printouts of the instructions. We missed the first puzzle because we didn’t realize the password was in all caps”[16], “We didn’t know if we needed to count each thing individually or if we count all the rugs as one … that was kind of confusing because our number was higher than it was supposed to be”[30]. In addition, students suggested introducing phased guidance to help them gradually adapt to the gamified learning model [30], “Without doing this first, I would be a little frazzled” [30].

Personalized Adaptation of Team Role Assignment

This sub-theme focuses on the optimization of team role allocation models, articulating students’ core demands for orderly collaborative division of labor and personalized adaptive arrangements. Students stated that clear role assignments were essential during team task implementation to reduce chaos caused by unstructured collaboration [29, 35, 40], “Wow, we had no planning, chaos” [29], “Moments where it’s like, is this right? Is this right? Or we just don’t know. I think we all had trouble like who should lead this? Like should there be a leader here? Can somebody take control? I think, because we all kind of stayed pretty respectful of each other and like equal at all times. So like there wasn’t a lot of delegating I think which could have been helpful at certain points” [35]. Meanwhile, students believed that role allocation should be humanely adjusted according to individual traits, taking into account different students’ adaptability and psychological state to avoid diminished participation motivation due to poor role matching [14, 15, 40], “Gamified activities are more challenging and require stronger abilities. As someone with social anxiety, I won’t perform well—I can’t handle it” [15].

Systematic Improvement of Evaluation and Feedback

This sub-theme focuses on the optimization of the evaluation and feedback system, articulating students’ core demands for a fair and systematic evaluative feedback mechanism. Students suggested that for the assessment of individual performance, synchronous scoring by multiple instructors based on unified criteria could replace the single-instructor rating model. This would reduce subjective bias, account for differences in individual contributions within teams, and avoid unfair overall scores caused by uneven individual effort [15], “Just like in a competition, having several instructors grade together would make it fairer” [15]. Meanwhile, students proposed that systematic debriefs should be conducted promptly after escape room teaching activities to identify problems and summarize experiences. Such debriefs would not only support the optimization of subsequent instruction but also help students recognize gaps in their cognition and practice, thereby deepening learning outcomes [39], “If the teacher provides a comprehensive evaluation post-assessment, we’ll get insights into our understanding and performance problems” [39].

In summary, based on student feedback, the optimization pathways proposed for the four key areas of scenario design, process guidance, team role assignment, and evaluation and feedback will facilitate the standardized implementation of escape room teaching in nursing education.

Discussion

This study systematically investigates escape room teaching in undergraduate nursing education, focusing on student experiences, competency transformation mechanisms and instructional optimization pathways. It identifies four types of contradictory student experiential tensions, three core pathways for nursing professional competency development, and four key elements for teaching optimization. The findings deepen the understanding of this instructional model, offering theoretical support and practical references for its evidence-based application. The integrated conclusions of this review hold moderate credibility and are applicable to teaching practice.

Theme 1 systematically identifies four contradictory experiential tensions in escape room teaching for undergraduate nursing education, clearly reflecting the complex emotions and conflicting experiences of students. The contradiction between immersive engagement and operational dilemmas essentially reveals nursing students’ dual demands for enjoyable and standardized instruction. While students value the appeal of immersive design [15, 35], the operational challenges demonstrate that educational innovation must not disregard procedural standards. In future practice, instructors should develop immersive scenarios while clarifying protocols and operational guidelines, such as providing teaching briefs [38, 43] prior to the activity, to balance enjoyment and standardization and prevent operational confusion from undermining learning motivation [16, 30, 31]. The opposition between exploratory excitement and performative anxiety highlights an imbalance between challenge and inclusivity in nursing education. Students’ enthusiasm for unknown challenges underscores the value of task-driven learning [39], suggesting that appropriate challenges can free students from rote learning. However, performative anxiety triggered by public settings indicates that instructional design should accommodate individual psychological differences and adopt personalized participation strategies. The tension between collaborative belonging and responsibility diffusion represents a core challenge in team-based nursing education. The sense of belonging and cohesion derived from collaboration confirms that escape room teamwork aligns well with the goal of cultivating collaborative competence in nursing education [29, 35, 41]. Nevertheless, imbalanced division of labor and diffusion of responsibility reduce collaboration to a formality, impairing student engagement and collaborative skill development [14]. The conflict between challenge-induced motivation and frustration-related inhibition emphasizes the importance of regulating instructional difficulty. Task progression can strengthen students’ professional self-efficacy [29], whereas excessive challenges easily lead to frustration [32]. Grounded in cognitive load theory [44], future instruction should scientifically calibrate task difficulty, design stepped challenges aligned with curriculum standards, and provide timely guidance to avoid negative learning experiences caused by cognitive overload.

Theme 2 systematically elucidates the intrinsic processes and subjective mechanisms through which escape room teaching promotes professional competency transformation in nursing students. Contextualized cognitive transformation reveals the core value of this instructional model in fostering critical thinking and reshaping cognitive patterns among nursing students [15, 30], helping alleviate the disconnection between theory and practice in traditional nursing education [32]. The study indicates that escape room teaching facilitates students’ shift from rote memorization to clinical critical thinking and from exam-oriented learning to clinical needs-oriented learning, which is consistent with experiential learning theory [45]. In addition, the intrinsic learning enjoyment stimulated by escape room teaching supports long-term knowledge retention and improves memory efficiency and accuracy [46, 47], providing strong support for the positive transformation of nursing students’ cognitive models. Future nursing education should design contextual tasks based on real clinical problems, reduce emphasis on knowledge memorization, and connect theory with practice through clinical decision-making guidance to help nursing students develop clinically appropriate critical thinking. Task-driven skill internalization demonstrates the practical significance of learning-by-doing in nursing skill development, clarifying the transformation from explicit skill learning to implicit application, while highlighting the role of high-pressure simulated scenarios in enhancing emergency response capabilities [35]. Future instruction should employ high-fidelity clinical tasks to strengthen practical training and incorporate diversified emergency scenarios, helping nursing students consolidate operational standards, establish emergency response frameworks, and address the limited scenario diversity of traditional skill training. Experiential professional competency development reflects the unique advantage of this teaching model in nurturing soft professional skills, enabling the endogenous growth of collaborative abilities [28, 30], humanistic care, and sense of responsibility [15, 35] beyond the constraints of theoretical lectures. This underscores the importance of experiential teaching in professional competency cultivation. Future educators may optimize scenario design by integrating interprofessional collaboration and concrete nurse-patient interaction modules, further exploring the differential effects of varied scenarios on nursing students’ competency development to enhance the precision of professional education.

Theme 3 systematically outlines the optimization pathways for escape room teaching in nursing education, targeting instructional weaknesses based on nursing students’ core needs and practical challenges to facilitate the standardized and refined implementation of this model. The authentic construction of clinical scenarios reflects the inherent relationship between clinical fidelity and learning immersion. Authentic scenarios can significantly enhance learning engagement [30], which aligns with relevant perspectives on multisensory stimulus-based scenario design [39]. Future teaching should optimize detailed configurations based on real clinical practice, employ appropriate physical props, and strengthen environmental immersion for emergency scenarios. Low-cost and reusable props may be explored, alongside a resource-sharing network, to overcome limitations of time, space, and materials [14, 48], thereby fully leveraging the supportive role of scenarios in learning experience and competency development. Clear guidance for procedural standardization is essential for the orderly delivery of gamified teaching. In response to student feedback about ambiguous procedures and insufficient guidance [16, 30], future instruction should establish a systematic procedural framework. Task milestones, activity rules, and learning objectives should be clearly communicated in advance through written handbooks and pre-session briefings, accompanied by stepped, progressive guidance to help nursing students adapt gradually to the gamified format, prevent learning disorientation, and ensure instructional effectiveness. Personalized team role allocation is key to improving collaborative efficiency and student engagement. To resolve issues of unbalanced roles and poor suitability reported by students, future teaching should adopt a scientific team-building and role-assignment mechanism that clarifies individual duties and team leadership responsibilities [28, 35]. Personalized role arrangements should be made according to students’ competencies, strengths, and psychological conditions, balancing collaborative efficiency and individual needs to ensure deep participation from every student. Systematic improvement of evaluation and feedback is critical to ensuring assessment fairness and enhancing formative guidance, as nursing students have high demands for equitable evaluation and timely feedback [15, 39]. Teaching may involve standardized scoring by multiple instructors, considering both team and individual performance. Meanwhile, timely debriefing and reflective summaries should be conducted to inform instructional iteration, help students identify their shortcomings, and deepen learning outcomes.

However, the included studies have several methodological limitations. First, none of the studies sufficiently reflected on the interaction between researchers and the research process, and potential biases were not explicitly addressed. This reduced the interpretive validity of individual studies and compromised the credibility of experiential findings. Second, some studies failed to clarify the alignment between philosophical perspectives and research methods [16, 28–38], resulting in a lack of consistent standards across studies, which impeded cross-study comparison and limited the rigor of the synthesized conclusions. Third, one study did not provide documentation of ethical approval [15], weakening the ethical rigor of the evidence base and affecting the generalizability and credibility of the synthesized findings. Overall, these limitations constrained the depth and integrity of the present evidence synthesis at methodological, epistemological, and ethical levels, and also hindered cross-study comparison and theoretical induction. Future empirical research on escape room teaching in nursing education should enhance methodological congruence, improve the transparency of researcher reflexivity, standardize the disclosure of ethical approval, and further advance the quality of evidence.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant objective consideration. First, the majority of included studies were concentrated in European and American regions, resulting in a relatively narrow geographical distribution; thus, the generalizability and global representativeness of the findings require further validation. Second, some heterogeneity existed in the teaching contexts across the included studies, which may influence the stability of the conclusions. Furthermore, this review only incorporated peer-reviewed literature published in Chinese and English, with no gray literature included, which entails a certain risk of language bias and publication bias.

Conclusion

This study employed qualitative meta-synthesis to systematically examine the application status and core characteristics of escape room-based teaching in undergraduate nursing education, with a particular focus on student experiences, mechanisms of competency transformation, and directions for instructional optimization. Rather than conducting quantitative evaluations of teaching effectiveness, the research prioritized students’ subjective perceptions and practical needs, thereby enriching the research dimensions of gamified learning in nursing education. The findings indicate that escape room pedagogy can facilitate cognitive restructuring, skill enhancement, and professional development among nursing students by stimulating learning interest and increasing engagement. However, the approach also faces challenges such as insufficient scenario authenticity, ambiguous instructional workflows, and unclear team role delineation, which compromise both teaching efficacy and student experience. Future efforts should center on nursing students’ experiences and needs to refine instructional processes, clarify collaborative team structures, and strike a balance between educational enjoyment and pedagogical rigor. By aligning instructional design more closely with clinical realities, escape room teaching can realize its educational potential and genuinely serve the cultivation of nursing talent, thereby providing robust support for the high-quality development of undergraduate nursing education.
