Effects of combined ultrasonic and microwave pretreatments on vacuum freeze-drying efficiency and quality of giant freshwater prawn (Macrobrachium rosenbergii)
Authors: Ruili Yuan, Zhiyu Xiong, Li Yuan, Wengang Jin, Ling Jiang, Ruichang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe trade-off between drying efficiency and product quality poses a major challenge in dried prawn processing. Vacuum freeze-drying (VFD) effectively retains the nutritional and sensory quality of aquatic products, yet its industrial application is restricted by high energy consumption. To address this, we investigated the individual and combined effects of ultrasound (US) and microwave (MW) pretreatment on the drying kinetics, energy consumption and comprehensive quality of prawn during VFD. Results showed MW pretreatment alone significantly reduced energy consumption but caused notable texture hardening and shrinkage, while US significantly improved tissue structure and increased rehydration rate. Combined US-MW delivered 35.33% energy reduction, avoiding MW-induced quality deterioration via forming uniform myofibrillar networks and increasing flexible β-turns. Drying kinetics fitted well to the Midilli model (R2 > 0.998), offering a reliable industrial process reference. This study provides a practical strategy to balance efficiency and quality for prawn and other aquatic products.Keywords: Giant freshwater prawn, Ultrasound, Microwave, Vacuum freeze-drying, Energy consumption, Quality

Introduction

Giant freshwater prawn (Macrobrachium rosenbergii), renowned as the ‘King of Freshwater Prawns’, is distinguished by its substantial size and high nutritional value, representing a premium aquatic resource rich in protein and low in fat (Sumi et al., 2025). According to the China Fishery Statistical Yearbook, China's aquaculture output of this species reached 245,655 tons in 2024 (Bureau of Fisheries and Aquaculture Administration, 2025), accounting for 60% of the global total. This makes China the world's largest producer of giant freshwater prawns. However, the supply of fresh prawns is seasonal, primarily available from July to November. Consequently, they are commonly processed into frozen, peeled prawns (prawn meat) to meet year-round market demand. Nonetheless, conventional frozen storage and transportation entail high costs, and prolonged frozen storage often leads to quality degradation due to repeated freeze-thaw cycles (Xu et al., 2024).

Dehydration is a widely employed preservation method for aquatic products, which operates on the principle of moisture removal to inhibit microbial growth and chemical reactions, thereby facilitating storage and transport (Shi et al., 2024). The dehydration of aquatic products typically encompasses natural sun-drying, hot air drying, heat pump drying, microwave drying, and vacuum freeze drying. Hot air drying stands as the most prevalent method among these techniques, operating on the principle of forced hot air convection to remove moisture. This technology offers advantages such as low equipment cost and simple operation. However, the heat transfer characteristics of hot air drying may cause surface hardening of prawn meat, hindering the migration of internal moisture (Cao et al., 2021). This not only prolongs the drying cycle but also severely impairs product quality. Driven by the demand for high-quality dried products, vacuum freeze-drying (VFD) has attracted considerable attention for its ability to maximally preserve the nutritional components and porous structure of aquatic products (Li et al., 2024). However, VFD still faces many challenges in its application in aquatic products, especially in prawn. Fresh prawn has a high moisture content, a dense muscle structure and a high protein content. Direct VFD may result in a longer drying period and higher energy consumption (Lee et al., 2024). Chang and Chen (2024) reached a similar conclusion in their drying study on krill, reporting that VFD faces challenges such as high energy consumption and high processing costs, and further proposed that combining microwave with VFD can significantly shorten the VFD time. Therefore, developing strategies to accelerate the VFD process for prawns while minimizing adverse effects on prawn quality is of paramount industrial and scientific importance.

Microwave (MW) and ultrasonic (US) pretreatment are currently well-established effective methods for improving the efficiency of VFD (Chang & Chen, 2024; Chen et al., 2023; Mondal et al., 2025). MW utilizes the high-frequency vibration of water molecules to generate volumetric heating, achieving rapid and efficient dehumidification (Wang et al., 2019). For example, Chen et al. (2023) demonstrated that MW assisted VFD of pineapples reduced energy consumption by 34.5% without significant quality loss compared to standalone VFD. However, unlike carbohydrate-dominant plant-based matrices, the thermal effect of MW may induce irreversible myofibrillar protein coagulation and muscle hardening in the muscle tissue of protein-rich giant freshwater prawns. As reported by Zhang, Shi, et al. (2024), the rapid heating of MW leads to myofibrillar denaturation and contraction, excessive muscle moisture loss, and ultimately results in the formation of a dense and hardened structure in the muscle tissue. In contrast, non-thermal technology US can produce rapid compression and expansion cycles, forming microchannels, promoting water migration and enhancing mass transfer efficiency (Li et al., 2017) and significantly improving the rehydration rate of dried products (Mondal et al., 2025). Specifically, Li et al. (2023) reported that US pretreatment could effectively regulate the myofibrillar protein structure of crustaceans and change the psize and aggregation degree of proteins. In addition, ultrasonic cavitation has been proven to disrupt the formation of dense protein aggregates, produce dispersed and disordered small protein aggregates, and further form a loose three-dimensional network structure (Sun et al., 2023). These advantages make the US a potential supplement to MW pretreatment, which can make up for the quality deterioration caused by single MW treatment in aquatic products. However, the synergistic effects of combined US-MW pretreatment on VFD of protein-rich aquatic products with respect to balancing energy savings and quality preservation have not been systematically investigated.

Accordingly, this study evaluated the individual and combined effects of US and MW pretreatments on the drying kinetics, energy consumption, and quality attributes of VFD giant freshwater prawns. Given the remarkable energy-saving advantages of MW pretreatment, this work combined it with US pretreatment, expecting that US would mitigate the detrimental effects of MW on product quality. The aim was to achieve a balance between processing efficiency and product quality, thereby enhancing the economic value of processed prawns.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Frozen meat of the giant freshwater prawn used in this experiment was obtained from Jiangsu Qiwu Aquatic Development Co., Ltd. (Yangzhou, China). The average weight of individual prawn meat samples was 13.01 ± 1.58 g, and the prawns were cultivated from April to October 2024, covering 4–5 harvest cycles. Frozen prawn meat (stored at −18 °C for less than 3 months) was used to align with industrial practices, with thawing at 4 °C for 12 h (Section 2.3) to minimize ice crystal-induced muscle damage. The initial moisture content of the samples (on a wet basis) was 85.34 ± 1.01%.

Food-grade salt was purchased from a local supermarket (Zhenjiang, China). HPLC-grade methanol was supplied by TEDIA Company (Ohio, USA). Sulfosalicylic acid and phosphoric acid were obtained from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Nucleotide standards were procured from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals were of analytical grade and acquired from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Eutectic point determination

The eutectic point of the prawn meat was established using Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) according to the method described by Zhang, Wang, et al. (2024). Measurements were carried out on a DSC3500 instrument (NETZSCH GmbH, Germany). Briefly, approximately 10 mg of finely minced prawn meat was accurately weighed into an aluminum sample pan, which was then hermetically sealed. The thermal program was set as follows: starting at 25 °C, cooling down to −30 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and maintaining this temperature for 5 min, followed by reheating to 25 °C at the same rate. The obtained thermograms were analyzed using the proprietary Proteus Analysis software, and the eutectic point was identified based on the characteristic thermal events observed on the DSC curve.

Sample treatment

Frozen prawn meat was thawed at 4 °C for 12 h. Specimens exhibiting physical damage or melanosis were discarded. The thawed prawn meat was blanched in a 100 °C water bath for 2 min, followed by cooling to room temperature with running tap water. This blanching step, which conforms to industrial processing standards for ready-to-eat prawn products, was applied to inactivate endogenous enzymes and reduce the microbial load. Subsequently, the prawns were transferred into polyethylene bags containing a 1.5% (w/v) NaCl solution for salt pretreatment at 25 °C, with a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:4 (w/v). Six prawns were allocated to each bag. The salt pretreatment was performed to impart flavor to the prawns and achieve osmotic dehydration. Salt uptake was measured using a salinometer (AZ8371, Hengxin Industrial Co., Ltd., China).

The samples were subjected to the following pretreatment protocols:

(1) Microwave pretreatment: After salting for 30 min, the packed samples were blotted dry on the surface, and then treated in a tunnel microwave dryer (TEBMD-2 M, Nanjing Hurui Microwave Technology Development Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) at 280 W for 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 min. After treatment, samples were subjected to the VFD procedure described below. The optimal microwave condition was selected based on quantifiable criteria: specific energy consumption (SEC) reduction ≥20% (industrial standard), changes in rehydration rate and whiteness within ±10% of control freeze-dried group (CF), relative increase in shrinkage rate ≤50%, and crispness not significantly lower than that of CF (P < 0.05). The optimal group was designated as the microwave-freeze-dried (MF) group.

(2) Ultrasound pretreatment: Samples in sealed bags were treated in a multi-frequency ultrasonic cleaner (WM-300GVDE/3, Shanghai Weimi Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at a fixed frequency of 40 kHz. Actual output power levels (60, 90, 120 W) and salting durations (15, 30, 45 min) were applied simultaneously with precise temperature control at 25.0 ± 0.5 °C. After treatment, samples were subjected to the VFD procedure described below. The optimal ultrasound condition was determined by applying the following constraints focused on quality improvement: a relative increase in shrinkage rate ≤5% and an increase in hardness ≤50%. Among the treatments satisfying these constraints, the one with the highest rehydration rate was selected. This optimal group was designated as the ultrasonic freeze-drying (UF) group.

(3) Combined pretreatment: Following optimal US pretreatment, samples were immediately given optimal microwave pretreatment and then dried using the VFD procedure described below. This group was designated as the ultrasonic-microwave freeze-drying (UMF) group.

(4) Control freeze-dried group (CF): After salting for 30 min, the packed samples were directly subjected to the VFD procedure described below.

Vacuum freeze-drying

All pretreated samples were pre-frozen at −40 °C for 5 h until the core temperature fell below the eutectic point. Drying was performed in a vacuum freeze dryer (FD-1 A-50E, Beijing Boyikang Experimental Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) at a chamber pressure of 10 Pa and a condenser temperature of −50 °C. All pretreated samples were spread evenly on the shelves of the freeze dryer and placed in the same position for each batch as much as possible. Periodic weighing was carried out to determine the moisture content, and drying was terminated when the moisture content reached ≤10% (wet basis).

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

The water distribution in pretreated prawn meat samples (Control, MW, US, and US-MW groups) before VFD was determined using a desktop pulsed LF-NMR analyzer (NMI20-060 V-I, Numaerk Electric Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) referring to the method of Xu et al. (2024). The CPMG (Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill) pulse sequence was applied with the following operating parameters: interval time of continuous scanning = 10,000 ms, echo time = 0.079 ms, number of echoes = 8000 times, number of scans = 4 times, and measurement temperature maintained at 30 ± 1 °C. The transverse relaxation time (T2) spectra were obtained by multi-exponential fitting of the CPMG decay curves, and the peak areas corresponding to different water fractions were analyzed to characterize water distribution in samples. For each biological replicate, three technical replicate measurements were performed, and the averaged results were used. Three independent biological replicates were conducted for each treatment.

Raman spectroscopy

The secondary structure of prawn proteins was analyzed following the protocol of Xu et al. (2025), using a laser Raman microscope (DXR, Thermo Co., USA). An appropriate amount of freeze-dried prawn powder was taken, and for each sample, two different positions were selected to collect Raman spectra. The obtained Raman spectra in the amide I region (1600–1700 cm−1) were baseline-corrected, deconvoluted, and fitted using PeakFit 4.12 software. The relative content of each secondary structure, including α-helix (1650–1660 cm−1), β-sheet (1600–1640 cm−1), β-turn (1660–1700 cm−1), and random coil (1640–1650 cm−1), was quantified based on the calculated area of the corresponding characteristic peaks.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Microstructural analysis was conducted using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; Model JSM-7800F, JEOL, Japan). A transverse section (approximately 1 mm thick) of the second abdominal segment of the giant freshwater prawn was cut following the protocol of Zhang, Wang, et al. (2024). The sections were freeze-dried, sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold, and observed at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. For each prawn, at least five different areas were imaged to ensure representativeness of the microstructure.

Analysis of volatile flavor compounds

Volatile organic compounds were characterized using an electronic nose (PEN3, Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany) with reference to the methodology of Tian et al. (2025). Briefly, 2.0 g of freeze-dried prawn sample was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial and equilibrated at 40 °C for 10 min prior to analysis. The measurement procedure was as follows: sample preparation time, 5 s; measurement time, 120 s; and sensor flushing time, 150 s.

Determination of free amino acid content

Free amino acids (FAAs) were determined according to Zhang et al. (2023) with minor modifications. Briefly, 1.5 g of VFD prawn powder was homogenized in 20 mL of 3% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid at 10,000 r/min for 2 min (T25 Ultra-Turrax, IKA, Staufen, Germany). After centrifugation at 10,000 ×g and 4 °C for 15 min (Centrifuge 5810 R, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and quantified using an automated amino acid analyzer (S—433D, Sykam, Eresing, Germany).

Determination of 5′-nucleotides content

The measurement of flavor nucleotides in freeze-dried prawn, including cytidine 5′-monophosphate (CMP), adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP), uridine 5′-monophosphate (UMP), guanosine 5′-monophosphate (GMP), and inosine 5′-monophosphate (IMP), was conducted following the method described by Tian et al. (2025). The processed samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and then analyzed via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, LC-20AD, Shimadzu, Japan) at 254 nm.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory analysis was conducted by a trained panel based on the method of Zheng et al. (2025) with minor revisions. Twenty panelists (10 males and 10 females, aged 22–26 years) were recruited to assess the freeze-dried prawn samples based on five key attributes: appearance, color, aroma, taste, and texture. Samples from each treatment group were presented in a randomized order and labeled with random three-digit codes to avoid bias. Panelists were instructed to cleanse their palates with purified water between samples and to wait until any residual taste had dissipated before evaluating the next sample. Detailed scoring criteria are provided in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Three independent biological replicates were performed for each treatment. Thus, for each treatment, data were obtained from three biological replicates (n = 3). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in IBM SPSS Statistics 27 software, with significance set at P < 0.05. Data visualization and graph plotting were conducted using Origin 2021b, and drying model fitting was implemented in MATLAB 2021b.

Results and discussion

Eutectic point

The thermal transition associated with water crystallization in the prawn meat was characterized by DSC. The resulting thermogram (Fig. 1A) showed a distinct exothermic peak, indicating the phase transition temperature. The measured eutectic point had a mean value of −13.34 °C, which was adopted as the key reference for determining the pre-freezing temperature. To ensure complete solidification and avoid structural defects or sublimation issues caused by residual liquid water, the pre-freezing temperature is generally set 10–20 °C below the eutectic point (Wang et al., 2022). Accordingly, a pre-freezing temperature of −40 °C was adopted in this study. This temperature is situated substantially below the highest recorded eutectic value, thus ensuring the unequivocal and complete freezing of the prawn meat and establishing a robust foundation for the ensuing freeze-drying operation.

Optimization of physical processing technology

Effect of MW pretreatment on the quality and energy consumption of VFD prawns

The impact of MW pretreatment time on both energy efficiency and product quality attributes of freeze-dried prawns is summarized in Table S3. Compared to the CF group, MW pretreatment significantly reduced (P < 0.05) the SEC from 35.82 kW·h/kg to 21.03 kW·h/kg, representing a substantial 41.29% reduction, which underscores the notable efficacy of MW in enhancing the energy efficiency of the VFD process. However, with MW time increasing from 0 to 2.5 min, the shrinkage rate of the prawn increased from 40.99% to 84.08% (P < 0.05), the rehydration ratio decreased from 365.37% to 276.47% (P < 0.05), and whiteness declined from 88.56 to 83.38 (P < 0.05), respectively. As observed from the appearance status in Table S3, excessive microwave exposure caused severe volume shrinkage, surface hardening and poor morphological integrity of prawn samples. The increase in shrinkage and decrease in rehydration capacity typically reflect irreversible deterioration of the tissue structure during drying, implying that prolonged MW treatment may trigger myofibrillar protein contraction and dense tissue formation, thereby reducing the internal pore structure and impairing the rehydration properties and appearance of the final product (Yan et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2025).

In terms of texture properties, with extended MW time, hardness, chewiness, and compactness increased significantly from 716.27 g, 3610.70 g, and 17.95 g to 5048.86 g, 28035.74 g, and 53.64 g (P < 0.05), respectively. This change may be related to intense water evaporation induced by microwave: the rapid internal vaporization and migration of water caused myofibrillar contraction and reduced intercellular space, ultimately resulting in a denser structure upon drying (Yan et al., 2021). Crispness first increased and then decreased, peaking at 1 min (17.67) and sharply declining to 3.33 at 2.5 min. This indicates that while moderate MW treatment can enhance crispness, excessive treatment may promote excessive densification of muscle tissue due to water loss, thereby causing texture hardening and loss of crispness (Fan et al., 2024).

In accordance with the quantifiable screening criteria established in Section 2.3, the 1.5 min MW treatment achieved a 31.43% reduction in SEC, with changes in rehydration rate and whiteness of less than 10%, and a marked increase in crispness (by 104%). Although longer MW times provided further energy savings, they led to a significant increase in shrinkage rate (relative increase exceeding 50%). Therefore, we selected 1.5 min as the optimal MW pretreatment time, and the corresponding group was designated as the MF group.

Effect of US pretreatment on the quality of VFD prawn

The effects of US pretreatment on product quality are compiled in Table S4. Notably, no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed in either shrinkage or whiteness between the CF group and most of the US-treated groups. In contrast, the rehydration ratio was markedly enhanced (P < 0.05) under all ultrasonic conditions. The most pronounced improvements were achieved with treatments at 90 W for 30 min and 90 W for 45 min, yielding rehydration ratios of 412% and 419%, respectively. This enhancement may be attributable to the formation of microchannels within the tissue matrix caused by ultrasonic cavitation. During subsequent pre-freezing, the water contained within these channels forms ice crystals, leaving behind a porous microstructure upon sublimation. This network of pores facilitates capillary action during rehydration, thereby significantly improving the water reabsorption capacity (Li et al., 2017).

Regarding textural properties, a distinct trend was observed whereby hardness and chewiness initially decreased with increasing ultrasonic time and power, reaching a minimum, and then increased again at higher intensities or longer durations. This non-linear response suggests that moderate ultrasonic energy may disrupt and loosen the myofibrillar structure, leading to a tenderizing effect (Bao et al., 2022). However, more intense or prolonged sonication fragments the myofibrils that have already been denatured during blanching. These protein fragments might expose more reactive sites that facilitate aggregation and cross-linking, thereby forming a denser and more compact protein network (Gao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). However, the specific mechanisms require further investigation.

Among all treatments, the 90 W/30 min and 90 W/45 min treatments satisfied the predefined constraints and achieved the highest rehydration rates (412% and 419%, respectively), with no significant difference between them. Therefore, to avoid unnecessary energy input, the 90 W/30 min treatment was determined as the optimal ultrasonic condition, and the corresponding group was designated as the UF group. The aforementioned results found that while suitable MW pretreatment significantly reduced the overall energy consumption of freeze-dried prawn (P < 0.05), excessive treatment duration impaired product quality by causing texture deterioration, increased shrinkage, and reduced rehydration. In comparison, US treatment improved texture properties, significantly increased rehydration ratio, and moderately reduced hardness.

To leverage the complementary advantages of US and MW pretreatments, a combined US-MW pretreatment was developed. The US treatment was first applied at 90 W for 30 min, followed immediately by MW treatment for 1.5 min. This experimental group, designated UMF, was established to evaluate its potential for synergistically optimizing energy consumption and final product quality.

Effects of different pretreatment methods on energy consumption and apparent quality of VFD prawn

The dehydration kinetics of prawns under the different pretreatment regimes are shown in Fig. 1B. The respective drying durations for the CF, MF, UF, and UMF groups to reach the target moisture content (≤ 10%) were 23, 16, 22, and 15 h. This corresponds to a reduction in drying time of 30.43%, 4.35%, and 34.78% for the MF, UF, and UMF groups, relative to the CF group. Notably, the initial moisture contents of the MW-involved groups (MF and UMF) were 73.04% and 70.24%, respectively, significantly lower than those of the CF and UF groups (P < 0.05). Based on dry-basis conversion, this corresponded to the removal of 44.36 g and 49.60 g of water per 100 g of raw sample for the MF and UMF groups, indicating substantial pre-dehydration prior to VFD. These moisture levels were already comparable to those achieved by CF and UF after 8 h of drying, directly reducing the water load and latent heat demand required for sublimation in the subsequent VFD stage.

This aligns well with the SEC results (Fig. 1C). The MF group showed significantly lower E1 but higher E2 than the other three groups (P < 0.05), whereas the UF group exhibited the opposite trend. This suggests that MW pretreatment achieves efficient moisture removal with low energy input, yet may induce structural densification that impedes water migration during VFD, thereby resulting in the elevated E2. In contrast, US pretreatment consumes more energy during pre-dehydration, yet its cavitation effects improve microstructure and reduce mass transfer resistance, resulting in significantly lower E2. For overall SEC, the UMF group exhibited the lowest SEC, followed by the MF group, with reductions of 35.33% and 28.50% compared to the CF group. This may be attributed to the synergy of US and MW. US pretreatment loosens tissue structure and creates microchannels, facilitating subsequent microwave-assisted efficient dehydration, which in turn reduces the sublimation load during freeze-drying. Thus, although pretreatment adds extra energy input, the substantial reduction in freeze-drying energy consumption leads to notably lower total energy use, highlighting the advantage of the combined pretreatment strategy. The details of the complete stage-specific energy input and water removal data for each group are provided in Table S5.

As shown in Table 1, compared to the CF group, the UMF group exhibited significant improvements (P < 0.05) in hardness, chewiness, crispness, and rehydration ratio, demonstrating the complementary effect of combined US-MW pretreatment on enhancing textural and rehydration properties. Relative to the MF group, the UMF sample exhibited higher crispness and rehydration ratio, as well as lower shrinkage (P < 0.05), indicating that the prior US application effectively alleviated the tissue over-shrinkage and impaired rehydration capacity associated with MW treatment alone. Furthermore, the UMF group outperformed the UF group in all measured textural parameters (P < 0.05), confirming that the incorporation of MW enhanced structural integrity and compensated for the textural weaknesses of US pretreatment alone.

The ability of the combined pretreatment to achieve lower shrinkage, higher rehydration, and improved texture is likely due to a two-stage mechanism: (1) Ultrasonic cavitation disrupts and loosens the structure of denatured myofibrils, generating uniform micropores that facilitate moisture migration and enhance rehydration performance (Li et al., 2023). (2) Subsequent MW treatment rapidly heats the sample, which may contribute to the structural rearrangement and aggregation of the protein network. This helps maintain structural integrity, reduce volumetric shrinkage during drying, and ultimately yields a product with desirable crispness, chewiness, and a high rehydration ratio (Zhang et al., 2022). The US-MW combination thus leverages the complementary advantages of both technologies, significantly improving textural quality while simultaneously reducing energy consumption.

Drying model fitting

Mathematical models were used to describe the effects of pretreatments on the freeze-drying kinetics of prawn meat during vacuum freeze-drying. Experimental MR data were fitted to seven thin-layer drying models, all of which adequately fitted the experimental MR values (Table S6). Among these models, the Midilli model showed the best fitting performance for all pretreatment groups, with R2 all above 0.998 and the lowest RMSE and χ2 values (Li et al., 2025). Visual comparison of experimental and predicted MR values (Fig. 1D) revealed a high consistency between predicted curves and experimental data points, further supporting the reliability of this model in describing the VFD kinetics of pretreated prawn meat.

Differences in model parameters and goodness-of-fit indices across groups reflected changes in sample structure and drying behavior induced by pretreatments. The UMF group exhibited a substantially higher drying rate constant (k = 0.0346) than the other three groups, whose k values ranged from 0.0175 to 0.0203 (Table S6). The time exponent n ranged from 1.354 to 1.674 (all >1), indicating deviation from a simple exponential form, which may be related to dynamic changes in internal moisture diffusion resistance during drying. Notably, the UMF group exhibited both the highest k value and the lowest n value (1.354), which could suggest more uniform moisture reduction and lower mass transfer resistance (Falsafi et al., 2026). The proportional coefficient (a) was close to 1 for all groups (Table S6), indicating that the model fits the initial MR condition.

Overall, differences in model parameters reflected the influence of pretreatments on drying kinetics. The UMF group exhibited the highest drying rate constant, consistent with its shorter drying time. The Midilli model provided good fits for all groups (R2 > 0.998), supporting its use to describe the freeze-drying behavior of pretreated prawn meat. These results provide a quantitative basis for interpreting pretreatment effects on drying kinetics.

Moisture distribution

Fig. 2A shows the effects of different pretreatments on moisture distribution in prawns before freeze-drying. Tightly bound water (T2b, 0.1–10 ms), immobilized water (T21, 10–100 ms) within myofibrillar and reticular structures, and free water (T22, 100–1000 ms) located extracellularly (Zhang, Shi, et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 2A and B, immobilized water constituted the predominant fraction (90–95%) in all pretreated samples, consistent with the research results of Cheng et al. (2024).

Values with different lowercase letters denote significant differences (P < 0.05).

Notably, all samples were blanched and brined before pretreatments. To exclude potential interference from differences in salt absorption, salt uptake was measured (Table S7). No significant difference was observed among the four groups, indicating that the brining step established comparable ionic conditions across all samples. Compared to the control group, the MW group displayed a significant decrease (P < 0.05) in the peak areas of T₂₁ and T₂₂, alongside an increase in T2b (P < 0.05). This change may be attributed to microwave-induced thermal effects, which promote further conformational rearrangement and aggregation of proteins. The contraction and aggregation of myofibrillar proteins narrow the interfibrillar spaces, thereby expelling some free and immobilized water (Wang et al., 2019). In contrast, the US group exhibited a decrease in the proportion of bound water compared to the control group. This trend suggests that the cavitation effects of ultrasound may loosen the aggregates of denatured myofibrillar proteins, potentially increasing the mobility of water within the tissue matrix (Gao et al., 2024).

The US-MW combined treatment exhibited a significant increase in the proportion of T2b and significant decreases in T21 and T22 (P < 0.05), indicating more efficient removal of free and immobilized water during the pretreatment stage. This change in water distribution is likely related to the combined effect of ultrasound and microwave pretreatments. Specifically, US-generated microchannels and structural loosening improved water migration, while subsequent MW heating may further induce protein structural transformation and enhance protein-water interactions, collectively contributing to the higher proportion of bound water observed in this group (Zhang et al., 2022). Such a water distribution pattern helps reduce the dehydration load during the subsequent freeze-drying process and is expected to induce the formation of smaller ice crystals during the freezing stage, thereby alleviating mechanical damage to the muscle microstructure caused by ice crystals and improving the texture and rehydration properties of the final product.

Secondary structure content

Changes in the secondary structure of shrimp proteins under different pretreatments are shown in Fig. 2C. Compared with the CF group, the α-helix content in the other three groups decreased significantly (P < 0.05), but their effects on protein conformational organization varied. Specifically, the β-sheet content increased significantly in the MF group, while the β-turn content increased significantly in the UF and UMF groups (P < 0.05). A reduction in α-helix content typically indicates unfolding of the protein secondary structure and exposure of hydrophobic groups (Liu et al., 2024). An increase in β-sheet content generally implies enhanced hydrogen bonding and stronger intermolecular interactions, suggesting that microwave treatment may increase the tendency for intermolecular association of proteins (Zhao et al., 2024). In contrast, the microjets and local shear forces generated by ultrasonic cavitation may loosen the protein structure that is partially denatured but not tightly aggregated, and induce a conformational transition toward a flexible state rich in β-turns. This is consistent with the decreasing trend in bound water proportion observed in this group (Fig. 2B).

In the UMF group, its conformational changes were consistent with the UF group, characterized by a decrease in α-helix content and an increase in β-turn content. This further indicates that the loose conformational foundation established by ultrasound pretreatment effectively alleviated the accumulation of β-sheets induced by subsequent microwave heating. Consequently, the UMF group maintained a flexible conformation dominated by β-turns. This more open protein structure may promote the exposure of hydrophilic amino acid residues, which in turn may facilitate hydrogen bonding between protein molecules and water (Li et al., 2023). This interpretation is consistent with the higher proportion of bound water observed in the UMF group during moisture distribution analysis (Section 3.5).

Microstructure

The microstructural changes induced by different pretreatments before VFD are shown in Fig. 3A. The control group exhibited localized fractures and pores, likely caused by heat-induced protein denaturation and subsequent tissue contraction. In the MW group, severe myofibrillar contraction and aggregation into dense bundles were observed, yielding a coarse and irregular surface topography. This phenomenon may be ascribed to volumetric heating generated by microwaves, which instigates rapid internal vaporization and forceful expulsion of steam, culminating in pronounced tissue shrinkage and structural collapse-a finding consistent with reports of compacted muscle fibers in microwave-processed aquatic products (Zheng et al., 2025). The US group displayed a notably uniform and porous network structure, which may result from ultrasonic cavitation. The microjets and local high-pressure zones generated by cavitation could disrupt and loosen the myofibrillar structure, thus promoting pore formation and structural homogeneity (Li et al., 2023). In contrast, the US-MW group exhibited a more continuous and well-organized structure, with no obvious fractures or loose regions, suggesting a synergistic effect of ultrasound-induced pre-dispersion and subsequent microwave-mediated rearrangement. This provided a structural foundation for efficient moisture migration during freeze-drying (Zhang et al., 2022), leading to the formation of a highly ordered and dense network structure.

Effects of pretreatment on the microstructure of prawn before (A) and after (B) freeze-drying.

The microstructural characteristics of the final VFD products are presented in Fig. 3B. The CF group maintained a relatively ordered myofibrillar arrangement with a compact structure and clear inter-fibrillar boundaries. This structural integrity is likely attributable to the stable protein gel network formed during blanching, which resisted disruption during the subsequent lyophilization process. In contrast, the MF group exhibited obvious myofibrillar aggregation, obscured boundaries, and distorted structures, which may reflect thermal aggregation and structural contraction caused by rapid water evaporation under microwave treatment. These observations agree well with the higher hardness and chewiness detected in texture analysis (Table 1). The UF group exhibited larger pores and cavities, which may be attributed to the loosening effect of ultrasound on the tissue structure. Ice crystals may expand and grow within the microchannels formed by ultrasound, exerting mechanical damage on the surrounding tissues. Such an open porous structure may reduce tissue density and facilitate water sublimation and migration during drying, which is consistent with its lower hardness and better rehydration capacity (Table 1).

Notably, the UMF group's microstructure appeared to integrate characteristics from both the MF and UF groups. The myofibrillar bundles were relatively well-organized without large cavities, yet numerous fragmented segments were present locally. This unique morphology likely arises from the synergistic interplay of the pretreatments: ultrasonic pre-disperses myofibrillar proteins, providing a homogeneous substrate for subsequent microwave treatment; microwave heating then induces moderate rearrangement and crosslinking of the dispersed proteins, which not only alleviates the excessive aggregation typically caused by microwave alone but also provides a stable structural foundation for the samples. This ultimately leads to a microstructure with both order and stability, endowing the product with excellent textural properties (Table 1).

Volatile flavor profile analysis

An electronic nose is a sensing device that mimics the structure and function of the human nose, allowing for discrimination of complex odor patterns and detection of relative variations in volatile profiles (Tian et al., 2025). The principal component analysis (PCA) plot (Fig. 4A) showed that the cumulative variance contribution rate of PC1 (74.2%) and PC2 (11.7%) reached 85.9%, indicating that the model effectively summarized the majority of information from the original dataset. The spatial distribution of samples in the PCA score plot reflected differential influences of the pretreatment methods on the volatile flavor profile of freeze-dried prawns. The CF, MF, and UMF groups were clustered relatively closely, while the UF group was distinctly separated from the others. This suggests that ultrasound treatment may alter the volatile profile, which is generally consistent with the results of Nie et al. (2025). In contrast, the MF and UMF groups exhibited volatile flavor profiles more similar to those of the CF group, as indicated by their closer clustering in the PCA plot.

PCA analysis based on electronic nose data (A); Electronic nose radar diagrams of prawn meat after different pretreatments (B); Sensory score radar plots of freeze-dried prawns after different pretreatments (C); Pictures of freeze-dried prawn samples after different pretreatments (D).

The electronic nose effectively characterized the impact of pretreatments on volatile compounds (Fig. 4B). Most sensors exhibited low response values across all dried samples, with the exception of the W1W sensor, which is primarily sensitive to sulfur-containing compounds such as thiols and sulfides. Such compounds are key flavor contributors in crustacean products and may synergize with umami amino acids and nucleotides to enhance the umami perception of the product (Cadwallader & Baek, 1998). The UF group displayed significantly lower response intensity on the W1W sensor compared to the other groups (P < 0.05), suggesting that US pretreatment may have altered the relative abundance of sulfur-containing volatile compounds, thereby resulting in the reduced sensor response of the electronic nose. This observation is consistent with the findings reported by Xi et al. (2023). Furthermore, the UMF group exhibited significantly higher response values at the W1C (aromatic compounds) and W5C (short-chain alkanes, aromatics) sensors compared to the CF group (P < 0.05), while showing notably weaker responses at the W2S (alcohols, aldehydes, ketones) and W3S (long-chain alkanes) sensors. These results indicate that different pretreatments produced distinguishable effects on the relative volatile profiles of VFD prawn. However, definitive identification and quantification of specific volatile compounds require further analysis using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS).

Free amino acid

The pretreatment methods significantly affected the composition and concentration of free amino acids (FAAs), key taste-active components in aquatic products (Table 2). Seventeen distinct FAAs were identified and quantified across all experimental groups, comprising 2 umami-type, 6 sweet-type, and 9 bitter-type amino acids. Notably, six sweet amino acids-threonine (Thr), serine (Ser), glycine (Gly), alanine (Ala), proline (Pro), and lysine (Lys)-collectively accounted for 59% of the total FAAs pool, indicating their major contribution to the prawn's characteristic flavor, which aligns with findings in crustaceans such as Penaeus vannamei (Duppeti et al., 2022). Compared with the CF group (612.18 mg/100 g), the total FAAs content in the MF (662.18 mg/100 g), UF (702.56 mg/100 g), and UMF (730.77 mg/100 g) groups were significantly increased (P < 0.05), along with significant increases in umami amino acids and lysine content.

The higher FAAs content in the MF group may be attributed to the unfolding of protein structure induced by microwave heating, which in turn promotes the conversion of amino acid residues in proteins into free amino acids (Fan et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024). For the UF group, the increase in total FAAs may be attributed to the physical effects of ultrasound, which disrupts the myofibrillar network and induces secondary structure transitions (e.g., decreased α-helix and increased β-turn content), potentially promoting the release and accumulation of FAAs (Zhang et al., 2022). Additionally, we determined the total FAAs in the liquid medium during ultrasonic treatment to be 83.69 mg/100 g for the UF group, a value considerably lower than the FAAs content retained in the UF prawn matrix (702.56 mg/100 g). This suggests that although ultrasound causes partial leaching of soluble FAAs into the liquid medium, its promotion of endogenous FAAs release from proteins dominates, ultimately leading to a net increase in FAAs content within the prawn matrix. However, our study did not measure the activity of endogenous proteases, so we cannot definitively distinguish between FAAs released via protein unfolding versus those generated by enzymatic hydrolysis, which represents an important area for future investigation.

The above results suggest that both ultrasound and microwave pretreatments may effectively promote the release and accumulation of endogenous free amino acids. The UMF group showed the highest total FAAs content among all groups, which may reflect a synergistic effect of the combined pretreatments in flavor enhancement. This offers a potential strategy for modulating the taste quality of dried prawn products.

Compared to the CF group, all three pretreatment groups showed a significant increase in the content of multiple flavor nucleotides (P < 0.05), indicating that both US and MW treatments promote the release and retention of these compounds. Among them, the MF group exhibited the strongest effect, with significantly higher levels of CMP, AMP, UMP and IMP than the UF and UMF groups (P < 0.05). Sensory evaluation

Sensory analysis serves as a critical tool for assessing the overall acceptability and market potential of food products (Fan et al., 2024). Freeze-dried prawn samples were assessed for appearance, color, aroma, taste, and texture, and overall acceptability was calculated based on the comprehensive scores of these indicators, with the sensory profile visualized in a radar chart (Fig. 4C) and the detailed quantitative data (mean ± SD) presented in Table S8. For appearance morphology, the MF group received the lowest score owing to obvious breakage and shrinkage (Fig. 4D), while no significant differences were observed among the CF, UF, and UMF groups (P > 0.05). Furthermore, appropriate texture and mouthfeel are important features of high-quality dried seafood products. In our study, the panelists found that the samples pretreated with UMF had the best texture and chewiness, and this score was significantly higher than the other three groups (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed among all groups in terms of color, taste, and aroma (P > 0.05).

Overall acceptability results showed that the UMF group had the highest normalized comprehensive score (14.86 points), while the MF group had the lowest (13.25 points) and was significantly lower than the other three groups (P < 0.05). Although microwave treatment alone could improve the taste of the product to some extent, its obvious defects in appearance largely offset its advantages in taste. In contrast, the combined ultrasound-microwave pretreatment, through the physical effects of ultrasonic cavitation and the release and retention of flavor compounds induced by microwave heating, achieved a better balance among appearance integrity, texture, and flavor. Comprehensively, the UMF pretreatment may effectively balance sensory attributes, potentially yielding high overall consumer acceptability.

Conclusion

This work developed an integrative approach to optimize VFD of giant freshwater prawns by balancing processing efficiency, energy consumption and product quality via physical field pretreatments. Specifically, the MF group exhibited substantially reduced energy consumption but negatively impacted appearance, morphology, and texture. In contrast, the UF group significantly improved tissue structure and rehydration capacity. The UMF group achieved synergistic optimization, cutting energy consumption by 35.33% while enhancing rehydration capacity and texture. This synergy is attributed to favorable structural modifications: the pretreatment sequence promoted the formation of a homogeneous microstructure and increased flexible β-turn protein conformations, contributing to improved water-binding characteristics and mitigated hardening. The drying kinetics were accurately characterized by the Midilli model (R2 > 0.998), providing a reliable theoretical model for process scaling.

This work offers theoretical and practical insights for the high-quality drying of prawn meat. However, it should be noted that the study was conducted only on frozen giant freshwater prawns at the laboratory scale. In addition, factors such as equipment configuration, continuous processing mode, and batch volume may influence actual energy efficiency in industrial settings. Therefore, future work should extend the investigation to fresh prawns and other aquatic species to assess the generalizability of the findings. Meanwhile, pilot-scale validation is needed to evaluate the scalability of these advantages under commercial production conditions.
