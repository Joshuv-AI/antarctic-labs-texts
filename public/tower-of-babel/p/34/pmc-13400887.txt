CT‐Based Reference Ranges for Canine Pyloric Thickness: Influence of Body Size and Normalization With Aortic Diameter
Authors: Jihoon Lee, Danbee Kwon, Sungsoo Kim, Ilyong Yoon, Kichang Lee, Hakyoung Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPyloric wall thickness reflects pyloric pathology. Prior related studies included small samples and did not consistently measure perpendicular to the pyloric axis. This multicenter retrospective study established body‐weight (BW)‐specific computed tomography (CT) reference ranges for pyloric thickness, measured in both cross‐sectional and transverse planes, in clinically normal dogs and evaluated relationships with BW, age, breed, and sex. Multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) was used to obtain a true cross‐sectional plane, and the transverse plane was used for practicality. CT images of 229 dogs were analyzed to measure pyloric thickness and calculate the pyloric thickness‐to‐aortic diameter (P/Ao) ratio. Consistent differences were observed between planes. Pyloric thickness showed a positive correlation with BW, with significant differences across BW groups. No significant association was found with age, nor based on neutering status, nor among breeds with similar BW. A minor difference between sexes was observed only in the transverse plane, likely reflecting oblique slicing. In the cross‐sectional plane, mean (±standard deviation [SD]) pyloric thickness was 3.61 ± 0.76, 4.21 ± 0.87, 5.10 ± 1.14, and 6.93 ± 1.18 mm for dogs weighing <5, 5–10, 10–20, and ≥20 kg, respectively. In the transverse plane, the corresponding values were 4.03 ± 0.82, 4.80 ± 1.03, 6.02 ± 1.40, and 7.75 ± 1.33 mm. P/Ao ratio was independent of BW, with means (±SD) of 0.74 ± 0.18 and 0.83 ± 0.20 in the cross‐sectional and transverse planes, respectively. Therefore, BW‐specific reference ranges may be used routinely, whereas the BW‐independent P/Ao ratio may facilitate assessment when BW is unavailable.Keywords: body‐weight index, canine, gastrointestinal wall, normal reference range, pyloric measurement

Introduction

Pyloric diseases are among the primary causes of gastric outlet obstruction in dogs and may result in clinical signs such as vomiting and weight loss [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Conditions, such as chronic hypertrophic pyloric gastropathy, gastric neoplasia, and inflammatory lesions, have been associated with increased pyloric wall thickness [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Thus, pyloric thickness is considered an important diagnostic parameter in evaluating these conditions.

Ultrasonography (US) is the primary imaging modality for assessing pyloric thickness in dogs [5, 9]. Although computed tomography (CT) evaluation of intestinal wall layering is inferior to that from US, it provides superior visualization of deep‐seated structures and is less affected by bowel gas interference; this may offer advantages in assessing pyloric thickness [10, 11, 12]. Although a normal reference range for pyloric thickness has been established using US [1, 2, 13], CT‐based pyloric thickness measurements do not always correspond. This highlights the need for an accurate CT‐based reference range for improved assessment [14].

Previous studies on pyloric thickness using CT have been limited by small sample sizes, with only 19 clinically normal dogs included in prior evaluations [14]. Furthermore, factors, such as BW, breed, and sex, have not been systematically evaluated as contributing factors [15]. Thus, further investigation is required to establish a comprehensive reference range that accounts for these variables.

Accurately measuring pyloric thickness on CT poses challenges due to the pylorus’ variable orientation. Assessments solely based on measurements in the transverse plane—oriented perpendicular to the spine—may be inaccurate due to oblique slicing. Multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) allows for cross‐sectional images, perpendicular to the pyloric axis, to improve measurement accuracy [15]. However, measurements in the transverse plane remain clinically practical because this plane is routinely used in standard CT interpretation.

The aims of this study were as follows: (1) to establish a CT‐based reference range for pyloric thickness in clinically normal dogs by comparing transverse and cross‐sectional plane measurements obtained using MPR; and (2) to evaluate the correlation between pyloric thickness and BW, and between pyloric thickness and age, and to assess the correlation between P/Ao ratio and BW, as well as the differences in pyloric thickness according to breed, sex, and neutering status. On the basis of these aims, it was hypothesized that (1) pyloric thickness would show a positive correlation with BW, (2) the P/Ao ratio would be independent of BW, and (3) pyloric thickness would show no significant association with age, breed, or neutering status.

Materials and Methods

Animals

This was a multicenter, retrospective observational study conducted between 2019 and 2024. The medical records and CT images of 340 dogs from multiple centers were collected from four veterinary clinics: Jeonbuk National University Animal Medical Center, Bundang Leaders Animal Medical Center, VIP Animal Medical Center, and NEL Animal Medical Foundation. The inclusion criteria were the following: (1) no clinical signs of gastrointestinal disease, (2) no severe distension or lesion of the pylorus, (3) no significant abnormalities in hematologic or blood chemistry analyses, and (4) a confirmed diagnosis of a non‐gastrointestinal disease. Additionally, dogs with body condition scores (BCS) outside the range of 3–7/9 were excluded. BCS was assessed using the standard 9‐point body condition scoring system recorded in the medical records at each institution. Dogs with a markedly angulated pylorus were also excluded. A markedly angulated pylorus was defined as excessive curvature of the pyloric canal that prevented obtaining a true cross‐sectional plane perpendicular to the pyloric axis despite MPR. The initial selection of dogs, based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, was performed by a second‐year radiology resident (J.L.); the inclusion or exclusion of each patient was subsequently reviewed and approved by a senior veterinary radiologist with over 10 years of experience (H.Y.).

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jeonbuk National University, Iksan‐si, Jeollabuk‐do, Republic of Korea (approval no. NON2024‐164).

CT Scan Protocol and Measurements

CT images were acquired using four scanners of three models: Brivo CT 385 (GE Medical Systems Co. Ltd., Beijing, China), Alexion 16 (Toshiba Medical Systems Co. Ltd., Otawara, Japan), and Revolution ACT (GE Medical Systems Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) with the following parameters: 110–120 kilovoltage peak (kVp), 80–200 milliampere‐seconds (mAs), 0.625–1.5 mm slice thickness, 0.75–1.0 s rotation time, and 0.938–1.375 collimation beam pitch. All CT scans were performed with the dogs in the sternal recumbency position, covering the region from the cranial margin of the diaphragm to the caudal endplate of the third lumbar vertebra. Transverse images were obtained in a cranial‐to‐caudal direction and oriented perpendicular to the spine. To minimize motion artifacts, scans were performed under breath‐holding whenever feasible. Iohexol (Omnipaque, 600–750 mgI/kg; GE Healthcare, Ireland) was administered as a contrast agent via the cephalic vein. Post‐contrast images were acquired 120–150 s after contrast administration. All images were analyzed in the delayed phase using RadiAnt DICOM viewer (Poznań, Poland) with the abdominal soft tissue window (window level = 40–45 Hounsfield units [HU]; window width = 400–450 HU).

The pyloric thickness was measured at the region where the pyloric wall appeared the thickest and the lumen narrowest (Figure 1A). MPR was first performed in the dorsal plane to align the pyloric canal to its physiological orientation (Figure 1B). Following this, MPR was conducted again in the sagittal plane to obtain a true cross‐sectional image of the pylorus (Figure 1C).

For measurements in the cross‐sectional plane, the wall thickness was determined as follows: When the lumen was clearly visible, indicating a non‐collapsed state, measurements were taken perpendicular to the inner wall at four quadrants, and the average of these values was calculated (Figure 1D). In cases where the pyloric canal collapsed, and the lumen was not clearly distinguishable, the smallest diameter of the canal was measured, and half of this diameter was considered to be the wall thickness (Figure 1E,F).

Pyloric thickness in the transverse plane, oriented perpendicular to the spine, was measured at the same anatomical level as the MPR‐based cross‐sectional image, but using a transverse orientation (Figure 2A). When a lumen was clearly visible, the wall thickness was measured perpendicular to the inner wall at two opposing regions along the pyloric canal, and the average value was calculated (Figure 2B). In cases where the lumen was not clearly distinguishable because of a collapse, the diameter perpendicular to the pyloric axis was measured, and half of this diameter was considered to be the wall thickness (Figure 2C).

(A) Pyloric thickness in the transverse plane is measured at the same anatomical location as for the cross‐sectional image obtained through multiplanar reconstruction. This anatomical location is identified in the dorsal plane. (B) At this level, pyloric wall thickness is measured in the transverse plane by taking measurements perpendicular to the inner wall at two opposing regions along the pyloric canal. (C) Another case of a collapsed pylorus is shown, where the diameter perpendicular to the pyloric axis is measured, and half of this diameter is used as the wall thickness. (D) The aortic diameter (double arrow) is measured in the transverse plane, in the mediolateral (right‐to‐left) direction, from outer wall to outer wall, at the same level used for pyloric thickness measurement as in (A) and (B).

Additionally, the diameter of the aorta (Ao) was measured in the transverse plane, in the mediolateral (right‐to‐left) direction, from outer wall to outer wall, at the same level where the pyloric thickness was measured on the transverse plane (Figure 2D). The P/Ao ratio was then calculated by dividing the pyloric thickness by the Ao at that plane.

All measurements were acquired and recorded by a second‐year radiology resident (J.L.) at the Veterinary Medical Imaging Department of the Teaching Hospital of Jeonbuk National University. All measurements were reviewed and approved by a senior veterinary radiologist with over 10 years of experience (H.Y.).

Statistical Analysis

In accordance with the central limit theorem, given the large sample size (n > 30), data were considered approximately normally distributed, whereas normality for smaller subgroups was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Pearson's correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between pyloric thickness and age. Linear regression analysis was performed to assess the correlation between pyloric thickness and BW, and between the P/Ao ratio and BW. An independent t‐test was conducted to evaluate differences in pyloric thickness between male and female dogs. Differences between neutered and intact dogs were also analyzed, using Welch's t‐test owing to unequal variances. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to compare pyloric thickness among BW groups and among breed groups with similar BW (n ≥ 10), to minimize the confounding effect of BW in interbreed comparisons. Paired t‐tests were conducted to assess mean differences between measurements obtained in the cross‐sectional and transverse planes. Levene's test was conducted to assess the homogeneity of variances. For normally distributed data with equal variances, one‐way ANOVA was performed. In cases of unequal variances, Welch's ANOVA and Games–Howell post hoc tests were used for the analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical analyses were performed by a second‐year radiology resident (J.L.) with graduate‐level training in biostatistics; the analyses were then reviewed by a senior veterinary radiologist with over 10 years of experience (H.Y.). Statistical significance was considered p < 0.05, whereas p < 0.001 was interpreted as highly statistically significant. Intra‐ and interobserver reliabilities for pyloric thickness and the P/Ao ratio in both the cross‐sectional and transverse planes were evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for absolute agreement with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

Animals

Of the 340 dogs, 229 were included in this study per the eligibility criteria. Of these, 129, 60, 21, and 19 were contributed by the four institutions, respectively. The animals were categorized into four BW groups: A (<5 kg, n = 90), B (≥5–<10 kg, n = 90), C (≥10–<20 kg, n = 34), and D (≥20 kg, n = 15). The distribution by sex and neutering status was as follows: 72 neutered females, 25 intact females, 117 neutered males, and 15 intact males. The mean age of the dogs was 9.34 ± 3.90 years, and the mean BW was 7.79 ± 6.73 kg. The breeds represented in the study included Maltese (n = 52), mixed (n = 30), poodle (n = 29), Pomeranian (n = 14), Yorkshire terrier (n = 13), cocker spaniel (n = 12), shih tzu (n = 11), schnauzer (n = 9), bichon frise (n = 6), Japanese spitz (n = 6), dachshund (n = 5), beagle (n = 4), Boston terrier (n = 3), Chihuahua (n = 3), French bulldog (n = 3), golden retriever (n = 3), Jindo (n = 3), Pekingese (n = 3), Cardigan Welsh corgi (n = 2), Italian greyhound (n = 2), Old English sheepdog (n = 2), Shetland sheepdog (n = 2), border collie (n = 1), chow chow (n = 1), Doberman pinscher (n = 1), Labrador retriever (n = 1), miniature pinscher (n = 1), papillon (n = 1), Samoyed (n = 1), Scottish terrier (n = 1), shar‐pei (n = 1), Shiba Inu (n = 1), Siberian husky (n = 1), and wolf shepherd (n = 1).

Differences Between Cross‐Sectional and Transverse Plane Measurements of Pyloric Thickness

Paired t‐test revealed that pyloric thickness measured in the cross‐sectional plane was slightly lower than that measured in the transverse plane (p < 0.001). The mean difference between the two planes was −0.586 mm.

Relationship Between Pyloric Thickness and BW

Correlation Between Pyloric Thickness and BW (Cross‐Sectional and Transverse Plane Measurements)

A strong positive correlation was found between pyloric thickness and BW in the cross‐sectional plane using MPR (p < 0.001, R = 0.709, R 2 = 0.503), indicating that pyloric thickness tends to increase as BW increases (Figure 3A). Similarly, in the transverse plane, a strong positive correlation was also observed (p < 0.001, R = 0.711, R 2 = 0.505), suggesting a consistent linear relationship between pyloric thickness and BW across both planes (Figure 3B).

Differences in Pyloric Thickness Among BW Groups (Cross‐Sectional and Transverse Plane Measurements)

Given the significant violation of variance homogeneity in both planes (Levene's test, p < 0.05), Welch's ANOVA was conducted separately for the cross‐sectional and transverse planes. A highly statistically significant difference in pyloric thickness across BW groups was observed in both the cross‐sectional (p < 0.001, Welch's F = 49.073) and the transverse (p < 0.001, Welch's F = 54.134) planes. According to Games–Howell post hoc tests, statistically significant differences were found among all BW groups in both planes (p < 0.05 for all comparisons), with the greatest difference consistently observed between the <5 and ≥20 kg groups: 3.327 mm in the cross‐sectional plane and 3.727 mm in the transverse plane (both p < 0.001). Correlation Between P/Ao Ratio and BW

Linear regression analysis revealed no statistically significant correlation between the P/Ao ratio and BW in either plane. Across the cohort, the mean P/Ao ratio (95% CI) was 0.74 (0.71–0.76) in the cross‐sectional plane and 0.83 (0.81–0.86) in the transverse plane. In the cross‐sectional plane, the correlation was very weak and not statistically significant (R = −0.128, R 2 = 0.016, p = 0.052; Figure 3C). Similarly, in the transverse plane, the correlation was also very weak and not statistically significant (R = −0.095, R 2 = 0.009, p = 0.151; Figure 3D). These findings suggest that the P/Ao ratio does not demonstrate a consistent linear association with BW. Intra‐ and Interobserver Reliability

The measurements were performed in duplicates by a second‐year radiology resident (J.L.), and all were repeated by a second‐year radiology resident (S.L.). S.L. was blinded to the measurements obtained by J.L. To assess intraobserver reliability, J.L. performed all measurements twice with an interval of more than 3 months between sessions.

Intraobserver reliability for pyloric measurements showed ICCs of 0.958 (95% CI: 0.946–0.968) in the cross‐sectional plane and 0.933 (95% CI: 0.914–0.948) in the transverse plane (both p < 0.001). For the P/Ao ratio, ICCs were 0.924 (95% CI: 0.902–0.941) in the cross‐sectional plane and 0.866 (95% CI: 0.830–0.895) in the transverse plane (both p < 0.001).

Interobserver reliability for pyloric measurements showed ICCs of 0.943 (95% CI: 0.926–0.956) in the cross‐sectional plane and 0.816 (95% CI: 0.746–0.865) in the transverse plane (both p < 0.001). For the P/Ao ratio, ICCs were 0.887 (95% CI: 0.853–0.913) in the cross‐sectional plane and 0.774 (95% CI: 0.649–0.848) in the transverse plane (both p < 0.001).

According to the guidelines proposed by Koo and Li [16], ICC values between 0.75 and 0.9 indicate good reliability, and those greater than 0.9 indicate excellent reliability. Overall, the results indicated good to excellent reliability for pyloric thickness and the P/Ao ratio in both planes (ICC range 0.774–0.958).

Institutional effects were evaluated using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post hoc analysis. Although a statistically significant overall institutional effect was identified in some analyses (p < 0.05), most pairwise comparisons did not show significant differences among institutions (p > 0.05), and the limited significant pairwise differences that were identified were not consistent across measurements.

Discussion

This study established BW‐specific CT reference ranges for canine pyloric thickness. BW emerged as the principal determinant of pyloric thickness. P/Ao ratio was independent of BW and functions as a normalization index across sizes. Although previous studies primarily relied on US‐based measurements of gastrointestinal structures [1, 13], CT‐based evaluations of pyloric thickness have been limited by small sample sizes [14]. By incorporating a broader range of breeds and sizes, this study provides a more comprehensive CT‐based reference range.

Pyloric thickness measurements were obtained using both cross‐sectional and transverse planes. A statistically significant difference was observed between the two planes (p < 0.001). Because this systematic difference may influence threshold‐based clinical interpretation, measurements obtained from the two planes should not be interpreted interchangeably, and plane‐specific reference ranges are required. Accordingly, these two planes were analyzed separately throughout the study to account for this difference. This difference is likely due to variations in pyloric orientation in the transverse plane. To minimize measurement error, MPR was conducted to obtain true cross‐sectional images, in accordance with previously described methods in veterinary gastrointestinal imaging [15]. In human medicine, cross‐sectional imaging has also been used in gastrointestinal evaluations [17], highlighting the importance of selecting an appropriate measurement plane. Measurements in the transverse plane are clinically practical and suitable for routine use. However, owing to the possibility of oblique slicing, cross‐sectional images aligned with the pyloric axis may be preferable when greater anatomical precision is needed.

Additionally, luminal collapse must be considered when measuring pyloric thickness. Despite changes in gastric distension, the pyloric canal diameter remains relatively stable, even when the stomach is maximally distended [1, 6], indicating that pyloric thickness is minimally affected by gastric distension. To further minimize the influence of variable distension on measurement accuracy, dogs exhibiting marked pyloric distension were excluded from the study. Consistent with previous research on luminal structures, such as the urethra [18], where wall thickness was estimated from collapsed lumens, this study applied a similar approach by using half the diameter of the pyloric canal to estimate pyloric wall thickness when the lumen was not visible. By applying MPR and considering luminal collapse during measurement, this study established a standardized and reproducible method for pyloric thickness evaluation using CT.

A significant positive correlation was observed between pyloric thickness and BW, consistent with prior studies on gastrointestinal wall measurements [9, 14]. Welch's ANOVA results further confirmed significant differences in pyloric thickness among BW groups, reinforcing the necessity of BW‐specific reference ranges rather than a universal threshold. Although BW is the principal determinant of pyloric thickness, the R 2 values (approximately 0.50) indicate that a substantial proportion of the variance remains unexplained. Unmeasured factors—such as varying states of fasting, individual anatomical differences, and potential subclinical pathology—likely contribute to this residual variance. This is also reflected in the relatively wide SD values observed within the BW groups.

Consistent with previous studies on organ size normalization using Ao—including evaluations of the common bile duct, pancreatic thickness, kidney, and portosystemic shunts [19, 20, 21, 22]—the present study found no significant correlation between P/Ao ratio and BW. This suggests that, unlike pyloric thickness, the P/Ao ratio remains relatively stable across a wide range of BWs. Accordingly, the P/Ao ratio may serve as a BW‐independent reference index for the assessment of pyloric thickness. From a clinical perspective, BW‐specific reference ranges may be used in routine or planned CT examinations when BW is known. In contrast, the P/Ao ratio may be useful when BW is unknown or uncertain, such as in emergency or retrospective settings. These two approaches should, therefore, be considered complementary rather than interchangeable.

In addition to BW, age was also considered a potential factor affecting pyloric thickness. However, no significant relationship between pyloric thickness and age was observed, suggesting that pyloric thickness remains relatively stable throughout the lifespan. This finding aligns with previous US studies on gastrointestinal wall thickness, where age has not been identified as a major influencing factor [12, 23]. Overall, these results indicate that pyloric thickness does not significantly vary with age in clinically normal dogs.

Sex‐related differences in pyloric thickness were also assessed. No significant differences were observed between the sexes in the cross‐sectional plane, whereas a statistically significant difference was noted in the transverse plane, with males exhibiting greater pyloric thickness than females. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in image orientation between the two planes, which can lead to oblique slicing in the transverse view. Although the transverse plane is often used in clinical practice, the observed sex‐related difference in this plane should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect orientation‐related variation rather than a true anatomical difference. Meanwhile, no significant differences in pyloric thickness were observed according to neutering status in either plane, suggesting that neutering may not substantially affect pyloric wall thickness in clinically normal dogs.

In comparisons limited to breeds with similar BWs (n ≥ 10), no statistically significant differences in pyloric thickness were observed. These findings indicate that breed plays a limited role in pyloric thickness, with BW serving as a more reliable determinant of pyloric thickness.

The intra‐ and interobserver ICCs demonstrated good to excellent reliability for pyloric thickness and P/Ao ratio in both planes, confirming that the measurement procedure was consistent and reproducible throughout the study.

In the present multicenter study, although limited institutional differences were identified, these were not consistent across measurements, suggesting that center‐related variation did not meaningfully influence the overall findings or the establishment of the reference ranges.

Qualitative evaluation of pyloric enhancement showed that the pyloric wall was clearly distinguishable from luminal fluid in most dogs. However, mural layering was not consistently delineated. Therefore, the study focused on the quantitative measurement of wall thickness rather than enhancement characteristics.

Although this study did not include a direct comparison with US measurements, previous studies have noted that CT‐derived measurements of gastrointestinal wall thickness may differ from values obtained using US because of fundamental differences in image acquisition, including limited layer delineation and the potential inclusion of adjacent enhancing tissues such as vasculature or fat [9, 11, 14]. These modality‐specific characteristics underscore the need for a CT‐based reference range, rather than relying on a US‐based one.

This study has some limitations. The cohort was skewed toward small‐breed dogs, and the relatively small number of dogs in the ≥20 kg BW group may limit the reliability of the reference range for large‐breed dogs. Histopathological evaluation was not performed, making it impossible to determine whether subclinical pathological changes in the pylorus influenced measurements. Owing to the retrospective design, a direct comparison between CT and US measurements within the same individuals could not be performed. Gastric distension was not standardized at the time of imaging; however, its impact on pyloric canal dimensions is likely minimal based on previous findings [6, 24]. In addition, normality for larger groups was assumed on the basis of the central limit theorem rather than formally tested, which should be considered a limitation of the statistical approach. Multiple statistical comparisons were performed across several outcomes in this study, and formal corrections for multiple comparisons were not applied. Accordingly, marginal findings, such as the sex difference observed in the transverse plane (p = 0.035), may not remain significant after more rigorous statistical correction. Finally, the power to detect very small effects in the relationship between P/Ao and BW was limited (≈0.48 for R 2 = 0.016 in the cross‐sectional plane, and ≈0.30 for R 2 = 0.009 in the transverse plane); however, the regression slopes and their 95% CIs (cross‐sectional, −0.007 to 0.000; transverse, −0.007 to 0.001) indicate that any potential associations would be clinically negligible.

In conclusion, this is the first large‐scale CT study to establish normal reference ranges for pyloric thickness in clinically normal dogs. As BW was the primary determinant of pyloric thickness, BW‐specific reference ranges were established using both cross‐sectional and transverse planes. The P/Ao ratio, with mean values of 0.74 ± 0.18 and 0.83 ± 0.20 in the cross‐sectional and transverse planes, respectively, provides a normalization tool for standardized interpretation across BWs. BW‐specific reference ranges and the BW‐independent P/Ao ratio can both be used to evaluate pyloric thickness.
