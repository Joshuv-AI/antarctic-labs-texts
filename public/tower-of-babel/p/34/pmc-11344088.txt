Comparison of dietary inflammatory index and inflammatory biomarkers between vegetarians and omnivores in Chinese population
Authors: Cenyu Wang, Ruizhen Wu, Die Yao, Zhiping Yu, Xiuhua Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Most previous studies on the association between vegetarian diet and inflammation have used only one inflammatory biomarker e.g., C-reactive protein (CRP) and the findings were generally inconsistent. Therefore, we conducted a cross-sectional study to investigate the correlation between diet and inflammation in Chinese vegetarians using dietary indices and multiple inflammatory biomarkers. 279 vegetarians and omnivores of the same sex and age recruited in Shanghai, 2016. 24-h dietary review questionnaire was collected and used to calculate Dietary inflammatory index (DII) and Energy-adjusted inflammatory index (E-DII) of both groups. In addition, energy intake matched vegetarian and omnivore recipes were designed by registed dietitions and used to calculate a theoretical DII. Five serum inflammatory biomarkers CRP, interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were measured. We found that vegetarians had significantly lower E-DII and theoretical DII than omnivores (P < 0.001). In contrast, the raw DII of vegetarians was almost the same with that of omnivores, probably due to lower energy intake in vegetarians than in omnivores (1367.97 ± 479.75 vs. 1724.78 ± 568.13, P < 0.001). Levels of TNF-α, IL-6, NLR and PLR were significantly higher in vegetarians than in omnivores while no statistical differences were found in CRP. In conclusion, a theoretical vegetarian diet with adequate energy intake as well as a balanced dietary intake showed good anti-inflammatory effects, though this was not fully reflected in vegetarian population in the real world, probably due to insufficient energy intake in the vegetarian population.

Introduction

Chronic inflammation is the risk factor for many chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease by affecting blood pressure levels and glucolipid metabolism1–3. Chronic inflammation levels are closely related to dietary patterns4.

Vegetarianism is a dietary pattern of partial or complete rejection of animal foods. In China, about 0.7% of the population is vegetarian5. Vegetarians usually consume more fruits and vegetables, grains, nuts, and soy products, containing more vitamin C, vitamin E, phytochemicals, unsaturated fatty acids, and dietary fiber. These nutrients in vegetarian diet produce anti-inflammatory effects through various mechanisms6,7. In addition, lower saturated fat and cholesterol in plant foods reduces inflammation in the intestinal environment8.

In recent years, researchers have examined the anti-inflammatory effects of vegetarian diets by comparing the differences in several serum inflammatory biomarkers e.g., CRP between vegetarians and omnivores. Consistently, a meta-analysis that included 21 cross-sectional studies also reported significant correlation between CRP and vegetarian diet9. However, many other inflammatory biomarkers such as TNF-ɑ, IL-6, IL-18, etc., were rarely tested in previous studies and no clear conclusions were obtained. Moreover, the sample sizes of those studies were relatively small and/or no matching between vegetarians and omnivores, thus no comprehensive and definitive conclusions can be drawn for the association between inflammation and vegetarian diet. On the other hand, vegetarian diets are highly variable depending on the environment and culture, and the above-mentioned studies, all conducted in Europe and the United States. There is no sufficient evidence on the inflammation status in vegetarian populations in Chinese. Therefore, we conducted this study to evaluate if vegetarian diets have any anti-inflammatory effect particularly in Chinese population.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Three participants were excluded due to questionnaire quality issues (Fig. 1). A total of 279 vegetarians and 279 age- and sex- matched omnivores were included in this study (Table 1), with 230 females in each group accounting for 82.4%. The mean age of the vegetarian group was (34.94 ± 8.69) years and that of the omnivores was (34.44 ± 8.81) years, with no statistical difference between the two groups. In addition, there was no statistical difference in height, education, physical exercise, and proportion of smokers. The mean weight of the vegetarian group was (55.61 ± 8.44) kg, which was significantly lower than the mean weight of the omnivorous group (59.57 ± 11.08) (P < 0.001). The porportions of people who drink alcohol among vegetarians (5.4%) was significantly lower than among omnivores (17.9%) (P < 0.001).The mean BMI of the vegetarian group was (20.98 ± 2.61) kg/m2, which was significantly lower than the mean BMI of the omnivorous group (22.38 ± 3.33) kg/m2 (P < 0.001). When comparing differences in inflammatory biomarkers between two groups, it showed that CRP was significantly lower in vegetarians than in omnivores (P = 0.008). Discussion

The current study examined the dietary intake and inflammation levels between vegetarian and age- and sex- matched omnivores in Chinese population. Though it appeared that vegetarian diet was more pro-inflammatory because of the similar DII and higher level of some inflammatory biomarkers than omnivorous diet, the energy adjusted DII and energy matched theoretical DII were significantly reduced in vegetarian diet indicating the anti-inflammatory effect of vegetarian diet.

The existing vegetarian diet did not exert the expected anti-inflammatory effect, probably due to the low total energy intake in vegetarians. According to the Chinese Dietary Reference Intakes for Nutrients10, the daily energy intake of young adult manual workers aged 18–49 years should reach 2024 kcal, while the average energy intake of vegetarians was only 1368 kcal in this study. In addition, the consumption of vegetables and fruits containing a large amount of anti-inflammatory nutrients11,12 e.g., vitamin C, vitamin E and anthocyanidins by vegetarians were typically low. In fact, after adjusted for energy intake and used energy matched diets, E-DII and energy matched theoretical DII were significantly lower in vegetarians than in omnivores showing the anti-inflammatory effect of the vegetarian diet.

Another reason accounting for not seeing the expected anti-inflammatory effect in the current study might be due to the unbalanced intakes of various food groups in vegetarians. For example, the intakes of fruits, vegetables, and nuts, which should be included more in a vegetarian diet, were not significantly higher in vegetarian diet. Consequently, the nutrient intakes of vitamin C, vitamin E, various phytochemicals such as beta-carotene and anthocyanins, and dietary fiber would be lower. On the other hands, vegetarians are usually at a disadvantage in obtaining some anti-inflammatory minerals such as Se and Zn and anti-inflammatory unsaturated fatty acids DHA and EPA from the diet because they do not consume seafood and animal viscera. From cultural perspective, vegetarian diets in China are often associated with religious beliefs or personal interests and have only begun to be treated as a dietary pattern for everyone in recent years. It is possible that a biased nutritional concepts among vegetarians, i.e., thinking that vegetarianism means eating only vegetables, or overconsuming staple foods could result in an unbalanced diet in vegetarians.

In the present study, TNF-α, IL-6, NLR and PLR were significantly higher in vegetarians than in omnivores while CRP was not statistically different between two groups. In most previous studies, CRP was the main inflammatory biomarker assessed and was consistently found lower in vegetarians than in omnivores. For example, in a cross-sectional study2 including 99 vegetarians and 99 matched non-vegetarians, serum hsCRP was significantly lower in vegetarians (p = 0.025). In another study of 282 individuals8, with 66 vegetarians, 102 ovo-vegetarians and 100 non-vegetarians, serum CRP levels were the lowest in the vegetarian group followed by ovo-vegetarian group and then non-vegetarian group (p = 0.007). However, in the present study, we adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol, physical activity in two models. There were no significant associations between CRP and vegetarian diet. (P > 0.05).

Currently, there is limited evidence on the TNF-α and IL-6 levels in vegetarians and the results are inconsistent. For example, to our best knowledge, only one study has measured the IL-6 level in vegetarians and reported significant higher IL-6 levels in healthy vegetarians than omnivores4. Several studies have measured TNF-α 4,8,13, however no study has reported significant differences in TNF-α between vegetarians and omnivores. In the current study, Chinese vegetarians seem to have higher TNF-α and IL-6 level however more research is warranted. NLR and PLR are two emerging biomarkers in recent years measuring the level of systemic inflammation in the organism14 and received more attention due to easily accessible and cost-effective 15. By measuring lymphocytes, neutrophils, and platelets, we found that the level of NLR and PLR was significantly higher in vegetarians than in omnivores for the first time. Few studies have examined the effects of different dietary patterns on NLR and PLR. A study16 found that a six-month individualized Mediterranean diet intervention did not significantly change the NLR and PLR of participants. In addition, another study17 found that increased polyphenol intake reduced elevated NLR and PLR. The higher NLR and PLR in vegetarians in this study might be due to inadequate intake of phytochemicals, including polyphenols.

The strength of this study is that it is one of the first studies using vegetarian population data in mainland China and has a large sample size, including 279 vegetarian age- and sex-matched vegetarian and omnivore pairs. This study contains comprehensive high-quality data including serological testing, detailed questionnaires and dietary assessments, and anthropometric testing. This study included a broad range of inflammatory biomarkers to analyze levels of chronic inflammation in vegetarians and omnivores. In addition, various dietary indexes, e.g., DII, E-DII, and theoretical DII were used to assess the inflammatory potential of the vegetarian diet.

The limitation of this study is that it is a cross-sectional study, and no cause-effect relationship can be tested. Also, we only used the 24 h dietary recall questionnaire to collect dietary information. Conducting 3 day 24 h food recall questionnaire or Food Frequency Questionnaire may be a better choice. Furthermore, the population selected for this study was a healthy population aged 18–60 years, which may not be extrapolated to other age groups.

In conclusion, this study is the first comprehensive analysis of inflammatory status in a vegetarian population in mainland China. Differences in TNF-α, IL-6, NLR and PLR were observed between vegetarians and omnivores. Meanwhile, E-DII and DII were both significantly lower in vegetarians after energy correction or recipe redesign to achieve a balanced intake of vegetarians and omnivores, suggesting a stronger anti-inflammatory effect. In the future, randomized controlled trials maybe needed to compare the anti-inflammatory effects of different vegetarian diet types to further explore the anti-inflammatory effects of vegetarian diets for the prevention of chronic diseases.

Methods

Study population

This was a cross-sectional study that included healthy vegetarians and their matched omnivores. A total of 282 vegetarians were recruited through online and offline in Shanghai during March to May 2016. Recruitment was initiated online by collaborating with vegetarian advocacy groups, utilizing internet advertisements and WeChat for outreach. Additionally, offline recruitment was conducted at events organized by these groups and at vegetarian restaurants. Meanwhile, 282 omnivores of the same sex as the vegetarians, with an age difference of 2 years or less and a similar lifestyle other than diet, were recruited among the friends and relatives of the vegetarians as a control group.

The inclusion criteria for both groups were: (1) Self-reported as a vegetarian (no restrictions on type of vegetarian) for more than one year. (2) Living in Shanghai more than six months. (3) Age 18–60 years old. (4) Sufficient literacy level to understand the content of the questionnaire.

The exclusion criteria for both groups were: (1) Diagnosed with disorders of nutrient absorption and abnormal energy metabolism. (2) Women who were pregnant or lactating in the past year (3) Diagnosed with precancerous lesions or cancer.

The participants in this study were drawn from our previous research. Therefore, G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich-Heine-Univesität Düsseldorf, Germany) “post hoc” was used to evaluate the power of the sample size. Assuming an α level of 5% and β level of 20%, and a medium effect size (d = 0.5), the power of sample size reached 0.99.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. All the research procedures were performed with the latest revised Declaration of Helsinki (2013). We confirm that informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

Data collection

A questionnaire was conducted face-to-face by professional dietitians to collect general conditions of the participants for potential confounders, including gender, age, education (associate and below, bachelor's, and master’s and above), duration of vegetarian diet (years), smoking (never smoked, ever or currently smoking), physical exercise (low, medium, high). Anthropometric measurements were obtained using RGZ-120 stadiometer to measure the participant’s height to the nearest 0.1 cm and Inbody 720 (Biospace) to measure the participant’s weight to the nearest 0.01 kg. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated by weight (kg)/height2 (m2).

Dietary intake measurement

The 24 h dietary recall method was used in this study. The types and amounts of all foods consumed by the study participants in the 24 h prior to the survey were recorded on a one-to-one basis by a researcher trained in nutritional dietary surveys. These items included staple foods, dairy products, soy products, vegetables and fruits, eggs, beverages, edible oils and condiments consumed during breakfast, lunch, dinner and extra meals. For omnivores, meat, poultry, and aquatic products were also included. If the respondents' diet varied greatly from their habitual diet within 24 h (e.g., eating out or fasting), the most typical day in the past 72 h was selected for recording.

DII and E-DII

Using collected dietary data, nutrient intake tables were created containing 28 dietary components required for the calculation of DII. Individual dietary component intakes were calculated based on Chinese Food Composition18 and the US 2017–2018 food and nutrient database to provide the basis for subsequent DII calculations. The calculation of DII showed in the next paragraph was based on an adjusted version by the University of South Carolina Cancer Prevention and Control Program group in 201319.

In this study, considering the high intake of plant foods in vegetarians, we selected 28 dietary components for DII calculations, including energy, carbohydrate, protein, fat, saturated fat, monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA), ω-3 fatty acid, ω-6 fatty acid, cholesterol, dietary fiber, vitamin A, vitamin B1, vitamin B2, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E, niacin, folic acid, iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg), selenium (Se), zinc (Zn), β-carotene, soy isoflavones and anthocyanins. For each dietary component, the global mean intake and its standard deviation were obtained based on the average of nutrition surveys across multiple countries. At the same time, the effect score for each dietary component was calculated based on its pro- and anti-inflammatory effect reported by a vast amount of literature. The data of ‘global mean intake’ ‘global standard deviation’ and ‘effect score’ were provided by the Shivappa.19. Then, The DII score for each dietary component was calculated as follow: The intake of the dietary components from the 24 h dietary recall was subtracted by its “global mean intake” and divided by its “global standard deviation” to obtain a z score. The z-score was converted to a percentile to minimize the effect of possible statistical right skewing, and the value was doubled and then subtracted by 1 and finally multiplied by the ‘effect score’. Afterwards, DII was obtained by summing up all 28 “DII scores for each dietary component”. Theoretical DII

In order to explore DII in a vegetarian diet with adequate energy intake and a balanced range of foods, we create a theoretical DII. Each of the 14 professional registed dietitians designed one vegetarian recipe and one omnivorous recipe, which are energy-equivalent(1800 kcal), based on the Vegetarian Dietary Guidelines and Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents. We used nutrients intake from recipes to calculate DII. We defined it as theoretical DII.

Inflammatory biomarkers

This study used a comprehensive set of serum inflammatory biomarkers: CRP, IL-6,TNF-α, NLR and PLR. Blood samples were collected from participants after at least 8 h of fasting and were centrifuged at 4000 × for 10 min. CRP, IL-6 and TNF-α were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (BioTNT, Shanghai, China) according to the kit instructions. NLR and PLR were from calculation.

Statistical analysis

Data were compared between vegetarians and omnivores. Samples with missing data were excluded. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean and standard derivation (SD) and tested using paired samples t-test. Skewed data were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) and tested using Wilcoxon paired rank test. Categorical data were expressed as number and percentage (%) and compared using chi-square test.

To compare the differences in inflammatory biomarkers between vegetarians and omnivores, skewed inflammatory biomarkers data including CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, NLR and PLR were log-transformed. We conducted multiple linear regression analyses to adjust for confounders and thus reduce confounding bias. Model 1 adjusted variables including age, sex and BMI. Model 2 further adjusted for variables education, smoking status, physical activity. Results from linear regression models were presented as β-coefficients and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

SPSS 22.0 was used for all the statistical analysis. All tests were two-sided, and the test level was set at 0.05. P < 0.05 was defined as a statistically significant difference.
