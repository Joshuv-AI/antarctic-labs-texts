Systemic immune-inflammatory index is independently associated with two-year mortality after periprosthetic femoral fracture
Authors: Julián Carlos Segura-Nuez, Juan Segura-Nuez, Carlos Martín-Hernández, Adrián Roche-Albero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriprosthetic femoral fractures (PFF) occur predominantly in older, frail patients and are associated with substantial mortality. While age, comorbidity, and frailty are established predictors of adverse outcomes, the prognostic value of systemic inflammatory markers in PFF remains unclear. This study aimed to identify independent predictors of 24-month mortality following PFF and to evaluate whether the Systemic Immune-Inflammatory Index (SII) is associated with mortality after adjustment for established clinical risk factors.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted including 186 consecutive patients admitted with PFF between 2019 and 2023. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 24 months. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify predictors of mortality. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-index and internally validated with bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations).ResultsDuring 24-month follow-up, 55 patients died (29.6%). In multivariable analysis, age (HR 1.06 per year; 95% CI 1.02–1.11; p = 0.003), male sex (HR 2.23; 95% CI 1.27–3.93; p = 0.006), and frailty (Clinical Frailty Scale; HR 1.69 per unit; 95% CI 1.35–2.11; p < 0.001) were independently associated with mortality. SII/100 remained independently associated with mortality after adjustment (HR 1.02; 95% CI 1.00–1.04; p = 0.027). Model discrimination was good (C-index 0.769), with minimal optimism after internal validation (corrected C-index 0.759).ConclusionsTwo-year mortality after PFF is primarily associated with patient-related vulnerability factors, including age, sex, and frailty. SII was independently associated with mortality after adjustment for established clinical predictors. However, the incremental improvement in model discrimination was modest, and further studies are required to determine its clinical utility and potential role in prognostic modelling.

Introduction

Periprosthetic femoral fractures (PFF) are a severe and increasingly prevalent complication in the context of population ageing and the expanding use of hip and knee arthroplasty [1–4]. These injuries occur predominantly in older, frail patients and are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality comparable to fragility hip fractures [5].

Previous studies have consistently identified advanced age, male sex, baseline functional impairment, clinical frailty, and comorbidity burden as major determinants of adverse outcomes following PFF [2–4, 6]. In this population, mortality frequently occurs within the first year after fracture, with reported 1-year rates ranging from 10% to 30% [3, 4, 6, 7]. Given the complex medical profile of these patients, management typically requires a multidisciplinary approach integrating orthopaedics, geriatrics, anaesthesiology, and rehabilitation services to optimise perioperative care, support postoperative recovery, reduce preventable complications, and facilitate functional rehabilitation [2, 8–10].

Beyond traditional clinical predictors, systemic inflammatory biomarkers derived from routine blood counts—such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and the Systemic Immune-Inflammatory Index (SII)—have emerged as potential indicators of biological vulnerability in older adults [11–16]. Elevated inflammatory indices have been associated with increased complications and mortality after hip fracture and arthroplasty, suggesting that inflammatory burden may provide additional biological information beyond age and comorbidity alone [11–16]. However, evidence regarding the prognostic value of inflammatory markers in patients with PFF remains limited.

The primary aim of this study was to identify independent predictors of 24-month mortality following periprosthetic femoral fracture. A secondary aim was to evaluate whether the Systemic Immune-Inflammatory Index (SII), measured at admission, is independently associated with mortality after adjustment for established clinical risk factors including age, sex, and clinical frailty.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study represents the development and internal validation of a multivariable prognostic model for 24-month mortality following periprosthetic femoral fracture (PFF).

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study including all consecutive patients admitted with a PFF between January 2019 and November 2023 at a single tertiary referral centre. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement and adhered to TRIPOD recommendations for prognostic model development.

Patients were eligible if they had a confirmed diagnosis of PFF and available follow-up data up to 24 months or until death. Fractures secondary to malignancy (primary bone tumours or metastatic disease) or metabolic bone disease other than osteoporosis were excluded. One patient was excluded due to loss to follow-up. Data were extracted from electronic medical records and independently reviewed by two investigators to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Outcome

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 24 months following PFF. Survival time was calculated from the date of admission to the date of death or to 24-month follow-up.

Candidate predictors

Predictors were pre-specified based on clinical plausibility and prior evidence regarding mortality after fragility and periprosthetic fractures. Demographic variables included age and sex.

Baseline vulnerability was assessed using Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [17], American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification [18] and Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) [19]. These instruments were selected because they evaluate complementary dimensions of baseline vulnerability, including comorbidity burden, perioperative physiological status, and frailty. Given conceptual overlap between these measures, they were not included simultaneously in the same multivariable model. The Clinical Frailty Scale was selected for the base model because it provides a multidimensional assessment of physiological reserve and has demonstrated strong prognostic value in geriatric fracture populations.

Laboratory parameters obtained at admission included haemoglobin concentration, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, and platelet count. From these, inflammatory indices were calculated:

Systemic Immune-Inflammatory Index (SII) = platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte countNeutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) = neutrophil count / lymphocyte count

All laboratory values corresponded to the first blood sample obtained at hospital admission, prior to surgery. In our institution, blood tests are routinely obtained on arrival to the emergency department when a femoral fracture is suspected. The interval between injury and blood sampling was not systematically recorded and therefore could not be adjusted for in the analysis.

Continuous predictors were retained on their original scale. Linearity with the log hazard was assessed graphically and, when appropriate, using restricted cubic splines.

Model development strategy

Time-to-event analysis was performed using Cox proportional hazards regression. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. A base clinical model including age, sex, and CFS was constructed based on clinical relevance and prior evidence. The SII was subsequently added to the base model to assess its association with mortality after adjustment for established clinical predictors and to explore its potential contribution to prognostic modelling. SII was rescaled (SII/100) to facilitate interpretation, and hazard ratios are reported per 100-unit increase. The number of predictors included in multivariable models was restricted to maintain an events-per-variable ratio ≥ 10, thereby limiting the risk of overfitting. No automated variable selection procedures were used.

Model performance and validation

Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-index. Calibration was evaluated by comparing predicted and observed survival probabilities at 24 months using calibration plots. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) to estimate optimism-corrected discrimination.

Missing data and statistical analysis

Missing data were minimal and considered non-differential; therefore, a complete-case analysis was performed. All analyses were conducted using Jamovi (version 2.6). Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were performed using Python (lifelines package).

Results

Patient cohort

Between January 2019 and November 2023, 187 patients with periprosthetic femoral fracture were assessed for eligibility. One patient was excluded due to loss to follow-up, resulting in a final cohort of 186 patients (Fig. 1). The cohort comprised 129 women (69.4%) with a mean age of 81.6 years (SD 9.7). Multivariable analysis

In the multivariable Cox model including age, sex, and CFS, all three variables were independently associated with mortality. After addition of SII/100, the inflammatory index remained independently associated with 24-month mortality (HR 1.02 per 100-unit increase, 95% CI 1.00–1.04; p = 0.027). The effect estimates for age, sex, and frailty remained stable in magnitude and direction (Table 4).

Model performance

The base clinical model (age, sex, and CFS) demonstrated good discrimination (C-index 0.767). After addition of SII/100, discrimination increased marginally to 0.769. Bootstrap internal validation using 1000 resamples demonstrated minimal optimism (0.011), resulting in an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.759. Calibration plots showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed survival at 24 months.

Kaplan–Meier analysis

For descriptive purposes, SII/100 was dichotomised at the cohort median (12.4). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated lower survival in patients with SII above the median; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (log-rank p = 0.164), supporting the decision to model SII as a continuous variable in subsequent analyses. Discussion

In this cohort of 186 patients with PFF, we observed a two-year mortality of 29.6%, underscoring the substantial vulnerability of this population. These figures are consistent with contemporary series reporting one-year mortality between 10% and 30% and two-year mortality approaching 30% in hip PFF cohorts [1, 2, 4, 5, 9, 20–23]. Beyond mortality, PFF has also been associated with high rates of acute medical complications and postoperative morbidity in several contemporary cohorts [3, 24, 25]. Collectively, these findings reinforce the concept that PFF behaves clinically more like a fragility fracture than an isolated mechanical event [1, 2, 4, 5, 20, 26].

Patient-related determinants of mortality

In our multivariable survival model, age, male sex, and frailty (CFS) were independently associated with 24-month mortality. This aligns with prior studies demonstrating that chronological age [21, 24, 25] and male sex [5, 24, 25] confer increased mortality risk following PFF. Several series have also highlighted the prognostic importance of baseline functional impairment and vulnerability [6, 9].

Importantly, although comorbidity burden (CCI) was associated with mortality in univariable analysis, it did not retain independent significance after adjustment for frailty. This pattern suggests partial conceptual overlap between comorbidity and frailty domains and supports prior observations that frailty may offer superior prognostic discrimination compared with comorbidity indices alone in orthopaedic populations [22, 24]. These findings emphasise that global physiological reserve, rather than fracture-specific characteristics, predominantly determines long-term survival after PFF. From a clinical perspective, systematic frailty assessment and early multidisciplinary optimisation appear essential in patients with PFF. Inflammatory profiling using routinely available biomarkers such as SII may help characterize biological vulnerability and could be explored in future prognostic models, although its clinical utility remains to be established [21, 27].

Consistent with previous reports [9, 23, 24], fracture location and prosthetic factors were not independently associated with mortality when patient-related characteristics were considered.

Systemic immune-inflammatory index

A central aim of this study was to evaluate the prognostic role of the SII in PFF. To our knowledge, this represents the first analysis of SII specifically in a periprosthetic femoral fracture population.

SII/100 remained associated with two-year mortality after adjustment for age, sex, and frailty. Although the magnitude of the hazard ratio per 100-unit increase was modest, the association persisted after accounting for established clinical predictors. This finding aligns with emerging evidence from hip fracture and arthroplasty literature demonstrating associations between elevated inflammatory indices (including SII and NLR) and adverse postoperative outcomes and mortality [11–16].

Systemic inflammatory activation likely reflects underlying biological stress, immune dysregulation, and diminished physiological reserve. In this context, SII may reflect aspects of biological stress and vulnerability that are not fully captured by conventional clinical assessments. Importantly, modelling SII as a continuous variable preserved statistical power and avoided arbitrary threshold selection, consistent with methodological recommendations in biomarker research [11, 12].

Although SII remained statistically associated with mortality, the incremental improvement in model discrimination was small (C-index 0.767 to 0.769). Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of substantial improvement in prognostic performance. Rather, they suggest that inflammatory burden may be associated with mortality risk and warrant further investigation in larger cohorts with external validation.

Model performance

The multivariable model demonstrated good discrimination (C-index 0.769), with minimal optimism after bootstrap validation (optimism-corrected C-index 0.759). These values fall within the range reported in previous prognostic studies in PFF and related fragility fracture populations [22]. Given the modest sample size and the limited number of predictors included, the stability observed in internal validation supports the robustness of the model. However, the modest number of events and absence of external validation limit the generalisability of the findings.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include consecutive case inclusion, complete 24-month follow-up, and a structured modelling approach incorporating frailty, demographic variables, and inflammatory burden. To our knowledge, this is the first prognostic analysis in PFF integrating clinical frailty assessment with SII.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-centre design may limit generalisability and introduce selection bias. Second, SII was calculated from a single blood sample obtained at admission, and temporal changes in inflammatory status could not be assessed. Third, the interval between injury and blood sampling was not systematically recorded and may have influenced inflammatory marker levels. Fourth, despite adjustment for age, sex, and frailty, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—including pre-existing inflammatory conditions, perioperative complications, and other clinical variables—cannot be excluded. Finally, the relatively limited sample size and number of events may have reduced statistical power and restricted evaluation of more complex prognostic models. In addition, only ten deaths occurred within the first 30 days after fracture, precluding a reliable assessment of the association between SII and very early mortality.

The modest incremental improvement in discrimination further suggests that SII should currently be regarded as a research marker rather than a clinically actionable prognostic tool. External validation in larger multicentre cohorts is required before routine clinical implementation can be considered.

Conclusion

In patients with periprosthetic femoral fractures, long-term mortality is predominantly associated with patient-related vulnerability factors, including age, male sex, and frailty. SII was independently associated with two-year mortality after adjustment for established clinical predictors. However, the incremental improvement in model discrimination was modest, and the clinical utility of SII for risk prediction remains uncertain. These findings support further investigation of inflammatory biomarkers in prognostic research involving patients with periprosthetic femoral fractures, ideally through larger multicentre studies with external validation.
