Do scales measuring internet addiction give compatible results? Comparison of two internet addiction scales among medical students
Authors: Ugurcan Sayili, Busra Kara, Ferdane Seyma Toplu, Sevde Nur Yilmaz Kose, Kevser Sak, Fevzi Duhan Berkan Kilickan, Gunay Can
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInternet addiction; excessive and uncontrolled use of the internet is a global problem that affects different segments of the population at different levels. There are many measurement tools in the literature that assess individuals’ internet addiction. The aim of this study was to analyze the agreement and compare the characteristics and results of the two most commonly used scales developed by Young and Chen to measure internet addiction.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between March 2023 and June 2023 among 5th- and 6th-year medical school students. The questionnaire consisted of a section that included questions about the sociodemographic and life characteristics of the students and the Young Internet Addiction Test (IAT) and the Chen Internet Addiction Scale (CIAS-R).ResultsThe prevalence of Internet addiction in 244 medical students was 15% according to the Young IAT with 50 as the cutoff, 56% according to the Young IAT with 30 as the cutoff and 11% according to the CIAS-R. There was moderate agreement (kappa coefficient: 0.50) between the CIAS-R and Young IAT (50); there was slight agreement (kappa coefficient: 0.17) between the CIAS-R and Young IAT (30). In addition, there was no significant difference between the Young IAT (50) and CIAS-R results. There was a significant, strong correlation (r = 0.81) between the Young IAT (50) and the CIAS-R. Although there was moderate agreement between the Young IAT (50) and CIAS, the agreement between the Young IAT (30) and CIAS-R was poor.ConclusionsThis study showed that the Young IAT and CIAS-R, which are most commonly used for determining internet addiction, did not function in as high agreement as expected. We think that the selection of the population to be applied in new scale development studies will contribute to the importance of selecting people with clinical diagnoses and generalizable to the society.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24695-9.

Background

The Internet provides many opportunities to increase people’s access to information and facilitate communication and daily life [1]. As of February 2025, it is estimated that 67.9% of the world’s population, i.e., 5.56 billion people, are internet users, and the number of people accessing the internet continues to rise [2]. However, excessive or unlimited use of the Internet can lead to Internet addiction, which can cause significant distress and functional impairments in daily life [1]. Internet addiction, also called “problematic Internet use” or “pathological Internet use”, is a global problem that affects different segments of the population at varying levels.

The term Internet addiction was first introduced by Ivan Goldberg in 1996, initially as a satirical concept adapted from the diagnostic criteria for substance addiction in DSM-IV [3]. Research on Internet addiction was first conducted by Dr. Kimberley Young in the USA in 1996; it was defined by adapting from the diagnostic criteria for pathological gambling in DSM-IV [4]. Young defined internet addiction as the inability to control the desire to use the internet, excessive use, an increasing need for time spent online, trivializing time spent offline, and experiencing irritability, tension, and restlessness when deprived, along with disruptions in school, work, social, and family life [5].

The American Psychiatric Association (APA) included Internet Gaming Disorder in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 (DSM-5); however, other Internet-related pathologies, such as Internet addiction, were excluded, despite being the subject of ongoing debate in the field. This exclusion highlights the need for further investigation and research to better understand and classify these conditions [6]. In addition, based on clinical evidence and public health needs, along with the demand to provide preventive measures and identify therapeutic approaches, the World Health Organization (WHO) decided to include gaming disorder in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD-11). Considering these developments, additional studies should be conducted in the field of internet addiction [7].

The prevalence of internet addiction varies among various societies and age groups. According to a meta-analysis investigating the prevalence of Internet addiction in the general population worldwide, the prevalence of addiction was reported to be 14.22%, while a study in China reported a prevalence of 36.7% in the general population [1, 8]. According to meta-analysis studies, the prevalence of internet addiction among medical students in different countries was 30.1%, and the prevalence of internet addiction among Chinese university students was 11.3% [9, 10]. Studies on adolescents from various countries report internet addiction rates between 3.0% and 29%, with higher rates observed in more recent studies, which may reflect increased internet usage among young people over the past decade [11, 12]. Recent studies conducted in Türkiye have indicated that the rate of internet addiction in adolescents varies between 10.2% and 38.2% [13–15].

The wide variation in internet addiction prevalence across societies and age groups can be attributed to several methodological factors. These include the use of various assessment instruments, inconsistent cutoff points across studies and cultures, and differences in population characteristics [16]. Additionally, existing scales measure different dimensions of addictive behavior, contributing to the observed discrepancies in reported rates [9–11, 17].

In the literature, there are many scales assessing the internet addiction of individuals worldwide; the two most commonly used tests are the Young Internet Addiction Test (Young IAT) and the Chen Internet Addiction Scale-Revised (CIAS-R) [18]. These two scales have been used in many language and cultural adaptation studies and are reported to be valid and reliable. However, this approach is based purely on correlations, validity and reliability coefficients, without clinical validation of the internet addiction diagnosis that these scales purport to measure.

The aim of this study was to analyze the agreement and compare the characteristics and results of the two most commonly used scales developed by Young and Chen to measure internet addiction. In addition, the study will examine the effects of demographic characteristics such as sex, age, body mass index, income status and habits such as frequency of reading books and physical activity on internet addiction.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study included 5th- and 6th-year medical students at Istanbul University-Cerrahpasa, Faculty of Medicine, between March 2023 and June 2023. The medical school in which this study was conducted has a large and diverse student population, comprising individuals from multiple regions of Türkiye; and the accessibility of this population facilitated comprehensive data collection.

The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi with the following parameters: 95% confidence interval (95% CI), expected prevalence (15%) and precision of the estimate (5%). A sample size of 196 was calculated. A design effect of 1.2 was taken to address missing data or an empty survey, and the sample size was determined to be 236.

The researchers administered the questionnaires face-to-face in the classrooms. Questions about the sociodemographic status and life characteristics of the students were asked (Supplementary File). The Young Internet Addiction Scale and the Chen Internet Addiction Scale was used.

Measures

Demographic variables

The demographic variables were sex, age, and grade, income status, body mass index (kg/m2).

Lifestyle Variables

Students were asked about their frequency of reading books and responses were recoded into three categories: once a month or less, once a month, and more than once a month. Physical activity was assessed as either sufficient or insufficient. Insufficient physical activity was defined as not engaging in exercise for at least 60 min twice per week.

Health and psychological measures

Students were asked, “Would you say your health is…?” for evaluating health status. The available response options included excellent, good, moderate, and bad. Responses were coded as bad/moderate or good/excellent. To evaluate body image perception, students were asked, “Do you think your body is ….?” Their response options on a 5-point Likert scale ranged from (1) “much too thin” to (5) “much too fat”. Responses were coded thin, normal and overweight [19]. To evaluate life satisfaction, students were asked, “Where on the ladder do you feel you stand at the moment?” for measuring Life satisfaction. The measure is visually represented as a ladder with 11 steps, ranging from 0 to 10. A score of 10 denotes the ‘best life possible’ for the individual, while a score of 0 signifies the ‘worst possible life’ [20]. According to the students’ answers to the Life Satisfaction question, values of 7 and above were grouped as high life satisfaction. Students were asked “At what level do you currently feel your quality of life is?” to score between 0 and 100.

Young internet addiction test (IAT)

The Turkish version of the IAT comprises 20 items aimed at gauging the extent to which Internet abuse affects various daily activities. Boysan et al. conducted a study on the validity and reliability of the Turkish version; a strong association between convergent validity and Cronbach’s alpha was reported as 0.93 [21]. For each question, the surveyed subject must answer on a 6-point Likert scale (rarely, occasionally, frequently, often, always, and does not apply), and each answer can add from 0 to 5 points, resulting in a final score varying from 0 to 100. The cut-off scores of the 6-point scale, as proposed by Young, classify respondents as normal internet users (0–30), mild (31–49), moderate (50–79), or severe (80–100) internet addiction. However, there is limited evidence evaluating the validity and practical utility of these cut-off thresholds [18].

For this study, we used two cutoff approaches: (1) the traditional cutoff of 50, categorizing scores 0–49 as ‘typical users’ and ≥ 50 as ‘problematic users’ [22], and (2) a lower cutoff of 30, distinguishing between regular internet usage (≤ 30) and internet addiction (31–100; mild, moderate and severe) [23].

Chen internet addiction scale-revised (CIAS-R)

The Chen Internet Addiction Scale-Revised (CIAS-R) is a 26-item self-assessment questionnaire with a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (does not fit me at all) to 4 (fits me completely). Ceyhan conducted a study of validity and reliability in Türkiye and reported that the indices were appropriate for confirmatory factor analysis. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.94, the test-retest correlation was high (r = 0.98), and the inter-item correlations were between 0.16 and 0.90, indicating good reliability [24]. The possible range of scale scores is 26–104. A higher score indicates greater severity of addiction to internet activities. The scale consists of five subscales: compulsive use (5 items); withdrawal symptoms (5 items); tolerance (4 items); interpersonal and health-related problems (7 items); and time management problems (5 items) [25]. In previous studies, 64 was used as a cutoff for Chen internet addiction; in this study, a person with a CIAS-R score ≥ 64 was classified as Internet addicted [26].

The Young’s Internet Addiction Test (IAT) is theoretically grounded in the diagnostic criteria for pathological gambling, whereas the Chen Internet Addiction Scale (CIAS) is conceptualized on the basis of diagnostic frameworks encompassing both substance dependence and pathological gambling. Both instruments assess overlapping underlying constructs [17, 18]. The existence of validity and reliability studies conducted in Turkish, the application of these scales to samples similar to the population in our study, and consequently their cultural and population-specific appropriateness, have ensured the selection of these scales.

The study was performed in accordance with the Ethics Guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the local Ethics Committee on 07.03.2023, approval number: E-83045809-604.01.01-638893. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for being included in the study.

Statistical analyses

Data evaluation and analysis were performed utilizing the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 21.0 for Windows (IBM Corp. in Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), and numeric variables are presented as medians (25th percentile–75th percentile). The normal distribution conformity of the variables was assessed through Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, along with Q-Q plot and histogram graphs. Since the continuous variables did not meet the assumptions of normal distribution, the Mann‒Whitney U test was used to compare two independent groups. Pearson’s chi-square test was used for comparisons of categorical independent groups. McNemar’s test was used for comparisons of categorical dependent groups. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (acceptable 0.7–0.95) was used to determine the internal consistency of the scale items [27]. The kappa statistic is commonly employed to assess interrater reliability. Cohen suggested that the kappa coefficient be interpreted as follows: ≤ 0, indicating no agreement; 0.01–0.20, indicating none to slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, indicating fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, indicating moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, indicating substantial agreement; and 0.81–1.00, indicating almost perfect agreement [28]. The Spearman correlation test was used to evaluate the relationships between the total scores of the various scales. The significance level of the statistical tests was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Prevalence

The total prevalence of IA was 11.5% (7.5–15.5%), 16% (11.4–20.6%), and 57.5% (51.3–63.7%) according to the CIAS-R, IAT-50 and IAT-30, respectively. Among females, the total prevalence of IA was 12.9% (5.77–20.03%), 20% (11.5–28.5%), and 64.7% (54.54–74.86%) according to the CIAS-R, IAT-50 and IAT-30, respectively. Among males, the total prevalence of IA was 10.7% (5.9–15.5%), 13.8% (8.44–19.16%) and 52.8% (45.04–60.56%) according to the CIAS-R, IAT-50 and IAT-30, respectively. (Table 1; Fig. 1).

As a result of the Chi-square analysis, according to the Young IAT (50), the percentage of IAs in individuals who read more than once a month (10.3%) was significantly lower than the percentage of IAs in those who read one book a month or less (20.4%), p = 0.032. According to Young IAT (30), the percentage of IAs in individuals who read more than once a month (45.8%) was significantly lower than the percentage of IAs in those who read one book a month or less (65.7%), p = 0.002. According to the CIAS-R, Internet addiction was significantly greater in people who had low life satisfaction (p = 0.018) (Table 2).

Scale agreement

When addiction status was evaluated with the Young IAT (50) vs. CIAS-R, 7.7% of participants were classified as internet addicted by both scales, 8.1% only by Young IAT (50), and 3.6% only by CIAS-R, with 80.3% classified as not internet addicted by either. For Young IAT (30) vs. CIAS-R, 11.4% were classified as internet addicted according to both, while 45.4% were classified as internet addicted only by Young IAT (30); all CIAS-R-positive cases were also positive on the Young IAT (30). Remaining participants were negative on both measures (Fig. 2).

There was moderate agreement, with a kappa coefficient of agreement of 0.50 (95% CI: 0.34–0.66) between the CIAS-R and Young IAT (50); there was slight agreement, with a kappa coefficient of agreement of 0.17 (95% CI: 0.11–0.24) between the CIAS-R and Young IAT (30). McNemar’s test revealed no significant difference in the prevalence rates of internet addiction between the Young IAT (50) and CIAS-R (p = 0.061), indicating similar overall detection rates despite the moderate agreement (kappa = 0.50) between the two instruments. However, McNemar’s test revealed a significant difference in the prevalence rates of internet addiction between the Young IAT (30) and CIAS-R (p < 0.001), with the Young IAT (30) detecting substantially higher rates of addiction, reflecting the poor agreement (kappa = 0.17) between these two approaches (Table 3).

Correlation and reliability

A significant and positive correlation was found between the total scores on the Young Internet Addiction Test and the total scores on the Chen Internet Addiction Scale (p < 0.001, r = 0.81) (Fig. 3). The Cronbach’s alpha value, used to evaluate the internal consistency of the scales, was calculated based on the total scores as 0.88 for the Young Internet Addiction Test and 0.93 for the Chen Internet Addiction Scale.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the disagreements in previous studies on Internet addiction by examining the differences in results between the two most widely used scales, the IAT and CIAS-R. It is found that the total prevalence of IA was 11.5% (7.5–15.5%) and 16% (11.4–20.6%), according to the CIAS-R and Young IAT-50, respectively. Among females, the total prevalence of IA was 12.9% (5.77–20.03%) and 20% (11.5–28.5%) according to the CIAS-R and IAT-50, respectively. Among males, the total prevalence of IA was 10.7% (5.9–15.5%) and 13.8% (8.44–19.16%) according to the CIAS-R and IAT-50, respectively. Many previous studies have reported that the prevalence of internet addiction is greater in males than in females [29, 30]; or that there is no sex difference, as shown in our study [31, 32].

Prevalence estimates among university students demonstrate substantial variation depending on the instrument and cutoff used. Studies using the IAT report rates ranging from 3.4 to 85% depending on the cutoff (30 vs. 50 vs. 70), while CIAS-R studies typically report lower rates (5.6–21.1%) [33–38]. This variation is exemplified in Mak et al.‘s multi-country study, where the same populations showed markedly different prevalence rates: in China, rates varied from 2.2% (IAT-70) to 17.1% (IAT-40) to 9.6% (CIAS-R); similar patterns were observed in South Korea and the Philippines [36]. These findings underscore the critical impact of instrument selection and cutoff criteria on prevalence estimates.

In this study, 7.7% of participants were classified as addicted by both the IAT-50 and the CIAS-R. In addition, 3.6% met the criteria only on the CIAS-R, and 8.1% only on the IAT-50, suggesting limited overlap between the two tools. The reasons for these differences may be that Internet addiction was not clinically diagnosed. The comparison of addiction classifications between the IAT-50 and the CIAS-R using the McNemar test revealed no significant difference (p = 0.061), implying that the two scales identified a similar proportion of individuals as addicted, with a moderate level of agreement (Kappa: 0.50). The Young Internet Addiction Scale emphasises compulsive use and classic addiction symptoms, based on the DSM-IV criteria for pathological gambling, whereas the Chen Internet Addiction Scale focuses on measuring functional impairment and psychosocial consequences. This difference in focus may cause the two scales to measure different dimensions of internet addiction and yield different prevalence rates.

Although a kappa value of 0.50 is statistically classified as a ‘moderate’ level of agreement, this value provides limited reliability for clinical decision-making. In the literature, particularly in health research, it is generally recommended that inter-rater agreement be considered reliable when kappa ≥ 0.60 and percentage agreement ≥ 80% [28]. Therefore, while the value of 0.50 observed in our study indicates a statistically significant overlap beyond chance between the scales, it should be interpreted with caution given the variability in individual-level classifications and its potential impact on clinical decisions [28]. However, when comparing the IAT-30 with the CIAS-R, 11.4% were identified by both, while a notably higher proportion (45.4%) were classified only by the IAT-30, with no unique cases identified by the CIAS-R. The comparison of addiction classifications between the IAT-30 and the CIAS-R using the McNemar test revealed a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001), indicating that the two scales classified participants differently. The low agreement between the scales (Kappa = 0.17) suggests substantial diagnostic inconsistency, with the IAT-30 clearly overdiagnosing internet addiction compared to the CIAS-R. The prevalence of Internet addiction estimated using the Young IAT with a cutoff of 30 (56%) was substantially higher than estimates from both the CIAS-R and the Young IAT with a cutoff of 50. This discrepancy Likely reflects a lower specificity of the 30-point cutoff, resulting in a higher number of false-positive classifications. Although this cutoff has been used in previous studies to identify mild levels of problematic internet use, its application in the present sample may have overestimated the prevalence. Consequently, caution is warranted when interpreting results derived from this threshold, particularly in epidemiological contexts where accurate estimation of population-level prevalence is critical.

The moderate agreement (Kappa: 0.50) observed between the CIAS-R and Young IAT (50) and the slight agreement (Kappa: 0.17) between the CIAS-R and Young IAT (30) indicates a considerable discordance that may challenge clinical decision-making and research interpretations. Clinicians screening for internet addiction in university populations might encounter inconsistent classifications, where some individuals are identified as addicted by only one scale. This variability could lead to under- or over-referral for interventions, affecting resource allocation and patient care. Particularly, the IAT-30 cutoff’s tendency to overestimate addiction prevalence raises concerns about false positives that could unnecessarily burden clinical services. For researchers, discrepancies in scale sensitivity and cutoff values contribute to heterogeneity in prevalence rates across studies, complicating data comparison and synthesis. These findings underscore the necessity of cautious interpretation and highlight the importance of developing standardized screening protocols to improve reliability and validity in both clinical and research settings.

In this study, life satisfaction in non-IA individuals was found to be greater according to the CIAS-R but not according to the IAT. In another study conducted on adolescents in China, the IAT was used, and low life satisfaction was found to be associated with Internet addiction [39]. Deutrom et al. reported that there was no direct relationship between life satisfaction and internet addiction; loneliness mediated this relationship [40].

According to the Young IAT, the rate of IA was found to be lower in those who read more than 1 book per month, but no difference was observed according to the CIAS-R. According to the IAT, the book reading frequency of participants defined as internet addicts was significantly lower than that of those who were not internet addicts (Young 30 (p = 0.002), Young 50 (p = 0.032)). A number of studies carried out in Türkiye have established a strong link between the low rate of reading books and internet addiction [41–43]. In many previous studies, a high income level was shown to be associated with internet addiction [44]. In this study, no relationship was found between income level and internet addiction according to the CIAS-R or the IAT, similar to the findings of other recent studies [45]. This finding may be due to the widespread and less expensive use of technology and the internet.

Limitations and strengths

Several Limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design of our study Limits causal inferences about internet addiction patterns. Second, our sample was restricted to 5th and 6th-year medical students from a single institution, which limits the generalizability of results to other populations, age groups, and educational backgrounds.

Third, this study relied entirely on self-reported data. Participants may have under- or over-reported their internet usage and related problems. Fourth, the absence of a gold standard diagnostic interview conducted by mental health professionals represents a significant limitation, as we could not validate scale results against clinical diagnoses of internet addiction.

Finally, cultural factors may influence scale interpretation, and the absence of clinical diagnoses limits our ability to determine which scale more accurately identifies true cases of internet addiction. However, the primary objective of our study was specifically to evaluate the agreement and differences between these widely used scales, rather than to establish clinical validity, which aligns with our research design and methodology.

Conclusion

Although there was moderate agreement between the Young IAT (50) and CIAS-R, the agreement between the Young IAT (30) and CIAS-R was poor. This study demonstrated that the Young IAT and CIAS-R, which are the most commonly used instruments for determining internet addiction, did not function with the high level of agreement expected in the literature.

Future research should prioritize several specific directions to address these methodological challenges: (1) conducting clinical validation studies that compare scale results with formal diagnostic assessments by mental health professionals; (2) developing culturally adapted versions of existing scales that account for local internet usage patterns and social norms; (3) establishing standardized cutoff points through large-scale, multi-center studies across diverse populations; and (4) employing mixed-methods approaches that combine quantitative assessments with qualitative exploration to provide depth of interpretation as to why scales differ in their definition of ‘addiction.’
