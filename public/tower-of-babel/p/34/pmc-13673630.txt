Draft hybrid genome assembly of the photosynthetic sacoglossan Elysia crispata provides insights into sacoglossan biology
Authors: Padmanabhan Mahadevan, Michael L. Middlebrooks, Nicholas E. Curtis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present here a draft genome of the photosynthetic sacoglossan sea slug, Elysia crispata (ecotype crispata) generated with a reference guided approach using Illumina short reads and long PacBio HiFi reads. The genome size is approximately 671 Mb with an N50 of 460 kb. BUSCO genome completeness was 98.2%, comprising 97.5% single-copy complete and 0.7% duplicated complete metazoan core genes, with an additional 0.7% partial core genes. This genome provided interesting observations such as the possible remnants of an endogenous retrovirus and the presence of polyketide synthesis proteins thought to protect the slug from photosynthetic damage, based on homology with characterized sacoglossan PKS proteins. Few genomes of photosynthetic sacoglossans are available and this draft genome of the E. crispata sea slug will serve as a valuable dataset to study sacoglossan biology, genetics, evolution, and photosymbiosis. In addition, our assembly strategy may serve as a guide to other researchers who also do not have Hi-C data to assemble their genomes, though this approach relies on the availability of a closely related reference genome for scaffolding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13157-0.

Introduction

The biology of sacoglossan sea slugs is notable for several reasons including their role as specialized marine herbivores [1–3], defensive use of algal secondary metabolites [4, 5] and the ability of some species to photosynthesize. The latter activity is possible due to a phenomenon known as kleptoplasty wherein the slugs sequester chloroplasts taken from their algal food inside cells lining their digestive tubules (reviewed in [6, 7]). The slugs are then able to maintain the sequestered chloroplasts for use in functional photosynthesis for a period of time which varies depending on both the slug species and the algae they have consumed [8–12].

When algae is available, slugs may replace chloroplasts by continuous feeding [13, 14]. Other sacoglossans, however, show reduced foraging behavior while they have active functional kleptoplasts [15] and others employ a variety of methods to prolong photosynthesis when algae is not available. For example, many species avoid high intensity light [16, 17] and others employ a combination of opaque parapodia and a behavioral response to shade chloroplasts [18–21] thus reducing photodegradation. Additionally, some sacoglossans have been known to employ biochemical mechanisms for prolonging photosynthesis such as synthesis of chlorophyll [22, 23] or a reversible xanthophyll cycle [20].

One of the most studied kleptoplastic sacoglossans has been Elysia chlorotica, which can maintain functional photosynthesis for over 9 months until the slugs reproduce and senesce [24, 25]. This sacoglossan is highly stenophagous only feeding the algae Vaucheria spp [24, 26]. Multiple studies have found evidence that E. chlorotica possess horizontally transferred algal nuclear genes [6, 22, 27], which offers a potential explanation for how this slug is able to maintain photosynthesis for such a long duration. Several genomic studies, however, have disputed this hypothesis, including the publication of the genome for E. chlorotica [28–30]. Notably, algal nuclear genes were not found in this genome, leaving open the question of how these slugs are able to maintain their kleptoplasts.

Even more intriguing are the few slugs that are able to not only undergo kleptoplasty but are able to do so with chloroplasts from multiple species of distantly related algae. Most notably among these are Plakobranchus spp. an Indo-Pacific species complex which can photosynthesize for a duration of at least 10 months [14] and Elysia crispata a Caribbean species which can photosynthesize for 3–4 months [15, 31]. Both of these slugs are highly polyphagous, able to feed on and sequester chloroplasts from a great variety of coenocytic green macroalgae [32–37]. How these slugs are able to maintain photosynthesis using a varied source of chloroplasts is not entirely clear, although Plakobranchus may be able to prolong the duration of photosynthesis by shading chloroplasts with opaque parapodia or burying in the sediment [14, 21, 38].

Elysia crispata has some ability to use its parapodia to shade chloroplasts [17] and the slug also has biochemical means of prolonging their function by synthesizing chlorophyll [23] an ability which it shares with E. chlorotica [22]. Although differences in the photosynthetic performance of E. crispata have been shown on different algal diets [10, 39], the slug is able to sequester chloroplasts from a great variety of algal sources [31, 32, 37] even sequestering chloroplasts from different algal species within the same cell [32]. Two distinct ecotypes are recognized for E. crispata. One ecotype primarily lives in low energy nearshore environments such as mangrove swamps is known as the clarki ecotype (formerly known as E. clarki [31]), and another associated with higher energy coral reef and reef rubble habitats is known as the crispata ecotype [34, 40]. Here, in an attempt to gain a better understanding of the biology and photosymbiosis of E. crispata, we present a draft genome of E. crispata using crispata ecotype specimens.

Previously, we characterized the sequence of an endogenous retrovirus found in E. chlorotica [41]. Retroviral elements related to fungal viral elements in Rhizophagus irregularis have also found in another sea slug, E. marginata [42]. The exact role of this virus in E. chlorotica has not yet been elucidated, although it may play a role in the annual senescence of this sea slug [43]. In this study, we searched for the presence of this endogenous retrovirus in our draft assembly and investigated whether it is related to the E. chlorotica virus.

Although the E. crispata genome presented here is a draft assembly, it can be a useful comparison with the genomes of other kleptoplastic sacoglossans. The first sequenced sacoglossan genome was the long term kleptoplastic slug E. chlorotica [30]. Following that genomes were sequenced for several more long-term kleptoplastic sacoglossans including E. crispata [44], E. timida [45], and Plakobranchus ocellatus [29] as well as E. marginata which is capable of short-term chloroplast retention [29]. Notably this genome is of particular interest for comparison with the previously published E. crispata genome [44] because the sequenced specimens are different ecotypes adapted for different habitats.

Materials and methods

Collection of specimens and sequencing

Specimens of E. crispata (Fig. 1) were collected by hand from Pickles Reef off of Key Largo, FL via scuba diving at ~5 m depth (24°59’14.51” N, 80°07’53.51” W). All specimens on that reef match the morphological characteristics of the crispata ecotype, most notably the well-developed foot [31, 40]. Slugs were immediately preserved in 100% ethanol until DNA extraction. The E. crispata tissue sample was sent to MrDNA (Shallowater, TX) for DNA extraction and sequencing. DNA was extracted using the PowerSoil® DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen). 250 mg sample was used for DNA extraction. The concentration of DNA was evaluated using the Qubit® dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Life Technologies). The library was prepared using KAPA HyperPlus Kits (Roche) following the manufacturer’s user guide. 25 ng DNA was used to prepare the library. The protocol starts with enzymatic fragmentation to produce dsDNA fragments followed by end repair and A-tailing to produce end-repaired, 5’-phosphorylated, 3’-dA-tailed dsDNA fragments. In the adapter ligation step, dsDNA adapters are ligated to 3’-dA-tailed molecules. The final step is library amplification, which employs high fidelity, low-bias PCR to amplify library fragments carrying appropriate adapter sequences on both ends. Following the library preparation, the final concentration of the library was measured using the Qubit® dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Life Technologies), and the average library size was determined using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). The library was diluted (to 6.0pM) and sequenced paired end for 300 cycles using the HiSeq system (Illumina). Both the Illumina and PacBio HiFi libraries were each prepared from a single individual.

Another sample of E. crispata tissue was sent to MrDNA (Shallowater, TX) for PacBio HiFi sequencing. DNA was extracted using DNeasy Plant Pro kit (Qiagen). 50 mg of sea slug sample was used for DNA extraction. DNA was eluted in 100 µL of EB buffer (Qiagen), and its concentration was measured using the Qubit® dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Life Technologies). DNA size was assessed on an E-Gel SizeSelect 2% Agarose Gel (Invitrogen) alongside a 1 Kb ladder, showing a band > 10 Kb. The sample underwent DNA damage repair using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences), followed by cleanup with the DNeasy PowerClean Pro Cleanup Kit (Qiagen) and re-quantification. Due to low yield, whole-genome amplification was performed with the REPLI-g Midi Kit (Qiagen). The amplified DNA was cleaned again with the PowerClean kit and quantified using Qubit. DNA was then sheared with a Covaris G-tube (Covaris Inc.), and fragment size was verified using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). A total of 1,500 ng of sheared DNA was used for library preparation with the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences), which included DNA damage and end repair, followed by barcode adapter ligation. Unligated fragments were removed by nuclease treatment, and size selection (> 6 Kb) was performed using a 0.75% agarose gel on the BluePippin system (Sage Science). The final library was quantified with the Qubit® dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and sized using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was carried out on the PacBio Sequel II platform (Pacific Biosciences) using a 30-hour movie time.

Assembly and annotation

The short Illumina reads and long HiFi reads were checked for quality using FastQC [46] and they passed the per base quality check. Basic statistics of the read sets were determined using rdeval [47]. The reads were then assembled using the MaSuRCA hybrid assembler v4.1.4 [48] using a synteny guided approach using the recently sequenced high-quality E. crispata reference genome [44]. The MaSuRCA assembler instructions specifically state not to do any cleaning, trimming, or error correcting of the Illumina reads before the assembly, so reads were fed directly into the assembler. BlobTools was then used to remove any bacterial contamination from the assembly [49]. The resulting assembly was then reduced using Redundans [50] to remove alternative heterozygous contigs and additional scaffolding with SAMBA [51]. Further scaffolding using the long reads was done using LongStitch [52] and the resulting assembly was finally scaffolded using reference based scaffolding using RagTag [53]. Genome completeness was assessed using BUSCO v5 [54] with the metazoan ortholog set using the gVolante webserver [55]. The genome was then softmasked using Red [56] and BRAKER3 [57] was used to annotate the genome using evidence from both protein sequence data from the reference E. crispata genome and RNA-Seq data from E. crispata (SRA accession number ERR12342469). The genome was also annotated using the ANNEVO ab-initio annotation tool which has shown very high accuracy [58]. Fatty acid synthase proteins were detected using the blastp option of DIAMOND [59] using E. chlorotica fatty acid synthase proteins as queries. Deacon [60] was used to extract reads from the Illumina and HiFi slug dataset that matched the E. chlorotica endogenous retrovirus. The E. crispata endogenous retrovirus was then assembled using PVGA, a virus focused assembler [61]. The resulting 1050 bp assembly was BLASTed against the core_nt and nr/nt databases in GenBank with E-value threshold of 1e-4. The BLAST hits were then aligned using MAFFT [62] with the --auto option and trimAl [63] was used to trim it using the -automated1 option. Maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed using IQ-TREE2 [64] using the -alrt and -B (ultrafast bootstrap) options with 1000 replicates with automatic model selection. Trees were visualized using the interactive tree of life viewer (iTOL) [65] and ggtree [66] in R. To find each integration site in the assembly, the 1,050 bp PVGA-assembled sequence was searched against the ragtag scaffolds using blastn (e-value cutoff 1 × 10-5), giving us coordinates for each hit. Flanking sequences of up to 10 kb on either side of each boundary were then pulled out with bedtools getfasta [67]. We aligned those flanking sequences to the published E. crispata reference genome (GCA_033675545.1) using minimap2 [68] to check that scaffold placement was consistent and that the gap size matched what would be expected from an ERV insertion. Read coverage across the combined flanking-plus-viral regions was assessed by mapping Illumina and HiFi reads with minimap2 and calculating per-base depth using Mosdepth [69].

The HI-FEVER endogenous viral element discovery pipeline [70] was used to identify high-confidence endogenous retroviral elements using four criteria: element_type = “likely-retro”, rvdb_fams contains “Retroviridae”, n_retros ≥ 5, and peptide_len ≥ 100 aa, yielding 110, 148 and 117 high-confidence hits for the E. crispata ragtag, E. crispata reference and E. chlorotica assemblies respectively. Protein sequences annotated as reverse transcriptase (RT) or pol polyprotein were extracted from the HI-FEVER output for each dataset and combined with the Elysia chlorotica virus reference RT (AQN68906.1; KY115607), yielding 92 query sequences in total. These were searched using DIAMOND blastp against the MINI-nr database (a clustered, taxonomically annotated subset of NCBI nr provided with the HI-FEVER pipeline), retaining hits with PID ≥ 30% and keeping the top 5 hits per query. Hit sequences were downloaded from NCBI using efetch and combined with the 92 query sequences to produce a final dataset of 185 sequences for phylogenetic analysis using IQ-TREE2.

Putative PKS proteins were identified by using the E. chlorotica PKS protein (QMU95645.1) as a query to DIAMOND blastp against our assembly proteins. InterProScan [71] (Pfam + SMART [72]) analysis was performed on the PKS proteins identified by DIAMOND. These identified PKS proteins were then compared against the Swiss-Prot database using DIAMOND blastp [73]. Swiss-Prot was used to obtain high confidence functional annotations since it is less redundant as well as manually reviewed and curated. A phylogenetic tree was built from the resulting hits using IQ-TREE2.

Slug genome coverage was calculated using Mosdepth using a BAM file generated by aligning the short and long reads to the genome assembly using Minimap2 and SAMtools [74]. The EBI Needle program [75] was used to calculate global pairwise percent identities between DNA/protein sequences. All programs were run with default options unless specified.

To search for potential horizontally transferred nuclear encoded algal chloroplast genes, two methods were employed. First, a list of potential nuclear encoded algal chloroplast genes was compiled by downloading all relevant chlorophyta gene sequences from GenBank using the keywords “chloroplast” “nuclear encoded”. This was combined with a previously constructed database of potential chloroplast genes (nuclear and chloroplast encoded) from an unpublished genome and transcriptome of the algal species Vaucheria litorea [76]. This dataset was searched against the raw, unassembled de novo Illumina reads from this study using the contaminant removal tool (Bowtie2 software and Qualimap [77, 78]) from the metagenomics module of OmicsBox 2.1.14 [79] to isolate potential gene matches. The resulting isolated reads were then identified using the blastx function in Diamond [59].

Second, eukaryotic genes were predicted from the partially assembled genome using AUGUSTUS [80] against the Chlamydomonas reinhardtii genome. The resulting gene sequences were annotated by first using the blastx function in Diamond [59]. Sequences were then mapped to GO terminology using the BLAST2GO [81] pipeline in the functional analysis module of Omicsbox 2.1.14 [79]. Annotated sequences were isolated by GO term “chloroplast” in Omicsbox and manually examined to determine if they were of nuclear origin.

Results

Raw read characteristics

The Illumina read set consisted of 473,634,538 reads with average read length of 128 bp and total read length of 60,041,781,906 bp and average per base quality of 27. The HiFi read set consisted of 2,176,412 reads with average read length of 7867 bp and total read length of 17,122,112,483 bp and average per base quality of 29. When we tried to assemble only the HiFi reads with hifiasm [82], which has been shown to be a top performer for assembling eukaryotic genomes [83], the resulting assembly was only 221 Mb in size which was far smaller than the expected size of 650-700 Mb. Therefore, we decided upon a hybrid assembly approach using both the short and long reads to maximize the quality and contiguity of the assembly.

Assembly characteristics

The main bacterial contaminants in the initial assembly before Redundans reduction were Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Bacillota. After filtering these out using BlobTools, the resulting contigs were reduced using Redundans. The E. crispata genome was approximately 671 Mb in size with a GC content of 36%. The N50 value was 460 kb, complete BUSCO recovery was 98.2%, and complete plus partial BUSCO recovery was 99.0% (Table 1). In comparison, the previously sequenced E. chlorotica genome [30] was 557 Mb with a comparable N50 of 442 kb. The average coverage across our genome was approximately 65X.

K-mer analysis using Merqury [84] showed a quality value (QV) of approximately 40 for our assembly which corresponds to 99.99% accuracy and an error rate of 9.86E-5. The higher the QV, the more accurate the consensus assembly is and the value of 40 for our assembly indicates an assembly with higher accuracy. The k-mer completeness value was 74.6% which is less than the desired > = 90–95% completeness, indicating that our assembly is less complete than optimal.

When compared with the reference E. crispata genome [44], our genome is somewhat less contiguous with 11,868 contigs versus 8089 in the reference. However, the N50 is comparable at 460 kbp compared to 458 kb for the reference genome. In terms of protein coding genes, there were 19,624 predicted by ANNEVO achieving 92.7% complete metazoan BUSCOs and 25,548 predicted by BRAKER3 achieving 89.7% complete metazoan BUSCOs. This is in contrast to 68,514 protein coding genes in the reference genome (27,767 in the high confidence gene set). The ANNEVO annotation showed markedly less duplication (0.7%) versus 12.7% in the BRAKER3 annotation, indicating high-quality non-redundant gene models produced by ANNEVO. Therefore, we decided to use the ANNEVO annotation for subsequent downstream analyses. Finally, the size of our final assembly is 671 Mb while the reference genome size is 786 Mb. The GC content for both genomes was 36%. The distance between the assembled genome and the reference genome using the Mash distance metric [85] was 0.0115 with p-value of 0, indicating high similarity between the two genomes. When the 2 genomes were compared using the QUAST assembly evaluation tool [86], there was a 75.8% of our assembly that aligned with the reference genome and there were 3938 misassembled contigs (based on contigs > = to 500 bp). The average nucleotide identity (ANI) between our assembly and the reference genome as determined by the skani tool [87] was 98.52% and the fraction of the reference covered by the alignments was 70.36%, while the fraction of the query that was covered by the alignments was 82.37%.

A dot plot was also constructed between our assembly and the reference genome using D-Genies [88] to examine regions of synteny between the two genomes. Unsurprisingly, Fig. 2 shows a major region of synteny along the diagonal between the two genomes. However, a dot plot may not capture the entire array of similarity between the two genomes. Figure 3A shows a bar graph of the overall similarity between the reference genome and our assembly. Intriguingly, it shows that there was no match for 12.45% of the reference genome with our assembly. A possible explanation for this is the somewhat different contiguity of our assembly versus fewer contigs in the reference genome. This may make it difficult for Minimap2 (the aligner D-Genies uses) to find matches between the two genomes. In order to further corroborate these findings, alignments between the two genomes using the CompareGenome tool [89] using BLASTN [90] showed that while some alignments were similar between the two genomes, many were dissimilar with lower alignment scores (Fig. 3B and C). Again, it is likely that BLASTN could not find high scoring matches with the reference genome due to the somewhat fragmented nature of our assembly. That is, when comparing assemblies of unequal contiguity using the CompareGenome tool, our less contiguous E. crispata assembly is likely artificially inflating the proportion of genes assigned to lower similarity classes. The coding sequences in our assembly are interrupted by contig breaks, therefore producing truncated BLASTN alignments that yield reduced similarity scores, displacing genes from the 95–100% class even when the underlying sequence is identical to the reference E. crispata genome. In an intra-species context such as comparing the E. crispata reference genome versus our E. crispata assembly, this artifact is most probably leading to an overestimation of genomic divergence that reflects differences in assembly quality rather than true sequence variation.

Analysis of putative endogenous viral elements

Previously, we reported the complete genome sequence of an endogenous retrovirus (ERV) found in E. chlorotica [41]. The retrovirus is expressed during the annual senescence of E. chlorotica every Spring. We found evidence to indicate that there is a decayed remnant of this endogenous retrovirus in E. crispata. We assembled a 1050 bp partial virus sequence from both Illumina and HiFi reads and performed BLAST analysis against both the core_nt (lower redundancy) and nr/nt (broader) databases of Genbank. Figure 4 (core_nt tree) shows the assembled 1050 bp virus clustering with the E. chlorotica endogenous retrovirus with good support (88.8% SH-aLRT/100% UFBoot) suggesting our assembled virus is closely related to the E. chlorotica virus. Figure 5 (nr/nt tree) shows our assembled virus in the same clade (94.1% SH-aLRT/95% UFBoot with) the E. chlorotica virus and the E. crispata reference genome assembly chromosome 14. Intriguingly, it clusters closely with the nudibranch, Doris verrucosa (with poor branch support) which is another species of sea slug, albeit non-photosynthetic. Both trees provide good evidence that our assembled virus is closely related to the E. chlorotica virus. Other interesting species of sea slug in the trees are the nudibranchs Phyllidia flava and Nemesignis banyulensis.

To characterize the retroviral content of the 1,050 bp element, we searched it against the E. chlorotica retrovirus KY115607 using tblastx. Detectable similarity is restricted to positions 5,116-6,060 of the reference virus, which is the 3’ end of the genome, consistent with all five integration sites mapping to this same region (Table 2). There is no reverse transcriptase, integrase, gag, protease, or envelope sequence, and the element is too degraded for any of those to survive. The longest ORF is 86 amino acids with a putative zinc finger domain, and BLASTP returns an E. crispata hypothetical protein as the top hit, with no significant similarity to any canonical retroviral gene product. Interestingly, one of the proteins encoded by the E. chlorotica virus also carries a zinc finger domain. We are therefore classifying this as a putative endogenous retroviral remnant based on its phylogenetic placement alongside known retroviruses in Figs. 4 and 5 and its sequence similarity to KY115607, not on the basis of intact retroviral gene domains.

We also BLASTed the 1,050 bp sequence against the published E. crispata reference genome (GCA_033675545.1) to characterize the locus that appeared in the nr/nt tree (Fig. 5). Five matching loci came back across five reference contigs (JAWDGP010003588.1, JAWDGP010000666.1, JAWDGP010001678.1, JAWDGP010000054.1, and JAWDGP010006603.1) at nucleotide identities of 93.7–97.9%, with each alignment spanning essentially the full 1,050 bp query. The sequence visible in the nr/nt tree as a reference genome entry is from JAWDGP010003588.1, which sits on chromosome 14 in the reference genome chromosome-level annotation and matches our assembled element at 97.9% identity. Tblastx of all five extracted reference loci against KY115607 confirmed they all map to the same 3’ region of the viral genome (approximately 5,116-6,060) as the five integration sites in our assembly, and none have intact retroviral gene features. Finding five concordant copies of this element in a reference genome built from a different ecotype using different sequencing technology supports genuine and stable genomic integration.

Five integration sites were identified across five scaffolds in our assembly, with sequence identities of 93.9–97.9% relative to the query. All five were confirmed by continuous Illumina and HiFi read coverage across both ERV-host junctions, with 100% of junction bases covered. Flanking host sequences at sites 1, 3, 4, and 5 mapped uniquely to the genome (MAPQ = 60), providing the strongest evidence for genuine integration (Table 2). Site 2 was considered less reliable due to a repetitive left flank mapping to five genomic loci and a large depth discontinuity at the left junction. BLASTN of each extracted ERV region against NCBI nt confirmed all five sites match the published E. crispata reference genome at 98–99% identity, and against core_nt the top hit for all five was the E. chlorotica endogenous retrovirus KY115607 (identity 92.9–97.2%, E-value = 0.0). Inspection of the ANNEVO gene annotations on the scaffolds carrying each site confirmed that annotated host genes are present in the flanking regions at four of five sites. The closest is at site 2, where an annotated gene ends just 1,427 bp upstream of the integration boundary, and at site 3 the nearest gene ends 1,608 bp upstream. Site 5 sits on a 38,151 bp scaffold where ANNEVO did not call any genes, attributable to scaffold length rather than non-host origin, as the scaffold BLASTs to the published reference at 93.9% identity. While PCR spanning each integration junction would provide the most direct confirmation, the original specimens and DNA are no longer available and continued fieldwork was not possible within the funding constraints of this project. Taken together, we think this constitutes strong evidence for genuine endogenous integration in E. crispata.

Given the degree of degeneration of this element and its restriction to the 3’ end of the reference virus, the pattern is more consistent with ancient, fixed integrations than with recent acquisition. That said, determining integration age would require LTR divergence estimates, which are unavailable given the truncated nature of the element. We therefore present this as a putative ERV remnant, noting that both scenarios are consistent with the data we have.

HI-FEVER analysis identified 317, 416, and 285 likely-retroviral elements in our E. crispata assembly, the E. crispata reference genome, and the E. chlorotica genome respectively, of which 110, 148, and 117 met our high-confidence Retroviridae criteria. E. crispata consistently harboured more retroviral elements than E. chlorotica, and the consistency of counts across independent assemblies suggests these are genuine genomic features rather than assembly artifacts.

The RT/pol phylogeny (Fig. 6A) shows that E. crispata RT-like sequences do not form isolated branches but instead group with RT sequences from E. chlorotica, Elysia marginata, and Plakobranchus ocellatus in well-supported clades, consistent with shared molluscan retroelements that have diversified within sacoglossan lineages. The clustering of E. crispata ragtag and reference sequences with E. chlorotica RT sequences suggests these elements either derive from related ancestral insertions or were acquired prior to sacoglossan divergence. The tree also contains bacterial and deuterostome sequences, which should be interpreted cautiously given the ancient and mobile nature of RT domains and the potential for database misannotation.

A second distinct RT lineage is visible in the lower portion of the tree (Fig. 6B), containing E. crispata ragtag and reference sequences alongside E. chlorotica, Plakobranchus ocellatus, and Elysia marginata sequences, further supporting the presence of endogenous retroelements shared across sacoglossans. Notably, the RT from the E. chlorotica retrovirus KY115607 clusters separately with Hyalella azteca, an amphipod crustacean, rather than with other sacoglossan sequences. This indicates that KY115607 represents a phylogenetically distinct ERV lineage, not closely related to the broader sacoglossan RT sequences identified here.

The five ERV integration sites identified in our assembly using the 1,050 bp PVGA-assembled sequence as a query correspond to the 3’ region of KY115607 (positions 5,117-6,060), which encodes only a short degenerate ORF and likely represents a degraded partial copy of the whole endogenous retrovirus. The distinct phylogenetic placement of KY115607’s RT domain, separate from all other sequences in the tree, suggests that the integration sites found using the KY115607-derived query represent degraded copies of this distinct lineage rather than copies of the broader sacoglossan ERVs shown in the main clades of the phylogeny. E. crispata appears to carry remnants of at least two different retroviral histories: the ancient sacoglossan lineage detected by HI-FEVER, and a degraded fragment related to the 3’ end of KY115607.

No evidence of algal nuclear gene transfer

All algal gene sequences which were discovered in the genome were chloroplast encoded matching the algal species Penicillus capitatus (tufA, rbcL, petA, psbA, psbD, psaA, and psaB). These genes were all found on a single contig in the assembled genome which resembles a partial chloroplast genome. Although polyphagous, some individual animals and even populations only have chloroplasts from one or two sources, so it is not unlikely that only one chloroplast species would be present in the assembly [33, 34]. No nuclear encoded algal genes were found.

Presence of fatty acid synthase-like polyketide synthase proteins

Fatty acid synthase-(FAS)-like polyketide synthase (PKS) proteins were detected in our E. crispata draft genome corresponding to the E. chlorotica protein, PKS1. These proteins were found in E. chlorotica previously [91] and serve to synthesize complex polyketides by sacoglossan animals. In particular, PKS1 modifies methylmalonyl-CoA to synthesize pyrones that are thought to protect the sacoglossan from damage associated with the photosynthetic process, based on homology with characterized sacoglossan enzymes [91]. Table 3 shows the E. crispata PKS proteins with the most complete PKS domain structure and Table 4 shows the evidence that the PKS protein with the most complete domain structure has a complete gene model. The first protein in Table 3 had the KS (Ketosynthase), AT (Acyltransferase), DH (Dehydratase), pKR (pseudo-Ketoreductase), ER (Enoylreductase), KR (Ketoreductase), ACP (Acyl Carrier Protein), and TE (Thioesterase) domains. It is the only one containing the TE domain (residues 2420–2673), which is required for release of the finished polyketide chain [92]. The other three proteins lack a TE domain and cannot independently terminate and release a product. JAWDGP010001353.1_RagTag-g1.t1 is also the most divergent from the E. chlorotica reference PKS (38.8% identity to QMU95645.1). We note that JAWDGP010001353.1_RagTag-g1.t1 contains a possible 57-residue internal duplication in the ER/KR region. HiFi coverage across this segment is lower than the rest of the gene, and the corresponding region is not recovered as a high-identity match in the published E. crispata reference genome, suggesting that this segment may reflect a gene-model artifact. Removing this segment would yield an adjusted length of 2,622 aa, which remains longer than the E. chlorotica reference PKS at 2,264 aa.

Discussion

Despite obtaining a less contiguous genome than the reference, we were able to glean valuable insights from another photosynthetic sacoglossan’s genome. We found evidence for the presence of a putative degraded retroviral remnant in E. crispata, but the full virus has been only shown to exist in the E. chlorotica genome [41, 43]. The E. marginata virus appears unrelated to the E. chlorotica and E. crispata viruses, but instead related to the Asfarviridae and an endogenous retrovirus in the fungus R. irregularis [42]. Interestingly, E. crispata does not undergo the annual senescence that E. chlorotica does [25, 43]. Given that E. crispata does not undergo annual senescence, the functional role of this endogenous retroviral remnant remains unclear. Whether viral expression in E. chlorotica triggers senescence or is instead initiated by it has not been established, and our data do not allow us to distinguish between these possibilities. Future work will involve a widespread survey of other sea slugs to determine if endogenous retroviruses exist in their genomes as well. It is also worth considering whether this element represents a recent integration or something much older. The evidence is more consistent with an older integration. The 1,050 bp sequence covers only the 3’ end of the reference virus, carries no intact retroviral gene domains, and has a longest ORF of just 86 amino acids. We also find five concordant copies of the same 1,050 bp fragment in an independent reference genome assembly of a different ecotype, a pattern more consistent with ancient, fixed integrations than with recent acquisition. That said, determining integration age would require LTR divergence estimates, which are unavailable given the truncated nature of the element. We therefore present this as a putative ERV remnant, noting that both scenarios are consistent with the data we have.

One apparent discrepancy is that while the 1,050 bp fragment is closely related to the 3’ end of the E. chlorotica virus KY115607, the RT sequence of KY115607 clusters with Hyalella azteca rather than other sacoglossans (Fig. 6B). However, the fragment only covers positions 5,116-6,060, which is entirely separate from the RT-encoding region of pol. Retroviruses frequently have mosaic genomes where different regions carry different evolutionary histories, so the two observations are not in conflict. KY115607 may have acquired its RT domain from a crustacean-associated lineage while retaining a 3’ region related to the sacoglossan endogenous copies identified here.

It is of particular interest to investigate why our genome assembly was less contiguous than the reference. One possible reason is that the sea slug tissue was stored in ethanol before DNA extraction. DNA in fish fin samples stored in ethanol was highly degraded within 24 h, while samples stored in DESS (20% DMSO, 0.25 M EDTA, NaCl saturated solution) were degraded only to a moderate extent [93]. An additional confounding factor is that it is not an easy task to extract high quality DNA from sea slugs given their highly slimy body constitution. Whole-genome amplification is also likely a contributing factor. We used the REPLI-g Midi Kit on the HiFi material because DNA yield from the ethanol-preserved tissue was too low to proceed without it. WGA can also cause issues: it can introduce uneven amplification coverage across the genome and, in rare cases, produce chimeric molecules, both of which can reduce assembly contiguity [94]. This is likely another contributing factor to the fragmented initial assembly. The HiFi-only assembly reaching only 221 Mb against an estimated genome size of 670 Mb probably reflects both the WGA coverage gaps and insufficient sequencing depth. The final hybrid assembly reaching 671 Mb at 98.2% BUSCO completeness supports that reading. In the future, we will store our samples in DESS or flash freeze them to avoid DNA degradation caused by ethanol storage. Another major reason for the lack of contiguity in our assembly is the absence of Hi-C data to order, orient, and scaffold our contigs [95]. For subsequent genomes, we plan to utilize this crucial technology to increase the quality of our assemblies. Although our assembly is not of reference genome quality, it is still a useful dataset for sacoglossan genomics and demonstrates that valuable information can still be gleaned from an imperfect assembly.

How kleptoplastic sea slugs are able to maintain functional chloroplasts for extended periods of time is of considerable interest. One potential answer to this was the horizontal transfer of algal nuclear genes to the slugs. Several earlier studies showed support for this hypothesis in E. chlorotica [22, 27, 96–101] and E. crispata [96], however recent genomic analysis of E. chlorotica [30] and Plakobranchus ocellatus, another long term kleptoplastic sacoglossan [29], both showed no evidence of horizontally transferred algal nuclear genes. The draft genome of E. crispata presented here also did not show any evidence of algal genes that may have been horizontally transferred. If horizontal gene transfer is not present then the maintenance of long term kleptoplasty in these sacoglossans species will require more explanation and exploration.

One area which has recently shown to be associated with maintaining functional chloroplasts in sacoglossans is fatty acid synthase like polyketide synthase (PKS) proteins as previously found in E. chlorotica [91]. These proteins may play a critical role as an antioxidant and in photosynthetic protection for kleptoplastic sacoglossans [91]. Homologs to this protein were also found when comparing the transcriptome of the kleptoplastic E. timida but not E. cornigera which does not retain functional chloroplasts [102, 103]. Homologs were also found in two additional long term kleptoplastic sacoglossans Plakobranchus cf. ocellatus and E. diomedea [91]. Given this pattern it is not surprising that we also found PKS genes in E. crispata which also maintains chloroplasts for long durations.

Sacoglossan sea slugs represent a rare example of opportunities to study local adaptation in marine herbivores and E. crispata may represent the best model organism for such studies [33, 104]. Populations in different locations utilize different food resources [32–34] and the slug has lecithotrophic development with a very short dispersal stage [31, 105] which can lead to some interesting difference between groups of slugs. The most striking differences, however, can be found between the two ecotypes of E. crispata. A variety of morphological and ecological differences have been noted between these two groups of slugs [31, 33, 34, 40] For example, the crispata ecotype, the focus of this study, has a thicker white foot and is found in areas of high water flow such as coral reefs and surrounding reef rubble [31, 40]. In contrast the clarki ecotype has a transparent foot and is typically found in low flow habitats such as mangrove swamps and mooring canals. Although molecular analysis of COI and H3 genes have determined that both ecotypes are the same species [40] it would be interesting to look for genomic differences between these two ecotypes. Although we were only able to examine the genome of the crispata ecotype, future studies comparing this to clarki ecotype specimens could prove fruitful.
