Societal constructions of weight stigma against preconception, pregnant, and postpartum women from community members' perspectives: a qualitative story completion study
Authors: Haimanot Hailu, Angela C. Incollingo Rodriguez, Helen Skouteris, Briony Hill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWeight stigma involves prejudiced attitudes directed towards larger-bodied people. Preconception, pregnant, and postpartum (PPP) women are particularly vulnerable to weight stigma due to societal expectations and prescriptive norms around women's bodies, along with body weight changes during these periods. However, there is limited understanding of the prevailing societal discourse and constructions driving this phenomenon. Therefore, we aimed to explore community members' perspectives to identify constructions of weight stigma towards PPP women living in larger bodies.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative story completion study. Adult community members residing in Australia were invited to complete three story stems. Each story stem featured a larger-bodied preconception, pregnant, or postpartum protagonist and their interaction with their friends, colleagues, or family. The participants were instructed to complete the story by writing the story about what they think, believe, and do, focusing on the treatment experienced by larger-bodied women. The data were analysed using inductive reflexive thematic analysis, informed by social constructionism.ResultsEighteen participants took part and completed 48 stories (mean word count = 272 words, range 94−708). Three overarching constructions were identified: (1) prevailing assumptions about weight stigma in PPP periods; (2) disguising fatphobia pertaining to PPP women as concern for the mother's and child's health; and (3) healthism pertaining to PPP women living in larger bodies.ConclusionsOur study highlights predominant norms and assumptions that drive weight stigma toward PPP women. These insights can inform future research, policy, and health promotion initiatives to identify targeted strategies to address these constructions. Ultimately, such efforts may contribute to creating a society that embraces body diversity and challenges weight stigma, positively impacting the health of PPP women and their children.

Introduction

Stigmatisation is a complex societal and cultural phenomenon where a person is identified as different and then devalued. This leads to loss of status, whereby individuals are perceived and treated as less competent, less worthy, and socially undesirable and opens the door for discrimination (Bos et al., 2013). More specifically, weight stigma, one of the last socially acceptable forms of bias (Puhl & Heuer, 2010), involves prejudice and biased attitudes directed at individuals based on their body weight or size, targeting people living in larger bodies (Puhl & Brownell, 2001). Larger body size is associated with perceptions of personal and moral failure where individuals are regarded with negative attributes such as laziness, lack of will power, and unattractiveness (Puhl & Brownell, 2001). Larger body size has also been found to override other characteristics of the individual and negatively impact their social identity, leading to further stigma and discrimination (Brewis et al., 2011; Carr & Friedman, 2005; Goffman, 1968). Weight stigmatisation is a common experience for people living in larger bodies (Vartanian et al., 2018). According to a daily diary study conducted with larger-bodied women, participants reported encountering an average of three weight-related stigmatising events each day (Seacat et al., 2016). Weight stigma is associated with negative outcomes, including decreased quality of life, psychological distress, stress, reduced healthcare seeking behaviour, and poorer physical and mental health (Puhl & Heuer, 2009). Despite growing recognition of the negative impact of weight stigma and an expanding body of literature on the phenomenon, a gap remains in our understanding of weight stigma towards preconception, pregnant, and postpartum (PPP) women. Preconception refers to the period before pregnancy irrespective of women's intention to conceive (Hill et al., 2020). Pregnancy encompasses the period from conception to childbirth. Postpartum refers to the period following childbirth, typically defined as the first six to twelve months after delivery, although some definitions extend this period to two years or more, particularly in the context of postpartum weight retention, which has implications for experiences of weight stigma (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021; Makama et al., 2021).

Weight stigma towards PPP women is of particular and growing concern due to a number of factors (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021). For instance, research indicates that societal expectations and prescriptive norms of women's bodies along with body weight changes during these periods may increase the vulnerability of PPP women to weight stigmatisation (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021; Hill et al., 2020; Incollingo Rodriguez & Nagpal, 2021; Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020; Makama et al., 2021). Notably, PPP women typically have frequent healthcare contact, and healthcare is known to be one of the most common sources of weight stigma, especially for PPP women (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021; Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020). Furthermore, weight stigma experienced during the PPP periods affects not only women but has intergenerational consequences due to its potential to result in poorer birth outcomes, intergenerational perpetuation of weight stigma, and resultant long-term maternal-child health concerns (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021; Incollingo Rodriguez & Nagpal, 2021).

In addition to healthcare, PPP women experience weight stigma in various other aspects of their everyday life across all societal domains. Recent systematic and narrative reviews that explored weight stigma through a socioecological lens highlighted that larger-bodied PPP women face weight stigmatisation from interpersonal relationships, organisational settings such as workplaces, the media, healthcare, and from community members and public policies (Dever et al., 2025; Hailu et al., 2024; Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021). On top of this, research from both the woman's perspective and community members' perspectives converge on the nature of this stigma. For instance, a groundwork study in this area conducted with a sample of 501 women indicated that larger-bodied pregnant and postpartum women commonly face weight stigma from the general public, family, friends, partners, strangers, and society in general (Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020). The PPP women reported receiving disparaging and judgemental comments, prejudicial assumptions in relation to their body weight, and overall negative treatment from members of the community. As a result, the women experienced profound feelings of guilt, humiliation, shame, and body image concerns (Dever et al., 2025; Hailu et al., 2024).

According to a survey conducted with community members from six countries encompassing Western and non-Western contexts, community members reported explicit and implicit weight bias and stigmatising attitudes towards larger-bodied PPP women, with higher levels of bias reported in Western countries (Hailu et al., 2025). This study also reported the presence of higher levels of controllability beliefs (ascribing body size to personal responsibility) and greater awareness of societal standards of body ideals, both driving higher weight bias and stigmatising attitudes. Therefore, tackling weight stigma is predicated upon an in depth understanding of the Western societal norms and discourses that shape people's beliefs and attitudes regarding body size. This is especially needed given weight stigmatisation negatively affects the physical, mental, behavioural, and psychosocial health of PPP women, with implications for their offspring's health (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021; Incollingo Rodriguez & Nagpal, 2021; Nagpal & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2024; Schwenk et al., 2025). These negative health outcomes include increased risk of depression, anxiety, eating disorders, chronic pain, and intrapartum caesarean birth, as well as decreased motivation to engage in health-promoting behaviours and reduced engagement with healthcare (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021; Incollingo Rodriguez & Nagpal, 2021; Nagpal & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2024; Schwenk et al., 2025). Collectively these challenges influence pregnancy and child health outcomes that may result in preterm birth, macrosomia (birth weight above 4000 grams), poor mother-infant bonding, and intergenerational perpetuation of weight stigma (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021; Incollingo Rodriguez & Nagpal, 2021; Nagpal & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2024; Schwenk et al., 2025).

While growing evidence indicates the pervasiveness of weight stigma and negative attitudes and beliefs towards PPP women from community members, important gaps remain. Specifically, there is limited understanding of the prevailing societal discourse and community constructions that are driving this phenomenon, including the shuttle from general weight stigma to PPP-specific stigma. In particular, there is virtually no research from the perpetrators' perspective except for one study that highlighted the pervasiveness of the problem but revealed limited insight into the underlying drivers (Hailu et al., 2025). Thus, we know little about the mechanisms reinforcing the status quo where weight stigma continues to be acceptable and rarely questioned (Puhl & Heuer, 2010).

Investigating these drivers of weight stigma, in turn, carries value to understanding the genesis of the above documented trends. First, people's individual attitudes and actions are frequently influenced by the collective norms of their social groups (Crandall et al., 2002; Deutsch & Gerard, 1955) and individuals tend to closely adhere to social norms when expressing prejudice or discrimination (Crandall et al., 2002). Second, the identification of these norms and associated discourse can provide important insights for consideration when designing weight stigma interventions in community settings. Third, general weight stigma literature indicates that living in a society that consistently stigmatises and discriminates against people living in larger bodies may lead individuals to internalise these negative attitudes towards themselves. The resulting internalised weight stigma (Durso & Latner, 2008) is strongly associated with negative health outcomes (Bidstrup et al., 2022; Emmer et al., 2020).

Given the research gap concerning community constructions and discourses around body size for PPP women against the backdrop of negative beliefs, body ideals, and norms, the present study used story completion tasks to unpack these underlying constructions. This method allowed us to discern the sociocultural meanings of body weight and weight stigma towards PPP women and how it is reflected in and further reconstructed from people's narratives (Clarke et al., 2017). Story completion is especially useful for exploring sensitive topics (Clarke et al., 2017,2019). This makes it an appropriate design for an investigation of weight stigma as it can circumvent respondents' tendency to give only socially desirable answers or perspectives to the research questions. Therefore, in this study we aimed to explore community members' constructions of weight stigma towards PPP women living in larger bodies. We were also interested in the social and cultural values and assumptions reflected in people's narratives about PPP women living in larger bodies and weight stigma. While we acknowledge gender diversity in the PPP space and agree with scholars in the use of gender inclusive language (MacKinnon et al., 2021), this study specifically focused on experiences of people who identify as larger-bodied women; and the language ‘PPP women’ used throughout reflects the study's scope.

Method

We employed a qualitative story completion design, a method where participants are requested to write a story based on a ‘story stem’, that is, a hypothetical scenario that is the opening sentence or beginning of a story designed by the researcher (Clarke et al., 2019). The story stems (Table 1) include enough details to provoke a response relevant to the topic being studied, yet they are deliberately left ambiguous to allow for a variety of natural responses (Braun et al., 2019). Participants therefore draw on their own values and experiences as well as prevalent discourses or constructions to make sense of the situation described and write a meaningful story (Clarke et al., 2019). Discourses or constructions are socially constructed ideas that are often held by people consciously or unconsciously and shape social behaviours or actions, forms of subjectivity, and power dynamics (Cushion et al., 2011). In this study, construction refers to the dominant societal and cultural discourses or narratives community members draw on in completing stories about larger-bodied PPP women and body weight (Kitzinger & Powell, 1995; Tischner, 2019). Using the social constructionist framework, the data allowed us to access the underlying sociocultural narratives that influence how community members makes sense of women's body weight and weight stigma (Clarke et al., 2017; Kitzinger & Powell, 1995; Tischner, 2019).

Story stems.

There are three main reasons why we adopted the qualitative story completion method in this study. Firstly, it enables access to a wide range of responses, including those that may be socially undesirable, making it particularly well suited to explore sensitive topics such as weight stigma (Clarke et al., 2017, 2019). Such suitability stems from the fact that participants respond to a hypothetical scenario (often in third-person) and they are not required to take personal ownership or justify the attitudes or behaviours depicted in their stories. The absence of personal ownership allows individuals to express social constructions or assumptions they might otherwise withhold. This is in contrast to the traditional methods such as interviews, where social desirability bias is more likely to shape responses. This is in accordance with stigma literature, which suggests that when stigma is assessed through direct methods, individuals may mask their true attitudes due to social desirability concerns (Bacon et al., 2001). Minimising social desirability bias was particularly important for our research question, given the sensitive nature of weight stigma. Secondly, the story completion method enabled us to explore the societal level drivers of weight stigma and constructions due to its theoretical flexibility and compatibility with a constructionist framework (Clarke et al., 2017, 2019). This was well aligned with our aim to examine how weight stigma towards larger-bodied PPP women is constructed at the community level reflected without the influence of social desirability bias. Lastly, the chosen method enables participant creativity and gives respondents full authority over the content and direction of their stories (Clarke et al., 2017, 2019). As such, participants do not need to have personal experience with the topic to meaningfully engage with the task, which was an important feature given our focus on societal constructions rather than individual experiences of weight stigma. Although still an emerging method, qualitative story completion has been successfully used to investigate a range of psychological and health-related issues, including mental health service provision, healthy eating and weight loss, health information seeking, caregiving relationships, COVID−19 and family life, ageism, and end-of-life care (Clarke et al., 2017, 2019; Hunter et al., 2023; Tischner, 2019; Urry et al., 2023). Overall, using this design we explored the underlying constructions and discourse in relation to women's body weight and weight stigma about larger-bodied PPP women in the community setting. This aligns with Kitzinger and Powell's (1995) study that employed a social constructionist framework within feminist psychology to examine relationships. They posited that the stories people told would reflect prevailing discourses about a particular topic, which in turn would produce social values and meanings.

Participants and recruitment

Members of the community (i.e. general public) who were 18 years old and above, who could read and write in English, had access to the Internet, and who resided in Australia were eligible to participate. Potential participants were recruited via advertisements (which represented images of people from diverse backgrounds and were developed to target and attract individuals of all genders and from diverse socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds and body sizes) using flyers on social media such as Facebook and X (formerly Twitter) and public places such as libraries and Shopping Centres in Melbourne, Australia, and via word of mouth. That advertisement solicited people's perceptions of treatment towards women of reproductive age living in larger bodies. Interested participants scanned a QR code or clicked a link to access the explanatory statement containing the study details, and then completed the consent form. Individuals who fulfilled the inclusion criteria and provided their informed consent and email addresses were contacted by author HH via email and directed to the story completion task on Qualtrics (Qualtrics). All participants received AUD$10 gift cards per story they completed in recognition of their time. The amount reimbursed was minimal and in alignment with National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research ensuring it did not encourage participants to take risks and was not considered as inducement (National Health and Medical Research Council ARCaUA, 2023). Recruitment occurred between June and October, 2024.

Procedure

After providing informed consent, participants were instructed to complete three stories with the order of story stem presentation counterbalanced (randomised) using Qualtrics (Qualtrics) survey software. The participants were instructed to complete the story by writing or typing (telling the story) about what they think, believe, and do, focusing on the treatment experienced by larger-bodied women in the wider community before, during, and after pregnancy. They were also given the option to audio record their story and upload it to Qualtrics if they prefer, but none of the participants used this option. Participants were also provided with reflective questions following the story stem to encourage them to write responses longer than one sentence and to provide additional detail (Supplementary file Table S1). Participants were instructed to spend at least 10 minutes (at least 200 words long) writing or typing each story. At the end of the stories, participants responded to some items regarding their demographic and reproductive characteristics.

To address the challenges associated with online recruitment and non-synchronous data collection such as fraudulent responses and ‘bots’, and the use of generative Artificial Intelligence (AI), we implemented several recommended measures (Gibson & Beattie, 2024; Jones et al., 2021). First, the study was accessible only via personalised links sent directly by the researcher, allowing us to ensure that only eligible participants received access. Second, the explanatory statement clearly indicated that the participants would not be compensated for AI-generated stories or fraudulent participation. Third, participants' IP addresses were checked to verify eligibility based on country of residence and to monitor for multiple entries from the same IP address. Finally, a Completely Automated Public Turing Test to tell Computers and Humans Apart (CAPTCHA) was used to support prevention of bot-generated responses. We also read and re-read the stories to see the tone, word choice, and any patterns that seemed unnatural and compared each story with our pilot stories (Gibson & Beattie, 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (43029). This study was reported per the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) guideline (Tong et al., 2007) (Supplementary file Table S2).

Story stems

In the current study, the story stems presented a hypothetical scenario involving larger-bodied PPP women and either their families, friends, or peers. The story stems introduced the idea that the larger-bodied women were either trying to conceive (reflecting preconception), were pregnant and announcing the news to peers (pregnancy), or involved the woman meeting her family for the first time after giving birth (postpartum). The stories introduced a larger-bodied woman in a particular scenario but left the details of the interaction (how their family, friends, or peers react to the news or first meeting) open. The scenarios were chosen to reflect situations that have been reported by either preconception, pregnant, or postpartum women in previous research to be those in which weight stigmatisation experiences often occur (Hailu et al., 2024,2025; Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021; Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020). The story stems (Table 1) were created by the research team and input from Lived Experience Experts (i.e. consumers who had experienced weight stigma in the PPP periods), and the steering and advisory groups of an Australian Research Council (ARC) Discovery Project Grant (DP220101107). The lived experience experts (n = 2) were members of the ARC Discovery Project Grant Advisory Group. All Advisory Group members (n = 8) read, gave feedback, and approved the document. The group had members with expertise in weight stigma, maternal health, public health policy, body image, health psychology, and consumer advocacy. The story stems were piloted with three participants prior to data collection. This is based on the recommendation to pilot the stems on the equivalent of 10−20% of the intended final sample (Clarke et al., 2017). No required changes were identified during piloting. The final sample size was determined based on Clarke et al.'s (2017) recommendation to recruit at least ten participants per story stem variation to gain rich data for meaningful analysis and saturation of data (Guest et al., 2006).

Data analysis

The data were analysed using inductive reflexive thematic analysis (Braun et al., 2023) and informed by social constructionism (Kitzinger & Powell, 1995). The key principle of the social constructionist approach is that our understanding and perception of the world do not necessarily reflect reality but are shaped by how the world is represented or constructed through language (Burr et al., 2017). Kitzinger and Powell suggest that using the social constructionist lens to analyse story completion data uncovers the contemporary discourse people draw upon in making sense of their experiences (Kitzinger & Powell, 1995). Accordingly, we interpreted the data as reflecting community constructions or discourse that shape the meaning of women's body weight and weight stigma in the PPP context.

We followed Braun and Clarke's six-step analysis process to inductively identify the community constructions reflected in the stories (Braun et al., 2023; Byrne, 2022). In step one, one author (HH) read and re-read the stories for data familiarisation and immersion. In step two, one author (HH) coded the data for content related to our aim. NVivo software (QSR International) was used to code all transcripts. In step three, three authors (HH, BH, and ACIR) collaboratively identified the patterns of meaning across codes and categorised the codes into initial potential themes, followed by potential constructions reflected in the stories. In step four, all authors discussed the themes and defined potential constructions. Following this, in step five, the constructions identified in participants' responses were discussed, defined, and named through wider team discussions and feedback from all co-authors. We created visual representations of the details of the steps we took in our analysis and complex relationships between concepts as a tool to support analysis (Supplementary file Figure S1). Finally, in step six, the manuscript draft was produced detailing the concepts and constructions underlying the stories. Some of the story excerpts have been shortened for inclusion in the manuscript to highlight the most relevant parts that directly explain a particular point. Spelling errors in the stories were corrected only when needed for clarity, but otherwise appear as given by the participants. Story excerpts are labelled with their corresponding story stem source (preconception, pregnancy, postpartum) to indicate which story they were extracted from.

Reflexivity statement

Reflexivity involves critically examining how one's social position and personal experiences may shape the research process and its outcomes (Berger, 2015). Author HH holds a master's degree, while authors ACIR, HS, and BH hold PhD degrees. All researchers identify as women and have expertise in public health, maternal health, weight stigma and qualitative research. The senior author (BH) describes her transition from a weight-focused approach to women's health to a weight-inclusive perspective, using a pragmatic lens to explore weight stigma in real-world contexts. Similarly, the author (ACIR) self-identifies as having transitioned from a weight-centric approach to studying health and stigma to a weight-inclusive approach within the maternal health sphere. Also, all authors acknowledge that they do not identify as individuals living in larger bodies and recognise their position as ‘outsiders’ to this experience. As such, subsequent analyses were largely conducted from an outsider perspective. Additionally, all authors are parents and have personal experience with the preconception, pregnancy, or postpartum phases. While efforts were made to remain objective during data analysis, we recognise that our understanding of weight stigma may be limited by not having lived experience.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Eighteen participants took part in the study (mean age 34.4 ± 10 years, range 20–59 years). The majority identified as female (n = 12) and White (n = 11), and self-perceived as not living in a larger body (n = 13). The majority of participants (n = 16) were within the reproductive age range. Participant demographic profiles are presented in Table 2. A total of 48 stories (preconception n = 17; pregnancy n = 15; and postpartum n = 16) were written by the participants. The variation in numbers reflects differences in the number of story stems completed by each participant. The mean word count of the stories was 272 words (range 94–708).

Community members' constructions of weight stigma against larger-bodied PPP women

Three overarching community members' constructions were generated during analysis. These constructions underlie the meanings, assumptions, and stereotypes of women's body weight and weight stigma in the PPP context that were reflected in participants' stories. The constructions included: (1) prevailing assumptions about weight stigma in PPP periods; (2) disguising fatphobia pertaining to PPP women as concern for the mother's and child's health; and (3) healthism pertaining to PPP women living in larger bodies.

Prevailing assumptions about weight stigma against PPP women

This construction reflects the widespread assumptions in community settings about how weight stigma pertaining to PPP women manifests, highlighting the underlying beliefs that facilitate weight stigmatisation. Here, the participants' stories depicted PPP women as consistently anticipating weight stigma (n = 27 stories), feeling self-conscious about their body weight (n = 19 stories), and justifying enacted weight stigma as well-intentioned (n = 20 stories).

In almost all the stories, participants began by noting that the protagonists felt anxious about sharing their reproductive-related news with others due to a fear of negative reactions related to their body weight. This portrayal of anxiety or perhaps apprehension reflected a broader belief that larger-bodied PPP women anticipate experiencing weight stigma in their interpersonal interactions. As demonstrated in the following two excerpts, the stories indicated that women's anxiety stemmed from concerns about negative reactions from friends, family, and colleagues regarding their body weight, as well as a fear of receiving unsolicited advice. These reasons suggest the perception that PPP women were hypervigilant about comments regarding their bodies.

“As Emily arrives at the party, she feels a mix of excitement and anxiety. She's been eager to share her plans to conceive with her closest friends, but also wonders how they would react. From Emily's past experiences and interactions…she is well aware that society often holds people to certain standards and maintains harmful stereotypes about body weight…” (Preconception, P16, male)

“Sofia had been having it in mind to announce to her work friends/colleagues about her being pregnant but she was concerned about some weight-related comments and lifestyle advice that she might be getting but nevertheless she decided to spill it to them during the lunch break at work.” (Postpartum, P10, female)

Even in the instances when direct negative reactions were not evident, the women were depicted as receiving it as a pointed silence, and it was not because people felt their weight was not an issue. This is reflected in the following excerpt: ʻ… Sofia later worried there were no comments or suggestions because her co-workers were behaving professionally, not because they didn't think or feel that her weight and/or lifestyle was an issue.’ (Pregnancy, P7, female)

Additionally, the stories portrayed larger-bodied women in all reproductive phases as self-conscious, insecure, and often feeling guilty about their weight. This portrayal suggests an underlying assumption that larger-bodied PPP women are uncomfortable with their body weight and size. This is exemplified as follows:

“While people do not say anything, Rachel feels like self-conscious and is aware of people looking at her when she eats unhealthy food, whether she has Elijah with her or not.” (Postpartum, P7, female)

“Emily would often be filled with a sense of hopelessness and guilt - where her choices were preventing having a child.” (Preconception, P13, male)

The stories showed that PPP women not only felt negatively about their weight, but also faced negative reactions when sharing their pregnancy-related news. Interestingly, in the preconception and pregnancy phases, while participants included congratulatory remarks in their stories, this was mixed with negative reactions and comments. This suggests a perceived social permission and entitlement to respond negatively, reflecting how weight-based reactions toward larger bodies are considered inevitable and normal. One of the preconception stories clearly portrayed this by dictating the dynamic following Emily's announcement to try to conceive ʻ…this conversation has made everyone participate in discussion loudly [about whether Emily is going to be successful in trying to conceive given her larger body]’ (Preconception, P1, female). In relation to this, out of 15 stories written in response to the pregnancy story stem, the majority (n = 10) portrayed larger-bodied pregnant women's experience of announcing their pregnancy as different from other women due their body size. Three stories indicated a similar experience regardless of body size, while two suggested the women's experience would be context-dependent. The negative reactions portrayed in the stories included hurtful comments, subtle glances, and condescending treatment. These occurred across all reproductive phases, as shown in the excerpts below. These negative reactions in the stories were mainly portrayed as stemming from a place of care and were deemed well-intentioned. They predominantly came from close others who were expressing concern, and felt justified in being blunt to motivate weight loss.

“​​After lunch had finished and Sofia moved back to her work station she became anxious, several of her colleagues appeared to be in quiet conversations in hushed tones. As Sofia went to fill her water bottle, she overheard two female colleagues talking where she caught - she is already big enough to be pregnant, while the other countered with 'how did she get pregnant, could you imagine…'.” (Pregnancy, P13, male)

“Please, mum, she's got heaps to spare. She's even gained weight after giving birth, so stop giving her more food, Rachel's sister who was sitting next to her laughed.” (Postpartum, P9, female)

Despite revealing assumptions underlying PPP weight stigma, the stories across all PPP phases also consistently featured support or reassurance that the women received from larger-bodied people. However, the content of the stories (stigmatising vs. non-stigmatising) did not vary by the self-perceived weight status of the study participants. Overall, the portrayal of PPP women as routinely anticipating weight stigma and normalising negative reactions as well-intentioned reflects a prevailing assumption about how weight stigma manifests in PPP contexts.

Disguising fatphobia pertaining to PPP women as concern for the mother's and child's health

This construction reflects underlying fatphobia toward larger-bodied PPP women often masked as concern for their health, their children, a healthy pregnancy, and desire for positive birth outcomes. The stories emphasised the complications and health risks associated with larger-bodied women conceiving and giving birth (n = 33 stories), while justifying these comments as helpful. Consequently, the perpetrators were not held accountable for negative actions, contributing to the pervasiveness and normalisation of weight stigma.

The stories primarily highlighted concerns from friends, family, and peers about the perceived ‘elevated health risks’ PPP women might face due to their higher body weight. The most commonly mentioned concerns included difficulty conceiving, risks associated with conception, gestational diabetes, negative impact on the baby, challenges with breastfeeding, risks of further weight gain, and difficulties with postpartum weight loss. The depiction of health risks and complications in the stories did not vary by the participants' gender. The excerpts below demonstrate this point by stating:

“One of her friends, Hannah, who is a third time mother would go on to suggest that she lose some weight firstly before she tries to conceive. She said it should be better for her health as it could be dangerous at the time of child birth all because of her weight.” (Preconception, P14, male)

“Sofia had already had to navigate and absorb the comments like these when she told her family - her own mother told her to prepare for gestational diabetes!” (Pregnancy, P13, male)

In the postpartum stories, some of the negative reactions towards the women were depicted indirectly, often through general talks about health or subtle comparison comments such as remarks about the newborn's looks as indicated in the excerpt below. This narrative reflects how individuals often mask weight stigmatising comments within indirect topics and how early in life cues of weight stigma can begin to emerge.

“Rachel is late to her family's home. When she arrives her younger sister Maxine opens the door. ʻLet me have a look at little Lucy. Oh my gosh, isn't she cute? Look at those big cheeks! She is chubby like her mum!’… ʻOh yes you're right Maxine. She is a chuboball. Just like her mum. You will have to go easy on giving the milk Rachel or she'll get fatter.’” (Postpartum, P15, female)

The underlying fatphobia was also reflected when the comments about the health risks and complications were justified as ‘helpful advice’ and meant to help PPP women enhance their chances of conception, a healthy pregnancy, or postpartum weight loss. Hence, this disguised fatphobia is closely linked to community construction one, around the prevailing assumptions about how weight stigma manifests in PPP contexts. An excerpt from one of the stories stated ʻ… Some of them felt that they had the right to give her advice, particularly about her weight, as this would be the healthiest thing for her pregnancy and would make her more capable of looking after her child.’ (Pregnancy, P9, female). These narratives continued to exist in the stories where women were portrayed as feeling ‘anxious’, ‘stressed’, ‘upset’, and experiencing ‘self-doubt’. These emotional reactions were in response to comments linking elevated health risks to the women’s body weight and overall stigmatising encounters. Not only were the health and pregnancy risks emphasised in the stories, but the women were also strongly recommended to consult a specialist and shouldn’t just visit their regular healthcare providers. For example, one participant wrote:

“No no no way! You can't see any regular GP for this. It's your baby!’ Santana explained. ʻYou want to make sure someone can follow you for any issues that might arise, someone who will see you for longer than 30 seconds and will actually look at you instead of searching the internet!.I know it seems like a hassle but some parents might have more complications than others…As she [Sofia] looked at Santana heading over to the cafe, she wondered if Santana was thinking about her weight.” (Pregnancy, P12, male)

This (over)emphasis on the health or pregnancy risks and complications based solely on the body weight reflects a discourse grounded in concern for health, a healthy pregnancy, and birth outcomes. This discourse represents a justification for the unsolicited advice and stigmatising attitudes towards larger-bodied PPP women. In reality, this discourse was not focused on helping the women improve their health, but reflected underlying fatphobia.

Healthism pertaining to PPP women living in larger bodies

The final construction represented the concept of ‘healthism’ as a pervasive aspect of weight stigma against PPP women held by community members. Healthism is a form of medicalisation that situates health and disease as an issue of individual control and a result of ‘good’ or ‘bad’ choices and behaviours (Crawford, 1980; Jimé et al., 2020; Mackert & Schorb, 2022). Specific to PPP weight stigma, this construction creates assumption that PPP women choose not to interfere with their larger body weight while they have the means to control it with simple lifestyle modification (Crawford, 1980; Jimé et al., 2020; Mackert & Schorb, 2022). This assumption then leads others to provide unsolicited nutrition and physical activity advice. This narrative represents a disregard for the broader factors that influence body weight, including the reproductive phenomena in women's lives. By doing so, it portrays body weight as entirely controllable and larger-bodied PPP women as irresponsible and lazy. Also, this construction contributes to prevailing assumptions by fuelling the blaming and shaming of larger-bodied PPP women for choosing to try conception, pregnancy, and childbirth. The stories reflected this construction by depicting women across all three reproductive phases receiving unsolicited and overly simplistic nutrition and physical activity advice.

As demonstrated by the excerpts from the stories, PPP women's friends, family, and peers emphasised the need for the women to adjust their diet and engage in more physical activity for quick and successful weight loss (n = 43 stories). They declared these behaviours as the first thing a woman should do before conception, during pregnancy, and after birth.

“However, Sofia was uncomfortable with some comments she heard shortly after. Susan, who was well-known for her strong views on health, advised Sofia to continue exercising and eating a balanced diet during her pregnancy. Another coworker named Mark told her to watch her weight gain so she wouldn't have too much to lose later.” (Pregnancy, P6, female)

“We assumed you would start a healthy lifestyle before trying to get pregnant. Amelia finally says.” (Preconception, P7, female)

Some of the stories indicated that the nutrition and physical activity advice sometimes extended beyond subtle suggestions to extreme intervention such as denying certain types of food to larger-bodied PPP women. For instance, an excerpt from a postpartum story regaled:

“Julie brings out a large Chocolate Mud Cake to the table. She slices it up and gives everyone a piece of cake served with custard except for Rachel. Don't want to put on more weight, do we Rach? Julie says smiling as she shovels a second piece of chocolate cake into her mouth.” (Postpartum, P15, female)

The stories also revealed that unsolicited and overly simplified lifestyle advice did not remain suggestions but were internalised by PPP women. This was evident in their avoidance of desired foods even during nutritionally demanding PPP periods as well as in their engagement in extreme measures to lose or control weight or the need to justify their physical activity and healthy eating habits. These scenarios are shown in the following excerpts:

“She loads up her plate and happily starts eating, but then she notices her mother frowning and feels embarrassed, she gives some of her food to her husband and claims she filled her plate too high by mistake. Baby brain!”(Postpartum, P4, female)

“…her hand shakes before she drops the fork, excusing herself before making her way hastily to the upstairs bathroom. As she opens the door, she beelines for the toilet, heaving everything in her stomach out. I can't, I can't: she repeats.” (Postpartum, P11, female)

“She acknowledged the advice but reassured everyone she was working with her healthcare provider.” (Preconception, P8, female)

Overall, this component of the stories emphasised nutrition and physical activity as a mainstay of weight loss, reinforcing the perception that weight loss was equivalent to health. It also portrayed the common belief that weight is controllable, that larger-bodied PPP women are responsible for managing their weight, and that they need to minimise their weight gain. This narrative appeared to be internalised by some PPP women, leading them to engage in harmful practices such as self-induced vomiting in efforts to lose or control their weight. In instances where weight loss efforts were not evident, the women were often blamed for a lack of willpower or self-discipline, reflecting healthism as a prevailing community construction.

Discussion

This is the first qualitative story completion study focused on weight stigma broadly speaking. It is also the first study to explore community members' constructions or discourses specific to preconception, pregnant, and postpartum women's body weight. This innovative method allowed us to identify underlying constructions and discourse that shape community members' attitudes towards PPP women's bodies. The three overarching community members' constructions reflected in participants' stories included prevailing assumptions about weight stigma in PPP periods, disguising fatphobia pertaining to PPP women as concern for the mother's and child's health, and healthism pertaining to PPP women living in larger bodies. Our study adds to the literature by demonstrating that weight stigma goes beyond individual attitudes, providing insight into how it is shaped and sustained by broader societal narratives and norms. It also reveals the underlying narratives that contribute to the disproportionate stigmatisation of PPP women. This information enhances our understanding of the prevailing constructions that influence whether and how community members enact, understand, or view weight stigma.

Our findings identified a number of assumptions regarding weight stigma in the PPP context, chief among them the assumption that it is normal for PPP women to anticipate facing weight stigma. This is problematic because it may facilitate further stigmatisation by oversimplifying the personal experiences of larger-bodied people; reinforcing a narrative that weight stigma is acceptable, expected, and normalised; and contributing to further negative stereotypes towards larger-bodied PPP women. This finding also implies that community members put the burden on the women to ʻdeal with’ the stigma regardless of its harms. This ultimately places the responsibility on women to endure the issue rather than on broader society to address it. Confirming this point, and consistent with existing weight stigma literature (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021; Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020), the stories portrayed PPP women being subjected to negative comments and treatment because of their body weight or size, without any negative repercussions to the perpetrators. The normalisation of weight stigma in the community not only reinforces the stigmatisation practices with the cost to the psychological, mental, physical, and social health of larger-bodied women (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021), but also makes stigma reduction strategies difficult. Therefore, it is essential to implement strategies that can challenge the normalisation of weight stigma, such as public awareness campaigns that highlight the negative impacts of weight stigma and harmful stereotypes. It is also crucial to enhance diverse body representation in the media and public communication channels, along with the implementation of inclusive public policies.

Our study also revealed the pervasiveness of fatphobia among community members, often disguised as a concern for healthy pregnancy and birth outcomes. This is particularly troubling because an inherent pathologisation of larger bodies under the guise of concern for health allows individuals to feel justified in openly stigmatising and discriminating against larger-bodied PPP women. In our study, stigmatisation and fatphobia towards larger-bodied women did not vary by participant characteristics such as age, which may be due to the fact that the majority of participants were within the reproductive age range. However, previous research has shown that stigmatising attitudes tend to decline with increasing age (Puhl et al., 2015). Furthermore, the automatic association of larger bodies with health risks reveals a social discourse that broadly disregards the complex determinants of health, healthy pregnancy, and birth outcomes. The stories depicted larger-bodied PPP women being subjected to assumptions about pregnancy and birth complications based solely on their body weight or size. These findings align with existing evidence in which even health professionals asserted elevated risks of complications based merely on weight, regardless of the women's overall health status (DeJoy et al., 2016). Being an area of further research, these assumptions are misleading since there is emerging evidence that indicates that the relationship between weight and health problems is only correlational (not causal), and the independent impact of stress due to weight stigma is not taken into consideration in those relationships (Muennig, 2008). In general, fatphobia towards larger-bodied PPP women and the resulting stigmatisation leads to internalised weight stigma, increased risks of health disparities, and perpetuation of broader health inequities (Westbury et al., 2023). Therefore, it is critical to enhance awareness about a holistic approach to health, one that goes beyond weight loss and recognises the complex interplay of physical, mental, and social wellbeing. Also, strategies that help to challenge widespread fatphobia—such as the representation of voices from diverse body experiences and questioning dominant body weight narratives—may proffer societal shifts toward both accepting body diversity and challenging weight stigma. This would have positive downstream impacts on the health of PPP women and their children.

We also identified healthism, which situates the problem of health and disease and its solutions as an individual responsibility (Crawford, 1980; Jimé et al., 2020; Mackert & Schorb, 2022), as a community construction. Healthism emerged through repeated portrayal of ʻlifestyle’ advice for PPP women. Through this rhetoric, the stories revealed and reinforced the incorrect narrative that body size can be easily managed through individual behavioural measures such as eating less and exercising more. Moreover, these stories echoed the narrative that larger-bodied PPP women lack the willpower and discipline to lose or manage their weight. This is in alignment with general weight stigma literature and work which identified that individuals who held strong beliefs about weight controllability exhibited more implicit and explicit weight bias and stigmatising attitudes (Hailu et al., 2025; Westbury et al., 2023).

In addition to perpetuating stigma, the healthism construction promotes a widely held social norm representing a simplistic association between thinness, health, and beauty while framing larger bodies as inherently unhealthy (Gard & Wright, 2001; Webb & Quennerstedt, 2010). As a result, it reinforces societal thin body ideal standards, which are disproportionately higher for women (Chrisler, 2012). All of these assumptions can be internalised by larger-bodied PPP women, leading to self-stigma, eating disorders, and body image concerns (Levinson et al., 2024). Also, this community construction may impact public policies and broader societal organisations. This can be explained by the intertwined relationship between weight stigma contexts or settings such as community (e.g., culture and society), government (e.g., public policy), and organisations across socioecological layers (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2021). Hence, healthism influences policies and interventions by placing full responsibility on individual behaviours, and potentially diverting strategies from broader determinants of health and body weight. Therefore, health promotion strategies and policies that focus on overall health behaviours such as healthy nutrition and physical activity rather than on weight loss and that are designed for all women in the PPP periods regardless of body weight would be beneficial. Furthermore, promoting weight-inclusive care across PPP periods in clinical settings, alongside raising awareness about common assumptions regarding the relationship between weight and pregnancy outcomes, could help shift the dominant body weight narrative in community settings.

We acknowledge the strengths and limitations of our work. First, this study used the innovative story completion design. This helped us ascertain the prevailing constructions that people's beliefs and actions are based on, which can at times be challenging to identify using interviews due to the sensitivity of the phenomenon (25, 32) or with survey instruments alone given the nuance of these structures. This also allowed us to corroborate across existing data sources, including prior qualitative and cross-sectional research (Hailu et al., 2025), complementing the predominantly top-down nature of the literature with this bottom-up approach. However, future research should consider complementing this approach with other methods that explore this topic, such as qualitative interviews and quantitative studies (Hailu et al., 2025), to provide a more comprehensive understanding of weight stigma from different perspectives. Second, our sample was comprised mostly of female and White-identifying individuals, which precludes an understanding of these constructions across other groups. Although we did not find variability in how the stories unfolded based on the participants' gender and ethnicity, future work might utilise story completion to apply an intersectional lens (Deol et al., 2024). Third, the framing of the advertisement or story stems may have had an unknown impact on the people who took part and their prevailing attitudes and belief systems. This is despite the careful lived experience and advisory group consultation and piloting of the story stems we conducted. Fourth, despite our effort to design a story stem that offered sufficient detail to elicit meaningful responses related to our research question while maintaining enough ambiguity to access participants' underlying assumptions and piloted the stem (Clarke et al., 2017), there is a possibility that the stems may have influenced participants' responses. Lastly, although our study focused on larger-bodied women and suggested that their experiences may differ due to their body size, future research could consider a comparative design that includes story stems featuring both women portrayed as larger-bodied and women who were not. Also, a comparative study could be designed to assess whether the stories vary based on participant characteristics such as socioeconomic status and reproductive characteristics such as parity, fertility, and miscarriage. This is regardless of the fact that we were able to demonstrate consistent themes from the data, despite variation in participant characteristics such as PPP stage or gender, suggesting weight stigma and its constructions may transcend individual characteristics. Therefore, future research would allow for an exploration of whether and how experiences differ based on both protagonist's body size and participant characteristics.

Conclusion

This is the first study to shed light on the prevailing community members' constructions of weight stigma and PPP women's body weight using a method that overcomes the challenge of socially desirable viewpoints. This insight contributes to the effort to tackle weight stigma because constructions or discourse influence the establishment and maintenance of body weight ideals, norms, and weight stigma in community settings. The findings reported here provide a nuanced understanding of assumptions underlying weight stigma in the PPP context, fatphobia disguised as concern for health, as well as healthism implied by weight controllability beliefs and the individual responsibility narrative. These findings are particularly relevant for PPP women due to the existing prescriptive norms and expectations about weight and weight gain during the reproductive periods. When weight is seen as controllable and weight stigma is justified by concern for health, PPP women are more likely to be blamed and face disproportionate stigma for ‘putting their child at risk’. Moreover, our findings show that community members normalise weight stigma and place the onus on women to endure or overcome it, rather than recognising the need for broader societal change.

Our findings also have implications for future research to identify effective strategies to tackle these foundational constructions held by community members such as by developing and evaluating socio-culturally grounded interventions. Additionally, public policies and health promotion initiatives should seek to avoid perpetuating harmful social norms and consider a holistic approach to health. This may mean avoiding a weight loss focus and informing strategies that help to improve environmental and socioeconomic factors that create health disparities. Some of the strategies and initiatives specific to PPP women include promoting weight-inclusive care across the PPP periods, as well as addressing common assumptions about the relationship between weight and pregnancy outcomes. Ultimately, these concerted efforts will help improve the overall psychological, physical, mental, and social health of PPP women and their children.
