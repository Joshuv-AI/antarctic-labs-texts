A prospective cohort study exploring salivary microbial predictors of white spot lesions during fixed orthodontic treatment
Authors: Zijian Cheng, Lu Gao, Dingwen Luo, Chunsheng Bi, Qianming Chen, Xiaoyan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhite spot lesions (WSLs) are a common adverse outcome of fixed orthodontic treatment, prompting the search for salivary microbial predictors for early risk identification.Materials and methodsSixty-four patients undergoing fixed metal self-ligating orthodontic treatment were categorized into WSL and non-WSL groups based on lesion development after 12 months. Clinical indices (DI-S, CI-S, OHI-S, GI) and unstimulated whole saliva samples were collected at baseline and after 12 months. The salivary microbiome was analyzed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing.ResultsThe prevalence of WSLs was 54.7% at the patient level. Clinical indices did not differ significantly between groups. Although overall microbial community structure was similar at baseline, several taxa were enriched in patients who later developed WSLs, including Actinomyces, Rothia and Granulicatella. After 12 months, the WSL group showed reduced phylogenetic diversity and a microbial profile enriched in acidogenic and anaerobic taxa. Functional prediction indicated increased carbohydrate metabolism and fermentative pathways in WSL patients.ConclusionsWSL development during fixed orthodontic treatment was associated with baseline salivary microbial differences and treatment-related ecological shifts. Salivary microbiome profiling may help identify patients at higher risk and support early risk stratification and preventive strategies in orthodontic care.Keywords: White spot lesion, orthodontics, microbial predictors, saliva, fixed appliances

Introduction

White spot lesions (WSLs) are a frequent complication of fixed orthodontic treatment, affecting 45.8%−68.4% of patients [1,2]. Clinically, WSLs appear as chalky, opaque areas adjacent to orthodontic brackets compromising aesthetics. Histologically, they result from subsurface mineral loss, primarily calcium and phosphate, from hydroxyapatite crystals in the enamel, increasing enamel porosity and light scattering [3].

Early enamel demineralisation may be partially reversed through remineralization mediated by salivary proteins [4], but orthodontic appliances disrupt microbial balance and hinder plaque removal, promoting biofilm accumulation, low pH, and progressive enamel loss [5]. Lesions become clinically visible once demineralisation reaches approximately 75 µm [6], may develop within four weeks [7], and often plateau after 6–12 months [8,9]. Many persist for years, causing lasting aesthetic concerns [10].

The pathogenesis of WSLs is driven by host, dietary, salivary, and microbial factors [11]. According to the ecological plaque hypothesis, community-level shifts matter more than individual pathogens [12]; for example, Actinomyces and Corynebacterium spp. are enriched during the white spot stage of early caries Studies have shown that specific microbial shifts occur during different stages of caries development [13]. In orthodontic patients, Candida and Lactobacillus spp. have been linked to WSLs [14], and Candida carriers show elevated Streptococcus mutans [15], suggesting microbial synergy. However, most studies have primarily focused on single limited taxa and did not control for oral hygiene. Notably, WSLs develop in 15–42% of patients with excellent oral hygiene [16], whereas some with poor hygiene remain unaffected, indicating that host susceptibility and baseline microbial composition may play important roles.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate whether patients who develop WSLs exhibit distinct salivary microbiome profiles compared with those who remain lesion-free during orthodontic treatment. By profiling salivary microbial diversity and composition before and after orthodontic therapy, we sought to identify potential microbial markers of susceptibility that could facilitate early risk prediction and personalised preventive strategies in orthodontic care. We hypothesised that patients who develop WSLs would show distinct baseline salivary microbiome features and more pronounced treatment-related ecological shifts than those who remain lesion-free.

Materials and methods

Study design

This observational clinical study enroled patients with permanent dentition malocclusion who underwent labial fixed self-ligating orthodontic treatment with metal brackets at the Department of Orthodontics, Stomatology Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, from September 2021 to March 2023 (Figure 1). An a priori sample size calculation was performed for the primary exploratory comparison of salivary microbiome diversity between patients who developed WSLs and those who remained WSL-free [17,18]. Using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) [19], with a two-sided α level of 0.05, 80% power, and an assumed effect size of 0.75, the minimum required sample size was 58 participants. After allowing for an anticipated 20% dropout or exclusion rate, at least 72 participants were targeted for enrolment.

Study flowchart. WSL: white spot lesion; OHI-S: oral hygiene index-simplified; GI: gingival index; DI-S: debris index-simplified; CI-S: calculus index-simplified.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were recruited from a cohort study approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval No. 2021-66R). The microbiome analysis received additional approval from the same committee (approval No. R20230197). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were good oral hygiene compliance (OHI-S categorised as good), no previous orthodontic or remineralization treatment, no dental restorations or enamel defects, and no history of systemic disease, smoking, alcohol consumption, or recent (within 3 months) antibiotic or corticosteroid use. Exclusion criteria were the opposite converse of these conditions. Based on post-treatment WSL status, patients were classified into the WSL group or non-WSL group.

Orthodontic treatment and WSL assessment

All participants received standardised oral hygiene instruction prior to treatment. Intraoral photographs were taken at baseline (T0) and 12 months post-orthodontic treatment (T1) using a Canon EOS 850D (F22, 1/200s, ISO 400) from five standardised views angles. WSLs were assessed using a modified Gorelick Index (score 0: smooth enamel, no lesion; score 1: localised white opacity/linear band; score 2: demineralisation or cavitation) [5]. Patients were classified as having WSLs if at least one new lesion with a Gorelick score ≥1 was identified at T1. Patients presenting with at least one new lesion were assigned to the WSL group. Image evaluation was performed independently by two trained and calibrated orthodontic residents in a darkroom setting. The examiners were blinded to group allocation. Any discrepancies between the two examiners were resolved by a third reviewer.

Clinical indices

Oral hygiene was evaluated using the oral hygiene index-simplified (OHI-S) [20], combining debris index-simplified (DI-S) and calculus index-simplified (CI-S), based on the proportion of debris or calculus on the tooth surface of six index teeth. Gingival health was assessed using the gingival index (GI) [21], scored 0–3 based on colour, swelling, and bleeding upon probing.

Saliva collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing

Participants were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking, toothbrushing, mouth rinsing, or applying lipstick for at least 2 hours before sample collection. All saliva samples were collected in the morning under standardised conditions. Approximately 2 mL of unstimulated whole saliva was collected into a sterile tube, immediately placed on ice for transport, and stored at −80 °C until analysis [22,23]. Bacterial DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [24]. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using primers 341F and 806 R [25]. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA). After quality inspection, libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE250, Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Raw FASTQ files were processed using QIIME2 (v2022.2). Quality filtering, denoising, merging, and chimera removal were conducted with the DADA2 plugin. The resulting high-quality sequences were subsequently clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity for downstream analyses [26]. Representative sequences were taxonomically assigned using the SILVA 16S rRNA database (release 138) at 99% similarity. Alpha- and beta-diversity indices were computed to evaluate within-sample and between-sample microbial variation, respectively. Group differences in beta diversity were assessed using both PERMANOVA and ANOSIM tests, which provide complementary information regarding group separation. When results are consistent, PERMANOVA was preferred, as it increases confidence in detecting community differences [27]. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion was further examined using PERMDISP to determine whether significant beta-diversity results could be influenced by differences in within-group dispersion. Differentially abundant taxa were first identified using LEfSe with a logarithmic LDA score threshold of 2.0. These results were subsequently validated using DESeq2, applying thresholds of |log₂FoldChange| > 1.5 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05. Taxa consistently identified by both methods were considered robust, while discrepancies were resolved by prioritising DESeq2 results due to its stricter statistical framework [28]. Core taxa were defined as bacterial taxa detected in all participants within each group. Correlations between microbial taxa and clinical parameters were analysed using redundancy analysis (RDA) and Spearman’s correlation, visualised via igraph in R (v4.0). Functional metagenomic prediction was conducted using PICRUSt2 [29]. Functional annotation was performed against the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (database). Pathways detected in fewer than 50% of total samples were excluded before statistical testing. Group differences in predicted microbial functions were assessed using ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test and Dunn’s test. Clinical data were analysed in SPSS (v26.0, IBM, USA); normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SD and compared using independent-sample t-tests, while non-parametric data were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis or Wilcoxon tests. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and white spot lesion prevalence

A total of 82 patients were initially enroled. During follow-up, 18 patients were excluded due to poor oral hygiene maintenance. Therefore, 64 patients (mean age: 23.39 ± 5.43 years) undergoing fixed metal self-ligating orthodontic treatment with complete data at both time points were finally included. The prevalence of WSLs at the patient level was 54.7% (35/64). Among 1,081 examined teeth, 125 teeth developed WSLs, yielding a tooth-level prevalence of 11.6%. WSLs were most frequently detected on the maxillary lateral incisors (right: 39.7% and left: 32.8%) and least frequently on the mandibular second premolars, with no cases detected at this site (Figure S1).

In the WSL group (n = 35), there were 6 males and 29 females (mean age: 22.83 ± 5.83 years). The control group (n = 29) included 11 males and 18 females (mean age: 24.03 ± 4.95 years). There were no statistically significant differences in age or sex distribution between groups (P > 0.05; Table 1). Similarly, the two groups did not differ significantly in clinical indices (DI-S, CI-S, OHI-S, and GI) at either baseline or post-treatment (P > 0.05). Following orthodontic treatment, OHI-S scores showed an upward trend in both groups, although the change did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, GI scores increased significantly after treatment (P < 0.05), indicating a general elevation in gingival inflammation during orthodontic therapy.

Baseline (T0) salivary microbiome profile

At baseline, α-diversity indices showed no significant difference between the WSL and non-WSL groups (P > 0.05; Figure 2A), indicating comparable species richness and evenness. Core microbiome analysis identified 35 bacterial phyla across all samples, with seven phyla consistently ranking among the top ten in relative abundance and collectively accounting for over 99% of total sequences. Among these, Actinobacteriota exhibited a significantly higher relative abundance in the WSL group (P < 0.05; Figure 2B).

(D) Differences in shared core genera between groups. (E) Volcano plot of differentially abundant species identified between WSL and non-WSL groups. PERMANOVA based on Bray–Curtis and weighted UniFrac distance matrices revealed no significant differences in β-diversity (P > 0.05 for both), suggesting that the overall microbial community structure remained similar between groups (Figure 2C). However, genus-level comparison identified specific taxa differences: Actinomyces, Rothia and Granulicatella were significantly enriched in the WSL group, whereas Fusobacterium was more abundant in the non-WSL group (P < 0.05; Figure 2D).

At the species level (Figure 2E), 11 species showed significantly higher abundance in the WSL group, including Prevotella scopos, Rothia mucilaginosa, Corynebacterium durum, Prevotella histicola, Haemophilus pittmaniae, Campylobacter gracilis, Leptotrichia hofstadii, Capnocytophaga sp., Corynebacterium matruchotii, Rothia dentocariosa and Selenomonas artemidis, indicating enhanced anaerobic metabolism and acidogenic potential. In contrast, Prevotella nigrescens, Treponema vincentii, Anaeroglobus geminatus and Veillonella sp. were significantly enriched in the non-WSL group, reflecting a more balanced, oxidative, and health-associated microbial community. These baseline differences indicate distinct microbial compositions between individuals who subsequently developed WSLs and those who did not.

Salivary microbial profile after 12 months of orthodontic treatment

After 12 months of treatment, the Faith_pd index was significantly lower in the WSL group, suggesting reduced phylogenetic diversity (Figure 3A). β-diversity analyses showed significant differences between the WSL and non-WSL groups at T1 based on Bray–Curtis distance (P < 0.001) and weighted UniFrac distance (P < 0.05) (Figure 3B). Because PERMDISP indicated significant dispersion heterogeneity for Bray–Curtis distance (F = 10.21, P = 0.002) but not for weighted UniFrac distance (F = 0.0155, P = 0.896), the Bray–Curtis result was interpreted cautiously, whereas the weighted UniFrac result supported true community-structure differences between groups. At the phylum level, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Fusobacteriota, and Actinobacteriota remained predominant (>95% cumulative abundance) in both groups. However, Bacteroidota was significantly enriched, whereas Fusobacteriota was significantly depleted in the WSL group (Figure 3C).

Shared genera analysis revealed six genera with significantly different relative abundances: Prevotella and Actinomyces were enriched in WSL, while Fusobacterium, Porphyromonas, Gemella, and Catonella were depleted (Figure 3D). At the species level, 20 species were significantly enriched in the WSL group at T1 (Figure 3E), including Bifidobacterium dentium, Veillonella atypica, Streptococcus mutans, Alloprevotella rava, Neisseria oralis, Rothia mucilaginosa and Prevotella pallens. These taxa, particularly Prevotella, Veillonella, and Streptococcus, are characteristic of acidogenic and plaque-mature microbial environments, suggesting a dysbiotic shift associated with lesion development. Conversely, the non-WSL group exhibited enrichment of Fusobacterium periodonticum, Tannerella forsythia, Capnocytophaga leadbetteri and Porphyromonas gingivalis, species typical of a mature yet ecologically balanced biofilm. These results indicate clear microbial divergence between the two groups following orthodontic treatment.

The effect of orthodontic treatment on salivary microbiota

Faith_pd index declined significantly in both groups after 12 months of orthodontic treatment (Figure 4A). However, significant β-diversity shifts were observed only in the WSL group (Figure 4B, P < 0.01), whereas no significant shift was detected in the non-WSL group, suggesting a more pronounced treatment-associated restructuring of the salivary microbiome in patients who developed WSLs. PERMDISP analysis showed no significant difference in within-group dispersion (F = 0.8359, P = 0.371), indicating that the observed shift was unlikely to be driven by unequal dispersion. Venn diagram and comparative taxonomic analyses revealed distinct compositional changes across orthodontic stages (Figure 4C, Table S1). At the genus level, the non-WSL group showed a larger number of shared core genera both before and after orthodontic treatment. Following orthodontic treatment, the overall overlap between groups increased; however, distinct ecological shifts were observed. At the species level, several taxa were newly detected at T1 within each group, and some appeared in both groups after treatment (e.g. Prevotella pallens, Capnocytophaga leadbetteri, and Campylobacter concisus), indicating that these taxa were treatment-emergent rather than group-exclusive. In the WSL group, newly detected taxa included Streptococcus salivarius and Prevotella salivae, whereas the non-WSL group showed newly detected Corynebacterium durum, Capnocytophaga sputigena, Selenomonas sp., and Neisseria elongata.

Analysis of shared core genera showed distinct treatment-associated shifts (Figure 4D). The non-WSL group exhibited increases in Actinomyces, Corynebacterium, Rothia, and Treponema. In the WSL group, Actinomyces and Prevotella increased, while Fusobacterium and Gemella decreased. At the species level, 19 species were significantly enriched at T1 in the non-WSL group, reflecting a microbial transition toward a more mature yet ecologically stable community. In contrast, 12 species were enriched at T1 in the WSL group, including Anaeroglobus geminatus, Prevotella denticola, Alloprevotella rava, Prevotella buccae, Streptococcus mutans, Actinomyces massiliensis, Bifidobacterium dentium, Capnocytophaga ochracea, Prevotella maculosa, and Prevotella salivae (Figure 4E). Several of these taxa have been associated with plaque biofilms, anaerobic niches, or carbohydrate-fermenting communities. Because saliva represents a mixed oral sample and may contain microorganisms shed from multiple oral surfaces, including dental plaque, these findings should be interpreted as salivary correlates of WSL-associated ecological changes rather than direct evidence of lesion-site dominance.

Correlation analysis between salivary microbiota and clinical indices

Redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed to assess the relationship between clinical indices and microbial composition, and PERMANOVA was used to evaluate statistical significance (Figure 5). GI and OHI-S showed positive correlations with microbial community variation, with OHI-S contributing the largest proportion of explained variance. However, these associations were not statistically significant (PERMANOVA, P = 0.241). Certain taxa, including Fusobacterium periodonticum and Porphyromonas pasteri, were more abundant in individuals with higher GI scores, whereas Veillonella atypica showed higher abundance in individuals with elevated DI-S values.

Microbial community functional prediction

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed distinct functional profiles between the WSL and non-WSL groups. At baseline, the WSL group showed greater representation of pathways related to anaerobic metabolism and inflammatory signalling pathways (Figure 6A). After orthodontic treatment, functional differences between the two groups became more pronounced, with the WSL group displaying increased enrichment of pathways associated with acidogenic metabolism and host–microbe interaction (Figure 6B). Notably, adipocytokine signalling, methane metabolism, and flavonoid biosynthesis were consistently enriched in the WSL group both before and after treatment. Conversely, core energy-related pathways and antioxidant defenses remained persistently downregulated. Interestingly, adipocytokine signalling displayed a bidirectional change, with lower representation at baseline and increased enrichment after treatment.

Discussion

White spot lesions (WSLs) remain one of the most common adverse effects of fixed orthodontic treatment. In the present study, 54.7% of patients developed WSLs within the first 12 months of therapy, consistent with previous reports [2], highlighting the substantial clinical burden of enamel demineralisation associated with bracket-based appliances. Previous studies have shown that orthodontic appliances create plaque-retentive niches that promote microbial accumulation and ecological shifts within the oral microbiome [30]. However, despite similar mechanical challenges, not all patients develop WSLs, suggesting that host- or microbiome-related susceptibility factors may play an important role. Our findings support the study hypothesis that patients who develop WSLs exhibit distinct baseline salivary microbiome characteristics and more pronounced treatment-related ecological shifts than those who remain lesion-free.

A notable finding of this study is that oral hygiene and gingival indices were comparable at the baseline between the WSL and non-WSL groups, yet only a subset of patients developed lesions. This pattern suggests that appliance-related plaque retention alone is insufficient to explain WSL occurrence. Rather, our findings support a model in which fixed appliances impose ecological stress on the oral environment, whereas individual susceptibility is at least partly determined by pre-existing salivary microbial characteristics. This interpretation aligns with the ecological plaque hypothesis of caries, in which lesion development reflects community-level dysbiosis under sustained environmental pressure [31]. Importantly, this interpretation is supported not only by comparable baseline clinical indices, but also by the baseline microbiome differences observed before lesion development.

The longitudinal design of this study enabled evaluation of microbial profiles before and during orthodontic treatment. Although overall alpha- and beta-diversity were comparable at baseline, several taxa including Actinomyces, Rothia and Granulicatella, were already enriched in patients who later developed WSLs. This finding directly supports the central premise of the present study that salivary microbial features detectable before clinical lesion formation may help identify patients at increased risk. These findings suggest that susceptibility to WSL formation may be embedded in pre-treatment microbial configurations rather than arising solely after bracket placement. Our findings are consistent with the pilot study by Catunda et al., which reported that pre-treatment salivary microbiome features and salivary Stephan curve kinetics may be associated with subsequent WSL development in patients wearing fixed appliances [32]. Collectively, these observations support the concept that salivary microbial signatures may precede clinically detectable enamel demineralisation.

After 12 months of treatment, clear microbial divergence emerged between the two groups. Patients who developed WSLs showed reduced phylogenetic diversity and a distinct beta-diversity profile compared with controls, indicating that orthodontic treatment may amplify pre-existing ecological differences. Notably, several taxa were consistently enriched across time points in patients who developed WSLs. These taxa are associated with acidogenic metabolism, early biofilm maturation, and adaptation to low-oxygen niches, processes closely linked to enamel demineralisation and biofilm pathogenicity [33–35]. At the genus level, Actinomyces remained elevated both before and after treatment and is well-recognised as an acid producer capable of generating lactic, acetic, and succinic acids, thereby contributing to enamel demineralisation [13]. At the species level, the persistent enrichment of P. scopos, R. mucilaginosa, C. durum and S. artemidis indicates the presence of stable microbial traits that may predispose individuals to WSL formation. Prevotella and Selenomonas species promote carbohydrate fermentation and organic acid production [36–38], whereas R. mucilaginosa and Corynebacterium species act as structural scaffolds in supragingival biofilms, supporting microbial coaggregation and maturation [39,40]. Enrichment of R. mucilaginosa has also been observed in patients with active caries [41,42], and recent studies combining 16S rRNA sequencing with machine learning have identified this species as a potential biomarker for predicting caries risk in young children [43]. Together, these microbial features may promote the development of a biofilm microenvironment that is more susceptible to acidification when orthodontic appliances introduce additional plaque-retentive niches.

Functional prediction analysis further supported this ecological interpretation. Compared with the non-WSL group, the salivary microbiome of WSL patients showed greater representation of pathways associated with carbohydrate metabolism, pyruvate fermentation, and anaerobic energy production, whereas pathways related to amino acid biosynthesis and oxidative phosphorylation were relatively more prominent in the non-WSL group. These predicted functional shifts suggest the emergence of a more fermentative microbial ecosystem that favours acid production and enamel demineralisation. A recent multi-omics study also demonstrated distinct microbiome and metabolome signatures associated with WSLs in orthodontic patients, supporting the concept that functional remodelling accompanies taxonomic shifts in lesion-prone individuals [44]. Because PICRUSt2 provides indirect functional inference, these results should be interpreted as indicative functional trends rather than direct measurements of metabolic activity.

Previous studies report a threefold increased risk of WSL in patients wearing fixed orthodontic appliances compared with untreated individuals [45]. WSLs, especially on anterior teeth, negatively impact smile aesthetics and treatment satisfaction [46]. Many studies have documented oral microbial shifts during fixed orthodontic therapy, often showing increased abundance of acid-producing anaerobes [47–49]. Our findings extend previous taxa-focused reports involving Actinomyces, Corynebacterium, Candida, and Lactobacillus by showing that WSL susceptibility is better understood as a community-level dysbiotic process, while still retaining biologically relevant contributions from individual taxa. Among the taxa enriched in WSL patients after treatment, Prevotella is notable for its acidogenic and biofilm-associated properties. Prevotella species possess diverse polysaccharide-degrading enzymes that contribute to biofilm maturation and carbohydrate fermentation [50]. Experimental studies also suggest that certain Prevotella species can activate Toll-like receptor 2, stimulating antigen-presenting cells to produce Th17-polarising cytokines such as IL-23 and IL-1, thereby promoting inflammatory responses within oral biofilms [51]. Recent studies further suggest that WSL-associated dysbiosis may extend beyond bacterial composition alone. Yang and colleagues reported enrichment of Candida albicans in orthodontic-associated WSLs and demonstrated interactions between fungal colonisation and the supragingival bacteriome [52]. Although our saliva-based design does not allow direct assessment of bracket-adjacent plaque or fungal-bacterial interactions, it suggests that a detectable salivary susceptibility signal may exist before overt lesion development. Saliva may therefore serve as a practical and non-invasive medium for microbiome-based risk stratification.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although 16S rRNA gene sequencing provides valuable phylogenetic information, its taxonomic resolution is lower than that of metagenomic sequencing, which limits species-level identification [53]. Second, the relatively modest sample size may have limited the statistical power for microbial biomarker discovery and increased the risk of both type I and type II errors. Therefore, the identified microbial predictors should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in larger independent cohorts. Third, saliva samples may not fully capture the site-specific microbial environment adjacent to orthodontic brackets where WSLs develop. Fourth, although saliva collection was performed under standardised morning conditions, menstrual and hormonal factors that may influence salivary microbiota were not specifically controlled. In addition, salivary physiological factors such as flow rate, buffering capacity, and dietary sugar exposure were not assessed in this study [54]. Future studies integrating site-specific plaque sampling, multi-omics approaches, and clinical risk indicators may help establish more robust predictive models for WSL development [55,56].

Conclusion

This study suggests that pre-existing microbial traits and treatment-driven ecological shifts jointly contribute to individual susceptibility to WSL development during fixed orthodontic treatment. Baseline enrichment of selected taxa, followed by reduced phylogenetic diversity and increased acidogenic and anaerobic features after treatment, supports the potential role of salivary microbial predictors in identifying patients at higher risk. Salivary microbiome profiling may therefore aid early risk stratification and personalised prevention. Future studies integrating metagenomics, site-specific plaque sampling, and salivary functional measures are needed to validate theses candidate markers and improve microbiome-based WSL risk assessment.
