Response to Letter to the Editor Regarding High-Definition Intravascular Ultrasound Versus Optical Coherence Tomography: Lumen Size and Plaque Morphology
Authors: Wei Wu, Shijia Zhao, Changkye Lee, Yash Vardhan Trivedi, Farzad Rezaeicherati, Ali Ghahremaninezhad, Yiannis S Chatzizisis
Published: 2026

We would like to thank Drs Lee, Onuma, and Serruys for their thoughtful feedback1 on our study2 and welcome the opportunity to clarify several points.

In our study, we used meticulously fabricated cylindrical silicone models of varying diameters, imaged with optical coherence tomography (OCT; axial resolution 10-20 μm; Abbott), high-definition intravascular ultrasound (axial resolution 22 μm; Boston Scientific), and microcomputed tomography (μCT; axial resolution 24 μm; X3000, North Star Imaging), the last of which served as ground truth. In response to the letter, we reimaged the 3.0 mm, 4.0 mm, and 5.0 mm models (N = 66 sections in total) using a different μCT scanner (axial resolution 9.5 μm; SKYSCAN 1273, Bruker) with 2.5-fold higher resolution. As shown in Figure 1, lumen measurements between the 2 μCT modalities were nearly identical (bias +9.8 μm; 95% limits of agreement, −29.6 to +49.1 μm; y = 1.008x − 0.044; R2 = 0.999), supporting the robustness of our ground truth. As Lee et al note, μCT—like any reconstructed imaging modality—carries segmentation, threshold, and voxel-resolution uncertainties; however, given its very high resolution (9.5 μm), it represents the closest tomographic approximation to true measurements, and therefore constitutes a legitimate and accurate reference.

Comparison of lumen diameter by 24 μm and 9.5 μm μCT. These results show very high agreement between the 2 μCT modalities.

In the cited work by Tsuchida et al,3 the authors used precision-drilled plexiglass cylindrical lumens with diameters ranging from 0.5 to 1.9 mm, approximating small epicardial coronary artery branches. Our study, by design, evaluated lumen diameters from 2.0 to 6.0 mm, the clinically relevant range of the main epicardial coronary arteries. The 2 studies are therefore not directly comparable, but rather complementary, as they address different scales of coronary lumen dimensions.

Several additional considerations support the robustness and consistency of our ground-truth modality. First, the OCT underestimation we observed (−170 ± 60 μm for mean lumen diameter, reaching 240 μm for diameters ≥5.0 mm) was 5- to 10-fold greater than our μCT voxel size of 24 μm; theoretical μCT segmentation variability of 1 to 2 voxels cannot account for a systematic bias of this magnitude. Second, the diameter-dependent nature of the OCT bias—worsening progressively with increasing lumen size—is consistent with known OCT physics, including refractive-index assumptions and beam geometry at depth rather than with a μCT reconstruction artifact, which would be expected to produce a fixed offset. Third, our findings converge with prior literature comparing OCT against multiple independent reference standards,4 and systematic OCT underestimation in larger lumens is not a μCT-specific observation. Having said that, we agree that “closer agreement with μCT” would, strictly speaking, be a more precise phrasing, though this does not alter the central message or validity of our findings. Going forward, hybrid validation frameworks combining μCT with optical microscopy or other bench technologies would further strengthen comparative intravascular imaging research. We are actively pursuing such work and would welcome collaborative engagement with Professor Serruys’ research center, whose contributions to intracoronary imaging are widely recognized.

Finally, the principal clinical message of our study—that the performance gap between the latest-generation high-definition intravascular ultrasound and OCT systems has narrowed substantially relative to earlier-generation platforms, motivating reconsideration of current minimal stent area cutoffs—does not depend on μCT being an absolute ground truth. It reflects the convergence of 2 contemporary intracoronary imaging systems toward a common dimensional reference, a finding that remains robust under any reasonable interpretation of the validation framework.
