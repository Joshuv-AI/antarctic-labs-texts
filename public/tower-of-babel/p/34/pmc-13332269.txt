Factors associated with medication adherence and perceived treatment barriers among Nepali women with polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome: A cross- sectional study
Authors: Sajana Basnet, Nirmal Raj Marasine, Shishir Paudel, Poonam Pant, Sabina Sankhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective management of Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) depends on sustained medication adherence, yet evidence on adherence patterns and treatment barriers in low-resource settings remains limited.ObjectivesThis study assessed factors associated with medication adherence and perceived treatment barriers among Nepalese women with PMOS.DesignCross-sectional study.MethodsA total of 145 women aged 18–49 years diagnosed with PMOS at an infertility center in Kathmandu were enrolled between December 2023 and May 2024. Data were collected via face-to-face interviews using the General Medication Adherence Scale (GMAS) and a Barriers Assessment Questionnaire. Chi-square tests and unadjusted odds ratios (uOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) identified associations.ResultsSuboptimal adherence was observed in 66.3% of participants and 65.5% reported high treatment barriers. The factors associated withsuboptimal adherence included never-married status (uOR: 4.01; 95% CI: 1.87–8.43), lower educational attainment (illiterate: uOR: 1.40; 95% CI: 1.02–4.46; primary level: uOR: 1.38; 95% CI: 1.12–5.22), homemaker (uOR: 0.61; 95% CI: 0.29–0.88) or unemployed status (uOR: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.28–0.98), residence outside Kathmandu Valley (uOR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.48–0.98), and smoking (uOR: 10.8; 95% CI: 1.23–95.2). High barriers were more likely among homemakers (uOR: 1.33; 95% CI: 1.13–2.80) and those outside Kathmandu Valley (uOR: 2.49; 95% CI: 1.21–5.14). Limited resources (p=0.028) and perceived symptomatic relief (p=0.011) were significantly associated with adherence.ConclusionMedication adherence was suboptimal, with barriers shaped by sociodemographic and healthcare access factors. Targeted interventions, rural outreach, patient education, and improved resource availability are essential to optimize PMOS treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Polyendrocine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) is one of the most common endocrine disorders affecting women of reproductive age, often beginning in adolescence. It is a leading cause of infertility, with a prevalence of approximately 6–13% among reproductive-aged women, and up to 70% of cases remain undiagnosed. 1 The condition is characterized by anovulation and/or the presence of multiple small cysts on the ovaries, elevated androgen levels, endocrine disturbances, and menstrual irregularities. 2 PMOS not only contributes to infertility and pregnancy-related complications, including miscarriages, but is also associated with an increased risk of ovarian, endometrial, and breast cancers. 3 Beyond reproductive health, it poses serious metabolic (e.g., diabetes, dyslipidemia, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, cardiovascular diseases) and psychological challenges (e.g., anxiety, depression, stress), all of which can substantially affect a woman’s quality of life.4,5

The pathophysiology of PMOS is complex and thought to involve dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian/adrenal axis. 6 Although an elevated luteinizing hormone (LH) to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) ratio and increased frequency of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) secretion are considered key contributors, the exact etiology remains unclear. 7 Management of this multifactorial condition primarily emphasizes lifestyle modifications such as regular physical activity, balanced diet, and smoking cessation as first-line interventions. Pharmacological treatments, including oral contraceptives, progesterone, anti-androgens, and metformin, are commonly prescribed to address reproductive, metabolic, and psychological manifestations. 8 These approaches have been shown to improve outcomes across multiple domains of PMOS.8,9

Treatment effectiveness heavily depends on patient adherence. However, adherence remains a critical challenge and is often reported as suboptimal among PMOS patients. 1 Contributing factors include patient-related issues such as time constraints, financial difficulties, psychological comorbidities, and lack of awareness, as well as treatment-related concerns such as high costs, prolonged therapy, side effects, and perceived inefficacy. 10 The patient–provider relationship also plays a significant role; insufficient counseling and support from healthcare professionals can further hinder adherence. Environmental factors, such as limited social support, poor healthcare accessibility, and lack of resources in developing countries like Nepal, further exacerbate these challenges10,11

The prevalence of PMOS in Nepal is estimated at 9.18%. 12 However, data on factors influencing medication adherence and perceived barriers to treatment remain scarce. This study aimed to assess the factors associated with medication adherence and perceived treatment barriers among Nepalese women with PMOS attending an infertility center. By addressing this gap, the study provides valuable insights to help healthcare professionals and policymakers design evidence-based interventions that improve adherence, overcome barriers, and optimize PMOS management in the Nepalese context. To address these gaps, this study was guided by the following research questions: (1) What is the prevalence and pattern of medication adherence among Nepalese women with PMOS attending an infertility center? (2) Which sociodemographic and clinical factors are significantly associated with suboptimal medication adherence? (3) What treatment barriers do these women perceive, and which factors contribute to a higher barrier burden?

Methodology

Study design, population and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2023 to May 2024 at an infertility center in Bijulibazar, Kathmandu, a well-known clinic for PMOS and infertility treatment managed by highly qualified doctors. The outpatient department (OPD) operates from 8:30 AM to 3:00 PM.

Sample size and sampling techniques

According to the previous study, the prevalence of PMOS was estimated at 10%. 14 Using the Cochrane formula, the required sample size (n) was calculated as: n=Z2⋅p⋅q/e2 where Z=1.96 (standard normal deviate at a 95% confidence level), p=0.1(prevalence), q=1−p=0.9, and e=0.05 (margin of error). Substituting these values, the calculated sample size was 138. To account for potential non-response or incomplete data, a 5% adjustment was applied, resulting in a final adjusted sample size of 145. A convenience sampling technique was employed, whereby all eligible women attending the infertility center during the study period who met the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent were invited to participate. This approach was chosen due to practical constraints in accessing a population-based sampling frame for PMOS patients in Nepal. While convenience sampling is prone to selection bias particularly volunteer bias, whereby women who are more engaged with their healthcare may be overrepresented and limits generalizability to the broader PMOS population, it is commonly used in clinical settings with comparable patient access. To minimize selection bias, all women presenting during the study period were systematically approached regardless of their clinical profile or treatment status, and refusal rates were documented.

Data collection

Prior to data collection, all interviewers (trained pharmacy graduates) underwent a standardized training session covering study objectives, questionnaire administration, participant communication strategies, and confidentiality protocols. A structured data collection guide was provided to ensure consistency in question delivery and response recording. Completed questionnaires were reviewed daily by the principal investigator to identify missing entries or inconsistencies, and any ambiguous responses were resolved through re-contact with participants where feasible. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a semi-structured questionnaire. Both pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments, along with laboratory reports were recorded by reviewing patient Kardex. The data collection tool was developed after reviewing published articles and was adapted to the study context with input from an endocrinologist, a statistician, and a supervisor. The questionnaire consisted of four sections. Section 1 included baseline details of participants, such as age, marital status, occupation, education, body mass index (BMI), family history, area of residence, lifestyle-related information, and signs and symptoms of PMOS. Section 2 comprised the treatment proforma, including laboratory reports and details of pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments. Section 3 contained the General Medication Adherence Scale (GMAS) to assess medication adherence. Section 4 included the Barriers Assessment Questionnaire, which covered physical, psychological, treatment-related, and healthcare-related factors. The original English-language questionnaire was translated into Nepali to facilitate data collection. The GMAS has been previously translated, culturally adapted, and validated into the Nepalese language by Shrestha et al., 15 and that validated Nepali version was used in this study. The barriers questionnaire was translated from English to Nepali using a forward-backward translation procedure: an independent bilingual translator rendered the questionnaire into Nepali, and a second translator back-translated it into English without reference to the original. Discrepancies between the original and back-translated versions were reviewed and resolved by the research team and a language expert to ensure conceptual equivalence and cultural appropriateness. The translated questionnaire was then reviewed by a panel of two clinical experts (an endocrinologist and a pharmacist) for content validity. It was pretested 10% of the sample, and only minor wording and typographical corrections were required. Internal consistency of the tool was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which yielded a reliability score of 0.82.

Measurements

Medication adherence and treatment barriers were the primary outcomes of the study. GMAS, consisting of 11 items, was used to assess patient’s adherence to treatment, yielding a score that categorizes adherence as high, good, partial, low, or poor. Each item offered four response options: always, mostly, sometimes, or never, assigned scores of 0, 1, 2, or 3, respectively. The total possible score was 33, with adherence levels classified as follows: ≤10 indicating poor adherence, 17–26 indicating partial adherence, 27–29 indicating good adherence, and 30–33 indicating high adherence. 15 The perceived barriers assessment questionnaire was designed to evaluate the challenges participants faced in managing PMOS. It covered three primary domains: physical and psychosocial factors, treatment-related factors, and healthcare system-related factors. Each of the 14 questions in this section was scored as 1 for a ‘yes’ response and 0 for a ‘no’ response, resulting in a total possible score of 14. A cutoff mean score of 6 was used: a score below 6 indicated low barriers to treatment, while a score above 6 indicated high barriers to treatment.4,11 Drugs were classified using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification System. 16

Statistical analysis

Data were digitized using Epi-Data 3.1 and subsequently exported to SAS Version 9.4 for analysis. Univariate statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean and standard deviation summarized participants characteristics. Chi-square test assessed differences in medication adherence and treatment barriers across independent variables. Medication adherence was categorized as optimal or suboptimal, and binary logistic regression was performed to examine associations between relevant factors and medication adherence. Unadjusted odds ratios (uORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used as the primary metrics to quantify associations between independent variables and outcome measures. Multivariable logistic regression to derive adjusted odds ratios (aORs) was considered; however, given the limited sample size (n=145) and the number of candidate covariates, the minimum events-per-variable threshold required for stable multivariable models could not be reliably met. Proceeding with multivariable adjustment under these conditions risked model overfitting and unreliable coefficient estimates. This decision is consistent with established guidance on regression modeling in small exploratory studies. Residual confounding therefore remains a limitation and should be addressed in future larger studies. Missing data were minimal; questionnaires with incomplete responses were excluded from the analysis on a per-variable basis and the extent of missing data is reported within each analysis. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the CiST College Institutional Review Committee (CiST-IRC; Ref.No.31/080/081). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. For participants with limited literacy, the consent form was explained verbally in Nepali with an impartial witness present. The witness signed to confirm the explanation, and participants gave consent using their thumbprint. Participants received comprehensive oral and written information about the study’s purpose and were clearly informed that their participation was entirely voluntary. They were also assured of their right to withdraw at any time if they felt uncomfortable during the interview. To ensure privacy and minimize interruptions, all interviews were conducted one-on-one in a separate room.

Results

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the 145 participants enrolled in the study. The majority of participants (75.9%) between 25-34 years of age, with a mean age of 28.94 ± 4.5 years. Most were married (89%) and identified as Hindu (95.2%). In terms of education, 41.4% had attained undergraduate-level qualification, and 42.1% were employed in non-governmental jobs. Nearly 44.0% of the participants resided outside the Kathmandu valley. A family history of PMOS was reported by only 13.1% of participants. Lifestyle factors such as smoking (4.1%) and alcohol consumption (17.2%) were relatively uncommon. Prior to their PMOS diagnosis, just 13.1% of participants engaged in regular physical exercise. BMI, classified according to WHO standards, showed that 43.4% had a normal BMI, 35.9% were overweight, and 14.5% of them were obese. Regarding physical symptoms, 86.2% did not report changes in body hair growth, while 4.1% reported hirsutism. Similarly, 82.8% did not observe significant changes in acne patterns. However, irregular menstruation was reported by 60.7% of participants, highlighting it as a common symptom of PMOS. Regarding treatment-related factors, 30.3% reported that side effects affected their willingness to adhere, while 82.8% reported experiencing relief from current treatment, which was significantly associated with adherence (p = 0.011).

In the domain of healthcare system-related factors, 43.4% reported that distance to the hospital affected regular visits, though this and other factors such as privacy during consultation and informational support from doctors were not significantly associated with adherence.

Table 6 presents the factors associated with medication adherence among women with PMOS. Suboptimal adherence was observed in 66.2% of participants, while 33.8% demonstrated optimal adherence. Discussion

This study assessed the factors associated with medication adherence and perceived treatment barriers among Nepali women with PMOS attending an infertility center. The findings revealed a low rate of optimal medication adherence alongside a high prevalence of perceived barriers, highlighting significant challenges in PMOS management in Nepal.

The suboptimal adherence rate aligns with prior studies from low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), where adherence among PMOS patients ranges between 25% to 40%.11,17 In the study, lifestyle-related factors, particularly forgetfulness and self-adjustment of medication regimens were the most common contributors to non-adherence. This is consistent with global evidence identifying forgetfulness, perceived lack of benefit, side effects, and socioeconomic constraints as the major determinants of poor adherence.10,18 Conversely, perception of symptomatic improvement significantly enhanced adherence, reinforcing literature that suggests belief in treatment effectiveness is a key motivator for sustained therapy. 19 Interestingly, most participants did not perceive treatment regimens or financial constraints as burdensome, which contrasts with findings from India and Iran, where high medication costs were major barriers.4,9

Sociodemographic disparities also influenced adherence. Never-married women and those residing outside Kathmandu Valley had higher odds of suboptimal adherence, possibly due to stigma surrounding PMOS-related infertility and limited access to specialized healthcare services.11,12 PMOS often carries psychosocial burdens such as stigma, poor body image, anxiety, and depression, which can discourage care-seeking behavior and long-term adherence in women. Marital status may further explain these differences, as married women may be more adherent due to fertility-related motivations, while never-married women may perceive less immediate benefit from strict adherence. Smoking was another predictor of non-adherence, in line with global findings linking tobacco use to poorer self-care behaviors and treatment compliance in chronic diseases. 5 Lower educational attainment was also associated with suboptimal adherence, highlighting the role of health literacy in self-management. 8

Regarding treatment barriers, limited availability of resources for PMOS management was significantly associated with adherence. This indicates that logistical challenges, such as lack of specialized care and diagnostic facilities, remain critical in resource-limited settings.5,10 Symptomatic improvement also influences treatment adherence, reflecting a common pattern in chronic conditions, where perceived progress encourages persistence. 20

Occupational and residential status further shaped perceptions of barriers. Homemakers were more likely to report barriers, possibly due to household responsibilities and limited autonomy affecting healthcare access. 11 Women living outside Kathmandu valley reported higher barriers, reflecting reduced awareness, fewer specialist services, and limited access to lifestyle counseling, consistent with other South Asian and global studies.5,14,21

Interestingly, health system-related barriers, such as distance to healthcare facilities, privacy, and lack of informational support were not significantly associated with adherence. Although participants did not express major dissatisfaction with providers, the absence of structured PMOS education and follow-up programs in Nepal may indirectly hinder long-term adherence. These findings emphasize the need for culturally adapted interventions, including family-centered education, stigma-reduction initiatives, decentralization of PMOS services, and integration of mental health support to improve adherence and outcomes.9,10

The psychosocial dimensions of PMOS management warrant particular attention. Women with PMOS attending infertility centers frequently experience anxiety, depression, and relationship challenges related to their fertility concerns, which can substantially undermine motivation and adherence to long-term treatment regimens. A recent comprehensive review by Ribeiro Neto et al. (2025) underscored the emotional and ethical complexities faced by individuals undergoing assisted reproductive techniques (ART), highlighting that infertility care must address emotional well-being alongside biomedical management. 22 These insights are directly relevant to our population, given that most participants were attending an infertility center and many reported concerns about infertility. Healthcare providers should therefore routinely screen for psychological distress among women with PMOS and integrate mental health support such as counseling, peer support groups, and psychoeducation into standard care pathways. For policymakers, the findings highlight the need for dedicated PMOS management programs at district and provincial health facilities to reduce the burden of traveling to urban centers. Specifically, decentralized outreach clinics, community pharmacist-led adherence counseling, and subsidized access to PMOS medications in rural areas are recommended. Increasing public awareness of PMOS through community health workers and targeted social media campaigns could also improve early diagnosis, reduce stigma, and encourage sustained engagement with treatment.

Strengths and limitations

This study addresses a critical evidence gap by providing a comprehensive analysis of medication adherence and perceived treatment barriers among Nepali women with PMOS, using validated and reliable tools, potentially making it the first of its kind in the country. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reliance on self-reported measures may have introduced recall and social-desirability biases. Social desirability bias is particularly relevant in this context, as participants may have overreported adherence to appear compliant with their clinicians’ recommendations. Recall bias may also have affected responses related to past medication-taking behavior, given that participants were asked to reflect on adherence patterns over an extended period. These biases are inherent to self-report instruments and may result in an underestimation of the true prevalence of non-adherence. Convenience sampling and the single-center, urban setting further restrict generalizability, as women in rural or semi-urban areas may face different healthcare access, sociocultural influences, and treatment-seeking behaviors. The findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and may not be representative of the broader Nepali PMOS population. Unmeasured confounders, such as mental health status and detailed socioeconomic variables, may also have influenced the findings. Furthermore, no missing data imputation was performed; cases with incomplete questionnaire responses were excluded from analysis, which may have introduced a minor exclusion bias.

The study also did not examine medication adherence across PMOS phenotypes (A-D, Rotterdam criteria), which could have offered insights into phenotype-specific challenges. In addition, associations were assessed using unadjusted odds ratios; more robust approaches such as multivariable regression were not feasible due to the limited sample size, raising the possibility of residual confounding. Larger studies should incorporate stratified analyses and advanced statistical methods to better adjust for confounders (e.g., age, fertility intentions, socioeconomic status) and inform personalized management strategies for women with PMOS. Regarding external validity, the findings from this urban, single-center study may not fully reflect adherence patterns or treatment barriers among women attending primary or community-level health facilities, or those in rural Nepal where healthcare infrastructure, specialist availability, and cultural contexts differ substantially. Generalization to other low- and middle-income country settings should also be approached cautiously, as healthcare system structures and patient profiles vary considerably. Future research should prioritize multi-center, population-based studies spanning urban, peri-urban, and rural settings to generate more representative estimates. Longitudinal cohort designs would be particularly valuable for examining adherence trajectories over time and identifying critical points of non-adherence during the treatment course. Intervention trials evaluating the effectiveness of pharmacist-led adherence counseling, mobile health (mHealth) reminders, and community-based PMOS education programs are also strongly warranted. Additionally, qualitative and mixed-methods studies exploring patients’ lived experiences, health beliefs, and decision-making processes around PMOS treatment would complement quantitative findings and inform the design of culturally responsive interventions.

Conclusion

This study revealed that medication adherence among Nepali women with PMOS was suboptimal, with a substantial proportion experiencing high treatment barriers. Non-adherence was associated with being unmarried, having lower educational attainment, residing outside the Kathmandu Valley, and smoking, while limited resources for PMOS management and perceived symptomatic relief emerged as key barriers. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions, improved healthcare access, and lifestyle counseling, along with expanded rural outreach and strengthened patient education to enhance adherence and long-term outcomes.
