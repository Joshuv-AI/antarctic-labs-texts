Bidirectional associations among handgrip strength, gait speed, and cognitive function in older adults: a longitudinal study in rural China
Authors: Qiushuo Li, Zhiyue Jiang, Hao Chen, Jin Hu, Shiqi Gui, Jing Wang, Xing Yang, Jingyuan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccumulating research evidence suggests that handgrip strength (HGS) and gait speed are independently associated with cognitive function in older adults, whereas the magnitude and temporal order of the associations among HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function remain unclear.MethodsThis study adopted a cohort study design. The participants were from the Guizhou Rural Older Adults’ Health Study (HSRO). Older adults aged 60 years or older were included. Gait speed was measured by asking participants to walk along a 4.6 m walkway, while HGS was measured using an electronic hand dynamometer. Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). To explore the associations among HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function, as well as how these variables changed over time in older adults, cross-lagged panel models and linear mixed-effects models were utilized. The results obtained from both of these models were reported with standardized coefficients.ResultA total of 819 participants were included in the analysis (median age 70 years; 60.0% females). The results of the cross-lagged path model showed a significant association between HGS in 2019 and cognitive function in 2022 (β = 0.057, P < 0.05). Meanwhile, an association was observed from 2019 MMSE to 2022 HGS (β = 0.105, P < 0.001). Cognitive function and HGS measured in 2019 were significantly associated with gait speed in 2022, with respective β values of 0.078 and 0.071 (P < 0.05 for both). However, gait speed in 2019 was not significantly associated with either cognitive function or HGS in 2022 (both P > 0.05). The results of the linear mixed effects model showed higher HGS was associated with higher cognitive function (β = 0.090, P < 0.001). Neither gait speed nor the interaction term between HGS and gait speed had a statistically significant association with cognitive function (P > 0.05).ConclusionIn rural older adults, a bidirectional association exists between HGS and cognitive function. The combined effect of gait speed and HGS did not improve the prediction of cognitive function. Future interventions integrating HGS training with cognitive exercises should be developed to further assess the potential synergistic benefits in cognitive and physical function among older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06466-8.Keywords: Cognitive function, Physical function, Handgrip strength(HGS), Gait speed, Cross-lagged panel models

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of dementia among China’s aging population is a pressing concern [1]. Dementia not only diminishes the quality of life for patients but also places a considerable burden on their families and society [2]. Currently, no effective treatment exists for dementia, which underscores the urgency of identifying at-risk individuals early and initiating timely preventive interventions to delay or avert cognitive decline. Studies have indicated that cognitive decline and physical function deterioration often occur simultaneously in older adults, suggesting a possible interaction between these two factors [3]. Because early-stage cognitive decline [4] and physical function decline [5] are both partly reversible, observing the association and chronology between the two is important in establishing interventions to promote health in older adults.

Emerging evidence indicates that handgrip strength (HGS) and gait speed, both reliable markers of overall physical health [6, 7], are independently associated with global cognitive function [8, 9]; however, the magnitude and temporal order of the associations among HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function remain unclear. In recent years, cognitive decline has been shown to be a prerequisite for decline in HGS or gait speed [10, 11]. Some studies have further shown a bidirectional association between HGS or gait speed and cognitive function [12, 13], while others have reported that the bidirectional association between the two and cognitive function does not hold true and is a unidirectional association [14–16]. As can be seen, the directionality of the association between HGS, gait speed and cognitive function is debated. This could be because both HGS and gait speed are markers of physical function and have a link [17, 18], resulting in a confusing effect when studying the association between a single factor like HGS or gait speed and cognitive function. However, few studies examined the directionality of the link between HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function in models. Given that existing research has emphasized the complex interactions and potential causality between HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function, further exploration of the associations and temporal sequences among the three is essential.

Cross-lagged path modeling is an analytical method that uses data from two or more panels in a longitudinal cohort to explore temporal relationships between interrelated variables [19]. Previous studies have utilized this approach to explore bidirectional associations between two variables to better understand how these variables affect each other over time [20, 21]. Recently, notable progress has been made in the sophistication of cross-lagged path models, particularly in their ability to analyze complex interactions among three variables [22]. Based on the same model, this study employs a cross-lagged path model to determine the magnitude and temporal order of the associations among HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function. The goal is to gain a deeper understanding of the intricate relationships between physical function indicators and cognitive function, and to provide valuable insights for formulating preventive and intervention strategies for the decline of physical function and cognitive ability. The study used a longitudinal research design and was conducted in a rural community in Guizhou Province, China.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a cohort study. The data originated from Guizhou Rural Older Adults’ Health Study(HSRO), which was previously described elsewhere [23]. Multi-stage cluster sampling was carried out in twelve selected villages, as detailed in Appendix Figure S1. The baseline survey was conducted in 2019, with a follow-up survey in 2022. Community-dwelling individuals aged 60 years or older participated in this survey. Participants were excluded if they had a serious mental or physical illness (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease) that prevented them from completing the survey. A total of 1795 older adults participated in the study, of whom 819 were ultimately included in the study analyses (see Appendix Figure S2 for details). All participants provided their informed consent, and the study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University.

Assessment of cognitive function

The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was used to assess the cognitive function of older adults. The MMSE consists of 11 items, and its results can quickly reflect the overall cognitive status of the respondents. The scale has a score range of 0–30, with lower scores indicating poorer cognitive function [24].

Measurement of HGS

HGS was measured using the electronic hand dynamometer, which was calibrated prior to use. The subjects were asked to assume a standing position with their elbows extended for the measurement. Three HGS measurements were taken for each subject, and the maximum value of the three measurements was recorded as the final HGS value (in kilograms, kg) to be included in the analysis.

Measurement of gait speed

The subjects were instructed to start walking from rest at their normal pace over a distance of 4.6 m. Then, timing commenced when the subjects’ toes crossed the starting line of the test area and ended when their toes crossed the finish line. A PC220 stopwatch was used to record the walking time (in seconds). Each subject walked twice, and the average of the two walking times was calculated. The gait speed was then computed using the formula: Gait speed (in meters per second) = 4.6 m/average walking time (in seconds).

Covariates

The following covariates were extracted from the baseline and follow-up surveys and were included in the linear mixed-effects models for analysis: age, gender (male or female), educational level (illiterate and semi-literate, primary and above), marital status (married or non-marital, where unmarried, separated, widowed, and cohabiting individuals were classified as non-marital), and annual household income (CNY/year: <10,000 yuan, 10,000–20,000 yuan, and ≥ 30,000 yuan). Smoking status was divided into two categories: no (including former smokers and never smokers) and yes (current smokers). Drinking status was similarly divided into no (including former drinkers and never drinkers) and yes (current drinkers). Physical exercise time was categorized as 0 min, 1–30 min, or > 30 min per day. Self-reported chronic diseases (yes or no) included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, cardiovascular diseases, respiratory diseases, and digestive diseases, among others. Subjective health was assessed on a five-point scale (very poor, poor, fair, good, or very good). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (in kilograms, kg) divided by height squared (in meters squared, m2). The Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale was used to assess the study participants’ abilities in daily living. Face-to-face interviews conducted by qualified investigators yielded the aforementioned data, with the exception of height and weight. Trained investigators used standardized procedures and equipment to measure the participants’ height and weight.

Statistical methods

Since the quantitative data in this study did not follow a normal distribution, we used median and interquartile range to describe the data. Categorical variables were described using frequency counts and composition ratios. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the 2019 and 2022 study subjects’ characteristics, HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function for statistical differences.

Cross-lagged panel models were used to analyse the associations of baseline HGS and gait speed with subsequent cognitive function, and the associations of baseline cognitive function with subsequent HGS and gait speed, to clarify the temporal sequence of associations among the three. In addition, we combined HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function two by two and discussed their interrelationships in three separate models to verify the robustness of the results.

We further analyzed the longitudinal associations among HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function using linear mixed-effects models, adjusting for possible covariates. Linear mixed-effects models could effectively handle unbalanced missing data in longitudinal studies through maximum likelihood estimation, and by random intercepts to account for the inherent differences in baseline cognitive or physical functions among individuals, thus enhancing the accuracy of parameter estimation [25]. In addition, we validated the longitudinal associations between the three, using cognitive function as the independent variable and HGS or gait speed as the dependent variable, and visualized the two-by-two interaction between the three based on the results of a linear mixed-effects models. Analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.3.0.The cross-lagged path model was constructed using the ‘lavaan’ package in R 4.3.0 software, with χ2/df < 5, CFI >0.90, and SRMR < 0.05, which indicates a good model fit [26]. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 819 older adults aged 60 years or older with a baseline median age of 70.0 (66.0, 75.0) years were included in this study; 328 (40.0%) were male and 491 (60.0%) were female. The majority of the participants’ educational level was at the illiterate and semi-illiterate level, accounting for 76.6%; the largest proportion of people (63.7%) were married; the percentage of people who smoked and drank was low (25.0% and 23.3%, respectively); the largest proportion (54.2%) engaged in physical activity of 0 min per day. The percentage of people with chronic diseases was 79.5%; the largest group of people with no exercise at all was 54.2%; the median BMI and ADL were 22.5 and 15.0, respectively; Details are provided in Table 1.

Cross-lagged path model of HGS, gait speed and cognitive function

The results of the cross-lagged path model showed that HGS in 2019 had a significant association with cognitive function in 2022 (β = 0.057, P < 0.05). Meanwhile, an association was observed from 2019 MMSE to 2022 HGS (β = 0.105, P < 0.001). Cognitive function and HGS measured in 2019 had a significant association with gait speed in 2022, with respective β values of 0.078 and 0.071 (P < 0.05 for both). However, gait speed in 2019 was not significantly associated with either cognitive function or HGS in 2022 (both P > 0.05). In addition, the autoregressive paths (from 2019 to 2022) of HGS (β = 0.547, P < 0.001), gait speed (β = 0.231, P < 0.001), and cognitive function (β = 0.629, P < 0.001) were significant, as shown in Fig. 1. Furthermore, this study broke down the trivariate cross-lagged path model to examine the interactions between HGS and cognitive function, gait speed and cognitive function, and the interplay between gait speed and HGS. The outcomes of these detailed analyses were consistent with the findings depicted in Fig. 1, see Figures S3 to S5 in the Appendix. The results indicated that all models were saturated, with a χ2/df = 0.00, SRMR = 0.00 and CFI = 1.00, indicating that the model fits the data well. Note that the majority of subjects were healthy participants, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to a broader group of older adults.

Longitudinal associations of HGS, gait speed on cognitive function

Table 3 showed the results of the linear mixed-effects models. Without adjusting any variables, the HGS (β = 0.236, P < 0.001) and gait speed (β = 0.156, P < 0.001)were positively correlated with cognitive function in the study population. After adjusting for the variables of age, gender, educational level, and marital status, etc., HGS was still significantly correlated with cognitive function (β = 0.090, P < 0.001), whereas gait speed (β = 0.035, P = 0.176) was not related to cognitive function. The difference in the interaction terms of HGS and gait speed before (β = 0.012, P = 0.575) and after (β = −0.025, P = 0.176) adjusting for relevant variables indicated that the interaction term of HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function scores was not statistically significant. Discussion

The current study, which simultaneously examined the relationship among HGS, gait speed, and cognitive function in the same model, revealed a bidirectional relationship between HGS and cognitive performance, meaning that baseline HGS correlated with subsequent cognitive function and vice versa. A significant association existed between baseline HGS and gait speed. Furthermore, both HGS and cognitive function at the baseline were associated with gait speed at the follow-up, but gait speed did not predict cognitive function or HGS at the follow-up. Linear mixed-effects modeling analyses confirmed longitudinal associations between HGS and cognitive function each other, and the association of gait speed and HGS on cognitive function did not interact. To our knowledge, this is the first study to simultaneously combine HGS and gait speed to explore the magnitude and direction of the interaction with cognitive function, providing a scientific evidence base for health interventions for older adults.

The results of the bidirectional association between HGS and cognitive function are consistent with the results of a longitudinal study in Korea [12]. Although the precise mechanism underlying the relationship between HGS and cognitive function is still unknown, some research suggests that cognitive function and muscle may be related to the same brain networks and regions [27]. The cerebral cortex and hippocampus, which support cognitive function and act as a “messenger” of cognitive decline, are linked to the cerebellum, which represents physically demanding tasks like HGS [28, 29]. Additionally, exercise reduces the risk of cognitive decline by increasing the size of the prefrontal and hippocampal brain areas, and older adults who maintain greater connectivity between the motor cortex (the dominant side) and the cerebellum and prefrontal cortex perform better on HGS tests [30]. Finally, vascular endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, insulin resistance, and oxidative stress can lead to physical decline and cognitive dysfunction [31–34], and evidence from these studies supports a bidirectional association between HGS and cognitive function. Since HGS is a modifiable factor, this suggests that physical exercise as well as strength training (especially muscle strength exercise of the upper extremity) may show health benefits in maintaining cognitive function. In the meantime, older adults should be encouraged to engage in relevant cognitive activities to enhance cognitive reserve to prevent the decline in physical function due to cognitive decline. However, a Dutch study showed that better cognitive function at baseline was associated with a slower decline in HGS, while lower baseline HGS was not associated with accelerated cognitive decline [14], which investigated older adults over the age of 85 years, whereas the present study investigated older adults over the age of 60 years, which may be related to age heterogeneity. Perhaps further research is still needed in the future regarding the bi-directional association between HGS and cognitive function at different ages.

Additionally, the present study showed that baseline cognitive function was significantly associated with subsequent gait speed, but not vice versa, consistent with the findings of a study from the United States [15]. But the results from the ELSA [35] and the CHARLS [13] showed a bidirectional association between cognitive function and gait speed, which may be related to the differences in the choice of the assessment tool, the follow-up duration, the population of the study, and the sample size. It is worth noting that the results of the present study, which included HGS and gait speed in the same model for analysis taking into account their mutual influence, showed a correlation between baseline HGS and subsequent gait speed, which may also be heterogeneous with the results of other studies. Therefore, further studies are needed to validate these findings and to more fully understand the underlying mechanisms.

In addition, the results showed that the interaction of HGS and gait speed on cognitive function was not statistically significant, which is in line with a recent cohort study from the UK Biobank [36]. However, a study from Australia concluded that combining HGS and gait speed had a greater predictive effect on dementia risk [37], which may imply that differences exist in the relationship between HGS and gait speed and cognitive function across different populations or study samples. Moreover, this study used cross-lagged path models and linear mixed-effects models, both of which finding an association between baseline HGS and follow-up cognitive function. However, no longitudinal associations were found between gait speed and cognitive function, suggesting that HGS has a greater association with cognitive function. The findings of this research highlight the significance of HGS as a reliable indicator of an individual’s physical mobility, which is intimately linked to cognitive function, and the necessity of preserving HGS in old age to sustain cognitive function, which may have the ability to identify early cognitive dysfunction prior to the manifestation of clinical symptoms.

Some studies have verified that HGS can be used as a predictor of gait speed [17], which is consistent with the results of our study. From a physiological mechanism perspective, greater HGS often implies better muscle strength and neuromuscular function, which are crucial for maintaining normal walking ability and stable gait speed [38]. Notably, we also revealed the finding that gait speed in baseline does not predict subsequent HGS in the study, which demonstrates the intricacies of the relationship between indices of physical function. A cross-sectional study of older women in Italy found no significant link between HGS and gait speed [39], suggesting that HGS and gait speed may reflect different health problems. Nonetheless, HGS may play a more important role in predicting physical functioning in older adults compared with gait speed, and more in-depth and comprehensive future studies are needed to further validate this.

Given these findings and their implications, acknowledging the limitations of our study is also important. Firstly, even though it was longitudinal, the follow-up period in this study was short, and short-term changes may not be sufficient to capture the development in physical and cognitive function, and further evidence is needed for the complex relationship between the three. Secondly, research has shown that HGS and gait speed are uniquely associated with specific cognitive function domains, such as mental intactness and episodic memory [13, 40]. In this study, we chose to use the MMSE mscale as the primary tool for assessing cognitive function. However, this scale has limitations in measuring specific cognitive function domains. Future research could consider using more comprehensive cognitive testing tools to further explore the interrelationships between HGS, gait speed, and specific cognitive function domains. Thirdly, participants may have different perceptions of usual gait speed, which may introduce measurement bias and result in a less sensitive indicator of gait speed. Additionally, because all participants in this study were older adults living in a rural area of Guizhou Province, China, and the majority of subjects were healthy participants, the findings and interpretations may not accurately reflect other regions, age groups, or populations with different health statuses. Lastly, our results remained significant with a small effect size even after all covariates were added. Therefore, when making decisions or developing interventions based on these results, comprehensively considering the magnitude of effect sizes is recommended to more thoroughly assess their potential implications. Notwithstanding these drawbacks, the study’s findings imply that HGS assessments can be a straightforward marker for spotting cognitive deterioration early on and that they should be further investigated in follow-up research.

Conclusions

In rural older adults, a bidirectional association exists between HGS and cognitive function. The combined effect of gait speed and HGS did not improve the prediction of cognitive function. Future interventions integrating HGS training with cognitive exercises should be developed to further assess the potential synergistic benefits in cognitive and physical function among older adults.
