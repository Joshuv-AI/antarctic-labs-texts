Profile of Bonnie Bartel
Authors: Jennifer Viegas
Published: 2023

“I am attracted by the beauty and complexity of biological systems that have arisen as life has been shaped by evolution,” says Bonnie Bartel, who was elected to the National Academy of Sciences (NAS) in 2016. A professor of biosciences at Rice University, Bartel has used innovative genetic approaches to address fundamental cell biological mysteries in the model organism Arabidopsis thaliana. The work has elucidated regulation and signaling of the plant growth hormone auxin, revealed some of the first known plant microRNAs, and advanced understanding of peroxisome biology. Her Inaugural Article (1) illuminates the interactions between peroxisomes, which catabolize fats, and lipid droplets, which store fats. Because this interorganelle cooperation occurs in nearly all eukaryotic cells, the findings provide insights into human metabolic diseases, agriculture, and biotechnology.

Sibling Scientists

Bartel was raised with two siblings in Goshen, Indiana, where her father was a ceramics artist and art professor at Goshen College. Her mother was a nurse. Bartel says, “My parents encouraged my siblings and me to develop our interests without worrying too much about the practical implications of where those interests might lead.”

Both she and her older brother, David, gravitated to science while they were students at Goshen High School. At Bethel College in Kansas, she was mentored by biology professor Wayne Wiens, with whom she also did research for a year following her graduation in 1983, when she earned a bachelor’s degree in biology with honors. She says, “He was so excited and knowledgeable about such a wide swath of biology—from biochemistry to cell biology to development—it amazes me in retrospect. In spite of his heavy teaching load, he always made time to talk with his students both in and out the classroom, hosting events at his home for biology majors and making each of us feel valued. I continue to try to channel Wayne’s excitement and wonder for biology in my lecture course.”

For graduate studies, she went to the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), joining the lab of biochemist Alexander Varshavsky. She says, “Alex is the most creative biologist I have known, and it was a privilege to be in his lab during the exciting times when the core components of the ubiquitin pathway were first being cloned from yeast and the N-degron pathway of protein degradation was being discovered.” Biologist Daniel Finley, now a professor of cell biology at Harvard University, was then a postdoctoral associate in Varshavsky's lab and took Bartel under his wing. Finley provided guidance and support as Bartel learned how to apply yeast molecular genetics approaches to understanding the ubiquitin system. She earned a PhD in biology in 1990.

Bartel says, “As I was contemplating my postdoctoral options, I was struck by the elegance and possibilities of the Arabidopsis model, which was then emerging as an ideal plant for applying genetic approaches to elucidating biological problems.” Renowned yeast geneticist Gerald Fink had just started an Arabidopsis group in his lab at the Whitehead Institute for Biomedical Research, so she joined the group in 1991 as a postdoctoral associate. She says, “A consummate geneticist, Gerry gave his postdocs remarkably free reign to explore and to develop projects that we could take with us as we established our own labs, but he was always there to ask the key question or provide cogent insights as we pondered our unexpected results or planned our next steps.”

Throughout these formative years, Bartel bounced ideas off her brother, who earned a doctorate in virology from Harvard University and is now a professor of biology at MIT as well as a core member of the Whitehead Institute and an investigator at the Howard Hughes Medical Institute. Bartel says, “We both go where the science leads us (2).”

Auxin Regulation and Signaling

Bartel has a longstanding interest in the plant hormone auxin, which promotes cell expansion, division, and differentiation, regulating developmental events and environmental responses. As a postdoctoral associate with Fink, Bartel identified the enzyme family that releases auxin from precursors and discovered that the endoplasmic reticulum compartmentalizes a distinct auxin production pathway (3).

In 1995, she became an assistant professor of biochemistry and cell biology at Rice University. She advanced to a full professorship in 2005. During her early years at the university, she and her lab members began to generate a wealth of tools, such as mutants, antibodies, and transgenic lines. Using such tools, they isolated novel auxin-signaling mutants and used them to illuminate the intersections of auxin signaling with other hormone pathways and previously unknown facets of auxin signaling (4). They also revealed the importance of different auxin sources for lateral root development, cotyledon expansion, root hair lengthening, and hypocotyl elongation (5).

Codiscovery of Plant MicroRNAs

After the discovery of the first microRNA in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans by developmental biologist Victor Ambros and colleagues in 1993, other groups found that these small RNAs perform essential roles and have homologs in humans. Bartel's brother encouraged her to help him search for microRNAs in plants. Together, they and colleagues discovered the first known plant microRNAs (6) simultaneously with teams led by molecular biologists James Carrington and Xuemei Chen.

A follow-up study determined that regulatory targets of plant microRNAs can be reliably predicted as messenger RNAs with near-perfect complementarity to microRNAs (7).

Bartel and colleagues deciphered functions of several conserved microRNA–mRNA target pairs. For example, they found that miR164 negatively regulates its mRNA targets to regulate organ separation (8) and that miR160 controls Auxin Response Factor 17 (ARF17) to direct auxin responses (9).

Peroxisome Biogenesis and Function

In 2000, Bartel and colleagues discovered that conversion of an auxin precursor to its active form occurs in peroxisomes (10). At the time, most studies on these organelles were carried out in mammalian and yeast systems, so Bartel set out to investigate peroxisomes in plants.

After isolating mutants defective in the biogenesis of peroxisomes (10, 11), Bartel helped reveal unanticipated interdependencies among peroxisome biogenesis factors (12) and novel pathways for degrading peroxisome lumenal proteins during developmentally controlled organelle remodeling (13, 14). She says, “We have uncovered multiple intriguing examples in which Arabidopsis peroxisomes more closely resemble mammalian peroxisomes (12, 15) than do yeast or nematode peroxisomes, suggesting that our studies provide unique insights into human peroxisome biogenesis disorders.” Bartel also exploited the large size of seedling peroxisomes to characterize unanticipated intralumenal vesicles within the organelles (16).

Pexophagy, the selective autophagy of peroxisomes, was long established to occur in yeast and mammals. Bartel’s group showed that this specialized form of autophagy occurs in plants as well (15). In a review published in 2016, she and coauthor Pierce Young explain that pexophagy helps maintain a healthy population of peroxisomes and occurs when a peroxisomal protease (LON2) is dysfunctional (17). Bartel, Young, and colleagues went on to reveal 21 loss-of-function mutations that disrupt six autophagy-related genes, findings that are helping to determine the specific roles of these genes (18).

Bartel and her team continue to illuminate the roles that the protein ubiquitin plays in peroxisome function. She says, “I have been fascinated by the many roles of ubiquitin in cell biology since my graduate research with Alexander Varshavsky when we cloned yeast UBR1 (19), the first gene identified to encode a ubiquitin-protein ligase.” Over the past decade, her lab has uncovered both the roles and structures of several ubiquitination peroxins and peroxisome membrane-localized enzymes (20–22).

Far-Reaching Insights

For Bartel’s IA (1), she and graduate student Melissa Traver discovered that a ubiquitin-protein ligase, MIEL1, which was known to mark nuclear transcription factors for degradation, also plays a role in the partnership between peroxisomes and lipid droplets. Lipid droplets are coated with proteins, including oleosin, to prevent the droplets from coalescing. The researchers designed a forward-genetic screen to uncover mutants in which oleosin was no longer degraded in a timely manner during seed germination.

One set of mutants was defective in MIEL1; Bartel and Traver went on to implicate this ligase in marking oleosin for degradation. Bartel says, “We were surprised to find that although MIEL1 targeted a lipid droplet protein, it localized to peroxisomes. This finding provides a mechanism to promote coat protein degradation specifically in lipid droplets that are next to peroxisomes.” The researchers also found that PIRH2, the human MIEL1 homolog, localized to peroxisomes when expressed in plants, suggesting that PIRH2 may have unexplored targets in humans.

The findings provide insights that may benefit agriculture and the industrial use of plants, such as the development of biofuels and lubricants. They may also help inform research on metabolic diseases, including cancer. PIRH2 helps degrade the protein p53, which is encoded by a gene that has been extensively studied due to its role in tumor suppression. Bartel hopes future research on PIRH2 in human cells or other animal models will determine whether or not the mechanism they identified occurs comparably in these systems. Using Arabidopsis, she is planning to examine how MIEL1's localization changes over time.

Commitment to Mentoring Students

Bartel has received several honors for her research. She was named a fellow of the American Association for the Advancement of Science in 2007 and a fellow of the American Society of Plant Biologists in 2011. In 2013, she was elected as a member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. She is equally proud of her awards for mentoring and education, which include being named a Howard Hughes Medical Institute Professor in 2006 and receiving the 2023 American Society of Plant Biologists' Excellence in Education Award. Bartel has mentored over 25 graduate students and postdoctoral associates and 120 undergraduate researchers, with many serving as coauthors on her published works.

She values the support of her husband, bioorganic chemist Seiichi Matsuda, who is the dean of graduate and postdoctoral studies at Rice University, and daughter Ella, a biologist working on native plant restoration in California. “Ella is our best experiment,” Bartel says. Regarding her husband, she says, “We have been partners since we were undergraduates, and having a partner who is also a scientist has been incredibly beneficial as we have navigated the various stages of academia together. As an added benefit, he is a chemist with interests in plant natural products, and his complementary knowledge has broadened my perspectives as we try to unravel the complexities of plant biochemistry.”
