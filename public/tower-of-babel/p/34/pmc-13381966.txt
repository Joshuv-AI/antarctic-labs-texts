A unified RVE-based mesoscale framework for predicting mechanical performance of conventional cement concrete and recycled aggregate slag-dolomite geopolymer concrete
Authors: P. Jiyad, Ayshath Baadira, Praveen Nagarajan, U. B. Jayadeep, Najeeb Manhanpally, Sudha Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Concrete is inherently heterogeneous but is often modelled as homogeneous at the macroscale for simplicity. Accurately representing this heterogeneity using mesoscopic modelling minimises reliance on extensive laboratory testing, enables efficient parametric studies, and thereby reduces both experimental effort and overall costs in developing sustainable cementitious composites. Mesoscale modelling, which treats concrete as a heterogeneous three-phase system comprising mortar, aggregate, and the interfacial transition zone (ITZ), offers a practical balance between accuracy and computational demand. It can simulate both the mechanical properties and the nonlinear behaviour of concrete, while capturing size effects that significantly influence these properties. It simplifies parametric investigations, deepens understanding of crack initiation and propagation, and facilitates the efficient generation of comprehensive datasets. Despite these advantages, mesoscale models can be computationally intensive due to numerous contact regions and thin ITZ layers. To address this challenge, the present study develops a random-aggregate Representative Volume Element (RVE) framework for simulating uniaxial compressive and tensile strength, as well as biaxial strength, of conventional ordinary Portland cement (OPC) concrete. Parametric studies are conducted to evaluate the effects of RVE size, aggregate shape, and ITZ thickness on the simulation of uniaxial compressive and tensile strength of OPC concrete. The optimal configuration, a 5 cm RVE with circular aggregates and ITZ thickness of 0.1 times the aggregate diameter, reproduced uniaxial compressive and tensile strengths while reducing the number of finite elements by approximately 90% relative to full-scale (15 cm) modelling, which significantly reduces computational time. The framework is validated under biaxial loading, demonstrating its capability to capture complex multiaxial behaviour. It is further extended to simulate the uniaxial compressive response of recycled aggregate geopolymer concrete (RAGPC), advancing low-carbon, circular-economy materials. The results demonstrate that the proposed mesoscopic RVE framework offers an effective, extensible, and computationally efficient approach for simulating the mechanical response of different types of concrete, highlighting its potential as a robust tool for sustainable cementitious composites.

Introduction

Concrete is a fundamental construction material, integral to global infrastructure. Its widespread use arises from its adaptability, strength, and durability, which support diverse applications such as buildings, bridges, tunnels, and dams. Despite its inherently heterogeneous composite nature, concrete is often modelled as homogeneous at the macroscale for simplicity. However, accurately simulating its behaviour under diverse loading and environmental conditions requires explicit consideration of internal heterogeneity and phase interactions among constituent phases1,2.

Experimental investigations such as uniaxial compression, tension, and biaxial loading have traditionally been used to evaluate the mechanical properties of concrete. Although these methods provide valuable insights into strength and fracture behaviour, they are often expensive, time-consuming, and limited in their ability to capture internal mesostructural mechanisms such as microcrack initiation and propagation3. For example, Kupfer et al.4 conducted seminal biaxial loading tests on concrete that remain widely used as benchmarks for numerical model validation, while van Mier5 provided detailed experimental observations of strain localisation and microcrack development under multiaxial stress states. These experimental studies, although foundational, are constrained in their ability to isolate the individual contributions of mesostructural features such as aggregate characteristics, ITZ properties, and phase distribution to the overall mechanical response. Multiscale modelling approaches capture concrete’s heterogeneous behaviour at different scales—nanoscale, microscale, and mesoscale—depending on the required accuracy and computational feasibility. At the mesoscale, concrete is typically represented as a three-phase system comprising coarse aggregates, mortar, and the interfacial transition zone (ITZ). At the microscale, mortar is further subdivided into fine aggregates and cement paste6, whereas nanoscale models consider cement paste as a multiphase system comprising pores, hydrated, and unhydrated cement particles. Although microscale and nanoscale models achieve higher accuracy, they demand substantial computational resources. Mesoscale modelling offers a practical alternative, balancing accuracy and computational efficiency7.

Concrete’s mechanical properties are strongly influenced by its mesostructure, particularly the distribution and properties of aggregates, mortar, and the ITZ. Mesoscale models realistically represent this microstructure and help elucidate failure mechanisms such as microcrack initiation, propagation, and coalescence into macrocracks8. They also facilitate the investigation of parameters such as constituent volume fraction, particle shape, orientation, void content, and stiffness. This, in turn, aids in predicting nonlinear responses, including strength, fracture behaviour, and size effects. Acting as a bridge between macro and micro scales, mesoscale modelling reduces dependence on extensive experimental testing, which is often impractical and costly at full scale. Moreover, such models can generate rich datasets via parametric studies, supporting the development of machine learning models for mix optimisation and performance prediction under varied conditions. Incorporating accurate constitutive behaviour for each phase thus provides a cost-effective and time-efficient means of evaluating mechanical performance.

From an analytical perspective, micromechanics-based homogenisation methods such as the Mori-Tanaka scheme and the self-consistent approach have been employed to estimate the effective elastic properties of concrete by treating it as a matrix-inclusion composite9,10. While these analytical frameworks provide efficient estimates of stiffness and can account for phase volume fractions and inclusion geometry, they are generally limited to the elastic regime and cannot capture progressive damage, strain localisation, or post-peak softening behaviour. Consequently, numerical methods that explicitly represent the mesostructure and employ nonlinear constitutive models remain essential for a comprehensive prediction of concrete mechanical performance.

Several numerical approaches have been proposed to model concrete at the mesoscale, including continuum-based finite element models (FEM), lattice element models, rigid body spring models, and discrete element methods (DEM)3. Lattice and rigid body spring models represent concrete using networks of interconnected elements, enabling effective simulation of crack initiation and propagation11–13. In contrast, DEM treats concrete as an assembly of interacting particles, making it suitable for capturing discontinuous fracture processes14,15. Cohesive zone models have been employed to simulate crack initiation and propagation along predefined interfaces such as the ITZ16–18. More recently, phase-field approaches to fracture have gained attention for mesoscale modelling of concrete, where sharp cracks are approximated using a diffusive damage field, enabling the simulation of complex crack patterns without explicit crack tracking19–22. These approaches have been applied to investigate fracture behaviour in heterogeneous materials, including tensile and compressive fracture19–21, and multiphysics-coupled strength prediction frameworks22. However, phase-field methods generally involve a higher computational cost and require additional fracture-related material parameters that may not be readily available for all concrete systems.

Compared with these approaches, FEM-based mesoscale models with damage plasticity formulations provide continuous stress–strain fields and allow straightforward implementation of nonlinear constitutive models3,23. Moreover, FEM enables explicit representation of aggregates, mortar, and the ITZ as solid elements, while avoiding the need for artificial contact definitions typically required in discrete modelling approaches24,25. As a result, the parameter calibration process is more straightforward, and the computational efficiency is generally higher, making FEM-based mesoscale models well-suited for parametric studies of heterogeneous concrete24–26. Therefore, in the present study, a FEM-based continuum mesoscale modelling approach is employed with the concrete damage plasticity (CDP) model to describe the constitutive behaviour of the mesoscale phases. The CDP model, implemented in the commercial finite element software ABAQUS, provides a well-established framework capable of representing both tensile cracking and compressive crushing in quasi-brittle materials, making it suitable for the uniaxial and biaxial loading conditions considered in this work26–29. Since the focus of the present study is on strength prediction and overall stress–strain response rather than detailed crack path tracking, the CDP model is adopted as the damage model, as it offers an appropriate balance between modelling capability and computational efficiency.

In FEM-based mesoscale models, random aggregate structures (RAS) can be generated using either image-based methods or parameterisation. Image-based methods utilise 2D sections or 3D X-ray computed tomography (XCT) scans of actual specimens, requiring physical casting and complex image processing30–33. In contrast, parameterisation defines aggregate geometry and spatial distribution through algorithms, enabling the generation of multiple statistically equivalent RAS configurations with identical volume fractions but varied orientations and positions30. Due to its flexibility and reduced computational cost, the parameterisation approach is adopted here.

The mesostructure, especially in terms of aggregate size, volume fraction, distribution, and ITZ properties, has a significant impact on strength, stiffness, and durability. While simplified two-phase models (aggregates and mortar) are sometimes used34, inclusion of ITZ as a distinct third phase yields a more realistic representation of concrete heterogeneity35,36. The ITZ, being the weakest and most porous region, often initiates cracking; thus, accurate modelling of its properties is critical37,38. Approaches such as shrinking aggregate particles39 or employing zero-thickness cohesive elements17,18,40 have been used to incorporate ITZ effects while easing meshing constraints. Although cohesive elements simplify mesh generation, explicitly modelling ITZ as a separate phase provides a more accurate representation38. While actual ITZ thickness ranges from 10 to 50 μm41,42, it is commonly modelled at 0.1–1 mm in mesoscale analysis to balance accuracy and computational feasibility41. Alternatively, ITZ thickness can be expressed as a fraction of aggregate size43. In this study, the influence of ITZ-to-aggregate diameter ratios on simulated uniaxial compressive and tensile strengths is evaluated. Another critical aspect is the shape of aggregates. In mesoscale models, aggregate shapes have been represented with varying levels of complexity, including circles and spheres27,44,45, ellipses and ellipsoids46,47, random polygons and polyhedra48,49, and Fourier-based shape descriptors50. Real aggregates are typically irregular; however, explicitly modelling irregular shapes significantly increases computational effort. This study evaluates both circular and elliptical aggregate representations to understand the effect of aggregate shape on simulated mechanical properties and computational efficiency.

Despite providing valuable insights, a key challenge remains: mesoscale modelling is computationally demanding, especially when explicitly incorporating thin ITZ layers. To address this, the present work focuses on identifying an optimal representative volume element (RVE) capable of capturing concrete behaviour accurately and efficiently. By adopting an RVE-based parameterisation approach, the developed model significantly reduces computational time compared to conventional mesoscopic random aggregate models while still capturing strength, stiffness, and damage evolution realistically. The appropriate RVE size depends on factors such as aggregate volume fraction, maximum aggregate size, and loading conditions51,52. In this work, different RVE sizes are assessed to identify a suitable one for simulating uniaxial compressive and tensile strengths and post-peak softening behaviour of ordinary Portland cement (OPC) concrete. The effects of aggregate shape and ITZ thickness are also investigated. The optimal RVE configuration identified from the parametric analyses is subjected to biaxial loading, with simulation results validated against experimental data to demonstrate the model’s ability to replicate multiaxial stress states.

The objective of the present work is to develop a computationally efficient and extensible mesoscale mechanical modelling framework for predicting the mechanical behaviour of heterogeneous concretes. OPC concrete is first used as a benchmark material to develop and validate the proposed RVE-based modelling approach, as extensive experimental data are available for comparison. After validation, the framework is extended to recycled aggregate concrete, a sustainable, low-carbon material system. The presence of recycled aggregates and multiple interfacial transition zones introduces complex mesoscale heterogeneity, making such materials particularly suitable for mesoscale modelling and enabling systematic parametric investigations of their mechanical behaviour. Among emerging sustainable recycled aggregate concrete systems, geopolymer concrete (GPC) incorporating ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), dolomite, and recycled coarse aggregates (RCA) has attracted increasing attention due to its lower environmental impact53. GGBS and dolomite enhance early strength, durability, workability and chemical resistance while reducing CO₂ emissions54–61, and RCA use mitigates natural aggregate depletion and construction waste generation62–66. The combined use of GGBS, dolomite, and RCA supports circular economy objectives, enabling low-carbon concrete with promising performance53. Within this context, recycled aggregate geopolymer concrete (RAGPC) emerges as a particularly promising sustainable solution; however, its mesoscale modelling remains largely unexplored. This work extends a rigorously validated RVE framework, developed for OPC concrete, to simulate the uniaxial compressive response of fully and partially replaced RAGPC. The extension highlights the adaptability of the framework for both conventional and emerging sustainable concretes.

The main contributions of this work are as follows: (i) a systematic parametric framework is developed to identify an optimal RVE configuration for mesoscale concrete modelling by evaluating the effects of RVE size, aggregate shape, and ITZ thickness on simulated mechanical response, addressing the notable scarcity of RVE size convergence studies for quasi-brittle materials such as concrete; (ii) the optimised RVE is employed to simulate biaxial loading behaviour and validated against experimental data, as mesoscale modelling of concrete under biaxial stress states using an RVE-based approach has rarely been reported in the literature; and (iii) the validated framework is applied, for the first time, to predict the uniaxial compressive strength of recycled aggregate geopolymer concrete incorporating GGBS, dolomite, and recycled coarse aggregates, with simulation results validated against experimental data obtained by the co-author, demonstrating the extensibility of the framework from conventional to emerging sustainable concrete systems.

Representative volume element (RVE)

The representative volume element (RVE) is the smallest domain of a heterogeneous material that statistically encapsulates its essential microstructural features. It enables the prediction of macroscopic behaviour through localised simulations. The concept, rigorously defined by Hill67, has since been generalised to heterogeneous materials with non-periodic microstructures. Subsequent studies have shown that RVEs can effectively predict the mechanical properties of composites with irregularly shaped and non-uniformly distributed inclusions68–70. Kanit et al.68 demonstrated that RVEs containing randomly distributed circular and elliptical inclusions can reproduce homogenised properties when appropriate boundary conditions are applied. Similarly, Gitman et al.69 and Drugan and Willis70 showed that strict geometric periodicity is unnecessary, provided the RVE is sufficiently large to achieve statistical representativeness.

These foundational studies support the notion that the strict geometric periodicity initially emphasised by Hill67 is not essential. Instead, it is critical to apply suitable boundary conditions — such as periodic boundary conditions - and ensure that the RVE is large enough to avoid artificial anisotropy71,72. This is particularly important for quasi-brittle materials exhibiting strain softening, where an inappropriate RVE size can lead to localisation effects and spurious results69,73. Development of random aggregate structure (RAS)

This study employs a parameterisation approach to develop mesoscopic RVE models of concrete. The mesostructure is generated using algorithms that parametrically define its geometry. Figure 2 illustrates the schematic framework adopted for the development and application of the unified RVE-based mesoscale modelling approach. Aggregates are modelled using different shapes, which are then positioned within a volume. Although real aggregates are irregular in shape, they can be idealised as circular or elliptical for mesoscale analysis of concrete to reduce the complexity involved in the analysis7,74–76. The mortar phase is represented by the space surrounding the placed aggregates. The initial phase of three-phase RVE modelling involves creating an RAS that closely resembles the actual characteristics of concrete, including the shape, size, and distribution of coarse aggregates. The generated RAS comprises two components: a collection of randomly distributed aggregate particles and the mortar matrix that occupies the space between these aggregates. The generation of the mesoscale models includes the shape generation of aggregates, the determination of the optimal particle size distribution (PSD), the placement of aggregates, the generation of the ITZ, and meshing. In this research, circular and elliptical-shaped aggregates were selected, taking into account the computational resources needed for the generation and analysis of irregular-shaped aggregates.

After the generation of aggregates, ITZ is generated by shrinking the aggregates to obtain the desired thickness of ITZ. The generation of the mortar phase is done by subtracting the volume of the aggregates and ITZ from the total volume of the specimen.

To ensure the proper distribution of aggregates in concrete, it is essential to adhere to the actual concrete mix gradation and volume fraction. This distribution, reflecting the particle size, can be represented through formulas, tables, or graphics. Fuller’s curve, a widely adopted aggregate distribution curve, aligns with the proposed limiting grading curves according to Wriggers and Moftah44. In practical concrete design, Fuller’s curve serves as a standard reference. Attributed to Fuller, this curve is renowned for achieving optimal compaction, density, and strength in concrete while also contributing to acceptable workability and resistance against segregation3. By understanding the proportions of different-sized particles in relation to the maximum size, concrete mix designs can be adjusted to achieve the desired balance of properties. Following the aggregate generation, a “take and place” method is employed for aggregate placement82. Algorithms are developed, and the generation of RAS is achieved using a parameterisation approach in MATLAB. In this approach, particles are systematically positioned in the volume, starting from the largest particle and progressing to the smallest, until the desired volume fraction is achieved. The coordinates for placing particles are determined by a random number generator, and subsequent checks are performed to identify any overlaps or gaps between aggregates. This straightforward and convenient method proves to be both simple and effective. In this study, εr = 0.05 was adopted, ensuring a finite mortar layer between neighbouring aggregates and preventing mesh distortion in the ITZ regions. After successful placement, the aggregate is stored with its original radius ri​. This approach is consistent with established mesoscale modelling practices, where the enlarged radius can be physically interpreted as a thin mortar film surrounding each aggregate44.

Aggregates are permitted to intersect the RVE boundaries, in which case the portions extending beyond the domain are clipped. This avoids the formation of an artificial aggregate-free zone near the edges, ensuring a more uniform spatial distribution of aggregates throughout the RVE domain. The influence of boundary clipping is minimised by the relatively small maximum aggregate size (12.7 mm) compared to the RVE dimension (50 mm).

Constitutive model and boundary conditions

To accurately simulate the mechanical response of concrete, appropriate material constitutive models must be employed in mesoscale modelling. Due to concrete’s quasi-brittle nature as a composite material comprising aggregates, mortar, and the ITZ, each phase exhibits distinct mechanical behaviour and thus requires separate constitutive models. In normal-strength OPC concrete, aggregates can be considered linear elastic, as failure typically initiates in the ITZ and mortar, leaving aggregates largely within the elastic range83. Accordingly, in this study, aggregates are modelled as linear elastic for OPC concrete, while the mortar and ITZ are described using the concrete damage plasticity (CDP) model. However, in the case of geopolymer concrete, the experimental compressive strength used in this study exceeds 70 MPa. At such high strengths, cracks may propagate through the aggregates, not merely around them. Therefore, to capture this behaviour accurately, CDP is also applied to the aggregates in the geopolymer concrete models84.

The CDP model is a constitutive model implemented in Abaqus, introduced by Lee and Fenves29. This versatile model simulates concrete behaviour under various loading conditions by considering both plasticity and damage mechanisms, particularly tensile cracking and compressive crushing. The CDP model is widely used in mesoscale analysis for modelling the constitutive behaviour of the mortar and ITZ in concrete, and its capability to incorporate the dynamic increase factor makes it adaptable for analysing rock, mortar, and ceramics under dynamic loading conditions. Many researchers have used the CDP model as the material model for mortar and the ITZ85–87. The material properties for mortar, aggregates, and the ITZ, as well as the five parameters of the concrete damage plasticity model, including dilation angle, flow potential eccentricity, ratio of biaxial and uniaxial compressive strength (fb/fc), invariant stress ratio, and viscosity parameter, were adopted from88,89, as summarised in Table 1. It is inferred from various literature sources that ITZ properties may range from 0.5 to 0.9 times that of mortar properties42,90–92.

To evaluate the homogenised response of the composite material, numerical simulations of uniaxial compressive and tensile loading were carried out using a displacement-based finite element framework. A controlled vertical displacement was applied incrementally to the top boundary of the RVE, while the bottom boundary was restrained in the vertical direction. To eliminate rigid body motion, the horizontal displacement of a single node at the bottom-left corner was restrained. For all simulations, periodic boundary conditions (PBCs) were applied on opposite faces of the RVE. In displacement-based finite element methods, such nodal displacement constraints ensure periodicity of deformation fields and eliminate artificial boundary effects, thereby yielding consistent homogenised properties72,93,94 Periodic boundary conditions applied to the RVE are given in Eq. (18).18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{v}_{i}}^{l}-{{v}_{j}}^{r}={{v}_{i}}^{a}-{{v}_{i}}^{b},\:i,j=\mathrm{1,2},\dots\:,n$$\end{document}

vil and vjr represent the displacements of nodes i and j located on the opposite edges of the finite element meshes of RVE. The relative displacement between nodes on opposite edges of the mesh is matched to the relative displacements of the master nodes, labelled a and b. PBCs are implemented in ABAQUS using linear multi-point constraint (MPC) equations. Nodes located on opposite faces of the RVE are paired based on their corresponding spatial coordinates, and displacement fluctuations between each paired node are constrained to be equal, ensuring periodic compatibility of the deformation field. This formulation ensures that the displacement difference between paired nodes is governed solely by the applied macroscopic strain through the master nodes. To avoid artificial stiffness, (i) a conforming mesh with identical node distributions is generated on opposite boundary edges, ensuring accurate one-to-one node pairing; (ii) master node displacements are applied incrementally to avoid sudden load application; and (iii) The RVE size is maintained sufficiently larger than the maximum aggregate size (RVE size to maximum aggregate size ratio greater than 3) to minimise boundary effects71,72.

From the simulation output, the homogenised stress-strain curves under both tension and compression are generated based on the computed response. The average vertical stress is determined by dividing the total reaction force at the bottom boundary by the cross-sectional area of the specimen7,42,95,96. Correspondingly, the vertical strain is calculated by dividing the imposed displacement by the initial height of the RVE7,42,95–97. In biaxial loading simulations, the same RVE modelling framework is extended to apply simultaneous loading in two perpendicular directions. PBCs are imposed on both pairs of opposite faces of the RVE to ensure displacement compatibility and enforce microstructural periodicity. Biaxial loading is simulated by incrementally applying prescribed displacements on the top and right edges, representing vertical and horizontal loading, respectively. The bottom edge is restrained in the vertical direction, and the left edge is restrained in the horizontal direction to prevent rigid body motion and support the application of PBCs. In this displacement-based finite element approach, PBCs are enforced by constraining nodal displacements on opposite faces to follow a linear profile, ensuring continuity across boundaries. All simulations employed four-node plane stress elements, and mesh sensitivity studies confirmed that the selected discretisation provided accurate and stable results with reasonable computational cost.

Results and discussions

Prediction of the behaviour of OPC concrete under uniaxial compression

To evaluate the mechanical response of OPC concrete, the developed RVE models were subjected to uniaxial compression. This section focuses on a series of parametric studies aimed at identifying the optimum modelling parameters, including RVE size, aggregate shape, and ITZ thickness. Table 1 summarises the material properties and CDP parameters used for all phases of OPC concrete88,89. Figure 5 shows a typical mesh sensitivity study for a 5 cm RVE with circular aggregates and ITZ thickness equal to 0.1D under uniaxial compression. Three mesh sizes − 0.01 mm, 0.05 mm, and 0.1 mm - were considered, yielding peak compressive stresses of 29.77 MPa, 29.96 MPa, and 30.22 MPa, respectively. Since the differences in stress-strain response were negligible, the coarsest mesh size of 0.1 mm was adopted for computational efficiency. Mesh sensitivity studies were conducted for all RVE sizes, aggregate shapes, and ITZ thicknesses considered in this study. For all configurations with ITZ thickness equal to 0.1D, a mesh size of 0.1 mm was found to be sufficient to achieve converged results. For configurations with ITZ thickness equal to 0.05D, a finer mesh size of 0.08 mm was required due to the reduced minimum ITZ thickness. Accordingly, a mesh size of 0.08 mm was adopted for all simulations involving ITZ thickness of 0.05D, while 0.1 mm was retained for all other configurations. In all cases, the variation in peak stress between successive mesh refinement levels was within 1%, confirming consistent convergence behaviour across all configurations. To account for the random nature of aggregate placement, five statistically independent RAS realisations were generated and simulated for each RVE configuration. The mean stress-strain response of the five realisations was used as the representative result for all subsequent comparisons. Figure 6 shows a typical example for the baseline configuration (5 cm RVE, circular aggregates, ITZ = 0.1D), illustrating the individual stress–strain curves of the five realisations alongside their mean response. The coefficient of variation of peak compressive stress across the five realisations was 1.12%, confirming the statistical stability of the model.

Selection of aggregate shape

Aggregate morphology, particularly shape and spatial distribution, plays a critical role in influencing the mechanical response of concrete in mesoscopic simulations. The degree of stress concentration and load transfer paths is strongly affected by aggregate geometry44. To systematically evaluate this effect, a parametric study was performed to identify the optimal aggregate shape for accurately predicting UCS.

In this study, RVEs with randomly distributed aggregates of different shapes were analysed, focusing on circular and elliptical geometries. Although aggregates in real concrete exhibit irregular angularity, only circular and elliptical shapes were considered to reduce computational complexity while maintaining physical relevance. Five aggregate shapes were investigated: circular particles and elliptical particles with aspect ratios of 1.25, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5. To summarise the parametric study, the optimal RVE configuration was identified based on the following objective criteria: (a) Convergence - the smallest RVE size beyond which the predicted strength shows negligible variation (less than 1%); the UCS varied by only 0.36% between the 5 cm and 7 cm RVEs (Table 2). (b) Accuracy - the selected configuration should predict experimental strengths within an acceptable deviation of less than 10%; the 5 cm RVE with circular aggregates and 0.1D ITZ thickness predicted UCS and UTS with deviations of 7.54% and 9.17%, respectively. (c) Computational efficiency- among configurations satisfying the accuracy criterion, the one with the fewest elements is preferred; the 5 cm RVE requires approximately 457,000 elements compared to 4.50 million for a full-scale 15 cm model, representing a 90% reduction. (d) Robustness - circular aggregates were preferred over elliptical ones due to their geometric isotropy, which eliminates orientation-dependent variability across random realisations. Based on these criteria, a 5 cm RVE with circular aggregates and an ITZ thickness of 0.1D was adopted as the optimised configuration for all subsequent analyses.

Prediction of the behaviour of OPC concrete under biaxial loading

Concrete is frequently subjected to complex loading conditions in civil engineering applications, including biaxial and triaxial stress states. In biaxial loading, loads act simultaneously in two perpendicular directions, making it essential to understand the resulting strength and failure mechanisms. Only a limited number of experimental and numerical studies have investigated concrete under biaxial loading99–102, with most numerical models treating concrete as a homogeneous material. Mesoscopic-level investigations that capture the detailed failure mechanisms and nominal strength of concrete under biaxial loading are still scarce. It is well established that concrete exhibits distinct failure modes, cracking patterns, and macro-mechanical responses under biaxial stresses compared to uniaxial loading102,103. In this study, the developed optimum 5 cm RVE model with circular aggregates, an ITZ thickness of 0.1D, and aggregates distributed according to Fuller’s curve is evaluated under biaxial loading.

In the present simulations, biaxial loading is defined as displacement-controlled loading applied simultaneously to the top and right edges of the RVE model. Three types of loading were considered: compression-compression (C-C), compression-tension (C-T), and tension-tension (T-T). In C-C loading, compressive displacements were applied to both the top and right edges of the specimen. In C-T loading, the top edge was subjected to compressive displacement while the right edge experienced tensile displacement. In T-T loading, tensile displacements were simultaneously applied to both edges. The numerical results were compared with experimental data and the classical biaxial strength envelope proposed by Kupfer et al.4. Consistent with experimental practice, biaxial loadings of equal and unequal magnitudes were applied along orthogonal directions. The resulting strengths were normalised with respect to the uniaxial compressive strength (fc) and expressed as stress ratios (f₁/fc and f₂/fc), where f₁ and f₂ denote the principal stresses as illustrated in Fig. 23. Table 8 summarises the strength ratios obtained under different biaxial loading conditions. Figure 24 displays the numerically generated biaxial strength envelope alongside the experimental envelope proposed by Kupfer et al.4. In the following analysis, all stress values are reported as positive for tension and negative for compression.

These findings demonstrate the model’s effectiveness in capturing complex multiaxial stress states and validate its suitability for studying concrete behaviour under realistic structural loading conditions.

Prediction of the behaviour of recycled aggregate dolomite-based geopolymer concrete

The development of sustainable, low-CO₂ concretes such as recycled aggregate geopolymer concrete (RAGPC) is an area of active research, driven by the need to reduce emissions and promote resource efficiency in construction. While the validated RVE mesoscale framework has proven effective for simulating the mechanical behaviour of OPC concrete, its applicability to sustainable alternatives must be established. Assessing the capability of this model to replicate the mechanical properties of different types of concrete, such as RAGPC, is therefore essential, as it enables reliable prediction of the performance of emerging green concretes. Hence, this study is extended to simulate the uniaxial compressive strength of RAGPC using the developed RVE model. Four-phase mesoscopic RVE models of recycled aggregate geopolymer concrete (RAGPC) were developed to simulate uniaxial compressive strength. The experimental program of Manhanpally et al. (2025)53 was taken as a reference for mix proportions and validation.

Development of four-phase mesoscale models for RAGPC

To perform mesoscopic analysis using the RVE approach on fully and partially replaced RAGPC, two types of coarse aggregates are considered: natural aggregates (NA) and RCA. The geometry of NA and RCA differs due to the adhered residual old mortar on the surface of RCA. NAs are modelled as consisting solely of original aggregate material, whereas RCAs are idealised as a three-phase system comprising the original aggregate, the adhered old mortar, and the associated old ITZ. For modelling simplification, the old mortar and the old ITZ surrounding the RCA are combined into a single equivalent layer referred to as the “old ITZ.” A new ITZ is assumed to form between this old ITZ and the surrounding new mortar. This new ITZ is modelled as a separate phase. A schematic illustration of the RCA geometry is shown in Fig. 28. Accordingly, the developed RVE model comprises four distinct material phases: aggregate, old ITZ, new ITZ, and mortar matrix.

Geometry of a typical RCA with two ITZ layers.

Four mixes were considered: NGPC (0% RCA), RAGPC10 (10% RCA), RAGPC40 (40% RCA), and RAGPC100 (100% RCA) with a total aggregate fraction of 43%. Representative RVE models generated for each mix are shown in Fig. 29. Materials and mix details are taken from a study by Manhanpally et al. (2025)53, which investigated the mechanical properties of RAGPC incorporating ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) and dolomite as the binder system. River sand was used as the fine aggregate, while natural coarse aggregates (NA) were partially or fully replaced with RCA obtained from construction and demolition waste. The detailed mix design and material characteristics are reported in53. Stress-strain curves obtained experimentally for the four mixes are shown in Fig. 30 and later used for validation of the numerical simulations.

Behaviour of RVE of RAGPC under uniaxial compression

Uniaxial compression simulations were performed for NGPC, RAGPC10, RAGPC40, and RAGPC100. Boundary conditions follow those described in earlier sections. The complete set of material properties and corresponding damage parameters used for all four phases is provided in Table 9. Certain parameters in Table 9 were obtained using empirical formulas. For instance, the biaxial compressive strength and the tensile strength were estimated as 1.15 × fc and fc /10, respectively104. The CDP model is used as the constitutive model for all material phases. CDP parameters are calibrated by comparing numerical predictions with experimental data28,105,106. The flow potential eccentricity (e = 0.1), biaxial-to-uniaxial compressive strength ratio (fb0/fc0 = 1.16), and the shape parameter (Kc = 0.667) were adopted uniformly across all NGPC and RAGPC phases from established values widely used for cementitious materials in the literature as these parameters govern the shape of the yield surface and plastic flow potential and are not sensitive to binder chemistry or concrete type27,86,87,89,91,107–111.

Since the viscosity coefficient is the most critical parameter for numerical convergence in CDP simulations108,112, it was calibrated first. The viscosity parameter was selected in relation to the time increment, with the calibration initiated using values less than 15% of the time step, following the recommendations of previous studies108,112,113. Given that values in the range of 0.0005 to 0.01 are most widely reported in the literature108,114–118, the baseline NGPC model was evaluated using viscosity coefficient values of 0.01, 0.005, 0.001, 0.0005, and 0.0001. As shown in Fig. 31, the best agreement with the experimental compressive stress–strain response was achieved at a viscosity coefficient of 0.005, while the dilation angle and mesh size were held constant at 35° and 0.1 mm, respectively, during this stage of calibration.

Fig. 31Calibration of the viscosity coefficient (VC) for the NGPC mesoscale model: comparison of simulated stress-strain responses under uniaxial compression for VC = 0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.005, and 0.01 against the experimental curve.

Calibration of the viscosity coefficient (VC) for the NGPC mesoscale model: comparison of simulated stress-strain responses under uniaxial compression for VC = 0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.005, and 0.01 against the experimental curve.

Following the viscosity coefficient calibration, the dilation angle was subsequently calibrated112. Four values within the recommended range of 30°-45° for cementitious materials108,119,120 were evaluated: ψ = 30°, 35°, 40°, and 45°. The peak compressive stresses obtained for these dilation angles were 70.59, 74.75, 76.21, and 77.12 MPa, respectively, against an experimental peak stress of 72.7 MPa. The influence of the dilation angle on the simulated stress-strain response was found to be relatively small compared to that of the viscosity coefficient, consistent with observations reported in the literature108, with a total variation of only 6.53 MPa across all tested values. Among the four values evaluated, ψ = 35° yielded the closest agreement with the experimental peak stress, with a deviation of only 2.82%, and was therefore selected for all further simulations. This value is also consistent with calibrated dilation angles reported in mesoscale concrete modelling studies26,89,109 and for geopolymer concrete109.

Since the maximum aggregate size and particle size distribution are consistent with those used for OPC concrete in the preceding section, the same optimised RVE size of 5 cm with circular aggregates is adopted for the RAGPC model. The total ITZ thickness of 0.1×Dagg, established through the OPC sensitivity study, is equally divided between the new and old ITZ layers (0.05×Dagg each). This equal-thickness assumption is consistent with the mesoscale modelling approaches of42,121,122 who used equal thickness for both ITZ layers. The old ITZ, representing the deteriorated region between the adhered old mortar and the original RCA surface, is weaker than the new ITZ. Its strength is taken as 0.8 times that of the new ITZ121,123–126. In mesoscale modelling of concrete, ITZ mechanical properties are generally reported in the range of 0.5–0.9 times those of the mortar matrix42,89–92. However, direct determination of the new ITZ properties remains challenging due to the difficulty of isolating this phase experimentally. Therefore, the new ITZ properties were determined through calibration against the experimental stress-strain response under uniaxial compression. New ITZ properties were taken as fractions of the mortar properties, and five ITZ-to-mortar property ratios—0.50, 0.60, 0.67, 0.70, and 0.75—were evaluated while keeping all other model parameters constant (µ = 0.005, ψ = 35°). Figure 32 presents the calibration of new ITZ properties, comparing stress-strain responses under uniaxial compression for NGPC obtained using different ITZ-to-mortar property ratios. The corresponding peak compressive strengths obtained from the mesoscale simulations were 66.27, 67.42, 69.62, 71.60, and 73.90 MPa, respectively, against an experimental value of 68.65 MPa. A clear monotonic increase in peak strength was observed with increasing ITZ-to-mortar property ratio, which is physically consistent with the role of the ITZ as the weakest phase in the composite; a stronger ITZ delays microcrack initiation at the aggregate-mortar interface, allowing the specimen to sustain higher loads before damage localisation. Among the five models, the 0.67 ratio yielded a peak stress of 69.62 MPa, corresponding to a deviation of only 1.41% from the experimental value - the least among all cases considered. Models with lower ratios (0.50 and 0.60) underestimated the experimental strength, as the excessively weak ITZ promoted premature interfacial damage, whereas models with higher ratios (0.70 and 0.75) overestimated the strength by increasingly suppressing ITZ-governed failure. Accordingly, the model with a relative strength of 0.67 was adopted for all subsequent analyses.

The proposed RVE models for NGPC, RAGPC10, RAGPC40, and RAGPC100 predicted the peak compressive stresses with deviations of 2.82%, 1.41%, 2.79%, and 4.29%, respectively, relative to experimental measurements. Similarly, the predicted ultimate strains corresponding to the peak stresses deviated by 3.16%, 6.86%, 4.88%, and 1.15% for NGPC, RAGPC10, RAGPC40, and RAGPC100, respectively.

A consistent reduction in compressive strength is observed with increasing RCA content (Fig. 33), in agreement with experimental results. This decrease is attributed to the inferior mechanical properties of the old ITZ and adhered mortar, which compromise stiffness and load-bearing capacity. The weaker old ITZ acts as a preferential site for crack initiation and propagation, while the adhered mortar increases local porosity and reduces the effective contact between aggregates and the surrounding matrix. Moreover, the difference in mechanical properties between adhered mortar particles and the new mortar creates localised stress concentrations, which further accelerate crack development and strength loss. These mechanisms are consistent with earlier observations on recycled aggregate concretes, where reductions in strength are typically reported as a direct function of RCA content. The variation in strain at peak stress among the mixes displayed a non-monotonic trend, with the highest strain recorded in RAGPC10. The irregular pattern in strain at peak stress arises from the combined effects of RCA distribution and the varying thickness of the old ITZ layer, which locally alters strength and crack propagation paths. Moreover, the interaction between old and new ITZ regions introduces non-uniform stress transfer, leading to non-monotonic strain behaviour across different RCA replacement levels. Overall, the mesoscopic simulations successfully reproduced experimental trends for both compressive strength and strain at peak stress. The results demonstrate the robustness of the developed four-phase RVE framework in simulating the complex influence of RCA and ITZ heterogeneity on the mechanical response of RAGPC, providing insights beyond those achievable through homogenised approaches.

Conclusions

The framework was systematically developed through parametric optimisation using OPC concrete, validated under uniaxial and biaxial loading conditions, and extended to RAGPC incorporating GGBS, dolomite, and recycled coarse aggregates, demonstrating its extensibility from conventional to emerging sustainable cementitious systems. The present work demonstrates that RVE-based mesoscale modelling provides a computationally efficient and generalisable alternative to full-scale mesoscale simulations for both conventional and sustainable concretes. The current framework is limited to two-dimensional analysis under quasi-static loading conditions. Future work could extend the approach to three-dimensional RVE models, triaxial stress states, dynamic or high-strain-rate loading, and other emerging cementitious systems such as fibre-reinforced geopolymer concrete, thereby broadening the applicability of the proposed methodology.
