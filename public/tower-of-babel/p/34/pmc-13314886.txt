Hierarchical deep Q-network for anomaly detection and interpretation in multidimensional time-series physical fitness data
Authors: Xiaoling Zhu, Jingchen Zhu, Rende Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper addresses the critical challenge of achieving both accuracy and interpretability in anomaly detection within complex multidimensional time-series physical fitness data. We design and implement an innovative framework based on a Hierarchical Deep Q-Network (HDQN) that effectively processes and learns the inherent multi-scale and multi-granularity characteristics of physical fitness data. The introduction of a hierarchical structure enables the model to capture subtle patterns at different levels of abstraction, from low-level sensor fluctuations to high-level behavioral patterns. The Deep Q-Network component trains an agent that learns an optimal policy for identifying nuanced and complex anomalous data points or sequences, moving beyond traditional threshold-based methods. Evaluated on a real-world dataset comprising 300 participants monitored over 60 days with 8-dimensional physiological metrics, our HDQN framework achieved superior performance with an F1-score of 0.811, outperforming traditional machine learning methods (Isolation Forest: 0.611, One-Class SVM: 0.575), deep learning approaches (LSTM Autoencoder: 0.703), and non-hierarchical Deep Q-Networks (Flat-DQN: 0.743). The hierarchical architecture inherently supports interpretation of detected anomalies by localizing contributory factors within specific data dimensions or temporal segments. Robustness analysis demonstrated that HDQN maintained stable performance with only 3.82% F1-score degradation under 10% Gaussian noise, significantly better than baseline methods. This amalgamation of hierarchical representation learning and deep reinforcement learning significantly enhances the precision of anomaly detection, provides meaningful explanations for identified deviations, and offers a more comprehensive understanding of an individual's dynamic health status, paving the way for proactive and personalized health interventions in physical education and sports science contexts.Keywords: Hierarchical deep q-network, Anomaly detection, Multidimensional time-series, Physical fitness data, reinforcement learning

Introduction

The proliferation of wearable sensors and smart health devices has led to an unprecedented surge in the volume and complexity of multidimensional time-series physical fitness data. This technological revolution has fundamentally transformed how we monitor, analyze, and understand human physical activity and physiological responses. Modern wearable devices can continuously track diverse physiological and behavioral metrics including heart rate variability, sleep patterns, activity levels, metabolic indicators, electrodermal activity, and skin temperature, generating vast amounts of data that hold immense potential for personalized health monitoring, early disease detection, optimization of training regimes, and enhancement of overall wellness outcomes.

In the context of physical education and sports science, this data-driven approach represents a paradigm shift from traditional observational methods to continuous, objective monitoring systems. Physical educators, coaches, and sports scientists can now access detailed longitudinal data about individual athletes or students, enabling more informed decision-making about training loads, recovery protocols, and performance optimization strategies. However, effectively harnessing this potential requires sophisticated analytical tools capable of identifying deviations from an individual's normal physiological or behavioral baseline—anomalies that could signify emerging health risks, suboptimal lifestyle choices, overtraining syndrome, inadequate recovery, or the onset of an injury.

The challenge of anomaly detection in physical fitness data is particularly acute because such data exhibits several complex characteristics that confound traditional analytical approaches. First, the data is inherently high-dimensional, with multiple physiological signals being recorded simultaneously, each potentially exhibiting different temporal dynamics and interdependencies. Second, there exist complex temporal dependencies where current physiological states depend not only on immediate past states but also on longer-term patterns and trends. Third, significant inter-individual variability means that what constitutes "normal" for one individual may be anomalous for another, necessitating personalized baseline models. Fourth, the presence of subtle, context-dependent anomalies that manifest differently depending on activity type, time of day, or other contextual factors makes detection particularly challenging.

Traditional anomaly detection techniques, often reliant on statistical thresholds or predefined rules, frequently fall short when confronted with these intricacies. Simple threshold-based methods fail to capture the complex, non-linear relationships between multiple physiological variables and cannot adapt to individual baselines or changing conditions over time. Statistical methods like z-score analysis or moving averages may identify obvious outliers but miss subtle patterns that could indicate early warning signs of health issues or performance degradation. Moreover, merely detecting an anomaly is often insufficient for practical application; understanding why a particular data point or sequence is flagged as anomalous—its interpretability—is crucial for generating actionable insights and facilitating informed decision-making by individuals, physical educators, coaches, and healthcare providers.

The interpretability challenge is particularly important in educational and sports contexts where stakeholders need to understand the reasoning behind automated recommendations. A physical education teacher receiving an alert about a student's anomalous activity pattern needs to know whether the anomaly relates to cardiovascular stress, inadequate sleep, or unusual activity levels to provide appropriate guidance. Similarly, a coach monitoring an athlete's training data requires specific information about which physiological systems are showing signs of overload to adjust training protocols effectively. Without interpretability, even highly accurate anomaly detection systems risk being dismissed as "black boxes" that generate alerts without sufficient justification for intervention.

To address these multifaceted challenges, this paper proposes and develops a novel framework for anomaly detection and interpretation in multidimensional time-series physical fitness data, leveraging a Hierarchical Deep Q-Network (HDQN). The HDQN architecture is specifically designed to tackle the complexities inherent in this domain through several innovative features. The hierarchical component allows the model to learn and represent features at multiple levels of abstraction and temporal granularities, mirroring the often-hierarchical nature of physiological processes and human behavior. For instance, anomalies might manifest as subtle shifts in low-level sensor readings (such as minute-by-minute heart rate fluctuations) that aggregate into significant deviations at a higher, more conceptual level (such as overall cardiovascular stress indicators or changes in sleep quality derived from multiple sleep stage metrics).

The Deep Q-Network (DQN), a cornerstone of deep reinforcement learning, is integrated to train an intelligent agent that learns to identify anomalous patterns by interacting with the data environment. In this framework, each detection decision is conceptualized as an action, and feedback—either from expert labels or through self-supervised signals—guides the learning process towards maximizing long-term detection accuracy. This approach allows the system to move beyond static rules and adaptively learn complex, non-linear decision boundaries for anomaly detection that can accommodate individual variability and evolving patterns over time.

Crucially, the hierarchical nature of the HDQN not only aids in capturing complex patterns but also provides a natural pathway for interpreting the detected anomalies. By examining the activations and decision processes at different levels of the hierarchy, the system can offer insights into which specific dimensions (such as heart rate versus sleep quality) or temporal segments (such as specific time periods during the day) contributed most significantly to an anomaly's identification. This capability is paramount for transforming raw anomaly flags into understandable and actionable health information that can inform interventions in physical education, athletic training, and personal health management contexts.

The significance of this work extends beyond technical innovation to practical impact in physical education and sports science. By providing a robust, interpretable anomaly detection system, we enable more proactive health monitoring approaches where potential issues are identified before they manifest as serious problems. For physical educators working with large classes, such systems can help identify students who may be experiencing health challenges or engaging in risky behaviors. For coaches managing athlete training loads, the system can provide early warnings of overtraining or inadequate recovery. For individuals monitoring their own health and fitness, the system offers personalized insights that can motivate behavior change and support wellness goals.

This manuscript details the design, implementation, and comprehensive evaluation of this HDQN-based system. We aim to demonstrate that by synergistically combining hierarchical representation learning with deep reinforcement learning, our framework can achieve superior accuracy in detecting diverse anomalies within multidimensional physical fitness data while simultaneously offering a degree of interpretability that is often lacking in other advanced "black-box" models. The ultimate goal is to provide a robust tool that empowers users, educators, coaches, and clinicians with timely and understandable alerts regarding potential health concerns, thereby fostering a more proactive and personalized approach to health and wellness management in educational and athletic contexts.

Literature review

Deep learning for time-series anomaly detection

Deep learning models have become the cornerstone for time-series anomaly detection due to their ability to automatically learn complex patterns and temporal dependencies from raw data without requiring extensive manual feature engineering. This capability is particularly valuable for physical fitness data where the relationships between different physiological variables and their temporal dynamics are often non-linear and difficult to specify a priori.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) networks, have been widely adopted for their proficiency in modeling sequential data. These architectures maintain internal memory states that allow them to capture long-range dependencies in time series, making them well-suited for detecting anomalies that manifest over extended temporal windows. Recent studies continue to refine these architectures for improved performance in health monitoring applications. Esmaeili et al. (2023) explored hybrid LSTM autoencoders for detecting subtle anomalies in physiological signals, demonstrating enhanced sensitivity over traditional autoencoders by combining the temporal modeling capabilities of LSTMs with the unsupervised learning framework of autoencoders. Their work showed that reconstruction error patterns could effectively identify anomalous physiological states in biomedical sensor data.

Transformers, with their attention mechanisms, have also shown exceptional promise in capturing long-range dependencies in time series while addressing some limitations of RNNs, such as difficulty in parallelization and challenges with very long sequences. Nguyen et al. (2023) introduced a Transformer-based framework for anomaly detection in multivariate wearable sensor data, highlighting its superiority in handling complex inter-sensor correlations through self-attention mechanisms that can dynamically weight the importance of different time steps and sensor channels. This approach is particularly relevant for multidimensional physical fitness data where correlations between different physiological variables (such as heart rate and activity level) are crucial for accurate anomaly detection.

Generative models like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are being adapted to learn the distribution of normal data, thereby identifying anomalies as deviations from this learned distribution. Yang et al. (2022) proposed a novel GAN architecture specifically for robust anomaly detection in noisy human activity data, demonstrating that adversarial training could produce more robust representations of normal behavior patterns that are less sensitive to noise and individual variations. The generator-discriminator framework allows these models to capture complex data distributions that might be difficult to model with simpler approaches.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are also employed, often in conjunction with RNNs, to extract local temporal patterns effectively. CNNs excel at identifying spatial or local temporal patterns through their convolutional filters, while RNNs can then model the longer-term dependencies between these extracted features. This hybrid approach has proven effective in various time-series analysis tasks, including anomaly detection in sensor networks and physiological monitoring systems.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are emerging as a powerful tool when sensor data can be represented as graphs, capturing relational dependencies for anomaly detection. In physical fitness monitoring, different physiological variables can be conceptualized as nodes in a graph with edges representing their correlations, allowing GNNs to model complex interdependencies that might be missed by methods treating each variable independently.

Reinforcement learning for anomaly detection

Reinforcement Learning (RL), particularly Deep Q-Networks (DQN), offers a dynamic approach to anomaly detection by training an agent to make optimal detection decisions through interaction with the data environment. While less common than supervised or unsupervised deep learning methods, RL is gaining traction for its ability to adapt to evolving data distributions and incorporate feedback in a principled manner.

The RL framework for anomaly detection conceptualizes the detection task as a sequential decision-making problem where an agent observes the current state (representing recent data patterns), takes an action (classifying the data as normal or anomalous), and receives a reward based on the correctness of its decision. This formulation naturally accommodates the temporal nature of anomaly detection and allows the agent to learn policies that consider not just immediate accuracy but also long-term performance.

Recent explorations focus on formulating anomaly detection as an RL problem where the agent learns a policy to flag anomalies to maximize a reward function, often related to detection accuracy and timeliness. Yang et al. (2025) investigated the use of DQN for adaptive anomaly thresholding in streaming sensor data, showing improved performance in non-stationary environments where the characteristics of normal and anomalous data evolve over time. Their work demonstrated that RL-based approaches could automatically adjust detection thresholds based on recent performance, addressing a key limitation of static threshold-based methods.

Another line of research by Belhadi et al. (2021) explored multi-agent reinforcement learning for distributed anomaly detection in large-scale sensor networks, which is relevant for comprehensive fitness tracking systems that may involve multiple interconnected devices or sensors. Multi-agent approaches can potentially model the complex interactions between different physiological systems or between multiple individuals in team sports contexts.

The challenge in applying RL to anomaly detection often lies in defining appropriate state representations, action spaces, and reward functions specific to the anomaly detection task in health data. State representations must capture sufficient information about recent data patterns to enable effective decision-making, action spaces must balance detection granularity with computational tractability, and reward functions must appropriately balance true positive detection against false alarm rates while potentially incorporating domain-specific costs and benefits.

Interpretability in anomaly detection

As deep learning models become more complex, their "black-box" nature poses a significant challenge, especially in critical applications like healthcare and education where stakeholders need to understand and trust automated decisions. Consequently, research into interpretable anomaly detection has surged, driven by both regulatory requirements (such as the "right to explanation" in some jurisdictions) and practical needs for actionable insights.

Techniques such as LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) are being adapted to explain predictions from time-series models. These post-hoc explanation methods can be applied to any model to provide insights into which features or time steps contributed most to a particular prediction. Abououf et al. (2023) demonstrated the application of SHAP to identify contributing features for anomalies detected by an LSTM in patient monitoring data, showing that SHAP values could highlight specific physiological variables and time periods that drove anomaly classifications.

Attention mechanisms, inherent in models like Transformers, also offer a degree of interpretability by highlighting salient time steps or features through learned attention weights. Veesam et al. (2025) focused on leveraging attention weights from Transformer models to provide visual explanations for detected anomalies in activity recognition data, demonstrating that attention patterns often corresponded to intuitive explanations of why certain data segments were classified as anomalous.

Developing intrinsically interpretable models, rather than relying solely on post-hoc explanations, is an active area of research. Rudin (2019)) argued compellingly for the use of inherently interpretable models in high-stakes decisions, noting that post-hoc explanations of black-box models may not always accurately reflect the model's true decision-making process and can sometimes be misleading. This perspective has motivated research into model architectures that provide interpretability by design, such as attention-based models or hierarchical structures where different levels correspond to interpretable concepts.

Hierarchical models for complex time-series analysis

Physical fitness data often exhibits hierarchical structures, with low-level sensor readings contributing to higher-level physiological states or activities. For example, individual heart rate measurements contribute to assessments of cardiovascular stress, which in turn relate to overall fitness status. Hierarchical models are well-suited to capture these multi-scale dependencies and have shown promise in various time-series analysis tasks.

Hierarchical RNNs and hierarchical attention networks have been proposed for tasks like activity recognition and health state prediction. Wang et al. (2023) introduced a hierarchical attention network for classifying complex human activities from wearable sensor data, demonstrating improved accuracy by modeling data at different temporal granularities. Their architecture processed data at multiple time scales—from second-level movements to minute-level activities to hour-level behavior patterns—allowing the model to capture both fine-grained details and broader contextual information.

The application of hierarchical principles to deep reinforcement learning, leading to Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL), is also an emerging area. HRL frameworks decompose complex tasks into hierarchies of subtasks, with higher-level policies selecting goals or subgoals and lower-level policies executing actions to achieve those goals. While direct applications of HRL to anomaly detection in fitness data are still nascent, the potential for learning policies at different levels of abstraction (such as detecting anomalies in specific exercises versus overall workout quality, or identifying short-term acute anomalies versus longer-term chronic patterns) is significant.

Recent works also explore hierarchical clustering and its combination with deep learning for pattern discovery in time series. Zamanzadeh Darban et al. (2024) provided a comprehensive survey of deep learning approaches for time-series anomaly detection, noting that hierarchical methods show particular promise for handling the multi-scale nature of many real-world time-series datasets. Some researchers are beginning to explore hierarchical approaches in federated learning for health data, which could be relevant for privacy-preserving anomaly detection across multiple institutions or user populations while maintaining data privacy.

This literature review underscores the rapid advancements in deep learning and RL for time-series analysis, particularly in health monitoring contexts relevant to physical education and sports science. However, the synergistic integration of hierarchical structures with Deep Reinforcement Learning for interpretable anomaly detection in multidimensional physical fitness data remains a less explored yet crucial research avenue. Our work aims to address this gap by developing a Hierarchical Deep Q-Network framework that combines the strengths of hierarchical representation learning, deep reinforcement learning, and interpretability mechanisms to provide accurate, robust, and understandable anomaly detection for physical fitness monitoring applications.

Algorithm introduction

The proposed Hierarchical Deep Q-Network (HDQN) framework is designed to effectively detect and interpret anomalies in multidimensional time-series physical fitness data. It synergistically combines a hierarchical feature extraction module with a Deep Q-Network (DQN) to learn complex patterns and make informed decisions about the anomalous nature of data segments.

Conceptual overview

The core idea underlying our HDQN framework is to first process the input multidimensional time-series data through a hierarchical structure that extracts features at varying levels of abstraction and temporal granularity. This hierarchical processing mirrors the natural organization of physiological and behavioral information, where low-level signals aggregate into higher-level states and patterns.

At the lowest level, the hierarchy might capture raw sensor fluctuations or basic kinematic features—for instance, individual heart rate measurements, step counts per minute, or instantaneous skin temperature readings. At intermediate levels, the model might represent short-term patterns such as heart rate variability over several minutes, activity intensity during a specific exercise bout, or sleep stage transitions. At the highest levels, the hierarchy encodes more complex, aggregated information such as overall cardiovascular stress indicators, daily activity patterns, or multi-day trends in recovery metrics.

These hierarchically processed features then form the state representation for a DQN agent. The agent is trained through reinforcement learning to take actions—specifically, classifying a given state (representing a time window of data) as either normal or anomalous. The reward mechanism guides the agent to learn a policy that maximizes detection accuracy while potentially incorporating other objectives such as minimizing false alarms or prioritizing detection of certain types of anomalies.

Importantly, the hierarchical nature of our framework serves dual purposes. First, it enables more effective learning by providing the DQN with rich, multi-scale representations that capture both fine-grained details and broader contextual patterns. Second, it facilitates post-hoc interpretation by allowing the system to trace which levels or features within the hierarchy contributed most significantly to an anomaly decision, thereby providing insights into the nature and causes of detected anomalies.

The anomaly detection task is framed as a reinforcement learning problem, where an agent learns to make optimal detection decisions through interaction with the data environment.

Conversely, in a general fitness monitoring application where frequent false alarms might lead to user fatigue and disengagement, one might set RFP relatively high to encourage the agent to be more conservative in flagging anomalies.

Anomaly interpretation

Once an anomaly is detected (when the agent selects the 'anomaly' action with high confidence), the hierarchical structure facilitates interpretation of why the data was flagged as anomalous. This interpretability is achieved through several mechanisms:

Feature Activation Analysis: By examining the feature activations ht(l) at different levels l, we can identify which levels of the hierarchy showed the most unusual patterns. For instance, if anomalies are primarily reflected in high-level features but not in low-level features, this suggests a pattern that emerges from aggregation rather than from acute, short-term deviations.

Dimension Attribution: Within each level, we can analyze which input dimensions contributed most to the learned representations using techniques like gradient-based attribution or attention weights (if attention mechanisms are incorporated). This allows us to identify whether an anomaly is driven primarily by cardiovascular metrics, activity levels, sleep patterns, or combinations thereof.

Temporal Localization: By examining how features evolve over the time windows at each level, we can pinpoint specific temporal segments that contributed most to the anomaly detection. This is particularly valuable for identifying when an anomalous pattern began or which specific events or behaviors preceded the anomaly.

Layer-wise Relevance Propagation: More sophisticated interpretation techniques like layer-wise relevance propagation can be applied to trace the contribution of specific input features through the hierarchical structure to the final anomaly decision, providing a detailed decomposition of how raw sensor readings influenced the detection.

These interpretation mechanisms transform the HDQN from a "black-box" detector into a system that can provide actionable insights. Experimental analysis

This section details the experimental setup designed to evaluate the performance of the proposed Hierarchical Deep Q-Network (HDQN) in detecting anomalies within multidimensional time-series physical fitness data. We describe the dataset utilized, the baseline algorithms used for comparison, the experimental results, and comprehensive analyses of our model's performance and robustness.

Dataset description

The experiments were conducted on a real-world physical fitness monitoring dataset comprising 300 anonymous participants recruited from university athletic programs and community fitness centers. This diverse participant pool included individuals engaged in various physical activities ranging from recreational fitness to competitive sports, providing a heterogeneous dataset representative of real-world monitoring scenarios.

Data was continuously collected via wearable sensors over a period of 60 days, with a sampling frequency of once per minute for most metrics. Data preprocessing involved several steps to ensure quality and consistency. First, z-score normalization was applied for each dimension per participant to mitigate inter-individual variability, ensuring that the models learned to detect deviations from individual baselines rather than being confounded by natural differences between individuals. Missing data points (approximately 3.2% of the dataset) were imputed using forward-fill followed by linear interpolation for gaps longer than 5 min. The time-series data was segmented into non-overlapping 60-minute windows to form input sequences for the models, providing sufficient temporal context while maintaining computational tractability.

The entire dataset was partitioned using stratified sampling to maintain balanced representation of different activity types and anomaly rates across splits: 70% for training (210 participants), 15% for validation used for hyperparameter tuning (45 participants), and 15% for final testing (45 participants). This participant-level splitting ensures that the test set represents truly unseen individuals, providing a more realistic evaluation of generalization performance.

Comparison algorithms

To benchmark the performance of the proposed HDQN framework, we compared it against four widely recognized and relevant anomaly detection algorithms representing different methodological approaches:

Isolation Forest (IF): An efficient ensemble-based anomaly detection method that isolates anomalies by randomly selecting features and split values. Anomalies are expected to have shorter path lengths in the tree structure, making them easier to isolate. This method is particularly effective for high-dimensional data and requires minimal assumptions about data distribution.

One-Class SVM (OC-SVM): A classic kernel-based boundary learning algorithm that learns a decision boundary around normal data points in a high-dimensional feature space. Points falling outside this boundary are classified as anomalies. We used a radial basis function (RBF) kernel, which is effective for capturing non-linear patterns.

LSTM Autoencoder (LSTM-AE): A deep learning model that learns to reconstruct normal time-series data through an encoder-decoder architecture. The encoder compresses the input sequence into a latent representation, while the decoder attempts to reconstruct the original sequence. Anomalies typically manifest as high reconstruction errors, as the model trained primarily on normal data struggles to accurately reconstruct anomalous patterns.

Standard Deep Q-Network (Flat-DQN): A non-hierarchical DQN model whose input directly utilizes flattened raw time-series windows or features extracted by a single, non-hierarchical neural network layer. This comparator was specifically chosen to isolate and evaluate the unique contribution of the hierarchical feature extraction module within the HDQN. The Flat-DQN uses the same DQN architecture and training procedure as HDQN but lacks the multi-level hierarchical processing.

All baseline methods were implemented using standard libraries (scikit-learn for IF and OC-SVM, TensorFlow/Keras for LSTM-AE and DQN variants) and their hyperparameters were carefully tuned using the validation set to ensure fair comparison.

Implementation details

The HDQN was configured with L=3 hierarchical levels, balancing model complexity with computational efficiency. The architecture of each level was designed as follows:

Level 1 (Low-level feature extraction): Employed 1D Convolutional Neural Networks with 64 filters of kernel size 5, followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation. This level processes raw input data to extract local temporal patterns and basic features. The receptive field W1=10min.

Level 2 (Intermediate feature aggregation): Utilized a GRU layer with 128 hidden units to capture temporal dependencies in the features extracted by Level 1. This level aggregates short-term patterns into intermediate representations. The receptive field W2=30min.

Level 3 (High-level pattern recognition): Employed another GRU layer with 64 hidden units to model longer-term trends and high-level behavioral patterns from the intermediate features. The receptive field W3=60min.

The DQN component utilized a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with 3 hidden layers (256, 128, 64 units respectively) with ReLU activations, taking the Level 3 output as input and producing Q-values for the two actions (normal, anomaly).

Training hyperparameters were set as follows: learning rate αDQN=1×10−4, discount factor γ=0.95, experience replay buffer size = 50,000 experiences, batch size = 32, target network update frequency = 1000 steps. The reward structure was set as RTP=+10, RTN=+1, RFP=−5, RFN=−15, reflecting the higher cost of missing true anomalies (false negatives) in health monitoring contexts.

Results and discussion

All models were trained and evaluated on the described real-world physical fitness dataset. Training was conducted on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs, with training times ranging from approximately 2 h for simpler models (IF, OC-SVM) to 18 h for the HDQN. Evaluation metrics included Precision, Recall, F1-Score, and Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC-ROC), providing a comprehensive assessment of detection performance.

Anomaly detection metric comparison

The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed HDQN framework achieved the best performance across all evaluation metrics, as shown in Fig. 2.

HDQN achieves the highest performance across all metrics, demonstrating the effectiveness of hierarchical feature extraction combined with deep reinforcement learning for anomaly detection in physical fitness data.

The HDQN achieved an F1-score of 0.811, Precision of 0.823, Recall of 0.799, and AUC-ROC of 0.876, representing substantial improvements over all baseline methods.

Compared to traditional machine learning methods, HDQN's advantage is significant. Isolation Forest achieved an F1-score of 0.611 (Precision: 0.634, Recall: 0.589, AUC-ROC: 0.721), while One-Class SVM achieved 0.575 (Precision: 0.601, Recall: 0.551, AUC-ROC: 0.698). These results highlight the limitations of traditional methods in capturing the complex, non-linear patterns inherent in multidimensional time-series physical fitness data. While these methods can identify obvious outliers, they struggle with subtle, context-dependent anomalies that require understanding of temporal dependencies and multi-dimensional relationships.

The LSTM Autoencoder, representing unsupervised deep learning approaches, achieved an F1-score of 0.703 (Precision: 0.718, Recall: 0.689, AUC-ROC: 0.789). While substantially better than traditional methods, the LSTM-AE's reliance on reconstruction error as the sole indicator of anomalies has limitations. Some anomalies may be well-reconstructed if they follow patterns the model has seen during training, while some normal but rare patterns may produce high reconstruction errors. Additionally, determining appropriate reconstruction error thresholds for anomaly classification can be challenging and may not generalize well across different individuals or contexts.

The Flat-DQN achieved an F1-score of 0.743 (Precision: 0.756, Recall: 0.731, AUC-ROC: 0.821), demonstrating that the reinforcement learning approach to anomaly detection is effective. However, the significant performance gap between Flat-DQN and HDQN (F1-score improvement from 0.743 to 0.811, representing a 9.2% relative improvement) strongly validates the value of the hierarchical feature extraction module.

This improvement can be attributed to several factors. First, real-world fitness data contains multi-scale, multi-granularity information where anomalous patterns can manifest at different temporal scales—from acute, short-term deviations to gradual, long-term trends. The hierarchical structure allows HDQN to capture patterns at all these scales simultaneously. Second, anomalies in physiological data are often subtle and highly context-dependent, requiring understanding of how low-level signals aggregate into higher-level states. HDQN's progressive abstraction from raw data through multiple levels enables it to construct more comprehensive and robust representations of "normalcy."

Third, the hierarchical architecture provides a form of implicit regularization, where each level learns representations constrained by the previous level's output, potentially reducing overfitting and improving generalization. Fourth, by processing information at multiple levels, HDQN can distinguish between noise or transient fluctuations at low levels and genuine anomalous patterns that persist across multiple levels of abstraction.

The higher Precision achieved by HDQN (0.823 vs. 0.756 for Flat-DQN) signifies its ability to better control false alarm rates, which is critical for practical health monitoring applications where excessive false alarms can lead to user fatigue and disengagement. The improved Recall (0.799 vs. 0.731) indicates that HDQN successfully identifies a larger proportion of true anomalies, enabling more timely interventions for potential health risks.

This robustness is important for practical deployment where exact optimal parameters may not be known a priori.

Results showed that after introducing 10% Gaussian noise, all methods experienced performance degradation, but to varying degrees. The HDQN method demonstrated the best robustness, with its F1-Score dropping by only 3.82%, significantly better than all baseline methods. In contrast, the other methods experienced more significant drops in their F1-Scores. For example, the One-Class SVM saw a drop of 13.04%, and the Isolation Forest dropped by 10.97%. This superior noise resilience can be attributed to several factors inherent in the hierarchical architecture:

First, the hierarchical structure provides a form of implicit denoising, where noise at lower levels is progressively filtered out as information is aggregated to higher levels. Random noise typically does not exhibit consistent patterns across multiple temporal scales, so the multi-scale processing helps distinguish genuine anomalous patterns from noise-induced fluctuations.

Second, the temporal aggregation performed by GRU layers at higher levels of the hierarchy naturally smooths out random fluctuations while preserving meaningful temporal patterns. This temporal smoothing is more sophisticated than simple moving averages, as the GRU learns which patterns to preserve and which to filter based on their relevance for anomaly detection.

Third, the reinforcement learning framework may contribute to robustness by learning decision policies that are inherently more stable, as the agent is trained to maximize long-term cumulative reward rather than making decisions based on individual noisy observations.

The strong robustness demonstrated by HDQN is particularly important for real-world applications where sensor data inevitably contains noise from various sources including sensor imprecision, motion artifacts, environmental interference, and transmission errors. A model that maintains high performance under noisy conditions is more reliable and practical for deployment in real-world physical education and sports monitoring contexts.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a Hierarchical Deep Q-Network (HDQN) framework for anomaly detection and interpretation in multidimensional time-series physical fitness data, addressing critical challenges in accuracy, robustness, and interpretability that limit existing approaches. Our framework leverages a hierarchical feature extraction module combining 1D CNNs for low-level pattern recognition and GRUs for higher-level temporal aggregation, integrated with a Deep Q-Network that learns an optimal policy for distinguishing between normal and anomalous patterns through reinforcement learning.

The experimental evaluation, conducted on a real-world dataset comprising 300 participants' physical fitness data collected over 60 days, demonstrated the superiority of the HDQN framework over several baseline methods. The HDQN achieved an F1-score of 0.811, substantially outperforming traditional machine learning algorithms (Isolation Forest: 0.611, One-Class SVM: 0.575), deep learning approaches (LSTM Autoencoder: 0.703), and non-hierarchical Deep Q-Networks (Flat-DQN: 0.743). This represents a 9.2% relative improvement over Flat-DQN, validating the critical contribution of hierarchical feature extraction in capturing the multi-scale, multi-granularity characteristics inherent in physical fitness data.

A key finding was that the hierarchical architecture enables the model to progressively abstract information from raw sensor readings through multiple levels—from low-level instantaneous fluctuations to high-level behavioral patterns and physiological state trends. This multi-scale representation allows HDQN to detect complex anomalies that require understanding of multiple information layers, such as subtle early warning signs of overtraining, context-specific behavioral anomalies, or patterns indicating emerging health risks that might be missed by methods operating at a single scale.

The robustness analysis provided further evidence of HDQN's practical value. Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis showed that the model maintains stable performance around its optimal configuration (L=3 hierarchical levels, αDQN=1×10−4 learning rate, W=60min window size), suggesting that the framework can be effectively deployed without extensive tuning for each new application. Noise resilience experiments demonstrated that HDQN maintains high performance even when test data is corrupted with 10% Gaussian noise, with only 3.82% F1-score degradation compared to 6.99–13.04% for baseline methods. This robustness is attributed to the hierarchical structure's implicit denoising properties and the temporal aggregation performed by recurrent layers.

Beyond detection accuracy, the hierarchical architecture inherently supports interpretation of detected anomalies. By examining feature activations at different levels of the hierarchy, the system can localize which specific data dimensions or temporal segments contributed most significantly to an anomaly decision. This interpretability is crucial for practical applications in physical education and sports science, where stakeholders need to understand not just that an anomaly occurred, but why it was flagged and what specific aspects of an individual's physiology or behavior are concerning.

The implications of this work extend across multiple domains within physical education and sports science. For physical educators working with large classes, HDQN-based monitoring systems can help identify students experiencing health challenges, inadequate recovery, or engagement in risky behaviors, enabling timely interventions. For coaches managing athlete training loads, the system provides early warnings of overtraining, inadequate recovery, or injury risk, supporting more effective periodization and individualized training adjustments. For individuals monitoring their own health and fitness, the system offers personalized insights that can motivate behavior change and support wellness goals with explanations that build understanding and trust.

Future work could explore several promising directions. First, integrating attention mechanisms within the hierarchical structure could further enhance both performance and interpretability by explicitly modeling which features and time steps are most salient for each decision. Second, investigating methods for improved interpretability, such as generating natural language explanations of detected anomalies based on the hierarchical feature analysis, would make the system more accessible to non-technical users (Li & Feng, 2025). Third, expanding the evaluation to diverse and larger datasets, potentially including different types of physiological signals (such as blood pressure, blood glucose, or advanced biomechanical metrics) and different populations (such as clinical patients or elderly individuals), would help establish the generalizability of the approach.

Fourth, exploring online learning capabilities where the model continuously adapts to each individual's evolving baseline as more data is collected could improve personalization and long-term accuracy. Fifth, investigating federated learning approaches where models are trained across multiple institutions or user populations while preserving privacy could enable the development of more robust and generalizable models without compromising sensitive health data. Finally, conducting prospective clinical studies to evaluate the real-world impact of HDQN-based monitoring systems on health outcomes, injury prevention, and training effectiveness would provide crucial evidence for clinical adoption.

In conclusion, the Hierarchical Deep Q-Network framework represents a significant advancement in anomaly detection for multidimensional time-series physical fitness data, offering superior accuracy, robustness, and interpretability compared to existing approaches. By synergistically combining hierarchical representation learning with deep reinforcement learning, our framework provides a powerful tool for proactive health monitoring and personalized interventions in physical education, athletic training, and wellness management contexts. The demonstrated ability to accurately detect diverse anomalies while providing meaningful explanations positions this technology as a valuable asset for empowering individuals, educators, coaches, and clinicians in their efforts to optimize health, performance, and well-being.
