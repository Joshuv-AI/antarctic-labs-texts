The associations of suicidal ideation with psychopathology and inflammatory cytokines in patients with chronic schizophrenia
Authors: Lewei Liu, Lili Zhao, Zihe Xiao, Liling Sun, Haojie Fan, Mingru Hao, Xin Zhao, Jiawei Wang, Yinghan Tian, Xianhu Yao, Wenzheng Li, Lei Xia, Huanzhong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicidal ideation (SI) is common in patients with chronic schizophrenia, but the exact mechanisms underlying its development are unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to initially assess the prevalence of SI and to thoroughly explore the potential associations between SI and general demographic factors, psychopathological characteristics, and inflammatory cytokines in patients with chronic schizophrenia.MethodsFrom May to December 2018, 302 patients with chronic schizophrenia were included in this study. A self-administered questionnaire was used to collect general demographic data, and a series of scales were used to assess SI, psychotic symptoms, depression, insomnia, involuntary movements, extrapyramidal side effects, and akathisia symptoms, respectively. Additionally, plasma levels of inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-17 A and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were measured. By logarithmic transformation with a base of 10, the values of Log IL-1β, Log IL-6, Log IL-17 A, and Log TNF-α were yielded. Among these, SI was designated as the dependent variable, with other psychopathological symptoms and inflammatory cytokines serving as independent variables, while general demographic factors were controlled for as potential confounders. Finally, multifactorial logistic stepwise regression analyses were performed to identify independent factors influencing SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were then used to assess the predictive value of each identified independent factor for SI.ResultsThe prevalence of SI (lifetime) and SI (last week) in patients with chronic schizophrenia was 36.4% and 8.0%, respectively. After controlling for general demographic factors such as age, Body Mass Index (BMI), and years of education, regression analyses showed that being female, Calgary Depression Scale (CDSS) score and Log IL-1β were independent correlates of patients’ SI (lifetime), while chlorpromazine equivalents, Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) score, CDSS score and Log IL-6 were independent correlates of SI (last week). Additionally, ROC curve analyses showed that the combination of both CDSS score and Log IL-1β items demonstrated better discriminative ability of SI (lifetime). And the four-item combination of chlorpromazine equivalents, PANSS score, CDSS score and Log IL-6 was a better predictor of SI (last week).ConclusionThere was higher overall risk of SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia. SI might be associated with psychotic symptoms, depression, insomnia, medication side effects, and increased levels of inflammatory cytokines. In clinical practice, doctors should take prompt preventive measures in patients combining suicidal risk factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07263-8.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe mental disorder characterized by cognitive and behavioral abnormalities. Based on the latest epidemiological survey in China, the lifetime prevalence of schizophrenia was 0.7% [1]. Schizophrenia is strongly associated with premature death. Compared to the healthy population, the life expectancy of patients with schizophrenia is reduced by 10 to 25 years [2]. Among many causes, suicide is considered to be one of the leading causes to death [3]. A recent meta-analysis confirmed that the risk of suicide mortality was 9.7 times higher in patients with schizophrenia than in the healthy population [4]. Suicidal behaviors are often viewed as a continuum with multiple stages, beginning with suicidal ideation (SI) as the earliest stage, which may progress to suicide planning, then to a suicide attempt, and ultimately to completed suicide [5]. Hence, SI is a critical indicator for understanding suicide in individuals. A meta-analysis showed that the risk of future suicide was 5.8 times higher in patients with schizophrenia accompanied by SI [6]. Therefore, there is a strong need to know the prevalence of SI and the risk factors in patients with schizophrenia.

Firstly, suicide might be associated with socio-demographic factors such as sex, age, education, physical illnesses, and so on. For example, a follow-up study of patients with schizophrenia in China showed that male patients were younger and had significantly higher rates of mortality, suicide, and homelessness compared to female patients [7]. Guo et al. found that the presence and number of chronic physical illnesses were significantly associated with individual elevated risk of SI [8]. In summary, these socio-demographic factors may be intertwined and collectively affect patients’ mental health and social functioning, thereby increasing their risk of suicide.

Additionally, numerous psychopathological symptoms are also key triggers for suicide. First of all, psychotic symptoms may directly contribute to the development of patients’ suicidal behaviors. Both Ding et al. and Dongxia et al. found a positive correlation between the suicide risk of patients with schizophrenia and the severity of their psychotic symptoms [9, 10]. Depressive symptoms are also one of the important risk factors for patients with schizophrenia. For instance, Upthegrove et al. noted that depressive symptoms were the most significant factor contributing to completed suicide in patients with schizophrenia, even more so than command hallucinations [11]. By analyzing data from a naturalistic clinical cohort, Kim et al. found that the more severe the depressive symptoms, the higher the risk of SI in patients with schizophrenia [12]. Next, insomnia is also a common and dangerous comorbidity in schizophrenia [13], with the prevalence of approximately 18% − 44% [14–16], and is often associated with a poor prognosis of illness. Previous studies showed that insomnia was significantly associated with SI in various psychiatric disorders [17]. Thus, whether insomnia increases the risk of suicide in patients with schizophrenia has also received attention from numerous researchers. For example, Miller et al. found that current insomnia symptoms could increase SI by 2.5 times in patients with schizophrenia after controlling for confounders [14]. In conclusion, the importance of psychopathological symptoms should be emphasized when discussing SI in patients with schizophrenia.

However, it is not enough to explain suicidal behaviors merely based on clinical symptoms, and these behaviors also involve intricate biological mechanisms. Of these, inflammatory factors have been the focus of many studies in recent years. Evidence suggests that inflammatory responses may be the important cause of the onset of schizophrenia [18, 19]. For example, a cross-sectional study found that thiol-disulphide parameters could serve as biomarkers for acute exacerbations of schizophrenia [20]. Moreover, beyond their involvement in disease onset, altered cytokines levels resulting from inflammatory responses have also been linked to suicidal behaviors. Currently, the associations between suicide and inflammatory cytokines have been preliminarily validated in different populations [21–23]. However, relevant studies in patients with schizophrenia were limited and controversial. Hoprekstad et al. found that inflammation was associated with suicide risk in patients with psychiatric spectrum disorders in a multicenter longitudinal study [24]. In contrast, by comparing patients with schizophrenia who died by suicide with those who died by non-suicide, Pandey et al. found that the levels of inflammatory cytokines in the brain tissues of the two groups did not show significant differences [25]. Therefore, it is necessary to further investigate the association between SI and inflammatory cytokines in patients with schizophrenia.

Although there were preliminary explorations on the current investigation and mechanisms of suicidal behaviors in patients with schizophrenia, the following limitations exist: (1) the number of related studies was high, but the findings on the associations between SI and clinical factors were not yet consistent; (2) relevant studies were limited in terms of clinical variables and remained relatively understudied for inflammatory markers; and (3) SI remained a relatively understudied area in patients with chronic schizophrenia compared to other suicidal behaviors, especially in China. A recent meta-analysis revealed that among 26 papers on the prevalence of SI in patients with schizophrenia (n = 5079), only 4 studies were conducted in China (n = 961) [26]. Thus, this study aimed to understand initially the prevalence of SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia and to explore comprehensively the associations of SI with general demographic factors, psychopathological factors, and inflammatory cytokines in this population. Based on the above aims, we hypothesized that: (1) patients with chronic schizophrenia are at a higher overall risk of developing SI; (2) there are associations between SI and several factors, including general demographic factors (e.g., gender), psychopathological factors (e.g., psychotic symptoms, depression, insomnia, adverse drug reactions), and inflammatory cytokines; (3) the above factors have predictive value for SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a cross-sectional study conducted between May and December 2018 in patients with chronic schizophrenia. All patients were from three medical units in Anhui Province: Chaohu Hospital of Anhui Medical University, Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital and Ma’anshan Fourth People’s Hospital. All enrolled patients were required to meet the following criteria: (1) Han Chinese, aged 18–75 years; (2) disease diagnosis consistent with the classification criteria for schizophrenia in the International Classification of Diseases 10th Edition (ICD-10); (3) total disease duration ≥ 5 years and (4) able to complete the entire study protocol. Exclusion criteria: (1) disease diagnosis consistent with other mental disorders (e.g., mental retardation, depressive disorders, drug-induced psychosis, etc.); (2) comorbidity with serious infections or other serious physical illnesses (e.g., cancer, etc.); (3) comorbidity with substance abuse (other than smoking and alcohol use); (4) history of using corticosteroids, immune-modulators, or other agents in the past year that might affect inflammatory levels; and (5) being pregnant or breastfeeding. During the investigation period, 334 patients met the inclusion criteria and were screened, but 32 patients were excluded due to a series of reasons such as lack of key data, unqualified blood specimens, etc., and 302 patients were finally included in statistical analyses. Measuring instruments

Socio-demographic characteristics

In this study, a self-designed questionnaire was used to collect general demographic information as follows: sex, age, body mass index (BMI), years of education, smoking status, comorbid physical illnesses status, age at onset, duration of illness, number of hospitalizations, as well as the type and dosage of antipsychotic medication taken. Furthermore, researchers uniformly converted the dose of antipsychotic medication taken to chlorpromazine equivalents (mg/day), and the conversion formula was referenced to the DDD method recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) [27]. Complete antipsychotic medication data for all participants can be found in the Annex: Supplementary data_Antipsychotic medication use information.

Suicidal ideation

The Chinese Version of Beck Suicide Ideation Scale (BSI-CV) was used to assess patients’ SI [28]. This scale consists of 19 items on a 3-point scale, and each item will ask about SI over two time periods (the most recent week and the most depressing week in the past). In this study, the first five items of this scale were used to assess whether patients had experienced SI or not. Patients were considered to have no SI if two of the choices in items 4 and 5 were scored 0, and vice versa for SI. Therefore, based on the assessment results of items 4 and 5 of the BSI-CV, all patients with chronic schizophrenia were divided into SI (lifetime) and non-SI (lifetime) groups, as well as SI (last week) and non-SI (last week) groups. Moreover, BSI-CV had been used in many studies in China due to its reliability [29, 30].

Psychotic symptoms

The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) was used to assess the severity of the subjects’ psychotic symptoms [31]. The scale is the most valid instrument for measuring psychotic symptoms and consists of 30 items, each rated on a 7-point scale. The higher PANSS score indicates more severe psychotic symptoms. The PANSS has excellent reliability and validity, and has been widely used in Chinese patients with schizophrenia [30, 32].

Depressive symptoms

The Calgary Depression Scale (CDSS) was used to assess patients’ depressive symptoms [33]. The scale contains 9 items on a 3-point scale ranging from 0 to 27, with higher total scores representing more severe depressive symptoms in patients. Currently, the CDSS has obtained satisfactory reliability and validity in Chinese patients with schizophrenia [34].

Insomnia symptoms

The Insomnia Severity Index Scale (ISI) was used to assess subjects’ self-perceived insomnia symptoms in last 2 weeks [35]. The scale consists of 7 items, each of which is scored on a 5-point scale ranging from ‘0 = none’ to ‘4 = very severe’. The total ISI score ranges from 0 to 28, with the higher the score, the more severe the insomnia symptoms. The Chinese version of ISI has been shown to be highly reliable in patients with schizophrenia [36].

Involuntary movement symptoms

The Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS) was used to assess drug-induced involuntary movements (especially delayed dyskinesia) [37]. This scale is a 12-item scale that assesses abnormal movements of face, mouth, limbs, and trunk, with a total score that reflects the severity of patients’ involuntary movements. Currently, the AIMS is widely used in Chinese patients with schizophrenia [38, 39].

Extrapyramidal side effects

The Rating Scale for Extrapyramidal Side Effects (RSESE) was used to assess possible extrapyramidal side effects during treatment with antipsychotics [40]. The scale consists of 10 items, each ranging from 0 (none) to 4 (always present) for assessing extrapyramidal side effects, and the total score ranges from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating more severe extrapyramidal side effects. In addition, the Chinese version of the RSESE demonstrates good reliability and validity in patients with schizophrenia [41].

Akathisia symptoms

The Barnes Akathisia Rating Scale (BARS) was used to assess and quantify patients’ akathisia symptoms [42]. The scale consists of 4 items, with higher total scores indicating more severe akathisia symptoms. Numerous clinical studies in China have utilized the BARS in patients with schizophrenia [43].

Inflammatory biomarkers

All patients with schizophrenia were taken blood samples from elbow veins between 6:00 and 8:00 am the next morning after overnight fasting. Subsequently, the blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min and the upper layer of plasma was taken for storage in a refrigerator at −80 °C until sent for testing. The plasma levels of inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, IL-17 A and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using commercially available kits (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine the normal distribution of continuous variables. To ensure that the measurements of inflammatory cytokines conformed to a normal distribution, we referred to previous studies that performed a logarithmic transformation with a base of 10 on the levels of these cytokines to obtain Log (X) values for Log IL-1β, Log IL-6, Log IL-17 A and Log TNF-α [44, 45]. These transformed values were used in subsequent statistical analyses. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (quartiles) [M (P25, P75)], and categorical variables were expressed as frequency (%). In univariate analyses, we used a variety of tests (including independent samples t-tests, Mann-Whitney U-tests, and chi-square tests) to compare the differences between the SI and non-SI groups on each variable. Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses were used to assess the correlations between variables. In the multifactorial analyses, SI was designated as the dependent variable, with other psychopathological symptoms and inflammatory cytokines serving as independent variables, while general demographic factors were controlled for as potential confounders. Logistic stepwise regression analyses were performed to determine the independent factors influencing patients’ SI. Females, CDSS score and Log IL-1β were independent influences of SI (lifetime), and chlorpromazine equivalents, PANSS score, CDSS score and Log IL-6 were independent influences of SI (last week). And the Wald χ² value was calculated using the Wald test to assess the significance of each independent variable in the logistic stepwise regression model. The model fit was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), where lower values indicate a better model fit. The AIC was calculated using the formula: AIC = 2k − 2ln(L), where k is the number of model parameters, and L is the maximum likelihood estimate. Finally, this study conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses and calculated area under the curve (AUC), to assess the predictive value of each independent factor on SI. A statistically significant difference was defined as P < 0.05 (bilateral).

Results

Socio-demographic data, psychopathological data and inflammatory cytokines levels in patients

A total of 302 patients with chronic schizophrenia were finally included, of which male patients were 182 (60.3%) and the mean age was (45.17 ± 11.67) years. Additionally, patients’ mean age at onset was (26.06 ± 8.44) years, mean duration of illness was (19.11 ± 10.48) years, mean number of hospitalizations was (5.63 ± 4.91), and mean chlorpromazine equivalents of antipsychotics taken was (454.81 ± 264.00) mg/day. The major antipsychotic drugs taken by patients included clozapine (n = 161), risperidone (n = 113), aripiprazole (n = 55), olanzapine (n = 42), quetiapine (n = 41), amisulpride (n = 15), perphenazine (n = 14), ziprasidone (n = 13), chlorpromazine (n = 11), sulpiride (n = 9), haloperidol (n = 8), perospirone (n = 3), and paliperidone (n = 1).

In terms of psychopathology, patients had a total PANSS score of (78.49 ± 24.01), CDSS score of (3.30 ± 3.39), ISI score of (3.61 ± 3.99), AIMS score of (2.12 ± 2.84), RSESE score of (3.91 ± 3.14) and BARS score of (1.15 ± 2.01) (Table 1). In terms of SI, in the total sample, the lifetime SI detection rate was 36.4% and the last week’s SI detection rate was 8.0%; in male patients, the lifetime SI detection rate was 31.9% and the last week’s SI detection rate was 8.2%; in female patients, the lifetime SI detection rate was 43.3% and the last week’s SI detection rate was 7.5%. In terms of inflammatory cytokine levels, the Log IL-1β was (0.45 ± 0.40) pg/mL, Log IL-6 was (−0.02 ± 0.48) pg/mL, Log IL-17 A was (0.70 ± 0.55) pg/mL, and Log TNF-α was (1.29 ± 0.21) pg/mL (Table 1).

Comparison of socio-demographic data, psychopathological data and inflammatory cytokine levels in the SI and non-SI groups

Univariate analyses showed that compared to non-SI (lifetime) group, patients in SI (lifetime) group were more likely to be female, had higher total scores of CDSS and ISI, and had higher plasma levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-17 A (all P < 0.05) (Table 2; Fig. 2). Compared to non-SI (last week) group, patients in SI (last week) group had higher chlorpromazine equivalents of medications, higher total scores of PANSS, CDSS, ISI, and RSESE, and higher plasma levels of IL-6 (all P < 0.05) (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Independent correlates of SI in patients

Firstly, a multifactorial logistic stepwise regression analysis with SI (lifetime) as the dependent variable revealed that females (OR = 1.981, 95% CI = 1.152–3.409, P = 0.014), CDSS score (OR = 1.382, 95% CI = 1.255–1.522, P < 0.001) and Log IL-1β (OR = 2.355, 95% CI = 1.192–4.652, P = 0.014) were independent influences of SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia (Table 3). In addition, the AIC value of the model was 330.85 and the AIC value of the model without Log IL-1β was 335.20, which indicated that the inclusion of Log IL-1β significantly improved the goodness of fit of the model while still penalizing complexity. When a multifactorial logistic stepwise regression analysis was performed with SI (last week) as the dependent variable, then chlorpromazine equivalents (OR = 1.002, 95% CI = 1.000–1.005, P = 0.028), PANSS score (OR = 1.029, 95% CI = 1.001–1.058, P = 0.046), CDSS score (OR = 1.449, 95% CI = 1.266–1.659, P < 0.001) and Log IL-6 (OR = 3.951, 95% CI = 1.568–9.959, P = 0.004) were independent correlates (Table 3). The AIC value for the model containing Log IL-6 was 109.39 and the AIC value for the model without Log IL-6 was 116.02, which also indicated that the model containing inflammatory cytokines had a better goodness of fit.

The predictive value of each independent factor for SI in patients

When the predictive value of each independent factor for SI (lifetime) was analyzed using the ROC curve, the results showed that: sex (AUC = 0.559, 95% CI = 0.492–0.627, P = 0.086); CDSS score (AUC = 0.755, 95% CI = 0.699–0.811, P < 0.001); Log IL-1β (AUC = 0.595, 95% CI = 0.529–0.661, P = 0.006); and both CDSS score and Log IL-1β combined (AUC = 0.765, 95% CI = 0.710–0.820, P < 0.001) (Table 4; Fig. 3). Compared to unifactorial prediction, the two combined CDSS score and Log IL-1β had a greater AUC, better identification for SI (lifetime) in patients with chronic schizophrenia, and higher sensitivity and specificity. Furthermore, when the predictive value of each independent factor for SI (last week) was analyzed using the ROC curve, the results showed: chlorpromazine equivalents (AUC = 0.688, 95% CI = 0.602–0.773, P = 0.002); PANSS score (AUC = 0.705, 95% CI = 0.613–0.797, P = 0.001); CDSS score (AUC = 0.833, 95% CI = 0.731–0.834, P < 0.001); Log IL-6 (AUC = 0.674, 95% CI = 0.562–0.786, P = 0.005); and the four combined (AUC = 0.883, 95% CI = 0.803–0.963, P < 0.001) (Table 4; Fig. 4). Compared to unifactorial prediction, the four-item combination of chlorpromazine equivalents, PANSS score, CDSS score, and Log IL-6 had a larger AUC, better identification of SI (last week) in patients with chronic schizophrenia, and higher sensitivity and specificity.

Discussion

This study comprehensively assessed the prevalence and related influencing factors of SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia through a multicenter cross-sectional survey. The results showed that in addition to traditional risk factors such as sex, depression, and adverse drug reactions, inflammatory cytokines might also be involved in the development of SI. The conduct of this study provided preliminary evidence for understanding the current state of SI associated with chronic schizophrenia and early intervention for it.

The prevalence of SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia

In this study, the prevalence of SI (lifetime) and SI (last week) in patients with chronic schizophrenia were 36.4% and 8.0%, respectively. These results were generally consistent with previous relevant studies [14, 26, 30]. For example, through a meta-analysis of 26 studies, Bai et al. found that the lifetime prevalence of SI was 34.5% in patients with schizophrenia [26]. Hao et al. conducted a survey in several hospitals in China and found that the prevalence of SI in the last week was 10% in patients with chronic schizophrenia [30]. However, it is worth noting that the detection rate of recent SI in this study was much lower than in Western countries (30% − 51%) [26]. On the one hand, this might be due to the fact that the subjects included in this study were chronic schizophrenics, most of whom received regular treatments, and thus reducing the likelihood of SI in short term. On the other hand, the socio-cultural context may also influence patients’ reporting of suicidal thoughts. In China, where the concept of family is strong and family members are closely connected, patients often receive substantial emotional support and life care from their families [46]. This family support network alleviates feelings of loneliness and helplessness among patients to a certain extent, thereby reducing the emergence of SI [47]. In addition, Chinese culture has traditionally viewed suicidal behavior as an extreme and unacceptable act, and this cultural perception may contribute to the stigma felt by patients with schizophrenia when reporting suicidal thoughts [48]. A recent epidemiological survey highlighted the underestimation of reported suicides due to the potential stigma attached to suicidal behaviors in Chinese society [49]. Further cross-cultural studies are needed in the future to confirm the differences in suicidal behaviors between China and Western countries. In conclusion, both this study and previous studies suggested that patients with chronic schizophrenia were at a higher overall risk of SI, and therefore, clinicians should regularly screen hospitalized patients for this condition.

The associations between SI and socio-demographic factors in patients with chronic schizophrenia

In terms of socio-demographic factors, this study found that the lifetime prevalence of SI was significantly higher in female patients than in male patients, but there was no significant sex difference shown in short-term SI. This result might be related to the following reasons. Firstly, female patients seemed to be more likely to comorbid affective symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and anger, which might increase the probability of SI. Also, some specific socio-cultural patterns might contribute to the increased suicide risk among female patients. For example, in real life, females often have to cope with multiple social roles (work, family, etc.), partner violence, lower economic status, sex discrimination, and so on, which causes females to suffer from more social pressures [50]. As for the biological mechanisms, compared to males, females’ brain circuits show unique activity patterns under stress, especially in regions such as the amygdala-frontal network, which suggests that females may have stronger activation in these regions under stress and this also explains why females are more susceptible to SI from a neuroscientific viewpoint [51]. However, the results of previous studies were controversial regarding whether there was sex difference in the suicide risk associated with schizophrenia. Similar to the results of this study, Woottiluk et al. found that there was slightly higher suicide risk in female patients than in males [52]. Correll et al. also found that females with schizophrenia had significantly higher suicide-related mortality rates than males by meta-analysis [4]. In contrast, however, studies by Ran et al. and Bareis et al. both showed higher suicide risk in males with schizophrenia [7, 53]. As well, it is worth noting that this study did not find a statistical difference in factors such as age, BMI, smoking, and duration of illness between the SI group and the non-SI group of patients with chronic schizophrenia, which was also inconsistent with the results of some previous studies [54, 55]. These inconsistent findings might be related to factors such as differences in assessment instruments, different time frames for assessments, as well as sample heterogeneity. Therefore, there remains a strong need for population-based studies with large samples to further explore the associations between suicide and socio-demographic factors in patients with schizophrenia in the future.

The associations between SI and psychopathological factors in patients with chronic schizophrenia

SI and psychotic symptoms

This study found that PANSS score was independently associated with recent week SI, but not lifetime SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia. This result suggested that psychotic symptoms might be involved in the mechanism of development for SI in schizophrenia. Numerous previous studies have reached similar conclusions [56], but the mechanisms involved might be complex. For one, there may be a direct effect of psychotic symptoms in increasing SI in patients. Firstly, there was evidence that schizophrenia might directly increase patients’ suicide risk through symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions [56, 57]. The results of a clinical antipsychotic drug trial also showed that psychotic symptoms such as delusions and hallucinations remained independently associated with SI in patients with schizophrenia after controlling for depressive symptoms [56]. Shawyer et al. noted that persistent, highly realistic hallucinations which were consistent with delusional beliefs tended to inspire greater compliance in individuals, and this further drove suicidal behaviors in patients [58]. For the other hand, the psychotic symptoms of schizophrenics may also indirectly induce individuals’ suicide through certain mediators. Patients with chronic schizophrenia often experience conditions such as cognitive decline, impaired social functioning, and financial difficulties [36], which can further increase individuals’ feelings of hopelessness and thus increase SI [59]. For example, in a cross-sectional study, Zhu et al. found that cognitive impairment might mediate the association between psychotic symptoms and SI in patients with schizophrenia [36]. Therefore, for patients with chronic schizophrenia, the presence and severity of psychotic symptoms may be important risk factors for the development of SI in short term. This finding suggests that clinicians should strengthen the monitoring of psychotic symptoms in this population as part of their daily practice and consider it an important indicator for suicide risk assessment. Through early screening and timely intervention, clinicians may be able to effectively reduce the risk of suicide and improve clinical outcomes for patients.

SI and depressive symptoms

Furthermore, this study found that the more severe the depressive symptoms in patients with chronic schizophrenia, the more likely they were to have SI, and this finding was generally consistent with the results of previous related clinical studies [11, 12]. For a long time, depression has been recognized as one of the most common comorbidities in patients with schizophrenia. This may be due to the fact that patients with schizophrenia often suffer from huge psychological burdens, stigma, and social adjustment disorders, which in turn lead to gradual development of depressive symptoms such as low mood. Also, depressive symptoms in patients with schizophrenia may stem from antipsychotic medications. Harrow et al. has indicated that prolonged dopamine receptor blockade could lead to the lack of pleasure and might induce depression [60]. And the possible explanations for the associations between depressive symptoms and SI in schizophrenia are as follows. Firstly, patients with schizophrenia comorbid depressive symptoms often experience feelings of hopelessness, guilt, and low worth, and these secondary psychological symptoms can eventually lead to suicidal thoughts [61]. Second, comorbid depressive symptoms may increase the frequency of acute exacerbations and re-hospitalizations, and reduce the degree and duration of remission, which in turn increases patients’ economic burden as well as suicide risk [11]. In summary, depressive symptoms may be a significant predictor of concomitant SI in patients with schizophrenia. In clinical practice, doctors should take active interventions to deal with depressive symptoms in patients with chronic schizophrenia, such as the combination of appropriate antidepressant medications, physiotherapy or psychotherapy [61]. For example, a meta-analysis that included 82 randomized controlled trials (n = 3608) assessed the effectiveness of antidepressant medications in patients with schizophrenia and found that trazodone, duloxetine, sertraline, and amitriptyline were effective in reducing comorbid depressive symptoms [62].

SI and insomnia symptoms

In this study, insomnia symptoms were also significantly associated with SI, but after regression analyses, no statistical significance was shown. In fact, there were already a large number of previous clinical studies and meta-analyses for schizophrenia that came to similar conclusions [13, 14, 63–65]. Sleep disorders may worsen psychotic symptoms, and the worsening disease increases patients’ suicide risk. For example, through a network analysis, Peng et al. showed that chronic schizophrenics with insomnia had more severe clinical symptoms and higher levels of SI [63]. Moreover, the association between insomnia and SI may also be related to depressive symptoms. Irwin et al. had demonstrated that depression and insomnia had a complex two-way relationship [66]. Also, through cross-sectional investigations in patients with schizophrenia, Carruthers et al. and Miller et al. found that the association between sleep disorders and SI became less significant when depressive symptoms were taken into account [14, 67]. Finally, insomnia may also affect emotional responses, causing suppression of individuals’ positive responses to stimuli, which in turn increases suicide risk [65]. Therefore, active interventions for insomnia symptoms are necessary to improve the clinical prognosis of patients with chronic schizophrenia.

SI and adverse drug reactions

In terms of antipsychotics, our study found that there were correlations between patients’ SI with higher chlorpromazine equivalents and extrapyramidal side effects. Firstly, patients who take higher doses of antipsychotics are often accompanied by more severe psychotic symptoms, which in turn are associated with higher suicide risk. Secondly, side effects such as extrapyramidal reactions caused by antipsychotic drugs may increase suicide risk by impairing individuals’ social functioning and quality of life [68]. Finally, depressive symptoms may also play a mediating role between extrapyramidal side effects and suicide risk in patients. Through studies in Chinese patients with schizophrenia, Xu et al. and Weng et al. found that extrapyramidal side effects caused by antipsychotics were significantly correlated with the severity of depression [69, 70]. Given the strong associations between depressive symptoms and suicidal behaviors, it was therefore hypothesized that patients with chronic schizophrenia would experience increased depression and consequent SI due to adverse drug reactions. Although the association between adverse drug reactions and SI was not statistically significant in this study, clinicians should still pay attention to the phenomenon and strengthen their assessments. Effective intervention for suicide risk may be enhanced through regular assessment of side effects and monitoring of blood drug concentrations.

The associations between SI and inflammatory cytokines in patients with chronic schizophrenia

More critically, the results of our study also showed that patients with chronic schizophrenia who exhibited SI had higher levels of plasma inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-17 A. These results were generally consistent with previous studies in different populations [21–24, 71]. For example, a meta-analysis that included 22 clinical studies (n = 1813) showed that inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 played an important role in individuals’ suicidal behaviors [71]. Among the mechanisms that may be involved are the following. Elevated levels of inflammation may lead to emotional states such as impulsivity, depression, and lack of pleasure, which significantly increase the likelihood of suicide. In addition, through specific biochemical pathways, inflammatory factors may play a role in modulating neural responses and behavioral tendencies, which in turn affects individuals’ susceptibility to suicidal behaviors. For example, Brundin et al. had found that pro-inflammatory cytokines were able to initiate the kynurenine metabolic pathway and affect the inflammatory responses of the nervous system and the metabolism of glutamate, thereby increasing individuals’ risk of suicide [72]. Inflammation may also lead to decreased concentrations of monoamine neurotransmitters (especially serotonin) in the brain, which may inhibit neurogenesis processes, affecting cognitive abilities and ultimately indirectly increasing the likelihood of individuals committing suicide [73]. Although there were studies that tentatively confirmed the links between suicidal behaviors and inflammatory cytokines, the current studies focusing on the associations in chronic schizophrenia patients remained quite limited. This study not only provided in-depth analyses of inflammatory cytokines levels in patients with chronic schizophrenia who exhibited SI, but also revealed the validity of IL-1β and IL-6 in predicting SI through ROC curve analyses. In addition, by comparing AIC values, this study found that the model incorporating inflammatory cytokines exhibited superior goodness-of-fit even after accounting for complexity penalties. Thus, we concluded that the inclusion of inflammatory cytokine data significantly enhances the clinical prediction of SI in patients with schizophrenia, contributing beyond what can be achieved by relying solely on clinical symptoms. In conclusion, our study provided new biomarkers for the prevention of suicidal behaviors in patients with chronic schizophrenia and might contribute to early identification. In addition, in the future, by modulating the level of inflammation in schizophrenia patients, such as through the use of specific anti-inflammatory drugs, may play a role in preventing suicide in individuals.

Innovations and limitations

Although numerous studies had extensively explored suicidal behaviors in patients with schizophrenia, most of them focused on patients with first or acute episodes, while few focused on patients with chronic schizophrenia. Moreover, there were also few studies discussing the associations between inflammatory cytokines levels and SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia. This study more comprehensively analyzed the associations between SI and sociodemographic factors, clinical symptoms and inflammatory cytokines in patients with chronic schizophrenia, and these findings provided some insights into understanding SI in this population.

However, this study also has the following limitations: (1) Due to the cross-sectional design of this study, it did not support the explicit inference of causality. Therefore, future longitudinal or interventional studies are necessary to further elucidate the causal relationship between SI and psychopathological symptoms as well as inflammatory cytokines in patients with schizophrenia. (2) The recall bias and the stigma attached to suicide might lead to a decrease in reporting of SI in patients. (3) Since the study subjects were recruited from only three hospitals in Anhui Province, the generalisability the of findings to other regions and populations may be limited. Future studies should be conducted in a broader range of regions and diverse populations with varying cultural backgrounds to further confirm the generalisability and applicability of these findings. (4) The small sample sizes of certain subgroups in this study (e.g., the last week SI group) somewhat limited the statistical power and may have affected the interpretation of the results. Future studies should consider expanding the sample size, particularly for these key subgroups, to enhance the statistical power and reliability of the findings. (5) Although this study collected chlorpromazine equivalents of patients’ current antipsychotic medications, it did not capture dynamic changes (such as dose adjustments, switches, or discontinuation) during treatment, nor did it systematically document prior medication history, both of which may introduce bias. It was also worth noting that previous studies have suggested that antipsychotic medications may also affect inflammatory cytokine levels [74], which might also interfere with the results of the study. Therefore, future studies should collect more comprehensive information on medication use to more accurately assess the impact of antipsychotics on SI in patients with schizophrenia. (6) The inflammatory cytokine levels were measured in peripheral blood rather than brain tissue, representing another limitation of this study. (7) Previous studies also suggested that psychosocial factors such as familial suicidal history may also have a significant impact on SI. Therefore, future studies need to integrate more variables to more systematically examine SI in patients with chronic schizophrenia.

Conclusion

Patients with chronic schizophrenia are at relatively high risk for SI, and SI may be associated with psychotic symptoms, depression, sleep disturbances, medication side effects, and increased levels of inflammatory cytokines. In medical practice, doctors need to be more alert to suicidal tendencies in patients with chronic schizophrenia and should provide timely interventions for those with comorbid suicidal risk factors. In addition, future interventions based on the modulation of inflammation levels may potentially play an important role in preventing suicidal behaviors in patients with chronic schizophrenia.
