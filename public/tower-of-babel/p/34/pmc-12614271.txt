Effect of Increased Power and Asphericity of Highly Aspherical Lenslets on Myopia Control Efficacy: A Contralateral Crossover Study
Authors: Rajkumar Nallour Raveendran, Wee Sing Ong, Yee Ling Wong, Stella Jinran Zhan, Chun Fan Lee, Si Ying Lim, Björn Drobe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe study aims to evaluate whether the myopia control efficacy of spectacle lenses with Highly Aspherical Lenslet Target (H.A.L.T.) technology could be further improved by a combined increase of power and asphericity of lenslets.MethodsFifty Singaporean myopic children aged 6 to 10 years were enrolled in a randomized, contralateral, crossover clinical trial. Two different myopia control spectacle lenses were tested: lenses with H.A.L.T. technology and lenses with maximized H.A.L.T. (H.A.L.T. MAX) technology. One group (N = 25) was assigned to wear H.A.L.T. MAX on the right eye and H.A.L.T. on the left eye, and the other group (N = 25) to wear the opposite combination for 6 months. The combinations were switched for the next 6 months. The primary outcome was the change in axial length (AL).ResultsAt baseline, mean (±SD) AL of eyes wearing H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses was 23.97 ± 0.84 and 23.99 ± 0.84 mm. After the first 6 months, mean (±SEM) axial elongation in eyes wearing H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses was 0.105 ± 0.016 and 0.043 ± 0.016 mm (P < 0.001), respectively. After the crossover, the corresponding mean AL change was 0.123 ± 0.014 and 0.077 ± 0.013 mm (P < 0.001). The differences in AL change between the two lenses were correlated with faster axial elongation with H.A.L.T. lenses in both phases.ConclusionsSpectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. MAX technology slowed axial elongation more effectively than spectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. technology and might be particularly beneficial for children with faster axial elongation.Translational RelevanceThe relationship between the optical characteristics of lenslets and axial elongation offers a new avenue for developing more effective myopia control lenses.

Introduction

Myopia is a significant global public health issue,1–3 especially in East and Southeast Asia, with 80% and 86% of 15-year-old children having myopia in Hong Kong3 and Singapore,4 respectively. Therefore, it is important to develop a management strategy to slow its progression rate. Optical interventions have been shown to be one of the most effective solutions,5 with spectacle lenses remaining as the safest and most universally applicable solution for children as the risks of side effects with spectacle lens correction are minimal.6

Spectacle lenses with different optical designs and mechanisms of action7–12 have been developed to slow myopia progression in children. In particular, spectacle lenses with concentric rings of highly aspherical lenslets and slightly aspherical lenslets13,14 do not concentrate light into a single focal plane; instead, each aspherical lenslet forms a caustic in front of the retina. The envelope of all the caustics constitutes a volume of non-focused light (VoNFL) aimed at slowing eye growth. In terms of optical characteristics, the VoNFL produced by lenses with slightly aspherical lenslets is narrower and much closer to the retina, whereas the VoNFL produced by lenses with highly aspherical lenslets is broader and positioned further in front of the retina (refer to Fig. 1 in Bao et al. 2022).13 Significantly better myopia control efficacy was observed in lenses with highly aspherical lenslets compared with lenses with slightly aspherical lenslets among Chinese children in a randomized trial,14 suggesting a dose-response relationship between VoNFL (due to the lenslets’ optical characteristics) and myopia control efficacy. Similarly, animal studies showed a dose-response relationship between imposed defocus and refractive compensation,15–19 with maximum compensation to myopic defocus that could occur up to approximately +9 diopters (D) for rhesus monkeys.20

Building on previous findings, this study investigates whether spectacle lenses with lenslets of greater average power and asphericity than those used in spectacle lenses with Highly Aspherical Lenslet Target (H.A.L.T.) technology13,14 would provide more effective myopia control. To achieve this, a novel lens design incorporating H.A.L.T. MAX technology was developed, where we have optimized the optical characteristics of lenslets to create a VoNFL that would be significantly broader and positioned further in front of the retina. We hypothesized that this lens design would result in superior efficacy in terms of slowing axial elongation compared with lenses with H.A.L.T. technology.

Methods

This was a 1-year randomized, controlled, double-masked, contralateral, crossover, superiority clinical trial with follow-up visits every 6 months. The trial was monocentric, conducted in the Essilor R&D Centre Singapore, Singapore, from November 2023 to January 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Parkway Independent Ethics Committee of the IHH Healthcare group, Singapore (PIEC/2023/034) and registered in clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06148870). Written informed consent and informed assent were obtained from parents and children. All procedures adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Intervention

Two different myopia control spectacle lens designs were tested in this study: (1) spectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. technology (commercially available as Essilor Stellest lenses); and (2) spectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. MAX technology. Both spectacle lenses are made of polycarbonate and have a spherical front surface with 11 concentric rings formed by contiguous aspherical lenslets. The central area of the lens without lenslets provides distance correction. The optical characteristics of the lenslets were calculated to generate a VoNFL in front of the retina at any eccentricity. All calculations were performed using ray tracing software, incorporating a myopic eye model from Atchison21 adjusted to match peripheral refraction data from Sng et al.4 The only difference between the two spectacle lens designs is the optical characteristics of the lenslets, resulting in different myopia control signals, that is, H.A.L.T. MAX lenses were designed to have VoNFL broader and further in front of the retina, compared to H.A.L.T. lenses (Fig. 1). The similar optical design and comparable visual acuity between the two lens designs ensured the feasibility of implementing contralateral study design.

Study Population

The following inclusion criteria were used: children aged 6 to 10 years with a manifest myopic spherical equivalent refraction (SER) between −0.50 and −4.75 D in each eye; astigmatism of ≤ 2.00 D; anisometropia of ≤ 1.00 D; and monocular best corrected distance visual acuity of 0.2 logMAR or better for both eyes. Children with and without prior myopia control intervention with spectacle lenses were included. However, children who were undergoing or had a history of myopia control interventions other than spectacles, specifically atropine or orthokeratology, were excluded. Children with binocular vision disorders, including amblyopia, strabismus, and those with ocular disease, were excluded from the study.

Contralateral and Crossover Trial

Fifty children who met the inclusion/exclusion criteria were enrolled in the study and randomized into 2 groups of 25 each (Fig. 2). One group was assigned to wear combination 1 (H.A.L.T. MAX lenses in the right eye and H.A.L.T. lenses in the left eye) for the first 6 months (phase 1) and then wear combination 2 (H.A.L.T. lenses in the right eye and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses in the left eye) for the next 6 months (phase 2). The other group was assigned to wear combination 2 for the first 6 months during phase 1 and then wear combination 1 for the next 6 months in phase 2. The participants, parents of the participants, and clinical investigators were masked from the types of interventions that each eye had received.

Flowchart of the study design showing randomized contralateral crossover design to evaluate the superiority of H.A.L.T. MAX lenses compared with H.A.L.T. lenses. RE, right eye; LE, left eye.

Participants were evaluated at baseline, 6-month, and 12-month visits. At each visit, axial length (AL) was calculated as the average of 3 measurements obtained using a Lenstar LS 900 instrument (Haag-Streit AG, Switzerland). Non-cycloplegic autorefraction was also performed (Wave Analyzer Medica 700+ [WAM700+] wavefront aberrometer, Essilor, France). In addition, subjective refraction using a phoropter (Vision R-800, Essilor, France) was performed, using the non-cycloplegic autorefraction values as the starting point, at 4 meters (m). Fogging, where positive spherical lenses were added until visual acuity was worse than +0.3 logMAR, was done to reduce accommodation, as cycloplegia was not performed. SER was calculated as sphere plus half negative cylinder. As safety measures, binocular vision assessments, including Worth-4-dot test (distance and near) and stereoacuity using preschool random dot stereogram, were measured at each visit. A telephone interview was conducted with the parents of participants within 2 to 4 days after dispensing to ensure adaptability to the study spectacles. Parent questionnaires were administered to monitor compliance and wearing time of study spectacles at the 6-month and 12-month visits. Ocular dominance was assessed by the hole-in-the-card sighting test and dominant handedness was assessed via questionnaire (“which hand do you use to write?”).

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was 1-year cumulative change in AL, estimated by summing the axial elongation in phase 1 (from baseline to the end of the 6-month follow-up visit) and phase 2 (from the end of 6-month to the end of the 1-year follow-up visit) computed for each lens group. Previous studies have identified axial elongation as the preferred outcome for assessing myopia control efficacy,22,23 as it directly relates to myopia-related risks24 and some treatments can alter refraction without affecting eye growth.23 Myopia progression based on non-cycloplegic autorefraction was included as an exploratory analysis.

Sample Size Calculation and Statistical Analysis

The intention-to-treat approach was adopted. Under the assumption that measures from the right and left eyes are independent and uncorrelated, the primary outcome is the cumulative change in AL over the 1-year period for each lens design. The crossover effect on axial elongation was evaluated using an adaptation of the Wellek and Blettner (2012)25 pre-test equation at the participant-eye level.

For this contralateral crossover study design, the sample size was calculated to detect a mean difference of 0.116 mm, assuming a standard deviation of 0.158 mm after a 1-year cumulative change in AL.13 Fifty participants were required for 90% power and a 5% significance level, with a dropout rate of 18%.

The analysis was conducted at two time points: (a) after the completion of phase 1 at the end of the 6-month visit, and (b) after the completion of phase 2 at the end of the 12-month visit. A paired t-test was performed separately at the end of phase 1 and phase 2 to compare the changes in AL between the 2 myopia control lens designs at 2 different time periods.

A linear mixed-effects model (LMM) with repeated measurements was conducted across both phases to estimate the effect of lens group on the changes in AL, adjusting for phase and accounting for within-participant and within-eye correlations. Moreover, additional subgroup analyses on the effects of additional factors such as age, rate of progression, gender, eye (right versus left eye), history of myopia control spectacle lenses, first-time eyeglass wear, and ocular dominance on AL changes were also conducted. For age, participants were grouped into either younger (less than or equal to the median age) or older age groups.

Results

All 50 participants completed the 1-year randomized crossover trial (no dropouts). The mean (±SD) baseline age of the participants was 8.6 ± 1.2 years (Table 1). Of 50 participants, 17 (34%) had a history of using myopia control spectacle lenses. At baseline, mean AL of eyes wearing H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses were 23.97 ± 0.84 and 23.99 ± 0.84 mm, respectively, and mean SER of eyes wearing H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses were −1.90 ± 0.97 and −1.90 ± 0.98 D, respectively. In addition, the baseline ocular characteristics between the right and left eyes of participants were also similar (Table 2).

Changes in Axial Length

In phase 1, mean (SE of mean) axial elongation from baseline was 0.105 (0.016) and 0.043 (0.016) mm in eyes wearing H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses, respectively (Fig. 3). Eyes wearing H.A.L.T. MAX lenses showed significantly less axial elongation than H.A.L.T. lenses (mean difference of −0.062, 95% confidence interval [CI] = −0.085 to −0.038, P < 0.001). Likewise, in phase 2, mean axial elongation from the 6-month visit was 0.123 (0.014) and 0.077 (0.013) mm in eyes wearing H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses, respectively, with eyes wearing H.A.L.T. MAX lenses again showing significantly less axial elongation than H.A.L.T. lenses (mean difference of −0.045, 95% CI = −0.065 to −0.025, P < 0.001). At the end of the 1-year randomized contralateral crossover trial, there was no significant difference in axial elongation between the two eyes, regardless of the assigned lens group (paired t-test: t(49) = 0.83, P = 0.22): 0.182 (0.025) mm for H.A.L.T. first; and 0.166 (0.025) mm for H.A.L.T. MAX first.

Changes in AL of eyes wearing H.A.L.T. (green) and H.A.L.T. MAX (yellow) lenses in phase 1 (solid lines) and phase 2 (dashed lines). The datapoints and the error bars represent the mean and standard error of the mean.

The results of LMM also revealed that, adjusting for phases, eye wearing H.A.L.T. MAX lenses had significantly slower axial elongation compared with the eyes wearing H.A.L.T. lenses (mean difference of −0.05 mm, 95% CI = −0.07 to −0.04, P < 0.001). Moreover, the analysis on the crossover effect on the axial elongation suggested that there was no evidence of crossover effect (t(98) = 0.449, P = 0.65). Given that no carryover effect was observed between the two phases, the estimated cumulative 1-year change in AL by adding the AL changes in both phases for H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses were 0.228 and 0.121 mm, respectively, with a difference of 0.107 mm in 1 year (Fig. 4).

Cumulative changes in AL for H.A.L.T. (green) and H.A.L.T. MAX (yellow) lenses in 1 year. Cumulative changes were estimated by adding the AL changes in both phases. The separation between the datapoints at 6 months denotes the crossover.

Comparing the axial elongation of eyes wearing H.A.L.T. MAX and H.A.L.T. lenses in each individual using z-score for proportions, 78% (39/50) and 72% (36/50) of eyes wearing H.A.L.T. MAX lenses had slower axial elongation than the fellow eye wearing H.A.L.T. lenses in phase 1 (z = 5.6, P < 0.001) and phase 2 (z = 4.4, P < 0.001), respectively.

Moreover, eyes wearing H.A.L.T. MAX lenses and H.A.L.T. lenses in both phases had a shortening of axial length. Twelve of 50 eyes in phase 1 (mean reduction of −0.12 mm) and 11 of 50 eyes in phase 2 (mean reduction of −0.02 mm) wearing H.A.L.T. MAX lenses showed a shortening of AL. Similarly, 6 of 50 eyes in phase 1 (mean reduction of −0.06 mm) and 4 of 50 eyes in phase 2 (mean reduction of −0.01 mm) wearing H.A.L.T. lenses showed a shortening of AL.

Negative values indicate that eyes wearing H.A.L.T. MAX lenses had slower axial elongation compared to the fellow eye wearing H.A.L.T. lenses. MAX lenses compared with H.A.L.T. lenses.

The effect of age on axial elongation with both myopia control lenses was examined by grouping the participants into either younger (less than or equal to the median age of 8.5 years) or older age groups (Fig. 6). The LMM including a lens group-by-age group interaction term showed a statistically significant interaction (P = 0.035). This indicated that the effect of the lens group on AL change varied between older and younger participants. When comparing AL changes from baseline between lens groups, using data from both phases and organized by age group, H.A.L.T MAX lenses revealed statistically significantly less axial elongation compared with H.A.L.T. lenses in both younger (mean difference of −0.069, 95% CI = −0.092 to −0.046, P < 0.001) and older (mean difference of −0.034, 95% CI = −0.057 to −0.011, P = 0.005) age groups.

The subgroup analyses of other factors, such as gender, eye (right versus left eye), history of myopia control spectacle lenses, first-time eyeglass wear, and ocular dominance revealed no statistically significant interaction with lens group on axial elongation (Supplementary Table S1).

Myopia Progression

In phase 1, mean myopia progression was −0.20 (SEM = 0.08) and −0.11 (SEM = 0.09) D in eyes wearing H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses, respectively. Similarly, in phase 2, the mean myopia progression was −0.22 (SEM = 0.07) and −0.10 (SEM = 0.09) D in eyes wearing H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses, respectively. The differences in myopia progression were not significantly different for either phase (mean difference of 0.09 D; P = 0.14 for phase 1 and mean difference of 0.12 D; P = 0.20 for phase 2). The estimated cumulative 1-year myopia progression for H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses was −0.42 and −0.21 D, respectively, with a difference of 0.21 D (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Adaptation, Wearing Time, and Binocular Vision

A telephone interview with the parents of the participants after dispensing showed that 44 children (88%) in phase 1 and 49 children (98%) in phase 2 adapted within 2 to 4 days, of which 26 (52%) in phase 1 and 32 children (64%) in phase 2 adapted immediately. Overall, 40 children (80%) in phase 1 and 45 children (90%) in phase 2 had no symptoms when adapting to the study spectacles. The mean (±SD) lens wearing time in phase 1 was 13.7 ± 2.2 hours/day and 13.4 ± 2.9 hours/day in phase 2. No device-related adverse effects were reported throughout the trial, with five non-device-related adverse events reported (such as the common cold).

Binocular vision assessments using Worth 4 dot test did not vary significantly from the baseline visit (no reports of suppression in the baseline and the follow-up visits). Similarly, stereoacuity did not vary significantly from the baseline visit, with mean (±SD) stereoacuity of 52.8 ± 19.7, 48.0 ± 14.0, and 44.1 ± 12.9 arc seconds at the baseline, end of phase 1 follow-up, and end of phase 2 follow-up visits, respectively.

Discussion

This randomized, contralateral, crossover trial among myopic children aged 6 to 10 years showed that axial elongation with spectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. MAX technology was significantly slower than with spectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. technology. This confirms the previous dose-response relationship between the optical characteristics of lenslets and the myopia control efficacy of the lenses.13,14 Additionally, no changes in binocular vision assessments were observed, and there were no intervention-related adverse effects during the clinical trial.

Previous studies that compared myopia control efficacy between spectacle lenses with slightly aspherical lenslets and highly aspherical lenslets indicated that myopia control efficacy might be associated with the optical characteristics of lenslets.13,14 The VoNFL produced by slightly aspherical lenslets had a width of 0.3 mm, positioned 1.0 mm in front of the retina, whereas the lenses with H.A.L.T. technology had a larger width of 0.7 mm positioned further at 1.2 mm in front of the retina, indicating that a wider VoNFL, which is further in front of the retina, would result in higher myopia control efficacy (refer to Fig. 1 of Bao et al. 2022).13 The results of the current study corroborate this dose-response relationship between the specifications of VoNFL and myopia control efficacy, as lenses with H.A.L.T. MAX technology, optimized to have enhanced myopia control signals by widening the VoNFL to 1.6 mm and positioning it even further at 2.3 mm in front of the retina, demonstrated slower axial elongation compared with lenses with H.A.L.T. technology. Moreover, these findings align well with previous animal studies that reported the dose-dependent effect between refractive development and optical characteristics such as lens power, asphericity, and peripheral defocus.15,20,26–28

The contrast theory of myopia suggests that increased contrast in the peripheral retina is associated with axial elongation,11 thus reducing peripheral retinal contrast may slow myopia progression.29 Previous studies have shown that myopia control lenses with lenslet designs, like lenses with H.A.L.T. technology, decrease image quality in the peripheral retina.30,31 The higher power and asphericity of lenslets in lenses with H.A.L.T. MAX technology may have further reduced peripheral retinal image quality, which could have contributed to the greater effect on slowing axial elongation compared to lenses with H.A.L.T. technology. Further investigation into how the lenslets in lenses with H.A.L.T. MAX technology affect peripheral retinal image quality is warranted.

In this contralateral, crossover trial, changes in AL were no different in the right and left eyes (0.17 and 0.18 mm, respectively) at the end of 12 months. This shows that unwarranted anisometropia was not induced in young children by implementing the contralateral, crossover trial. As participants had worn H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses for 1 year and no carryover effect was observed between the 2 phases, a cumulative 1-year change in AL suggests an additional slowing of axial elongation by 0.107 mm in eyes wearing H.A.L.T. MAX lenses compared with eyes wearing H.A.L.T. lenses. Moreover, the analysis of the effect of progression rate on the AL changes suggested that eyes showing faster progression with H.A.L.T. lenses had more benefits in terms of slowing axial elongation using H.A.L.T. MAX lenses. The subgroup analysis of the effect of age on the AL changes also revealed that younger children (aged ≤8.5 years) had more benefits in terms of slowing axial elongation using H.A.L.T. MAX lenses compared with H.A.L.T. lenses. However, confirmation with a larger sample randomized controlled trial is needed.

In this study, we implemented a randomized, contralateral, crossover trial to evaluate the superiority of spectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. MAX technology in myopia control, against an established efficacious myopia control solution – spectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. technology.13,14,32 Whereas the lack of a control group wearing single-vision lenses may hinder direct interpretation of myopia control efficacy, ethical concerns have been raised about withholding treatment in control groups.33 Therefore, the use of approved or proven myopia control treatments as the control group in superiority or noninferiority trial designs is highly recommended33 and has been adopted in this present study with a contralateral crossover design. Only a few studies have implemented crossover designs in spectacle lenses (bilateral crossover design)34 and contact lenses (contralateral crossover design).35 This study opens future possibilities for the use of contralateral crossover trial designs for evaluating myopia control efficacy for spectacle lenses.

The use of the contralateral study design accounts for environmental, behavioral (such as outdoor, light exposure, and near work time), and other unknown factors that could potentially influence the study outcomes. Another advantage of using contralateral study designs is that it allows children with a history of using other myopia control spectacle lenses to be included (34% of children having a history of using myopia control spectacle lenses in this study). As no significant effect of the history of myopia control spectacle lenses on the axial elongation of the eyes wearing H.A.L.T. and H.A.L.T. MAX lenses was observed in this study, contralateral study designs may offer greater flexibility in terms of recruitment of participants into trials, especially with the increasing awareness and uptake of myopia control treatments.36 Additionally, with two different myopia control lenses tested using a contralateral design, the absence of a control single-vision lenses group can increase retention of study participants, as demonstrated by no dropouts in this study.

There are a few limitations in this study. Cycloplegia was not performed in the trial for assessing myopia progression. However, axial elongation was the primary outcome for this study, and myopia progression was an exploratory analysis. Another limitation of this study was the absence of a washout period, which is typically used in classical crossover designs, hence, a true treatment effect in phase 2 cannot be fully evaluated. However, it should be noted that rebound effects with optical interventions are less likely compared with other forms of interventions.37 In addition, the study duration may be insufficient to fully quantify myopia control efficacy, as the comparison between the two lens designs was conducted over 2 separate 6-month phases.

Conclusions

Spectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. MAX technology exhibit slower axial elongation compared with spectacle lenses with H.A.L.T. technology, particularly for children who experience faster axial elongation. A dose-response relationship was observed between the optical characteristics of lenslets (power and asphericity) and the efficacy of myopia control. Furthermore, the research highlights the ethical and effective use of contralateral, crossover study designs to establish the superiority of one myopia control spectacle lens over another, especially for similar lens designs.
