Temperature-Dependent Spontaneous Resolution of a Tetrahedral Chiral-at-Nickel(II) Complex under Supramolecular Control
Authors: Yuanfei Liu, Hitoshi Ube, Kenichi Endo, Mitsuhiko Shionoya
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough stereochemical control of carbon centers is a well-established technique in modern synthetic chemistry, that of tetrahedral metal complexes with a stereogenic metal center remains difficult due to the dynamic nature of their coordination bonds. Here we report the synthesis of a tetrahedral d8 high-spin chiral-at-nickel(II) complex composed exclusively of achiral ligands and the supramolecular control of its temperature-dependent spontaneous resolution in crystals. Under certain conditions, complex molecules with the same absolute configuration of the nickel(II) center grow into conglomerate crystals with a helically arranged structure due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding. This process is highly spontaneous, does not require any chiral sources, and is closely related to the origin of homochirality in biological systems. The obtained enantiopure nickel(II) complex will be a new type of redox-active chiral source for asymmetric synthetic chemistry.Keywords: chiral-at-nickel(II) complex, tetrahedral nickel(II) complex, spontaneous resolution; conglomerate crystals; temperature-dependent crystallization

Chiral-at-metal complexes with a stereogenic metal center due to the asymmetric arrangement of ligands1 have recently shown promises as a new type of chiral catalysts2−12 and chiroptical materials.13,14 However, chiral-at-metal complexes are mainly limited to octahedral d6 low-spin complexes2−10,15,16 and half-sandwich d6/d4 low-spin complexes,1 and tetrahedral metal complexes have scarcely been reported,17−21 even though tetrahedral geometry is one of the most basic structural motifs that can adopt asymmetric structures, as indicated by the carbon-centered chirality of the tetrahedral form.22,23 This is because the labile bonding nature of tetrahedral metal complexes causes fast ligand exchange or flattening, resulting in a loss of the configurational stability of the metal center.24 In particular, it was reported that, without chiral ligands, tetrahedral chiral-at-metal complexes rapidly racemized within minutes at room temperature.17−19 Against this background, our group recently reported the first example of a tetrahedral chiral-at-zinc(II) complex with a stable absolute configuration of the metal center consisting only of achiral ligands.20 The configurationally stable stereogenic zinc center underwent fast stereoinversion only in the presence of alcoholic protons, thus allowing asymmetric induction25,26 using a chiral aminoalcohol as an auxiliary agent. In fact, the absolute configuration of the zinc center is exclusively biased to one side. On the other hand, when the stereogenic metal center is inert toward stereoinversion in solution, the pair of enantiomers can be separated by diastereomeric precipitation,27 chiral chromatography28,29 or spontaneous resolution.30−32 In the crystallization of racemic compounds in solution, when optically pure crystals are formed with only molecules of the same configuration, the crystals are called conglomerate crystals, and the phenomenon is called spontaneous resolution.30−32 Since this optical resolution method does not require other chiral sources and is easy to operate, it is hoped that a more general and reproducible crystallization method will be established.

Nickel(II) has attractive properties as a transition metal ion that exhibits a unique redox potential in a tetrahedral coordination structure, as seen in the active centers of natural nickel enzymes such as [NiFe]-hydrogenase.33,34 Nickel has also recently been used as a functional center for photo- and electrochemical reactions.35−39 Here we report the construction of an optically pure, tetrahedral d8 high-spin chiral-at-nickel(II) complex 1 with an unsymmetric achiral tridentate ligand (Figure 1). In this design, the tridentate ligand fixes the absolute configuration of the stereogenic nickel center, leaving a labile coordination site to activate the substrate for catalysis. In this study, chiral-at-nickel(II) complexes were optical resolved by rare temperature-dependent spontaneous resolution40 that is distinct from asymmetric induction and diastereomeric separation methods.

Spontaneous resolution of enantiomers, observed not only in crystals but also in supramolecular self-assembly in solution, has revealed that noncovalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding in crystal packing structures play an important role in the transfer of chirality from molecules to the bulk.31,32 By such a supramolecular process, a single homochiral nickel(II) complex was expected to grow into highly asymmetric structures: from helices and three-dimensional superstructures to conglomerate crystals in a highly asymmetric manner. The resulting conglomerate crystals are homochiral (enantiopure) and can be used for asymmetric redox-active catalysis and chiral physical properties.

First, L2– was generated by dropping nBuLi in n-hexane into the ligand H2L in THF at room temperature, which was then mixed with a THF solution of [NiIICl2(2,4-lutidine)2] and stirred at 50 °C for 12 h. Crystallization by liquid–liquid diffusion of THF/hexamethyldisiloxane at room temperature afforded a racemic tetrahedral chiral-at-nickel(II) complex rac-[NiIIL(ClLi(thf)2)] (rac-1) as violet block crystals in 83% yield (Figure 2a). The resulting crystals were analyzed and characterized by elemental analysis, NMR spectroscopy, ESI-MS spectroscopy, single-crystal X-ray diffraction, and UV–vis absorption spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectrum of rac-1 in benzene-d6 showed a paramagnetic shift (Figure 2b), suggesting that rac-1 is in a high-spin state (d8, S = 1). Neither free L2– nor H2L was observed in the 1H NMR spectrum. The proton signals of the methyl group of 1,3-diketiminate at −43.3 ppm and −62.3 ppm (Hf, Hh) and the trifluoromethanesulfonanilide at 13.6 ppm and −4.0 ppm (Hβ) indicate that L2– is not dissociated from the nickel center in solution.41,42

In addition, the protons at 2.9 ppm (Hs) and 1.3 ppm (Ht) of THF are shifted upfield compared to 3.54 and 1.43 ppm of free THF, indicating that THF remains within the coordination sphere of rac-1. In the ESI-MS spectrum (Figures S5 and S7), several species of nickel complexes with L2– and Cl– coordinated to nickel ions were observed. These data suggest that rac-1 maintains its solid-state structure in solution. In the UV–vis absorption spectrum, rac-1 showed a broad absorption in 1,2-dichloroethane in the range 500 to 700 nm (Figure S4), probably due to the d–d transition of nickel ions.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction revealed that L2– coordinated tridentately to the nickel ion, forming a highly conjugated planar 6-membered chelate ring and a boat-shaped 7-membered chelate ring (Figure 3). L2– and Cl– bridged the nickel and lithium ions in a 6-membered dinuclear ring, and the two metal ions are both found to take a tetrahedral shape. To quantitatively evaluate the degree of distortion, the geometry index τ4 = [360° – (α + β)]/141° was calculated with α and β as the two largest angles.43 The value of τ4 = 0.86 for the nickel ion in rac-1 indicates that the complex has a slightly distorted tetrahedral geometry (Figure S10). Other than the stereogenic nickel center, the sulfur atom of the sulfonyl group was also a tetrahedral stereogenic center, and its absolute configuration was closely linked to the nickel center (Figure S12). Due to the electrostatic interaction between the lithium and Cl– ions, the bond between the nickel and Cl– ions was weak and easily ligand-exchanged with neutral ligands such as nitrile, alcohol, and amine species (Figures S13, S18, S20, and S21).

First, we attempted asymmetric induction based on the biased equilibrium between diastereomers by coordinating a chiral auxiliary ligand to the nickel(II) ion to obtain enantiopure complex 1. However, the ratio between diastereomers did not change significantly even in the presence of amino alcohols, which was previously effective for zinc complexes, and the asymmetric induction method remains a challenge. In examining various crystallization conditions, we found that rac-1 can be optically resolved by spontaneous resolution in a temperature-dependent manner (Figure 1). Liquid–liquid diffusion of THF/n-hexane at −18 °C yielded conglomerate crystals (Table 1, entry 1), while racemic crystals were obtained under the same conditions except at 25 °C (Table 1, entry 2). The absolute configuration of complex 1 of each conglomerate crystal was determined from crystal refinement data with Flack parameters44−48 ranging from −0.002(5) to 0.045(6) (Table S1). The appearance and crystal lattice of conglomerate and racemic crystals were very different. The space group of the conglomerate crystal is orthorhombic P212121, and only complex 1 of the same configuration is packed in a unit cell. In contrast, the space group of the racemic crystal is monoclinic P21/c, and the two enantiomers are both packed in a unit cell with one THF solvent molecule near each enantiomer (Figure 3). The bond lengths and angles between the central nickel and the other atoms were almost identical.

Next, various crystallization conditions were investigated to establish a highly selective and reproducible spontaneous resolution method (Table 1). Increasing the amount of the poor solvent n-hexane at −18 °C resulted in the formation of twin crystals in which several conglomerate crystals were aggregated (entry 3). This may be because the crystallization process was too fast; when the method was changed to slow evaporation (entry 4) and vapor diffusion (entry 5) at 25 °C, twin crystals and racemic crystals were obtained, respectively. Even when the solvent system was changed (entries 6,7), only racemic crystals were obtained. When racemic crystal seeds were added to crystallize under the condition of entry 1, racemic crystals were obtained instead, indicating that crystal nucleation is also essential for achieving spontaneous resolution. In summary, low temperature, appropriate solvent, slow crystallization process, and nucleation were considered to be the key points for achieving spontaneous resolution.

To clarify the formation process of the conglomerate crystals of complex 1, the crystal structure was carefully examined. Taking a conglomerate crystal containing only RNi-1 as an example, along the crystallographic c-axis, neighboring RNi-1 molecules interact with each other through C–H···F hydrogen bonds (DC···F = 3.275 Å, θ = 129.5°) (Figure 4a),49 forming a one-dimensional left-handed M-helix (Figure 4c). In particular, the RNi-1 molecules formed a left-handed M-helix and the SNi-1 molecules formed a right-handed P-helix with a pitch of 22.7 Å (Figures S31 and S32). Furthermore, on the diagonal of the a- and b-axes, adjacent M-helices interact with each other via C–H···O=S hydrogen bonds (DC···O = 3.279 Å, θ = 125.3°) and C–H···F hydrogen bonds (DC···F = 3.459 Å, θ = 134.5°) in a head-to-tail, antiparallel manner (Figure 4b,d, green and pink helices). In contrast, no significant interactions between adjacent parallel M-helices were observed in the b-axis direction (Figure 4d, green and blue helices). The hydrogen bonds between the helices allow the M-helix assembly to expand into a higher three-dimensional superstructure, eventually forming conglomerate crystals. On the other hand, no significant noncovalent bonding was observed in the racemic crystal structure. Considering that spontaneous resolution of complex 1 occurs only at low temperatures and that, according to the Wallach’s rule, racemic crystals are more stable in most cases,50,51 the formation of conglomerate crystals is possibly kinetically favored and that of racemic crystals is likely to be thermodynamically favored. It is noteworthy that the hydrogen bonds found in the conglomerate crystals of complex 1 are classified as relatively weak hydrogen bonds,52,53 indicating that there may be no significant difference at room temperature in the activation energies for the formation of conglomerate and racemic crystals. This explains why such a rare temperature-dependent spontaneous resolution occurs at low temperatures.

In conclusion, we have synthesized a tetrahedral d8 high-spin chiral-at-nickel(II) complex 1 using only achiral ligands and isolated each optically pure enantiomer by spontaneous resolution. The temperature dependence of the spontaneous resolution of complex 1 is a very unusual phenomenon and is presumably due to the subtle difference in activation energies to pack molecules between conglomerate and racemic crystals, resulting from relatively weak hydrogen bonds among molecules with the same absolute configuration. Starting from the achiral building blocks of nickel(II) ions and ligands, the molecular chirality of the nickel(II) complex is progressively transferred to 1D helices, 3D superstructures, and conglomerate crystals containing only one enantiomer. This chirality transfer process is closely related to the origin of homochirality54,55 in biological systems and sheds light on the concept of crystal engineering.56 Preliminary studies of configurational stability of complex 1 in solution showed that only 2% of stereoinversion occurred after 2 weeks in benzene-d6 at 19 °C (Figures S34 and S35), suggesting that optically pure complex 1 can also be used as a stable chiral source. This study expands the category of configurationally stable tetrahedral chiral-at-metal complexes with a valence-variable metal ion center. Further investigation of configurational stability, redox activity, and applications in asymmetric reactions would provide a new type of redox-active chiral source for asymmetric synthetic chemistry.
