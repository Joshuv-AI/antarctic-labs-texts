Aesthetic Deep Orbital Fat Decompression for Proptosis of Nonthyroidal Origin: A Case Series of 23 Patients
Authors: Kihei Yoshida, Tomoyuki Kashima, Ryo Kikuchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:This study sought to assess the safety, efficacy, and cosmetic outcomes of aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression for nonthyroidal proptosis.Methods:A retrospective review was conducted on 23 patients who underwent bilateral aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression at the Oculofacial Clinic Tokyo between 2022 and 2024. Preoperative and postoperative assessments included Hertel measurements, interpupillary distance, intraocular pressure, best corrected visual acuity, and fusion image area. Procedures were performed by senior attending surgeons, with outpatient follow-up conducted by attending physicians and clinical fellows.Results:The mean volume of resected fat was 3.0 ± 1.0 mL. Hertel measurements significantly decreased from 19.0 ± 2.7 to 16.7 ± 2.8 mm (P < 0.001), interpupillary distance narrowed from 66.2 ± 3.5 to 63.9 ± 3.1 mm (P < 0.001), and intraocular pressure reduced from 16.2 ± 2.9 to 14.0 ± 2.4 mm Hg (P < 0.001). Best corrected visual acuity and fusion image area remained stable. No permanent visual loss or diplopia was observed during the follow-up period. Minor complications, including transient diplopia and mild ocular discomfort, resolved spontaneously without the need for intervention.Conclusions:Aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression appeared to be a safe and effective procedure for congenital proptosis, achieving significant cosmetic and functional improvements. However, future prospective investigations are warranted to establish its long-term safety and efficacy.

Takeaways

Question: What are the safety and effectiveness of aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression in patients with nonthyroidal proptosis?

Findings: In 23 patients, aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression showed no significant complications and improved proptosis, interpalpebral distance, and intraocular pressure.

Meaning: Aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression is associated with improvements in both functional and cosmetic outcomes in patients with nonthyroidal proptosis.

INTRODUCTION

Proptosis, or forward displacement of the globe, arises from an increase in orbital content volume or an elongation of the axial length, and can be caused by a variety of conditions, including thyroid eye disease (TED), inflammatory disorders, tumors, posttraumatic hemorrhage, vascular anomalies, and congenital factors.1 Among these, congenital proptosis is not a clearly defined pathological entity but often presents as an anatomical variation due to an elongated axial length or relatively excessive orbital fat, significantly impacting patients’ aesthetic appearance and psychological well-being.2–5 Particularly among Asian populations, anatomical characteristics such as thick orbital fat, a single eyelid fold, and abundant retro-orbicularis oculi fat4,6 accentuate the appearance of prominent eyes and puffy eyelids, leading to an increasing number of young individuals seeking cosmetic correction.

Traditionally, orbital decompression surgery has been widely performed for thyroid eye disease,7–14 but its application for purely cosmetic purposes remains limited.15 Conventional decompression techniques often involve transcutaneous orbital bone removal, which carries a risk of postoperative scarring, visual dysfunction, and ocular motility disturbances.8 In contrast, transconjunctival deep orbital fat decompression offers cosmetic benefits with reduced postoperative discomfort, providing a less invasive and more aesthetically favorable alternative, particularly suited for cosmetic enhancement.16,17 However, there is a notable lack of studies evaluating the effectiveness and safety of aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression specifically for congenital proptosis, especially those using a minimally invasive approach without bone removal.

In this study, we retrospectively reviewed the clinical outcomes of aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression performed for nonthyroidal proptosis at the Oculofacial Clinic Tokyo between 2022 and 2024. This procedure involves selective removal of deep orbital fat through a transconjunctival approach, aiming to achieve globe recession, reduction of eyelid puffiness, narrowing of the interpupillary distance (IPD), and a lowering of intraocular pressure (IOP), thereby offering both cosmetic and functional improvements. The aims of this study were to demonstrate the safety, efficacy, and predictability of this minimally invasive technique and to propose it as a novel therapeutic option in the field of cosmetic orbital surgery.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

This was a retrospective case series conducted at Oculofacial Clinic Tokyo. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

From 2022 to 2024, 23 patients with congenital proptosis underwent bilateral deep orbital fat decompression at the Oculofacial Clinic Tokyo in Japan. They were followed up for at least 3 months and evaluated retrospectively. Patients with TED or previous orbital decompression surgery were excluded. All patients underwent preoperative orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to confirm the absence of inflammation or other orbital diseases, and thyroid markers (free triiodothyronine, free thyroxine, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor antibody) were confirmed to be within normal levels through blood tests.

Surgical Team and Outpatient Care

All surgical procedures were performed by 6 senior attending surgeons specialized in oculoplastic and orbital surgery at the Oculofacial Clinic Tokyo. Preoperative and postoperative outpatient evaluations were conducted by senior surgeons and clinical fellows on a rotating basis. Each patient’s clinical course, including imaging assessments, visual function testing, and postoperative follow-up, was reviewed and supervised by attending physicians to ensure consistency and quality of care.

Examination Items

Refraction, axial length, best corrected visual acuity, IOP, Hertel measurement, IPD, and the fusion image area (FIA) examined by binocular single vision test were measured preoperatively and at 1 and 3 months postoperatively. Refraction and IOP were measured using TONOREFⅢ (Topcon, Tokyo, Japan). Eye axial length was measured via MRI. The extracted fat was also weighed. Visual acuity was converted from decimal to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution units. The FIA was measured using the Goldmann perimeter to identify the positions where diplopia occurs, and the average angles for the 4 directions (0, 90, 180, and 270 degrees) were calculated. Under normal conditions, vision extends 50 degrees in each of the 4 directions—up, down, left, and right—and this average was considered a score of 50 degrees. When eye movement disorders or diplopia occurred, this value decreased. The amount of Hertel change per 1 mL of fat was calculated for each orbit individually by dividing the change in Hertel measurements from preoperative to 3-month postoperative values by the amount of fat removed. The IPD change per 1 mL of fat was calculated for each patient by dividing the change in IPD from preoperative to 3 months postoperative values by the average amount of fat removed from both eyes.

Surgical Procedure

All procedures were performed as outpatient procedures under general anesthesia using a microscope (M320; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The surgical technique was adapted from our usual orbital fat decompression surgery for thyroid eye disease. Because these procedures were performed for aesthetic purposes, preoperative consultations were conducted to discuss the desired degree of globe recession. Based on these discussions, along with facial photographs and orbital MRI scans, the amount of fat to be removed was determined preoperatively.

The surgery started with the downward retraction of the lower eyelid using a flat hook, and the lower eyelid conjunctiva was horizontally incised with spring scissors, down to the level of the lower eyelid retractor. (See Video 1 [online], which demonstrates conjunctival incision of the lower eyelid [right eye] using spring scissors down to the lower eyelid retractor. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia and is shown at 2× speed.) The inferolateral orbital fat capsule was opened with scissors to expose the superficial orbital fat. The fat was then gently removed with scissors, taking care to preserve the capsule.

The deeper inferior lateral orbital fat was accessed by bluntly dissecting the orbital septum with malleable retractors. Deep extraconal and intraconal fat was carefully excised using toothless forceps, taking care to avoid excessive resection and vascular injury. (See Video 2 [online], which shows the excision of inferolateral orbital fat, including both superficial and deep compartments.) Particular care was taken to avoid excessive fat removal that could result in a sunken appearance. Superficial orbital fat excision was kept minimal, and the majority of decompression was achieved through removal of deep fat. Additionally, fat removal around the inferior rectus muscle compartment was performed with caution to prevent postoperative inferior displacement of the globe.

The process was repeated for the inferomedial orbital fat. Following the removal of superficial orbital fat, the fat beneath the lacrimal caruncle was methodically excised. The deep inferomedial extraconal and intraconal fat was removed in the same manner as the inferolateral fat. (See Video 3 [online], which shows the removal of fat beneath the lacrimal caruncle followed by deeper fat excision.) The removed fat was placed into the outer sheath of an empty syringe after the blood was blotted with gauze and the volume was measured. Once the predetermined amount of fat had been removed, the conjunctiva was left unsutured, ensuring no epithelial entrapment, and was returned to its original position (Fig. 1).

Analysis

The patients’ frontal facial photographs were imported into ImageJ, and the corneal diameter (white-to-white) was set at 12 mm.18,19 The IPD was then measured using image analysis.

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel. The paired t test was used to compare the parameters before and after treatment. A value of P less than 0.05 with 95% confidence interval was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

The mean age of patients was 30.3 (range, 18–53) years. Among the 23 patients, 10 (43.5%) were men and 13 (56.5%) were women. The mean refraction was -5.0 ± 4.4 diopters. One patient (4.4%) was emmetropic, whereas the other 22 (95.6%) patients were myopic. The mean axial length measured by MRI was 24.9 ± 1.5 mm. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the surgical and functional outcomes of aesthetic orbital fat decompression. *Statistically significant, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E384.)

Surgical Outcomes

The mean amount of fat removed was 3.0 ± 1.0 mL. The average amount removed from the inferonasal side was 1.4 ± 0.6 mL, whereas the average amount removed from the inferotemporal side was 1.6 ± 0.5 mL.

During 3 months, the mean Hertel measurement decreased from 19.0 ± 2.7 to 16.7 ± 2.8 mm. The decrease was statistically significant (P < 0.001). The amount of Hertel change per 1 mL of fat was 0.9 ± 0.7 mm. Mean IPD reduced from 66.2 ± 3.5 to 63.9 ± 3.1 mm. Significant differences were observed in IPD reduction (P < 0.001). For every 1 mL of fat removed from both eyes, the IPD decreased by 0.8 ± 0.4 mm. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E384.) A representative case is shown in Figure 2. In this patient, a total of 4.2 mL of fat was removed from each eye, resulting in a reduction in Hertel measurements from 23.0 to 20.0 mm bilaterally and a narrowing of IPD from 62.7 to 60.5 mm. The FIA remained stable at 50 degrees, and no diplopia was observed.

Functional Assessments

The mean best corrected visual acuity at baseline and at 1 and 3 months was −0.06 ± 0.04, −0.05 ± 0.05, and −0.06 ± 0.04 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution, respectively. There were no statistically significant differences between baseline and 1 month (P = 0.62) or between baseline and 3 months (P = 0.74). Mean IOP measurements at baseline, 1 month, and 3 months were 16.2 ± 2.9, 14.4 ± 2.5, and 14.0 ± 2.4 mm Hg, respectively. The reduction in IOP was significant at both 1 and 3 months postoperatively compared with baseline (P < 0.001). The mean FIA at baseline and at 1 and 3 months was 49.8 ± 0.8, 48.3 ± 3.9, and 49.0 ± 2.8 degrees, respectively. The difference was statistically significant between baseline and 1 month (P = 0.049), but not between baseline and 3 months (P = 0.069). (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E384.) No postoperative visual or visual field deficits were observed. Figure 3 illustrates another case in which 5.8 mL of fat was removed from each eye, leading to a decrease in Hertel measurements from 18.0 to 14.0 mm and a reduction in IPD from 65.8 to 62.8 mm. Although slight exotropia was noted in upward gaze postoperatively, the patient had no diplopia in the primary position.

Complications

The observed complications included eye pain in 2 (8.7%) patients, transient diplopia in 4 (17.4%) patients, and abnormal cheek sensation in 1 (4.3%) patient, all of which resolved during the follow-up period (Table 1). Other complications reported after decompression surgery, such as retrobulbar hemorrhage, blindness, and infection, were not observed in any of the patients.

DISCUSSION

The anatomical characteristics often seen in Asian patients, such as thick preseptal fat and prominent eyes, may increase demand for orbital aesthetic procedures.4,6 In this context, deep orbital fat decompression can serve as an effective option not only for Asian patients but also for individuals of other ethnicities seeking globe recession and cosmetic refinement (Figs. 2, 3).

Orbital decompression surgery has been developed for the treatment of exophthalmos caused by thyroid eye disease.8–14 This procedure has numerous variations, with different targets including superior, inferior, lateral, and medial orbital bones, and different approaches including transcutaneous and transconjunctival techniques. Additionally, it involves both fat decompression20–22 and bone decompression techniques.8 By avoiding skin incisions, the transconjunctival inferior orbital fat decompression we performed offered the advantages of a shorter recovery time, no visible scars, and the flexibility to make precise adjustments to the degree of globe recession.

To further enhance decompression and achieve a more natural aesthetic outcome, superomedial orbital fat excision was selectively incorporated into the procedure in later cases. This modification allowed for more balanced recession without increasing the risk of postoperative complications, although additional studies are needed to validate its long-term benefits.

In orbital fat decompression for TED, it has been reported that the eyeball typically moves backward by about 1 mm for each 1 mL of fat removed.22–24 In this study, we found that in orbital fat decompression for congenital proptosis, removal of 1 mL of fat led to a 0.9 ± 0.7 mm recession of the eye. These variations may be due to differences in pathologies. In TED, there is an increase in fat, resulting in ocular protrusion, whereas with the congenital ocular protrusion in this study, there is no fat gain but rather a long eye axis similar to that of myopic eyes. Because there was no fat gain, it was thought that the fat would be taken from a deeper site, the orbital apex, resulting in a greater globe recession with a smaller amount of fat resection.

There were previous reports indicating that orbital fat decompression for TED lowered IOP.8,25 This is thought to be due to the decrease in intraorbital pressure resulting from the reduction of increased intraorbital tissue. This study also found that, even in cases of congenital ocular protrusion, which, unlike thyroid eye disease, does not involve increased intraorbital pressure, a decrease in IOP was also observed.

This study showed that removing 1 mL of fat from each eye reduced the IPD by 0.8 ± 0.4 mm. There were no previous reports specifically addressing orbital fat decompression and changes in IPD for congenital proptosis, but the results were similar to those reported for TED.26

In this study, more than 95% of the patients who requested surgery were myopic. In addition to the Asian eyelid anatomy, the elongated axial length caused by myopia may have contributed to eyelid protrusion, which likely motivated them to seek treatment. Because the possibility of visual dysfunction after surgery cannot be ruled out, the indication for this procedure should be strictly determined. However, it can be effective for patients with proptosis or wide-set eyes with increased IPD and should be considered as a treatment option.

In cases where aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression alone is insufficient to achieve the desired degree of globe recession, orbital bone decompression may be considered as an additional procedure. Careful preoperative evaluation and patient counseling are essential to determine the most appropriate surgical approach.

Limitations

The procedures were performed by a total of 6 attending surgeons at our institution, and the analysis was not conducted by a single surgeon. The Hertel measurements were not performed by the same examiner, which may have introduced measurement errors, potentially resulting in discrepancies of up to 2 mm.27,28 Myopia was determined solely by refraction testing, and the axial length was measured using MRI, which served as a reference value. The sample size was small, and long-term follow-up was not conducted; thus, the long-term prognosis remains unknown.

CONCLUSIONS

In this retrospective case series, aesthetic deep orbital fat decompression for nonthyroidal proptosis appeared to be a safe, effective, and predictable procedure, resulting in globe recession, reduced eyelid puffiness, decreased IPD, and lowered IOP. However, given the limitations of this study—including the small sample size, short follow-up period, and retrospective design—further large-scale prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings and to establish the long-term safety and efficacy of this technique.
