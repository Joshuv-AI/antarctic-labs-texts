Combined Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction With Bone‐Patellar Tendon‐Bone Autograft and Anatomical Lateral Collateral Ligament and Proximal Tibiofibular Joint Reconstruction Using Semitendinosus Allograft
Authors: Ryan J. Froom, Alexi J. Panos, Nate J. Dickinson, Molly E. Kessenich, Matthew T. Provencher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiligamentous knee injuries involving anterior cruciate ligament rupture and lateral‐sided instability, including proximal tibiofibular joint instability and lateral collateral ligament deficiency, present unique challenges in restoring knee stability. This Technical Note describes a reproducible surgical technique for anatomic reconstruction of the anterior cruciate ligament using a bone‐patellar tendon‐bone autograft and combined lateral collateral ligament and proximal tibiofibular joint reconstruction using a single semitendinosus allograft. Emphasis is placed on proper graft orientation, tunnel placement, and sequential fixation to restore stability in both anterior‐posterior and medial‐lateral planes of the knee.

This technique video describes the treatment of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and combined lateral collateral ligament (LCL) and proximal tibiofibular joint (PTFJ) reconstruction using a single semitendinosus allograft. Our patient is a 25 year old female who sustained an acute injury skiing with accompanying knee pain and swelling. She failed non‐operative treatment. Preoperative imaging showed torn ACL, LCL and damaged PTFJ. Post injury the patient experienced immediate pain and swelling. Both Lackman and Pivot Shift were positive on examination. The patient was also varus positive at 0° and 30° with increased anterior translation of PTFJ. The anterolateral fibular head is palpated to identify the LCL insertion. The lateral head of the gastrocnemius is elevated off the posterolateral fibular head. A 6 mm tunnel is drilled from anterior to posterior across the fibular head and a suture passer is inserted to shuttle a passing stitch. An additional tibial tunnel was made on the anterolateral flat portion of the tibia just anterior and superior to Gerdy's tubercle for the PTFJ reconstruction, protecting the neurovascular structures during drilling and reaming. A passing wire is used to stage the passing suture. After all tunnels for the LCL and PTFJ are drilled and passing sutures are placed, attention is turned to the ACL reconstruction portion of the case prior to LCL and PTFJ fixation. The ACL bone‐patellar tendon‐bone autograft harvest and ACL reconstruction are done in a standard anatomical fashion as previously described in the literature. After the ACL reconstruction is complete, attention is turn back to the LCL and PTFJ fixation. A semitendinosus allograft is prepared by placing Krakow sutures at both ends. One end of the graft is anchored into the femoral tunnel and secured in place with a 7 by 23 mm PEEK interference screw. Next, the graft is carefully passed beneath the iliotibial band and biceps femoris and then threaded through the fibular head from anterior to posterior. With the knee bent at 70°, the graft is fixed in the fibular head using another 7 by 23 mm PEEK interference screw. The remaining portion of the graft is then brought from posterior to anterior through the previously drilled tibial tunnel of the PTFJ. With the knee positioned at 30° of flexion and in neutral rotation, tension is applied to the graft while the fibular head is manually held in place. An 8 by 30 mm BioComposite interference screw is then used to secure the graft, completing the stabilization of the PTFJ. Finally, any excess graft is trimmed away. Multiligamentous knee injuries involving anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture, lateral collateral ligament (LCL) deficiency, and proximal tibiofibular joint (PTFJ) instability represent a complex clinical challenge, particularly in high‐level athletes following traumatic knee injuries. The PTFJ serves as a critical stabilizer of the lateral knee, functioning as a sliding articulation connecting the lateral tibial condyle and fibular head (FH). Although historically considered uncommon and frequently overlooked, recent literature has emphasized that PTFJ instability is a notable and often misdiagnosed source of lateral‐sided knee pain and mechanical dysfunction. 1 , 2

Jabara et al. highlighted the clinical significance of the PTFJ by estimating a 9% incidence of instability among patients sustaining multiligament knee injuries. 3 Further, symptomatic PTFJ instability has been shown to respond more favorably to surgical reconstruction compared to conservative, nonoperative approaches, which frequently fail to provide lasting symptomatic relief. 4 , 5 Surgical stabilization of the lateral knee, including anatomic reconstruction of the LCL, contributes inherent stability to the PTFJ, addressing both medial‐lateral and anteroposterior instability. 6 , 7 , 8 Thus, comprehensive, multiplanar surgical reconstruction becomes critical in patients seeking to restore knee stability, alleviate symptoms, and ultimately improve quality of life and return to pre‐injury activity levels.

Currently, few techniques comprehensively address concurrent ACL insufficiency alongside lateral knee complex injuries involving both the LCL and PTFJ. Combining an ACL reconstruction using bone‐patellar tendon‐bone (BPTB) autograft with simultaneous anatomical reconstruction of the LCL and PTFJ using a single semitendinosus allograft has the potential to address multiplanar instability effectively and efficiently.

This article describes a reproducible, anatomical surgical technique using a BPTB autograft for robust ACL reconstruction, along with simultaneous anatomical reconstruction of the LCL and PTFJ using a single semitendinosus allograft. Emphasis is placed on correct graft orientation, precise tunnel placement, and strategic sequencing of graft fixation to achieve restoration of stability in the anterior‐posterior, medial‐lateral, and rotational planes of the knee (Video 1).

surgical technique

Preoperative Evaluation

A thorough clinical examination is performed to assess the extent of ligamentous instability. Preoperative imaging confirms ACL rupture and suggests lateral‐sided injury and PTFJ instability (Figure 1). Examination under anesthesia is used to evaluate pivot shift, Lachman, varus stress, and anteroposterior instability of the PTFJ. Indications for surgery include symptomatic instability, high activity level, and failure of conservative management. The patient is counseled on risks, benefits, and surgical expectations.

Right knee sagittal and coronal MRI showing an ACL and LCL tear with proximal tibiofibular joint disruption. Green arrow points to LCL tear; blue arrow points to ACL tear. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; LCL, lateral collateral ligament.)

Patient Positioning and Preparation

The patient is positioned supine with a lateral post, and general anesthesia is administered with optional regional nerve blocks, including a sciatic indwelling catheter that is placed dry in the preoperative holding area, allowing for adequate examination of the peroneal nerve postoperatively and subsequent dosing thereafter. Prophylactic IV antibiotics are administered. A well‐padded thigh tourniquet is applied and anatomical landmarks are identified, including Gerdy's tubercle, the FH, the lateral joint line, lateral epicondyle of the femur, and the posterior apex of the lateral femoral condyle. The leg is exsanguinated, and a thigh tourniquet is inflated.

Lateral Exposure and Peroneal Nerve Neurolysis

The knee is flexed to 90°, and a lateral curvilinear incision is made from the lateral epicondyle extending distally toward Gerdy's tubercle and FH. Skin flaps are developed and elevated in all directions. Dissection proceeds through the interval between the iliotibial band (ITB) and biceps femoris (BF), taking care to identify the common peroneal nerve as it emerges posterior and deep to the BF across the proximal fibular neck. A comprehensive neurolysis is performed distally by releasing the overlying fascia of the lateral compartment, and proximally beneath the BF. A vessel loop is gently placed around the nerve to maintain visibility while preventing traction.

Anatomic LCL and PTFJ Reconstruction

The native LCL is identified anterior to the BF and posterior to the ITB and tagged with a #2 FiberWire (Arthrex, Naples, FL). Tension on the tag shows LCL laxity and FH mobility. The femoral LCL origin is marked at the lateral epicondyle. A 2 cm ITB split is made, and a Beath pin is drilled in an anteromedial trajectory and overreamed with a 6 × 30 mm reamer (Figure 2). A passing suture is placed.

A right knee via a lateral incision in the supine position with the knee flexed to 90°, the dLCL is identified in the interval between the ITB and biceps femoris. A tag/traction suture is placed in the native dLCL. A Beath pin is drilled in an anteromedial trajectory at the proximal origin of the LCL (pLCL) for the reconstruction. (dLCL, distal lateral collateral ligament; ITB, iliotibial band; GT, Gerdy's tubercle, pLCL, proximal origin of the LCL.)

The anterolateral FH is palpated to identify the LCL insertion. The lateral head of the gastrocnemius is elevated off the posterolateral FH. A 6‐mm tunnel is drilled from anterior to posterior across the FH, and a suture passer is inserted to shuttle a passing stitch (Figure 3). Hemostats are used to create a graft passage channel under the BF and ITB.

The LG is elevated off the posterolateral FH. A 6 mm tunnel is drilled from anterior to posterior across the FH, and a suture passer is inserted to shuttle a passing stitch. (FJ, fibular head; LG, lateral head of the gastrocnemius.)

An additional tibial tunnel was made on the anterolateral flat portion of the tibia, anterior to Gerdy's tubercle in a bicortical fashion for the additional tibiofibular joint reconstruction (Figures 4 and 5). Care is taken to protect the neurovascular structures during drilling and reaming and a stay suture is passed in a similar fashion as for the LCL reconstruction for later graft passage and fixation. Before proceeding with graft tensioning, attention should be turned to the ACLR and knee arthroscopy.

ACL BPTB Autograft Harvest and Arthroscopy

A midline anterior incision is made to harvest a 10 mm central third of the patellar tendon, including 20 and 25 mm bone plugs from the patella and tibia, respectively. Bone plugs are shaped and drilled. Diagnostic arthroscopy is performed through anteromedial and anterolateral portals. The notch is debrided and the ACL stump is removed. Meniscal and chondral surfaces are assessed and addressed as needed.

ACL Reconstruction

A retrograde 10 mm femoral socket is drilled using a FlipCutter (Arthrex, Naples, FL). A tibial tunnel is drilled at the ACL footprint. The BPTB graft is passed in retrograde fashion, and the femoral plug is secured using an 8 × 20 mm titanium interference screw (Arthrex, Naples, FL). Tibial fixation is achieved with a 9 × 20 mm screw and remaining graft sutures secured with a 5.5 mm PEEK SwiveLock anchor (Arthrex, Naples, FL).

LCL and PTFJ Graft Fixation

A semitendinosus allograft is prepared with Krakow sutures in each end (Figure 6). One limb is docked in the femoral tunnel and secured with a 7 × 23 mm PEEK interference screw (Arthrex, Naples, FL). The graft is passed under the ITB and BF and then through the FH from anterior to posterior (Figure 7). With the knee at 70° of flexion, valgus stress applied, and tibia internally rotated, it is fixed in the FH using a 7 × 23 mm PEEK interference screw (Arthrex, Naples, FL). The residual graft is passed posterior to anterior through the previously drilled PTFJ tibial tunnel. With the knee at 30° flexion and neutral rotation, anterior tension is applied to the graft while the FH is manually reduced. An 8 × 30 mm BioComposite interference screw (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is inserted, completing PTFJ stabilization (Figure 8).

Closure and Postoperative Protocol

Autograft bone from the BPTB harvest and tibial tunnel reaming is placed into harvest sites. Standard layered closure is performed. PRP may be injected intra‐articularly. A hinged knee brace is applied in full extension. Postoperatively, the patient remains toe‐touch weightbearing for 4 to 6 weeks with controlled range of motion from 0° to 90°. Progressive rehabilitation includes passive motion, strength, and neuromuscular control. Return to play is expected at 9 months.

discussion

Combined ACL, LCL, and PTFJ instability represents a severe multiligament knee injury requiring careful surgical reconstruction to achieve optimal functional outcomes. This Technical Note details a reproducible surgical approach for anatomical reconstruction, integrating robust ACL stabilization using BPTB autograft with comprehensive lateral stabilization via an LCL and PTFJ reconstruction using a single semitendinosus allograft.

The use of BPTB autograft for ACL reconstruction provides reliable bone‐to‐bone healing, superior fixation strength, and proven long‐term outcomes compared with soft‐tissue grafts. 8 Concurrent LCL and PTFJ reconstruction using semitendinosus allograft addresses multiplanar lateral instability, reducing persistent lateral knee symptoms and functional impairment that frequently accompany isolated ACL procedures. 4 , 7 , 9 Importantly, this comprehensive reconstruction is essential to prevent postoperative complications such as residual instability, chronic lateral knee pain, and secondary osteoarthritis. 5 , 7

Nonetheless, the described technique is not without limitations (Tables 1 and 2). It requires extensive surgical exposure, meticulous peroneal nerve dissection, and precise anatomical knowledge of the lateral knee structures. Furthermore, the additional graft material and fixation hardware increase the cost and complexity of the procedure compared with isolated ligament reconstructions or suture button techniques. 10 , 11 However, we assert the advantages in achieving complete knee stability significantly outweigh these considerations.
