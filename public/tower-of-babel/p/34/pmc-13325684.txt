Clinically Relevant Bleeding in Individuals With Cancer: Insights From a Nationwide Cohort Study
Authors: Ming Y. Lim, Radhika Gangaraju, Omid Jafari, Zihan Yang, Joyce W. T. Tiong, Elizabeth C. L. Chiang, Shengling Ma, Jun Y. Jiang, Justine Ryu, Barbara D. Lam, Mrinal Ranjan, Ang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer care is often complicated by coagulopathy leading to thrombosis and bleeding. While venous thromboembolism (VTE) has been extensively studied, bleeding remains an underestimated threat. To address this knowledge gap, we leveraged the Epic Cosmos database to determine the impact of cancer‐associated clinically relevant bleeding (CRB) in 2 455 332 individuals with incident cancer encounters from 215 US health systems between 2018 and 2024. In the 12 months leading up to the cancer diagnosis, the period prevalence of CRB was 5.3% in the overall cohort, highlighting CRB as a common early presentation preceding cancer diagnosis. In the 12‐month window after cancer diagnosis, the cumulative incidence of CRB was 6.3% in the overall cohort, 7.0% in those receiving systemic antineoplastic therapy, and up to 15.0% among individuals on certain baseline anticoagulants. In addition to age, sex, and race, factors appreciably associated with CRB on multivariable analysis included cancer types, metastatic disease, systemic therapy, anticoagulant or antiplatelet use, higher comorbidity index, recent hospitalization, recent history of bleeding or VTE, and selective laboratory features (hemoglobin, platelets, albumin, eGFR). Furthermore, the onset of CRB as a time varying covariate was independently associated with mortality in multivariable adjusted analysis: intracranial hemorrhage (HR 4.17, 95% CI 4.11–4.24); gastrointestinal bleeding (HR 2.52, 95% CI 2.50–2.55); other bleeding (HR 2.46, 95% CI 2.44–2.49). Together, these findings reveal a substantial and overlooked risk of bleeding in cancer care and underscore a need for validated bleeding risk models that can be integrated alongside VTE prediction tools to guide personalized thromboprophylaxis decisions.

Introduction

Individuals with cancer are predisposed to venous thromboembolism (VTE) and bleeding complications due to a complex derangement in coagulation [1, 2, 3, 4]. The risk of cancer‐associated VTE (CAT) has been well characterized [5], with a 12‐month incidence ranging from 3.4% to 4.5% [6, 7, 8]. Several validated clinical risk assessment models were developed incorporating cancer type, stage, and individual patient characteristics, of which the Khorana score remains the most widely adopted and validated [9, 10, 11, 12]. Clinical trials have further demonstrated that thromboprophylaxis can reduce CAT risk in selective high‐risk individuals with cancer, leading to its recommendation in treatment guidelines [13, 14]. Despite these recommendations, implementation remains challenging due to concerns about bleeding risk inherent to this population [15, 16]. While both CAT and bleeding are associated with increased morbidity and mortality [17, 18, 19], there is limited understanding of the epidemiology and risk factors for bleeding in individuals with cancer.

Most contemporary studies reporting bleeding risk in individuals with cancer included those taking anticoagulation for management of CAT [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Consequently, current estimates of bleeding incidence and risk in individuals with different cancer types at the initial time of diagnosis are lacking. There are some data from the placebo arms of ambulatory thromboprophylaxis clinical trials or from prospective studies of individuals with advanced cancer receiving systemic therapy [28, 29, 30, 31]. A meta‐analysis of thromboprophylaxis trials using low‐molecular‐weight heparin (LMWH) in unselected individuals with cancer reported a clinically relevant bleeding (CRB) rate of 2.4% in the placebo arm at a median follow‐up of 10 months [32]. A subsequent meta‐analysis of thromboprophylaxis trials using direct oral anticoagulants in individuals with cancer with a Khorana score ≥ 2 found a CRB rate of 4.3% in the placebo arm at 6 months [31]. In contrast, a single‐center prospective study that included 791 individuals with advanced cancer observed a significantly higher CRB incidence of 9.8% at 6 months [30]. These disparate estimates highlight the need for large‐scale epidemiological studies that are powered to differentiate distinct clinical risk predictors among different cancer types in diverse populations. Understanding the epidemiology and risk factors for bleeding in individuals with cancer will provide opportunities to develop risk assessment tools for bleeding and pave the way for precision medicine approaches in selecting appropriate individuals for thromboprophylaxis.

In this study, we investigated the incidence, patterns, and risk factors for cancer‐associated bleeding and subsequent mortality in individuals with newly diagnosed cancer in a nationwide, contemporary electronic health record (EHR) cohort representative of the United States (US) population.

Methods

Data Source

Study data came from Epic Cosmos, a collaborative dataset from Epic health systems with more than 300 million patients from more than 300 unique health systems. The community represents patients from all 50 states, D.C., and selective sites in Canada, Lebanon, and Saudi Arabia. The current count values for patients, hospitals, and clinics are available on https://cosmos.epic.com/. The study was deemed non‐human participants research by the institutional review board at Baylor College of Medicine.

To ensure each patient was counted only once across different health systems, longitudinal patient‐level data from different encounters were linked and deduplicated through the Care Everywhere network. Data from Epic Cosmos are updated every 2 weeks to create the Expertly Determined De‐Identified (EDDI) Data Set, a limited data set that has been curated, mapped to standard codes, and deidentified with shifted dates and unstructured text removed. For this study, we used the EDDI Data Set refreshed on December 30, 2025 limited to participants residing within the US.

Participants

Figure 1 illustrates the cohort selection process. Because the number of sites joining the Epic health system community is constantly increasing, we only included individuals from sites that had contributed complete, continuous, and cancer‐relevant EHR data.

Flow diagram on study cohort selection from Epic Cosmos database.

From eligible sites, we used invasive primary cancer International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) codes in the final billing or encounter diagnosis tables to define newly diagnosed cancer from January 1, 2018 to December 31, 2024. Specifically, we required two or more codes > 30 days apart in pre‐specified non–primary care face‐to‐face (F2F) encounters (details in Table S1). Data capture for lookback and follow‐up windows extended 1 year before and after the eligible cancer cohort (January 1, 2017 to December 30, 2025). Exclusion criteria included the presence of primary or secondary cancer diagnosis encounter or problem > 90 days before first cancer diagnosis, the receipt of systemic antineoplastic therapy before first cancer diagnosis, no F2F encounter in the 12 months prior to cancer diagnosis, no F2F follow‐up beyond the first month after cancer diagnosis unless early event or death, pre‐malignant or in situ cancer stage, or age < 21 (Figure 1).

The overall cohort was the primary analytic cohort meeting all previous inclusion and exclusion criteria (index date = time of first cancer diagnosis encounter). To assess if the CRB incidence was different among those receiving systemic therapy, we created a sub‐cohort of treated individuals who received early systemic therapy within 90 days of diagnosis (index date = time of first systemic therapy encounter).

Outcomes

Primary outcome included incident CRB, defined as the first occurrence of either major bleeding (MB) or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (CRNMB), unrelated to surgeries or procedures, using a validated ICD‐10‐CM phenotyping algorithm using the final billing diagnosis code from either inpatient admission (any position) or emergency department (primary position) encounters [33]. To ensure accuracy of this algorithm, we performed a systematic review of peer‐reviewed manuscripts on ICD‐10‐CM bleeding codes and identified 545 non‐duplicated bleeding codes from six relevant studies [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. Two physicians independently reviewed and removed ambiguous codes, including all the post‐surgical and post‐procedural bleeding or anemia codes, resulting in a final list of 280 bleeding codes (Table S2). We then performed an adjudication study using 400 individuals with cancer at a single Epic health system site, where the precision (positive predictive value) and recall sensitivity of the ICD‐10‐CM algorithm were 92% and 85%, respectively, for CRB within 1 year (Table S3) [33].

Secondary outcomes included intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, and all‐cause mortality. The death date and last‐known follow‐up date were extracted directly from Epic Cosmos without additional linkage to external databases. Individuals were followed from the index date until the first outcome event, the censor date (last F2F encounter before an encounter gap of more than 6 months), death (if occurring no later than 3 months after the censor date), or December 30, 2025.

Exposure Variables

Baseline individual‐ and cancer‐specific data were determined using variable lookback windows (Table S4). The following baseline data were extracted: Age; sex; race; ethnicity; Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) code; Social Vulnerability Index (SVI); cancer type (based on 27 consolidated types); cancer stage; systemic therapy types within 90 days of diagnosis (classified based on the First Databank drug database and subdivided into categories: cytotoxic chemotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitor, targeted therapy, or endocrine therapy); components from the National Cancer Institute Comorbidity Index (NCI‐CI) [40]; body mass index (BMI); recent hospitalization of > 3 days; history of VTE, arterial thromboembolism, and bleeding; and existing active prescription of anticoagulation, antiplatelet, or strong CYP3A4‐inducing or ‐inhibiting medications. The following laboratory data were also extracted using Logical Observation Identifiers, Names, and Codes (LOINC): white blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, alanine transaminase, total bilirubin, albumin, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) based on the Cockcroft‐Gault formula. Missing data within laboratory values were coded as unique features, and no imputation was performed. Due to significant missingness in documented cancer stage, we defined metastatic disease using ICD‐10‐CM codes (C78.%, C79.%, C80.%, C7B.%).

Statistical Analysis

Cumulative incidence of CRB, ICH, and GIB was estimated using competing risk analysis to account for early cancer‐related mortality. The cumulative incidence of each outcome at 12 months was compared across all study years and by each of the 27 distinct cancer types, in both the overall cohort and the sub‐cohort of individuals receiving early systemic therapy. The outcome incidence was also compared by active anticoagulant prescription at the time of cancer diagnosis. The median follow‐up time was determined by the reverse Kaplan Meier method.

To determine early etiologic risk factors for CRB, we assessed associations for all baseline clinical variables. To select parsimonious features while preventing model overfitting, we used Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) in the glmnet package of R to fit a Cox proportional hazards model with an L1 penalty, where the optimal penalty parameter, λ, was selected based on cross‐validation to maximize the concordance index within 1 standard deviation along with clinical relevance (Table S5). Cancer type, anticoagulant, and antiplatelet were locked in during the feature selection process (no penalization). The features that remained in the best model were then used to fit a final cause‐specific multivariable Cox regression with a robust variance estimator while treating systemic therapy initiation within 12 months as a time‐varying covariate. To test the final model's generalizability, we created simulated datasets using published summary data from the AVERT/CASSINI placebo arms [28, 29] (n = 679) and the CAT‐BLED study [30] (n = 791) (Table S6). We then applied the final multivariable model built from the early treatment sub‐cohort to generate the predicted versus reported 6‐ and 12‐month incidence from each simulated dataset.

To determine the association between the onset of major subtypes/location of CRB and mortality, we employed a similar variable selection and modeling strategy to that above, except with death as the new outcome variable and locking in cancer type, metastatic disease, and systemic therapy as unpenalized covariates. The final multivariable Cox regression model included both cancer‐specific risk factors at the index date and discrete CRB onsets as time‐varying covariates. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Participants

Our final cohort for analysis included 2 455 332 individuals with a new cancer diagnosis from 215 health systems in all 50 states of the US between 2018 and 2024 (Figure 1). Characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1 and Table S7. The geographic distribution of our cancer cohort was similar to the population derived from the US census (Figure S1). The median age at cancer diagnosis was 67 years (interquartile range [IQR], 58–74), and 47.4% were male. The race and ethnicity breakdown was 72.6% White, 11.7% Black, 3.2% Asian or Pacific Islander, and 5.4% Hispanic. Most (80.9%) of the cohort lived in a metropolitan area. The most frequent cancers were breast (20.2%), prostate (14.8%), and lung (10.8%); 19.1% had metastasis; and 34.3% received systemic antineoplastic therapy within 90 days of cancer diagnosis (842 789 individuals in this sub‐cohort). Only 7.6% had an active anticoagulant prescription at the time of cancer diagnosis with 4.0% on apixaban, 1.5% on rivaroxaban, 1.2% on warfarin, 0.8% on enoxaparin, and 0.1% on dabigatran and others.

Prevalence and Incidence of Clinically Relevant Bleeding

The period prevalence for CRB within 6 and 12 months on or before cancer diagnosis was 4.8% and 5.3%, respectively, of which 2.4% occurred concurrently at diagnosis (Table S8). Cancer types with a high proportion of bleeding on or before diagnosis included acute leukemia, cervical, bladder, uterine, upper GI, colorectal, liver, and kidney cancers.

The median follow‐up was 605 days for CRB assessment. The cumulative incidence of CRB at 6 and 12 months after cancer diagnosis was 4.4% and 6.3%, respectively (Figure 2, Table 2). Among the sub‐cohort of individuals receiving early systemic therapy, the cumulative incidence of CRB was marginally higher (4.8% at 6 months, 7.0% at 12 months). The annual incidence of CRB remained similar throughout the study period (Figure S2). Among all subtypes, GIB was most frequent (3.0% at 12 months) while ICH was relatively rare (0.7% at 12 months).

When assessing the 12‐month incidence of CRB by cancer type in the overall cohort (Figure 3), individuals with acute leukemia (25.3%), liver (15.4%), and gastric/esophageal (14.9%) had the highest CRB, while those with thyroid (1.5%), breast (2.0%), and prostate (3.0%) had the lowest (Figure 3). The subsets of individuals with GI bleeding and ICH followed similar patterns, except that GI bleeding was more frequent among GI malignancies whereas ICH disproportionately affected those with brain cancers and acute leukemias (Figures S3–S4). The sub‐cohort of individuals receiving early systemic therapy mostly followed similar trends as the overall cohort, although excessive CRB risks were observed in advanced non‐prostate genitourinary, sarcoma, melanoma, and thyroid cancers.

As an exploratory analysis, we examined the incidence of CRB in individuals with and without active anticoagulant prescription at the time of cancer diagnosis (Figure S5). The cumulative incidence of CRB was lowest in individuals who were not on anticoagulation (4.1% at 6 months, 5.8% at 12 months). Those on low‐dose anticoagulants had a higher incidence of CRB, ranging from 5.4% to 11.0% at 12 months. Individuals on full‐dose anticoagulants had a higher incidence, ranging from 11.8% to 15.0% at 12 months (Table 2).

Risk Factors for Clinically Relevant Bleeding

In the multivariable analysis, many factors were significantly associated with CRB (Table 3). Type of cancer had the strongest association. Compared with breast cancer, cervical cancer (adjusted hazard ratio, HR: 4.05), acute leukemia (HR: 3.96 for acute lymphoblastic leukemia [ALL] and 3.68 for acute myeloid leukemia [AML]), upper GI (HR: 3.61), pancreatic (HR: 3.61), and liver cancers (HR: 3.57) were associated with the greatest risk. Other notable associations included metastatic disease (HR: 1.54), history of bleeding (HR: 1.68), and higher NCI‐CI (HR: 1.43 for every 1‐point increase). Baseline hemoglobin < 10 g/dL, platelets < 50 × 109/L, albumin < 3.5 g/dL, and eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m2 were all associated with CRB risk (HRs between 1.32 and 1.50). Initiation of systemic therapy was associated with marginally increased CRB risk (HRs between 1.20 and 1.40). Older age, male sex, non‐White race, history of VTE, and recent hospitalization were also marginally associated with increased CRB risk (HRs between 1.09 and 1.31).

Compared to individuals not on antithrombotic agents at the time of cancer diagnosis, those on low‐dose direct oral anticoagulants were marginally associated with increased risk of CRB (HRs 1.14 for apixaban, 1.09 for rivaroxaban). This association was not seen for low‐dose enoxaparin (HR 0.69). Full‐dose anticoagulants were independently associated with CRB (HRs 1.28 for apixaban, 1.42 for rivaroxaban, 1.56 for dabigatran, 1.59 for warfarin, and 1.46 for enoxaparin) (Table 3). Both aspirin (HR 1.12) and clopidogrel (HR 1.26) were also marginally associated with increased CRB risk.

Generalizability of Current Cohort and Bleeding Risk Model

When we applied the multivariable model built from the early treatment sub‐cohort, the predicted 6‐month CRB rate in individuals enrolled in the AVERT/CASSINI clinical trial placebo arms was 4.9% (95% CI 4.7–5.1) (vs. 4.7% reported by the trials). Similarly, the predicted 6‐month CRB rate in the CAT‐BLED cohort was 9.1% (95% CI 8.6–9.6) (vs. 9.8% [95% CI 7.6–12.1]) reported in the original study (Table S6).

Impact of Bleeding on Overall Mortality

The median follow‐up for mortality assessment was 1154 days. In the overall cohort, the 1‐ and 5‐year all‐cause mortality after cancer diagnosis was 9.4% (95% CI 9.4–9.4) and 26.3% (95% CI 26.2–26.3). Most of the features selected in the CRB model were also relevant for mortality, except for the exclusion of baseline antithrombotic therapy and the inclusion of low body weight and congestive heart failure. After adjusting for these confounders (Table 4), the development of CRB (HR 3.26 [95% CI 3.24–3.29]), specifically ICH (HR 4.17 [95% CI 4.11–4.24]), GIB (HR 2.52 [95% CI 2.50–2.55]), and other bleeding (HR 2.46 [95% CI 2.44–2.49]), as time varying covariates were independently associated with increased mortality.

Discussion

In this nationwide representative cohort of 2.5 million individuals with incident cancer diagnosis in the US over 7 years, the 12‐month period prevalence for CRB leading to cancer diagnosis was 5.3% and the 12‐month cumulative incidence of CRB after diagnosis was 6.3%, which comprised predominantly of GIB. Therefore, bleeding is both a frequent symptom in the period preceding cancer diagnosis as well as a notable complication associated with cancer. This finding was similarly replicated in a sub‐cohort of 842 789 patients receiving early systemic therapy (aka the treated cohort), suggesting that CRB was not only a complication related to anti‐cancer therapy, but rather reflective of the underlying cancer and individual characteristics. In multivariable analysis, we found the following factors to be statistically and clinically relevant: selective cancer types, metastatic disease, history of bleeding, increased comorbidities, concurrent anemia, thrombocytopenia, hypoalbuminemia, and low creatinine clearance, initiation of systemic therapy, and baseline exposure to therapeutic anticoagulation. Finally, the onset of CRB was associated with a 3.3‐fold higher risk of death, with different bleeding locations contributing to varied risk. Taken together, these findings reveal a substantial and often overlooked risk of bleeding in cancer patients and underscore a need for validated bleeding risk models that can be integrated alongside VTE prediction tools to guide personalized thromboprophylaxis decisions.

The 6‐month incidence of CRB in the current epidemiology study among unselected individuals with cancer initiating systemic therapy (4.8%) was similar to the rate from randomized controlled trials for prophylactic anticoagulation (4.7% among placebo arms) but lower than the rate from a recent prospective observational cohort study in Vienna (CAT‐BLED; 9.8%). The AVERT and CASSINI trials enrolled individuals initiating systemic therapy with a Khorana Score of 2 or higher but also excluded individuals at risk for bleeding. The CAT‐BLED cohort enrolled all individuals regardless of baseline bleeding risk but had higher proportions of individuals with advanced risk factors, such as distant metastasis (65.5% vs. 19.1%), upper GI (3.4% vs. 2.0%), liver (4.8% vs. 1.5%), pancreas (10.4% vs. 2.5%), and head and neck cancers (11.1% vs. 4.3%) when compared to our study. We hypothesized that the differences in the proportion of individuals with risk factors in these cohorts could potentially account for the discrepancy in the CRB incidence observed. To test this theory, we applied our multivariable CRB model using simulated data from these previous studies and found that we could closely replicate the study‐specific incidence predictions. This finding provided reassurance that our population was generalizable and our CRB ascertainment algorithm was adequate.

Our study also has distinct contrasts with several recent studies that examined the incidence and risks of CRB in individuals with cancer associated thrombosis receiving long‐term anticoagulation [24, 25]. First, since only 8% of individuals were on anticoagulant at the time of initial cancer diagnosis and treatment, findings from anticoagulant treated cohorts may not be generalizable to the full oncologic population. By including all individuals at the time of cancer diagnosis, our current study provides credible estimates for this missing link and highlights the important risk of bleeding when considering VTE thromboprophylaxis. Second, while most previous studies primarily focused on individuals receiving cancer‐directed systemic therapy, we examined the CRB incidence both at the time of initial diagnosis (prior to therapy) and in a sub‐cohort of individuals receiving early systemic therapy (treated cohort). This distinction is relevant in cancer associated thrombosis studies because individuals requiring systemic therapy often have heightened risk of VTE compared to the general cancer population. This is particularly salient in non‐prostate genitourinary cancers, gynecologic cancers, sarcomas, and melanomas [41], because these cancers are typically surgically managed at early stages. Similarly, we also found excessive CRB risks in advanced non‐prostate genitourinary, sarcoma, melanoma, and thyroid cancers requiring early systemic therapy compared to the general cancer population. In contrast, gynecologic cancers had elevated CRB risk with and without systemic therapy. It is also non‐surprising that acute leukemias (with prolonged thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy), hepatocellular carcinomas (with concurrent cirrhosis), pancreatic cancers (with tumor invasion), and mucosal tumors (upper GI cancer, bladder, and cervical) are among the cancer types with the highest CRB risk. The similarity in cancer types for VTE and CRB highlights the complex dysregulation of hemostasis in advanced malignancy.

Our multivariable risk model for CRB also merits further discussion. While we employed LASSO to select parsimonious features, the sheer sample size of our dataset allows even small differences in HRs to be statistically significant. While we retained many of these features like age, sex, and race for model transparency, we caution against over‐interpretation clinically. While systemic therapy appeared to have moderate associations with CRB (HR 1.20–1.40), this risk was overshadowed by the significantly stronger association with cancer type (HR 1.12–4.05) and metastatic stage (HR 1.54). Furthermore, it is reassuring that individuals on baseline low‐dose apixaban and rivaroxaban for non‐CAT prophylaxis‐related reasons at the time of cancer diagnosis only had small increases in CRB (HRs 1.09–1.14), although those on therapeutic doses had expectedly higher risk depending on the anticoagulant class (HRs 1.28–1.59). This too should be interpreted with caution as these exposures often reflect historical comorbidities and should not be extrapolated to future anticoagulant initiation. Notably, many of the important risk factors for CRB, such as cancer type (especially upper GI, pancreatic, and ALL), metastatic stage, systemic therapy, history of VTE, recent hospitalization, anemia, and hypoalbuminemia, are also known risk factors for VTE [10]. Without a validated risk model that includes CRB or excluding everyone meeting anticoagulant trial exclusion criteria, further refinement on VTE risk assessment models alone is unlikely to swing the pendulum toward widespread adoption of thromboprophylaxis in individuals with cancer.

In addition to known mortality risk factors such as cancer type, stage, treatment, and comorbidities, we also demonstrated that bleeding had an independent impact (HR 3.26) on all‐cause mortality. This was similarly reported in the CAT‐BLED study (HR of 5.8). The increased mortality risk observed with CRB again parallels that from VTE, where the associated mortality risk ranged from 1.9 to 4.8 depending on the cancer type [41, 42, 43]. Due to the lack of cause‐specific mortality data, we were not able to determine if the elevated mortality risk was directly attributable to the CRB (case fatality) or as a consequence of the broader decline in health associated with cancer and its treatment.

One of the major strengths of our study is the large contemporary cohort (2018–2024) of diverse individuals with cancer distributed across all 50 states in the U.S. including non‐academic centers, rural areas, and underserved communities, making our results generalizable (Figure S1). We were also able to capture CRB from EHR data using a validated algorithm with moderately high precision (92%) and recall (85%) when validated in an independent dataset of 400 patients. The similarity of CRB incidence in our study when compared with simulated datasets from previous published studies provides further reassurance on the reliability of the bleeding algorithm. In contrast to a previous study reporting lower accuracy of ICD codes from emergency department (any position) [38], we found that restricting to the primary position (principal diagnosis) significantly improved the precision. Therefore, in contrast to previous studies on CRB in cancer patients receiving anticoagulants that relied on inpatient discharge diagnosis only [20, 25], we extended our outcome capture to those presenting to the emergency department with primary bleeding concerns. Nonetheless, we had to exclude outpatient bleeding encounter as this lacked precision and would have led to significant over‐estimation of actual incidence.

There are also limitations inherent to EHR database studies. First, we were unable to differentiate between major and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding despite our phenotype validation since these were not prospectively captured and adjudicated outcomes. Second, while Epic EHR comprises a significant portion of the healthcare systems in the US, participation in Cosmos remains voluntary. It is possible that patients could move in and out of healthcare systems with Epic to create interval missingness. To mitigate this concern, we censored all follow‐up beyond a 6‐month interval with no F2F encounters. Third, we made several assumptions in our simulated model when applying the multivariable risk model to predict CRB rates from the previously published cohorts. These assumptions could have influenced the actual performance of the risk model. Fourth, certain key cancer metrics, such as performance status, frailty, and cancer‐specific surgery, could not be ascertained. These measures were difficult to obtain accurately in an observational study of this scale with diverse cancer types, and we had included a validated cancer comorbidity index to capture overall health status of the participants. Fifth, the missingness in laboratory data at the time of initial cancer diagnosis could be related to a healthy status effect where individuals who were less sick or with early‐stage cancers did not have routine laboratory measurements. Since the laboratory value was not missing at random, imputation would have led to incorrect results, so we treated them as discrete levels instead. Fortunately, the missing category had a similar HR as the baseline laboratory group. Sixth, while we required two or more ICD codes > 30 days apart in pre‐specified non–primary care F2F encounters for cancer diagnosis, it is possible to include some individuals without an active cancer, though the sub‐cohort of individuals receiving early systemic therapy showed similar outcome trends. Finally, while we captured anticoagulant and antiplatelet prescriptions using prescription data, certain over‐the‐counter medications such as aspirin were likely under‐captured in the current study. Since the goal of the study was not to exclusively assess the impact of anticoagulant on CRB, we only captured the baseline exposure and did not study it as a time‐varying exposure.

In summary, we report the incidence and risk factors for cancer‐associated bleeding and their association with mortality in a contemporary cohort of individuals with incident cancer diagnosis in the US. Bleeding risk varied significantly depending on the underlying cancer, treatment, and patient‐specific factors. Risk assessment models for CRB are needed to complement existing VTE risk models for effective clinical decision‐making in individuals with cancer.
