180-Degree Nuclear Flip: A Safety and Efficacy Assessment of a Phacoemulsification Technique for Cataract Nuclei of Grade III and Below
Authors: Ruibo Chen, Tao Xu, Guorong Shi, Chunyan Xi, Mengqing Yin, Kai Hu, Bing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis prospective randomized controlled trial aims to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of a 180° nuclear flip technique in phacoemulsification cataract surgery, comparing it with the traditional endocapsular approach for cataracts of Emery–Little grade III and below.MethodsA total of 142 patients were randomly assigned to either the 180° nuclear flip group (n = 71) or the conventional endocapsular phacoemulsification group (n = 71). The primary outcomes measured were total phacoemulsification time (TPT) and effective phacoemulsification time (EPT). Secondary outcomes were the incidence of postoperative corneal endothelial cell loss (ECL) at 7 and 28 days, as well as intraoperative complications including posterior capsule rupture (PCR).ResultsThe mean TPT was substantially shorter in the flip group (43.75 ± 17.16 s) compared to the conventional group (58.45 ± 22.27 s) (P < 0.0001). Similarly, the mean EPT was also significantly reduced in the flip group (11.08 ± 5.25 s vs. 14.87 ± 8.54 s, P = 0.0046). There were no PCR cases in the flip group, whereas there was one case (1.4%) in the traditional group. At 28 days postoperatively, the mean ECL rate of the flip group (3.7%) was lower than that of the conventional group (4.9%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance.ConclusionThe 180° nuclear flip technique is a safe, efficient, and effective method for managing cataracts of grade III hardness and below. It may help reduce the risk of complications, improves intraoperative stability by creating a more spacious operating field, and significantly reduces phacoemulsification time.Video available for this article.Trial Registrationchictr.org.cn identifier, ChiCTR2500105241.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01435-9.Keywords: Phacoemulsification, Nuclear flip, Cataract surgery, Endothelial cell loss, Randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Over the past decades, phacoemulsification (PHACO) surgery has gradually become the gold standard for the treatment of cataract [1–3], gaining widespread adoption due to the advantage of smaller incisions, less tissue damage, excellent safety, and rapid visual rehabilitation [4, 5]. The cataract surgical rate (CSR) has been reported to increase annually worldwide [6]. For example, in China, CSR increased around tenfold in just 20 years, from about 300 (per million population) in the early 2000s to over 3000 (per million population) by 2025 [7]. Even higher rates were seen in non-first-tier cities. Intraoperative treatment of the lens nucleus continues to be one of the most important phases as the number of cataract procedures increases. Among the available techniques, endocapsular phacoemulsification, where nucleus chopping and emulsification are performed entirely within the capsular bag, has become one of the mainstay techniques for this purpose [8–10]. Although intraoperative nucleus management has become safer with femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS), the exorbitant cost of the equipment has prevented broad adoption, especially in non-first-tier cities in developing countries [11, 12]. Additionally, some contraindications, such as corneal opacities and tiny pupils, limit the usage of FLACS.

As a result of the deep working position and the confined operating space, procedural missteps may result in complications such as posterior capsule rupture (PCR), corneal endothelial cell loss (ECL), and even lens dislocation into the vitreous cavity [13, 14]. Refractive cataract surgery has grown more common in recent years because of the growing availability of refractive intraocular lenses (IOLs), including toric and multifocal designs [15, 16]. Compared to surgeries with monofocal IOLs, these refractive procedures have a near-zero tolerance for complications. Events such as PCR or endothelial decompensation can significantly compromise the visual acuity and quality of vision.

Therefore, this prospective, single-blind, dual-center randomized controlled trial (RCT) was designed to compare the efficacy and safety of a 180° nucleus flip technique, performed with a conventional viscoelastic device, against the standard endocapsular method in PHACO with less than grade III hardness, thereby providing evidence to guide technique selection.

Methods

Patients

This prospective, multicenter, single-masked randomized clinical trial was conducted at Changzhou Branch Hospital of Xiamen Eye Center Affiliated to Xiamen University (Purui Eye Hospital) and Gaoyou Bright Eye Hospital between July 2025 and September 2025. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Meeting for Clinical Research at Xiamen Eye Center, Xiamen University (XMYKZX-KY-2025-033). All participants were recruited after obtaining informed consent. The study was registered on www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2500105241).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: Age-related, complicated, and metabolic cataracts with nuclear hardness grade III or below.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Cataracts with nuclear hardness grade IV or higher; (2) cataracts with dislocation or subluxation of the lens; (3) nystagmus; (4) corneal endothelial dysfunction; (5) shallow anterior chamber; (6) congenital or traumatic cataract; (7) cataract combined with glaucoma; (8) posterior synechia of the iris; (9) small pupil; (10) corneal opacity.

Randomization

Patients were randomized 1:1 to receive either regular endocapsular phacoemulsification (group A) or 180° nuclear flip (group B). Randomization was undertaken by using an online random number generator, and then the study personnel at each study site opened sealed, sequentially numbered envelopes containing randomized surgical group information.

Surgical Instruments

The Associate Dual with a 0.8-mm bimanual irrigation/aspiration set (Dutch Ophthalmic Research Center, DORC) was used for all phacoemulsification procedures. A specular microscope (Topcon sp-1p, Japan) was used to count the number of corneal endothelial cells. All surgeries were performed under an operating microscope (OPMI LUMERA 300 from ZEISS, Germany). All surgical instruments were from Suzhou Mingren, China. The intraocular lenses (IOLs) for both groups were from Eyebright, Beijing, China.

Key Points of the Surgery

Patients were assigned using a single-masked, randomized approach. All surgeries in both the experimental and control groups were performed by the same experienced surgeon (Bing Chen). Compound tropicamide eye drops were used four times, every 10 min, to induce mydriasis prior to surgery. Proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops were used three times at 5-min intervals to induce topical anesthesia.

A standard three-plane clear corneal incision was created with a 2.2-mm keratome, followed by the injection of sodium hyaluronate into the anterior chamber. A side-port incision was made at the limbus with a 15° knife. Next, a continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis (CCC) with a diameter of 5.5–6.0 mm was then created. A complete hydrodissection was performed to achieve separation of the nucleus from the cortex. For both groups, the phacoemulsification settings were the same: 30% ultrasonic power, 350 mmHg vacuum pressure, and 30 ml/min aspiration flow rate.

In the control group, conventional endocapsular phacoemulsification was performed. Figure 1 displays the intraoperative schematic for the experimental group. A 120° bent irrigation cannula was placed with its tip gently pressing against the lower-middle third of the nucleus after hydrodissection. By applying the “seesaw principle,” with simultaneous irrigation and clockwise rotation, the surgeon flipped the nucleus upward by 180°, causing it to prolapse out of the capsular bag. The nucleus was then either partially displaced into the anterior chamber or situated between the iris and anterior capsule. The posterior lip of the incision was gently depressed to allow for the egress of some aqueous fluid and ophthalmic viscosurgical device (OVD), thereby lowering intraocular pressure (IOP). The detailed surgical procedure is demonstrated in Video 1 (see video in the online/HTML version of the manuscript or follow the digital features link under the abstract). Subsequently, OVD was injected above the nucleus to reposition it securely in the space between the iris and the anterior capsule.

Intraoperative images of 180° nuclear flip. a, b Following hydrodissection, the tip is gently pressed against the lower-middle third of the nucleus. c The ophthalmic viscosurgical device (OVD) repositions the nucleus into the posterior chamber

The superficial cortex was then initially sucked from the nucleus by the phaco tip, which was beveled down. The tip was then embedded into the nucleus to perform a chopping maneuver, dividing it into halves, then quarters, and so on, until the entire nucleus and the cortical cushion were emulsified and aspirated. The remaining surgical steps were completed following the standard phacoemulsification procedure.

Intraoperative and Postoperative Evaluation Indicators

Intraoperative evaluation indicators include (1) total phacoemulsification time (TPT), effective phacoemulsification time (EPT), and EPT/TPT ratio; (2) incidence of intraoperative complications such as PCR and vitreous prolapse.

Postoperative evaluation indicators include (1) the proportion of corneal endothelial cell loss at 7 and 28 days postoperatively; (2) uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA), IOP, and posterior capsule opacification.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis and graphical representation of baseline preoperative patient characteristics and postoperative outcome measures were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software). The Shapiro–Wilk test or Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed to assess data normality. For datasets failing normality assumptions, sinusoidal or logarithmic transformations were attempted to normalize the distribution. If normality was not achieved post-transformation, the Mann–Whitney U test was utilized for subsequent analysis. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Participants

A total of 142 patients were randomized in the final analysis according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle (Fig. 2), with 71 patients randomly assigned to the conventional phacoemulsification group (group A) and 71 to the 180° nuclear flip group (group B). The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex distribution, systemic parameters (blood sugar, systolic and diastolic blood pressure), and ocular measurements (axial length, anterior chamber depth, corneal curvature, baseline logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) visual acuity, IOP, and endothelial cell density), showed no statistically significant differences between group A and group B (all P > 0.05, Table 1). These results indicate that there was no heterogeneity between the two groups at baseline.

Analysis of ECD also revealed no statistically significant difference between the groups at 7 days and 28 days postoperatively (P = 0.53 and P = 0.32, respectively). As illustrated more clearly in Fig. 3, the mean percentage of endothelial cell loss at 28 days was lower in group B (3.7%) than in group A (4.9%), despite the absence of statistical significance. Following surgery, both groups displayed a decrease in ECD, which is a common postoperative response. However, the curve for group B remained consistently above that of group A at both the 7-day and 28-day follow-up points, indicating less reduction and better preservation of endothelial cells in the 180° nuclear flip group.

Discussion

The normal lens is a biconvex structure measuring approximately 4–5 mm in thickness and 9–10 mm in diameter, with a relatively flat anterior capsule and a more convex posterior capsule [17, 18]. On the basis of this anatomical structure, the 180° nuclear flip procedure rotates the lens nucleus and prolapses it out of the capsular bag into the space between the iris and the anterior lens capsule, thereby fully exposing the nucleus and cortex. The posterior chamber provides more working room than the capsular bag, and the phaco tip does not need to be pointed too downward during phacoemulsification, lowering the possibility of injuring the posterior capsule. The physical depth of the anterior chamber, posterior chamber, and lens combined is approximately 8 mm, a space that is further expanded under continuous irrigation. As nuclei with a hardness of grade III or below often have a smaller diameter [19], these factors create sufficient operating space for the 180° nuclear flip. To minimize bias in the present study, these variables were controlled: all surgeries were performed by a single, highly skilled surgeon; the same brand and model of phacoemulsification system, OVD, and balanced salt solution were used throughout; and intraoperative parameters, including ultrasound energy and flow rate, were standardized for all procedures.

On the basis of years of accumulated experience and clinical practice, we have identified three crucial elements for the 180° nucleus flip technique: (1) A 120° bent irrigation cannula is utilized once complete hydrodissection has been achieved to separate the cortex from the nucleus. The lower-middle part of the nucleus is gently pressed by the cannula’s tip at the bend. By applying a “seesaw principle” while simultaneously irrigating and rotating clockwise, one can flip the nucleus upward 180° and prolapse it out of the capsular bag. (2) The flipped nucleus is positioned between the iris and anterior capsule, or partially/completely dislocated into the anterior chamber. To reduce IOP, OVD and irrigation fluid can partially escape through a gentle dip of the incision’s posterior lip. Subsequently, OVD is injected above the nucleus to reposition it securely in the space between the iris and anterior capsule, or partially within the capsular bag. (3) Throughout the entire procedure, standard, near-in situ phacoemulsification is used to handle the nucleus.

Our findings showed that the 180° nuclear flip technique resulted in a statistically significant decrease in both mean TPT and EPT when compared to the traditional method, with no significant differences in baseline patient or surgical characteristics between the two groups. The mean TPT and EPT were reduced by approximately 15 s and 3.8 s, respectively. Although modest in absolute terms, these reductions are clinically relevant, as cumulative ultrasound energy is a well-established contributor to corneal endothelial cell loss, and even small decreases in EPT may translate into meaningful benefits for endothelial preservation. Furthermore, while the difference in postoperative endothelial cell loss did not reach statistical significance, the lower loss rate in the experimental group (3.7% vs. 4.9%) suggests a clinically relevant protective effect on the cornea. Previous studies have indicated that FLACS does not show a statistically significant advantage over conventional phacoemulsification regarding endothelial cell protection [20]. In this context, our 180° nuclear flip technique provides a valuable surgical strategy and reference for optimizing endothelial preservation. We consider that this enhanced efficiency stems from the primary mechanics of our technique. By flipping the nucleus into the posterior chamber, the surgeon frees the structure, either partially or completely, from the confinement of the capsular bag. This repositioning improves the followability of the nuclear fragments to the phaco tip under high vacuum, facilitating easier engagement. Consequently, the surgeon has more control over the procedure, which increases the effectiveness of phacoemulsification.

The incidence of PCR during cataract surgery varies as a result of surgical difficulty, doctors’ experience, and other factors. With advances in equipment and surgical techniques, the incidence of PCR nowadays can generally be controlled at around 1%, with some statistics showing a range of 0.45% to 7.9% [21, 22]. PCR can occur during multiple stages of cataract surgery, such as nucleus management, cortical aspiration, intraocular lens implantation, and viscoelastic removal. The complications observed in the control group, one instance of PCR and two cases of zonular fiber rupture, all occurred during the management of the nucleus and cortex. This finding particularly indicates that even for a highly experienced surgeon, it is crucial to have enough operating space during surgery. However, given the small number of events, no statistical comparison was performed for complication rates, and these observations should be interpreted with caution.

It is worth noting that the concept of supracapsular phacoemulsification was first introduced by Maloney et al. as early as 1997, in which the nucleus was prolapsed out of the capsular bag through a sequence of hydrodissection, spontaneous lens tilt, optional nuclear rotation, and a viscoelastic-assisted transposition maneuver [23]. The underlying philosophy of their approach shares notable similarities with our technique, both aiming to relocate the nucleus to a more spacious surgical plane to facilitate safer and more efficient emulsification. However, there are some differences in technical detail and study design. In our technique, a 150° L-shaped viscoelastic cannula is used after hydrodissection, and the nucleus is flipped 180° out of the capsular bag within approximately 2 s by applying the “seesaw principle,” making the maneuver considerably simple and fast. Furthermore, while the original report by Maloney et al. was primarily a descriptive account of the surgical technique, our study provides a systematic evaluation through a prospective RCT, with comprehensive assessment of key outcome measures including TPT, EPT, ECD, and PCR. We believe these findings add meaningful evidence to support the clinical value and broader applicability of this surgical concept.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the postoperative follow-up time was limited to 1 month. A longer-term evaluation would be necessary to comprehensively assess outcomes. Secondly, all surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon. This was intentional, as standardizing the surgical operator helped minimize performance variability and isolate the effect of the technique itself. However, this approach inevitably introduces limitations in terms of generalizability, and whether the technique can be consistently reproduced across surgeons with varying levels of experience remains to be evaluated in future studies. Thirdly, the findings of this study are applicable only to patients with cataracts of nuclear hardness grade III or below, as this was a key inclusion criterion. Cataracts with grade IV or V nuclear hardness are typically associated with a larger nuclear volume and diameter. This increased size leads to a relative narrowing of the anterior and posterior chambers, reducing the available space for the nuclear flip maneuver. Future studies with larger sample sizes would further strengthen the statistical power for detecting differences in low-frequency outcomes such as PCR, and longer follow-up periods are encouraged to build upon these findings.

Conclusions

The 180° nuclear flip technique is a safe and effective approach for managing cataracts of grade III hardness and below. Compared with the conventional endocapsular method, this technique significantly reduces both total and effective phacoemulsification time, while maintaining comparable postoperative outcomes. By flipping the nucleus out of the capsular bag, the technique creates a more spacious operating field, which may contribute to better intraoperative control and endothelial preservation. These findings suggest that the 180° nuclear flip is a practical and efficient alternative for nucleus management, particularly in settings where advanced technologies such as femtosecond lasers are not readily available.
