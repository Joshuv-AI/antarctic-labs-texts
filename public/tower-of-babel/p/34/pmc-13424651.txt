Effect of early skin-to-skin contact after vaginal birth on neonatal stress and day-5 bilirubin levels: a randomized trial
Authors: Nazmi Kanad, Betul Yavuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to examine the effects of early skin-to-skin contact (SSC) on neonatal stress as the primary outcome and—together with early initiation of breastfeeding—on fifth-day total serum bilirubin (TSB) levels as a secondary outcome. Mother–newborn dyads were randomly assigned to the control (n = 30) or SSC group (n = 30). Neonatal stress was assessed immediately after birth, under a radiant warmer, and during intramuscular (IM) vitamin K injection using the Newborn Stress Scale (NSS); the SSC group was evaluated five minutes after SSC initiation. TSB levels were measured on the fifth day post-discharge. NSS scores did not differ significantly between groups immediately after birth or under a radiant warmer (p > 0.05). Five minutes after SSC began, the NSS score was 0.00 [1.00]. However, within the intervention group, NSS scores significantly decreased five minutes after starting SSC compared with initially after birth. During injection, NSS scores were significantly lower in the SSC group than in the control group (p < 0.05). Fifth-day TSB levels did not significantly differ between the groups (p > 0.05). Early SSC was associated with lower neonatal stress during IM vitamin K injection and with reduced stress scores after SSC initiation. However, it does not significantly affect early bilirubin levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-60971-3.

Introduction

The evidence supporting the practice of skin-to-skin contact (SSC) after birth is robust, indicating multiple benefits for both mothers and babies1. Baby-friendly hospital initiatives worldwide recommend immediate and continuous SSC after birth2.

Newborns transition from a dark, narrow, and fluid-filled intrauterine environment to a wide, bright, cold, and dry extrauterine environment after birth3. Furthermore, newborns may experience additional stress during labour4, invasive postnatal procedures such as heel prick blood collection and venipuncture, and routine procedures such as separation from the mother for a short time to measure body weight5. Therefore, it is recommended to not separate newborns from their mothers except for important medical reasons6 and to initiate SSC as soon as possible after birth so that the newborn can cope with “birth stress”6,7. In the literature, several studies have examined the effect of SSC initiated between the newborn and the mother immediately after birth on breastfeeding success8,9. Moreover, it has been reported that pain levels and heart rates10 decrease during intravenous access11 and IM injection12,13 in newborns receiving kangaroo care. However, the implementation of SSC as a routine practice to reduce neonatal stress after birth and during invasive procedures—such as IM injections (vitamin K, hepatitis B vaccine) and heel lance sampling—is limited in most healthcare institutions.

Hyperbilirubinemia is usually observed in term newborns as physiological jaundice in the first 24–72 h of life, peaks on days 4–5, and starts to decrease in the following days14. Colostrum, which is a natural laxative present in breast milk, facilitates the removal of meconium in newborns and results in bilirubin excretion in the stool. The literature reports that providing breast milk to newborns during the first hours of life and then providing daily feedings 9–10 times per day ensures low total serum bilirubin (TSB) levels15,16. The potential effect of SSC on bilirubin levels is likely indirect rather than direct. Early SSC may facilitate earlier initiation and increase the frequency of breastfeeding1, which in turn may promote meconium passage and bilirubin excretion15,16. Therefore, any observed association between SSC and bilirubin levels should be interpreted within this indirect physiological framework.

To the best of our knowledge, there is limited evidence the effects of SSC on both postnatal stress in newborns and TSB levels, particularly in relation to early initiation of breastfeeding. Moreover, the literature includes studies evaluating newborns’ responses to intramuscular (IM) vitamin K or vaccine injections at single or multiple time points using the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS)12,17–19, behavioural measures20, and physiological parameters such as heart rate and oxygen saturation10. However, only a limited number of studies have specified the timing of initiation of SSC following birth18,19. Therefore, further evidence is needed to better characterize the temporal pattern of stress responses following early SSC, particularly in the delivery-room setting. In Türkiye, early initiation of breastfeeding is encouraged, and SSC is supported; however, its routine implementation may be challenging in clinical practice because of factors such as staffing limitations.

Nurses play a key role in reducing neonatal stress through early SSC initiated in the first minutes of life following vaginal delivery and in preventing increases in bilirubin levels associated with early breastfeeding initiation. Within this context, this study aimed to examine the effects of early skin-to-skin contact (SSC) on neonatal stress as the primary outcome and—together with early initiation of breastfeeding—on fifth-day total serum bilirubin (TSB) levels as a secondary outcome.

Study hypotheses:

H1

Early SSC after normal delivery may reduce neonatal stress during IM vitamin K injection.

H2

Early SSC after normal delivery may influence TSB levels through early initiation of breastfeeding.

Methods

Participants

This study is a prospective, randomized, controlled experimental trial, that was registered retrospectively (date: 13 April 2023) at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05812222). The study was approved by the Noninterventional Ethics Committee of the University (approval number: 2020/09–16; date: 4 June 2020) at which the study was conducted (approval number: 2020/13; date: 6 April 2020). This study was performed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written consent was obtained from the study participants.

This study was conducted between 15 June 2020 and 31 March 2021, with newborns and their mothers who had a vaginal delivery in the delivery room of the maternity ward of a university hospital in Western Türkiye. This study adhered to the specific guidelines of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) standards.

The inclusion criteria for mothers were as follows: (1) had a planned vaginal delivery; (2) were aged 18–45 years; (3) had a singleton pregnancy; (4) completed gestational week 37; (5) gave consent to participate in the study; (6) had no barriers to breastfeeding [no human T-cell lymphotropic virus (HTLV-1), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), or active tuberculosis]; (7) had no known health problems [such as diabetes, gestational diabetes, hypertension, preeclampsia, renal failure, cardiac problems, or psychiatric disorders]; and (8) had no AB0 blood type or Rh incompatibility. The inclusion criteria for newborns were as follows: (1) no obstacle to suckling, such as frenulum (tongue tie) or palate problems; (2) no health problems or congenital diseases; (3) a 5th-minute Apgar score of ≥ 8; and (4) no need for neonatal resuscitation after birth.

Power analysis was performed using G*Power software to determine the sample size. The effect size was calculated as Cohen’s w = 0.710 using the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS) ratios obtained in Kashaninia et al.12. The minimum sample size of the study was calculated to be 26 participants in each group (total n = 52) on the basis of an effect size of 0.710, a significance level of 0.05, and 80% power.

Eligible mother–newborn dyads were assigned using simple randomization by coin toss at enrolment. No allocation concealment was used. A total of 94 mother–newborn dyads were assessed for eligibility, of whom 3 declined to participate, resulting in 91 randomized dyads. Participants were allocated to the skin-to-skin contact (SSC) group (n = 45) or the control group (n = 46), and all received the allocated intervention. Measures

Parent and newborn information form

This form was prepared by the researchers and included 15 questions about sociodemographic information for the mother and the baby.

Newborn stress scale (NSS)

The NSS is used to determine stress levels in premature infants. Permission and methods for use of the scale in term infants were obtained from one of the scale developers, Assoc. Prof. Dr. Sibel Serap Ceylan, via e-mail. The NSS measures stress on a 3-point Likert-type scale across 8 subgroups: facial expression, body colour, respiration, activity level, comfort, muscle tone, extremities, and posture. Each subgroup was evaluated on a scale between 0 and 2 points, with a minimum possible score of 0 and a maximum possible score of 16 points. A higher score indicates a greater stress level of the baby. In the original validation of the NSS, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranged between 0.65 and 0.8121. The scale has been utilized in study involving newborns between 36 and 41 weeks of gestation22.

Total serum bilirubin (TSB) measurement

TSB values were obtained from hospital laboratory records measured via biochemical serum analysis during the routine outpatient follow-up visit on postnatal day 523. To ensure consistency, TSB results were obtained from the same laboratory using standardized calibration protocols. Serum bilirubin measurements were used, as transcutaneous bilirubin measurement devices were not available in the clinical setting at the time of the study. TSB levels are recognized as the gold standard for blood bilirubin measurements24. This timing aligns with international guidelines stating that peak TSB concentrations generally occur between 72 and 120 h of age in healthy newborns23. No newborns in the study received phototherapy prior to the day-5 TSB measurement.

Newborn follow-up chart

This chart was used to record the TSB level of the newborn on postnatal day 5 and the frequency of breastfeeding. TSB levels were evaluated as a secondary outcome of the study.

SSC information brochure

This brochure was prepared by researchers in line with the literature and included information about early SSC in normal deliveries and its benefits for mothers and babies.

Body weight measurement

Newborns’ body weight was measured in the delivery room using an SECA digital infant scale with a maximum capacity of 20 kg and a sensitivity of 10–20 g. The device was calibrated on September 30, 2019, by OKM Organize Kalibrasyon.

Procedure

Pregnant women who planned to have a vaginal delivery were initially interviewed between 08:30–16:30 on Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday, to inform them about the purpose and procedure of the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all pregnant women who participated in the study. The “Parent and Newborn Information Form” was completed by all participants through face‒to-face interviews, after which the pregnant women were transferred to the delivery room.

In the first 30 s after birth, the newborns were rapidly evaluated to determine whether they needed neonatal resuscitation. The umbilical cords of all the newborns were cut within 30–60 s after birth, and the newborns were placed under a radiant heater. After the initial steps of drying and aspirating the secretions in the mouth and nose were applied to the live newborns who did not need neonatal resuscitation, their body weight, head circumference, and height were measured and recorded on the “Newborn Follow-up Chart.”

The primary outcome of this study was the effect of early SSC on neonatal stress after birth. The secondary outcomes were (1) the effect of early SSC, in conjunction with early initiation of breastfeeding, on neonatal bilirubin levels; and (2) the rate of exclusive breastfeeding on postnatal day 5.

In this research, the stress level of the newborns was assessed using NSS. The bilirubin levels of the newborns were determined by TSB assessment, which is routinely checked on the 5th day of life after birth.

Measurements made in the research included the assessment of neonatal stress levels using the NSS. All NSS assessments were conducted by the researcher, who was aware of group allocation. TSB levels were measured on postnatal day 5 as part of routine clinical practice. In addition, mothers were asked about their infants’ feeding status (exclusive breastfeeding or breastfeeding combined with formula) on postnatal day 5.

At the time the study was conducted, SSC was not part of routine care in the study setting. Therefore, SSC was applied to the intervention group, while the control group received standard care. Owing to the nature of the intervention, blinding was not feasible.

Intervention group

The researcher informed the pregnant women in the intervention group before delivery by the researcher using the “SSC Information Brochure.” After birth, the newborn was diapered, given a hat, and underwent routine hospital procedures. After these procedures were completed (approximately 2–3 min after birth), the newborn, wearing a diaper and cap, was placed in the prone position on the mother’s bare chest and covered with preheated towels to initiate SSC in the delivery room. SSC was maintained until the mother–infant dyad was transferred the maternity ward. Breastfeeding occurred during SSC. The stress levels of the newborns were determined using the NSS and measured at four time points, namely, 1-immediately after birth, 2-under a radiant heater, 3-five minutes after SSC initiation, and 4- during IM vitamin K injection while SSC was ongoing, to capture neonatal stress across baseline, environmental, postintervention, and procedural conditions. During IM vitamin K injection, the newborn remained in a prone position on the mother’s chest in continuous SSC.

Control group

Pregnant women and newborns in the control group received routine hospital practices. In this routine, the newborn was placed under a radiant heater for assessment and care, remaining physically separated from the mother until all maternal invasive procedural steps were completed (approximately 25–30 min). While breastfeeding was initiated within the first hour, it was performed without SSC. SSC was not part of routine care and was therefore not implemented in the control group. The first breastfeeding session was performed in the maternity ward. The stress level of the newborns were determined using the NSS and measured at three time points: 1-immediately after birth, 2-under a radiant heater, and 3-during IM vitamin K injection, to capture neonatal stress across baseline, environmental, and procedural conditions. In the control group, IM vitamin K was administered while the newborn was placed under a radiant warmer.

After evaluations were completed by the researcher at all time points, mothers and newborns in both groups were transferred to the maternity ward. On the fifth day of life, parents returned to the hospital with their newborns for routine assessments. The nutritional status and TSB level of the newborns in both groups were recorded on the “Newborn Follow-up Chart” by the researcher.

Analysis

The study data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 20.0 package software. Numerical data are presented as the means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, and min–max values, whereas categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages. The normality of the distributions was examined using the Shapiro‒Wilk test. As the continuous outcome variables (NSS scores, day-5 total serum bilirubin levels, and percentage weight-loss values) did not satisfy the normality assumption, non-parametric procedures were used throughout. Effect size estimates were additionally computed in R25 using the rstatix26 and effectsize27 packages.

The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between groups, and Fisher’s exact test was applied when the expected cell-frequency assumption was not met. Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and the corresponding effect size was reported as the rank-biserial correlation coefficient (r = |Z|/√N) and interpreted according to Cohen’s benchmarks as small (r ≥ 0.10), medium (r ≥ 0.30), or large (r ≥ 0.50).

Within-group comparisons of repeated NSS measurements across follow-up time points were performed using the Friedman test, and the effect size was reported as Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W), interpreted using the same Cohen thresholds (small: W ≥ 0.10; medium: W ≥ 0.30; large: W ≥ 0.50). When the Friedman test produced a statistically significant result, pairwise post hoc comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction to identify which time points contributed to the observed differences while controlling the family-wise type I error rate. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Discussion

The stress of birth enhances neonatal lung aeration, improves cardiac performance, and promotes energy mobilization7. However, postnatal IM injections and other invasive procedures may induce pain and stress, potentially leading to long-term adverse effects on brain development and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis28. SSC between mothers and infants helps mitigate this stress by promoting the release of oxytocin and beta-endorphins, regulating vagal tone, and inhibiting nociceptive responses29.

In this study, the stress levels of newborns in both groups were assessed using the NSS scale immediately after birth, under radiant heating, and during IM vitamin K administration, as well as at the 5th minute of SSC initiated 2–3 min after birth in the intervention group. The absence of a significant difference between the groups immediately after birth and under radiant heating indicates that both groups had comparable baseline stress levels at the beginning of the study.

In this study, stress levels in the intervention group were markedly low at the 5th minute of SSC initiated 2–3 min after birth. Moreover, the significantly lower stress levels observed during IM vitamin K administration in the intervention group, while SSC was ongoing, than in the control group, further supports the effectiveness of SSC. Consistent with our findings, studies conducted in the delivery room have reported that pain scores measured after vitamin K injection were lower in the intervention group when SSC was initiated immediately after birth18 or within the first minutes of life19. Similarly, in studies where the timing of SSC initiation was not specified, pain scores after vitamin K injection12 and vaccination17 were also reported to be lower in the intervention group than in the control group.

In further support of these findings, it was reported that newborns in the SSC group had lower heart rates and higher oxygen saturation levels than those in the control group did10. Similarly, behavioural pain response scores after hepatitis B vaccination were lower in the SSC group20. Overall, these findings support the effectiveness of SSC in reducing neonatal stress and pain during early postnatal procedures. Institutions may consider structured protocols and staff training to support timely SSC after birth where clinically feasible. In the within-group evaluation of the intervention group, NSS scores measured at the 5th minute of SSC initiated within the first 2–3 min after birth were significantly lower than those observed immediately after birth and under radiant heating. These findings may be explained by the role of SSC in supporting physiological stability through buffering maternal separation and environmental stimuli such as light and noise30. In addition, hearing the mother’s heartbeat during SSC simulates intrauterine life and facilitates the newborn’s adaptation to extrauterine life1. Consistent with these findings, Kaya and Zengin (2023) reported that, in within-group comparisons, pain scores under radiant heating were higher than those observed during SSC and after IM vitamin K injection19.

Although the NSS score of the control group also decreased somewhat over time—likely because of the thermal stability provided by radiant heaters—such environments lack the neurohormonal benefits of direct maternal contact. Consistent with these findings, Kaya and Zengin (2023) reported that, in within-group comparisons, pain scores were higher following IM vitamin K injection than under radiant heating and during the postprocedure recovery period19. These findings further support the notion that thermal support alone may be insufficient to achieve the level of stress and pain reduction provided by SSC. Although the NSS findings were consistent with previous studies, the lack of assessor blinding should be considered when interpreting the results.

In this study, the rate of exclusive breastfeeding of newborns in the intervention group on day 5 after birth was higher than that in the control group. A study conducted in India revealed that the rate of exclusive breastfeeding among newborns in the intervention group receiving SSC was higher than that in the control group on days 4–5 of life31. Giang et al. reported that the rate of exclusive breastfeeding was greater among infants who underwent SSC and that long-term continuous SSC increased the duration of breastfeeding32. Another study that examined the effect of SSC on exclusive breastfeeding among term newborns reported that early SSC increased the exclusive breastfeeding rate of healthy newborns during the first six weeks of life33.

Compared with formula-fed newborns, those who are exclusively breastfed tend to defecate more frequently, which may facilitate bilirubin excretion through urine and stool34. No statistically significant difference in day-5 TSB levels was observed between the groups in this study. Therefore, the potential effect of breastfeeding associated with early SSC on bilirubin levels could not be clearly demonstrated. Although the intervention group had higher exclusive breastfeeding rates, no significant between-group differences were observed in percentage weight loss at 24 h or on day 5. This finding may be related to the use of breast milk combined with formula supplementation in the control group. However, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia is a multifactorial condition influenced by developmental factors, feeding patterns, bilirubin production, hepatic clearance, underlying conditions, and genetic predisposition15. In this context, newborns in the control group—who were early-term—may have had a baseline risk of hyperbilirubinemia owing to immature hepatic enzyme activity and underdeveloped suck–swallow coordination35. Furthermore, the reliance on a single TSB measurement on postnatal day 5 represents an additional limitation, which may have limited the ability to fully evaluate bilirubin dynamics over time. Therefore, the bilirubin findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive evidence of the absence of an effect. Future studies incorporating serial serum or transcutaneous bilirubin measurements may provide a more comprehensive evaluation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, there was a time constraint as the research was conducted as part of a thesis study. Second, the study was carried out in a single centre; therefore, the results cannot be generalized to all newborns. Third, NSS assessments were conducted by an unblinded researcher, and the lack of inter- and intraobserver reliability analyses and video-based reassessment may have introduced observer bias and limited measurement reliability. Fourth, NSS has not been formally validated for healthy term newborns in the delivery room environment; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, the study was registered retrospectively on ClinicalTrials.gov. Sixth, the use of coin-flip randomization without allocation concealment and restricted recruitment periods may have introduced selection bias. Therefore, the potential influence of selection bias cannot be completely excluded and should be considered when interpreting the study findings. Seventh, the statistically significant difference in gestational age between groups may have influenced neonatal adaptation, feeding performance, and bilirubin levels, and should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings. Eighth, the single-time TSB measurement and lack of relevant clinical data limited the assessment of bilirubin dynamics and the clinical interpretation of jaundice. In addition, hydration indicators (e.g., urine/stool output and wet diaper count) were not recorded, which may limit the evaluation of factors influencing bilirubin levels. Ninth, maternal anatomical factors affecting breastfeeding (e.g., flat or inverted nipples) were not assessed, which may have influenced breastfeeding outcomes. Finally, owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, patient admission was periodically interrupted because physicians and personnel working in the obstetrics and gynaecology units and the delivery room tested positive for COVID-19. This situation led to case losses during the data collection phase and may have affected the clinical workflow.

Conclusions

Early SSC reduced NSS-assessed stress responses during IM vitamin K injection. SSC may support breastfeeding, which could theoretically promote bilirubin elimination; however, this study did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in day-5 TSB. In the first 24 h after birth, almost all the newborns in both study groups were exclusively breastfed, whereas on the fifth day, this rate was higher in the intervention group.

On the basis of the results of this study, the following steps are recommended: 1- training programs about early SSC should be organized for mothers and health professionals, and its use should be increased; 2- nurses experienced in initiating early SSC should be assigned to the relevant units of health institutions; and 3- further studies with larger sample sizes should be conducted to examine the possible effects of breastfeeding initiated together with early SSC on neonatal bilirubin levels and to evaluate different parameters in detail.
