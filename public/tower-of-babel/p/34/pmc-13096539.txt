Multi-representation thermal features for enhanced defect analysis in pulse thermography
Authors: Mohammed Salah, Naoufel Werghi, Davor Svetinovic, Stefano Sfarra, Yusra Abdulrahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AI-driven pulse thermography (PT) has become a crucial tool in non-destructive testing (NDT), enabling automatic detection of hidden anomalies in various industrial components. Current state-of-the-art PT segmentation and depth estimation networks rely on thermal features extracted from Principal Component Thermography (PCT) or Thermographic Signal Reconstruction (TSR) representations. However, relying on PCT and TSR independently constrains the performance of PT inspection models, as these representations possess complementary semantic features. To address this limitation, this work proposes PT-Fusion, a novel feature fusion network for enhanced analysis of subsurface defects in PT setups. PT-Fusion introduces novel fusion modules, Adaptive Weighing Fusion Gate (AWFG) and Gating Enhanced Decoding Block (GEDB), to adaptively fuse thermal features extracted from PCT and TSR representations. A novel data augmentation technique is also proposed based on random data sampling from thermographic sequences to address the scarcity of PT datasets. PT-Fusion is benchmarked against state-of-the-art PT inspection models, including U-Net, attention U-Net, 3D-CNN, TransUNet, and Swin-UNet on the Université Laval IRT-PVC dataset. The results demonstrate that PT-Fusion outperforms U-Net, attention U-Net, and 3D-CNN architectures in defect segmentation and depth estimation accuracies with a margin of 10%. Compared to TransUNet and Swin-UNet, the results show that PT-Fusion’s performance is on par with the aforementioned models, with fewer parameters. Video Link.

Introduction

Over recent years, the growing demand for quality control has driven the emergence of advanced inspection processes in various production lines, such as the aerospace1,2, automotive3,4, and construction5 industries. This paradigm shift led to the adoption of Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) techniques to detect subsurface defects and assess the structural integrity of industrial components. As a result, a wide range of NDT techniques have been developed to detect subsurface defects, including radiography, ultrasonic testing, and infrared thermography (IRT). These methods provide reliable evaluation of materials and assemblies without impairing their functionality, ensuring the safety and longevity of critical components. Moreover, the continuous advancement of sensing technologies and data-driven algorithms has enabled higher sensitivity and automation in NDT inspection processes, making them indispensable in modern manufacturing environments.

IRT has garnered significant attention due to its simplicity, low cost, and non-contact nature6. These advantages have demonstrated the potential of IRT for inspection of aircraft parts7–10, construction pipelines5,11–13, and artworks14,15. Among IRT instrumentation setups, pulse thermography (PT) is valued for its efficiency in inspecting large areas and its capability to rapidly identify subsurface defects16. Meanwhile, recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have been adopted in PT setups to enhance the quality of the extracted features to improve inspection accuracies. For instance, AI-driven PT inspection algorithms have been proposed for defect classification17,18, segmentation19,20, and depth estimation2,9,21. In addition, the integration of deep learning with thermal signal processing has enabled more robust interpretation of complex thermal transients, reducing dependency on manual thresholding or handcrafted features19,22–24. Such integration not only enhances the detection sensitivity but also contributes to developing fully automated thermographic inspection frameworks suitable for large-scale industrial applications.

State-of-the-art AI-based PT methods rely on features extracted from principal component thermography (PCT)25 and thermal signal reconstruction (TSR)26 representations for defect analysis. While these representation paradigms demonstrated considerable potential, extracting features from PCT and TSR independently limits the performance of PT inspection models. For instance, PCT tends to enhance defect visibility while TSR models the temporal evolution of the pixel responses. To address this limitation, this work introduces PT-Fusion, a novel fusion network that fuses features extracted from PCT and TSR complementary representations for enhanced subsurface defect analysis in PT setups. Related work

PT has established itself as a key NDT technique for the detection of subsurface defects in industrial components. In addition, the recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have significantly enhanced PT methodologies, enabling automated defect analysis27. As a result, extensive investigations have emerged on AI-driven PT leveraging learning-based features, with various architectures proposed for defect detection, segmentation, and depth estimation. For instance, a broad spectrum of defect detection models has been developed, demonstrating the potential of PT. Muller et al.28 introduced a ConvLSTM network for the detection of flat defects in forged steel parts. Consequently, Ankel et al.29 extended convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for elliptical defect detection. Other state-of-the-art detection networks30 have been developed for defect detection, such as YOLOv521 and Faster R-CNN17. Since these methods are purely convolutional, the quality of features extracted by CNN-based architectures is limited by the network kernels’ receptive fields. Thus, an attention-based defect detection network was introduced to simultaneously learn local and global features, circumventing the limited receptive fields of CNNs23. Other methods leverage generative models for data augmentation and progressive training for improved learning of defect detection networks31,32.

Similarly, a wide stream of defect segmentation and depth estimation networks have been proposed relying on learning-based features in the context of PT. Fang et al.33 performed an experimental study on state-of-the-art network architectures, such as U-Net and ResNet, for subsurface defect segmentation. Zhou et al.19 introduced the attention U-Net for enhanced defect segmentation compared to its traditional U-Net counterpart. On the other hand, Morelli et al.34 developed a residual convolutional network for heat-invariant defect segmentation in lock-in thermography. The aforementioned networks are inherently 2D with minimal consideration of the significant temporal information embedded in thermographic sequences. Thus, Dong et al.2 proposed a 3D-CNN accounting for temporal information embedded in raw thermal sequences for enhancing defect segmentation accuracies. However, all these networks are completely convolutional, and the limited receptive field of CNNs degrades the quality of the constructed feature maps. Consequently, several works leveraged attention-based segmentation architectures to extract features rich with global spatial and temporal features. Schmid et al.35 and Xie et al.36 proposed temporal attention mechanisms for improving learned features from temporal sequences. In addition, an attention-based PHM-IRNET network architecture was proposed as a self-supervised learning framework for segmentation in industrial thermographic inspection37.

In all of the aforementioned learning-based frameworks, networks learn to extract thermal features from single representations such as raw thermal sequences, pulse-phase thermography (PPT)38,39, PCT25,40,41, or TSR26,42,43. Learning-based representations also exist14,22, but also act as single inputs to deep neural networks. However, the aforementioned representations possess semantic features that are rather complementary. For instance, PCT tends to enhance defect visibility while TSR models the temporal evolution of the pixel responses. Unlike state-of-the-art methods, this work proposes learning these complementary features in an end-to-end fusion network, PT-Fusion. PT-Fusion passes PCT and TSR representations into CNN heads, and the extracted feature maps are fed to novel feature fusion blocks, AWFG and GEDB, to dynamically learn spatial and temporal features. The fused features serve as our multi-representation thermal features and are decoded for enhanced defect segmentation and depth estimation.

The rest of the article is structured as follows. Results section presents the experimental validations of the proposed methodology tested on the IRT-PVC dataset. The Methods section outlines the proposed fusion network and data augmentation strategy. Finally, Conclusions section presents conclusions, findings, and future aspects of the proposed framework.

Results

IRT-PVC dataset

The work of this paper is evaluated on the IRT-PVC dataset21. The IRT-PVC dataset is a publicly available pulse thermography dataset designed for subsurface defect analysis and depth estimation on PVC samples. The dataset comprises 38 inspection sequences collected from 3D-printed PVC specimens containing back-drilled artificial defects at controlled depths. The defects are manufactured in two geometries, circles and squares, and at depths between 2.5 mm and 4.5 mm. Each inspection sequence consists of a pulsed heating followed by passive cooling, captured using a FLIR SC5000 infrared camera at a spatial resolution of 320\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}256 pixels with a 10 Hz acquisition frequency for a duration of 181 s, producing approximately 1810 frames per sample. Heating is applied using two 4 kJ xenon flash lamps, synchronized through a computer-controlled trigger to ensure reproducible thermal excitation conditions. For the groundtruth segmentation masks, each specimen in the dataset is accompanied by precise defect location and depth labels that were generated using a refined 3D–CAD-assisted labeling procedure. The CAD-based method provides pixel-accurate defect masks by projecting the 3D geometry of each specimen onto the infrared image plane and aligning it with the corresponding PCT frames. This alignment enables direct extraction of the true defect size and depth, resulting in high labeling accuracy for reliable evaluation.

The obtained results outline several key findings. First, PT-Fusion demonstrates consistent results across all defect depths as highlighted in Table 1. This confirms that the model maintains robustness in predicting both shallow and deeper subsurface defects, avoiding bias toward a specific depth range. Second, according to Table 3, model performances fall around 0.7 when trained on either PCT or TSR representations. When these representations are concatenated at the input, the mIoU increases to 0.766 for U-Net and 0.804 for attention U-Net. In addition, a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10\%$$\end{document} increase in the recall and precision is witnessed. However, concatenation at the input does not ensure optimal model performance. Hence, PT-Fusion architecture incorporating the fusion modules, AWFG and GEDB, effectively guides the network learning performance towards enhanced feature extraction and defect segmentation, where it outperforms the aforementioned methods by achieving an mIoU of 0.869, a Recall of 0.907, and a Precision of 0.939. Note that the attention U-Net with a single input resembles a PT-Fusion network without multi-representation inputs as no fusion blocks are required. As such, as a nearly \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10\%$$\end{document} increase is witnessed across all metrics compared to the attention U-Net, this demonstrates that the features from both representations are indeed complementary. For instance, the proposed architecture inherently learns to adaptively identify the weighted contribution of the extracted features from PCT and TSR representations to achieve state-of-the-art segmentation accuracy. Other methods, such as 3D-CNN, leverage 3D convolutions to extract spatial and temporal information simultaneously. Still, the network performance falls short compared to PT-Fusion. We hypothesize that this is due to the engineered sequence compression of the input discussed in2. This highlights that PT-Fusion learns to leverage the enhanced defect visibility from PCT images and the temporal information of the pixel responses modeled in TSR in an efficient and enhanced multi-representation thermal features. Moreover, compared to transformer-based architectures, Swin-UNet and TransUNet , PT-Fusion outperforms Swin-UNet across all metrics with fewer parameters. For instance, Swin-UNet possesses nearly 69.81M parameters while PT-Fusion possesses 24.09M parameters. Compared to TransUNet, PT-Fusion falls slightly short in terms of mIoU but outperforms the aforementioned network in terms of recall and precision with approximately quarter the number of paramaters, i.e. TransUNet comprises of 109.54M parameters. Effect of data augmentation

The obtained network performance is heavily dependent on the diversity of the dataset. We demonstrate the need for the proposed augmentation strategy by training PT-Fusion on the original IRT-PVC dataset augmented using the common augmentation techniques, i.e., random translations, rotations, and shearing. The third study revolves around incorporating PPT representation in place of PCT and TSR inputs, or along these representations. PPT is a common thermography representation that performs a frequency-domain analysis of the transient thermographic sequence, where the phase component is known to be relatively invariant to non-uniform heating and surface emissivity variations. Table 10 outlines the performance of PT-Fusion when PPT is utilized as input in place of PCT and TSR or in addition to the aforementioned representations. The obtained results show that when combining PPT with PCT, the performance remains on the same order of magnitude as when using TSR with PCT. This is expected, since PPT models the heat-diffusion dynamics in PT in the frequency domain. In contrast, fusing PPT with TSR does not lead to similar improvements; the obtained metrics are close to those of the attention U-Net when using TSR alone. This is because TSR and PPT capture similar thermophysical information due to the duality between temporal and frequency domain representations of the heat-diffusion process. Finally, when combining PPT, TSR, and PCT in PT-Fusion, no significant improvements are obtained and the results remain consistent compared to combining PCT and TSR only. In other words, the addition of PPT on top of PCT and TSR can be interpreted as a saturation of input representations, where the PCT–TSR pair already provides the optimal trade-off between efficiency and accuracy for PT inspection tasks.

Generalizibility evaluation

In addition to the conducted ablation studies, we further evaluate the generalization capability of PT-Fusion with respect to defect geometries. The IRT-PVC dataset comprises 38 samples, where 18 have square shape defects and 18 have circular defects. Accordingly, PT-Fusion was trained using the augmented dataset of one defect type, tested on the other, and vice versa, simulating a scenario where defect geometries encountered during testing are not seen during training. This study was essentially added to test whether our framework tends to overfit to defect shapes or can generalize to defects of unseen geometries during training. Table 13 summarizes the obtained binary segmentation and depth estimation performances. When trained on square defects and tested on samples with circular defects, PT-Fusion achieved an IoU of 0.857, MAE of 0.0096 mm, RMSE of 0.0112 mm, and correlation coefficient of 0.985. Similarly, training on circular defects and testing on samples of square defects resulted in an IoU of 0.853, MAE of 0.0089 mm, RMSE of 0.0119 mm, and correlation coefficient of 0.983. In both testing scenarios, the proposed framework demonstrates the ability to generalize without overfitting to defect geometric priors during and training and preserves consistent performance on unseen defect geometries during testing. In addition, the network shows consistent orders of magnitude across all metrics when trained on either defect type.

Discussion

The findings presented in this paper highlight the effectiveness of PT-Fusion in leveraging complementary thermal representations for accurate and reliable defect analysis. By jointly learning from PCT and TSR inputs, the network successfully integrates spatial contrast enhancement with temporal heat-diffusion dynamics, resulting in more discriminative feature representations. This is highlighted in both segmentation and depth-estimation tasks, where PT-Fusion consistently outperforms conventional architectures such as 3D-CNN and attention U-Net. On the other hand, compared to transformer-based baselines, PT-Fusion is able to perform on part with SwinUNet and TransUNet with significantly fewer computational requirements. In addition to the obtained defect analysis accuracies, a key strength of the proposed framework lies in its ability to generalize beyond the specific defect geometries seen during training. The cross-geometry experiments demonstrate that PT-Fusion captures the underlying thermophysical behavior of heat propagation rather than memorizing superficial geometric cues, a property critical for real-world inspection scenarios. Furthermore, the results confirm that the introduced spatiotemporal augmentation strategy enhances data diversity, mitigates overfitting, and improves robustness under limited dataset conditions, a critical challenge in NDT research. Finally, the ablation studies reveal that the AWFG and GEDB fusion modules balance local feature extraction and long-range dependency modeling without the excessive computational burden. This design ensures scalability and practical deployability in industrial settings where inference speed is crucial.

Overview

The direct approach for fusing thermal features from both representations is by either concatenating them at the input or concatenating their feature maps in the intermediate layers. However, the network, in such cases, does not inherently learn the weighted contribution of the extracted features; thereby, limiting its performance. PT-Fusion, shown in Fig. 5, learns to adaptively integrate the complementary features in PCT and TSR images. Given a pulse thermography sequence. PT-Fusion processes these representations using two parallel CNN encoder branches to extract features from each input representation. The encoded feature maps are then integrated using two fusion modules: the Adaptive Weighing Fusion Gate (AWFG), which combines PCT and TSR features at the bottleneck, and the Gating Enhanced Decoding Block (GEDB), which fuses the multi-representation features with the decoder outputs throughout the decoding path. The fused features are finally decoded to generate pixel-wise predictions for either multi-class defect segmentation or simultaneous binary segmentation and defect depth estimation. To train the deep architecture of PT-Fusion and alleviate the scarcity of thermography datasets, a spatiotemporal augmentation strategy is introduced to diversify the thermal responses. Given a raw PT sequence, temporal subsampling is first applied to simulate variations in heat diffusion rates and acquisition durations. Additive noise perturbations are then injected to mimic sensor noise and acquisition artifacts encountered in real inspection settings. The proposed augmentation technique preserves the underlying thermal diffusion characteristics of the defect while generating a wider distribution of thermal appearances, enabling PT-Fusion to learn robust spatial–temporal thermal features under limited data availability.

Data augmentation

As the IRT-PVC dataset comprises 38 inspection sequences, the dataset size is scarce, and training the deep architecture of PT-Fusion remains challenging. While traditional augmentation techniques, such as random rotations, translations, and shearing, introduce spatial variability, they fall short in addressing the spatiotemporal nature of thermographic data. These sequences require augmentations that account for both spatial and temporal dimensions to effectively enhance dataset diversity. Therefore, this work proposes a spatiotemporal augmentation strategy, illustrated in Algorithm 3, to address this limitation. The proposed method involves three steps. First, subsets of thermographic sequences are randomly sampled from the original inspection sequences to introduce temporal variability. Second, additive Gaussian noise is applied on the randomly sampled sequences to further augment the dataset temporally. Finally, PCT and TSR tensors are generated from the randomly sampled sequences as new training samples in the expanded dataset. During training, traditional spatial augmentation techniques are applied dynamically to the generated tensors to enhance the network’s generalizability.

Implementation and training details

After formulating the PT-Fusion architecture incorporating the CNN heads, AWFG, and GEDB with its training data, a Bayesian optimization process over the network is employed to select its hyperparameters. The optimization procedure is initialized with 10 random samples, followed by 40 Bayesian-guided iterations using a Gaussian process surrogate with Expected Improvement acquisition48, i.e. 50 trials total. Each trial was trained for 30 epochs with early stopping, resulting in a computational budget of approximately 1,500 training epochs; hence, approximately 25 GPU hours on RTX 4070. In addition, all network weights were initialized with the PyTorch Glorot Uniform initializer. The hyperparameters include the number of convolutional layers, the number of filters, the kernel sizes, and the batch size. During the optimization process, the network weights are initialized using the built-in Glorot Uniform initializer in PyTorch. The resulting optimal network parameters are listed in Table 14. Conclusion

Infrared thermography (IRT) became prominent in the field of NDT, enabling the detection of subsurface defects in industrial components. Particularly, pulse thermography (PT) is valued for its efficiency and fast nature. Current state-of-the-art PT inspection networks learn to extract thermal features from PCT and TSR image representations independently for defect segmentation and depth estimation. This limits the performance of the trained networks as these representations possess complementary semantic features. Thus, this work proposes PT-Fusion, an attention-based fusion network that fuses thermal features extracted from PCT and TSR representation for enhanced segmentation and depth estimation of subsurface defects. The network architecture introduces two feature fusion modules, namely, Adaptive Weighing Fusion Gate (AWFG) to adaptively learn the weighted contributions of the input representations and Gating Enhanced Decoding Block (GEDB) to dynamically fuse the encoded features with outputs of the PT-Fusion decoders. In addition, due to the scarcity of PT datasets, a novel spatiotemporal data augmentation technique is proposed to mitigate this challenge and facilitate the training of PT-Fusion. The network is tested on the Université Laval IRT-PVC dataset for defect segmentation and depth estimation. PT-Fusion is also benchmarked against state-of-the-art PT network architectures, such as U-Net, attention U-Net, 3D-CNN, Swin-UNet, and TransUNet. The results demonstrate that PT-Fusion outperforms the aforementioned models with an IoU of 0.882 and a depth estimation error of 0.0082 mm. Moreover, a generalizability study has been conducted to test the generalization capability of PT-Fusion with respect to defect geometries. The obtained results highlighted that the proposed framework is able to generalize to unseen defect geometries and does not overfit to geometric priors during training. Future work will aim at constructing larger and more diverse inspection datasets comprising different materials and capturing a wider range of thermal responses and defect types. Consequently, research efforts will be directed towards extending PT-Fusion to lock-in thermography setups and inspection of other materials such as CFRP and PLA. In addition, experimental studies will be conducted on the efficacy of self and cross-attention mechanisms during the inspection of materials with varying thermal conductivities.
