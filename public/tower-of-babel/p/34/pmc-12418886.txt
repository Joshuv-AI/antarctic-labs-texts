Case report: Surgical disconnection for medically refractory epilepsy in ARID1B-related Coffin-Siris syndrome
Authors: Xiao-Lai Ye, Cui-Jing Wang, Min-Zhi Yin, Ru-En Yao, Ying Zhou, Fo-Yang Fan, Qin-Chuan Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) may occur in Coffin–Siris syndrome.•Genetic testing is recommended for children with DEEs.•Consider timely surgical treatment for suitable genetic epilepsy cases.Keywords: Coffin–Siris syndrome, Epilepsy, Surgery

Introduction

Coffin–Siris syndrome (CSS) is a rare multiple congenital anomaly syndrome, first described by Coffin & Siris, 1970 as a triad of intellectual disability, coarse facial features, and hypoplasia of the fifth digit. Associated findings described in subsequent case series include microcephaly, growth insufficiency, hirsutism, hypotonia, and hearing loss [[1], [2], [3]]. Neuroimaging abnormalities are present in approximately 50 % of cases, with corpus callosum dysgenesis and Dandy–Walker malformations being the most frequently reported structural anomalies [4]. Although epilepsy occurs in a small subset of CSS patients, reported cases generally demonstrate good responsiveness to antiseizure medications (ASMs) [5]. We present a genetically confirmed ARID1B-related CSS case exhibiting medically refractory epilepsy associated with right frontal lobe pachygyria and polymicrogyria – a neuropathological constellation not previously described in the CSS literature.

Case description

A right-handed 3-year, ten-month-old girl with a p.Gly434Alafs*12 mutation of the ARID1B gene experienced her first epileptic seizure at 3 years of age, with impaired awareness, drooling, deviation of the eyes, upper and lower limb hypertonia evolving to a tonic-vibratory phase, followed by clonic twitches asynchronously affecting both sides of the body. Seizures occurred initially only during sleep and later also during wakefulness, lasting up to 5 min and self-limiting. The seizure frequency was weekly at onset and rapidly increased up to daily, despite polytherapy with lamotrigine, valproic acid, levetiracetam, and lacosamide. She also experienced focal to bilateral tonic clonic seizures lasting for more than 30 min (status epilepticus) accompanied by moderate fever.

She was born at 40 weeks of gestation after an uncomplicated prenatal period. The motor development was mildly/ moderately delayed: she raised her head up at 8 months, sat with assistance at 9 months, and walked independently at 24 months. She spoke only single syllables or reduplicated words (e.g., “baba”, “mama”) but learned approximately 20 words. The child's features include a large nose, thick eyebrows, and hypertrichosis (Fig. 1). Along with the aggravation of epileptic seizures, the retrogression of speech and motor function was noted. She spoke no words and could not understand simple commands to complete the neuropsychiatric evaluation. She had difficulty feeding and walking by herself and exhibited a tremor whenever she moved or rested (Myoclonic epilepticus status), with a preference for the head, trunk, and both hands at the time of hospitalization. Her 10-year-old brother and parents were healthy. Her maternal grandfather-in-law, who had a history of epilepsy, had unfortunately passed away.

Clinical Photographs and Neurophysiological Findings. Clinical photographs show thick eyebrows, a broad nasal bridge with anteverted nostrils, a broad philtrum, and thick upper and lower lip vermilions, hypertrichosis (A), and low posterior hairline (B), and normal phalanges of all fingers (C) and toes without nail hypoplasia (D). Electroencephalography showed epileptic discharges in the right frontal, central and paracentral areas (E).

Comprehensive metabolic testing was negative. A 0.46 cm minor atrial septal defect was discovered by cardiac ultrasound. The interictal scalp video electroencephalography (EEG) showed abundant or sub-continuous high-voltage spikes and spike-waves involving the right frontal, central and paracentral areas (Fig. 1). The ictal EEG showed continuous discharge of spikes involving the right central and paracentral regions without recruitment corresponded with mild left finger myoclonic seizures. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) showed focal pachygyria with polymicrogyria over the right mid-frontal region. There is apparent cortical thickening with an irregular cortical surface, a ‘stippled’ grey–white junction, and white matter blurring involving the right frontal area (Fig. 2). Polymicrogyria diagnosis was confirmed using the diagnostic criteria established by Leventer et al. [6]. Fluorodeoxyglucose–positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) showed diffuse hypometabolism in the right hemisphere with hypermetabolism in right frontal lobe. Whole-exome sequencing analysis identified a heterozygous frameshift variant in the ARID1B gene: c.1293_1311del (p.Gly434Alafs*12) at genomic position Chr6:g.157100097 (GRCh37). The diagnosis of CSS complicated by medically refractory epilepsy, developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) was ultimately established.

PET show diffuse hypometabolism in the right hemisphere (B [arrow heads]) with multi-focal hypermetabolism (B [asterisks]) in right frontal lobe. Post-surgical T2-weighted Axial MRI images (C) and T1-weighted sagittal MRI images (D) show the disconnection line. Intraoperative photograph of right frontal lobe before (E) and after disconnection of the epileptogenic zone (F).

Following comprehensive presurgical evaluations, a customized disconnection of the epileptogenic zone in the right frontal lobe was performed using a patient-specific three-dimensional cerebral model for anatomical guidance. The postoperative course was uneventful. Histopathological examination of the specimen within the disconnected frontal cortex confirmed the diagnosis of FCD type IIa as defined by the International League Against Epilepsy Task Force (ILAE) (Fig. 3). She has been clinically seizure-free (Engel IA) and is making developmental gains in speech and strength. This continued to be true at 18 months’ post operation with continued polypharmacy including valproate, lamotrigine, and lacosamide.

Histopathological images of the specimen show the features of FCD type IIa. Hematoxylin-eosin staining (200×) shows the presence of cortical dyslamination, and blurred grey–white matter junction without any layered neocortical organization (A). Neurofilament staining (200×) (B), and glial fibrillary acidic protein staining (200×) (C) show the glial cells. Neuron-specific nuclear protein staining (40×) (D, [arrow heads]) and Hematoxylin-eosin staining (40×) (E, [arrow head]) show the dysmorphic neurons devoid of balloon cells.

Discussion

Coffin-Siris syndrome is a rare syndrome, caused by mutations in genes encoding components of the BAF complex [1,7]. ARID1B is the most frequently mutated gene, accounting for 51–75 % of CSS cases [3,8,9]. This radical alteration generates a truncated protein product predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay, resulting in haploinsufficiency of the ARID1B protein. As a critical subunit of the BAF chromatin-remodeling complex, ARID1B deficiency disrupts epigenetic regulation of gene expression – the established disease mechanism for autosomal dominant CSS. CSS include variable degrees of developmental and cognitive delay predominantly affecting speech, hypotonia, dysmorphic facial features (including sparse scalp hair, thick eyebrows, long eyelashes) and hypertrichosis [10].

Epilepsy has been described in one-third of patients harboring pathogenic variants of the ARID1B gene (6q25.3), one of the genes potentially responsible for CSS. The median age of onset of the seizures was 4 years, and all reported patients showed a good response to anticonvulsive therapy [5]. Those that do develop seizures in the literature at large, generally respond well to treatment, with very few failing multiple medications or requiring surgical or dietary intervention for their epilepsy. Our patient exhibited DEE with progressive speech and motor regression. This challenges the prevailing notion of generally favorable epilepsy outcomes in ARID1B variants, highlighting the potential for DEEs. According to a report from the international CSS registry, seizures were controlled with ASMs in all patients except one, who underwent vagus nerve stimulation implantation [11]. Among 14 registry patients with seizures, 3 out of the 14 patients (21 %) exhibited features associated with LGS [12]. Proietti et al. described one patient refractory to multiple medications [13].

CSS arises from pathogenic variants in chromatin remodeling genes, predominantly disrupting midline cortical development. While 40 %-62.5 % of CSS patients exhibit corpus callosum agenesis or Dandy-Walker malformations [1,3,14], our case expands the neuropathological spectrum through the observation of the coexistence of pachygyria and polymicrogyria, which may predispose patients with CSS to medically refractory epilepsy. These overlapping structural anomalies create hyperexcitable neural networks through aberrant synaptic connectivity and impaired inhibitory circuitry [15]. This condition typically manifests within the first three years of life, progressing to DEEs [16]. Polymicrogyria, classified as an malformations of cortical development resulting from abnormal post-migrational neuronal organization [6,[17], [18], [19]], is associated with epilepsy in 65 %–87 % of cases, with seizure onset predominantly occurring before age five [6]. Notably, approximately 65 % of polymicrogyria-related epilepsy cases develop drug resistance, underscoring the need for surgical evaluation in these patients [6,20,21].

This report expands the phenotypic spectrum of ARID1B-related CSS and illustrates the importance of early genetic testing for DEEs, along with surgical evaluation and treatment for genetic epilepsies where applicable. At 18 months post-surgery, our patient achieved complete seizure freedom (Engel class IA) and demonstrated measurable gains in speech articulation and motor strength. The DEEs are among the most challenging epileptic conditions to treat. In addition to frequent, drug-resistant seizures, children with DEEs often exhibit numerous comorbidities that significantly impact their quality of life and that of their families [22]. Genetic testing should be considered a standard component of the pre-surgical work-up for pediatric patients with DEEs. Given the low likelihood of achieving seizure control with pharmacotherapy, surgical intervention should be considered early in the course. Resective or disconnective surgeries aiming to remove the focal epileptogenic lesion or disconnect the affected (part of the) hemisphere offer the highest chances of postsurgical seizure freedom and ASM withdrawal. Furthermore, emerging evidence reveals meaningful seizure reduction following epilepsy surgery in the majority of children with a genetic etiology [23]. Clinically, this report highlights the necessity for comprehensive molecular profiling in CSS patients with seizure presentations and advocates for timely surgical evaluation in those exhibiting medically refractory epilepsy. Future research should prioritize genotype-phenotype correlations to refine prognostic stratification and therapeutic algorithms for ARID1B-related epilepsy.

Conclusion

The present study documents a case of medically refractory epilepsy arising in a pediatric patient with ARID1B-related CSS, accompanied by pachygyria and polymicrogyria. Clinically, this case broadens the phenotypic spectrum of ARID1B-related CSS, underscores the importance of early genetic testing for DEEs, along with timely surgical evaluation and treatment for genetic epilepsies where applicable.
