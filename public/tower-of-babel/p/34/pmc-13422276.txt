Attention capture and satisfaction of search in dental radiography: an eye-tracking study on the impact of dominant pathologies on visual search behavior among dental students
Authors: Furkan Ozbey, Ozge Ozuturk Tokman, Elif Sadik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effect of visually dominant cyctic lesions on dental students’ visual search behavior and the occurrence of the satisfaction of search (SOS) phenomenon during panoramic radiograph interpretation using eye-tracking technology.Materials and methodsA within-subject eye-tracking study was conducted with 60 dental students. Participants completed two viewing sessions: panoramic radiographs containing only subtle findings (carious lesions and small apical radiolucencies; S0) and the same radiographs with an added visually dominant cystic lesion (S1). Eye movements were recorded using a Tobii Pro Spark eye tracker. Fixation-based and visit-based metrics were calculated for predefined areas of interest. Paired comparisons between sessions were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsIn the presence of a dominant cystic lesion (S1), fixation count and total fixation duration directed toward subtle lesions were significantly reduced, whereas time to first fixation was significantly prolonged (p < 0.01). For carious lesions, visit count decreased from 2.40 ± 1.40 to 0.95 ± 0.90 (p = 0.001), and total visit duration declined from 0.88 ± 0.55 s to 0.31 ± 0.28 s (p = 0.002). Similar reductions were observed for apical radiolucencies. Overall viewing duration was shorter in S1 than in S0 (19.07 s vs. 37.51 s). Heatmap and scan path analyses showed a concentration of visual attention on the dominant lesion, with fewer and shorter visits to subtle findings.ConclusionThe presence of visually dominant cyctic lesions was associated with reduced visual attention to subtle findings and shorter overall viewing duration during panoramic radiograph viewing. These findings are consistent with an SOS-like attentional pattern in dental radiology and highlight the importance of systematic scanning strategies in dental education.Keywords: Dental radiology education, Eye-tracking, Panoramic radiography, Satisfaction of search, Visual search behavior

Introduction

Panoramic radiography is a fundamental imaging modality in dental practice because it enables simultaneous evaluation of a wide range of dental and maxillofacial structures within a single image [1]. However, the wide anatomical coverage and visual complexity of panoramic radiographs can split the observer’s attention, making small or low-salience findings harder to detect—especially for less experienced dental students [2, 3]. In this context, eye-tracking technology provides an objective method for examining visual attention allocation, scan patterns, and inspection strategies during radiographic interpretation [3, 4]. Previous studies in dental imaging have shown that visual search behavior differs according to the observer’s level of experience and may influence radiographic interpretation performance [2, 5].

Various cognitive and perceptual errors have been reported in panoramic radiograph interpretation, including excessive attention to dental structures, neglect of peripheral and extra-dental regions, unsystematic scanning behavior, reduced inspection under high cognitive load, and overlooking subtle findings in the presence of more conspicuous abnormalities [2, 5–9]. A prominent example is the satisfaction of search (SOS) phenomenon, which refers to the premature termination of visual search after the detection of an obvious abnormality, leading to omission of additional clinically relevant findings [10, 11]. Given the wide field of view and high information density of panoramic radiographs, SOS may be particularly important for dental students, in whom subtle lesions may be overshadowed by dominant cyctic lesions.

Recent studies indicate substantial diagnostic error rates among dental students when interpreting panoramic radiographs, particularly for subtle lesions in the presence of visually dominant cyctic lesions [12, 13]. Missed diagnoses in panoramic radiography may lead to delayed treatment, disease progression, increased treatment complexity, and compromised patient outcomes [14, 15]. These clinical consequences underscore the need for educational strategies that address systematic cognitive biases during radiographic interpretation [16]. This bias is theoretically supported by the biased competition model of visual attention and the saliency-based attention framework. These models propose that highly salient stimuli may automatically capture attention and disrupt goal-directed search behavior [17, 18]. In line with these models, studies in medical imaging have shown that highly salient findings may attract attention early and contribute to premature search termination [19, 20].

Despite the well-established SOS literature in medical radiology, quantitative eye-tracking evidence for this phenomenon in dental panoramic radiography remains limited. To our knowledge, only one previous study has examined SOS in periapical radiographs, and no study has systematically investigated the attentional dynamics associated with dominant versus subtle findings in panoramic radiographs using both fixation-based and visit-based eye-tracking metrics [21]. Furthermore, the specific influence of dominant cystic lesions on the detection of subtle findings such as caries and apical radiolucencies has not been quantified in dental students, who represent an important target group for educational interventions.

Understanding SOS-like visual attention patterns in dental radiography has educational and potential clinical relevance. Although the present study did not assess diagnostic accuracy, reduced attention to subtle findings may represent a perceptual condition that increases the risk of overlooking clinically relevant abnormalities during radiographic interpretation. For dental students transitioning to clinical practice, maintaining systematic visual search despite the presence of conspicuous findings is an important competency. By quantifying changes in attentional allocation caused by dominant cyctic lesions, this study may provide an evidence base for developing educational interventions aimed at improving systematic radiographic viewing behavior.

The null hypothesis of this study was that the presence of a visually dominant cystic lesion would not significantly alter fixation-based or visit-based eye-tracking metrics directed toward subtle findings in panoramic radiographs among dental students. Accordingly, this study aimed to determine whether dominant cyctic lesions reduce fixation count and total fixation duration directed toward subtle findings, prolong time to first fixation, and reduce revisitation patterns during panoramic radiograph interpretation.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University (Decision No: 2025/716). Participants were provided with general information about the study procedures and data confidentiality, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment. To minimize demand characteristics, the specific study hypothesis was not disclosed before testing.

Study design and sample selection

This study was designed as a two-session, within-subject experimental eye-tracking study aimed at evaluating the SOS effect in panoramic radiographs.

A total of 60 volunteer dental students (30 females and 30 males) enrolled in the clinical phase of their education at the Faculty of Dentistry, Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University, were included. Prior to data collection, a power analysis based on paired measurements (difference between two dependent measures) was conducted using G*Power software (v3.1.9.4). Assuming a two-tailed α = 0.05, statistical power (1 − β) = 0.90, and a medium effect size (dz = 0.50), the minimum required sample size was calculated as 47 participants. To account for potential data loss, 60 participants were recruited.

Eligible participants had normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity, successfully completed the nine-point calibration procedure using the Tobii Pro Spark eye-tracking device, and provided voluntary participation consent. All participants were fourth- or fifth-year dental students who had completed formal coursework in oral and maxillofacial radiology, including at least 20 h of didactic instruction and 10 h of practical training in panoramic radiograph interpretation, and had interpreted approximately 50–100 panoramic radiographs during their clinical training. Exclusion criteria were prior participation in eye-tracking research, current use of medications affecting attention or visual processing (e.g., sedatives, stimulants), history of diagnosed attention deficit disorder, self-reported fatigue or illness on the day of testing, and the presence of strabismus, nystagmus, significant oculomotor or neurological disorders, or unsuccessful calibration.

The sample comprised 60 participants with a mean age of 22.65 ± 0.54 years (range: 22–24 years). All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and 15% (n = 9) used corrective lenses during the experiment.

Participants were instructed to freely inspect the images without searching for a predefined lesion or establishing a diagnosis, because task-specific instructions could have altered natural visual search behavior and attenuated the SOS effect. The aim was not to assess diagnostic accuracy, but to quantify changes in attention allocation to subtle findings in the presence of a clinically salient dominant cyctic lesion.

Eye-tracking environment and equipment

Eye-tracking recordings were conducted in a quiet, controlled room at the Faculty of Dentistry, Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University. Ambient illumination was maintained at approximately 300–350 lx, and environmental noise was kept below 40 dB. Participants were seated upright on an adjustable chair without head stabilization, consistent with the remote recording principles of the eye-tracking system.

Eye movements were recorded using a Tobii Pro Spark eye-tracking device (Tobii AB, Stockholm, Sweden) with a sampling rate of 60 Hz. Data collection and analysis were performed using Tobii Pro Lab software (version 25.19), and fixations were identified using the software’s default I-VT (Velocity-Threshold Identification) filter.

Panoramic radiographs were displayed on a 16-inch IPS monitor (1920 × 1080 resolution, 24-bit color depth, 60 Hz refresh rate, 250 cd/m² brightness, 1000:1 contrast ratio). Monitor brightness and contrast settings were checked for consistency prior to each recording. A nine-point calibration procedure was applied for each participant, and recordings with a data loss rate exceeding 20% were planned to be excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1).

Image material and AOI definitions

Digital panoramic radiographs were selected from the archive of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University. All images were acquired using a Planmeca ProMax 2D digital panoramic unit (Planmeca Inc., Helsinki, Finland) with standardized exposure parameters (65 kV, 5 mA, 14 s).

The radiographs were prepared in two stages. In the first stage, four baseline panoramic radiographs containing only carious lesions and small apical radiolucent areas were used, representing images without a dominant cyctic lesion (Fig. 2a–d). In the second stage, visually conspicuous cystic lesions were digitally added to the same four baseline radiographs to create the dominant cycticlesion condition (Fig. 3a–d).

Panoramic radiographs used in the first stage, containing carious lesions and small apical radiolucent areas without a dominant cyctic lesion (a–d)

Panoramic radiographs used in the second stage, in which visually conspicuous cystic lesions were digitally added to introduce a dominant cyctic lesion (a–d)

The dominant cystic lesions were digitally created in Adobe Photoshop by an oral and maxillofacial radiologist (E.S.) based on panoramic radiographs of histopathologically confirmed cases. Anatomical dimensions, lesion borders, and radiographic density were adjusted to ensure visual realism. The inserted cysts were placed to avoid interference with existing dental findings or disruption of the overall anatomical structure and were distributed across different quadrants to avoid spatial bias. This approach allowed controlled within-image comparisons. It also helped isolate the attentional effects of a visually salient pathology while minimizing variability across images, consistent with previous SOS research in dental radiology [21].

The realism and anatomical conformity of the digitally added dominant cystic lesions were evaluated independently by two oral and maxillofacial radiologists (F.O. and O.O.T) using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not realistic at all, 5 = completely realistic/indistinguishable). The mean realism score was 4.50 ± 0.30, and interobserver agreement was excellent (ICC = 0.85).

The use of digitally modified radiographs was considered ethically appropriate because all original images were anonymized, the modifications were created solely for research purposes, and the methodology is consistent with established experimental practices in imaging research [22, 23].

Areas of interest (AOIs) were manually defined by consensus between two experienced oral and maxillofacial radiologists (F.O. and O.O.T). AOIs were classified as dominant cystic lesions, carious lesions, and small apical radiolucent areas (subtle lesions), and AOI boundaries were kept constant across both experimental sessions (Fig. 4a–h).

Overview of areas of interest (AOIs) on panoramic radiographs. For each case, baseline images and their corresponding versions with digitally added dominant cystic lesions are presented side by side

Procedure and stimulus presentation

The eye-tracking experiment was conducted in two separate sessions. In the first session, participants viewed panoramic radiographs containing only carious lesions and small apical lesions. The second session was conducted one month after the first, during which the same panoramic radiographs were displayed with digitally added dominant cystic lesions. A one-month interval was used to reduce potential recall effects. Previous eye-tracking studies in radiology education suggest that memory for specific radiographic features declines substantially within 2–4 weeks, thereby reducing the risk of recognition-based bias [24–26]. A fixed-sequence design was used, with S0 preceding S1 for all participants. Counterbalancing was not used because the S1 stimuli were manipulated versions of the baseline radiographs shown in S0. Presenting S1 first could have influenced subsequent visual search in S0 through carry-over and recognition effects.

In both sessions, participants were instructed to freely inspect the panoramic radiographs. They were not asked to establish a diagnosis, mark findings, or provide verbal or written feedback. No time restriction was imposed on image evaluation; participants progressed through the images at their own pace using a keyboard key press. This approach was deliberately chosen to observe the natural termination of the visual search process and to elicit the SOS effect.

The order of image presentation was randomized for each participant prior to each session using the Design module of the Tobii Pro Lab software. Participants were informed about the study as a general “visual inspection study,” without explicit reference to the SOS phenomenon, in order to reduce potential demand characteristics.

Eye-tracking metrics

Eye-tracking analysis was conducted using fixation-based and visit-based parameters to characterize visual attention allocation and scanning behavior. Fixations were identified using the I-VT (velocity-threshold identification) algorithm (velocity threshold = 30°/s; minimum fixation duration = 60 ms).

Fixation-based metrics

Fixations represent periods of relative ocular stability during which visual information is processed. The following fixation-based parameters were calculated for each area of interest (AOI):

Fixation Count: Total number of fixations recorded within an AOI during image evaluation. This parameter reflects the frequency of information sampling within a region.

Fixation Count: Total number of fixations recorded within an AOI during image evaluation. This parameter reflects the frequency of information sampling within a region.

Total Fixation Duration (s): Cumulative duration of all fixations within an AOI. This metric represents the total time spent processing visual information in a given region.

Total Fixation Duration (s): Cumulative duration of all fixations within an AOI. This metric represents the total time spent processing visual information in a given region.

Average Fixation Duration (s): Mean duration of individual fixations within an AOI (total fixation duration divided by fixation count). This parameter reflects the average processing time per fixation.

Average Fixation Duration (s): Mean duration of individual fixations within an AOI (total fixation duration divided by fixation count). This parameter reflects the average processing time per fixation.

Time to First Fixation (s): Time elapsed from image onset to the first fixation within an AOI. This metric indicates the temporal priority assigned to a region during visual search.

Time to First Fixation (s): Time elapsed from image onset to the first fixation within an AOI. This metric indicates the temporal priority assigned to a region during visual search.

First Fixation Duration (s): Duration of the initial fixation within an AOI. This parameter may reflect initial cognitive processing upon first engagement with the region.

First Fixation Duration (s): Duration of the initial fixation within an AOI. This parameter may reflect initial cognitive processing upon first engagement with the region.

Visit-based metrics

A visit was defined as a continuous sequence of one or more fixations within the same AOI, bounded by fixations outside that AOI. Visit-based metrics capture the broader organizational structure of visual scanning behavior.

Visit Count: Total number of entries (including re-entries) into an AOI during image evaluation.

Visit Count: Total number of entries (including re-entries) into an AOI during image evaluation.

Total Visit Duration (s): Cumulative duration of all visits to an AOI, including all fixations within each visit episode.

Total Visit Duration (s): Cumulative duration of all visits to an AOI, including all fixations within each visit episode.

Average Visit Duration (s): Mean duration of individual visit episodes (total visit duration divided by visit count).

Average Visit Duration (s): Mean duration of individual visit episodes (total visit duration divided by visit count).

Fixation-based metrics characterize micro-level attentional processing (individual information sampling events), whereas visit-based metrics reflect macro-level scanning organization (region entry and re-entry patterns). Together, these parameters provide a comprehensive description of visual attention distribution and search strategy.

Heat map and scan path analysis

To visualize the distribution of visual attention across the entire participant cohort, aggregated heat maps were generated for each conspicuity level using Tobii Pro Lab software. Heat maps represent the spatial density of fixations, with warmer colors (red) indicating areas of concentrated visual attention and cooler colors (green, blue) representing lower fixation density. Individual scan path maps were also generated to illustrate the temporal sequence and spatial trajectory of eye movements for representative participants. Scan paths display the order of fixations (numbered circles) connected by saccadic movements (lines), providing insight into the search strategies employed by individual students.

Eye-tracking variables and data analysis

For each AOI, the following variables were analyzed: fixation count, total fixation duration, average fixation duration, time to first fixation, visit count, total visit duration, and average visit duration. An AOI was considered visually inspected if at least one fixation was recorded within its boundaries.

Fixations were identified using the Tobii I-VT algorithm (velocity threshold = 30°/s, minimum fixation duration = 60 ms, maximum dispersion = 0.5°), consistent with established standards in eye-tracking research [3]. Data quality was assessed for each participant using the following criteria: (1) calibration accuracy < 1.0° visual angle; (2) data loss < 20% per trial; (3) valid fixation rate > 80% of total recording time; and (4) absence of systematic drift (defined as > 2° deviation from calibration points during validation). All 60 participants met these quality criteria for both sessions, and no datasets were excluded due to data quality issues.

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and paired comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Effect sizes for Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were calculated as r = |Z|/√N, where Z represents the standardized Wilcoxon test statistic and N represents the number of paired observations included in each comparison.

Results

All 60 participants completed both experimental sessions, and no datasets were excluded because of calibration failure or excessive data loss. Mean calibration accuracy was 0.58 ± 0.12° visual angle, and the average data loss rate was 8.3 ± 4.2% (range: 2.1–18.7%), remaining below the prespecified 20% threshold in all recordings. The study sample comprised 60 dental students (30 females, 30 males) with a mean age of 22.65 ± 0.54 years.

In the presence of a dominant cystic lesion (S1), fixation count and total fixation duration decreased, whereas time to first fixation increased for both carious lesions and apical radiolucent areas. For carious lesions, fixation count decreased from 3.02 ± 2.10 in S0 to 0.84 ± 0.95 in S1 (p < 0.001), and total fixation duration declined from 0.98 ± 0.62 s to 0.26 ± 0.23 s (p < 0.001). Time to first fixation increased from 1.65 ± 0.90 s to 4.10 ± 1.60 s (p < 0.001). For apical radiolucent areas, fixation count decreased from 1.98 ± 1.55 to 0.67 ± 0.80 (p = 0.003), while time to first fixation increased from 1.90 ± 1.00 s to 3.60 ± 1.50 s (p = 0.008).

Discussion

This study provides objective eye-tracking evidence that the presence of visually dominant cystic lesions was associated with an SOS-like redistribution of visual attention during panoramic radiograph viewing. In the dominant cyctic lesion condition (S1), both fixation- and visit-based metrics toward subtle findings were reduced. In addition, time to first fixation was prolonged, indicating delayed and diminished attention to secondary findings such as carious lesions and apical radiolucencies [7, 21, 27, 28]. These findings support the biased competition model of visual attention, suggesting that highly salient stimuli attract more attention [17]. In panoramic radiography, dominant cystic lesions with high contrast and spatial extent may draw visual attention, thereby reducing the attention allocated to smaller, less conspicuous abnormalities [28–32].

The fixation-based findings suggest that stimulus-driven salience may disrupt systematic visual search by capturing attention early and delaying inspection of other regions [2, 28, 30, 33]. At the same time, the visit-based findings indicate that participants not only looked less often at subtle lesions, but also returned to them less frequently and for shorter durations. Together, these patterns suggest that SOS in this context is not simply a decision-related ‘found-and-stopped’ error. Rather, it appears to reflect a broader perceptual narrowing process that affects both the depth and organization of visual search [5, 6, 27]. The convergence of fixation- and visit-based metrics therefore suggests that dominant cyctic lesions may alter the distribution and organization of visual attention, rather than merely redirecting gaze to a single region [2, 5, 6].

From an educational perspective, these findings highlight the limitations of intuitive or unsystematic “look-and-find” approaches during panoramic radiograph interpretation. In the presence of a dominant cyctic lesion, students allocated less visual attention to more common but less conspicuous findings, such as carious lesions and small apical radiolucencies. Accordingly, structured reading strategies, checklist-based scanning approaches, and eye-tracking–based feedback may help reduce susceptibility to SOS. These approaches encourage a more systematic and complete review of all anatomical regions before attention is anchored to a single salient abnormality [6, 33, 34].

The potential implications of this attentional pattern may extend beyond dental education. In routine dental practice, panoramic radiography is a fundamental imaging modality [1], and the presence of a conspicuous finding may draw attention away from concurrent but subtler abnormalities, consistent with the SOS phenomenon [2, 5–11]. This may be clinically relevant because insufficient visual attention to caries or apical pathology could increase the risk of overlooking these findings in routine interpretation [14, 15]. Our findings therefore support the value of systematic scanning behavior and quality-oriented interpretation strategies in dental radiology training and practice [6, 33, 34].

The present study has several strengths, including the standardization of panoramic radiographs, consensus-based AOI definition, the combined use of fixation- and visit-based metrics, and a within-subject design that reduced interindividual variability. In addition, the use of manipulated versions of the same baseline radiographs allowed controlled comparison between conditions and helped isolate the attentional effect of the dominant cyctic lesion.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the sample consisted exclusively of fourth- and fifth-year dental students, which limits generalizability to experienced clinicians. It is plausible that specialists with greater expertise and more developed cognitive schemas may be better able to resist attentional capture and maintain systematic search through greater cognitive flexibility. Future studies should therefore compare different expertise levels to determine whether SOS decreases with professional experience.

Second, the study used a task-free design. Although this approach was deliberately selected to observe natural visual search behavior and minimize task-induced bias, it may not fully reflect clinical situations in which observers actively search for specific pathologies. Third, diagnostic accuracy was not measured. While eye-tracking metrics provide valuable information about attentional allocation, they do not directly establish whether reduced attention to subtle lesions results in missed diagnoses. Future studies should therefore combine eye-tracking with diagnostic performance outcomes.

Fourth, the eye-tracking device operated at 60 Hz. Although this sampling rate is sufficient for fixation- and visit-based analyses, it may not capture very brief or subtle eye movements with high precision. Higher-frequency systems may allow more detailed characterization of saccadic dynamics and microsaccadic behavior. Fifth, the external validity of the findings is limited by the controlled laboratory setting and the use of digitally modified radiographs. Clinical interpretation in practice occurs under more variable conditions, often in the presence of time pressure and additional patient information. Although the one-month interval and the rationale for fixed sequencing were intended to minimize recall and carry-over, a residual order effect cannot be fully excluded. In addition, the number of stimulus images was limited to four baseline panoramic radiographs and their corresponding modified versions. Although this design allowed controlled within-image comparisons between the baseline and dominant cyctic lesion conditions and reduced image-related variability, the limited stimulus set may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader clinical image populations. Future studies should include larger and more systematically varied image sets to confirm whether similar attentional patterns are observed across different lesion types, locations, and radiographic presentations. Finally, individual cognitive factors such as working memory, attentional control, and perceptual expertise were not evaluated and may also influence susceptibility to SOS.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates, through eye-tracking data, that the presence of a dominant cystic lesion on panoramic radiographs was associated with significant changes in the visual search behavior of dental students and reduced visual attention allocated to subtle findings, such as carious lesions and small apical radiolucencies. In the session in which a dominant cyctic lesion was introduced, reductions in fixation-based and visit-based metrics directed toward subtle findings, together with delayed initial attentional orienting, suggested an SOS-like attentional pattern during panoramic radiograph viewing. These findings underscore the importance of systematic scanning strategies and educational approaches aimed at enhancing cognitive awareness during the evaluation of panoramic radiographs.
