Comparing great apes reveals human-specific ZEB2 roles in neural development
Authors: Jeong-Eun Lee, Vladimir M Jovanovic, Amanda Jager Fonseca, Sebastian Streblow, Katja Ettig, Stefano Berto, Katja Nowick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHumans differ from other primates in various traits, despite nearly identical protein-coding sequences. Understanding the evolution of these differences requires studying transcriptional regulation. Here, we examine ZEB2, a transcription factor crucial for immune and neural development, to explore its regulatory divergence across great apes. Using B-lymphoblastoid cells, chosen as experimental model system due to the availability of biological replicates for three great ape species, we show that, in addition to conserved ZEB2 targets, human ZEB2 is distinct in regulating a larger repertoire of genes implicated in neuronal development. ZEB2 knock-down in human, chimpanzee, and orangutan B-lymphoblastoid cells followed by transcriptome profiling uncovered human-specific regulatory differences, especially in nervous system-related genes. Additional analysis using single-cell RNA-Seq and brain organoid data identified cell-type-specific differences in ZEB2 expression and regulated genes between humans and other apes, most pronounced in ventral progenitors and neurons. Moreover, human-specific ZEB2 targets are enriched in non-coding genes, suggesting an expanded and possibly rewired regulatory network. Our study demonstrates that species differences in ZEB2 regulation can be detected in a controlled cell system and validated in neural contexts. More broadly, we provide new insights into the functional divergence of TFs across closely related species and how regulatory shifts can contribute to phenotypic evolution.

Introduction

Humans exhibit unique cognitive abilities linked to distinct brain morphology and molecular traits that set them apart from non-human great apes, including the genera chimpanzees, gorillas, and orangutans. Key differences include an extended neurogenesis period, resulting in a neocortex three times larger than that of chimpanzees (Lui et al. 2011; Florio and Huttner 2014; Benito-Kwiecinski et al. 2021), supporting advanced cognitive functions such as language, memory, and decision-making (Enard et al. 2002; Kalebic et al. 2018; Suzuki et al. 2018). Genes like ARHGAP11B (Kalebic et al. 2018), NOTCH2NL (Suzuki et al. 2018), FOXP2 (Enard et al. 2002), and ZEB2 (Benito-Kwiecinski et al. 2021) have been implicated in these evolutionary developments.

The transcription factor (TF) ZEB2 (Zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 2, also known as ZFHX1B and SIP1) is essential for brain development and neuronal differentiation (Hegarty et al. 2015; Benito-Kwiecinski et al. 2021). Further, it takes part in epithelial–to–mesenchymal transition (EMT), immune cell development and oncogenesis (Comijn et al. 2001; Seuntjens et al. 2009; El-Kasti et al. 2012; Weng et al. 2012; Rogers et al. 2013). Mutations in one ZEB2 allele lead to haploinsufficiency in humans causing Mowat-Wilson syndrome, characterized by intellectual disability, microcephaly, seizures, distinctive facial features, and Hirschsprung disease (Mowat 2003; El-Kasti et al. 2012). Compared to gorillas, humans show a delayed role for ZEB2 in transitioning neural epithelial cells to basal radial glial cells, which, combined with a faster cell cycle, expands the neural progenitor pool, contributing to increased neuron numbers and larger brain size in humans (Benito-Kwiecinski et al. 2021). Our previous studies revealed that ZEB2 is expressed at significantly higher levels in the human adult prefrontal cortex compared to chimpanzees (Nowick et al. 2009). In humans, but not chimpanzees, it acts as a central hub in a TF network (Nowick et al. 2009), suggesting recent evolutionary differences in interaction partners and target genes of ZEB2. These functions of ZEB2, along with the phenotypes caused by its mutations, make ZEB2 a prime candidate for studying its impact on unique neural traits and human-specific cellular evolution.

To investigate the functional difference of ZEB2 in great apes, we performed knock-down (KD) experiments followed by RNA-Seq in human, chimpanzee and orangutan B-lymphoblastoid cells (henceforth B-cells). This cell model enabled us to work with three biological replicates per species under standardized conditions, providing the statistical power and experimental control necessary to robustly detect species differences in ZEB2 functions. Although not brain-derived, B-cells offer a tractable and reproducible system for this initial comparative analysis. We found that human but not chimpanzee and orangutan ZEB2 targets in B-cells are significantly enriched for genes involved in neuronal development and brain function. We subsequently also identified species differences in a more physiologically relevant context, by analyzing publicly available single-cell RNA-Seq and brain organoid datasets from great apes (Kanton et al. 2019; Benito-Kwiecinski et al. 2021). Together, our analyses reveal conserved and species-specific ZEB2 functions, with a notable shift in humans toward regulating genes implicated in neural development.

Results

Genes affected by ZEB2 KD in human B-cells have neuronal functions

We started by performing ZEB2 KD experiments with two specific siRNAs in B-cells of three human, chimpanzee and orangutan individuals (Fig. 1a). Samples cluster by genus, with the effect of the KD on overall gene expression patterns being comparatively low (Fig. 1c). Upon ZEB2 KD we detected 97, 76, and 626 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05) in humans, chimpanzees, and orangutans, respectively (Fig. 1b; Table S1). DEGs found only in one primate were considered as lineage-specific. Most DEGs were down-regulated across genera, suggesting that ZEB2 may act as a direct activator contrary to its previously characterized repressive role in TGF-β signaling (Postigo and Dean 1999; Verschueren et al. 1999; Verstappen et al. 2008) or as an indirect activator by repressing another repressor. An example of a human specifically up-regulated gene is GATA3 (Table S1), which is important for B-cell differentiation (Wan 2014). Interestingly, in humans but not the other primates, neuronal genes like SNCG, SNAP25, and PPFIA2 (Table S1), which are associated with synaptic and neurotransmitter functions and several brain disorders (autism, schizophrenia, dementia) (Nishioka et al. 2010; Najera et al. 2019; Marcó de la Cruz et al. 2024), were among the down-regulated genes. Furthermore, ZIC1 is down- and DLX3 is up-regulated by human ZEB2 (Table S1). This pair of TFs is expressed at the border between neural ectoderm and superficial ectoderm during the formation of the neural plate border (Esmaeli et al. 2024), where ZEB2-regulated EMT occurs. By differentially regulating the expression of DLX3 and ZIC1 ZEB2 seems to play a role in early neural and ectodermal cell fate decisions. Notably, DLX1 and DLX2, which are paralogs of the ZEB2-repressed DLX3, are known activators of ZEB2 expression (McKinsey et al. 2013).

In addition to comparing DEGs between genera, we also singled out genes that differed between genera in how they changed in response to ZEB2 KD and termed those genes diffDEGs (Fig. 1a; Table S1, see Methods). This resulted in 70 diffDEGs in humans vs chimpanzees, 101 in humans vs orangutans, and 85 in chimpanzees vs orangutans (Fig. 1d; Table S1). Over 50% of diffDEGs were non-coding genes (Fig. 1i), which is a higher percentage than observed for DEGs (Fig. 1h). 28 diffDEGs showed human-specific expression changes with a consistent expression change in the opposite direction in the other two genera, indicating a functional switch in humans (Fig. 1g, marked red). For example, SHANK3, a gene linked to autism (Zhou et al. 2016), was up-regulated in humans but down-regulated in chimpanzees and orangutans following ZEB2 KD. Fifteen of these human-specific diffDEGs code for proteins and showed enrichment in three categories (Fig. 1f; [1], [2], and [3]), including neuronal differentiation and cell morphogenesis functions, while no neuron-related functional enrichment was observed in chimpanzee- or orangutan-specific diffDEGs (Table S2).

Species-specific expression pattern during neuronal development in relation to ZEB2

After identifying indications of unique human ZEB2 functions related to neuronal development in B-cells, we followed up by investigating evolutionary functional differences of ZEB2 in a neuronal context. To this end, we analyzed a neuronal RNA-Seq dataset (Benito-Kwiecinski et al. 2021) derived from human and gorilla cells, spanning stages from iPSC through seven time points of neuronal differentiation (Fig. 2a). PCA revealed species-specific clustering (Figure S1a). Consistent with prior findings, ZEB2 expression started earlier in gorillas than humans (Figure S1b). To identify potential ZEB2 target genes, we screened for genes correlated with ZEB2 expression across time points (Fig. 2c; Table S3). We found more correlations in humans, with a notable increase in negative correlations (66% in humans versus 56% in gorillas), suggesting more repressive ZEB2 activity in developing human neurons. Of these, 1,607 genes were correlated in both species, with only 60 genes displaying opposing correlations, indicating a high degree of consistency across species. Many of these correlated genes have known ZEB2-related functions, such as regulation of cell motility/migration, morphogenesis and developmental processes (Fig. 2c; Table S4). In addition, we determined 5,838 genes that were exclusively correlated with human ZEB2: negatively correlated genes were enriched for ion homeostasis and immune response functions, while positively correlated genes were associated with nervous system development, including head/brain development and neuronal differentiation.

We next zoomed into ZEB2 functions in individual cell types, utilizing cerebral organoid scRNA-Seq datasets from human and chimpanzee (Kanton et al. 2019) (Fig. 2a). We identified nine cell clusters representing various neural developmental stages from iPSCs to organoids (Fig. 2b; Figure S1d and e; see also Methods), as in the original study. ZEB2 was most highly expressed in developmentally older cell types, such as GM/S dorsal progenitors/radial glia, ventral progenitors, and cortical neurons (Fig. 2b and d; Figure S1c and e), with higher expression levels generally in chimpanzees across all cell types except mesenchymal-like cells (Fig. 2d). We determined conserved and species-specific ZEB2-correlated genes for each cell type (Table S5). For many cell types, ZEB2-correlated genes were more similar between species than between cell types of the same species (Fig. 2f). Cortical neurons, mesenchymal and GM/S dorsal progenitors/radial glia cells showed the highest overlap in ZEB2 correlated genes between humans and chimpanzees (Fig. 2e and f; Table S5). On the other hand, ventral progenitors and neurons as well as neural stem cell (NSC) displayed strong divergence in ZEB2 correlations. Despite ventral progenitors and neurons expressing ZEB2 in both species at relatively similar levels (Figure S1c), this cell type was characterized by a large number of human-specifically correlated genes (Fig. 2d and e; Table S5). In contrast to chimpanzee ventral progenitor cells, correlation patterns of human ventral progenitors and neurons clustered more closely with cortical neurons and GM/S dorsal progenitors and radial glia cells (Fig. 2f).

Across all cell types, 214 genes were correlated with ZEB2 in both species. They are involved in metabolism, cellular organization, and development (Table S6). Human-specific correlations included 789 genes enriched in cell cycle and neurogenesis functions, while 268 chimpanzee-specific genes were linked to axonogenesis (Table S6), a process taking place at later developmental stages (Benito-Kwiecinski et al. 2021; Schörnig et al. 2021). While mesenchymal and cortical neurons had more ZEB2 correlated genes in chimpanzees, GM/S dorsal progenitors/radial glia cell type and ventral progenitors had more correlated genes in humans, pointing to a stronger importance of ZEB2 in those cells in either species and/or potential species-specific functions of ZEB2 in those cell types. Interestingly, though, for cortical neurons, many enriched functions were quite similar between shared and human-specifically ZEB2 correlated genes, suggesting that functions of cortical neurons could be achieved by a set of shared ZEB2 correlated genes, but also have human-specific modifications (Fig. 2g; Table S6). In contrast, in GM/S dorsal progenitors/radial glia, enriched functions varied notably between shared and human-specific correlations, suggesting new ZEB2 functions in humans. Functional differences were also seen between positively and negatively correlated genes; e.g. in ventral progenitors and neurons in humans, positively correlated genes were enriched in signaling and cognition-related GO categories, whereas negatively correlated genes were enriched in metabolism, RNA biosynthesis, and gene expression (Fig. 2g). Taken together, our findings indicate that the functional divergence of ZEB2 and its target gene regulation differs between cell types and tissue contexts, with human-specific roles emerging particularly in neural developmental stages like ventral progenitors.

Discussion

Our cross-species analysis confirms that ZEB2 maintains a core set of ancestral regulatory functions. Beyond this conserved basis, our data also reveal a significant remodeling of ZEB2's regulatory landscape in the human lineage. Crucially, these evolutionary changes appear to have preferentially targeted genes associated with neuronal functions and neurodevelopmental disorders. This consistent enrichment, observed across different analyses and datasets, strongly implies that the rewiring of ZEB2's regulatory network has been a significant factor in the evolution of the human brain. Remarkably, we repeatedly detected a high proportion of non-coding genes among the potential human-specific ZEB2 targets. For example, 33% of diffDEGs in RNA-Seq data from B-cells were lncRNAs. Many of those non-coding genes seem to not have orthologs in non-human primates. Though limited lncRNA annotations in non-human primate genomes warrant cautious interpretation, our findings align with recent reports on human-specific lncRNAs (Zimmer-Bensch 2019). Given the regulatory roles of lncRNAs (Perdomo-Sabogal et al. 2014) and considering the emerging roles of lncRNAs in human brain evolution (Zimmer-Bensch 2019) it seems likely that ZEB2 contributes to this evolution by recruiting lncRNAs into its gene regulatory networks.

A key consideration in interpreting our findings is the use of B-cells for our KD experiments. While these cells are not neuronal in nature, they offered a unique advantage for testing our primary hypothesis: whether ZEB2 exhibits species-specific regulatory activity. The availability of matched cell lines with three biological replicates per species allowed us to perform these experiments under highly controlled conditions and detect robust, statistically supported interspecies differences in ZEB2 functions. Ultimately, the same experiments should be performed in a neuronal context, however, this is still difficult to achieve using iPSC-derived neurons or organoids due to the lack of sufficient biological repeats and interspecies differences in differentiation dynamics. Nevertheless, our findings were supported by the analysis of independent, brain-relevant transcriptomic datasets from great ape organoids and single-cell RNA-Seq, which confirmed the existence of species differences. Thus, we view our experiments as a discovery platform for identifying evolutionarily relevant regulatory differences, providing an important first step and a prioritized set of candidate targets to pursue in neuronal or brain systems.

In summary, our results reveal that ZEB2 maintains some conserved regulatory functions across primates. However, it also displays significant species-specific differences. Our results strongly suggest a unique regulatory role of human ZEB2, especially regarding genes associated with brain functions and nervous system development even in B-cells.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culturing

B-Lymphoblastoid cell lines immortalized by Epstein–Barr virus of three individuals in each primate genus were used in this study: human lines (GM18558, GM18960, GM19240), chimpanzee lines (Judith, Leo, Maryke), and lines from two orangutan species (Guchi, Jago, JinJing). Human B-cells were purchased from the Coriell Institute for Medical Research. NHPs B-cells were obtained from the Biomedical Primate Research Center. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 media [Sigma Aldrich, cat: R0883] supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum [Sigma Aldrich, cat: F1051], 2 mM L-glutamine [Sigma Aldrich, cat: G7513], and 5,000 U/ml Pen/Strep [Sigma Aldrich, cat: P4333] at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

siRNA design and ZEB2 KD experiment

siRNA transfection was performed following the DharmaFECT 1 protocol. Transfection was conducted with either a negative control siRNA or one of three different siRNAs designed for targeting ZEB2 (ZEB2-siRNA1: 5′- GGCAUAUGGUGACACACAA—3′, ZEB2-siRNA2: 5′- CUACGUACUUUAAUAGAUU—3′, ZEB2-siRNA3: 5′- GAACAGACAGGCUUACUUA—3′. Untreated cells served as an additional control. First, one individual cell line per genus was used for testing transfection and KD efficiency. Cells were harvested 3, 6, 12, and 24 h after transfection and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction using Qiagen RNeasy kit [Qiagen, cat: 74004]. KD efficiency for ZEB2 was tested by quantitative reverse transcription (qRT)-PCR with TaqMan™ [Thermo Fisher, cat: 4351370] (Figure S2a to c). The two best performing siRNAs, ZEB2-siRNA2 and ZEB2-siRNA3, and the time point of 6 h were chosen for knocking-down ZEB2 expression in all B-cells using the same protocol. Total RNA was extracted 6 h after transfection from untransfected cells, the negative control and the two ZEB2 KD samples, and ZEB2 amounts were again determined with qRT-PCR and TaqMan™ (Figure S2d). The same RNA was used for RNA-Seq.

RNA-Seq library preparation and sequencing

For RNA-Seq, 500 ng of total RNA underwent library synthesis using TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit v2 [Illumina, cat: RS-122-2001] following the manufacturer's protocol. The resulting barcoded libraries were cleaned up and quantified with the KAPA Library Quantification Kits—Illumina/Universal [Roche, cat: KK4824] on a TaqMan™ 7500 Real Time PCR System. A pool comprising up to 10 libraries was used for cluster generation at a concentration of 10 nM on an Illumina cBot. Sequencing of paired-end 100 bp fragments was performed using an Illumina HighScan-SQ sequencer at the sequencing core facility of the IZKF Leipzig (Faculty of Medicine, University Leipzig) employing version 3 chemistry and flow cell according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Read processing, mapping, and gene quantification

The sequencing quality of RNA-Seq was assessed using FastQC v.0.11.8 (Andrews 2010). Subsequently, adapter sequences and low-quality reads were trimmed using cutadapt v. 2.10 (Martin 2011). The preprocessed FASTQ files of RNA-Seq were then mapped to their respective references obtained from UCSC Table Browser, which included hg38 (Raney et al. 2024), panTro6 (The Chimpanzee Sequencing and Analysis Consortium 2005), ponAbe3 (Kronenberg et al. 2018), and gorGor6 (Gordon et al. 2016). The alignment of RNA-Seq datasets was conducted using HISAT2 v.2.1.0 (Kim et al. 2019). The final RNA-Seq read sequences were annotated to human genomic features (GENCODE Human release 33; GRCh38.p13 [Frankish et al. 2019]) using FeatureCounts (Liao et al. 2014). The assignment was done with parameters “-M”, “-O”, and “-- fraction” to increase the assignment rate by allowing multi-mapping, assignment of reads to all their overlapping meta-features, and assignment of fractional counts. The resulting BAM files were “humanized” by converting the non-human primate genome coordinates into the human genome, accomplished using crossmap.py v. 0.4.0 (Zhao et al. 2014) and UCSC liftover files (Hinrichs et al. 2006) to address potential species-specific biases in genome annotations and mapping efficiency.

Analysis of differential gene expression

The annotated RNA-Seq reads were utilized for conducting differential gene (DE) expression analysis using the R Bioconductor package DESeq2 v.1.38.3 (Love et al. 2014) in R 4.2.2 (R Core Team 2022). To discern the species-specific effects of ZEB2 KD while controlling for effects on an individual biological replicate and the difference between two siRNAs, we adopted a nested approach, whereby the design of the DESeq2 model was set as “species + species:biorep + species:condition”. Initially, focus was directed towards genes specifically altered by ZEB2 KD in each species, followed by an examination of the significant differences in ZEB2 KD effects between species. Genes showing significant differential expression (DEGs and diffDEGs) were defined by the P-value threshold (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05).

scRNA-Seq data preparation

The preprocessed data, including alignment and annotation, were accessible in the RDS file format on Mendeley Data provided by authors of the original study (Kanton et al. 2019), and we specifically used those derived from human and chimpanzee samples. Seurat v. 4.3.0 (Hao et al. 2021, 2024) was applied to further process these RDS files in R 4.2.2 (R Core Team 2022). For each Seurat object corresponding to each species, we conducted normalization and variance stabilization using the SCTransform function. Integration of these datasets was performed using the PrepSCTIntegration after selecting a list of informative features through the SelectIntegrationFeatures. Subsequently, PCA was applied to the integrated Seurat object. The top 24 PCs, determined by ElbowPlot, were employed for clustering with a resolution of 0.1. The choice of the resolution parameter underwent multiple examinations to ensure correspondence with the marker gene expression, aiming for the most comprehensive cell type annotation. Cluster identities were assigned based on cluster gene markers as determined by FindAllMarkers with a minimum percentage of cells expressed set to 0.25 and a log-fold change threshold of 0.25. The marker gene expression profiles of these clusters were then compared against established marker gene expressions compiled from databases, eg PanglaoDB (Franzén et al. 2019), and the original publication (Kanton et al. 2019). After a thorough examination of the top 10 and 100 marker genes in each cluster, we determined that the comparison with the original paper offered the most promising results with the best overlap in cell types between clusters and marker genes. Therefore, we adopted this approach for our analysis.

Pearson correlation test

We performed the Pearson correlation tests to identify genes with functional relationship to ZEB2 in each cell type in scRNA-Seq data. The P-values from the correlation test were then adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and a significance threshold of an adjusted P-value of 0.05 and correlation coefficient |r| > 0.5 was applied. The same approach was applied to the RNA-Seq in neurons data (Benito-Kwiecinski et al. 2021) derived from differentiating iPSCs to neurons within a narrower time span (day 0, 2, 3, 5, 10, 25) than scRNA-Seq data (Kanton et al. 2019) (up to 4 months-old organoids) (Fig. 2a). For RNA-Seq in neurons we conducted the Pearson correlation test for genes expressed across the entire time frame. The significance threshold for this correlation test was set to an adjusted P-value of 0.05, with a correlation coefficient |r| greater than 0.1. The sample sizes in the RNA-Seq in neurons dataset were comparable between humans and gorillas. In contrast, sample sizes in scRNA-Seq varied, primarily due to differences in the number of cells across species and cell types. This variability was further amplified by the selective inclusion of only ZEB2-expressing cells, (Figure S1c and d). All cell types, excluding stem cells (which were of marginal interest in this study), included at least 250 cells, thereby ensuring adequate statistical power (Schönbrodt and Perugini 2013).

GO group and KEGG pathway enrichment

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of all genes identified in our study, eg genes closest to peaks and DEGs/diffDEGs, were conducted using ShinyGO v. 0.80 (Ge et al. 2020). Background genes for the DEGs and diffDEGs identified in RNA-Seq in B-cells and for the correlated genes in RNA-Seq in neurons were defined as those with a sum of normalized read counts greater than 10 per gene. For correlated genes detected in scRNA-Seq in neurons, background genes were those with a normalized read count greater than zero in the respective cell type. GO results and KEGG pathways were filtered using a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.05, with a minimum of 5 enriched genes or 10% of the input gene set. We primarily examined enrichments within “Biological Processes” and “Molecular Functions”, selectively displaying enriched groups in the figures, while complete lists of significantly enriched GO terms can be found in the Supplementary Tables.
