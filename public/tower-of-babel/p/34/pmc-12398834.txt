Introduction

There is growing interest among certain consumer segments in food products that provide enhanced safety, greater transparency, and perceived health benefits [1]. This trend aligns with global challenges such as population growth, climate change and limited natural resources, which demand urgent innovations for a more sustainable food system [2]. Achieving this balance requires innovative solutions that enhance food production efficiency while minimizing ecological harm [3].

In this context, functional foods, which incorporate bioactive compounds with health-promoting properties, have gained much attention in the last few years [4,5]. Emulsions present an excellent technical approach for addressing these products, as they can encapsulate and protect bioactive compounds from degradation and control their release [5]. Oil-in-water emulsions are based on oil droplets dispersed in an aqueous phase, stabilized by emulsifying agents that prevent coalescence and enhance stability. These emulsifiers are typically amphiphilic molecules that adsorb at interfaces, reducing interfacial tension and forming a protective layer [6,7]. Typical emulsifiers include proteins, polysaccharides, phospholipids, surfactants, and solid particles [8]. The pH plays a crucial role in protein-stabilized emulsions because it directly influences the solubility, charge, and structural behavior of proteins. Proteins have an isoelectric point (pI) where their net charge is zero, leading to reduced solubility and a higher tendency to aggregate, which destabilizes emulsions [9].

Two primary approaches are used to produce emulsions: high-energy and low-energy methods. High-energy methods apply intense mechanical forces to break up macroscopic phases, typically using homogenization devices such as high-pressure homogenizers or ultrasonic homogenizers [10] whereas low-energy methods rely on spontaneous emulsification under specific conditions (phase inversion, temperature) [11]. Nanoemulsions, characterized by small droplet size, offer enhanced physical stability and bioavailability of lipophilic compounds due to their high surface area and reduced gravitational separation [8,12]. Nanoemulsions effectively encapsulate hydrophobic drugs, enhancing loading and delivery while protecting them from degradation and immune clearance [13].

Domestic crickets (Acheta domesticus) present high protein content and essential minerals such as copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), and zinc (Zn), making them valuable for dietary supplementation [14,15]. These properties have prompted interest in using cricket protein in food applications, including gluten-free products for individuals with celiac disease, who often suffer from mineral deficiencies [15]. Furthermore, cricket protein hydrolysates are being explored for their antioxidant and bioactive properties [16]. Although recent studies have investigated the emulsifying capacity of cricket protein, such as the work by Han & Heinonen (2022), these works primarily focus on processing parameters and lack a detailed evaluation of interfacial and techno-functional properties [17]. Thus, there is a clear research gap regarding the in-depth characterization of cricket protein as a natural emulsifier in food-grade emulsions, particularly in combination with functional oils such as linseed oil, and the effect of ultrasonication processing on their stability.

As the population increases, food security is a significant challenge for today’s society, with hunger and malnutrition being among the biggest problems still affecting some regions of the world [18]. The FAO estimates that meat production will need to increase significantly, by approximately 196 million tonnes by 2050, to meet projected demand [19]. In this way, the exploration of alternative sources of protein such as insects is a key to mitigate the incompatibility of conventional resource use [20].

Finally, linseed (or flaxseed) oil is a rich source of the omega-3 fatty acid α-linolenic acid, with an oil composition of approximately 9 % saturated, 18 % monounsaturated, and 73 % polyunsaturated fatty acids, presenting an ideal ω-6:ω-3 ratio [21]. It also contains various bioactive components, including dietary fiber, phenolics, lignans, and essential vitamins and minerals such as P, Mg, K, Fe, and Zn, making it an ideal candidate for functional food formulations [22]. Beyond its nutritional benefits, flaxseed oil has been associated with enhancing mental and physical stamina, alleviating fatigue, and contributing to the regulation of the aging process [21].

The purpose of this study is to develop nanoemulsions using linseed oil as the oil phase and cricket protein as the emulsifying agent. This formulation aims to serve as a functional matrix for food industry applications and support more efficient, nutritious food production. Ultrasonication will be applied to improve emulsion stability and droplet size, while stabilizers will be incorporated to optimize rheological properties.

Therefore, the present study aims to evaluate the emulsifying capacity of cricket protein as a natural agent in oil-in-water emulsions formulated with linseed oil. Firstly, the influence of pH was investigated in the interfacial properties of cricket protein. Furthermore, it systematically examines how ultrasonication parameters, including amplitude and processing time, influence the physicochemical characteristics and stability of these emulsions. We hypothesize that cricket protein, combined with optimized ultrasonication conditions, will significantly reduce droplet size and improve the stability of linseed oil emulsions. The findings of this research could support the development of sustainable nanoemulsions as delivery systems in functional foods, contributing to nutritional improvement and environmental sustainability.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cricket flour with a high content of cricket protein (70 % wt) was from Origen Farms (Spain) and used as an emulsifying agent. Linseed Oil was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany), and for interfacial measurements, it was purified with Florisil 30–60 mesh (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). The solutions of cricket flour were prepared with Milli-Q water. To enhance their rheological properties, we have added two well-known gums used in the food industry: Guar Gum from QUIMIPUR (Campo Real, Madrid, España) and Advanced Performance Xanthan Gum (APXG) from CP Kelco (Cumberland, Atlanta, USA).

Emulsion development

The emulsion formulation under study consists of 10 % wt linseed oil, 1 % wt cricket flour, and 89 % wt distilled water.

All emulsions were prepared with a total mass of 100 g. Firstly, the solution for continuous phase was prepared at the pH studied (7, 10, 11, 12, 12.5 and 13) by adding NaOH 1 M. After the preparation of the continuous phase, a primary homogenization was carried out by adding the dispersed phase. For this purpose, a rotor–stator system called Ultraturrax T50 basic IKA WEKKE (IKA, Staufen, Germany) was used. In this way, to prepare the emulsion, the rotor S50-G45F of the device is introduced into the continuous phase and carried out at a speed of 2000 rpm for 1 min, introducing the dispersed phase in the first 30 s. The speed is then increased to 6000 rpm for another minute. The secondary homogenization is then carried out with the aim of reducing the size of the droplets [23,24]. In this way, the Ultrasonic Processor CV334 (Sonics & Materials, Newtown; USA) operating at an output power of 120 W is used. The study of the influence of the pH was made with the amplitude and time fixed, taking values of 50 % and 5 min, respectively.

In the second stage of the work, which studies the influence of two processing variables on secondary homogenization, time and amplitude variables are studied by means of combinations of 1 min, 5 min and 9 min and 25 %, 50 % and 75 % respectively. In this step the pH was fixed at 12.5 and pulse of 5/5 in ultrasonic processor device. During the study of the secondary homogenization variables with this equipment, cooling by a cold-water bath was used to avoid overheating of the sample. All other emulsification steps were carried out at room temperature.

Ultrasonication is a widely used high-energy method for emulsion preparation, relying on the intense shear forces produced by acoustic cavitation to disrupt and reduce the size of dispersed phase droplets. This process leads to finer and more stable emulsions. Previous studies have shown that parameters such as amplitude, sonication time, and frequency significantly affect the mean droplet diameter, droplet size distribution, and long-term stability of emulsions produced by this technique [25]. Therefore, ultrasonic processing was used in this work as a secondary homogenization step to enhance emulsion properties.

Antioxidant activity of the cricket flour

The ABTS assay involves the scavenging of the ABTS radical, which is a more drastic radical chemically produced. It is often used for screening complex antioxidant mixtures such as plant extracts, beverages, and biological fluids. The current version of this assay involves the production of a stable ABTS radical cation with a blue-green chromophore absorption, generated by the oxidation of ABTS with potassium persulfate. Antioxidant activity of natural products is determined by the discoloration of ABTS, by measuring the reduction of absorbance at 734 nm. The extent of discoloration, expressed as the percentage inhibition of ABTS+, is determined as a function of concentration and calibrated against Trolox as the reference standard [26].

Surface and interfacial activity

The Sigma 701 tensiometer (Biolin Scientific Oy, Tietäjäntie, Finland) has been used to measure both surface and interfacial activity employing the software OneAttension and the Dü-Nouy Ring. The small vessel was used for the measurements, with 10 measurement points recorded at a speed of 20 mm/s. A linseed oil density value of 0.931 g/cm3, measured experimentally, was used as the reference value for interfacial measurements.

The independent or response variable studied was the Sauter mean diameter (d3,2) obtained through laser diffraction measurements. The method used for optimization is response surface methodology (RSM), which will be performed using the ECHIP statistical software. Response surface methodology is a set of mathematical techniques used to model and analyse problems where a variable of interest is influenced by others [30].

Rheological measurements

The rheological tests are performed using an AR2000 rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle, United States) equipped with a 40 mm rough plate-plate sensor, applied to the optimal emulsion developed.

Two tests have been carried out: frequency sweep, which is a type of oscillatory test, and flow curve. In general, when performing oscillatory tests, a stress sweep is usually conducted first, followed by a frequency sweep. On one hand, the stress sweep was carried out by fixing a frequency of 1 Hz, and the elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G″) are measured as a function of the applied stress. On the other hand, the frequency sweep was performed at a fixed stress value lower than the critical stress observed in the stress sweep to ensure measurements are taken within the Linear Viscoelastic Region (LVR). Flow curves were conducted from 0.2 to 24 Pa using a multi-step protocol (30 s per point). Every rheological measurement was made at 20 °C.

Statistics analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results. Standard deviations are represented either as error bars in the figures (when visible) or as percentage values in the figure captions. Statistical comparison was done using the one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test to identify specific differences among groups.

Influence of pH

Fig. 2A illustrates surface tension analysis in cricket protein solutions as a function of protein concentration at varying pH values. The surface tension decreases rapidly with increasing cricket protein concentration up to 0.2 g/L (0.02 wt%), indicating its surface activity. Beyond this concentration, surface tension stabilizes at approximately 42, 36, 39, 39, 39, and 34 mN/m for solutions at pH values of 7, 10, 11, 12, 12.5, and 13, respectively, when the emulsifier concentration exceeds 1 g/L (0.1 wt%), signifying saturation of the air–water interface with surfactant. These results provide knowledge of the optimal pH value at which cricket protein most effectively reduces surface tension. The results suggest that cricket protein exhibits enhanced solubility and greater surface tension reduction at higher pH values.

Surface tension of solutions of cricket protein as a function of emulsifier concentration; the standard deviation of the measured parameters was lower than 4 % (A), interfacial tension between linseed oil and solutions of cricket protein as a function of emulsifier concentration; the standard deviation of the measured parameters was lower than 6 % (B) and droplet size distribution as a function of different pH values of the continuous phase (C).

Fig. 2B presents the interfacial tension values for cricket protein solutions and linseed oil as a function of surfactant concentration. The trend is similar to surface tension: it decreases quickly at first and then stays steady at higher surfactant concentrations. At approximately 1 g/L (0.1 wt%) of cricket protein, the interfacial tension plateaus at around 9, 6, and 5 mN/m for solutions at 7, 10, and 11 pH values, respectively. The values obtained are significantly lower than those of the system without any emulsifier. However, interfacial tension measurements were not possible for pH values of 12, 12.5, and 13. During these experiments, it was observed that when adding oil to the aqueous phase at these pH values, the oil merged with the aqueous phase. This phenomenon could be attributed to the significant reduction in interfacial tension at higher pH values, effectively leading to mixing of water and oil, turning the interface undetectable by the equipment.

The results are consistent with another study, where a higher protein solubility was observed at pH values around 12 [14]. This higher solubility can be attributed to unfolding the protein structure at basic pH, which exposes hydrophilic groups and enhances the ionization of acidic amino acids. Consequently, this improves solubility and offers significant functional advantages in food processing applications. Moreover, the increased solubility at high pH values, such as pH 12, facilitates a greater reduction of surface and interfacial activity, enhancing the protein's potential for use in formulations or other applications where protein functionality is critical.

Fig. 2C shows droplet size distributions (DSDs) for emulsions formulated with linseed oil and cricket protein as a function of pH. Firstly, it is interesting to highlight that the DSDs are bimodal in all the cases, regardless of the pH used. This points out that the protein is not able to form uniform emulsions with this processing. In addition, there is a shift in the DSD towards smaller droplets with pH. This fact is also observed in the Sauter diameters shown in Table 3. This tendency seems to be stabilised at pH 12–13. Although no significant differences are observed for the Sauter diameters at pH 12, 12.5, and 13, the smallest Sauter diameter is obtained for pH 12.5. On the other hand, the span is a parameter indicative of the distribution's polydispersity. It may be important to consider it, since pH 12.5 has the best span values along with pH 13 and pH 7.

However, the polydispersity seems to have the opposite trend. Hence, processing the emulsions at higher amplitudes provokes a reduction in droplet size but a higher polydispersity, possibly due to an over-processing effect. On the other hand, Fig. 3B shows the DSDs for emulsion processed at 50 % of amplitude as a function of processing time. It seems that there must be a factor (possibly the lack of protein) that limits the formation of less polydispersed distributions and displaces them to smaller sizes; a larger quantity of emulsifying agent is necessary not only to reduce the diameter of the drops but also to ensure their stability and prevent recoalescence. For this purpose, only the Sauter diameter (d3,2) was used as the response variable, as it is the most characteristic and commonly used parameter in droplet size distributions [32]. The value of the correlation coefficient (R2) for the model obtained (Eq. (4)) was 0.98, which indicates a strong and reliable fit of the quadratic model to the experimental data. It is important to highlight that droplet size is directly related to emulsion stability, as smaller and more uniformly distributed droplets typically result in enhanced resistance to destabilization mechanisms such as creaming, coalescence, or flocculation.

The FESEM micrograph reveals spherical particles with relatively uniform sizes, each measuring approximately 500–1000 nm in diameter. The droplets are very compact. Enhacement with rheological modifiers

To obtain a nanoemulsion with high physical stability, two stabilizers, guar gum (GG) and advanced performance xanthan gum (APXG), were incorporated into the formulation previously identified as optimal, prepared with the continuous phase at pH 12.5 and processed by ultrasonication for 9 min and 38 s at 63 % amplitude. Four different concentrations of both rheological modifiers were used, ranging from 0.125 % to 0.5 % by weight. Fig. 6 shows the flow curves for the emulsions with APXG or GG in different concentrations as rheological modifiers fitted with the power-law model. A progressive increase in the viscosity of the emulsions is observed as a function of the modifier concentration. It is observed that at the same concentration of rheological modifier, APXG can increase the viscosity more. This fact is also shown in the values of K (Table 5). In addition, the higher values of n for emulsions containing APXG compared to GG are related to a higher structuration grade of the systems. These facts suggest that the higher viscosity provoked by APXG could potentially minimize the mobility of the droplets and reduce destabilization processes.

The frequency sweeps for emulsions formulated with GG and APXG are shown in Fig. 7. A higher concentration of guar gum increases values of both the elastic (G′) and viscous (G″) modulus. For GG, the viscous modulus (G″) remains higher than the elastic modulus (G′) among the entire frequency range, indicating a predominantly viscoelastic liquid behaviour. In the case of APXG, as the concentration of the rheological modifier increases, both G′ and G″ also increase. In addition, for concentrations above 0.25 wt%, G′ surpasses G″, suggesting the formation of a gel-like structure with a stronger internal network. Furthermore, the overall values of G′ and G″ are consistently higher for APXG compared to GG, indicating that APXG confers more significant structural enhancement and viscoelasticity to the emulsions. This enhanced viscoelasticity may be indicative of improved stability and resistance to the destabilization processes in the APXG-based systems. This rheological behaviour is very similar to the zein-diutan gum and zein-guar gum complexes [34].

Frequency sweep of emulsions as a function of guar gum concentration (A), and advanced performance xanthan gum concentration (B). The standard deviation of the measured parameters was lower than 6 %.

Significant improvements in rheological properties were observed at APXG concentrations of 0.25 % and above, where viscosity increased notably and the systems exhibited more gel-like behaviour, as confirmed by both the flow curve parameters and viscoelastic measurements. In contrast, GG showed less pronounced effects on viscosity and structuration within the tested concentration range.

Physical stability of the emulsions

The Turbiscan Stability Index (TSI) measured at 13 and 21 days for emulsions formulated without a rheological modifier, with 0.5 % wt GG, and with 0.5 % wt APXG is shown in Fig. 8. The TSI values indicate the physical stability of these systems over time. The results show that APXG provides better stabilization than GG and the emulsion without a modifier. While GG does not significantly enhance stability, as shown by its high TSI values, APXG effectively prevents destabilization processes, such as creaming, and maintains low TSI values even after 21 days. The emulsion without a modifier is initially stable but becomes less stable over time, highlighting the importance of using an effective modifier like APXG for long-term applications.

Variations in Turbiscan Stability Index (TSI) for optimal emulsion developed without rheological modifier, 0.5 wt% guar gum (GG) and 0.5 wt% advanced performance xanthan gum (APXG).

The TSI results align with the rheological study of the systems. Guar gum (GG) showed the poorest physical stability, followed by the system without a rheological modifier, while APXG delivered the best results. This correlation is supported by the rheological data, which demonstrate that APXG provides superior viscoelastic properties and flow behaviour, contributing to its enhanced stabilization performance and preventing destabilization processes.

Conclusions

Cricket protein has proven to be an effective natural emulsifier, as indicated by the sharp reduction in surface and interfacial tension with increasing concentration, especially under alkaline conditions. For pH values of 12, 12.5, and 13, interfacial tension measurements were infeasible due to the mixing of oil and aqueous phases, suggesting a significant reduction in interfacial tension at higher pH. The DSD of the emulsions developed was bimodal regardless the pH. A shift towards smaller droplet sizes occurred at higher pH, stabilizing at pH 12–13. This fact is in concordance with the interfacial study. The smallest Sauter diameter (0.54 μm) and span values were achieved at pH 12.5, making it the optimal pH for further studies.

Ultrasonication emerged as a critical factor in tailoring the structural and stability characteristics of the emulsions. Higher amplitudes and extended sonication times significantly reduced droplet size; however, these conditions also increased polydispersity, suggesting the onset of over-processing and droplet recoalescence in the absence of sufficient stabilizing protein. This emphasizes the need for precise control of ultrasonic energy input to avoid structural destabilization. A response surface model determined that 63 % amplitude and 9 min 38 s represented the optimal conditions, minimizing energy consumption while ensuring efficient droplet disruption and size uniformity.

To further reinforce emulsion stability, two polysaccharides—Guar gum (GG) and advanced performance xanthan gum (APXG)—were tested. Both enhanced viscosity, but APXG had a more pronounced effect, especially at concentrations above 0.25 wt%, where it promoted gel-like behavior (G′ > G″), leading to improved viscoelasticity and resistance to destabilization mechanisms such as creaming and coalescence. Therefore, APXG was identified as the most effective stabilizer among those tested.

In conclusion, the integration of cricket protein as an emulsifier, combined with optimized ultrasonication parameters and APXG stabilization, offers a promising strategy for the development of stable, sustainable nanoemulsions.

To further this research, future studies should evaluate the sensory properties, digestibility, and functional performance of the optimized cricket protein emulsions within real food matrices. This would provide valuable insight into their commercial viability, consumer acceptance, and potential integration into sustainable food products.
