A Supramolecular Antibiotic Targeting Drug-Resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa through the Inhibition of Virulence Factors and Activation of Acquired Immunity
Authors: Cheng Jiang, Lei Zheng, Yu-jie Yan, Miao Wang, Xiao-Jing Liu, Jing-Yao Dai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa is an exceptionally resilient opportunistic pathogen, presenting formidable challenges for treatment due to its proclivity for developing drug resistance. To address this predicament, we have devised a self-assembled supramolecular antibiotic known as dHTSN1@pHPplus, which can circumvent the drug resistance mechanism of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and effectively combat Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection by impeding the secretion of key virulence factors through the inhibition of the type III secretion system while simultaneously mobilizing immune cells to eradicate Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Furthermore, dHTSN1@pHPplus was ingeniously engineered with infection-targeting capabilities, enabling it to selectively concentrate precisely at the site of infection. As anticipated, the administration of dHTSN1@pHPplus exhibited a remarkable therapeutic efficacy in combating dual resistance to Meropenem and imipenem in a mouse model of P. aeruginosa lung infection. The results obtained from metagenomic detection further confirmed these findings, demonstrating a significant reduction in the proportion of Pseudomonas aeruginosa compared to untreated mice with Pseudomonas aeruginosa-infected lungs. Additionally, no notable acute toxicity was observed in the acute toxicity experiments. The present study concludes that the remarkable efficacy of dHTSN1@pHPplus in treating drug-resistant P. aeruginosa infection confirms its immense potential as a groundbreaking antibiotic agent for combating drug-resistant P. aeruginosa.Keywords: nanoparticles, antibiotics, Pseudomonas aeruginosa resistance, dHTSN1, T3SS

Introduction

The bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a highly resistant opportunistic pathogen commonly encountered in hospital and medical settings, posing significant challenges for treatment due to its propensity to develop drug resistance. Pseudomonas aeruginosa has diverse resistance mechanisms and robust adaptations, including biofilm-mediated resistance and formation of multidrug-resistant persistent cells.1−5 Conventional antibiotics aim to eliminate the bacteria, thus subjecting them to antibiotic selection pressure, which always induces drug-resistant mutations in bacteria to escape death.6 In this study, we avoided drug selection pressure by blocking Pseudomonas aeruginosa’s ability to infect the host rather than directly killing Pseudomonas aeruginosa itself, thus avoiding the development of drug resistance. Pseudomonas aeruginosa infects the host mainly relying on the secretion of virulence factors by the secretion system.7Pseudomonas aeruginosa possesses multiple secretion systems, ranging from T1SS (type I secretion system) to T9SS (type IX secretion system), all of which are associated with infection, and can secrete virulence factors or other products (e.g., proteolytic hydrolyzing enzymes, phosphatidylethanolamine specific phospholipases, etc.) that work in concert with virulence factors to cause infection in the organism.8 It was found that Pseudomonas aeruginosa can be classified into many different isolates based on the different phenotypes of exosome genes, among which ExoS-secreting strains accounted for more than 60%, strains that secrete both ExoS and ExoU constitute over 90% of the isolates, with the coexpression of ExoS and ExoU genes being rare within the same strain.9 Notably, the ExoS genotype accounted for the largest proportion of the isolates, and this group of P. aeruginosa is more pathogenic and most infectious compared to other P. aeruginosa isolates9.

Based on FENG’s study,10 dihydrotanshinone I (dHTSN1) has a potent inhibitory effect on T3SS, which can competitively bind to the active center of the PscE-PscF-PscG trimer, thereby disrupting the secretion function of the T3SS and inhibiting the release of the virulence factors such as ExoS and ExoU. Consequently, Pseudomonas aeruginosa is not directly killed, and therefore is not under selective survival pressure,11 which in turn makes it less susceptible to drug resistance.12 However, simply inhibiting the release of Pseudomonas aeruginosa virulence factors does not eliminate the bacterium but rather diminishes its ability to infect host cells. Complete elimination of Pseudomonas aeruginosa typically requires the involvement of the body’s immune cells. Based on LIU’s study, we found a CD81 protein affinity peptide derived from amino acids 710–725 of the HCV E2 protein (named pHP by the group, with the amino acid sequence ASWAIKWEYVVVLLFLL).13−15 Since CD81 protein is widely expressed on the surface of macrophages, T cells, and B cells, we leveraged pHP peptide to enhance the recruitment of these immune cells, thereby eliminating Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In view of the fact that P. aeruginosa can create an acidic environment at the infection site16 and its bacterial membrane consists of negatively charged lipopolysaccharide (LPS),17 we modified the hydrophobic C-terminal end of the pHP peptide by adding polar, basic amino acids to the tail end of the peptide and elevating its isoelectric point (pI, isoelectric point) (PI value of about 8.89). The final peptide obtained was made to be positively charged in both acidic and neutral environments, thereby enhancing the ability of the peptide to affix to Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In addition, the addition of polar amino acids at the hydrophobic C-terminal end endowed it with hydrophilicity, result in an amphiphilic polypeptide,18,19 and we named the modified polypeptide pHPplus (with the amino acid sequence ASWAIKWEYVVLLFLLHHEHHHHHRRC). With the property that amphiphilic peptides can self-assemble,20 pHPplus can self-assemble with dHTSN1 into nanoparticles with a diameter of about 200–300 nm (dHTSN1@pHPplus). The self-assembled nanoparticles possess the following functions: (1) Concentrate in the tissue at the infected site through the gap of the degenerated endothelial cells at the infected site by relying on the nanoparticle EPR effect (enhanced permeability and retention effect);21 (2) Recruit CD81+ immune cells (including macrophages, T cells) through affinity for the CD81 protein with the help of pHPplus peptide in nanoparticles;22 (3) Leverage the cation-rich characteristics of nanoparticles to adhere to the anion-rich Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacterial membrane, and inhibit the secretion of virulence factors from Pseudomonas aeruginosa T3SS by the dihydropanaxanthone I in the nanoparticles. Therefore, the purpose of enhancing the immune ability of the organism, and the treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections was achieved (Figure 1). In this study, we examined the physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles, investigated their functions of inhibiting the secretion of virulence factors by Pseudomonas aeruginosa and enhancing the recruitment of immune cells. Furthermore, the study meticulously evaluated the therapeutic effects and acute toxicity of these nanoparticles in Pseudomonas aeruginosa lung-infected mice and epidermal trauma-infected mice. These findings not only provide a potential therapeutic option for drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections but also pave the way for a new approach and direction in antibiotic development.

Ultimately, the goal of treating Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection was achieved.

Results and Discussion

Currently, β-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones and polymyxins are commonly utilized in the treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections. Nevertheless, in recent years, the proportion of carbapenem-resistant antibiotic isolates among clinical isolates of Pseudomonas aeruginosa has increased significantly due to the extensive usage of various types of antibiotics, especially carbapenem-resistant ones.23,24 According to the CDC 2022 Antibiotic Resistance Threat Report, there were 28,800 documented cases of multidrug resistant (MDR) Pseudomonas aeruginosa-associated nosocomial infections and 2500 fatalitiesin 2020. This marked a 32% escalation in the incidence rate of MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa nosocomial infections when juxtaposed with figures from 2019.25 The 2022 China National Bacterial Resistance Surveillance Report stated that the nationwide average rate of Pseudomonas aeruginosa drug resistance to carbapenems stood at 16.6% (October 2021–September 2022).26 Conventional antibiotic antimicrobial mechanisms mainly include inhibiting bacterial cell wall formation, affecting bacterial protein synthesis, increasing bacterial cell membrane permeability, and impeding bacterial nucleic acid synthesis.6 The ultimate objective is to directly target the bacteria achieve either bacteriostatic or bactericidal effects,6 consequently intensifying the pressure of antibiotic selection and leading to the emergence of drug resistance. Recent strategies for treating Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections have involved the utilization of engineered phages, antimicrobial peptides, antibiofilms, and nanodelivery systems to deliver antimicrobial or bacteriostatic drugs to achieve therapeutic efficacy. However, they may also contribute to the development of acquired resistance in Pseudomonas aeruginosa by inducing alterations in its metabolic pathways.6,27 The primary mechanisms of bacterial resistance encompass the secretion of enzymes that inactivate antibiotics, activation of efflux pumps to expel antibiotics from the cell, alteration of cell wall permeability to impede drug entry, and modification of drug target structures.6,28 In this study, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which is resistant to two antibiotics, Meropenem and imipenem, was investigated. The mechanisms of resistance included the deletion of the membrane pore protein OprD2 (conferring resistant to imipenem), high expression of the efflux pump on the bacterial inner membrane (resulting in resistant to Meropenem), and the production of carbapenemases.29 In order to avoid the resistance mechanism caused by drug selection pressure, numerous researchers have proposed unconventional antibiotic therapies for the treatment of infections with microorganisms. Examples include antimicrobial peptides,30,31 efflux pump inhibitors to inhibit drug resistance,32,33 phage therapy34−36 and immunomodulator agents.37,38 The T3SS was mentioned several times. Inspired by these unconventional antibiotic therapeutic mechanisms, we utilized dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticles to inhibit the secretion of virulence factors by suppressing T3SS to reduce the damage caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa on the host organism without directly exerting bactericidal effects. Wherefore, we sought to avoid creating drug selection pressure and consequently mitigate the emergence of drug resistance issues.

Morphology and Characterization of Self-Assembled Nanoparticles dHTSN1@pHPplus

The dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticle solution was prepared according to the method outlined, with each milliliter containing 0.02 mg of dHTSN1, 0.06875 mg of pHPplus, and 2.275 mg of Zn2+. During the synthesis process, variations in the size and morphology of the self-assembled nanoparticles occurred due to microshear differences during solution stirring. Irregular round-like nanoparticles with diameters ranging from 100 to 200 nm were visible under electron microscopy (Figure 2a). The solution was detected by high-performance liquid chromatography, revealing an absorption peak at 280 nm corresponding to the original solute, as well as a nascent nanoparticle absorption peak between 35 and 40 min (Figure 2b), suggesting that the nanoparticles of dHTSN1@pHPplus were successfully synthesized. The hydrodynamic diameter of the resulting nanoparticles was approximately 505.9 nm and the Zeta potential value was about +9.45 mV (Figure 2c).

Characterization of nanoparticle. (a) Irregular round-like nanoparticles with a diameter of 100–200 nm were observed under the electron microscope; (b) high-performance liquid chromatograms of three solutions of dHTSN1, pHPplus peptide and dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticles, which displayed the presence of a new substance absorption in the dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticle solution at 35–40 min mark, suggesting the successful synthesis of self-assembled nanoparticles; (c) nanoparticles have a hydrated particle size of about 505.9 nm and a zeta potential of about +9.45 mV.

Subsequently, we performed an in-depth examination of dihydrotanshinone, PHP peptides, and nanoparticles through infrared spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The findings indicated that postself-assembly, the distinctive spectral peaks of both dihydrotanshinone and PHP peptides were evident in the nanoparticles’ infrared spectra (Supplementary Figure 6). Moreover, the XPS survey and elemental fine spectra of the nanoparticles showed no signs of novel chemical bond characteristics (Supplementary Figure 7). Collectively, these results suggest that during self-assembly, peptides engage in hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic forces with other molecules, culminating in the emergence of structured assemblies. Zinc chloride, as a source of zinc ions, plays a pivotal role in this process, enhancing the mutual interactions between peptides and dihydrotanshinone, which in turn stabilizes the assembly. The amino acid residues within the peptide sequence coordinate with zinc ions, preserving the peptide’s conformation. The hydrophobic regions of dihydrotanshinone interact with the hydrophobic domains of the peptides, with its carbonyl groups further coordinating with zinc ions to bolster the stability of the self-assembled structures. Ultimately, these synergistic interactions result in the generation of stable nanostructures or complexes endowed with unique morphologies and functionalities.

Fluorescence Colocalization of dHTSN1@pHPplus with Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Macrophages

In addition to recruiting CD81+ immune cells, the pHPplus peptide in dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticles can target Pseudomonas aeruginosa with the help of its cation-rich property to affinity the anion-rich Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacterial membrane. To verify this mechanism, we observed the fluorescence localization of nanoparticles with macrophages and P. aeruginosa by fluorescence confocal microscopy. The results revealed a significant number of red M2 probe-labeled P. aeruginosa (Figure 3b) colocalized with green F ITC-labeled nanoparticles (Figure 3c) in the macrophage cytoplasm (Figure 3f). Additionally, scattered P. aeruginosa was observed to colocalized with nanoparticles outside the cytoplasm (indicated by white arrows in Figure 3b–f), confirming a strongadhesion of nanoparticles to P. aeruginosa and a subsequent phagocytosis of both by the macrophage. This dual affinity of nanoparticles for Pseudomonas aeruginosa and CD81+ immune cells via pHPplus enhances macrophage recruitment, thereby leading to phagocytosis of more Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

Functional Verification of dHTSN1@pHPplus

Although inhibiting the secretion of virulence factors by suppressing T3SS can disable the primary ability of bacteria to infect host cells. It does not eliminate the bacteria themselves, leaving them subject to further clearance by the body’s immune cells. Upon bacterial invasion, macrophages and T cells are the first two key immune cells involved in the immune response, both of which express CD81 protein on their surface.13−15 The dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticles designed in this study feature a pHPplus peptide designed to specifically affinity the CD81 protein. The self-assembled dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticles synthesized from this peptide not only inhibit the T3SS but also enhance the recruitment of macrophages and T cells through the pHPplus peptide, thus achieving the purpose of further bacterial clearance.

To verify the recruitment capability of dHTSN1@pHPplus for CD81+ immune cells (e.g., macrophages, T lymphocytes, etc.) through its affinity for CD81 protein, we employed T lymphocytes as representative markers. We analyzed the presence of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and CD4+/CD8+ double-positive T cells in the alveolar lavage fluid of four groups of mice treated with dHTSN1@pHPplus, dHTSN1, pHPplus solution and saline. This assessment aimed to confirm the effectiveness of these treatments in recruiting T cells. The results showed that after 4 h of nanoparticle titration, the percentage of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and CD4+/CD8+ double-positive T cells in the alveoli of mice in the dHTSN1@pHPplus-treated group differed significantly from those in the dHTSN1@pHPplus peptide group, the dHTSN1 group, and the saline group (Figure 4a). Although the percentage of positive cells was lower in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group compared to the pHPplus peptide group (27.39 vs 32.51%, P < 0.05), indicating that the efficacy of the self-assembled nanoparticles in recruiting T lymphocytes was reduced compared to that of pHPplus peptide. It was still significantly higher than that of the dHTSN1 group (27.39 vs 20.21%, P < 0.01) and saline group (27.39 vs 5.81%, P < 0.0001), confirming that the self-assembled nanoparticles retain an effective ability to recruit T lymphocytes, thus enhancing the clearance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Meanwhile, several studies have confirmed the feasibility of this option to attenuate the infection by inhibiting the secretion of virulence factors39−41 and neutralizing virulence factors.42−46 For instance, Shi et al. demonstrated that inhibition of the secretion of virulence factors by Salmonella T3SS reduced its pathogenicity.47 Similarly, Liu’s study illustrated that inhibition of the expression of virulence genes in C. aerogenes (agrB/agrD, virS/virR) hinders the synthesis of virulence factors, consequently hindering infection.48 The ability of dHTSN1@pHPplus to inhibit ExoS, a pivotal virulence factor of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, was detected by Western blot to confirm that dHTSN1@pHPplus could inhibit the secretion of ExoS through the inhibition of T3SS, thus alleviating the Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection. The results revealed a substantial reduction in ExoS protein secretion by Pseudomonas aeruginosa upon the addition of dHTSN1@pHPplus, with a decrease of 64.03% (P < 0.0001) compared to the blank control group and a 50% reduction (P < 0.0001) compared to the chloride control group. These findings provide compelling evidence that dHTSN1@pHPplus significantly inhibited the secretion of the key virulence factor ExoS by the P. aeruginosa type III secretion system (Figure 4b).

Additionally, and a small amount of radioactive signals also was detected in certain areas of the livers of these mice, while no discernible radioactive signals were observed in the rest of the mice.

Recent studies have shown that similar pathophysiological changes exist both at the site of infection and locally in the tumor: inflammatory changes in local tissues and heightened vascular permeability.49,50 Such conditions facilitate the penetration of antitumor drugs particularly large molecule drugs like liposomes and nanoparticles, into tumor tissues through the vasculature with enhanced permeability and persist within the tumor for extended periods, enhancing their targeting efficacy.49,50 This mechanism is termed the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect.49,50 Since the analogous microenvironment exists locally in the infection,49,50 we further investigated whether dHTSN1@pHPplus could target the infected lesions via the EPR effect. We injected dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticles, synthesized from FITC-labeled pHPplus peptide, into the tail vein of both healthy mice and mice infected with Pseudomonas aeruginosa in the lungs. Concurrently, physiological saline was also injected into separate groups of mice as a control. Following a 4-h interval, the fluorescence intensity within each organ of the mice was assessed using a small animal in vivo imaging instrument. As shown in Figure 4c, radioactive signals were exclusively detected in mice with Pseudomonas aeruginosa lung infections who were injected with dHTSN1@pHPplus via the tail vein. These signals were primarily located in the lungs, exhibiting the highest intensity of 1.46e9 [p/sec/cm2/sr]/[μW/cm2]. In addition, low radioactivity signals existed in a few areas of the liver, while no obvious radioactivity signals were detected in the remaining organs or in the organs of the other groups of mice, which indicated that dHTSN1@pHPplus had already been concentrated at the site of infection (see Figure 4c). This observation strongly suggests that dHTSN1@pHPplus could target aggregation at the site of infection through the EPR effect.

Treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa-Infected Mice with dHTSN1@pHPplus

We further evaluated the therapeutic efficacy in Pseudomonas aeruginosa lung-infected mice and epidermal trauma-infected mice following tail vein injection of dHTSN1@pHPplus. The control group was injected by tail vein with tanshinone I (Tanshinone I is a tanshinone analogue similar to dHTSN1 and possesses antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects51). As illustrated in Figures 5a and 6a, inflammatory responses were observed in both lung tissue and epidermal trauma tissue. In the lung tissue sections, the inflammatory reaction was significantly more pronounced in the tanshinone I group, with a substantial infiltration of immune cells and eosinophils spanning the full field of view. The alveoli were obviously deformed and incompletely distended under the extrusion of the surrounding inflammatory cells. Conversely, in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group, the alveoli remained intact and well-distended, presenting a stark contrast to the observations in the tanshinone I group. In the epidermal trauma tissue, subcutaneous immune cell infiltration was exhibited in both groups between 48 and 96 h. However, by 120 h, there was a notable reduction inimmune cell infiltration in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group, whereas it continued to be aggravated in the tanshinone I group.

In blood, tissue homogenate, and alveolar lavage fluid, IL-1β, IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α indexes in lung-infected mice at 24–72 h and epidermal trauma-infected mice at 48–96 h showed an increasing trend. And the levels of the above indexes in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group were significantly lower than those in the tanshinone I group. Furthermore, the levels of the aforementioned indexes significantly decreased in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group at 96 h after lung infection, and in the epidermal trauma-infected mice at 120 h. In contrast, the tanshinone I group continued to increase in these indices over time, and the difference between the two treatments was significant (P < 0.001). The above results indicated that dHTSN1@pHPplus achieved the best therapeutic effect at 96 h (lung infection) and 120 h (epidermal trauma infection), respectively. It is noteworthy that IL-10, recognize as an anti-inflammatory factor, showed an initial decrease followed by an increase over time in both the lung and epidermal trauma infection model groups without therapeutic drugs (Supplementary Figure 2b and Figure 4b). In contrast, it showed a continuous decreasing trend in the dHTSN1@pHPplus and tanshinone I treatment groups. This may be related to the anti-inflammatory properties of tanshinones applied in the experiment. Fu et al. have elucidated that tanshinone IIA could activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway to mediate the anti-inflammatory effect.52 Additionally, Yang et al. demonstrated that tanshinone I and tanshinone IIA/B could alleviate inflammation by modulating the NF-κB pathway.53 Moreover, Cui et al. predicted, through a web-based pharmacological approach,that tanshinone I and cryptotanshinone could exert anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting IL-6, TNF, MAPK8, CXCL8, PTGS2, STAT3, and AKT1.54 This finding is consistent with observations in Min Gao’s study, in which septic mice treated with tanshinone analogues showed significantly lower levels of IL-10 than the control group.55 The application of tanshinones attenuated the stimulation of IL-10 secretion by the inflammatory response. It is plausible that the inhibition of IL-10 secretion is influenced by sustained high levels of IFN-γ, which may contribute to this observed change.56 Furthermore, except for the elevated levels of ALT and AST observed in lung-infected mice, and Cr in epidermal trauma-infected mice, the dHTSN1@pHPplus group had lower blood and biochemical indices than the tanshinone I group (Figures 5c and 6c). Although the levels of ALT (mean value of 35.15 U/L), AST (mean value of 170.71 U/L), and Cr (mean value of 0.35 mg/dL) were elevated in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group of mice compared to the tanshinone I group, they remained within the normal range.57 Additionally, alveolar lavage fluid and epidermal trauma tissue homogenate were subjected to bacterial culture. In lung-infected mice, the number of strains in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group showed a tendency to increase and then decrease, with the highest number of strains at 72 h, and then began to decrease at 96 h. Conversely, the tanshinone I group showed a sustained trend of increase in the number of strains, which was in line with the changes in the inflammation index. In epidermal trauma infections, the number of strains in both groups was sustained to decrease, with the dHTSN1@pHPplus group exhibiting significantly lower strain counts compared to the tanshinone I group.

Further, we conducted macro-genomic assays on alveolar lavage and epidermal trauma tissue homogenates at 48 h post-treatment. The results demonstrated that the percentage of macro-genomic P. aeruginosa in the dHTSN1@pHPplus treatment group was reduced by 75.44 and 85.59%, respectively, compared to untreated P. aeruginosa-infected mouse (lung-infected mice treated vs untreated = 1.13 vs 76.57%, epidermal trauma-infected mice treated vs untreated = 7.69 vs 93.28%, Figures 7a, 8a and Supplementary Figures 3a, 5a).Moreover, the relative species abundance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa among the bacteria measured was reduced by 33% and 98.2%, respectively (lung-infected mice treated vs untreated = 2 vs 35%, epidermal trauma-infected mice treated vs untreated = 0.8 vs 99%, Figures 7b, 8b and Supplementary Figures 3b, 5b). The above results provide robust evidence of the significant therapeutic effect of dHTSN1@pHPplus against Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection.

Acute Toxicity Evaluation of dHTSN1@pHPplus

We conducted an acute toxicity evaluation experiment on nanoparticles dHTSN1@pHPplus. The experimental group was injected intraperitoneally with 250 ul of dHTSN1@pHPplus, while the control group was injected intraperitoneally with an equivalent volume of saline, and each was observed for 9 days. The results of inflammatory factors detected in blood and lung tissue homogenate revealed significantly lower levels of TNF-α in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group compared to the saline group in the blood on day 9 and lung tissue homogenate on days 7 and 9 (P < 0.001). Additionally, blood biochemical indexes indicated a slight elevation in AST and ALT levels in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group compared to the saline group (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in the remaining inflammatory and biochemical indexes in the blood specimens and lung tissue homogenate specimens between the two groups of mice (P > 0.05) (Figure 9c). Although there were statistically significant differences in the inflammatory factor TNF-α levels in lung tissue homogenate, as well as the AST and ALT indexes in blood, when compared with those in the control group. The lung tissue sections from the two groups of mice showed multifaceted cystic vesicles with a structurally intact appearance, normal alveolar epithelial structure and morphology, and the distribution of capillaries in the alveolar septum. And the liver tissue sections showed a tightly ordered arrangement of hepatocytes with clear borders and normal structures of the central vein, confluence area and hepatic sinusoids, without any signs of cholestasis. There was no significant difference in body weight between the two groups (7 days: P = 0.2186, 9 days: P = 0.0941) (Figure 9a). TNF-α is an indicator of inflammation, and the lower levels of TNF-α in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group do not imply the toxic damage induced by the nanoparticles in mice. We attribute this change to the inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway by dHTSN1 in dHTSN1@pHPplus, an effect corroborated by prior studies.58−60 Moreover, while statistically significant differences were noted in the AST and ALT indexes between the dHTSN1@pHPplus group and the saline group on day 9, these differences were minor. Notably no statistically significant variations were observed between the two groups on day 7, and no significant damage was evident in the liver tissue sections. Therefore, we concluded that dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticles did not elicit significant acute toxic reactions in mice following a single high-dose (250ul) intraperitoneal injection of dHTSN1@pHPplus (Figure 9), which confirms the favorable biocompatibility of these nanoparticles.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a novel nanoformulation dHTSN1@pHPplus was constructed for the treatment of drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection. This formulation has the following advanced features: (1) Inactivation of the main pathogenic ability of Pseudomonas aeruginosa by inhibiting the secretion of virulence factor through the suppression of Pseudomonas aeruginosa T3SS by dHTSN1, thus reducing bacterial pathogenicity without causing drug selection pressure; (2) Reducing the local inflammatory reaction of the infection with the help of anti-inflammatory effect of dHTSN1 itself to alleviate the symptoms of the infection; (3) Enhance the recruitment of immune cells expressing CD81 protein through pHPplus peptide(e.g., macrophages, T cells, B cells, monocytes, etc.); (4) dHTSN1@pHPplus can also be adhered to the surface of Pseudomonas aeruginosa due to the electric charge effect, thereby endowing dHTSN1 with the ability of targeting to Pseudomonas aeruginosa; (5) Label Pseudomonas aeruginosa to promote the aggregation of immune cells to Pseudomonas aeruginosa, thereby enhancing the phagocytosis and killing of Pseudomonas aeruginosa by the immune cells of the organism to achieve the purpose of eradicating Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection; 6. Utilizing the nanoparticle’s EPR effect facilitates the enrichment of dHTSN1@pHPplus to the site of infection. The above six points can synergistically enhance the therapeutic effect on drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection.

In subsequent studies, we will focus on unconventional antibiotic treatment strategies for drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections. First, Pseudomonas aeruginosa possesses multiple secretion systems (e.g., T1SS, T2SS, etc.), and we can further develop the compounds that can simultaneously inhibit multiple secretion systems, so that the pathogenicity of Pseudomonas aeruginosa can be further reduced. Second, Natural Killer cells (NK), as an important class of innate immune cells, can also rapidly recognize and kill Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Investigating compounds that can recruit NK cells or other pivotal immune cells involved in mediating the immune response could be significantly enhance the clearance of P. aeruginosa locally. In addition, considering the presence of T3SS in other Gram-negative bacilli including E. coli and Salmonella enterica.61 We intend to further investigate the therapeutic effects of dHTSN1@pHPplus and its derivative nanoparticles in E. coli, Salmonella enterica, and other infections. Our goal is to the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of this novel nanoformulation. In conclusion, dHTSN1@pHPplus particles not only provide a potential novel nanoformulation for the treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections, but also introduce a new paradigm for the treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa as well as other drug-resistant bacteria, which is of great significance.

Methods

Synthesis and Characterization of Nanoparticles dHTSN1@pHPplus

Nanoparticles were synthesized by self-assembly: (1) Dissolve 0.2 mg of dHTSN1 in 0.2 mL of DMSO to prepare the dHTSN1 solution., and immerse the ultrasonic shaker in a 20 °C water bath for 10 min; (2) 2.75 mg/mL pHPplus peptide solution was configured; (3) 0.35 mL of 0.1 mol/L ZnCl2 standard solution was taken, to which 0.025 mL pHPplus peptide solution was added, shaking on the oscillating shaker for 30s, ultrasonic shaker 20 °C water bath for 10 min, and then left to stand for 30 min; (4) The mixed solution prepared in step 3 was placed in the ultrasonic shaker 20 °C water bath, to which was added 0.02 mL of dHTSN1 solution in the same time, and then the total volume of the solution was adjusted to 1 mL, and the solution was shaked in ultrasonic shaker 20 °C water bath for 10 min again. Thus, dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticle solution was obtained. dHTSN1 was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., ZnCl2 solution was purchased from Tianjin Kono Instrument Co., and the pHPplus peptide was provided by Xiao-Jing Liu’s group in the Department of Infectious Diseases of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University, with the sequence of ASWAIKWEYVVLLFLLHHHHHHHHHRRC. dHTSN1 solution obtained from the above steps, pHPplus peptide solution and the self-assembled dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticle solution were subjected to HPLC analysis (Waters E2695 Liquid Chromatograph), respectively.

Strains, Cells and Growth Conditions

Pseudomonas aeruginosa was a dual-resistant strain, exhibiting resistance to Meropenem (MEM) and Imipenem (IPM), and was provided by the Department of Laboratory of Xijing Hospital. They were cultured on agar plates (Shanghai Yuanpei Bioscience and Technology Co.) and incubated at 37 °C overnight, and the subsequent subcultures utilized were derived from colonies isolated from this plate. raw264.7 Macrophages were purchased from Beijing BioBioWay Biotechnology Co., and subcultured in DMEM medium (Shanghai Yuanpei BioTechnology Co.) and cultured overnight at 37 °C. The subsequent subcultures used in the experiments were derived from cells isolated from this plate.

Mouses and the Establishment and Validation of the Mouse Model

We used 7–8-week-old Balb/c mice (female) weighing 20 ± 2 g for the experiments (purchased from Beijing Baiotai Biotechnology Co.) During the study period, we maintained the mice in a constant temperature (25 ± 2 °C) and constant humidity (60 ± 10%) environment, given standard pellet feed and sterile water, and all underwent 7 days of acclimatization feeding. This study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with ethical regulations such as the International Guidelines for Biomedical Research on Animals, and was approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of the Air Force Specialty Medical Centre. The establishment and validation of the mouse model for Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection have been reported in the Supporting Information.

Functional Verification of dHTSN1@pHPplus

dHTSN1@pHPplus Inhibits Pseudomonas aeruginosa ExoS Secretion

Preconfigured medium for experimental and control groups. The medium of the nanoparticle group was added with sterile water, 2% DMSO (purchased from Beijing Biotec Biotechnology Co.) and preconstructed dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticles; The chloride control group was added with sterile water, 2% DMSO, 5 mM EDTA, 20 mM MgCl2 (purchased from Beijing Biotec Biotechnology Co.) (EDTA can generate complexes with Mg2+ ions, simulating the liquid environment rich in free Cl– ions after depletion of Zn2+ ions in the configuration of nanoparticles); The blank control group was added with sterile water and 2% DMSO. Drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa was cultured in the above three groups of medium for 4 h. After the completion of the incubation period, centrifugation was carried out to separate the supernatant, which was then collected. Precooled TCA was added into the supernatant to make the final concentration 10%, and then the mixture was vortexed to oscillate and placed on the ice for 1 h so that the proteins were fully precipitated. Subsequently, Western blotting was performed to detect the ExoS protein following adequate protein precipitation.

Fluorescence Colocalization of dHTSN1@pHPplus with Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Macrophages

The dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticles were synthesized using the FITC-labeled pHPplus peptide (purchased from Abcom). The Pseudomonas aeruginosa cell membrane was labeled using the M2 red fluorescent probe (purchased from BioElite) and the macrophage nucleus was labeled using DAPI (purchased from BioElite). Fluorescence localization of dHTSN1@pHPplus, drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa membranes, and macrophage nuclei was observed by fluorescence confocal microscopy after 4 h of coincubation.

Flow Cytometry of Mouse Lung Tissue Homogenates

Balb/c female mice were divided into four groups: dHTSN1@pHPplus-treated, dHTSN1-treated, pHPplus-treated, and saline-treated group. 10ul nanoparticles of dHTSN1@pHPplus solution was dripped into the nasal cavities of both sides in the dHTSN1@pHPplus group, 10ul of dHTSN1 solution in the dHTSN1 group, 10ul of pHPplus solution in the pHPplus group, and 10ul of saline in the saline group. Lung tissues were harvested after 4 h and immunofluorescence flow cytometry of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was performed on the lung tissue homogenates.

dHTSN1@pHPplus Targets Infected Lesions

Healthy mice and Pseudomonas aeruginosa lung-infected mice were selected to create an experimental group and a control group. The experimental group was injected with 50ul of dHTSN1@pHPplus nanoparticle sample solution synthesized using FITC (purchased from Abcom) labeled pHPplus peptide via the tail vein, while the control group was injected with 50ul of saline in the tail vein. Fluorescence enrichment in various organs of the mice was observed by the Small Animal Imaging Instrument (SAI) after 4 h.

Effectiveness of dHTSN1@pHPplus in the Treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa Infection

Treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa Lung Infection

Instantly established Pseudomonas aeruginosa lung-infected mice were divided into experimental and control groups. Eight hours later, the experimental group was injected with 50 ul of dHTSN1@pHPplus sample solution via the tail vein. This injection was repeated once every 24 h for a total of three injections. In comparison, the control group was injected with tanshinone I(Tanshinone I,51 purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., is a compound similar to dHTSN1 and possesses antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties.). The two groups of mice were tested for IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-γ, TNF-α, ALT, AST, Cr, BUN, and blood routine indexes in the blood at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, respectively. Additionally, the alveolar lavage fluid and lung tissue homogenate were also tested for IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-γ, and TNF-α. Lung tissue sections were also stained with HE staining for histopathological analysis.

Treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa Epidermal Trauma Infection

Immediately established Pseudomonas aeruginosa epidermal trauma-infected mice were divided into experimental and control groups. Twenty-4 h later, the experimental group was epidermally titrated with 10ul of the dHTSN1@pHPplus sample solution, which was then administered every 24 h. Meanwhile, the control group was administered the tanshinone I solution mentioned earlier. The two groups of mice were tested for IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-γ, TNF-α, ALT, AST, Cr, BUN, and blood routine indexes in the blood at 48, 72, 96, and 120 h, respectively. Additionally, the epidermal trauma tissue homogenate was also tested for IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, while epidermal trauma tissue sections were stained with HE staining.

Evaluation of dHTSN1@pHPplus Drug Toxicity

Balb/c female mice were utilized in this study. The experimental group was injected intraperitoneally with 250ul of dHTSN1@pHPplus sample solution, while the control group was injected with 250ul of saline, and the general conditions of the mice, such as body weight, were monitored throughout the experiment. On day 7 and 9 respectively, the mice were tested for IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-γ, TNF-α, ALT, AST, Cr, and BUN in blood and lung tissue homogenate. Additionally, liver, kidney, and lung tissue sections were stained with HE for histopathological analysis.
