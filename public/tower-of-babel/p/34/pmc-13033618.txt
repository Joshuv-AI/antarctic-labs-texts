Autologous Blood Clot Therapy for Wounds: Investigating the Chemotactic Effect on PBMCs and Fibroblasts in Diabetes
Authors: Kayla Howard, Priyanka Dhanraj, Kayleigh Armstrong, Mari van de Vyver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTopically applied autologous blood clot therapy (TABCT) is a low‐cost regenerative modality proposed for complex wounds, yet its biological mechanism of action remains poorly defined. We investigated whether factors released from autologous blood clots promote peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) chemotaxis and fibroblast migration, and whether these effects are altered in individuals with metabolic syndrome/type 2 diabetes (MetS/DM). Twenty‐two participants (controls n = 12; MetS/DM n = 10) were recruited. Autologous clots were generated ex vivo, cultured for 24 h, and the secretome analysed for total protein, PDGF‐BB, P‐selectin and CCL‐5. PBMC basal activation (ROS production, phagocytosis, migration, Ca2+ flux) was assessed, followed by live‐cell chemotaxis assays. Human dermal fibroblast migration was evaluated using a scratch assay. MetS/DM‐derived PBMCs demonstrated heightened basal activation, including increased ROS production, migration velocity and sustained intracellular Ca2+ flux. Clots from MetS/DM participants released less total protein but significantly higher P‐selectin levels, consistent with platelet hyperactivation. Despite containing measurable growth factors, clot‐derived secretomes did not significantly enhance PBMC migration velocity or distance travelled, although directionality modestly increased. Similarly, secretome exposure failed to stimulate fibroblast‐mediated wound closure (< 30% closure across groups). Pretreatment of blood with metformin, prednisone or amoxicillin had negligible effects on secretome composition or functional outcomes. These findings demonstrate that while TABCT‐derived secretomes exhibit chemotactic properties, they do not substantially promote immune cell recruitment or fibroblast migration in vitro. The clinical benefits of TABCT may therefore be attributable primarily to its fibrin scaffold properties rather than bioactive factor release. Further mechanistic and translational studies are warranted.Keywords: calcium, diabetes, fibroblasts, PBMCs, P‐selectin, wound healing

Introduction

Wound healing is one of the most complex physiological processes. Disruption of this highly orchestrated process due to either microbial infection or underlying pathology leads to the development of chronic wounds [1, 2, 3, 4]. These complex wounds pose a significant challenge for low‐resourced environments that don't have access to advanced regenerative modalities. The use of topically applied autologous blood clot therapy (TABCT) as a cost‐effective regenerative modality has been proposed [5], yet there is a lack of published studies on its biological mechanism of action.

This autologous therapy is thought to mimic the initial haemostasis response following injury. To prevent blood loss, vasoconstriction occurs immediately, followed by platelet activation, adhesion and aggregation at the site of injury [6, 7, 8]. This coincides with the release of clotting factors resulting in the deposition of a fibrin clot which serves as a temporary matrix [1, 6, 7]. The activated platelets release multiple growth factors from their alpha granules including P‐selectin, platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ), insulin‐like growth factor (IGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which initiate the repair process through recruitment of immune cells and activation of fibroblasts [9, 10]. This interaction of soluble mediators and growth factors with the extracellular matrix is a crucial first step in the healing cascade. The premise for TABCT is thus based on this initial response; it consists of blood taken from the patient that is then mixed with kaolin powder and calcium gluconate suspension to hasten clot formation and is applied topically to cutaneous wounds to facilitate healing [11]. It is not manipulated, separated or augmented and is therefore considered a low‐risk autologous biological therapy. A consensus statement from a panel of wound care experts has recommended its use in complex wounds for patients that cannot undergo sharp debridement or if the entire wound bed cannot be visualised. It is stated that the autologous blood clot can completely fill wound voids and form a protective barrier against bacterial ingress and infection [5, 11]. The foundational knowledge underlying the consensus statement is from reviews of the literature and the current working hypothesis is that the TABCT assists in maintaining a moist wound bed, contributes to autolytic debridement and stimulates the normal healing cascade through the controlled release of growth factors and cytokines over several days [12]. Yet there are no studies to support this proposed mechanism of action and it is unclear if the release of bioactive factors is altered in patients with metabolic disturbances given the known multifactorial cellular dysregulations evident in diabetic patients [13, 14, 15].

A primary objective of this ex vivo study was therefore to assess if the bioactive factors released by autologous blood clots promote immune cell chemotaxis and fibroblast migration to facilitate healing and how it is altered in patients with metabolic dysfunction. A secondary objective was to determine if regularly prescribed pharmaceutical drugs adversely affect the ability of the TABCT to stimulate the progression of healing.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC #S22/05/085) as well as the Research Ethics Committee: Biosafety and Environmental Ethics (REC:BES #BEE‐2022‐24,822) at Stellenbosch University. Permission to conduct this research at Tygerberg Hospital was granted by the Western Cape Department of Health (WC_202208_006) in accordance with the Tygerberg Hospital Research Policy and Protocol. This study was conducted according to the ethical guidelines and principles for research as per the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participant Recruitment

Twenty‐two (n = 22) individuals volunteered to participate in the study and provided informed consent. Upon recruitment, the participants were screened according to the following inclusion/exclusion criteria: not pregnant, not an active smoker, no history of drug/alcohol abuse, no known infectious disease (HIV, TB, COVID‐19), no signs of flu or cold‐like symptoms, no additional medical conditions/disease (asthma, cancer, autoimmune disease).

Anthropometric measures included height (cm) (portable stadiometer), weight (kg) (standard platform scale, Ms3400 Scale Digital 300Kg with Height Rod), hip and waist circumference (cm). Body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2) was calculated using the following formula: body weight (kg)/height squared (m2). Blood pressure (mmHg) was measured in the supine position using a blood pressure monitor (Contec CMS5100 Patient Monitor). A random blood glucose measurement (mmol/L) was obtained using a simple finger needle prick and a glucometer (Contour plus, BAYER, Germany) and HbA1c levels (%) were noted from the clinical files. Each participant's lipid profile (total cholesterol (mmol/L), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) (mmol/L), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) (mmol/L) and triglycerides (TGS, mmol/L)) was determined using the Affinion 2 point‐of‐care device (Abbott). The atherogenic index was calculated using the following formula: (total cholesterol–HDL)/HDL.

PBMC Isolation and Functional Analysis

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from whole blood collected in the BD Vaccutainer ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes. The whole blood was carefully layered upon an equal volume of Histopaque‐1077 (Sigma‐Aldrich), and the PBMCs were isolated using density centrifugation (1850 RPM for 30 min@RT). The opaque layer containing the PBMCs was transferred to a new tube, washed twice with phosphate buffer solution (centrifuged (1450 RPM for 10 min@RT)) and resuspended in freezing media and stored at −80°C. The freezing media consisted of 200 μL Human Serum Albumin (HSA) (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) (50 mg/mL stock), 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) and 70% Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Capricorn Scientific, Germany). For each participant, two vials of PBMCs were cryopreserved. When the PBMCs were utilised for the respective assays, the cells were thawed by placing the cryovials in a water bath (37°C) for 1 min, followed by gradual addition of prewarmed standard growth media (SGM). The SGM consisted of 4.5 g/dL high glucose DMEM with ultra‐glutamine (BE12‐604F/U1; BioWhittaker, Lonza, Switzerland), 10% FBS (S0615; Biochrom Superior, Switzerland) and 2% penicillin/streptomycin (DE17602E; BioWhittaker). The cell suspension was incubated for 1 h under standard culture conditions (5% CO2 in a humidified incubator) before performing a cell count and seeding cells for subsequent experiments.

The basal activity of the individual patient‐derived PBMCs was determined by assessing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production using the 2,7‐dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH‐DA) fluorescent probe (ElabScience ROS Fluorometric assay kit) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The mean fluorescent intensity (MFI) was measured using the VICTOR Nivo Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, South Africa). Phagocytic activity of PBMCs was determined by culturing the cells in the presence of FITC‐labelled rabbit Ig‐G coated latex beads (Caymen Chemical, USA) (1:100) for a period of 4 h at 37°C, 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. At the end point, the cells were washed and subsequently stained with Trypan Blue quenching solution to quench any surface‐bound FITC. This was necessary to distinguish between internalised beads (phagocytosed) and surface‐bound beads. The nuclei of cells were stained with NucBlue Live Ready Probes and microscopy performed (Nikon Eclipse TI2 fluorescent microscope, x20 objective). The phagocytic capacity of the PBMCs was assessed by quantifying the number of internalised beads.

The Fluo‐4 calcium imaging kit (Life Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to assess the intracellular signalling and mobilisation of Ca2+ within PBMCs using an established protocol. The cells were plated (75,000 cells/cm2) in 8‐well chamber plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. The Fluo‐4/AM loading solution was prepared which constituted 12.5 μL 100× Powerload Concentrate, 1.25 μL 1000X Fluo‐AM, 1.25 mL SGM, 12.5 μL 100× Probenecid Stock and 125 μL Neuro Backdrop Background Suppressor solution. The culture media was removed, and the cells were gently rinsed with prewarmed sterile PBS, after which 150 μL of the Fluo‐4/AM loading solution was added. The cells incubated for 15 min at 37°C, followed by a 30 min incubation at RT. The loading solution was removed, and the cells were rinsed with prewarmed PBS. To each well 50 μL of live cell imaging solution was added. Imaging was performed using the Zeiss Axio Observer 7 inverted microscope (Zeiss Group, Germany) at 40× magnification using the green, fluorescent channel/FITC channel (excitation 494 nm/emission 506 nm). For each sample, three technical repeats were performed and analysed using ImageJ software. The total area under the curve (AUC), peak AUC, peak intensity and peak duration were determined by quantifying the mean fluorescent intensity of the flux over time.

Autologous Blood Clot Induction, Culture and Harvesting of the Secretome

Blood samples collected in citrate tubes (Vacucare; Sterile Sodium Citrate Blood Collection Tubes; UK) were used to ‘spike‐in’ pharmaceutical drugs prior to the induction of whole blood clots. The whole blood samples from each participant were divided into five equal volumes (900 μL) and treated with the human equivalent dose (HED) of either metformin (D150959‐5G; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), prednisone (P6254‐1G; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), amoxicillin (A8523‐5G; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) or untreated (0 μg/mL PBS vehicle control) (Table 2). The ‘spiked‐in’ blood samples were allowed to incubate for 30 min at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator before inducing the whole blood clot.

Following incubation, 200 μL of coagulation mixture [10% calcium gluconate (PHR2669; Supelco, Merck, USA) in 5 mL phosphate‐buffered saline containing 35 mg kaolin powder (Kaolin K7375; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA)] was added to the ‘spiked‐in’ whole blood and coagulation was allowed to occur over a period of 10 min. The clots were subsequently transferred into 6‐well culture plates (Nest Biotechnology, China) (500 μL clot per well) containing 1500 μL Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Media (DMEM) (10,566,016; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, SA) and incubated for 24 h at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. The conditioned media (clot secretome) was harvested, centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min to discard any cellular debris, and the supernatant stored at −80°C until subsequent use.

Protein concentration within the clot secretome was determined spectrophotometrically using the Direct UV method (Nano‐drop, Jenway; 7415, Cole‐Parmer, USA). The concentrations of platelet‐derived growth factor‐BB (PDGF‐BB) (E‐EL‐H1577, Elabscience, USA), P‐selectin (SEA569Hu; Cloud‐Clone Corp, USA) and CCL‐5 (Proteintech, USA) were determined using sandwich ELISA assays and the absorbance read at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan GO 1.00.40; Thermo‐Scientific Group, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

PBMC Chemtoxis Assay: Determining the Chemotactic Effect of the Autologous Blood Clot‐Derived Secretomes

PBMC migration towards the chemotactic stimuli of the respective autologous blood clot secretome was assessed using live cell imaging. The participant‐derived PBMCs (30,000 cells) were seeded in the bottom left corner of each well of 8‐well chamber dishes and allowed to adhere for 1 h under standard culture conditions. The PBMCs were stained with NucBlue Live Ready Probes (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to allow visualisation of the cell nuclei and enable the tracking of cellular movement. Following adherence, 75 ul of the respective autologous clot‐derived secretome for each treatment group were added to the opposing top right corner of the well to create a chemotactic gradient. The Zeiss Axio Observer 7 inverted microscope (Zeiss Group, Germany) was used for live cell imaging to capture the migratory paths of the PBMCs. Time lapse images were captured every 4 min for 40 min, at 20× magnification. Trackmate plugin (ImageJ Java Script) was used to identify and assess individual cell trajectories, whereafter the IBIDI Chemotaxis and Migration Tool was used to analyse the migration pathways and quantify velocity (μm/s), distance (accumulated and Euclidean) (μm) and the directionality index.

Dermal Fibroblast Scratch Assay: Determining the Stimulatory Effect of the Autologous Blood Clot‐Derived Secretomes

Human dermal fibroblast (HDFs) (106‐05a; Lot #1454, Cell Applications, US) cells were cultured in accordance with standard cell culture procedures in growth media (seeding density 5000 cells/cm2). Growth media consisted of 4.5 g/L high glucose DMEM with ultra‐glutamine (BE12‐604F/U1; BioWhittaker, Lonza, Switzerland), 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (Pen/Strep) (DE17602E; BioWhittaker) and 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) (S0615; Biochrom Superior, Switzerland). A monolayer of cells 80%–90% confluent in 48‐well plates was scratched using a P200 pipette tip to induce a thin wound in a straight line down the middle of each well. Immediately after injury, the cells were exposed to the participant‐derived autologous blood clot secretome and the rate of wound closure was monitored over a period of 24 h. Three images of each well were taken under the microscope (Olympus CKX41; inverted phase contrast cell culture microscope, Germany) at 10× magnification directly after inducing the scratch (0 h) and following 4, 6, 8, 10 and 24 h of subsequent incubation at 37°C. Image analysis was performed using Fiji (Image J software) to determine the wound area. Percentage wound closure (%) was calculated using the following formula: [(wound area 0 h—wound area nh)/(wound area 0 h)] × 100. The scratch assay was conducted in triplicate for each treatment condition per participant.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K‐S) normality test with Lilliefors correction and the Shapiro–Wilk test were used to assess the normal distribution of data. Descriptive data are reported as either/N (%), means (SD) (normally distributed) or median (interquartile range) (non‐parametric data). Mann–Whitney non‐parametric test was done to determine differences between control and MetS/DM groups. Multivariate analysis (ANOVA) with Sidak's multiple comparisons test was done to determine differences between treatment groups. The threshold for significance was accepted at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant Characteristics

All the participants were recruited from the same sociodemographic area. The metabolic parameters for the control participants (n = 12) were within the expected normal range, whereas the individuals with MetS/DM (n = 10) were slightly older (C 42 ± 6; MetS/DM 49 ± 9 years) (p < 0.05) (Figure 1A), obese (C 28 ± 6; MetS/DM 39 ± 9 kg/m2) (p < 0.01) (Figure 1B) and had elevated blood glucose levels (C 5.2 ± 0.7; MetS/DM 12.5 ± 5 mmol/L) (p < 0.001) (Figure 1C) with increased systolic blood pressure (C 125 ± 8; MetS/DM 157 ± 30 mmHg) (p < 0.01) (Figure 1D). Compared to controls, the MetS/DM participants furthermore had dyslipidaemia as evident in lower HDL (C 2.38 ± 0.94; MetS/DM 1.16 ± 0.7 mmol/L) (p < 0.01), higher triglyceride (C 1.05 ± 0.39; MetS/DM 1.67 ± 0.59 mmol/L) (p < 0.05) levels, a higher cholesterol:HDL ratio (C 3.5 ± 0.9; MetS/DM 4.5 ± 0.9) (p < 0.05) and atherogenic index (C 1.19 ± 0.94; MetS/DM 3.01 ± 1.16) (p < 0.01) (Figure 1E,F). Taken together these data confirm the presence of metabolic dysfunction within the MetS/DM group.

Basal PBMC Activation Status

At baseline (without stimulation) the PBMCs derived from MetS/DM participants showed signs of hyperactivity in comparison with cells derived from controls (Figure 2A–D). The release of ROS was significantly higher (1.12× fold, p < 0.01) in the MetS/DM PBMCs (Figure 2A), and although the same trend was evident with regard to phagocytic activity, this was not significant due to individual variability (Figure 2B). The PBMCs from MetS/DM participants also had increased movement speed, as was evident in a higher migration velocity (C 9 ± 5; MetS/DM 16 ± 6 μm/s) (p < 0.05) compared to controls (Figure 2C). Given the absence of stimulation at baseline, the movement of cells was random in both groups with a directionality index of 0.43 (Figure 2D). The intracellular Ca2+ flux capacity was also assessed and is presented in Figure 2E. In the control participants, following the peak flux, intracellular Ca2+ levels returned to baseline. However, in the MetS/DM participants, following the initial flux, intracellular Ca2+ levels steadily increased. Upon quantification, a trend (p = 0.0653) was evident in the total AUC between the control (1.46 ± 1.61 AUC) and MetS/DM (4.92 ± 4.67 AUC) groups (Figure 2E).

Autologous Blood Clot Secretome

The total amount of protein released and the constituents of the clot secretome were assessed after a period of 24 h in culture. A representative image illustrating the autologous blood clot in culture is presented in (Figure 3A). Quantification of the total amount of protein released from the clots relative to the initial clot size indicated that total protein levels tended to be lower in the clots derived from the MetS/DM participants compared to controls. This observation was significant in the secretome of clots pretreated with either metformin (C 12.7 ± 4.6; MetS/DM 8.3 ± 2.6 μg/mL, p < 0.01) or amoxicillin (C 12.8 ± 2.7; MetS/DM 8.9 ± 3.4 μg/mL, p < 0.01), whereas prednisone (C 8.9 ± 2.9 μg/mL) significantly (p < 0.05) reduced the total protein content compared to metformin (C 12.7 ± 4.6 μg/mL) in the control participants only (Figure 3B). The pharmaceutical drug pretreatment did not have an effect on the levels of PDGF‐BB, P‐selectin or CCL‐5 within the clot secretome of the respective groups (Figure 3C–E). There were however consistently higher levels of P‐selectin detected in the clot secretome derived from MetS/DM participants (MetS/DM average range 4707–5414 pg/mL) (p < 0.01) compared to controls (C average range 2304–2860 pg/mL) (Figure 3D).

Chemotaxis Effect of the Clot Secretomes on PBMC Migration

The chemotactic effect of the clot secretome was assessed by analysing PBMC migration patterns in the respective treatment groups (Figure 4A–E). In the presence of the clot secretome, PBMCs had a slower migration velocity (Figure 4B) and travelled shorter distances (Figure 4C,D) compared to standard culture conditions. The clot secretome did, however, have a chemotactic effect and increased the directionality of PBMC migration (Figure 4E). In the absence of any intervention, the PBMCs derived from MetS/DM participants showed more directional movements towards the chemotactic signal compared to Controls (C 0.4 ± 0.1; MetS/DM 0.6 ± 0.14, p < 0.01) (Figure 4E). The pharmaceutical drug pretreatments did not significantly alter the migration patterns of PBMCs in the presence of the respective clot secretomes.

Stimulatory Effect of the Clot Secretomes on Fibroblast‐Mediated Wound Closure

The ability of the clot secretome to stimulate fibroblast migration was assessed using an in vitro scratch assay (Figure 5A). In comparison with standard culture conditions, the clot secretome reduced the overall rate of wound closure and did not initiate fibroblast migration with < 30% of wound closure evident in the respective groups (Figure 5B). A significant difference in wound closure was evident in the Metformin treatment group, with more pronounced wound closure evident in the fibroblasts exposed to clot secretome derived from control (24% ± 3%) compared to MetS/DM (17% ± 4%) (p < 0.05) participants.

Discussion

Complex non‐healing wounds pose a significant challenge, especially in low‐resourced environments that don't have access to advanced regenerative modalities [16, 17, 18]. The use of autologous blood clot as a biological scaffold for the treatment of complex wounds has been proposed due to its ease of access, cost‐effectiveness, and its ability to modulate the wound environment [12, 19]. TABCT is recommended for patients in which the entire wound cannot be visualised safely and are therefore not surgical candidates to undergo sharp debridement and/or for individuals in which tendon/bone is exposed [11]. TABCT is thought to form a protective barrier that prevents bacterial ingress, maintains a moist wound bed, induces autolytic debridement and recruits/delivers factors that are essential for healing to commence [11, 12]. The mechanism of action is purely theoretical and based on existing knowledge of the physiological healing responses—with haemostasis and platelet aggregation being the first step in the healing process. Platelet hyperactivation and maladaptive phenotypes are, however, known to be associated with microvascular complications in diabetes and are thought to be driven by insulin resistance [20, 21, 22]. Indeed, this study illustrated excessive P‐selectin release, indicative of platelet hyperactivation, in obese diabetic participants [23, 24]. These individuals with metabolic dysfunction also showed signs of PBMC activation at baseline, and although the autologous clot secretome had a slight chemotactic effect, it did not significantly promote PBMC migration, nor did it stimulate fibroblast‐mediated wound closure in vitro. We furthermore investigated if regularly prescribed pharmaceutical drugs such as Metformin (anti‐diabetic), prednisone (anti‐inflammatory) and amoxicillin (antibiotic) affected the chemotactic and stimulatory effect of the clot secretome and demonstrated any observed effects to be negligible.

The participants enrolled in this study were obese, had impaired glucose control, showed signs of dyslipidaemia and elevated blood pressure levels, all of which are characteristic of diabetic individuals with multiple co‐morbidities at risk for developing chronic wounds [25], whereas the control participants were metabolically healthy. This enabled a direct comparison of the endogenous platelet and PBMC activation statuses associated with the disease pathology. In agreement with literature, constitutive PBMC activation was evident in the MetS/DM participants with elevated production of ROS [26, 27], higher rate of movement [28] and a secondary prolonged increase in Ca2+ levels following the initial flux. This observation is likely caused by store‐operated calcium entry (SOCE), which links endoplasmic reticulum (ER) store depletion to the opening of plasma membrane calcium channels [29, 30]. It is, however, beyond the scope of this study to identify the exact disease mechanisms. Dysregulation of intracellular calcium has recently been proposed as a central pathological amplifier in diabetes [31] and warrants further investigation. Despite platelet and PBMC hyperactivation, the total protein content released by the autologous clots tended to be lower in the MetS/DM participants, especially with metformin and amoxicillin pretreatment. However, the amount of PDGF‐BB and CCL‐5 released from the clots was not affected by pretreatment of autologous blood prior to clot induction, with the respective clot secretomes not having a substantial impact on immune cell recruitment or fibroblast migration.

The safety and efficacy of TABCT have been shown in several studies. In a minipig model, TABCT induced either partial or complete re‐epithelisation of full thickness wounds after 18 days, whereas control wounds showed significantly less re‐epithelialisation over the same time period [32]. Similarly, two pilot studies, one assessing the efficacy of TABCT in nine patients with chronic wounds and the other assessing its safety in 20 patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) illustrated its safety and efficacy [33, 34]. In agreement with these earlier studies, two multicentre randomised controlled trials illustrated improved percentage area reduction (PAR) and shorter healing times in the majority of DFU patients receiving TABCT [35, 36]. Although these studies, mainly conducted by the same research group, have suggested that TABCT is therapeutically effective and reported only minor complications, this therapy remains unsuccessful in a subset of patients. A study by Wang et al. reported skin necrosis in 28% of individuals with Wagner grade 4 DFUs following leukocyte‐platelet fibrin clot treatment [37]. A further limitation of these clinical studies is the lack of more in depth mechanistic investigations and only using PAR over time as an outcome measure. In the current study we illustrate for the first time that the bioactive components released from the autologous clots do not significantly enhance PBMC migration nor stimulate fibroblasts mediated wound closure in vitro. Suggesting that the beneficial effects observed in clinical studies can more likely be attributed to the presence of a fibrin matrix scaffold rather than the release of bioactive factors.
